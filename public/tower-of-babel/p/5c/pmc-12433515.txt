A hierarchical MgF2/polyurethane/pitavastatin coating alleviates degradation and enhances endothelialization of bioresorbable magnesium alloy stents
Authors: Zhenglong Dou, Yan Fu, Shuiling Chen, Manfred F Maitz, Wengtai Zhang, Keyun Li, Jingcheng Zheng, Zhen Zhang, Nan Huang, Zhilu Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRare-earth-free magnesium (Mg) alloy bioresorbable stent (BRS) exhibits significant potential in vascular intervention due to its exceptional biosafety. However, its susceptibility to corrosion complicates surface functionalization and renders existing coating strategies ineffective for degradation-remodeling kinetics, resulting in delayed re-endothelialization and excessive lumen loss. Herein, a hierarchical MgF2/polyurethane (PU)/pitavastatin (PTV) coating system is constructed on Mg-Zn-Mn BRS using elastomeric PU as an intermediate layer. Studies confirm the PU layer effectively accommodates stent deformation, alleviates stress concentrations, and confines corrosion propagation triggered by deformation-induced MgF2 microcracks. The in situ formed MgF2 layer concurrently decreases substrate reactivity, establishes stable interfaces with PU, and synergistically enhances the corrosion resistance. The surface PTV-loaded poly-L-lactic acid layer maintains sustained drug release through PU-mediated interfacial stability while serving as an initial corrosion barrier. In vivo evaluations demonstrate the MgF2/PU/PTV-functionalized stent significantly suppresses neointimal hyperplasia in rabbit models while achieving synchronized degradation-remodeling kinetics. This hierarchical coating architecture, which synergistically integrates controlled drug elution with degradation modulation, provides a viable solution to clinical challenges of post-implant restenosis and vascular remodeling mismatch.Keywords: Bioresorbable stents, Corrosion control, Hierarchical coating, Magnesium alloys, Re-endothelialization

Introduction

Coronary intervention has evolved from balloon angioplasty and bare-metal stents to drug-eluting stents (DES), significantly improving clinical prognosis for cardiovascular patients [1,2]. However, accumulating evidence associates permanent DES retention with complications such as late stent thrombosis (LST), chronic inflammation, and vasomotor dysfunction [3,4]. Bioresorbable stents (BRS) have recently emerged as a major advance in the field of cardiovascular innovation, thanks to new clinical understanding of the risks associated with permanent retention of DES. BRS provides a fundamental solution to these concerns of DES and achieves the groundbreaking idea of “intervention without permanent implantation” [[5], [6], [7]]. The two primary categories of concentration for BRS materials currently are metals and polymers. Despite being the first BRS to get FDA approval, Abbott Vascular stents were ultimately withdrawn from the market due to clinical data showing significantly higher rates of target lesion failure and LST compared to DES [8]. Metallic BRS have better mechanical properties, which enable thinner stent designs and significantly improve vascular lumen patency maintenance [[9], [10], [11]]. The majority of the metallic BRS that are now being developed are systems that are based on Mg, iron, and zinc. Zinc-based stents have mechanical flaws like strain softening and inadequate work hardening ability [12]. While alloying changes have shown some initial success, their long-term safety calls for further research. Iron-based stents are limited by excessively slow degradation rates. The therapeutic effectiveness of new designs that use nitriding to build a zinc sacrificial layer with poly-L-lactic acid (PLLA) drug coatings is still being evaluated, although they show promise in controlling degradation cycles [4]. In contrast, Mg-based stents are considered a more promising candidate for clinical application due to their exceptional biocompatibility and controlled degradation behavior [13]. Magmaris is the sole clinically approved Mg alloy stent, utilizing a WE43 alloy matrix with high rare-earth content paired with a PLLA-sirolimus (SRL) drug-eluting coating [14]. However, follow-up data indicate the presence of undegraded residues two years post-implantation [15], potentially attributable to insoluble rare-earth intermetallic phases [16]. Additionally, the long-term biosafety of rare-earth elements remains controversial [16,17]. Notably, Biotronik is actively pursuing Mg-Al alloy stent development [18], signposting an industry trend toward micro-alloyed and rare-earth-free (REF) designs [[19], [20], [21]]. Although such strategies show promise for achieving full stent degradation and absorption within one year, their clinical translation faces hurdles, including mismatched degradation-vascular remodeling and difficulties in functional modification. Premature degradation results in insufficient mechanical support duration, potentially leading to vascular elastic recoil, local accumulation of degradation byproducts, hydrogen gas aggregation, and a risk of hemolysis [22]. Most critically, intrinsic hyperreactivity of Mg surfaces disrupts functionalized coating durability while accelerating interfacial delamination throughout degradation stages.

To overcome the temporal mismatch between degradation kinetics and vascular remodeling alongside surface functionalization challenges in Mg alloy stents, researchers have proposed structural topology optimization [23,24] and organic-inorganic composite coatings [25,26]. Although Finite element analysis-driven structural design improves corrosion homogeneity via homogenizing stress distribution, its corrosion modulation efficacy remains intrinsically limited by electrochemical dissolution propensity of Mg alloy [7,23]. This thermodynamic constraint has positioned surface engineering as the central focus in Mg alloy BRS development [27]. Nevertheless, traditional single-layer coatings fail to reconcile the tripartite demands of degradation controllability, mechanical stability, and biofunctional efficacy under pulsatile hemodynamic conditions. For instance, while MgF2 coatings enable in situ passivation layer formation [28,29], their low fracture toughness promotes crack initiation and propagation at stent bending sites during radial deformation, ultimately triggering accelerated localized corrosion [30]. Composite coating architectures provide multifunctional integration pathways, notably drug-eluting polymer coatings that couple viscoelasticity with therapeutic delivery [27,31]. Recent studies have explored bifunctional coatings, such as PLLA composites incorporating surface-functionalized silica nanoparticles, which significantly reduce Mg corrosion while enhancing endothelial compatibility [32]. Systems fabricated by layering PLA/PLLA over MgF2 substrates demonstrate enhanced corrosion resistance [25,26]. However, polyester-induced acidification and stress-strain mismatch still provoke selective corrosion at strain-concentrated regions [33]. Although poly (1,3-trimethylene carbonate) (PTMC) yields near-neutral degradation byproducts [34], its inadequate mechanical strength leading to coating failure continues to constrain its protective efficacy [30]. Therefore, the development of advanced polymer systems that integrate mechanical resilience, biocompatibility, and controllable corrosion modulation has emerged as a central challenge in surface engineering for Mg alloy BRS.

Polyurethane (PU), versatile polymers with broad applications, have advanced notably in recent years. The creation of thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) in particular has transformed industrial applications and material properties. TPU is a block copolymer with alternating soft and hard segments [35], it has shown significant potential in medical device applications due to its tailorable mechanical properties, thermoplastic processability, and proven biocompatibility [[36], [37], [38]]. TPU are classified into aliphatic and aromatic types based on the chemical structure of their isocyanate components. Aromatic TPU, containing benzene rings, may release carcinogenic and cytotoxic aromatic amines during degradation [39]. In contrast, aliphatic TPU exhibits superior biocompatibility, making it more suitable for biomedical applications. The biocompatibility of PU has been clinically established in long-term implantable devices such as artificial hearts and artificial blood vessels [40]. Its elastic modulus closely matches that of biological tissues, while its thermoplastic nature and solubility in organic solvents enable uniform coating deposition on geometrically complex devices. However, its application in surface functionalization of Mg alloy BRS remains underexplored. It is crucial to emphasize that the high toughness of PU and its vascular tissue interfacial compatibility confer distinct advantages in Mg alloy stent coating systems. Therefore, PU holds promise for overcoming the functional limitations of traditional polymer coatings, providing a strategic pathway toward developing hierarchical Mg alloy stent coatings with unified integration of mechanical adaptability, corrosion regulation, and biological functionality.

The therapeutic effectiveness of vascular stents for lesion repair is greatly aided by drugs. As antiproliferative drugs, paclitaxel, SRL and its variants are mainly used in current DES and Magmaris stents. Although SRL suppresses neointimal hyperplasia, it increases the risk of late-stage thrombosis because it delays endothelial repair non-selectively [[41], [42], [43]]. To address this issue, recent studies have proposed novel strategies based on coating structure design. For example, a bilayer coating prepared via sequential spraying-comprising an inner layer of polyethyleneimine (PEI) and an outer layer of PLGA/PEI loaded with SR-has been successfully applied to WE43 Mg alloy stents. This multifunctional coating shows significant potential in vascular stent applications by effectively inhibiting neointimal hyperplasia while simultaneously promoting re-endothelialization, thereby helping to prevent in-stent restenosis (ISR) [44]. Despite progress in structural optimization and drug carrier systems, the limitations of the mechanisms of currently used drugs make it crucial to develop new agents capable of selective cellular modulation. This requires alternative drugs that can selectively regulate smooth muscle cells (SMCs) proliferation and facilitate vascular repair. Pitavastatin (PTV) is primarily known as a lipid-lowering agent, however, emerging evidence indicates that it also possesses multiple vasoprotective properties [45]. By activating endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), PTV not only enhances endothelial functional integrity but also exerts anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects through modulation of the NF-κβ/ROS signaling pathway, thereby attenuating vascular inflammation and oxidative injury [46]. Notably, it demonstrates unique cell selectivity by promoting endothelial cells (ECs) proliferation and migration while simultaneously inhibiting excessive SMCs proliferation [47], thus establishing a balance between endothelial repair and hyperplasia inhibition. This multi-effect mechanism accelerates vascular functional recovery while simultaneously inhibiting the progression of atherosclerotic plaques and the pathological process of ST [48], underscoring its potential as a candidate molecule for drug coating in vascular interventional therapy.

Here, the Mg-Zn-Mn (ZM21) alloy is used as a stent platform in this study. The utilization of a REF Mg alloy greatly improves biocompatibility and eliminates concerns about possible toxicity and long-term accumulation of rare-earth elements, which promotes long-term clinical safety and translational potential in comparison to commonly used clinical stents made from high rare-earth-content Mg alloys. A hierarchically functionalized coating strategy was engineered for Mg-Zn-Mn (ZM21) stents aimed to overcome the current challenges of degradation rate mismatch with vascular remodeling in REF Mg alloy stents and the constraints in functional modification strategies. An MgF2 passivation layer at the base, a PU layer in the middle, and a PTV drug layer on top make up this hierarchically coating structure. In addition to stabilizing the interface of PTV coating, the PU-mediated intermediate layer efficiently mitigates stress concentration-induced localized corrosion acceleration in MgF2 that occurs during stent deformation. The passivation effect of MgF2 synergistically enhances the corrosion resistance of highly reactive substrates while significantly improving interfacial stability with PU. An initial corrosion barrier is created by the top PTV drug layer, which also regulates the local inflammatory environment and levels of oxidative stress by regulated release of PTV. Additionally, PTV effectively inhibits platelet activation and monocyte chemotaxis. This synergistic effect promotes endothelial regeneration and reduces excessive proliferation of vascular SMCs, thereby significantly lowering the risk of ISR (Fig. 1A). Moreover, it avoids the endothelial dysfunction commonly associated with the non-selective inhibitory effects of SRL, a drug widely used in conventional DES. Coordination of corrosion regulation and biological functions is achieved through the integrated inorganic-organic-functional hierarchical design. This design demonstrates dynamic matching between stent degradation and vascular repair. During the initial implantation stage, which lasts for one month, mechanical integrity and lumen patency are maintained to speed up endothelialization. In the mid-term, which lasts for three to six months, there is controlled degradation to match the progression of tissue repair. Complete absorption is expected within one year, which effectively avoids the accumulation of degradation products and delayed inflammatory responses caused by long-term retention in traditional metal stents (Fig. 1B). This hierarchically composite coating system that synergistically optimizes corrosion resistance and bioactivity, providing an innovative strategy to advance the clinical translation of REF Mg alloy BRS.

Preparation of the MgF2/PU/PTV composite coating

The preparation of MgF2/PU/PTV composite coating was done through the following steps: First, the MgF2 coating was fabricated according to the method described in Ref. [30]. Briefly, the ZM21 stents or sheets were immersed in 40 % HF solution under shaking (300 r min−1) at 25 °C for 8 h, then washed with AE and dried. Next, a PU solution in tetrahydrofuran (THF, 2 mg mL−1) was sprayed onto the MgF2-coated surface using an ultrasonic atomization spray coater at a flow rate of 0.05 mL min−1, with a nozzle-to-substrate distance of 8 mm and a rotational speed of 180 r min−1. After 30 spraying cycles, the MgF2/PU coating was obtained, and the samples were subsequently heat-treated at 130 °C for 1 h. Then, PTV was dissolved in a PLLA solution at mass ratios of 5 %, 10 %, and 20 % (with dichloromethane as the solvent, 2 mg mL−1) and sprayed onto the surface at a flow rate of 0.05 mL min−1 for 15 spraying cycles. Finally, the samples were dried in a vacuum oven at room temperature for 12 h to complete the fabrication of the MgF2/PU/PTV composite coating (Fig. 2A). The stent with the drug-loaded layer prepared by spraying PTV dissolved at a mass ratio of 10 % in a PU solution (with THF as the solvent, 2 mg mL−1) using the same spraying parameters is denoted as MgF2/PU-PTV.

Material characterizations

The micro-structural morphology of the coating was examined using high-resolution scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JSM-7001F, Japan). Macroscopic surface features of the coating on the stent after crimping and expansion were characterized by optical microscopy (NSZ-608T, Nanjing Jiangnan Novel Optics Co., Ltd., China). To measure the coating thickness, the stent was first cut using microscissors, and the cross-sections were exposed by mounting the samples vertically on the stage. Elemental distribution and content were analyzed through area scan mode using energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, Bruker XFlash 6–60, Germany). The interfacial adhesion strength between coatings was measured using a peel test at a peeling angle of 90°, with the testing machine operated at a speed of 25 r min−1 (MK-BL-X, Dongguan Maike Instrument Equipment Co., Ltd., China). The radial support force of the stents was tested using an electronic universal testing machine (AGS-X 2 kN, Shimadzu, Japan) at a loading rate of 1 mm min−1. The chemical structures of MgF2, MgF2/PU, and MgF2/PU/PTV composite coatings were characterized using a grazing angle attenuated total reflection Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (GATR-FTIR, Nicolet 5700, USA). The water contact angle (WCA) of the samples was measured using a Krüss GmbH DSA 100 Mk 2 goniometer (Hamburg, Germany) to evaluate the wettability of the coating. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, ThermoFisher Nexsa, America) was employed to investigate the surface elemental composition and chemical states of the samples.

Crimped and then expanded bare stents, MgF2, MgF2/PU, and MgF2/PU/PTV modified stents were immersed in 250 mL of SBF at 37 °C under static conditions. Throughout the duration of the test, the SBF remained unchanged. Hydrogen release was monitored and recorded at various time points. Samples were collected at 1 min, 6 h, 12 h, 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, 14 days, 21 days, 38 days, and 45 days for further analysis. In vitro biocompatibility testing

Extract preparation

The preparation of extract solutions followed ISO 10993–5:2009 and relevant guidelines [50,51]. The sterilized sheets were used to prepare extraction media at a fixed mass-to-volume ratio (0.2 g mL−1). The culture media included ECs medium (90 % DMEM + 10 % FBS), SMCs medium (50 % SMCM + 45 % RPMI 1640 + 5 % FBS), and RAW 264.7 macrophage (MΦ) culture medium (90 % RPMI-1640 + 10 % FBS). After 72 h of incubation at 37 °C, extracts were filtered through 0.22 μm membranes for subsequent assays. Prior to cell culture experiments, the filtered extracts were diluted tenfold according to established protocols [51]. The pH value was measured using a pH meter (LC-PH-4S, China), and the Mg2+ concentration was determined using ICP-MS (iCAP TQ, Thermo Scientific, USA).

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein ECs (HUVECs), human umbilical artery SMCs (HUASMCs), and MΦs were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. All cell lines were maintained and passaged in a cell culture incubator at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. Subculturing was performed when cells reached approximately 80 % confluence. Subsequently, cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells mL−1 in 24-well plates or confocal dishes. After cell attachment, the culture medium was removed, and 1 mL of the respective extract was added to each well according to the experimental groups. To evaluate the effects of different stent samples on the inflammatory regulation, LPS was incorporated into the MΦs culture medium at a concentration of 100 ng mL−1 as a pro-inflammatory agent. Cells cultured in medium without extracts and LPS served as the control group, while those cultured in medium containing only LPS were designated as the model group.

Cellular functional assays

Cell proliferation and viability analysis: The proliferation and viability of HUVECs and HUASMCs were assessed after incubation with the extracts for 24 and 72 h using a CCK-8 assay kit. Following the viability assay, the cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde and subsequently stained with either rhodamine 123 or SF555-phalloidin combined with DAPI for morphological analysis. An inverted fluorescence microscope (Thermo Scientific EVOS M5000, USA) was employed to capture and analyze the cellular morphology.

Gene Expression Analysis: RNA extraction and gene expression profiling followed standardized protocols. Briefly, total RNA was isolated using TsingZol reagent (TSP401, Tsingke Biotech, China), and cDNA was synthesized with HiScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed on a QuantStudio 3 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) using Taq Pro Universal SYBR master mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), following the manufacturer's thermal cycling parameters. Gene expression was quantified via the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method, normalized to GAPDH. Primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

Evaluation of in vitro immunomodulatory capacity

Immunofluorescence: For simultaneous detection of M1/M2 macrophage polarization markers, double immunofluorescence labeling was performed using established protocols. Briefly, adherent cells were fixed and permeabilized prior to immunostaining. Primary antibody incubation was performed at 4 °C for 16 h using anti-CD86 (rabbit monoclonal antibody, 1:500; Abcam) and anti-CD206 (mouse monoclonal antibody, 1:500; Abcam) in parallel experiments. After three washes with PBS, cells were incubated with species-specific secondary antibodies, including goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor™ 488 and goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor™ 555 (1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific), for 1 h at ambient temperature (25 °C) under light-protected conditions. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (5 μg mL−1) for 5 min prior to imaging. Fluorescent images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSCM; Carl Zeiss LSM 980, Germany), and quantitative fluorescence intensity analysis was performed using ZEN 3.3 software (Blue edition).

Cytokine quantification: To evaluate pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, cell culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min to remove cellular debris. The concentrations of interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) were determined using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's protocol (R&D Systems).

Intracellular ROS generation was assessed using CellROX™ Deep Red reagent. Cells were loaded with 5 μM CellROX™ probe for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by a 10 min incubation with 10 μg mL−1 hoechst 33342 for nucleus staining. Fluorescence signals were captured using an LSCM, and ROS levels were quantified with ZEN 3.3 software.

Platelet adhesion analysis

Platelet adhesion was evaluated through a series of sequential processing steps. Initially, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) was isolated from citrate-anticoagulated blood (9:1 v/v) by centrifugation at 1500 rpm for 15 min. Following a 45 min incubation with PRP, samples were washed with PBS, fixed with glutaraldehyde, dehydrated using a graded ethanol/isoamyl acetate series, dried, and gold sputter-coated. SEM was then performed to assess platelet adhesion density and activation morphology.

Hemolysis test

The hemocompatibility of blood-contacting materials was evaluated through a standardized hemolysis test. Briefly, fresh anticoagulated whole blood (10 mL) was diluted with 0.9 % sodium chloride solution (8 mL) and pre-incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, 0.2 mL of the diluted blood was introduced to test samples equilibrated in 10 mL saline and incubated for 60 min at 37 °C. Parallel controls were established: normal saline (0.9 % NaCl) served as the negative control (0 % hemolysis baseline), while deionized water provided the positive control (100 % hemolysis reference). Post-incubation, all solutions were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min to pellet cellular debris. The supernatant hemoglobin content was quantified spectrophotometrically at 540 nm using a microplate reader, with triplicate measurements per sample. Exvivo and in vivo hemocompatibility

In vitro circulatory model construction and thrombosis evaluation

To simulate physiological hemodynamic conditions, an in vitro circulatory circuit model was established in New Zealand white rabbits (weight 3.0 ± 0.5 kg, Chengdu Dashuo Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.). All animal experiments adhered to the Laboratory Animal Administration Rules of China and were approved by the Dongguan People's Hospital Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee (Approval No.: IACUC-AWEC-202401502).

A microsurgical technique was used to anastomose the left common carotid artery and the right external jugular vein to construct a closed-loop circulatory system [[52], [53], [54]]. Pre-compressed bare Mg alloy stents and surface-modified stents (MgF2, MgF2/PU, MgF2/PU/PTV) were implanted at the catheter midsection of the circulatory system, and blood circulation was maintained for 2 h (Fig. 7D). After the experiment, the catheter containing the sample was removed, photographed, and the thrombus mass was weighed. Subsequently, the samples were fixed in a 2.5 % glutaraldehyde solution at 4 °C for 12 h, followed by dehydration using a gradient ethanol series (30 %, 50 %, 70 %, 90 %, and 100 % v/v) and overnight drying at room temperature. Finally, the surface morphology of the stents was characterized using SEM.

In vivo thrombosis evaluation

To evaluate the anti-thrombosis performance of the MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents, a 2-h in vivo stent implantation was performed. Stents from the experimental group (MgF2/PU/PTV) and the bare group were implanted into the proximal bilateral iliac arteries using balloon catheters. Two hours post-surgery, euthanasia was carried out to collect vascular samples, which were then fixed in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde for 24 h. The samples were subsequently dehydrated, dried, and sectioned longitudinally. SEM was employed to observe thrombus attachment on the stent surfaces.

In vivo implantation of stents

In vivo stent implantation

Adult male New Zealand white rabbits were randomly assigned to four groups. Bare and MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents were deployed using a 2.75 × 18 mm balloon catheter system. After anesthesia induction with sodium pentobarbital (30 mg kg−1, intravenously), stents were implanted bilaterally into the common iliac arteries via percutaneous intervention, ensuring that the implanted vessel segment had a diameter match >90 %. After 3 days, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months of implantation, the experimental animals were euthanized, and arterial tissues containing the stents were harvested for subsequent analysis.

Micro-CT analysis

To assess the degradation characteristics of the stents, micro-CT scanning was performed on the bare and MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents at three time points: 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months. Imaging was conducted using the μCT 100 system with the following settings: 70 kV tube voltage, 114 μA current, and 230 ms exposure time, yielding tomographic images with a spatial resolution of 6.6 μm. Three-dimensional reconstruction was performed using Avizo Lite 2019 and Mimics Research 21.0 software, and the lumen loss rate and residual volume of the stent were quantified.

Histological analysis

Arterial samples were randomly grouped for morphological and histological assessments. For SEM analysis, the stented vessel was transversely sectioned into two parts. One segment was longitudinally cut along its axis and preserved in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde for 24 h. After gradient dehydration and drying, the samples were transferred to a vacuum sputtering system for gold coating and subsequently analyzed using SEM to examine the microstructure.

For immunofluorescence analysis, the samples were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 4 h and then underwent permeabilization as follows: incubation with 0.5 % Triton X-100 (prepared in PBS) at room temperature for 30 min, followed by blocking with 1.5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA/PBS) at 4 °C for 12 h. The antibody incubation procedure was as follows: mouse anti-rabbit CD31 primary antibody (1:100, containing 1.5 % BSA) was incubated at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:200, containing 1.5 % BSA) at 4 °C for 12 h. Cytoskeletal and nuclear staining was performed using SF555-labeled phalloidin (1:1000, containing 1.5 % BSA) for 24 h in the dark and DAPI (1 μg mL−1, containing 1 % BSA) for 10 min at room temperature. Fluorescence images were collected using LSCM, and image analysis was performed using ZEN 3.3 software.

The remaining vessel samples were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 24 h, followed by dehydration through a gradient ethanol series and clearing with xylene before paraffin embedding. Sections (5 μm thick) were prepared using a Leica RM2235 microtome and subjected to Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemical (IHC). The stained sections were mounted with neutral balsam and imaged using an Olympus CKX41 optical microscope. Histological features and IHC positivity were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Statistics and data analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (v30.0). Intergroup comparisons utilized Student's t-test (two groups) or one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test (multiple groups). Each experiment included three technical replicates to ensure methodological consistency. Statistical significance was defined as ∗p ≤ 0.05, ∗∗p ≤ 0.01, and ∗∗∗p ≤ 0.001, with asterisks denoting significance levels in figures.

Results and discussion

Characterization of the coatings

Morphology and mechanical properties of the coatings

The in vivo degradation rate of Mg alloy BRS is intrinsically correlated with the mechanical adaptability and interfacial stability of surface coatings [3]. The surface morphologies of coated stents under crimped and expanded configurations were characterized using SEM, allowing for a full evaluation of the mechanical performance of different coatings (Fig. 2B and C). Experimental results demonstrated that bare stents maintained structural integrity without fracture or crack formation, confirming their operational safety. Although MgF2 stents exhibited no macroscopic fractures during crimping and expansion (Fig. 2C), microscopic cracks were observed on the stent surface after expansion (inset in Fig. 2C). Cross-sectional morphology further confirmed that these cracks penetrated to the substrate (Fig. S1B and D), which can be attributed to the inherent brittleness and limited plastic deformability of the MgF2 coating [30]. These microcracks may serve as infiltration channels for corrosive biological media, potentially compromising coating integrity, forming local corrosion cells, and accelerating localized corrosion. In contrast, MgF2/PU-coated stents preserved smooth and continuous surface topography throughout plastic deformation processes, demonstrating enhanced deformation compatibility. This behavior suggests effective stress dissipation through elastic deformation during both crimping and expansion procedures. Notably, the PTV coating of PLLA-based exhibited remarkable mechanical stability. Its maintained structural continuity under complicated deformation patterns confirms the special potential of this material to coordinate mechanical resilience with regulated drug loading capacity.

EDS surface analysis (Fig. 2D) revealed uniform Mg distribution on bare stents, while MgF2-coated stents exhibited distinct F signals confirming successful MgF2 layer formation. In MgF2/PU and MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents, the disappearance of Mg/F signals and uniform oxygen (O) and carbon (C) distribution indicated complete surface coverage by PU and PTV coatings. A homogeneous distribution of Mg is observed on the surface of the unmodified Mg stent, according to EDS imaging of the stent cross-section (Fig. 2E), whereas the signal from the carbon element in the conductive adhesive is principally responsible for the enrichment of C. A dense layer with a thickness of approximately 0.9 ± 0.1 μm is formed on the surface of the MgF2 stent, and the EDS elemental mapping confirms its chemical composition. In the MgF2/PU and MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents, the PU and PTV drug coatings had thicknesses of approximately 8.6 ± 3.2 μm and 6.1 ± 2.9 μm, respectively, with EDS analysis demonstrating the distribution of C within these layers. Furthermore, the elemental mapping of the MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stent clearly illustrated its hierarchical structure: the MgF2 conversion layer formed the innermost layer, the intermediate PU layer adhered firmly to the MgF2 surface, and the outermost PTV drug layer was tightly bound to the PU coating.

A peel strength test revealed an interfacial adhesion of about 15.25 N for the MgF2/PU coating and 17.41 N for the MgF2/PU/PTV coating (Fig. S2 B). Both the MgF2/PU and MgF2/PU/PTV samples revealed the underlying MgF2 layer after peeling, according to EDS analysis (Fig. S2 C). This suggests that the PTV-loaded PLLA coating has a significantly greater adhesion to PU than PU does to MgF2. This enhanced adhesion is primarily due to the chemical nature of the materials involved. MgF2 is an inorganic, non-polar ionic crystal with a dense and chemically inert surface, limiting its interaction with the organic PU to weak van der Waals forces or hydrogen bonding and lacking significant chemical bonding or formation of an interpenetrating polymer network at the interface. By contrast, both PU and the PTV-loaded PLLA are organic polymers. When PTV and PLLA dissolved in dichloromethane are sprayed onto the PU layer, the residual dichloromethane might swell or partly dissolve the PU surface, enabling the polymer chains to interpenetrate and entangle during the curing process. As a result, strong physical entanglement and interdiffusion occur between the PU and the PTV-loaded PLLA, forming an interpenetrating polymer network or a “solvent-welded” interface, which significantly enhances the interfacial adhesion strength.

It is evident that there are differences in the thickness of the PU coating between the MgF2/PU and MgF2/PU/PTV coated stents, which is primarily related to the ultrasonic spray coating process. Stent struts partly obstruct atomized droplets during spraying, resulting in significant differences in the amount of droplets deposited at different locations. This causes coating thickness differences, with the outside surface usually thicker than the interior [55]. Meanwhile, the presence of F element signals in the MgF2/PU and MgF2/PU/PTV stents confirms the existence of the MgF2 coating; however, its distribution is broader than that in the MgF2-coated stent. The enlarged distribution of the F element is mainly attributed to the encapsulation of fragmented MgF2 residues in the PU layer during sample preparation, as well as the subsequent adhesion of the PU containing these fragments to the Mg substrate. These findings confirm the successful fabrication of the MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents and further validate their strong interfacial adhesion and potential for safe clinical application.

Chemical characterization of the coatings

XPS and FTIR analyses further confirmed the chemical composition of the coatings. As shown in Fig. 2F, the classic Mg peaks (1303 eV, 354 eV, 304 eV, 90 eV, 52 eV) were seen in the bare samples. Additionally, there was an O 1s peak at 534 eV, which was attributed to surface oxidation [56], and a C 1s peak at 286 eV. It is generally believed that the C 1s peak is caused by the ubiquitous carbon contamination in the environment, namely MgCO3, which is generated when surface MgO/Mg(OH)2 reacts with atmospheric carbon dioxide [57]. In MgF2-coated samples, weakened O/C signals and prominent F 1s (685 eV) and F KL1 (834 eV) peaks confirmed MgF2 formation. MgF2/PU-coated samples showed a new N 1s peak (399 eV, from urethane) and vanished Mg/F signals, with enhanced C 1s intensity, verifying full PU coverage. The XPS analysis of the MgF2/PU/PTV-coated samples revealed the characteristic peak of F1s at 685 eV, indicating the successful loading of PTV. Additionally, a significant decrease in the N 1s peak at 399 eV was observed, which can be attributed to the lower nitrogen content in PTV compared to PU, leading to a reduction in the N 1s signal.

The FTIR analysis revealed that MgF2 exhibited no characteristic peaks, as it is an inorganic material (Fig. 2G). The MgF2/PU coating displayed characteristic peaks identical to those of pure PU, with peaks at 3500-3300 cm−1 attributed to the stretching vibrations of hydrogen bonds formed between the urethane (N-H) and adjacent carbonyl (C=O) groups in PU. Peaks at 2952 cm−1 and 2853 cm−1 corresponded to the symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of aliphatic C-H bonds. Additionally, the peak at 1721 cm−1 was assigned to the stretching vibration of C=O in the amide I band, while the amide II band, corresponding to N-H bending and C-N stretching, appeared at 1537 cm−1. The asymmetric stretching vibration of aliphatic C-O-C bonds was observed at 1121 cm−1 [58]. In the MgF2/PU/PTV coating, the characteristic absorption peaks of PU were not observed. Instead, peaks corresponding to PTV were detected, including the C-H stretching vibration at 2908 cm−1, the asymmetric COO− stretching vibration at 1556 cm−1, and the characteristic C-F peak at 769 cm−1 [59]. Furthermore, PLLA-related peaks were also identified, such as the ester carbonyl (C=O) stretching vibration at 1759 cm−1 and the asymmetric bending vibration of methyl groups (CH3) at 1460 cm−1 [60]. These findings confirm the successful construction of the MgF2/PU/PTV composite coating.

WCA tests revealed distinct hydrophobicity trends across modified surfaces (Fig. 2H). MgF2-coated samples exhibited a 56 % increase in contact angle (from 65.1 deg to 95 deg), confirming the intrinsic hydrophobicity of MgF2; MgF2/PU-coated surfaces achieved higher hydrophobicity (109.5 deg). The PTV drug-eluting coating displayed reduced hydrophobicity than PU. These results collectively validate the successful hierarchical design and construction of the composite coating system.

In vitro corrosion behavior

Corrosion morphology

Although a quantitative in vitro-in vivo correlation has not been established, in vitro corrosion tests are widely used early in stent development as a cost-effective screen for degradation behavior [61]. As depicted in Fig. 3A and Fig. S3, comparative analysis of bare stents versus surface-modified stents reveals distinct degradation patterns following SBF immersion. The bare stents exhibited rapid gas evolution within 1 min and experienced complete structural collapse within 6 h. An key component of SBF, the artificial pH buffer Tris, has been demonstrated to considerably speed up Mg corrosion when compared to PBS or Hank's solution, suggesting that the use of SBF as an immersion medium accelerates the corrosion of bare stents [[62], [63], [64]]. In striking contrast, surface-modified stents demonstrated progressively improved corrosion resistance. The MgF2-coated stents generated little localized bubbles in the curved regions, preserving mechanical stability for up to 6 h, but fully structural collapse by day 3. The formation of bubbles in the curving regions of both bare and MgF2-coated stents is probably associated with corrosion caused by stress concentration. Studies have shown that tensile stresses in Mg alloys facilitate the accelerated propagation of cracks in corrosive environments, resulting in faster corrosion [65]. For vascular stents, stress concentration occurs at the curved regions during crimping and expansion processes, making these areas prone to initial corrosion [23,24]. Additionally, the MgF2-coated stents develop microcracks during deformation under crimping and expansion (Fig. 2C and Fig. S1), allowing solution infiltration through these cracks at the curved regions and leading to preferential localized corrosion. The MgF2/PU bilayer configuration remarkably delayed degradation, with structural failure becoming evident only after 45 days; the tri-layered MgF2/PU/PTV composite demonstrated sustained durability, maintaining structural integrity throughout the 45-day immersion period. This corrosion resistance profile fundamentally validates the rational design strategy of hierarchical coating architectures, highlighting their potential for extended in vivo service lifetimes.

The microscopic morphology of corroded stents is presented in Fig. 3B. The bare stent exhibited an extensive cracks network by corrosion-induced after 6 h of immersion, indicative of severe crevice corrosion. EDS analysis revealed elevated O and C content at corroded regions (Fig. 3C and D), suggesting the formation of Mg(OH)2 and MgCO3 corrosion products [56,57]. In contrast, the MgF2 stent maintained structural integrity after 6 h, though significant corrosion occurred specifically at deformation zones. This localized degradation primarily originated from coating fractures and stress corrosion caused by stent crimping and expansion processes (Fig. S1), consistent with the observed generation of hydrogen bubbles during immersion (Fig. S3). The MgF2/PU bilayered coating stents exhibited enhanced corrosion resistance, maintaining surface integrity through day 21. However, significant degradation was observed by day 45, with the inset illustrating features of localized corrosion on the stent. EDS analysis further revealed localized accumulation of Mg2+ in the raised areas of the coating, which may be attributed to the tensile stress concentrated at these regions after crimping and expansion of the stent [24]. The tensile stress stretches the polymer molecular chains, increasing the intermolecular spacing and generating more microcracks within the microstructure. This creates pathways that facilitate water permeation, ultimately promoting corrosion in these areas. Most remarkably, the MgF2/PU/PTV composite coating showed negligible morphological changes during the initial 21 days. While crack formation in the PTV layer became apparent after 45 days, its prior effectiveness in preventing direct solution contact with the PU layer significantly enhanced corrosion resistance. This time-dependent degradation profile conclusively validates the synergistic enhancement mechanism of the multilayer protection system, demonstrating its synergistic corrosion resistance through temporal gradient effects.

Release behaviors of hydrogen and Mg2+

The inherent thermodynamic instability of Mg in aqueous environments triggers a galvanic corrosion process coupled with hydrogen evolution, where the hydrogen evolution rate directly indicates degradation behavior of Mg alloy. This principle was applied to assess the corrosion behavior of Mg alloy stents with different surface coatings. The schematic configuration of the hydrogen collection device for in vitro corrosion testing is illustrated in Fig. 3E. Fig. 3F presents the hydrogen evolution profiles of various coated stents during 7-day immersion in SBF. The bare stent exhibited the fastest hydrogen evolution rate, reaching a plateau within 12 h of immersion, suggesting complete degradation within this period. In comparison, the MgF2-coated stent demonstrated a reduced hydrogen evolution rate relative to the bare stent, entering its plateau phase after 3 days of immersion. This delayed stabilization indicates extended degradation enabled by the MgF2 coating's protective properties. Notably, both MgF2/PU and MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents showed negligible hydrogen evolution throughout the 7-day immersion period, further demonstrating the long-term corrosion-suppressing properties of these two coatings.

To further investigate the corrosion behavior of composite-coated stents, pH variations and Mg2+ release in solution were systematically monitored at predetermined immersion intervals (Fig. 3G and H). The pH values remained relatively stable throughout the testing period, a phenomenon attributed to the self-buffering capacity of the SBF solution that maintained systemic pH homeostasis [62]. Mg2+ release profiles revealed distinct degradation patterns among different stent groups. The bare stent demonstrated the fastest Mg2+ release rate, achieving a solution concentration of 93 μg mL−1 by day 7, indicating near-complete degradation within this time frame. While the MgF2 stent showed a moderate initial release rate, its cumulative Mg2+ concentration ultimately converged with that of the bare stent at day 7, suggesting comparable final degradation extent despite delayed onset. In contrast, the MgF2/PU-coated stents exhibited minimal Mg2+ release during the initial 7 days, followed by gradual ion release reaching 68 μg mL−1 at day 45. This release pattern confirms the effective corrosion protection during early-stage immersion of PU coating. The MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents demonstrated optimal performance, showing negligible Mg2+ release within the first 14 days and controlled slow release thereafter, indicating superior barrier properties against corrosive medium penetration.

Mass loss measurements corroborated these findings, displaying consistent trends with Mg2+ release data (Fig. 3I). Calculated degradation rates quantified these observations (Fig. 3J). Bare stents exhibited the highest corrosion rate (8.56 ± 0.61 mm y−1), followed by MgF2-coated stents (2.24 ± 0.69 mm y−1). Significantly lower corrosion rates were seen in the PU-modified stents compared to the conventional Mg alloys and pure Mg [66,67], as demonstrated by the MgF2/PU group (0.07 ± 0.01 mm y−1) and the MgF2/PU/PTV group (0.06 ± 0.01 mm y−1). These results strongly demonstrate that the hierarchical MgF2/PU/PTV composite coating can significantly enhance the corrosion resistance of Mg alloy stents and effectively regulate their degradation kinetics, highlighting their promising potential for vascular stent applications.

Radial strength

While corrosion morphology and Mg2+ release kinetics provide substantial evidence for evaluating degradation behavior, clinical applicability mandates that stent materials maintain sufficient radial strength to ensure vascular patency throughout the critical vascular remodeling period [3]. To address this fundamental functional requirement, the time-dependent radial strength evolution of various coated stents was systematically monitored during SBF immersion. As illustrated in Fig. 3K, bare stents exhibited complete loss of radial strength within 6 h of immersion. The macrostructural integrity of the stent covered with MgF2 was maintained, but its mechanical support substantially decreased after 6 h of immersion and nearly collapsed after 3 days (Fig. S4 A). This paradox between structural preservation and mechanical failure was attributed to localized severe corrosion compromising load-bearing capacity. Notably, MgF2/PU-coated stents preserved >90 % initial radial strength during the first 14 immersion days, followed by gradual strength reduction (Fig. 3L and Fig. S4). The MgF2/PU/PTV composite coating demonstrated superior mechanical stability, retaining approximately 60 % residual strength after 45-day immersion. This controlled mechanical degradation behavior is closely associated with the hierarchical protection mechanism of the coating system. The PTV drug elution layer establishes an initial corrosion barrier to prevent the initial penetration of corrosive substances; the PU barrier layer significantly delays the penetration of corrosive media; the MgF2 layer serves a chemical passivation function, impeding corrosion by obstructing the further penetration of corrosive entities. This further substantiates the exceptional corrosion control performance of the MgF2/PU/PTV coating. This feature holds promise for achieving a dynamic match between the stent degradation rate and vascular repair in clinical applications.

In vitro cytological evaluation

Optimization of PTV loading concentration and drug release kinetics

Mechanical injury induced by stent implantation triggers a cascade of pathological responses, including acute thrombosis, localized inflammation [68], and aberrant migration/phenotypic transformation of vascular SMCs, ultimately leading to ISR [69]. Timely restitution of functional endothelial barriers represents a critical therapeutic target to interrupt this vicious cycle. However, oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory mediators in pathological microenvironments often impede endothelial repair [70,71]. Statins (e.g., PTV) have gained attention due to their pleiotropic pharmacological effects. This study utilizes PLLA-loaded PTV to achieve the following functions (i) enhanced ECs regeneration, (ii) modulation of MΦs polarization for inflammatory microenvironment remodeling, (iii) suppression of SMCs hyperproliferation, and (iv) scavenging of excess reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby accelerating functional vascular repair [48,72]. Notably, therapeutic efficacy requires a precise balance between pro-reparative effects and cytotoxicity. Therefore, the dose-dependent effects of PLLA coatings loaded with 5 %, 10 %, and 20 % PTV on HUVECs and HUASMCs proliferation, as well as MΦ-mediated inflammatory modulation, were systematically evaluated to optimize the parameters of PTV coatings.

CCK-8 assay results showed that after 24 h of culture, the 20 % group significantly inhibited HUVECs viability, whereas only the 10 % group significantly promoted HUVECs proliferation at 72 h. Rhodamine staining results were consistent with the CCK-8 assay findings (Fig. S5). Furthermore, all drug-loaded groups significantly suppressed HUASMCs proliferation (Fig. S6). Further analysis revealed that all drug-loaded groups significantly reduced TNF-α and IL-1β expression compared to the control group (Fig. S7A and B), while only the 10 % and 20 % groups significantly inhibited IL-6 expression (Fig. S7C). A heatmap integrating HUVECs/HUASMCs proliferation and MΦs cytokine data identified 10 % PTV loading as optimal for balancing endothelial repair, SMCs inhibition, and inflammation regulation (Fig. S8). Therefore, MgF2/PU/PTV coatings with 10 % PTV were selected for further studies.

Next, the drug release characteristics of the MgF2/PU/PTV and MgF2/PU-PTV composite-coated stent were systematically evaluated, with particular focus on its long-term PTV release behavior. The release profile displayed a distinct biphasic release pattern (Fig. S9A and B). An initial near-linear phase (0–3 days) releasing 7.22 ± 0.38 μg PTV (14.64 ± 0.82 % of total loading), followed by cumulative release reaching 28.18 ± 0.64 μg (56.34 ± 1.15 %) by day 15. Beyond this phase, the release rate exhibited progressive attenuation while maintaining sustained drug delivery throughout the 60-day observation period, ultimately achieving 37.61 ± 2.65 μg cumulative release (77.15 ± 5.70 % of total loading). These results demonstrate that the MgF2/PU/PTV composite-coated stent, in which PTV is loaded into a PLLA matrix as the third coating layer, provides a stable and sustained drug release profile under an in vitro simulated human plasma environment (pH 7.4, 37 °C). In contrast, a comparative evaluation revealed a significantly faster release of PTV when it was directly loaded into the PU matrix of MgF2/PU-PTV coated stents (Fig. S9C and D). On day 1, the cumulative PTV release reached 14.81 ± 0.89 μg, corresponding to 23.70 ± 1.39 % of the total loading. By day 3, the cumulative amount increased to 27.42 ± 1.17 μg (44.92 ± 1.30 %). The pronounced burst release observed with the PU-loaded system within the first 3 days highlights its inability to provide controlled, long-term drug delivery. This rapid initial release is undesirable for vascular stent applications, as it may lead to suboptimal local drug concentrations over the required therapeutic period and potentially increase risks associated with high initial drug dosing. These findings further justify the incorporation of a PLLA-based drug-loading layer in the composite system, which effectively mitigates premature drug release and ensures a prolonged therapeutic window.

ROS scavenging and MΦs polarization modulation

Activated MΦ continuously produce reactive ROS during oxidized low-density lipoprotein phagocytosis and the release of pro-inflammatory mediators, leading to a significant increase in oxidative stress levels [73]. Elevated oxidative stress disrupts vascular homeostasis through mitochondrial dysfunction and DNA damage while activating pro-inflammatory pathways (e.g., NF-κβ), establishing a self-perpetuating “oxidative stress-inflammation” feedback loop that accelerates neoatherosclerotic plaque formation [74]. To disrupt this vicious cycle, the development of ROS-scavenging coating materials emerges as a critical strategy for interrupting disease progression. In this study, the ROS-scavenging capacity of MgF2/PU/PTV composite coatings was comprehensively assessed. The results demonstrated a significant increase in ROS-specific red fluorescent signals within the MΦs of the model group compared to the control group (Fig. 4A). Notably, the ROS levels in the bare group, MgF2-coated group, and MgF2/PU group remained comparable to those observed in the model group. In contrast, the MgF2/PU/PTV-coated group demonstrated a significantly reduced ROS signal intensity. These observations were corroborated by quantitative fluorescence intensity analysis, revealing values of 56.90 ± 8.08 (model group), 59.44 ± 6.10 (bare stent group), 54.26 ± 11.85 (MgF2-coated group), 54.49 ± 4.38 (MgF2/PU group), and 14.54 ± 7.22 for the MgF2/PU/PTV-coated group (Fig. 4B). This antioxidant effect likely originates from the multi-target mechanisms of PTV, including mitochondrial protection and Nrf2 antioxidant pathway activation [75].

MΦ polarization plays a pivotal regulatory role in the progression of atherosclerotic plaques. Pro-inflammatory M1 MΦs accelerate plaque progression by persistently secreting cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, whereas anti-inflammatory M2 MΦs promote plaque stability by releasing mediators like TGF-β and IL-10 [76]. Dual-label immunofluorescence analysis revealed LPS-induced M1 polarization in the model group, evidenced by elevated CD86 fluorescence and reduced CD206/CD86 ratio (0.89 ± 0.06) (Fig. 4C, D and Fig. S10). Bare, MgF2, and MgF2/PU groups showed no statistical difference in CD206/CD86 ratios compared to the model group. Strikingly, MgF2/PU/PTV coatings significantly enhanced CD206 expression (Fig. 4C), elevating the CD206/CD86 ratio to 5.59 ± 0.28 (Fig. 4D and Fig. S10), indicating PTV-mediated M2 polarization.

To further investigate the anti-inflammatory mechanism of MgF2/PU/PTV coatings, we performed qPCR to detect and quantify the expression levels of inflammation-related genes. The results revealed that the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines iNOS and IL-1β was significantly upregulated in the bare group, which may be attributed to the rapid degradation rate of the material [77]. Previous studies have shown that Mg2+ at moderate concentrations (3–6 mM) exhibit certain anti-inflammatory effects [[78], [79], [80]]. However, in our study, the Mg2+ concentration in the extract of the bare group reached 9.24 ± 0.25 mM (Table S3), which greatly exceeds the physiologically effective range and may induce cytotoxic effects. Although the Mg2+ level in the MgF2 group was lower (2.02 ± 0.04 mM), no significant anti-inflammatory effect was observed, likely due to the concentration being below the effective threshold. In addition, Mg2+ is not the sole factor influencing MΦs polarization in the corrosive microenvironment of Mg-based materials [81]. Alkaline pH also plays a critical role. Wu et al. reported that a mildly alkaline environment (pH 8.2) could induce MΦs polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype [82]. According to our measurements, the extract pH values of the bare and MgF2 groups were 8.9 ± 0.05 and 8.30 ± 0.03, respectively (Table S3). Such elevated pH levels may contribute to oxidative stress and the activation of inflammatory signaling pathways, thereby promoting M1 polarization and potentially masking or counteracting the intrinsic anti-inflammatory effects of Mg2+ [[81], [82], [83]]. In contrast, the MgF2/PU/PTV coating markedly suppressed the expression of pro-inflammatory markers iNOS and IL-1β while upregulating anti-inflammatory markers Arg 1 and YM1 (Fig. 4E–H), suggesting a potent anti-inflammatory regulatory effect. This dual regulation may be attributed to: (i) the MgF2/PU/PTV composite coating effectively controlling Mg corrosion, thereby preventing the formation of an alkaline microenvironment and excessive Mg2+-induced cellular stress; and (ii) the anti-inflammatory activity of PTV, which acts through inhibition of the NF-κβ signaling pathway (Fig. 4I) [84].

The above results demonstrate that the MgF2/PU/PTV coating not only exhibits excellent ROS scavenging ability but also modulates MΦs polarization toward the M2 phenotype, thereby alleviating inflammatory responses. This dual effect is crucial for promoting vascular repair and stabilizing atherosclerotic plaques following stent implantation.

Vascular ECs and SMCs functional regulation

A dense, functionally intact ECs layer enhances anti-thrombogenic capacity and regulates vascular tone through bioactive molecule secretion (e.g., nitric oxide, NO), playing a pivotal role in post-implantation vascular remodeling [85,86]. Rapid endothelialization following stent deployment is therefore critical. To this end, the effect of the MgF2/PU/PTV coating on ECs proliferation was evaluated. Phalloidin/DAPI staining of HUVECs cultured for 24/72 h demonstrated superior endothelial-promoting effects in the MgF2/PU/PTV group compared to the bare group at 24 h (Fig. 5A). By 72 h, MgF2/PU/PTV group exhibited significantly enhanced HUVECs proliferation, confirming excellent biocompatibility. Notably, the bare group exhibited noticeable endothelial cytotoxicity at 72 h, which may be attributed to the rapid degradation of the bare material, leading to excessive accumulation of degradation byproducts [87]. Previous studies have reported that when the concentration of Mg2+ in extract solutions exceeds approximately 200 μg mL−1 (equivalent to ∼8.23 mM), cell viability typically drops below 80 % [[87], [88], [89]]. In our study, the Mg2+ concentration in the DMEM extract from the bare stent group reached 9.98 ± 0.13 mM (Table S4), indicating that excessive Mg2+ levels are likely a key contributor to the observed cytotoxicity. The underlying mechanism may involve increased osmotic pressure that disrupts cell membrane stability and interferes with calcium-dependent signaling pathways and membrane receptor functions, ultimately disturbing essential physiological processes [51,90,91]. In addition, degradation of Mg alloys can trigger “alkaline shock” and “osmotic shock” responses, further intensifying cellular toxicity [81]. The optimal pH range for most cell types is typically between 7.4 and 7.8; deviations from this range, whether toward acidity or alkalinity, may disrupt protein synthesis and potentially induce apoptosis [92]. Notably, Mg degradation results in the accumulation of hydroxide ions (OH−), creating a highly alkaline microenvironment that is detrimental to cell viability and function. In our study, the pH of the extract from the bare stent group increased significantly to 9.16 ± 0.23 (Table S4), indicating pronounced alkaline stress, which may further compromise endothelial survival. This adverse effect on endothelial viability was further confirmed by CCK-8 assay results (Fig. 5B and C).

Statins, with vascular endothelium as a primary pharmacological target, are known to modulate endothelial function during AS and angiogenesis [93]. PTV exerts endothelial protective effects by upregulating eNOS expression while suppressing pro-inflammatory mediators like intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), facilitating post-injury re-endothelialization [94]. qPCR analysis revealed significant eNOS upregulation and ICAM-1 downregulation in the MgF2/PU/PTV group (Fig. 5D and E), indicating dual functionality in promoting ECs proliferation and alleviating inflammatory responses to accelerate vascular repair.

Suppressing SMCs hyperproliferation during the early post-implantation phase is critical for mitigating the risks of LST, ISR, and device failure. To evaluate the anti-proliferative effect of the MgF2/PU/PTV coating on SMCs, HUASMCs proliferation was assessed using phalloidin/DAPI staining and CCK-8 assays. The results demonstrated that the MgF2/PU/PTV coating significantly inhibited HUASMCs proliferation at both 24 and 72 h (Fig. 6A, B and C). Notably, at 72 h, the bare stent group also exhibited moderate inhibition compared to the control group. This effect may be attributed to the high concentration of Mg2+ (11.48 ± 0.20 mM) and the alkaline pH (9.27 ± 0.16) in the extract (Table S5), consistent with cytotoxicity observed in ECs caused by excessive degradation byproducts (Fig. 6A and C). Gene expression analysis further revealed that the MgF2/PU/PTV group significantly upregulated the expression of contractile SMCs markers α-SMA, while downregulating the expression of migration and inflammation-associated markers vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) (Fig. 6D and E), indicating that the composite coating effectively mitigates inflammation and suppresses SMCs migration. Contractile SMCs are closely associated with vascular homeostasis and functionality, maintaining vascular tone and resisting vascular injury [95]. Under PTV regulation, SMCs transitioned to a contractile phenotype, effectively suppressing abnormal proliferation and migration, which may contribute to a reduced risk of ISR.

Hemolysis

Hemolysis rate is considered a critical screening parameter, especially for implants that come into direct contact with blood. Hemolysis analysis revealed statistically significant differences among the experimental groups (Fig. S11). The bare stent group exhibited a marked hemolytic response, with a hemolysis rate of 14.67 %, consistent with previously reported high hemolytic activity of unmodified Mg alloys [87,96]. However, established standards stipulate that only materials with a hemolysis rate below 5 % are considered acceptable for medical use [97]. Therefore, the bare group fails to meet the hemocompatibility requirements for biomedical implants. The elevated hemolysis rate observed in the bare group is likely related to the substantial increase in local pH during degradation. Studies have shown that even at Mg2+ concentrations as high as 1000 μg mL−1, the direct damaging effect on red blood cells remains minimal; instead, the high pH (>11) resulting from material degradation is the main cause of significant hemolysis [87,96]. This can be attributed to the release of degradation products during the corrosion of Mg alloys. Previous research has demonstrated that, under static testing conditions, hydroxide ions generated during Mg degradation tend to accumulate at the material–solution interface rather than dispersing uniformly throughout the medium. This local buildup of OH− ions can create a highly alkaline microenvironment near the Mg surface (pH 11–12), which may compromise red blood cell membrane integrity and trigger hemolysis [98,99]. In contrast, the surface-modified samples demonstrated significantly improved hemocompatibility, with hemolysis rates for all coated groups maintained below 1 %, well within the safe threshold. This remarkable performance can be attributed to the effective regulation of Mg degradation, further highlighting the critical role of protective coatings in minimizing blood cell damage caused by Mg alloy degradation. Consequently, the modified Mg alloy stents are better suited to meet the demands of clinical applications.

Ex vivo and in vivo hemocompatibility

Platelet adhesion and activation on material surfaces constitute critical initiating factors for acute thrombosis following vascular stent implantation. Activated platelets release procoagulant factors and inflammatory mediators that further promote atherosclerotic plaque formation and progression [100,101]. Therefore, suppressing platelet activation represents a pivotal strategy for enhancing stent anti-thrombogenicity. In this study, SEM was used to assess platelet adhesion density and activation status on different sample surfaces. As shown in Fig. 7A, the bare group exhibited the highest platelet adhesion density, with extensive platelet aggregation and rupture, likely due to its rapid corrosion rate [102]. In contrast, the MgF2 group showed a reduced platelet adhesion density, consistent with previous findings, which may be attributed to the improvement of corrosion resistance [26,103]. However, the platelet activation rate remained elevated (99.6 ± 1.3 %), suggesting limited efficacy of the MgF2 coating in suppressing platelet activation. Remarkably, the MgF2/PU and MgF2/PU/PTV groups exhibited significant antiplatelet effects, particularly in the MgF2/PU/PTV group (Fig. 7A, B and C). The superior performance of MgF2/PU/PTV likely stems from synergistic mechanisms (i) the PU layer forms a dense hydrophobic barrier that mitigates blood-material interactions [104], and (ii) PTV further suppresses platelet activation through multifaceted biological pathways, including downregulation of platelet activation markers, modulation of intracellular redox states, and regulation of platelet-endothelial crosstalk [48]. Collectively, the MgF2/PU/PTV composite coating effectively suppresses both platelet adhesion and activation through a synergistic mechanism involving surface engineering, controlled corrosion kinetics, and regulation of platelet activation pathways.

An ex vivo blood circulation model was created by placing stent samples into polyvinyl chloride conduits for 2 h blood circulation simulations in order to further evaluate dynamic thrombogenic responses under haemodynamic circumstances (Fig. 7D). Macroscopic analysis revealed distinct red thrombi formation on both bare and MgF2-coated stents (Fig. 7E), consistent with previous research findings [30]. These observations suggest that the MgF2 coating may lack anti-thrombogenic properties under dynamic hemodynamic conditions. In contrast, MgF2/PU-coated stents developed minimal thrombi localized to bending regions, while MgF2/PU/PTV-coated surfaces remained virtually thrombus-free (Fig. 7E). Quantitative analysis of the thrombus weight (Fig. 7F) confirmed this trend: with the bare and MgF2 stent exhibiting the highest thrombus weight, followed by MgF2/PU stent, with MgF2/PU/PTV stents showing the lowest thrombus formation. A negative correlation between thrombus weight and luminal patency rate (Fig. 7G), further validated the enhanced thromboresistant performance of the MgF2/PU/PTV coating. SEM analysis of stent surfaces revealed a dense fibrin network on the bare stents. The MgF2-coated stents exhibited a dispersed fibrin network encapsulating RBCs. MgF2/PU surfaces showed minimal fibrin deposition and RBCs adhesion, while MgF2/PU/PTV coatings maintained pristine surfaces with negligible thrombus formation (Fig. 7H). These findings collectively demonstrate the improved thromboresistant performance of the MgF2/PU/PTV coating under dynamic ex vivo conditions.

To overcome the limitations of static and ex vivo models in accurately simulating physiological thrombosis, particularly tissue factor release, platelet activation, and coagulation dynamics triggered by vascular endothelial injury, an in vivo evaluation was conducted using a rabbit iliac artery stent implantation model (Fig. 7I). SEM analysis of explanted stents (Fig. 7J) revealed dense thrombus layers on bare stent surfaces after 2-h implantation. In contrast, MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents demonstrated minimal thrombus deposition. These results confirm the excellent hemocompatibility and anti-thrombogenic performance of the MgF2/PU/PTV coating in physiologically environments.

In vivo degradation behavior and biocompatibility

Endothelialization analysis

Long-term implantation studies were conducted in a rabbit iliac artery model to systematically evaluate the biocompatibility and degradation profiles of bare and MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents at 3 days, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months post-implantation (Fig. 8A). Rapid endothelial regeneration is widely recognized as a critical determinant of long-term efficacy for cardiovascular stents. Thus, the endothelial coverage of the bare and MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents was first analyzed at different implantation time points. SEM imaging at 3 days post-implantation revealed distinct morphological differences between the two groups. The bare stents exhibited minimal endothelial coverage, with evident thrombus adhesion on the exposed stent struts. In contrast, the MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents demonstrated the formation of a more continuous neointimal layer with negligible thrombus deposition (Fig. 8B). There was an apparent distinction in the tissue covering between the two stents at the 1 month. The struts of the bare stent exhibited obvious neointimal hyperplasia (region A) and incomplete coverage of the stent struts (region B), as seen in Fig. 8C. The MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents formed an integrated re-endothelialization, seamlessly interfacing with the surrounding arterial tissue. The endothelialization rate reached 100 %, which is significantly higher than that of the second-generation Magmaris Mg alloy stent (51.7 ± 21.2 %) [15]. The lower endothelialization rate observed with the Magmaris stent at one month may be related to its use of SRL. As an antiproliferative drug, SRL inhibits neointimal hyperplasia but also non-selectively impairs ECs function, thereby delaying the endothelialization process [[41], [42], [43]]. In contrast, PTV exhibits unique cell selectivity, promoting the proliferation and migration of ECs while inhibiting the excessive proliferation of SMCs [47]. Additionally, the macroscopic tissue images reveal that the bare stent structure collapses, leading to tissue shrinkage and deformation, which is associated with the quick corrosion of the bare stent, compared to the MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stent (Fig. 8C).

Immunofluorescence analysis corroborated these findings at the cellular level. Confocal microscopy revealed significantly higher CD31-positive ECs density on MgF2/PU/PTV-coated surfaces compared to bare stents (Fig. 8D). CD31 expression on bare stents displayed discontinuous distribution, aligning with SEM-observed coverage defects (region B, Fig. 8C), while MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stent surfaces exhibited uniform endothelial layers with continuous “honeycomb-like” CD31 patterning and characteristic tight junction architecture. Quantitative fluorescence analysis further confirmed elevated intensities for CD31 (green), cytoskeletal markers (red), and nuclei (blue) on MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents (Fig. 8E and F). These results collectively demonstrate the enhanced pro-endothelialization capacity of MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents, facilitating accelerated endothelial recovery to enhance long-term patency and mitigate thrombotic risks.

Micro-CT analysis and in vivo degradation behavior

Micro-CT-based three-dimensional reconstruction was employed to systematically assess in vivo degradation kinetics over a period of 1, 3, and 6 months post-implantation (Fig. 8G). Bare stents exhibited rapid degradation, with substantial structural loss observed by 1 month, near-complete degradation at 3 months, and only residual granular degradation products remaining at the 6-month time point. In contrast, MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents maintained structural integrity at 1 month, showing localized corrosion only at stress-concentrated regions (bending zones), consistent with in vitro degradation patterns. Progressive degradation occurred in MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents by 3 months, particularly at stress-concentrated regions, though overall stent contours remained identifiable. At 6 months, the stent exhibited further degradation, with most of its structure resorbed, yet a porti on of the stent framework remained intact.

Quantitative analysis revealed LL rates of 43.5 ± 2.2 %, 49.2 ± 3.6 %, and 55.9 ± 2.9 % for bare stents at 1, 3, and 6 months, respectively, significantly higher than those of MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents (8.6 ± 2.4 %, 30.1 ± 4.2 %, and 43.6 ± 4.3 %) (Fig. 8H). This progressive LL primarily correlated with degradation-induced radial force reduction and subsequent vascular recoil [30,105]. Residual stent volume analysis further validated the degradation-modulating capability of MgF2/PU/PTV coatings. Bare stents retained merely 7.9 ± 2.4 %, 5.8 ± 8.2 %, and 0.5 ± 0.6 % of initial volume at 1, 3, and 6 months, respectively, versus 91.1 ± 9.5 %, 52.5 ± 3.2 %, and 22.0 ± 4.0 % for MgF2/PU/PTV-coated counterparts (Fig. 8I). These findings confirm that the MgF2/PU/PTV coating provides sufficient mechanical support during early implantation while delaying degradation kinetics, thereby maintaining lumen patency and reducing vascular recoil risks.

In vivo biocompatibility evaluation

To further assess the biocompatibility and dynamic regulatory effects of MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents on vascular repair, H&E and IHC analyses (α-SMA, CD68) were performed to characterize tissue responses at sequential timepoints. H&E staining at 3 days post-implantation revealed stark contrasts between groups: Bare stents exhibited minimal endothelial coverage with exposed struts, whereas MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents demonstrated continuous neointimal layer formation across most regions (Fig. 9A). IHC indicated significantly higher α-SMA expression in the MgF2/PU/PTV stents, suggesting the coating promoted contractile phenotype differentiation of SMCs, which is critical for vascular functional stabilization. The expression of CD68 showed no significant differences among the groups at this stage, which may be attributed to the influence of the early acute inflammatory response (Fig. 9E, F and G).

(E–G) IHC analysis at different implantation time points (E), with quantitative assessment of α-SMA (F) and CD68 (G) expression. The lumen area of the bare stents decreased from 6.02 ± 0.03 mm2 at day 3 post-implantation to 1.01 ± 0.02 mm2 at 1 month. Additionally, the percentage of area restenosis increased from 1.16 ± 0.51 % to 64.58 ± 1.51 %, and the neointimal thickness increased from 4.16 ± 3.28 μm to 262.20 ± 34.18 μm (Fig. 9B, C and D). These findings are consistent with previous reports on unmodified Mg alloy stents. As an illustration, the area restenosis rate and neointimal thickness of AZ31 stents implanted in domestic pigs for 28 days were 61.5 % and 480 μm, respectively [106]. Similarly, unmodified Magic stents implanted in Bama pigs for 28 days showed an area restenosis rate and neointimal thickness of 47.49 ± 30.35 % and 382.9 ± 154.18 μm, respectively [107]. Rapid restenosis may be attributed to mechanical support loss as a result of the rapid degradation of stents after implantation, which induces vascular recoil [107]. It is important to note that the lumen area, percentage of area restenosis, and neointimal thickness of MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents were 4.59 ± 0.14 mm2, 33.73 ± 3.12 %, and 79.39 ± 30.07 μm, respectively, at the same time point. These values were significantly lower than those of bare stents and stainless steel stents in the literature (area restenosis rate: 59.00 ± 29.31 %, neointimal thickness: 636.49 ± 227.34 μm) [107]. This enhancement could be attributed to the excellent corrosion resistance of the MgF2/PU/PTV coating, which markedly reduced post-implantation degradation and maintained a relatively intact mechanical structure (Fig. 8G), thereby providing effective mechanical support to the vessel. In addition, the initial release of PTV inhibited SMCs proliferation and promoted endothelial function recovery, resulting in significantly reduced restenosis rates and neointimal thickness. The IHC analysis further demonstrated that MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents maintained the contractile phenotype, with significantly increased α-SMA expression and markedly reduced infiltration of CD68-positive cells (Fig. 9E, F and G).

At 3 months, the bare stents exhibited pathological vascular remodeling, characterized by accelerated lumen narrowing (neointimal thickness: 367.61 ± 144.79 μm; area restenosis rate: 77.07 ± 1.01 %) (Fig. 9A, B, C and D). The expression of α-SMA in the bare stents continued to decline, indicating chronic inflammation and a phenotypic switch of SMCs to a synthetic phenotype, which further exacerbated restenosis. In contrast, the MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents effectively suppressed neointimal hyperplasia, maintained a high level of α-SMA expression, and markedly reduced CD68-positive cell infiltration (Fig. 9E, F and G).

By 6 months, the bare stents were almost completely degraded, with most of the supporting regions covered by newly formed tissue; however, residual stent particles were still visible, accompanied by severe restenosis and marked neointimal hyperplasia (area restenosis rate: 80.95 ± 1.42 %; neointimal thickness: 537.46 ± 101.23 μm), consistent with the micro-CT findings. In contrast, the MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents exhibited partial coverage of newly formed tissue in some segments, indicating that complete degradation had not yet occurred. In this group, neointimal thickness (172.11 ± 40.46 μm) and area restenosis rate (55.03 ± 1.39 %) were significantly lower than those of the bare stents (Fig. 9A, B, C and D), and also lower than the lumen diameter stenosis rates reported for Everolimus-Eluting and SRL DES at 1-year follow-up (69.8 ± 15.1 % and 70.55 ± 14.79 %, respectively) [108]. However, the neointimal thickness was greater than that of SRL DES (96 ± 63 μm) but comparable to that of Zotarolimus-Eluting stents (166 ± 73 μm) [109]. Although second-generation DESs such as SRL DES significantly reduce excessive neointimal proliferation [110], ISR still tends to occur after DES implantation [111]. Studies have shown that the incidence of ISR within 6–8 months after implantation of BMS, first-generation DES, and second-generation DES is 30.1 %, 14.6 %, and 12.2 %, respectively [112]. In contrast, biodegradable stents can completely degrade after vascular remodeling, restoring normal vascular structure and vasomotor function, thereby avoiding long-term complications associated with permanent metallic implants and potentially improving ISR rates.

Notably, compared with the 3-month data (area restenosis rate: 64.13 ± 3.01 %; neointimal thickness: 208.81 ± 50.78 μm), the MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents at 6 months showed a significant improvement in restenosis rate, indicating a trend toward positive remodeling. This is consistent with previous studies [113] and may be related to the disappearance of mechanical stimulation on the vessel wall during stent degradation. Furthermore, IHC staining confirmed that the MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents induced high α-SMA expression and significantly reduced CD68-positive cell infiltration, demonstrating their long-term regulatory advantage in tissue repair (Fig. 9E, F and G). In summary, the in vivo results indicate that the MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents offer substantial benefits in promoting vascular repair and reducing restenosis.

Corrosion mechanism of MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents

The in vitro corrosion behavior and in vivo degradation characteristics of MgF2/PU/PTV-coated stents jointly reveal their multistage synergistic regulatory mechanism. As shown in Fig. 10, this composite coating achieves precise control over the corrosion kinetics of Mg alloy stents through the its layered structure: Stage I (Structural Stabilization): The intact three-layer coating system effectively blocks physiological medium penetration through physical barrier effects. Stage II (Crack Initiation): The coupling effects of vascular cyclic mechanical loads and stent expansion residual stress induce microcrack nucleation in the PLLA drug-carrying layer. During this stage, water molecules penetrate the coating, leading to the hydrolysis of ester bonds and initiating localized [4], preferential degradation of the PTV layer. Stage III (Medium Penetration): Cracks propagate along the PLLA layer and reach the PU interface. The viscoelastic intermediate PU layer, through molecular chain relaxation, significantly delays the diffusion of corrosive media, serving as a dynamic diffusion barrier and gradually forming solution diffusion pathways. Stage IV (Interfacial Reaction): Penetrated media undergo hydrolysis with the MgF2 layer, generating dense Mg salt deposits and establishing a temporary chemically protective interface. Stage V (Controlled Hydrogen Evolution): The geometric confinement effects of coating microchannels enable hydrogen gas generated by Mg substrate corrosion to release gradually through corrosion microchannels, preventing localized gas pressure accumulation. Simultaneously, the slow degradation of the Mg substrate helps neutralize the local pH decrease caused by the degradation of the PTV-loaded PLLA, thereby mitigating inflammatory responses [114,115]. Notably, the hydrogen generated by Mg degradation has also been demonstrated to possess anti-inflammatory effects [116]. Stage VI (Synergistic Degradation): The synchronized degradation of the metal substrate and residual coatings forms biocompatible corrosion products until complete absorption. This mechanism achieves the unification of structural integrity maintenance and controlled degradation in Mg alloy stents through the sequential failure of gradient barriers and dynamic regulation of corrosion reactions.

Stage I: Structural stabilization period; Stage II: Stress-Induced cracking period; Stage III: Medium penetration period; Stage IV: Interfacial reaction period; Stage V: Controlled hydrogen evolution period; Stage VI: Synergistic degradation period.

Limitations in this study

This study has some limitations. First, the stent was implanted into the healthy iliac artery model of New Zealand white rabbits, and healthy blood vessels may differ from human arteries in an atherosclerotic environment. Therefore, further research is required to confirm its biological effects in diseased animal models that more closely resemble physiological situations in humans (such as small pathological pig heart vessels). Additionally, longer follow-up studies are required to fully determine its clinical application potential. Second, The fatigue life of the stent, a crucial preclinical assessment parameter for stents, was not assessed in this study. Therefore, further testing of the fatigue life is needed to assess the stent's durability in environments that closely resemble real-world conditions.

Conclusions

In summary, we developed a PU-mediated hierarchical composite coating strategy that effectively regulated corrosion and biological function in ZM21 BRS, thereby offering a promising alternative to rare-earth-containing Mg alloys stents. In vitro degradation kinetics investigations have shown that the hierarchical structure coating creates a gradient-controlled corrosion barrier system via time-dependent modulation of several functional layers. This multi-stage regulatory strategy significantly improves the common problem of uneven degradation associated with conventional Mg alloy BRS under physiological conditions, hence substantially lowering the danger of “collapse-type” failure. The coated stent successfully inhibited intimal hyperplasia and enhanced endothelium repair, according to in vivo trials. More significantly, the rate of degradation matched the vascular tissue regeneration process, preventing LL and the inflammatory reaction that comes with fast deterioration. Not only may this new strategy be used in the field of cardiovascular stents, but it also provides new insights for studying surface modification in other implanted biodegradable devices.
