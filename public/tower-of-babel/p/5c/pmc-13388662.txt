Role of garnet shaping the 660-km seismic discontinuity
Authors: Takayuki Ishii, Hiroshi Kojitani, Masaki Akaogi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe 660-km discontinuity is commonly attributed to the post-spinel transition of ringwoodite to bridgmanite and ferropericlase, yet it does not fully explain observed structural complexity. Here, we examine the influence of majorite garnet, the second most abundant mantle mineral, on the post-spinel transition using high-pressure and high-temperature experiments. We find that the post-spinel transition pressure in garnet-bearing systems differs from that in garnet-free systems but remains consistent with the post-garnet transition of majorite garnet to bridgmanite. This suggests that the post-spinel transition in a multi-component mantle occurs as a linked reaction induced by the post-garnet transition. Moreover, the occurrence of garnet transformations consistently explains the roughness structures and dynamics of the 660-km discontinuity observed near cold and hot mantle flows. These results indicate that the average mantle comprises not a mechanical mixture of heterogeneous lithologies but rather a homogeneous lithology with a pyrolite-like bulk composition.Subject terms: Mineralogy, Geophysics

Introduction

The 660-km seismic discontinuity separates the mantle transition zone and the lower mantle, and it is among the most important boundaries in the Earth’s interior because it controls mantle dynamic processes, such as slab stagnation1. Mineral-physics studies based on widely accepted compositional models of the mantle, such as pyrolite, suggest that the major constituent of the transition zone is (Mg,Fe)2SiO4 ringwoodite (Rwd), which decomposes into bridgmanite (Bdm) + ferropericlase (fPe) (post-spinel transition) at pressures near the 660-km discontinuity (23.4 GPa)2–10. Because this transition boundary has a negative Clapeyron slope, the post-spinel transition works to hinder the movements of the subducted slabs and upwelling mantle plumes, possibly causing slab stagnation and plume ponding around the 660-km discontinuity. We note that the post-spinel transition in Mg2SiO4 (Fo) has been regarded as an appropriate approximation of the post-spinel transition because the effects of Fe2+ on the post-spinel transition are negligible: the differences in the transition pressure and width change are less than 0.1 GPa and 0.01 GPa, respectively, because of the incorporation of Fe2+7.

Seismological studies have widely revealed depressions and elevations of the 660-km discontinuity beneath subduction zones and hotspots11, respectively, which have been generally interpreted as being due to the post-spinel transition with a negative Clapeyron slope2–10. Large depressions beneath subduction zones (e.g., Tonga and Mariana) down to a depth of 750 km12–18, which are extremely deep and insufficient to be interpreted by the post-spinel transition, were observed, which can be explained by the akimotoite (Aki), MgSiO3-rich ilmenite-type phase, to Bdm transition with a relatively high negative Clapeyron slope instead8. Depressions down to depths of 690–705 km in hot plumes have also been observed beneath Iceland and Hawaii19,20, which can be attributed to the transformation of MgSiO3–Al2O3 garnet (Grt), the second most abundant mineral in the mantle transition zone, to Bdm, the so-called post-garnet transition21–28, because the Clapeyron slope is positive21,25,29. We emphasize that the presence of alumina, the fourth most abundant oxide component in mantle rock30, is essential for forming the 660-km discontinuity in hot plume regions because Al2O3 component increases the post-garnet transition pressure31,32 although that in the MgSiO3 system occurs at relatively low transition pressures (20–21 GPa) compared with pressures of observed 660-km roughness21,25.

The origin of the 660-km discontinuity structure should be comprehensively interpreted in a model mantle composition, possibly rendering the role of the post-garnet transition process important even along average and cold subducting slab geotherms. Recent advanced studies have suggested that the post-spinel transition in the Fo system is consistent with the average depth of the 660-km discontinuity8–10. On the other hand, our experimental study revealed that the post-spinel transition in pyrolite, in which the mineral assemblage is Rwd + Grt, occurs at slightly lower pressures with a lower slope than that in the Mg2SiO4 system, even at 1400–1600 °C, corresponding to hot slab to average mantle temperatures33. The difference in the post-spinel transition is probably caused by minor components, such as Fe3+ and Al3+, because of the negligible effect of Fe2+7. This suggests that the post-garnet transition may also affect the post-spinel transition. Thus, clarifying the effects of minor components of Fe3+ and Al3+ on the interaction between the post-spinel and post-garnet transitions is essential for understanding the structure and dynamics around the 660-km discontinuity. However, their effects are poorly understood. In this study, we clarified the reactions in a realistic mantle composition by examining the effects of the post-garnet transition in MgO–SiO2–Al2O3–FeO–Fe2O3 system on the post-spinel transition pressure and Clapeyron slope in Mg2SiO4 by means of the multi-sample cell technique33 using a multi-anvil press (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Via the use of this technique, we compared the post-spinel transition in Mg2SiO4 with those in MgO–SiO2–Al2O3–FeO–Fe2O3 system charged in the same high-pressure cell assembly and simultaneously applied the same pressure (P), temperature (T) and time conditions. We found that the post-spinel transition occurs through successive reactions combined with the post-garnet transition. On the basis of our results, we examine seismological structures at a depth of approximately 660 km and provide comprehensive explanations of the 660-km discontinuity structures in a realistic mantle composition.

Results and discussion

Effects of Fe3+ and Al3+ on the post-spinel transition

We found distinctive differences in post-spinel transition pressures between Fe,Al-free and Fe3+- and/or Al3+-bearing systems (Fig. 1). We compared the post-spinel transitions in Fo, Fo + [85 mol% MgSiO3 + 15 mol% Al2O3] = 6:4 (weight ratio) (represented as Fo+En85Crn15 hereafter), and Fo + [85 mol% MgSiO3 + 15 mol% (Fe3+, Al)2O3] = 6:4 (weight ratio) (represented as Fo+En85FA15 hereafter) systems to examine the effects of Al3+–Fe3+ coupling and Al3+ alone (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 11 and Supplementary Table 1). In the Fo system, Rwd transformed to MgSiO3 Bdm + MgO periclase (Pe) at 23.6–24.0 GPa and 1200–1600 °C (Fig. 2a). The Rwd–Bdm+Pe transition in the Fo system exhibited a negative slope of approximately –1 MPa/°C, which is generally consistent with those reported8–10. In the Fo+En85Crn15 system, the assemblages of Aki + Rwd + Grt and Rwd + Grt changed to those of Bdm + Pe + Grt above 23 GPa at 1200–1400 °C and 1600 °C, respectively, with almost no temperature dependence (Fig. 2b). Thus, compared with those in the Fo system, the transition pressure and Clapeyron slope in the Fo+En85Crn15 system were lower and less negative, respectively. The results clearly indicate that Al3+ affects the transition pressure and slope of the post-spinel transition. In the Fo+En85FA15 system, the phase relations were essentially the same as those in the Fo+En85Crn15 system (Fig. 2c), suggesting that the post-spinel transition pressure and Clapeyron slope were affected mainly by Al3+ (Fig. 2d). We note that the recovered Rwd and fPe in the Fo+En85FA15 system contained some Fe (Supplementary Data 1), suggesting that some of the Fe3+ in the starting material was reduced to Fe2+ because the Re capsule environment produced relatively reducing conditions34 We also note that pressure and temperature uncertainties may be as large as ±0.5 GPa and ±25 °C, respectively (see Methods for details), implying that the phase boundaries determined in this study include substantial uncertainty. Nevertheless, several observations support the reliability of our results: (1) as shown in Figs. 2 and 3, the synthesized phase assemblages demonstrate relative pressure differences of 0.2–0.3 GPa associated with each applied press-load step were detectable, suggesting that the phase boundary slopes are better constrained, compared with those expected from the above mentioned pressure uncertainty; (2) we performed reversal experiments at 1200 °C just below the phase boundaries shown in Fig. 2 using high-pressure phases, i.e., Bdm + Pe in the Fo system, Bdm + Grt + Pe in the Fo+En85Crn15 system, and Bdm + Grt + fPe in the Fo+En85FA15 system as the starting materials. Portions of the high‑pressure phases transformed to low‑pressure phases, i.e., Rwd±Aki (Supplementary Fig. 2), confirming the phase boundary locations by both forward and reversal experiments; (3) the present Mg2SiO4 post‑spinel transition boundary agrees with that recently determined in situ8 as mentioned above, indicating that the post‑spinel transition slope in Grt‑bearing systems is smaller than in the Fo system. Moreover, the post‑spinel transitions in pyrolitic compositions have shown nearly zero or slightly negative slopes23,33,35, further supporting our conclusion.

We also compared the post-spinel transitions among the Fo, (Mg0.9Fe²⁺0.1)2SiO4 (Fo90Fa10), and Fo+En85FA15 systems (Supplementary Figs. 3, 4, and 9 and Supplementary Table 4). In the Fo and Fo+En85FA15 systems, the phase relations were identical to those observed in the simultaneous comparison experiments shown in Fig. 2. These results further support pressure uncertainty in this study is less than 0.5 GPa as discussed above. In the Fo90Fa10 system, the assemblage Bdm + fPe formed from Rwd at nearly the same P–T conditions as in the Fo system, although the phase boundary broadened by 0.2–0.5 GPa due to the coexisting region of Rwd, Bdm and fPe phases in the binary system (Supplementary Fig. 4a). These observations indicate that Fe²⁺ has essentially no influence on either the transition pressure or the Clapeyron slope of the post-spinel transition, consistent with previous studies reporting only minor Fe²⁺ effects2,7.

To further assess the role of Fe valence, we conducted additional experiments at 1600 °C using Fo, Fo90Fa10 + Fe, and Fo+En85FA15 + ReO₂ starting materials under controlled oxygen fugacities (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 10 and Supplementary Table 6). Adding Fe metal to Fo90Fa10 and ReO2 to Fo+En85FA15 samples was expected to impose Fe–FeO (IW) and Re–ReO2 (RRO) buffer conditions, respectively, thereby stabilizing predominantly ferrous iron in the former and ferric iron in the latter36,37. The RRO buffer is ~6 log units more oxidizing than IW but still below the magnetite–hematite buffer, implying that some Fe²⁺ may remain. In the Fo90Fa10 + Fe system, partial oxidation of Fe metal resulted in slightly elevated Fe contents in the recovered phases (Supplementary Table 6). Despite these differences in oxygen fugacity and Fe valence, the post-spinel transition pressures in the Fo, Fo90Fa10 + Fe, and Fo+En85FA15 + ReO2 samples were indistinguishable from those obtained without buffer control at 1600 °C (Supplementary Fig. 5). These results confirm that both Fe3+ and Al3+ reduce the transition pressure and slope of the post-spinel transition. Given that experiments in Fe3+–Al3+-bearing and Fe3+-free, Al3+-bearing systems yielded consistent results (Fig. 2), we conclude that Al3+ is the primary factor controlling the pressure and slope of the post-spinel transition.

Role of the post-garnet transition on the post-spinel transition

To better understand how Al affects the post-spinel transition process, we compared the Bdm formation pressures in Fo, 88 mol%MgSiO3–12 mol%Al2O3 (En88Crn12), and Fo + 88 mol%MgSiO3–12 mol%Al2O3 (Fo+En88Crn12) systems (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Figs. 7 and 12 and Supplementary Tables 2 and 7), in which the alumina content was fixed at a pyrolite model composition31. At 1600 °C, in the En88Crn12 system, Grt + Aki transformed to Bdm + Grt at 23.2 GPa in the presence of a small amount of stishovite, whereas the post-spinel transition occurred at 23.6 GPa in Fo (Fig. 3a). In the Fo+En88Crn12 system, the post-spinel transition occurred at the same pressure as the En88Crn12 composition. This finding indicates that the Bdm formation reaction in the Fo+En88Crn12 system occurred because of the interaction with the coexisting Grt. Thus, our study revealed that the post-spinel transition is induced by the post-garnet transition in a pyrolitic composition.

The 660-km discontinuity controlled by the post-garnet transition

The post-spinel transition induced by the post-garnet transition presented in this study can explain the origin of the 660-km discontinuity (Fig. 4). The average depth of the 660-km discontinuity has been estimated to be 660 ± 10 km (23.4 ± 0.4 GPa)39–44. Recent studies have shown that the pressure of the 660-km discontinuity agrees with that of the post-spinel transition in Fo, as mentioned above8–10. This study reveals that the post-spinel transition in Fo occurs at higher pressure and exhibits a steeper negative slope than the Al, Fe-bearing Fo system does (Fig. 2). When appropriate average mantle temperatures, 1600–1700 °C45,46, are chosen at a depth of 660 km, the post-spinel transition in the Fo and Al, Fe-bearing Fo systems occurs at nearly the same pressure (<∼0.2 GPa). The relationship in phase transition pressure between pure Fo and pyrolite reported by our previous studies23,33 is the same as that between pure Fo and Al, Fe-bearing Fo systems. These results lead to the conclusion that the post-spinel transition controlled by the post-garnet transition in a pyrolitic system is responsible for the 660-km discontinuity in the average mantle.

Interpretation of the roughness of the 660-km discontinuity

Our results suggest comprehensive interpretations of the 660-km discontinuity structures caused by the phase transitions of Grt and Rwd in a multi-component mantle (Figs. 4 and 5). Significant depression of the 660-km discontinuity down to a depth of 750 km beneath subduction zones11–18 has been explained by the Aki–Bdm transition in MgSiO3, as mentioned above8. However, the results of the present study demonstrate that the effects of alumina should be considered when the seismological observations are interpreted. A previous study on the MgSiO3–Al2O3 system have suggested that Aki is a more dominant phase than Grt along cold slab geotherms because of the Grt‒Aki transition47. In this case, compared with Bdm, alumina strongly partitions into Aki, and the transition pressure shifts to higher pressures with a negative slope (Fig. 4). Note that Al2O3 enrichment in Aki is up to 25 mol%48, and we therefore define such Al-rich ilmenite-type phases as Aki in this study. This suggests that the Aki–Bdm transition in the MgSiO3–Al2O3 system can be applied to explain the depression (Fig. 5)31,47. Another possibility is the metastable persistence of Rwd, pyroxene, and/or Grt because of these sluggish transformation kinetics into higher-pressure phases49–52. We note that large depressions have been observed not only within slabs but also around slabs12–18. Such observations are not explained by our model, suggesting that other factors such as local chemical heterogeneity produced by subducted basaltic crusts and harzburgite may be required.

Depressions of the 660-km discontinuity beneath hotspots were observed in the central part of the plumes, whereas the outer parts were elevated20. Thus, it has been interpreted that depressions at the hottest parts of the plumes (inner part) are caused by the post-garnet transition (Fig. 5). Our recent studies that precisely determined the phase relationships of pyrolite23,33 revealed that with increasing temperature, the volume fraction of Rwd decreases because of its partial decomposition to Grt + fPe (25–40 vol% for Rwd at 1800–2000 °C corresponding to hot-plume temperatures26–28. This suggests that the dominant Bdm-forming reaction around the 660-km discontinuity becomes the post-garnet transition. We note that these studies revealed nearly zero temperature dependence of the Bdm-forming reaction up to 2000 °C, which is not able to explain the large depression in hot plumes. However, it is necessary to reexamine the phase boundary because pressure estimation was performed using pressure calibrants that were not well established at such high temperatures for quenching multi-anvil experiments53.

The post-spinel transition induced by the post-garnet transition presented in this study suggests that the Bdm-forming transition boundary follows the temperature dependence of the post-garnet transition. A thermodynamic calculation study using calorimetric data revealed that the post-garnet transition boundary in the MgSiO3–Al2O3 system has a positive slope of 2 ± 1 MPa/°C above 1600 °C at 6–12 mol%Al2O3-bearing systems48, whose Al2O3 contents correspond to the Grt compositions in pyrolitic mantle31. Thus, the post-garnet transition boundary in a pyrolitic mantle is positive and can explain the depression in the hottest parts of hot plume regions. In contrast, the post-spinel transition in harzburgite with a lower alumina content (0.65 wt%)54 has a steeper negative slope even at hot plume temperatures than that of pyrolite (4.45 wt%)30 and occurs at depths shallower than 660 km23,55. This is probably due to the insufficient alumina content in Bdm to generate excess alumina components (Reaction 2). Consequently, the post-garnet transition in harzburgite does not significantly contribute to the post-spinel transition, which therefore behaves essentially as it would under garnet-absent conditions. Thus, the depression of the 660-km discontinuity around the hot plumes is not explained by phase transitions of harzburgite. These results support that the mantle is not a mechanical mixture of harzburgite and basaltic crust56 but exhibits a widely accepted composition, such as pyrolite, because the mixture model would not yield depressions55.

Influence on mantle dynamics around the 660-km discontinuity

Our results demonstrate that the Clapeyron slope of the post-spinel transition under presence of Grt is less negative than –1 MPa/°C for the pure Mg2SiO4 system (Fig. 2). A reduced Clapeyron slope implies a weaker buoyancy across the 660-km discontinuity, diminishing the effectiveness of this boundary as a barrier to slab penetration. This interpretation is consistent with seismic tomography studies and geodynamic simulations showing that some subducting slabs penetrate into the lower mantle1,57–59. At the same time, seismological observations also reveal slabs that stagnate above the discontinuity. Our results suggest that such stagnation is unlikely to be caused by the post-spinel transition itself, given its nearly zero Clapeyron slope57–59. Instead, previous mineral-physics studies indicate that the Aki-Bdm transition8 and the persistence of metastable minerals such as olivine and pyroxene in cold slabs51,52,60–62 can generate alternative resistance and/or rheological weakening capable of producing slab stagnation.

Our results support this view: in cold slabs, these non-post-spinel transition processes may provide resistance that promotes temporary stagnation and deflection, whereas in warmer slabs, the nearly zero post-spinel transition boundary facilitates smoother descent into the lower mantle. In hot plume regions, an expected positive Clapeyron slope of the post-garnet transition, as discussed earlier, may accelerate plume ascent across the 660-km discontinuity, potentially contributing to the thinning or seismic invisibility of hot plumes above ~1000 km depth63. We note that our Clapeyron slopes were determined using the quench method and therefore may include still relatively large pressure-temperature uncertainties. Future in-situ high-pressure experiments will be essential for more quantitatively assessing the influence of the Clapeyron slope on mantle dynamics around the 660-km discontinuity.

Methods

Preparation of starting materials

We conducted three types of experiments to compare the post-spinel transition in Mg2SiO4 with (i) the post-spinel transitions in the Mg2SiO4–Fe2SiO4 and Mg2SiO4–MgSiO3–Al2O3–Fe2O3 systems, (ii) the bridgmanite-forming transitions in the Mg2SiO4–MgSiO3–Al2O3 and MgSiO3–Al2O3 systems, and (iii) the post-spinel transition in the Mg2SiO4–Al2O3 system.

Six kinds of starting materials, Mg2SiO4 forsterite (Fo) and mixtures of Fo + fayalite (Fa), Fo + MgSiO3 enstatite (En) + Al2O3 corundum (Crn), Fo + En + Crn + Fe2O3 hematite (Hm), En + Crn, and Fo + Crn, were prepared as mixtures of oxides. Fo was prepared from a mixture of MgO (Wako Co., 99.9%) and SiO2 (Aldrich Co., 99.9%) with a Mg/Si of 2 by heating at 1500 °C for 75 h. En was made from a mixture of MgO and SiO2 with a Mg/Si ratio of 1. The mixture was heated at 1670 °C for one hour and quenched in water, forming MgSiO3 glass. The glass was heated at 1300 °C for 63 h and crystallized as En. Fa was synthesized from a mixture of Fe2O3 (Wako Co., 99.9%) and SiO2 at a 1:1 mol ratio at 1180 °C for 24 h under oxygen fugacity controlled by a mixture of H2, CO2, and Ar with a volume ratio of 1:1:2. These synthetic compounds were identified as single-phase materials with Fo, Fa and En compositions using a powder X-ray diffractometer and a scanning electron microscope with an energy dispersive X-ray spectrometer.

For experiment (i), Fo and Fa were mixed at a molar ratio of 9:1 (Fo90Fa10 hereafter). Mixtures of Fo, En, and Crn (Alfa Aesar, 99.9%) and Fo, En, Crn, and Hm were prepared as Fo: (85 mol%En + 15 mol%Crn) = 6:4 (weight ratio) and Fo: (85 mol%En + 7.5 mol%Crn + 7.5 mol%Hm) = 6:4 (weight ratio) (Fo+En85Crn15 and Fo+En85FA15 hereafter) to examine the effects of the linked effects of Al and Al–Fe on the post-spinel transition. Fe metal (Alfa Aesar, 99.5%) and ReO2 (Wako Co., 99.9%) were added to Fo90Fa10 and Fo+En85FA15, respectively, with a weight ratio of 9:1, to conduct phase equilibrium experiments under controlled oxygen fugacity. For experiment (ii), En and Crn were mixed at a molar ratio of 88:12, which was the expected pyrolitic composition31 (En88Crn12 hereafter). A mixture of Fo, En, and Crn was prepared with a weight ratio of Fo: 88 mol%En + 12 mol%Crn = 6: 4 (Fo+En88Cor12 hereafter). For experiment (iii), Fo and Crn were mixed to obtain Fo100-xCrnx (x = 5, 10, and 15) (molar ratio) compositions.

High-pressure experiments

High-pressure and high-temperature experiments were performed with a Kawai-type 6–8 multi-anvil apparatus installed at Gakushuin University, Japan. We used tungsten carbide anvils with a truncated edge length of 2.5 mm combined with a semi-sintered Cr2O3-doped MgO octahedra with a 7-mm edge length to generate pressure. Multi-hole Re capsules were employed to accommodate the samples in the experiments (Supplementary Fig. 1)33,55,64. The starting materials that were kept in a furnace at 110 °C were packed in three or four holes with a diameter of 0.2 mm in a single multi-hole Re capsule that was 1.0 mm in diameter and 0.7 mm in height. Both sides of the Re capsule were capped by two 25 µm thick Re discs. The Re capsule with the discs was placed into the central part of a tubular rhenium furnace in a pressure medium. A LaCrO3 sleeve for thermal insulation was located outside the furnace. BN sleeves and lids were inserted between the Re capsule and the heater as electrical insulators. LaCrO3 rods were placed at both ends of the BN capsule for thermal insulation. A Pt/Pt–13%Rh thermocouple of 0.1 mm in diameter was inserted into the centre of the assembly to measure the temperature at the surface of the furnace. The effect of pressure on the electromotive force of the thermocouple was not corrected.

In each run, the samples in the capsule were compressed to the target pressure at an almost constant rate for approximately 2–4 h and heated to the desired temperature at a rate of approximately 100 °C/min. The samples were kept for 3 h at pressures of 22.3–24.7 GPa and temperatures of 1200, 1400 and 1600 °C. High-P and T experiments were conducted in the pressure interval of 0.2–0.5 GPa at each temperature. After quenching by switching the electrical power supply off, the samples were slowly decompressed to ambient conditions for 12 h. Pressure at room temperature was calibrated against pressure load by the same method as that described in the previous study33. Further, the pressure at high temperature was corrected using the post-spinel transition in Mg2SiO48, the akimotoite–bridgmanite transition in MgSiO38, and the post-garnet transition in Mg3Al2Si3O1224. In our group, several pressure calibrations using the present multi-anvil press and cell assembly were performed over the years, and the variation in generated pressure at the same press load was generally ±0.5 GPa33,64–66. Thus, the absolute pressure estimated for a given press load contains an uncertainty of ±0.5 GPa. On the other hand, relative pressure differences of 0.2–0.3 GPa associated with each applied press-load step were detectable because phase changes were observed within these pressure increments (Figs. 2 and 3). We estimate the temperature uncertainty to be within ±25 °C based on the following considerations. (1) Differences in the power-temperature relationships among runs were within 10 W. A heating rate of approximately 20 W per 100 °C above 500 °C was generally applied, indicating a temperature reproducibility better than ±50 °C. (2) A recent study using CaAl2O4–MgAl2O4 calcium ferrite samples to evaluate temperature gradients in a Re-heater cell—identical to our design except for the use of ZrO2 as the thermal insulator—reported no detectable compositional variations, implying axial and lateral temperature gradients of less than 10 °C/mm67. Because LaCrO3, used as the thermal insulator in our assembly, provides stronger thermal insulation due to its opacity68, the actual gradient in our cell is expected to be even smaller. (3) The samples were enclosed in a relatively large Re capsule, and the high thermal conductivity of Re further reduces temperature gradients within the sample chamber. We confirmed that there were no compositional changes in the phases of the samples in the Mg2SiO4–Fe2SiO4 and Mg2SiO4–MgSiO3–Al2O3–Fe2O3 systems heated at 1200 °C for 3 h and 10 h, suggesting that a heating duration of 3 h is enough to achieve chemical equilibrium in our experiments (Supplementary Tables 1 and 6). Although experiments at 1400–1600 °C for 3 h should have also reached chemical equilibrium because of higher temperatures, our previous works on Mg2SiO4–Fe2SiO4, MgO–SiO2–Al2O3, and natural-rock systems also support chemical equilibrium under these conditions23,33,48,64,69.

Two reversal experiments were performed at 22.7 and 23.6 GPa and 1200 °C for 10 h, just below the phase boundaries determined in Fig. 1, using starting samples of Bdm + Pe in the Fo system, Bdm + Grt + Pe in the Fo+En85Crn15 system, and Bdm + Grt + fPe in the Fo+En85FA15 system. The starting materials for the reversal experiments were synthesized at 24 GPa and 1400 °C for 3 h from Fo, Fo+En85Crn15, and Fo+En85FA15 samples described above. These starting materials were ground in ethanol using an agate mortar for several minutes until they became fine powders. Four-hole multi-sample chambers were used to load powders of these three starting materials, together with Fo in the fourth hole, which served to confirm the normal reaction.

Analyses of the run products

Recovered Re capsules holding the samples were fixed on a glass slide with epoxy resin and polished to expose the samples in the Re capsule for post-analyses. The phases of the starting materials and the recovered samples were identified with powder and microfocus X-ray diffractometers (Rigaku RINT 2500 V), respectively. X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were performed with CrKα or Cu Kα radiation from a rotating anode operated at 45 kV and 250 mA or 40 kV and 30 mA, respectively. The diffraction patterns of the recovered run products were collected near the central part of each hole of the Re capsule using a collimated X-ray beam 50 μm in diameter, and the samples were exposed for 600 s.

Chemical composition analysis of the phases in the run products was conducted using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (JEOL JMS-6360) with energy dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDS) (Oxford INCA X-sight). SEM was performed at an acceleration voltage of 15 kV and a probe current of 0.43 nA. Each phase was analysed by a fine-focused electron beam approximately 1 μm in diameter. The compositions of each phase were generally determined by averaging the data of 10–20 analysis points. Analytical errors were estimated as two standard deviations of the mean. The standard materials used for composition analysis were natural forsterite for Mg and Si, natural fayalite for Fe, and synthetic corundum for Al.
