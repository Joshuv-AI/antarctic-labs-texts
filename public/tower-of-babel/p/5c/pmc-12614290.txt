Probabilistic template matching for detecting resting‐state functional MRI language network in brain tumor patients
Authors: Jian Ming Teo, Vinodh A. Kumar, Alexander M. Khalaf, Kyle R. Noll, Sherise D. Ferguson, Chibawanye I. Ene, Sujit S. Prabhu, Max Wintermark, Ho‐Ling Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntersubject variation among patients with brain tumors complicates the template matching process for detecting the resting‐state (rs) functional MRI (fMRI) language network when using independent component analysis (ICA).PurposeThis study aimed to develop methods that use a probabilistic language atlas to incorporate intersubject variation in brain tumor patients into the template matching process.MethodsThis retrospective study included 79 patients with brain tumors (average age, 50 ± 15 years) who underwent presurgical task‐based (tb)‐fMRI and rs‐fMRI at clinical 3T scanners. At varying template generation thresholds (τ), binary and probabilistic templates were obtained from the language atlas. A binary template developed using healthy individuals and applied in published studies was tested as a control. Binary templates with goodness‐of‐fit (GOF) and probabilistic templates with weighted GOF (wGOF) or Jensen–Shannon distance (JSD) were used for template matching to recommend language networks from ICA. Dice coefficient and Pearson correlation with respect to tb‐fMRI were assessed. Qualitative evaluation was performed by two neuroradiologists. Significant differences in Dice coefficients and Pearson correlations between different template matching methods, and significant differences between ratings were determined with the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, with p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.ResultsCompared to the control, recommendations from methods involving probabilistic language templates had significantly higher (p < 0.05) Dice coefficients across τ = 0% to τ = 35%. Pearson correlation results followed similar trends to those for Dice coefficient. Dice coefficients with the control were found to be 0.247 on average. Peak Dice coefficients with using wGOF and JSD for template matching were found to be 0.349 and 0.350, on average, respectively. Likert score results indicated significantly superior performance (p < 0.05) in identifying ICA components that contain the language network by using probabilistic language templates. 58 and 52 of 79 recommendations for the control were found to contain the language network (Likert scores ≥4) based on rater 1 and 2, respectively, which increased to 69–73 with template matching using wGOF and JSD.ConclusionsThe proposed application of probabilistic templates derived from a language tb‐fMRI atlas of patients with brain tumors and probabilistic template matching methods can improve detection of rs‐fMRI ICA language network in brain tumor patients.

INTRODUCTION

The current clinical standard for presurgical mapping of eloquent brain areas, including the language networks, is using functional MRI (fMRI) to detect blood oxygenation level‐dependent (BOLD) contrast associated with task paradigms. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 However, recent studies have indicated that approximately one‐third of the patients who needed presurgical mapping had limited task‐based fMRI (tb‐fMRI) results. 6 , 7 Resting‐state fMRI (rs‐fMRI) has long been proposed 8 , 9 as an alternative method to tb‐fMRI for presurgical mapping by measuring spontaneous BOLD signal fluctuations while the patient is at rest. 10 , 11 Since rs‐fMRI has reduced requirements for patient compliance compared to tb‐fMRI, it is well‐suited for patients with brain tumors who are experiencing conditions that hinders their ability to comply with language task paradigms. 12

Complexities associated with single subject analysis of rs‐fMRI continue to frustrate clinical adoption of rs‐fMRI, and data‐driven approaches to single subject analysis such as independent component analysis (ICA) are no exception. 13 , 14 As a blind source separation technique, ICA can efficiently provide clinicians with a set of candidate functional networks, from which one or several networks relevant to the surgery can be identified for the patient. 15 , 16 , 17 There are two key impediments for applying ICA in clinical analysis. The first issue is the uncertainty concerning the appropriate total number of extracted components declared before ICA processing (i.e. model order). 16 , 18 This can be addressed by performing a direct search over all ICA components from various ICA model orders and recommending one based on the highest GOF. 19 The second is the challenge of identifying the relevant network from a set of ICA components, especially after considering the distortions in functional anatomy among patients with brain tumors. In the literature, automated detection of the language network is typically performed by template matching with a binary language template and a goodness‐of‐fit (GOF) function. 15 , 20

The current templates used for template matching in literature were developed from healthy individuals. 15 , 16 , 19 , 21 For patients with brain tumors, the presence of nearby lesions can distort functional anatomy, 22 causing functionally active areas to fall into null regions of a binary template, thus compromising the fidelity of the template matching process. Recently, a probabilistic language atlas that accounted for variations in functional areas was developed from patients with brain tumors. 23 This study aimed to develop and test a new template matching approach using templates derived from this atlas along with novel templating matching functions for recommending ICA components that better localize language areas for patients with brain tumors. The new approach included the use of non‐binary template matching functions with the probabilistic atlas and improved direct search of ICA language components.

METHODS

This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board at our institution, and patient informed consent was waived. A presurgical language fMRI dataset of 111 consecutive patients with brain tumors between December 28, 2020, and April 26, 2022, was initially considered for this study. This set of patients were not involved in the development of the language atlas of a previous study. 23 One patient was excluded for not having a rs‐fMRI scan. Sentence completion (SENT) tb‐fMRI was used to evaluate rs‐fMRI template matching results. Consequently, five patients were excluded for lacking SENT tb‐fMRI data. Overall, three patients were excluded due to exceeding our motion criteria and 23 patients were excluded for not demonstrating significant SENT tb‐fMRI activations in both left anterior and posterior primary language areas (PLAs; see Image Processing section). Thus, 79 patients (37 male and 42 female patients; mean age, 50 ± 15 years) were included in the final evaluation. Additional information and clinical characteristics of patients are included in Table S1.

Image acquisition

MRI scans were performed on 3T clinical scanners (GE HealthCare, Milwaukee, WI). fMRI scans were acquired using a T2 *‐weighted gradient‐echo echo‐planar imaging sequence (repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE) = 2000 ms/25 ms; flip angle = 90 degrees; parallel imaging acceleration factor = 2; matrix size = 64 × 64; 4‐mm thickness and no gap; in‐plane resolution = 3.75 × 3.75 mm2; readout bandwidth = ± 250 kHz; duration = 4 and 6 min for tb‐fMRI and rs‐fMRI, respectively). The rs‐fMRI was performed before the SENT tb‐fMRI. During the rs‐fMRI, the patients were asked to keep their eyes open and fixated on a crosshair, not move their head, not to think of anything in particular, and not to fall asleep. The SENT paradigm included six cycles of 20‐s control and 20‐s task blocks. Each task block consisted of providing the patient with incomplete sentences, and patients were tasked with thinking of a word to be filled into a blank. During the control blocks, patients were shown gibberish sentences (composed of non‐word letter strings) in a format resembling that in the active block. Anatomical images were obtained using a 3D T1‐weighted inversion recovery‐prepared spoiled gradient‐echo sequence (TR/TE/inversion time (TI) = 6.1/2.1/400 ms; flip angle = 20 degrees; 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.2 mm3 voxel; matrix size = 256 × 256), a T2‐weighted FLAIR sequence (TR/TE/TI = 10000/142/2250 ms; 1.0 × 1.0 × 2.0 mm3 voxel), a T2‐weighted fast‐spin echo sequence (TR/TE = 5800/108.9 msec; ETL = 16; 0.8 × 1.3 × 2.0 mm3 voxel); and a postcontrast 3D T1‐weighted SPGR sequence (TR/TE = 5.8/2.3 msec; flip angle = 20°; 1.1 × 1.1 × 1.4 mm3 voxel).

Image processing

Image processing was performed with AFNI 24 (for fMRI analysis excluding spatial normalization, ICA and template matching), SPM12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/) (for spatial normalization), FSL 25 (for ICA) and in‐house Python scripts 26 (for template matching).

Preprocessing of rs‐fMRI followed a pipeline previously described in the literature, 19 which included slice‐timing correction, motion correction, six‐parameter rigid body registration with T1W image (using AFNI align_epi_anat), median filtering (using AFNI 3dTsmooth with “‐med” option), polynomial detrending of order = 4, low pass filter of 0.1 Hz, nuisance regression (using averaged time series from individual patient's cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) and white matter (WM) masks obtained from SPM12 Segment, and translational and rotational head motions), spatial smoothing with 8‐mm full width and half maximum Gaussian kernel, and volume scrubbing with interpolation of removed volumes using in‐house Python scripts following the ArtRepair algorithm. 27 Each patient's T1‐weighted image was spatially normalized to MNI space to obtain the deformation field that was used to spatially transfer CSF and WM masks to the patient space for the nuisance regression process. After image preprocessing, rs‐fMRI was processed as described in experimental protocol.

Preprocessing of SENT tb‐fMRI included motion correction, slice‐timing correction, six‐parameter rigid body registration with T1W (using AFNI align_epi_anat), normalization to MNI space and spatial smoothing with 6‐mm full width and half maximum Gaussian kernel. A general linear model with a canonical hemodynamic response function was used to generate t‐value activation maps. Significantly activated clusters (p < 0.05, family‐wise error corrected) were determined using AFNI 3dClustSim to obtain the cluster threshold at the uncorrected cluster‐forming threshold of p < 0.0001. Patients lacking significant activations in left PLA of the AAL3 atlas, including regions of interest (ROIs) of inferior frontal gyrus, opercular part, inferior frontal gyrus, triangular part, superior temporal gyrus, and middle temporal gyrus, were excluded. Respectively, the first two ROIs were considered left anterior PLA while the latter two were considered left posterior PLA. These ROIs primarily cover Brodmann Area 44, 45, 22, and 21, respectively.

For both tb‐ and rs‐fMRI, patients were excluded if their head motion had more than 2‐mm translation or 2° rotation. In addition, patients were excluded if more than 20% of their volumes were bad during the volume scrubbing process for rs‐fMRI.

Experimental protocol

An overview of the experimental protocol of this study is presented in Figure 1. For each patient's rs‐fMRI, after preprocessing, a single run of FSL MELODIC ICA 28 with model orders of 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 and MELODIC estimate (η) was performed. To identify a recommended language network from all ICA components across model orders obtained for each patient, direct search template matching was implemented.

This study considered two different types of language network templates for template matching: binary templates (i.e., mask of binary values) and probabilistic templates (i.e., mask of float values obtained from a probabilistic atlas based on SENT fMRI 23 ). The process of extracting binary and probabilistic templates is described in the Template Generation section below.

For template matching with ICA components, we considered three types of template matching functions: GOF, weighted GOF (wGOF), and Jensen–Shannon distance (JSD). 29 The arithmetic considerations of the three functions are described under the Template Matching Functions section below.

Collectively, the direct search template matching methods evaluated included the following. The control method (CONS [η:30]) used the Shirer language template 30 with the GOF function 20 for direct search template matching across ICA model orders of 10, 20, 30, and η per an algorithm previously described in the literature. 19 Method GOFB [η:30] replaced the template in CON S [η:30] with a binary template generated from the probabilistic atlas. Method GOFB [η:50] used an expanded set of model orders of 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50, and η. Methods wGOFP [η:50] and JSDP [η:50] replaced the binary template in GOFB [η:50] with a probabilistic template and used wGOF and JSD, respectively, for template matching.

Template generation

This study used the SENT atlas, 23 which was developed by overlapping binary masks of significant activations of SENT tb‐fMRI from presurgical brain tumor patients. Binary templates were derived from the SENT atlas by applying a template generation threshold (τ) of greater than 0%–35%, with a step of 1%, onto the probabilistic atlas and binarizing surviving voxels. Probabilistic templates are derived from the probabilistic atlas wherein τ (0%–35%) is used as a means of truncating the SENT atlas such that voxels surviving the threshold remain unchanged, while other voxels are set to 0.

To derive PLA constraints, the probabilistic atlas was first truncated at τ greater than 5%, after which watershed segmentation was used to obtain watershed parcels. 31 Weighted‐sum assignment was performed to assign each parcel to a single label corresponding to an anatomical ROI from AAL3. 32 In weighted‐sum assignment, the sum of probabilities (voxel values) for each watershed parcel within each AAL3 anatomical ROI was first calculated, after which the watershed parcel was assigned to the anatomical label corresponding to the AAL3 anatomical ROI for which the parcel has the highest sum of probabilities. Watershed parcels that did not overlap with any AAL3 anatomical ROIs were disregarded. Parcels on both hemispheres assigned to the AAL3 PLA ROI 23 labels were combined into binary mask PLA functional constraints. When applied to a language template, voxels outside the PLA constraints were set to 0, and voxels within the constraints retained their original value. Template matching functions

GOF

The GOF calculated for ICA extracted components was the difference between the mean of z‐scores inside Zin¯ and outside Zout¯ of the binary language template. 20 (1) GOF=Zin¯−Zout¯

The component with the largest GOF was considered to be in best agreement with the language template. Positive and negative z‐scores in the ICA component z‐map were considered for GOF.

wGOF

wGOF is a generalization of the GOF to take advantage of the probabilistic templates. Using the SENT probabilistic atlas P∋{p}, probabilistic templates Pin∋{p} (τ > 0%–35% with or without PLA constraints) and Pout ∋{1−p,} (voxels not surviving threshold or PLA constraints) were applied to calculate wGOF as follows: (2) wGOF=∑Xin∘Zin−∑Xout∘Zout|Xin=Pin∑Pin;Xout=Pout∑Pout where Xin and Xout are probability density maps. Like GOF, the component with the largest wGOF was considered to be in best agreement with the language template. Positive and negative z‐scores in the ICA component z‐map were considered for wGOF.

JSD

JSD is a distance metric from information theory that measures the similarity of two probability densities and can be understood in two ways: (i) a measurement of the square root difference between Shannon entropy of the mixture distribution from both the probability density template and the probability density of the positive z‐map compared to the average Shannon entropy of its constituents. 29 , 33 The smaller this difference, the less surprising it is to conclude that the two probability densities resemble each other, thus informing the eventual top recommendation for JSD. (ii) The intuition of JSD can be grasped by inspecting the calculation process of JSD and recognizing that it involves a voxel‐wise determination of log2 relative overlap of the probability density of the template and the probability density of the positive z‐map.

Prior to calculating JSD, the probability density map X from a probabilistic template and the probability density map Y from a positive valued z‐map extracted from ICA (values < 0 were taken to be 0) were prepared. Then, JSD between X and Y was calculated by: (3) JSDX,Y=D(X||M)+D(Y||M)2|M=X+Y2, where M is the mixture model of X and Y (voxel‐wise average), and (4) D(XorY||M)=∑i∈NFXiorYi,Mi (5) Fx,y=xlog2xy0∞x>0,y>0x=0,y≥0otherwise, where D(XorY||M) is the log2 relative entropy as defined by Equations (4) and (5) and X(i), Y(i), and M(i) are corresponding values of voxel i (of N voxels) from X, Y, and M, respectively. Equation (5) implies that negative values result in infinity, hence negative z‐scores cannot be used in the calculation of JSD. Only positive z‐scores in the ICA component z‐map were considered for JSD. When calculated using log2 relative entropy, JSD is constrained to the range of 0–1, where 0 implies X and Y are identical and 1 implies X and Y are completely different with no overlap. 29 The component with the smallest JSD was recommended to be the closest to the language template. Scipy implementation of JSD was used. 34

Result evaluation

As outlined in previous literature describing CONS [η:30] direct search template matching, the ICA component in best agreement with the language template by measure of the template matching function is considered to be the recommended ICA component (also referred to as a recommendation). 19 To examine the effect of τ on recommendations, for each method, the top recommended ICA component at each τ (i.e., 0%–35%) was obtained. For quantitative evaluation of the recommendations, we considered two commonly used FSL MELODIC mixture model probability thresholds (MM‐P; i.e., MM‐P > 0.5 and MM‐P > 0.95) for thresholding and binarizing ICA z‐maps. 28 , 35 , 36 , 37 Voxels corresponding to positive z‐scores were considered for the calculation of evaluation scores. For each patient, spatial agreement of rs‐fMRI ICA component voxels and tb‐fMRI activations within AAL3 ROIs were assessed using Dice coefficient in patient space. In addition, the temporal correlation of rs‐fMRI time courses averaged over the tb‐fMRI activation voxels compared to rs‐fMRI ICA component voxels was assessed using Pearson correlation.

The qualitative evaluation of the recommended ICA components from CONS [η:30], wGOFP [η:50], and JSDP [η:50] was performed independently by two board‐certified neuroradiologists with expertise in clinical fMRI (18 and 4 years of experience, respectively, including neuroradiology fellowship) using a Likert survey. Based on the quantitative analysis, τ used for wGOFP [η:50] and JSDP [η:50] corresponding to the highest Dice coefficient at MM‐P > 0.5 were used. The recommended components from the five different methods were assessed with three questions: “Does this ICA result contain the language network?” (i.e., “Language”), “Does this ICA result contain other functional networks?” (i.e., “Other”), and “Does this ICA result represent artifact or primarily contain noise?” (i.e., “Noise”). For each question, raters were asked to select one of the following: “Strongly Disagree”, “Disagree”, “Neutral”, “Agree”, “Strongly Agree”, ordered from 1 to 5, respectively. An example of the Likert survey is included in Supporting Information 1. Across all methods, recommended ICA components were presented in random order and identical components were consolidated to avoid a repeated rating for each neuroradiologist.

Statistical analysis

For the quantitative evaluation, Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was used to determine the difference in Dice coefficient and Pearson correlation between methods at each τ with the level of significance set at a Bonferroni‐corrected alpha of 0.05. For the qualitative evaluation, inter‐rater agreement was assessed with the quadratic kappa. Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was then performed to determine significantly different ratings in the Likert survey between methods at a significance level of Bonferroni‐corrected alpha 0.05.

To determine the influence of tumor volume and tumor‐to‐PLA distance, tumor ROI was first determined for each patient by automated segmentation using the cancer imaging phenomics toolkit (CaPTk) 38 with four images as input (pre‐ and postcontrast T1‐weighted images, T2‐weighted image, and FLAIR image). Of the 79 patients, six were found not to have T2‐weighted image within 2 weeks before the surgery, and no tumor mask was generated for one patient. Therefore 72 patients were included in the analysis of tumor volume and tumor‐to‐PLA distance. Tumor‐to‐PLA distance was calculated by surface‐to‐surface distance between tumor and SENT tb‐fMRI activations (family‐wise error–corrected threshold) within AAL3 PLA ROIs. Pearson correlation was calculated between tumor volume vs. Dice and Likert scores; and also for tumor‐to‐PLA (both anterior and posterior) distance vs. Dice and Likert scores.

Supplementary analysis

By substituting the SENT atlas developed from brain tumor patients with the LaNA atlas developed from healthy subjects, 39 direct search template matching methods wGOFL [η:50] and JSDL [η:50] were performed. Spatial agreement of recommended rs‐fMRI ICA component voxels and tb‐fMRI activations within AAL3 ROIs were assessed using Dice coefficient in patient space.

RESULTS

Figure 3 demonstrates the agreement of the recommended component by template matching methods, without and with PLA constraints, with the tb‐fMRI language localizations. Dice coefficients with CONS [η:30] were found to be 0.247 on average. Without PLA constraints, the Dice coefficients of wGOFP [η:50] and JSDP [η:50] were significantly higher than those of CONS [η:30] across all τ (p < 0.05; Figure 3a). At τ = 0%, the Dice coefficients of GOFB [η:30] were significantly lower (p < 0.05) than those of CONS [η:30]. GOFB [η:30] and GOFB [η:50] demonstrated a ramp‐up from τ = 0% to τ = 5%, with both becoming significantly higher (p < 0.05) than that of CONS [η:30] at τ = 5%. GOFB [η:50] was significantly higher (p < 0.05) than GOFB [η:30] at τ = 6%. wGOFP [η:50] was significantly higher (p < 0.05) than GOFB [η:30] from τ = 0% to τ€= 7% and significantly higher (p < 0.05) than GOFB [η:50] from τ = 0% to τ = 4%. JSDP [η:50] was significantly higher (p < 0.05) than GOFB [η:30] from τ = 0% to τ = 2% and significantly higher (p < 0.05) than GOFB [η:50] from τ = 0% to τ = 1%. No significant difference was found between wGOFP [η:50] and JSDP [η:50]. GOFB [η:30] achieved its peak Dice coefficient of 0.333 at τ = 17%. GOFB [η:50] achieved its peak Dice coefficient of 0.349 at τ = 16%. wGOFP [η:50] achieved its peak dice coefficient of 0.349 at τ = 8%. JSDP [η:50] achieved its peak dice coefficient of 0.350 at τ = 12%. Similar trends were observed for Pearson correlation, as demonstrated in Figure 3c.

DISCUSSION

This study introduces several methodological advances in using template matching for detecting the rs‐fMRI language network in patients with brain tumors, including employing templates generated from a probabilistic atlas developed from patients with brain tumors. 23 Such advances include wGOF direct search template matching, JSD direct search template matching, and applying PLA constraints. Our results show that direct search probabilistic template matching using wGOF or JSD performs as well as or better than GOF over a large range of τ applied to a probabilistic language atlas developed using patients with brain tumors. Low τ values should be avoided for GOF binary template matching, which suffers from a ramp‐up phenomenon due to the loss of template specificity associated with a large template. However, probabilistic template matching with wGOF and JSD is relatively insensitive to τ selection and can be used at low τ taking advantage of the probabilistic nature of the template to avoid the loss of template specificity. Weak Pearson correlations (p > 0.05) between tumor volume and tumor‐to‐PLA distance with Dice and Likert scores for CONS [η:30], wGOFP [η:50], and JSDP [η:50] (Table S4) indicate limited influence of the volume and distance to PLA of a patient's tumor on performance.

Previously, template matching relied on binary templates extracted from atlases developed using healthy individuals to detect where functionally active language areas could be in ICA components. 15 , 16 , 20 , 35 The current study proposed the use of templates extracted from a probabilistic language function atlas developed using patients with brain tumors and investigated if accounting for intersubject variation is beneficial to the template matching process in patients with brain tumors. 23 Within the framework of binary templates, careful management is required to balance the trade‐off of using a large enough template to detect the language network while avoiding loss of template specificity (Figure 2). This problem can be partially resolved by finding a patient‐specific τ for binarization; however, such a process can be cumbersome and limits the use of small τ (Figure 3). We realized that the coupling of spatial extents and probability values in the probabilistic atlas can be used to resolve the aforementioned size‐specificity trade‐off.

We envisioned this work to bridge the use of clinical tb‐fMRI and rs‐fMRI for localization of language areas in patients with brain tumors. Hence, we intentionally used a clinical SENT tb‐fMRI atlas instead of an rs‐fMRI atlas because the American Society of Functional Neuroradiology recommends SENT as a clinical tb‐fMRI language paradigm owing to its relative robustness in detecting both the posterior PLA and anterior PLA in patients. 12 , 23 We are aware that templates generated from the SENT atlas incorporate our assumptions of left hemisphere dominance and a larger anatomical region for the localization of the posterior PLA compared to the anterior PLA. Such assumptions are reasonable in view of other language templates applied to clinical research. 15 , 19 , 21 , 30 We acknowledge that the hemispheric asymmetry in the template can have consequences for inferring laterality from recommended ICA language components of template matching; however, modelling and measuring these consequences falls outside the scope of the current study. As the template incorporates our prior assumptions, template matching in this study cannot determine if the rs‐fMRI language network and the tb‐fMRI language network are the same entity. The potential increased sensitivity for detecting the language network with ICA will need further validation studies against intraoperative cortical stimulation or post‐operative language deficit assessments.

With the use of the probabilistic atlas, wGOF is a natural next step after GOF by generalizing the calculation of “averaging over voxels” in GOF as the “sum weighted by probability density.” GOF can be understood as a special case of wGOF when a binary template is used, both proving a measure of overall ROI‐averaged agreement with the template. Strictly adhering to the mathematical definition used for metric spaces, 40 wGOF is only a measure of similarity and not a metric of similarity. Although the use of a measure of similarity for template matching is acceptable, we suggest the careful application of wGOF, as the intuition of wGOF can act contrary to the objective of template matching. Similar to using GOF, during template matching using wGOF, the recommended component is solely determined by the largest difference in average of z‐scores inside and outside the template. As such, wGOF provides no mathematical guarantee of finding positive z‐scores inside the template. Unsurprisingly, wGOF is intimately affected by the spatial distribution and magnitude of z‐score for which differences can arise from different preprocessing or z‐standardization found in different implementations of ICA. 34 , 41 Another spillover consideration is that when more than one model order is used, as in the case of the direct search template matching, the ICA models of different model orders can result in z‐maps extracted with different z‐ranges associated with its model order. Therefore, in direct search template matching with wGOF, the components of different model orders may not be compared against each other evenly. This effect may have partly contributed to the bias toward larger model orders in Figure 4e as τ is increased. On the whole, our results (Figure 3) support the use of the image processing steps, 19 including FSL MELODIC, as wGOF performs as intended and recommends components in agreement with tb‐fMRI with the probabilistic language atlas developed using patients with brain tumors.

To address concerns with wGOF, we proposed JSD as an information‐theoretic solution that provides a measure of the overall voxelwise agreement with the template. As negative z‐scores were set to 0 for its calculation, JSD template matching comes with the mathematical guarantee that z‐maps without a single overlapping voxel between the probability density of the positive z‐map and the probability density template cannot be recommended. Depending on the use case, the use of JSD should be made together with the consideration if the resulting degeneracy of negative z‐scores is acceptable. JSD comes with two additional useful characteristics for template matching. First, being a distance metric that attempts to measure relative overlap with log2 ratios in its formulation, JSD induces a more predictable behavior. Second, when implemented with log2 relative entropy, JSD is bounded between 0 and 1. These characteristics allow for even comparisons across components extracted from ICA of different model orders used in a direct search. Although JSD is more complex than wGOF, in our experience there is minimal difference in computational time between wGOF and JSD. Using an M1 Pro Macbook Pro, for each patient with ∼200 components each, we find that wGOFP [η:50] takes an average time of 0.8s to make a recommendation while JSDP [η:50] takes an average of 0.9s to make a recommendation.

Our work demonstrates that binary or probabilistic templates of language networks can be used to detect the language network (Figure 5 and Figure S4). The patients presented in Figure 5 illustrates how recommendations from template matching with a binary template using GOF (i.e. CONS [η:30]) can be dissimilar to recommendations from matching with a different probabilistic template using wGOF and JSD. In Patient 5(A), recommendations from the binary template matching and the probabilistic template matching are similar. Patients 5(B)‐(E) present cases when CONS [η:30] failed to recommend the best corresponding ICA language component, which can possibly be driven by three factors: (1) CONS [η:30] using a direct search up to ICA model order 30 instead of 50, (2) smaller size of the Shirer template used in CONS [η:30] providing inadequate coverage for the functionally active regions, (3) equal voxel weights in the binary template. The recommendation from CONS [η:30] for Patients 5(C) and 5(E) were likely limited by the factor (1) as recommendations from wGOFP [η:50] and JSDP [η:50] came from model orders 40 and 50. However, since recommendations from wGOFP [η:50] and JSDP [η:50] for 5(B) and 5(D) came from within model orders 10–30 and η, it is likely that the recommendations by CONS [η:30] were limited by factors (2) and (3). The appearance of connectivity in rs‐fMRI ICA language network outside of the Broca's and Wernicke's area varies between patients, and further studies are required to investigate how consistently can such connectivity be detected with rs‐fMRI ICA. 42

wGOFP [η:50] and JSDP [η:50] perform well at low τ (templates that span a very large volume in the brain) as they do not suffer from the loss of template specificity compared to a large binary template. Past τ = 5%, Dice coefficients, and Pearson correlation between methods GOFB [η:50], wGOFP [η:50], and JSDP [η:50] become very similar, indicating similar template matching performance. We would not recommend attempting different threshold in routine clinical use. Rather, the use of a threshold ∼10% as applied in our rater's study should suffice in most cases. The use of lower τ to obtain a larger probabilistic template can accommodate patients with pronounced distortions in functional anatomy comes with less penalty compared to binary template matching. This finding is observed even at τ = 0, showing that not taking a threshold on the probabilistic atlas is a feasible option for probabilistic template matching.

As proposed originally, the direct search algorithm over various ICA model orders is an effective approach to circumvent the uncertainty of selecting an appropriate ICA model order. 19 By referencing other literature involving the application of ICA for language localization, we judged that a higher model order (up to 50) should have been considered, 18 , 43 , 44 which was supported by better spatial and temporal agreements after the inclusion of higher model orders (Figure 3). The dominance of larger model orders at higher τ in Figure 4 is likely a consequence of using increasingly smaller templates that require more separation of rs‐fMRI networks by ICA. Notably, regardless of τ, we found that the top recommended component from template matching does not always come from a single model order or MELODIC's estimate. Considering smaller steps between ICA model orders or increasing the range of ICA model orders may improve agreement with significant activations from tb‐fMRI, but this approach requires increased computation time. With 6 ICA model orders (10, 20, 30, 40, 50, and MELODIC suggested η), the computation time is approximately 1 min per 10 ICA model orders, hence the computation time on the ICA step is approximately 5 min when parallelized on a modern CPU.

The results of the Likert survey provide qualitative assessment by radiologists of the suitability of direct search probabilistic template matching for detecting the language network. Our results indicate that the language network is detected more frequently with wGOFP [η:50] and JSDP [η:50] without and with PLA constraints compared to CONS [η:30]. However inter‐rater agreement for wGOFP [η:50] and JSDP [η:50] without PLA constraint is lower than CONS [η:30]. Interestingly, when PLA constraints were used, the frequency of detecting the language network decreased slightly, while inter‐rater agreement increased and surpassed that of CONS [η:30]. High inter‐rater reliability can mean agreements not only in good performances (high Likert scores) but also in bad performances (low Likert scores). One plausible explanation is that inter‐rater agreement increases when a non‐language ICA component is recommended. The slightly reduced performance when using PLA constraints could be attributed to broader language regions, such as secondary language areas, being missed. Understanding the effect of incorporating language areas other than PLAs in template matching will require further investigation.

Our results are from an ideal scenario in which clinical characteristics of the atlas and template are aligned with those of the patients. To examine the influence on language network detections when using an atlas with different clinical characteristics from patients, we further tested a scenario in the supplementary analysis which our probabilistic language atlas was replaced by a probabilistic language atlas developed from healthy individuals (i.e., the probabilistic functional atlas of the language network (LaNA)). 39 The results indicated that for direct search probabilistic template matching, intersubject variation among patients with brain tumors could not be fully accounted for when using an atlas developed using healthy individuals even after varying τ values (i.e., sizes of the functional areas in templates). In a scenario in which alignment of clinical characteristics is not met, JSD is preferable to wGOF as it is less reliant on a patient‐specific threshold (as noted by a lack of ramp‐up behavior). However, the practical benefit of not using a threshold will need to be observed in automated analysis for a larger patient cohort. Differences in template matching results using templates from SENT atlas and LaNA atlas derives from the differences in how probability is distributed in the two atlases. As illustrated in Figure S3, the SENT and LaNA atlas are relatively similar in central tendencies for primary language areas. However, the LaNA atlas additionally shows more intensity in the temporal poles, occipital lobes, and medial inferior frontal areas. These differences could be attributed to: (1) The SENT atlas was developed with the sentence completion paradigm, 12 , 23 while the LaNA atlas was developed with language localizer. 39 , 45 (2) Tumor‐induced variation in functional anatomy 22 results in less consistent overlap between individual tb‐fMRI activation in the development of the SENT atlas, reducing the overall probability values while increasing attributable volume (most noticeable in left hemisphere anterior PLA), although central tendencies in several regions may align with Shirer and LaNA atlases.

Our work presents several limitations, the first being that this is a single‐institution study involving tb‐fMRI and rs‐fMRI data of patients with brain tumors from limited scanner platforms. However, the methods we developed were intended to be agnostic of input data, thus based on use case, our template matching methods can be repurposed to other atlases. Second, we advise caution when applying the probabilistic SENT atlas of patients with brain tumors together with our template matching algorithms to patients with other lesion‐based illness or a cohort of different clinical characteristics. For other patient populations, careful consideration whether the template is still representative of appropriate language localization in the population is recommended. Third, this work does not resolve the challenge of using ICA wherein one network could be split into several ICA components. As previous approaches using template matching attempted detecting one best ICA component as the language network, our work remains in accordance with these approaches. 15 , 16 , 19 Attempting to search all combinations at a given model order = x is highly computationally expensive (e.g., 2x‐1 components combinations for model order = x). This is highly computationally expensive and future studies will be required to develop efficient approaches.

CONCLUSIONS

This study developed probabilistic template matching methods for detecting the ICA language network component from rs‐fMRI for the presurgical evaluations of patients with brain tumors. Using templates generated from a probabilistic language atlas developed based on patients with brain tumors and the proposed methods resulted in a significant improvement compared to using the previously applied binary template developed based on healthy individuals.
