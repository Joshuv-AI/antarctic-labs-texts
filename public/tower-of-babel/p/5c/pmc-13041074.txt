Gender-dependent preferences of medical professionals in treatment choices for irritable bowel syndrome: results from an exploratory study
Authors: Rosel Sturkenboom, Brigitte A. B. Essers, Ad A. M. Masclee, Daniel Keszthelyi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIrritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) is a highly prevalent, chronic disorder of the Gut-brain interaction associated with a high healthcare utilization burden. The treatment of IBS remains challenging due to numerous treatment options and heterogeneity in IBS symptoms. Therefore, for effective decision-making, insight into management strategies and treatment preferences of both patients and physicians is important. In our previous study, the most-preferred treatment among IBS patients was dietary intervention, subsequently pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy. However, the preferences of physicians remain unclear. AimsThe aim of this study was to explore treatment preferences for IBS among physicians and explore gender differences. MethodsA labeled discrete choice experiment (DCE) survey was filled in, containing twelve scenarios with each three alternatives (medication, diet, psychotherapy). Five attributes were included in the treatment scenario: effectiveness, time until response, time until recurrence, side effects and treatment burden. The preference weights were analyzed using a mixed logit model.ResultsIn total, 52 physicians completed the DCE survey (58% female). The majority of these respondents were gastroenterologists (51.9%), followed by gastroenterology trainees (34.7%). The most-preferred treatment was pharmacotherapy, subsequently dietary intervention and psychotherapy. Effectiveness, treatment burden and mild side effects were reported to be important treatment characteristics. Female physicians chose dietary interventions and psychotherapy more often than male physicians. ConclusionsPharmacotherapy was the most-preferred IBS therapy among physicians. Female physicians may be more inclined to choose non-pharmacological interventions compared to males. However, future studies should further investigate gender differences and their impact on management strategies for IBS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04660-5.

Introduction

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a prevalent disorder of gut-brain interaction [1].

It is characterized by recurrent abdominal pain and associated with disordered bowel habits, as per the Rome IV criteria [2]. IBS symptoms impact the individuals’ quality of life (QoL) and the high prevalence of this disorder contributes to increased demands of healthcare facilitations, representing up to 25% gastroenterologist visits [3]. Treating IBS symptoms remains challenging, because of the presence of a clinically heterogeneous population and the fact that no single treatment will suit all patients. The main focus of management covers the predominant symptomatology at presentation, the severity of symptoms, the presence of psychological comorbidities and the impact on the QoL [4–6].

The engagement of the treating physician during consultation is an important factor by which patients are informed of the different treatment options. To improve compliance and in the end treatment outcomes, the identification of patients’ treatment preferences during decision-making is crucial. Our previous study revealed that IBS patients generally favor dietary interventions above pharmacotherapy or psychotherapy [7]. However, preferences of physicians themselves may also be of relevance in therapeutic decision-making during consultation. Treatment choices are often influenced by physicians’ preferences and experience rather than being solely based on scientific evidence [8, 9]. This could be clarified by the lack of evidence-based guidance on the optimal treatment order during medical decision-making, especially in IBS patients where there is a lot of heterogeneity present and no single effective treatment option. However, this factor was not thoroughly reported before in relation to IBS treatment. These preferences may be driven by patient characteristics but also by physicians’ gender reflective of the unconscious role of social bias in medical decision making. To measure treatment preferences, a quantitative preference method called a discrete choice experiment (DCE) is widely used [10, 11]. This is a quantitative method where participants are offered series of hypothetic choices of two or more treatment alternatives characterized by attributes and their levels. We performed a discrete choice experiment to explore treatment preferences for IBS among different physicians and to explore if treatment preferences differ depending on gender, e.g. male and female physicians.

Materials and methods

Study population

Respondents of the DCE included registered physicians practicing all over the Netherlands. All clinicians were currently treating IBS patients. All physicians, registered Dutch gastroenterologists (n = 674) and gastroenterology trainees (n = 215), were invited by mail via the Dutch Association for Gastroenterologists using a survey link, designed in Qualtrics.

The attributes and attribute levels were obtained using semi-structured interviews with patients and surveys among physicians, as described previously [7, 12]. Design of DCE

The combination of the treatment alternatives, attributes, and their levels resulted in 324 combinations (34 * 41). Therefore, a fractional factorial design was chosen for this DCE.

A D-efficient design with parameters set to zero was used in Ngene with 36 choice sets divided into 3 blocks of 12 (D-error of 0.0079). Physicians were randomly assigned to one block of twelve choice questions.

A labeled design was chosen to make the alternatives (treatment options) more realistic.

In addition, although effectiveness and treatment response are generic, the other attributes are treatment specific. Therefore, assigning these therapies as unlabeled alternatives in the choice set, such as ‘alternative A’, would not capture and explain the whole load of the therapy [13].

The treatment alternatives include: pharmacotherapy, dietary therapy, psychotherapy, because these treatments are the most widely used therapy categories for IBS. Respondents were presented with these three therapies, each characterized by various levels of the five attributes.

Effectiveness and time to response were treated as generic attributes, meaning all levels were applicable for each treatment. Pharmacotherapy included a treatment specific attribute side-effects. All treatments shared the same attributes recurrence and frequency of appointment but with alternative specific levels while dietary interventions and psychotherapy also included the attribute time required. The categorical parameter, side effects, was effect coded [14]. The other attributes.

were treated as continuous parameters.

The DCE questionnaire included an introduction, instruction and questions involving demographic characteristics. The choice sets were represented with four different patient vignettes, representing different patient profiles. Each profile differed in predominant symptom (moderate abdominal pain or defecation problems) and anxiety symptoms (mild anxiety present or not present). Age and gender of the patient was not included in the profile because these two characteristics were considered to be less relevant with regard to recommending a specific type of IBS therapy. Every patient profile was followed by three choice sets. Physicians completed in total twelve choice sets, next to the demographic questions. The complete survey was added as supplementary file 1.

A pilot study was conducted to analyze the accuracy and comprehensibility of the survey by eleven gastroenterologists of the MUMC+ (n = 11). The survey was clear and comprehensible to the respondents. An example choice set was added to the instruction part.

The final design was updated with a Bayesian efficient design using the estimated parameters of the analysis of the pilot study. Also, the four patient vignettes were enclosed as interaction terms in the statistical design. The D-error of the updated design was 0.04.

Statistical analysis

Qualtrics (Qualtrics, Provo, UT) was used to administer the questionnaire and collect data. Sociodemographic outcomes were evaluated by descriptive analysis in IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 for Macintosh (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). Nlogit software (Econometric Software, Inc., Plainview, NY, USA) version 6.0 was used to analyze the preference weights of physicians by means of a mixed logit model. All data of all respondents were included in the final analysis.

The preferences of respondents were evaluated according to random utility theory [15].

This theory assumes that a respondent derives utility (benefit) from the attributes of a product or intervention etc [11]. The relative utility score patients derive from a treatment is expressed by a constant and different coefficient, reflecting the effect of a particular attribute level on the utility score. The sign of a coefficient shows whether an attribute has a positive or negative effect on utility. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. The analysis was conducted following the ISPOR guidelines [16].

First, a simple multinominal choice model, was conducted to analyze the treatment preferences of the physicians. A more advanced model, the mixed logit model with interactions between patient characteristics (moderate abdominal pain or defecation problems) and anxiety (mild or not present and the attributes effectiveness, time to response, time to recurrence of symptoms after discontinuation of therapy and treatment burden, was conducted to examine if patient characteristics influence treatment preferences. None of these interactions were significant. Subsequently, we explored if gender or number of years work experience would have an effect on the preference for a treatment.

The estimation of the final mixed logit model, including interactions between gender/work experience and the attributes effectiveness/treatment burden, was carried out using Halton draws, with 2000 simulations. The attributes effectiveness, time to response, time until recurrence and treatment burden, were set as random-specified parameters in this model with a normal distribution.

Next, subgroup analysis for women and men separately was performed, based on the significant interactions. The models for the subgroups included two interactions between effectiveness & treatment burden with years of work experience. Only two attributes, effectiveness and treatment burden were significant. The average choice shares for the different treatments were calculated by applying Nlogit’s simulation command using the mixed logit estimation results.

Results

Baseline characteristics

In total, 52 physicians completed the discrete choice experiment, of whom 58% was female. The majority of respondents were gastroenterologists (51.9%), followed by gastroenterology trainees (34.7%). A small number was general practitioner (3.8%), pediatric gastroenterologist (1.9%) or gastroenterology nurse practitioner (7.7%). The average years of work experience with treating IBS patients was 11.5 years (SD 10.3, range 1–36). All baseline characteristics are reported in Table 2.

Discussion

The exploratory findings from this discrete choice experiment showed that pharmacotherapy was the most-preferred IBS treatment among physicians followed by dietary intervention and finally psychotherapy. Female physicians tended to choose more often for dietary interventions and psychotherapy compared to their male colleagues. These results imply that female and male physicians may have different styles of practice with female physicians being more inclined to choose non-pharmacological interventions.

Physicians’ preferences with regard to the treatment for IBS symptoms remain unexplored. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to address this using a discrete choice experiment. Previously, an online survey was conducted in Belgium, exploring the diagnostic and therapeutic approach of IBS-D patients among gastroenterologists and general practitioners [17]. Spasmolytics and dietary interventions were the preferred therapies in both groups of physicians as first-line treatment. A systematic review on the diagnosis and management of IBS in primary care, showed similar results where general practitioners mainly focused on dietary advice and drug therapy (antispasmodic drugs, fiber supplements or antidepressants [18]. Psychotherapy (i.e. hypnotherapy) was not often prescribed. This could be related to the reluctancy of GPs were when it comes to referring patients for psychological therapies [19].

The overall preference for pharmacology among physicians is in contrast to the preference for dietary treatment among patients previously identified by our group and may in fact reflect dissimilarities in attitudes of patients’ vs. physicians’ with regard to IBS treatment. One of the most-salient findings of our study is the differences in treatment preferences according to physician gender. The preference for dietary interventions of female physicians aligns more closely with the findings indicating that IBS patients generally favor dietary interventions above pharmacotherapy or psychotherapy [7]. Male physicians, on the contrary, favored pharmacotherapy above dietary interventions and psychotherapy. Therefore, during consultation, physicians should be aware that the patients’ wishes are not necessarily in line with their own perspective. As a consequence, the identification of what patients favors during IBS management remains essential in order to establish a therapeutic alliance.

This gender difference in physician preference on the management in health care indeed deserves more attention as they may reflect unconscious, gender-driven biases. Berthold et al. reported that female physicians tend to have better overall outcomes and adhere more closely to the guidelines than their male colleagues [20]. However, men seem to adopt new prescription drugs faster than female physicians, after adjusting for various factors such as risk preferences and personality traits [21]. In addition, a recent study reported on the impact of physician gender on health care utilization in gastroenterology [22]. Patients seen in initial consultation by a female gastroenterologist had a significant reduction in health care utilization when compared to male gastroenterologists.

Because the majority of IBS patients are female, a better physician/patient gender concordance may be in benefit of female physicians. This may be an important factor in decision making.

A recent systematic review, analyzing the association between physician sex and patient outcomes, showed that the treatment outcomes such as mortality, hospital readmissions, were significantly lower in patients treated by female physicians. Also, the patient outcomes were better when female patients were managed by female physicians [23]. Possible other reasons for these better patient’s outcomes rather than this physician/patient gender concordance, might include better communication skills and greater willingness to work with an interprofessional team when compared to male colleagues. These study results raise more attention of different styles of practices based on physician’ gender and may lead to better understanding of various practice approaches in general. Hence, future studies investigating the impact of different health care management between male and female physicians in IBS may be of importance. Further research will reveal more crucial elements in clinical decision making and might be implemented in medical training.

The current study has a number of strengths. The input for the DCE design was based on a combination of sources as recommended by the guidelines [11]. The attributes and levels were extracted from surveys with physicians, a literature review and an expert panel [12]. We used patient vignettes to make the choice sets more comprehensible and realistic [24]. The labeled design used also helped in making this survey more reflective of current clinical practice and increase the validity of the DCE outcome [13]. However, some of the respondents may have chosen their preferred therapy regardless of the presented levels of the attributes. Since our analysis showed that there was a labeled effect of dietary intervention present compared to pharmacotherapy, it is possible that dietary interventions were chosen more often despite the presented levels.

There are also some limitations of this study to address. We invited different types of healthcare providers in different regions of the Netherlands to explore the IBS treatment preferences; e.g., gastroenterologists, residents and general practitioners; all with varying lengths of working experience. The index cohort is therefore representative for the physicians involved in IBS healthcare in the Netherlands. However, only 52 physicians completed our discrete choice experiment, which could represent a selection bias of physicians who have frequent consultations with IBS patients and/or are more interested in treating DGBIs. As this survey was sent out online by the Dutch Association for Gastroenterologists to all registered gastroenterologists (≈600) and residents (≈200) in the Netherlands, the response rate was limited. Response rates for DCEs based on postal surveys was previously shown to be 50.44% (S.D. 19.46) [25], also indicating that the cognitive burden including more attributes decreases response rates. In addition, a high level of attributes (> 5) diminishes the response rates. Compared with eight choice sets, response rates were higher with fewer (3–7) and > 8 choice sets. Moreover, the relevance to the surveyed population affects the response rate. In our study, we used twelve choice sets. A clarification of our low response rate could be that this DCE was assumed to be too time-consuming and may not be relevant for gastroenterologist not interested in this condition. However, other DCE studies involving physicians showed comparable response rates [26–29].

ln conclusion, this explorative study using a discrete choice experiment among physicians addressing IBS treatment preferences demonstrated that (1) physicians primarily prefer using pharmacotherapy above dietary treatments and psychotherapy and (2) female physicians tend to prefer non-pharmacological options more often than their male counterparts. These findings warrant further research to understand the potential impact of different treatment preferences for IBS according to physician gender on for example treatment satisfaction and outcomes. This may lead to increased awareness of different styles of practices during clinical consultation of the IBS patient and pave the way forward to improve the management of IBS patients.
