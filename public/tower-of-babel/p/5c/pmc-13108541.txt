A Phase 1 Study Evaluating the Pharmacokinetics and Safety of Avacopan in Participants with End‐Stage Kidney Disease on Hemodialysis
Authors: Caroline Mohrmann, Rajneet K. Oberoi, Stephen D. Flach, Thomas C. Marbury, Pedro Hernandez, Manoj Gokhale, Celeste R. MacElrevey, Caitlin Sampson, Hamid Amouzadeh, Krischan Hudson, Vijay V. Upreti, Jessica A. Ward
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe effect of end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD) and hemodialysis (HD) on avacopan exposure is unknown. This Phase 1 study evaluated the pharmacokinetics (PK) and safety of avacopan and its active metabolite M1 in healthy participants and participants with ESKD requiring HD following a single 30‐mg oral dose administered with food. Healthy participants (N = 6) received one dose of avacopan, while participants with ESKD (N = 7) received a single 30‐mg oral dose during each treatment period 18 days apart: once 4 h before HD and once on a non‐HD day. PK parameters included area under the plasma concentration–time curve from time 0 to last quantifiable concentration (AUClast), AUC from time 0–48 h (AUC0–48), and maximum concentration (Cmax). Comparisons were based on geometric least squares mean (GLSM) ratios with 90% confidence intervals. Relative to healthy participants, avacopan exposure on the non‐HD day in participants with ESKD was similar, with GLSM ratios of 0.862 for AUC0–48, 1.12 for AUClast, and 1.03 for Cmax. Corresponding M1 exposures were lower in participants with ESKD, with GLSM ratios ranging from 0.639 to 0.658. Within the ESKD group, avacopan exposure on the HD day was lower than on the non‐HD day (GLSM ratios: 0.699–0.914), while M1 exposure was comparable between periods. Adverse events were mild to moderate and consistent with the known safety profile of avacopan. These findings indicate that ESKD or HD has no clinically meaningful impact on avacopan or M1 exposure, and dose adjustment may not be necessary in this population.

Introduction

Avacopan is an oral complement 5a receptor 1 (C5aR1) antagonist approved in the United States, European Union, and other countries at a dose of 30 mg twice daily (BID) with food as an adjunctive therapy to standard treatment for adult patients with severe active granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) and microscopic polyangiitis (MPA). 1 GPA and MPA are subtypes of antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody‐associated vasculitis (AAV), a group of rare, chronic autoimmune diseases with potentially organ‐threatening complications. 2 Such complications include kidney impairment that can progress to end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD) and require hemodialysis (HD). 2 , 3 The pivotal Phase 3 ADVOCATE study demonstrated that in combination with rituximab or cyclophosphamide, avacopan was noninferior to a prednisone taper in inducing remission at 26 weeks and superior in sustaining remission at 52 weeks in patients with GPA or MPA. 4 Treatment with avacopan also resulted in improved quality of life and fewer glucocorticoid‐related toxicities. 4 Phase 2 studies and a Phase 3 study demonstrated that treatment with avacopan improved estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in patients with GPA or MPA who had kidney involvement and a baseline eGFR of ≥15 mL/min/1.73 m2. 4 , 5 , 6

The pharmacokinetic (PK) and safety profiles of avacopan have been characterized in diverse clinical settings including in healthy volunteers, in combination with other therapies, under fed and fasted conditions, and in individuals with mild to moderate hepatic impairment. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 In a population PK analysis evaluating eGFR as a covariate for avacopan, no dose adjustment for avacopan was considered necessary in individuals with varying degrees of kidney function impairment. 7 Although patients with GPA or MPA and eGFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m2 were excluded from pivotal studies, real‐world studies of avacopan have included these patients, including those undergoing dialysis, 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 in addition to case series reports of recovered kidney function after avacopan use in this population. 19 , 20 , 21 Understanding the impact of ESKD and HD on the PK of avacopan is essential for guiding appropriate dosing and clinical use in this setting, as dialysis can alter the elimination of drugs and their metabolites in individuals with ESKD. 22 Avacopan demonstrated consistent PK across prior Phase 1 studies with rapid absorption (tmax ∼2–4 h), 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 and is highly bound to plasma proteins (>99.9%), as is its primary hydroxylated metabolite M1. 7 , 9 Both avacopan and its active metabolite M1 are eliminated predominantly via hepatic metabolism (∼77%), with minimal renal (∼10% in urine, of which <0.1% was unchanged parent drug). 7 These findings underscore the need for early dedicated renal impairment studies to assess how ESKD and HD may influence PK of avacopan.

At present, the impact of dialysis on the PK of avacopan and M1 has not been characterized. To address these gaps, the present Phase 1 study evaluated the PK and safety of avacopan and its active metabolite M1 in healthy participants (eGFR ≥90 mL/min) and in participants with ESKD (eGFR <15 mL/min) requiring HD.

Methods

Study Design

This open‐label, single‐dose, multicenter, parallel‐group, Phase 1 trial (NCT06468826) evaluated the PK and safety of avacopan in healthy participants (healthy participant group) and participants with ESKD requiring HD (ESKD group). The study protocol and all study amendments were approved by the Salus Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences International Ethical Guidelines, and Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

The primary objective was to evaluate the PK of avacopan and M1 after a single dose of avacopan in healthy participants and participants with ESKD requiring HD. The secondary objective was to evaluate the safety of avacopan.

Study Participants

Adults aged 18–75 years with a body mass index (BMI) of 18 to <40 kg/m2 were eligible for enrollment. Eligibility was determined at screening by calculating eGFR using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease formula. 23 In the healthy participant group, eligibility required an eGFR ≥90 mL/min and no history of kidney disease. The ESKD group comprised participants with ESKD requiring HD (eGFR <15 mL/min; Figure 1). Healthy participants were group matched to the ESKD cohort based on age, sex, and BMI to achieve comparable demographic distributions to characterize the effect of renal impairment using parallel‑group comparisons consistent with regulatory guidance. 24

Study schema. Potential participants were screened and classified based on eGFR (calculated by chemical panels drawn on screening visits, 28 days prior to first dose administration). CRU, clinical research unit; eGFR, estimated glomerular filtration rate; ESKD, end‐stage kidney disease; HD, hemodialysis; OP, outpatient; PK, pharmacokinetics.

Key exclusion criteria were history of uncontrolled or unstable cardiovascular, respiratory, hepatic, gastrointestinal, endocrine, hematopoietic, psychiatric, or neurological disease; hospitalization within 28 days before screening; major surgery within 6 months; evidence of rapidly deteriorating kidney function; significant infections; hyperkalemia; a current functioning organ transplant and/or use of immunosuppressants; positive pregnancy test or lactation in female participants; and male participants with a pregnant partner or partner of childbearing potential not willing to inform their partner of participation in the study.

Treatment Groups and Procedures

Participants were screened within 28 days before the administration of the study drug. In the healthy participant group, each participant received a single 30 mg oral dose of avacopan (three 10 mg capsules) with food on Day 1 of the 18‐day study period (Figure 1). In the ESKD group, each participant received a single 30 mg oral doses of avacopan (three 10 mg capsules with food) during each treatment period 18 days apart. During the “HD day” period, a 30 mg dose was given 4 h before the HD session (Day 1). A 4‐h interval between dosing and initiation of HD was maintained to allow avacopan to reach its absorption peak (tmax ∼3–4 h) 10 prior to dialysis, thereby minimizing any potential impact of HD on early drug absorption and ensuring reliable PK characterization. Participants continued to receive HD after dosing on Days 3, 5, and 8. During the “non‐HD day” period, a second 30 mg dose was given a day after HD (Day 1), with subsequent HD on Days 3, 5, and 7 (Figure 1). Additional HD sessions were permitted apart from study days based on clinical need. Intensive PK sampling occurred through Day 18. There was no washout between Day 18 of the HD day period and the start of the non‐HD day period. All participants were admitted to the clinical research unit on Days −1 through 8, followed by outpatient visits on Days 12, 15, and 18 (end‐of‐study visit).

Endpoints

Primary endpoints included PK parameters of avacopan and M1, including maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), area under the plasma concentration–time curve from time 0 to 48 h after dosing (AUC0–48), AUC from time 0 to time of last quantifiable concentration (AUClast), and AUC from time 0 extrapolated to infinity (AUCinf), and HD clearance of avacopan and M1 from plasma (CLD). Secondary endpoints were treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs) and serious adverse events (SAEs).

Pharmacokinetic Sampling and Assessments

Blood samples for PK assessment were collected by venipuncture or peripheral intravenous catheter in healthy participants and the ESKD group, or from the HD catheter or arteriovenous fistula in the ESKD group. Samples were obtained before dosing and on Day 1 (at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, and 16 h after dosing), Day 2 (24 and 36 h after dosing), Day 3 (48 h after dosing), Day 4 (72 h after dosing), Day 5 (96 h after dosing), Day 6 (120 h after dosing), Day 7 (144 h after dosing), Day 8 (168 h after dosing), Day 12 (264 h after dosing), Day 15 (336 h after dosing), and Day 18 (408 h after dosing). Sampling windows were ±10 min on Day 1; ±1 h on Day 2; ±2 h on Days 3–8; and ±4 h on Days 12, 15, and 18. In the ESKD group, during the HD day period, plasma samples pre‐ and post‐dialyzer and pooled dialysate samples were collected on Day 1 at 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 h after the start of HD and after the end of HD. During the non‐HD day period, PK samples were obtained before starting HD on Days 3, 5, and 7. Plasma samples (50 µL) of avacopan and M1 were processed by protein precipitation and analyzed on an Agilent 1100 HPLC system coupled to a SCIEX API 5000 triple‑quadrupole mass spectrometer operating in positive ion mode (Altasciences, Quebec, Canada). Dialysate samples were treated with 10% Triton X‐100 at a ratio of 2% v/v. Chromatographic separation of plasma samples was achieved on an XBridge C18 column (50 × 4.6 mm, 3.5 µm) at 75°C using mobile phase A (0.2% formic acid in water) and mobile phase B (methanol:acetonitrile 70:30 v/v) under a gradient elution. The method employed multiple‑reaction monitoring transitions of m/z 582.27 to 137.10 for avacopan, m/z 598.27 to 339.20 for CCX168‑M1, and m/z 586.30 to 137.10 for the internal standard (CCX168‑d4). The calibration ranges were 0.2–100 ng/mL for avacopan and 1–100 ng/mL for M1. In addition to the PK endpoints of avacopan and M1, the following parameters were evaluated: time of the maximum observed plasma concentration (tmax), time of last quantifiable concentration (tlast), apparent plasma terminal elimination half‐life (t1/2), AUC from time 0 (just before the start of HD on Day 1; arterial plasma) to the end of HD as determined from samples collected pre‐dialyzer (AUCpre‐dialyzer), AUC from time 0 (just before the start of HD on Days 1 and 3; venous plasma) to the end of HD as determined from samples collected post‐dialyzer (AUCpost‐dialyzer), and extraction ratio during HD (ERD), calculated using Day 1 values using the formula: 100 × (AUCpre‐dialyzer − AUCpost‐dialyzer)/AUCpre‐dialyzer). The molar potency‐adjusted geometric mean exposures of total active moiety (TAM) of avacopan was calculated based on Cmax, AUClast, and AUC0–48: PKparameter_TAM = PKparameter_avacopan/molecular weight of avacopan + (PKparameter_M1/molecular weight of M1) × (IC50 of avacopan/IC50 of M1), where the molecular weight of avacopan is 581.64 g/mol, the molecular weight of metabolite M1 is 597.65 g/mol, the half‐maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value for avacopan is 1.7 nM, and the IC50 for M1 is 3.0 nM. 8 The apparent elimination t1/2 was estimated from the terminal log‐linear portion of the concentration–time profile used to determine the terminal elimination rate constant (λz). The terminal phase was defined as the final log‐linear portion of the profile with quantifiable concentrations. When insufficient data were available to reliably characterize this phase (e.g., limited late quantifiable concentrations), t1/2 was derived from the observable log‐linear portion of the profile.

Safety Assessments

Safety assessments included TEAEs, SAEs, physical examination, clinical laboratory findings (hematology, chemistry, and urine analysis), vital signs, and 12‐lead electrocardiogram (ECG).

Statistical Analysis

The sample size (six participants per group) was selected empirically and was consistent with prior renal‑impairment PK studies. 24 Given the between‑participant variability observed in earlier avacopan PK studies (∼20%–45%), 10 enrolling six participants per renal‑function group provided adequate precision, with an estimated 95% confidence interval (CI) half‑width of ∼20%–30% of the mean PK exposure metric. Approximately 12 participants were planned for enrollment, with six in each group. Data analysis was performed by SAS statistical software (v9.4). PK assessments were carried out in the PK population (defined as all participants who received ≥1 dose of avacopan and had evaluable PK data) using noncompartmental methods in Phoenix WinNonlin (Certara, v8.3.5). Safety was evaluated in the safety population (defined as all participants who received ≥1 dose of avacopan and had ≥1 postdose safety assessment). Geometric mean and geometric percent coefficient of variation (CV) were estimated for all PK parameters except tmax, tlast, and ERD. Hypothesis testing was two sided at a 0.1 significance level. A mixed model regression analysis estimated the natural log‐transformed AUC0–48, AUClast, AUCinf, and Cmax with kidney function group and sex as fixed effects and body weight as a covariate. For each PK parameter, the least squares mean (LSM) and geometric LSM (GLSM) for each participant group, the difference in LSMs between the test and reference participant groups (ESKD [non‐HD day] vs healthy participant group; ESKD group, HD vs non‐HD day), GLSM ratios, and the corresponding 90% CIs were calculated. Adverse events were coded as per the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities version 27.0.

Results

Demographics and Baseline Characteristics

The study was conducted between June 18, 2024, and October 5, 2024. A total of 13 participants were enrolled (healthy participant group: N = 6; ESKD group: N = 7). Healthy participants were selected to reflect the mean and distribution of age, sex, and BMI of the ESKD group. Median participant age was 55 (range, 37–62) years, with a majority being male (84.6%) and Black or African American (61.5%). Demographic characteristics were generally balanced between the groups (Table 1). One additional participant was added during the study due to concern that an adverse event (mild–moderate) in a participant in the ESKD group would limit full participation; however, all participants were able to complete the study.

Pharmacokinetics

In both healthy participants and participants with ESKD, avacopan concentration–time profiles were multiphasic, with an initial rapid decline in plasma concentrations through about 12 h after dosing, followed by a slower decline over the subsequent 1–2 days and a prolonged low‐concentration terminal phase (Figure 2a). As reflected in the noncompartmental t1/2 estimates, the intermediate phase was on the order of ∼40–50 h in healthy participants and the ESKD HD day period, which is the dominant component of avacopan disposition. Whereas the non‐HD day period in ESKD showed a longer t1/2 (mean: 209 h) based on the late low‐concentration terminal phase, consistent with the multiphasic profile and limited quantifiable data in this phase. Geometric mean t1/2 of avacopan during the non‐HD day period in participants with ESKD was 209 h, which was longer than that in the ESKD HD day period (36.7 h) and in healthy participants (46.6 h; Table 2). Based on the geometric CVs, the between‐participant variability for AUClast, AUC0–48, AUCinf, and Cmax was comparable among healthy participants (22.8%–44.9%), those with ESKD requiring HD (HD day period: 41.7%–53.9% and non‐HD day period: 34.8%–49.0%; Table 2). A mixed model regression analysis was performed to assess the effect of ESKD on avacopan exposures. In the ESKD group (non‐HD day) versus healthy participants, avacopan AUC0–48 was 14% lower (GLSM, 642 vs 744 h ng/mL; GLSM ratio [90% CI], 0.862 [0.580–1.28]), AUClast was 12% higher (GLSM, 877 vs 781 h ng/mL; GLSM ratio [90% CI], 1.12 [0.690–1.83]), and Cmax was 3% higher (GLSM, 105 vs 102 ng/mL; GLSM ratio [90% CI], 1.03 [0.775–1.38]; Table 3). The corresponding M1 AUC0–48 was 34% lower (212 vs 323 h ng/mL; 0.656 [0.558–0.772]), AUClast was 36% lower (270 vs 422 h ng/mL; 0.639 [0.491–0.832]), and Cmax was 34% lower (16.7 vs 25.3 ng/mL; 0.658 [0.549–0.787]). During the HD versus non‐HD day periods within the ESKD group, avacopan AUC0–48, AUClast, and Cmax decreased by 9% (599 vs 656 ng/mL; 0.914 [0.762–1.10]), 30% (661 vs 945 ng/mL; 0.699 [0.585–0.836]), and 20% (86.7 vs 108; 0.800 [0.622–1.03]), respectively, while being comparable for M1. AUCinf was not assessed due to >20% extrapolation in the ESKD group (non‐HD day period). Additionally, to assess the impact of HD, avacopan exposures during the HD and non‐HD day periods within the ESKD group were compared and showed reductions of 30%, 9%, and 20% in AUClast, AUC0–48, and Cmax, respectively, in the HD day period (Table 3).

For M1, median tmax was 4 h (healthy participant group), 6 h (ESKD group HD day period), and 3 h (ESKD group non‐HD day period) after dosing (Figure 2b). Geometric mean t1/2 was similar across healthy participants (42.6 h) and ESKD groups (HD day period: 35.8 h; non‐HD day period: 51.4 h; Table 2). Geometric % CV for AUC0–48, AUClast, AUCinf, and Cmax were comparable across groups (Table 2). Relative to those in the healthy participant group, M1 exposures were lower in the ESKD group non‐HD day period for AUClast, AUC0–48, AUCinf, and Cmax (36%, 34%, 13%, and 34%, respectively); within the ESKD group, HD, and non‐HD day period exposures were comparable (<5% difference with the exception of AUCinf; Table 3). Plasma protein binding for avacopan and M1 could not be determined as concentrations of unbound avacopan and M1 in plasma were below quantification limits. Because dialysate concentrations of avacopan and M1 were below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ), CLD was approximated by assuming dialysate concentrations equal to the assay LLOQ for the entire collected volume (∼120 L), calculating the corresponding maximal amount removed and dividing by plasma exposure during the HD period to obtain an upper‐bound estimate of CLD. Based on this approach, overall CLD estimates were <0.093 L/h for avacopan and <2.13 L/h for M1, representing <1% of apparent oral clearance. Of the seven participants with ESKD, the ERD was set to 0 for four participants since AUCpost‐dialyzer was higher than AUCpre‐dialyzer; for the remaining three participants, ERD ranged from 7.67% to 17.2%. These ERD values indicate that, at most, ∼8%–17% of the circulating avacopan could be extracted across the dialyzer during a single HD session. Given that dialysate concentrations were below the assay LLOQ and the estimated CLD values constituted <1% to 2% of total apparent oral clearance, HD contributed minimally to overall drug elimination.

The molar potency‐adjusted TAM PK parameters are presented in Table 2. Compared with the healthy participants group, the ESKD group (non‐HD day period) showed an 18% lower AUC0–48, similar AUClast (<2% difference), and no effect on Cmax (<1% difference). Within the ESKD group, TAM exposures were 7%, 18%, and 27% lower for AUC0–48, AUClast, and Cmax during the HD versus non‐HD day period.

Safety

Four participants experienced TEAEs (healthy participant group: N = 2; ESKD group: N = 2), and two participants experienced treatment‐related TEAEs of Grade 1 or 2 severity; all events resolved by the end of the study (Table 4). No fatal outcomes, SAEs, or discontinuations due to TEAEs occurred. No TEAEs were reported during the ESKD non‐HD day period. No significant changes were observed in clinical laboratory tests, vital signs, 12‐lead ECGs, or physical examinations.

Discussion

In this Phase 1 study, avacopan and M1 PK parameters were broadly similar in participants with ESKD requiring HD and healthy controls, with only modest differences in TAM exposure and negligible removal by HD. During the non‐HD day period, the prolonged apparent terminal t1/2 (209 h) likely reflects estimation based on sparse low‐concentration data in the terminal phase rather than a substantive change in overall drug disposition, which is dominated by an intermediate phase with an effective half‐life of ∼50 h in healthy individuals. 7 The extended t1/2 observed in the non‐HD day period was driven by the low‐concentration terminal phase for ESKD compared with healthy participants, where the time span utilized for t1/2 calculation included the more rapid beta‐rate of elimination that appears to dominate overall avacopan disposition. Although M1 exposures were >30% lower in participants with ESKD compared with those with eGFR >90 mL/min, both avacopan and M1 were active and TAM (parent plus metabolite) exposures were comparable across groups.

Previous PK data have shown no clinically significant differences between avacopan and M1 exposure across a wide range of baseline eGFR values (14–170 mL/min). 7 In states such as severe kidney impairment, hypoalbuminemia may occur and alter drug PK by decreasing protein binding, thereby increasing the unbound fraction. 25 This effect may increase the volume of distribution and shorten elimination half‐life of drugs. This study specifically investigated whether ESKD and HD altered PK for patients with ESKD requiring dialysis. The PK profiles of avacopan and M1 in participants with ESKD were largely similar to those in healthy participants. The limited impact of ESKD and HD on avacopan and M1 exposures likely reflects their very high plasma protein binding (>99.9%), which restricts free drug available for glomerular filtration and dialyzer clearance. 7 Despite high plasma protein binding, there is no published evidence of loss of avacopan due to proteinuria. Furthermore, the comparable PK exposures observed in this study in participants with ESKD and healthy controls suggest that changes in protein binding are unlikely to have a clinically meaningful impact. Consistent with prior data, avacopan is predominantly eliminated via hepatic metabolism and biliary excretion, with renal and dialytic clearance contributing negligibly to total clearance.

Real‐world reports have described the use of avacopan in patients with severe kidney impairment, including those requiring dialysis, without an apparent loss of effectiveness, although controlled data in this population remain limited. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Exposure parameters of M1 (AUC, tmax, and tlast) were lower in ESKD participants, particularly on the non‐HD day period, while avacopan exposures were similar when compared to those in healthy participants. Renal impairment does not directly affect avacopan clearance, as avacopan undergoes extensive hepatic metabolism and has minimal renal elimination. Given that TAM exposures were consistent across groups, these differences in the exposures are not clinically significant. In addition, AUC0–48 exhibited smaller differences between HD and non‐HD periods (geometric mean: 599 and 656 h ng/mL) than AUClast (geometric mean: 661 and 945 h ng/mL) because AUC0–48 captured only the early and intermediate phases of the concentration versus time profile, whereas AUClast was driven by prolonged terminal phase concentrations extended to Day 18, which differed substantially between periods. Overall, exposure parameters for the healthy participants group were comparable to that of participants in the ESKD group during the non‐HD day period, and HD was associated with no pharmacokinetically relevant increase or decrease in peak exposure. These results demonstrate that HD did not meaningfully alter systemic exposure or apparent clearance. An ERD of 0 in four of the seven participants in the ESKD group suggested limited removal of avacopan during HD. Given that avacopan and M1 are >99.9% protein bound and dialysate concentrations were below quantifiable limits, HD contributed negligibly to total clearance (<1% to 2%). Despite minimal dialytic removal of avacopan, as evidenced by below‐quantifiable dialysate concentrations and low ERD, modestly lower avacopan exposures were observed when dosing occurred prior to HD compared with the non‐HD period. These differences are unlikely to reflect direct clearance by the dialyzer and instead are consistent with variability related to dosing timing, early disposition, or physiologic changes associated with the HD setting. Importantly, the magnitude of these differences was small and did not result in clinically meaningful changes in overall avacopan exposure.

The safety profile was consistent with the established safety profile of avacopan. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11

Study limitations include the inability, due to analytical constraints (samples below LLOQ of 0.075 ng/mL), to directly assess plasma protein binding of avacopan and M1 in this study population, although both compounds are known from prior studies to be highly protein bound. Additionally, the small sample size, although typical for Phase 1 renal‐impairment studies, limits the interpretation of the geometric mean exposure estimates. Plasma protein binding was not evaluated using radiolabeled spiking, which is an additional study limitation.

Conclusion

The PK profile of avacopan remains largely unchanged in participants with ESKD requiring HD compared with healthy participants. Despite modest differences in individual PK parameters (e.g., reduced M1 exposure and lower avacopan exposure on HD days), the overall avacopan TAM exposure was within ∼20% of that in healthy participants, and HD contributed <1% to 2% to total clearance. Dose adjustment of avacopan may not be necessary in participants with ESKD requiring HD. The safety of avacopan in participants with ESKD requiring HD was consistent with the established safety profile of avacopan.
