Respiratory Syncytial Virus Epidemiology and Clinical Burden in High-Risk and ≥ 50-Year-Old Adults in Low- to Middle-Income Countries: An Artificial-Intelligence-Enabled Systematic Literature Review
Authors: Adriana Guzman-Holst, Digant Gupta, Amandeep Kaur, Vikas Verma, Arnas Berzanskis, Yolanda Penders, Désirée A M Van Oorschot
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLimited data are available on the epidemiology and clinical burden of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) among adults with underlying medical or immunocompromising conditions (“high-risk adults”) and ≥ 50-year-old adults in developing countries.MethodsTo better understand the impact of RSV in these populations, a systematic literature review of articles published from the year 2000 onward reporting RSV data among high-risk 18–59-year-old adults and ≥ 50-year-old adults in low, lower-middle, upper-middle, and selected high-income countries was undertaken. Searches were run on Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online (MEDLINE) and Excerpta Medica DataBASE (EMBASE), and were supplemented by additional searches (e.g., congress abstracts, gray literature). A combination of artificial intelligence models was used for title/abstract screening. After this, full-text screening of inclusions was conducted, followed by prioritization.ResultsOverall, 77 citations were selected for final inclusion. Of these, 69 reported outcomes related to RSV epidemiology and clinical burden, and are reported in this article. There were limited data on RSV incidence, prevalence, disease severity, and subtype distribution. Adequate evidence was available for RSV positivity among patients with respiratory illnesses, seasonality, complications, and mortality. Incidence in ≥ 65-year-olds was in the range of ~10–178 episodes per 1000 person-years across studies. Ranges for RSV positivity among patients with different underlying respiratory conditions were 1.5–31.9% and 0–9.1%, in high-risk and ≥ 50-year-old adults, respectively. Case fatality rates of up to 15.2% and 27.0% were reported across studies for high-risk and > 60-year-old adults, respectively.ConclusionsOverall, there were considerable evidence gaps for RSV epidemiology among high-risk and ≥ 50-year-old adults in developing countries. However, available data indicate a substantial negative health impact of RSV on these populations, highlighting the need for further data generation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-025-01220-4.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Epidemiology, Respiratory syncytial virus

Key Summary Points

There are limited data on the epidemiology and clinical burden of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) in vulnerable populations, including older adults and those with underlying conditions, in developing countries.In this systematic literature review (SLR) of RSV epidemiology among 18–59-year-old high-risk and ≥ 50-year-old adults in low, lower-middle, upper-middle, and selected high-income countries, a substantial burden of RSV was identified across 69 included studies.Overall, included studies had a low-to-moderate risk of bias, and ranges of RSV positivity among high-risk and ≥ 50-year-old adults with underlying respiratory conditions were 1.5–31.9% and 0–9.1%, respectively.Adequate data were available regarding RSV seasonality, with an increase in prevalence during the rainy or winter seasons, and RSV-associated complications and mortality, with overall case fatality rates consistent with, or slightly higher than, previously reported.However, significant evidence gaps were identified in areas such as RSV incidence, prevalence, and subtype distribution, as well as a considerable degree of heterogeneity between studies, limiting the conclusions that could be drawn and highlighting the need for further research.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a common respiratory virus that contributes to the significant morbidity and mortality associated with acute respiratory infections (ARIs) worldwide [1–5]. While it generally causes nonspecific cold-like symptoms, RSV can lead to severe disease, hospitalization, and sometimes death in infants, adults with underlying medical or immunocompromising conditions (i.e., “high-risk adults”) [6], and adults in older age groups [7–9]. RSV infections in high-risk and aging adults therefore have important clinical significance, with recent analyses finding that the RSV-related mortality rate in adults > 70 years surpassed that of children < 5 years [8].

Despite the increasingly recognized impact of RSV on the health of high-risk and aging adults [6], most available data focus on infants and children, with data on RSV-related disease in adult populations being scarce [10, 11]. This scarcity of data is particularly prominent in low, lower-middle, and upper-middle income countries due to a lack of awareness and routine surveillance of RSV, underdiagnosis, and reporting challenges in many of these countries, including those in Latin America, Southeast Asia, the Middle East, and North Africa [12, 13]. Understanding the burden of RSV in these countries is further complicated due to many countries being found in tropical and subtropical regions [14], where infection patterns are related to changes in humidity and rainfall [15, 16].

There is therefore an unmet need for a better understanding of the epidemiology and clinical burden of RSV among high-risk and aging adults in low, lower-middle, and upper-middle income countries, to inform strategies for improving awareness among healthcare professionals and the public of the impact of RSV on these vulnerable populations. The aim of this systematic literature review (SLR) was to better understand RSV disease epidemiology and burden among these two adult populations in these countries and identify existing gaps in the available literature.

Methods

Search Strategy

The SLR was conducted in accordance with guidelines specified in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and Preferred Reporting Items for SLRs and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) to facilitate a robust methodology [17, 18].

Structured literature searches were run on 8 December 2023, on Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online (MEDLINE®) and Excerpta Medica DataBASE (EMBASE®), using the disease and objective facets presented in Table S1, combined using Boolean operators. Articles published from 2000 onward were included.

Supplementary searches were conducted for abstracts from relevant congresses, and bibliographies of recent systematic reviews and narrative reviews, both published between 2021 and 2023. Keyword-based literature searches were conducted in PubMed, Google, Google Scholar, and additional regional databases. A list of the congresses and databases searched is provided in Table S2. Lastly, gray literature (i.e., country surveillance reports) was identified for countries that provide periodic data on RSV positivity and seasonality.

Article Selection, Screening, and Prioritization

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Articles were included if they provided RSV data for high-risk adults aged 18–59 years or adults aged ≥ 50 years, and if studies were conducted in low, lower-middle, upper-middle, and selected high-income countries, predominantly in Latin America, Asia, the Middle East, and Africa. A list of the included countries is provided in Table S3. Articles in English, and articles in selected local languages (Arabic, Portuguese, Spanish, Turkish, and Vietnamese) were included. For high-risk adults, studies that included data outside of the predetermined age range (i.e., < 18 years and ≥ 60 years), that still encompassed this age range, were included.

High-risk adults were defined as those with underlying medical conditions (e.g., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, cardiovascular disease) and those with immunocompromising conditions (e.g., hematologic malignancies, human immunodeficiency virus [HIV], hematopoietic stem cell transplant [HSCT]). Other conditions often deemed as risk factors for other respiratory diseases (i.e., flu or pneumococcal disease) were also considered.

Final eligibility excluded methodology publications, theoretical studies, letters, and non-peer-reviewed journal abstracts and articles. Animal, in vitro, and other nonhuman studies were also excluded.

Screening

Artificial-Intelligence-Enabled Screening

Due to the wide scope of this literature review, a combination of large language (artificial intelligence; AI) models, including Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) and Generative Pre-trained Transformer (GPT)-4, were used for AI-enabled title/abstract screening of articles (Table S4). This approach was developed in response to the time- and resource-intensive nature of large SLRs, and was previously demonstrated to achieve a practical sensitivity of up to 98% in title/abstract screening [19].

In this AI-enabled screening approach (Fig. S1), a testing dataset of 100 randomly selected titles/abstracts was human-screened and then tested against the AI approach for each screening parameter to assess its accuracy. In cases of inadequate sensitivity, the AI approach was further optimized until sufficient sensitivity (≥ 90%) was obtained.

After the sensitivity of the AI approaches for each screening parameter was confirmed to be adequate, AI-enabled screening was conducted for the entire dataset. This produced a list of included and excluded articles, automatically coded on the basis of the AI algorithm with reference to the reason for exclusion. A final validation was conducted, whereby a random validation dataset comprising 100 randomly selected titles/abstracts was human-screened and then tested against the final AI inclusion/exclusion decisions to confirm adequate sensitivity of the entire screening process. Sensitivity at this step was 100% and validation was complete.

Human Screening

The list of included articles produced by AI-enabled screening underwent further title/abstract screening by a single human reviewer. Articles included after title/abstract screening were subjected to full-text screening, also by a single human reviewer followed by quality check by a second reviewer, to determine eligibility according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Prioritization

To avoid duplicating information and to provide a useful overall summary, articles were further screened on the basis of study design, sample representativeness, sample size, duration of follow-up, recency of data, and relevance to research objectives. Preference was given to well-conducted systematic reviews and large longitudinal studies with representative samples. The prioritized publications were assessed to be of high quality and an adequate representation of the overall literature.

Data Extraction

Data were extracted into a predefined Excel data extraction template by a single reviewer and independently checked by a second reviewer. Any discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer.

The following RSV-related data were extracted: prevalence, incidence, positivity (among patients with respiratory illness, defined via the case definitions used in the source articles), subtype distribution, seasonality, disease severity, complications, mortality outcomes, diagnostic methods and treatment patterns, health-related quality of life, healthcare resource utilization, and direct, indirect, and societal costs. Definitions of the key terms used are provided in Table S5.

For incidence, due to limited availability of data for the two study populations, studies reporting RSV-associated hospitalization rates or incidence of RSV outpatient visits in the general population, and studies reporting incidence separately for different age groups (including adults aged ≥ 50 years), with risk and/or comorbidities not reported, were also reviewed. Similarly, for prevalence, due to limited available data, an article reporting prevalence data for individuals aged > 14 years was included, with separate data for those aged ≥ 50 years not provided.

Data extraction parameters also included article details, geography, study characteristics, and patient characteristics. Data were extracted separately for 18–59-year-old high-risk adults and ≥ 50-year-old adults. Where articles did not specify the age group, mean or median age was taken into consideration for categorization into these two populations.

Quality Assessment

The included articles were critically appraised for potential bias using assessment criteria appropriate for the study type during the data extraction stage. For observational studies, the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used [20], which categorized overall study quality as low (0–3 points), moderate (4–6 points), or high (7–9 points). For systematic reviews, A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR) was used [21]. Quality assessments were not performed for conference abstracts or weblinks.

Focus

This review article focuses on the following RSV-related outcomes of the SLR: prevalence, incidence, positivity, subtype distribution, seasonality, disease severity, complications, and mortality outcomes.

Here, we descriptively summarize the relevant identified literature. No meta-analysis or quantitative synthesis was conducted. For RSV positivity, in articles where the proportion of patients who had respiratory illness with RSV was not directly reported, this was calculated for presentation purposes only using the number of patients with RSV and the number of patients with respiratory illness.

Ethics

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Included Studies

Structured literature searches returned 8336 articles (Fig. S2). Following AI-enabled title/abstract screening, 1033 articles were included; after title/abstract screening of these by a human reviewer, 405 articles remained. Sensitivity of the AI-enabled screening across the screening parameters was 97.6–100.0% for the testing dataset, and 100.0% for both the inclusion and final validation datasets. Final practical sensitivity was 95.0%. Definitions of the screening parameters are provided in Table S5.

Additional supplementary searches returned 106 articles, which underwent full-text screening alongside the output from the structured literature searches to produce 162 eligible citations in total.

Overall, 77 citations were prioritized for extraction and reporting (Fig. S2). Of these, 69 were relevant to the outcomes reported in this article; others will be reported separately [22].

Study Quality

Most articles were of medium–high quality; 42 articles were rated ≥ 6/9 and 15 were rated 5/9 on the NOS. One article each was rated 8/11 and 7/11 on the AMSTAR scale. Quality assessment was not possible for five articles (one weblink and four conference abstracts) due to the limited methodological information available.

Prevalence of RSV

Of the articles selected for inclusion in the SLR, none reported the prevalence of RSV among 18–59-year-old high-risk adults. One article provided relevant data on prevalence among ≥ 50-year-old adults, and as mentioned previously, the countries from which these data originated were not reported [23]. This SLR, published in 2023, reported that RSV prevalence in non-outbreak settings in nursing and care homes was 2–24% in adults aged ≥ 60 years [23].

A second publication, a Chilean cross-sectional study published in 2015, reported RSV prevalence of 0.044 per 10,000 persons in individuals aged > 14 years between 2000 and 2005 [24].

Incidence of RSV

Due to the very limited data on RSV incidence among community-dwelling adults, studies reporting RSV-associated hospitalization rates or incidence of RSV outpatient visits in the general population were also reviewed. As the number of articles remained limited for 18–59-year-old high-risk adults (n = 2) and in ≥ 50-year-old adults (n = 4), ten articles reporting data separately for different age groups (including adults aged ≥ 50 years) were also reviewed and summarized along with the findings for ≥ 50-year-old adults. Table S6 provides a summary of the included articles.

18–59-Year-Old High-Risk Adults

A prospective cohort study from India, published in 2022, reported that among adults ≥ 18 years who underwent HSCT, the RSV incidence rate was 0.82 episodes per 100 patient months [25]. Additionally, a prospective cohort study by a severe ARI (SARI) surveillance group in South Africa, published in 2017, provided patient-level data on RSV hospitalization rates among adults aged ≥ 18 years with HIV [26]. This study reported a 2010 RSV hospitalization rate of 106 per 100,000 individuals in HIV-positive adults aged 18–44 years [26].

≥ 50-Year-Old Adults

Six studies reported episode-level or patient-level RSV incidence among ≥ 50-year-old adults [23, 27–31], including two studies that reported data separately for different age groups [30, 31]. Of these, four were from Asia and one from Africa; one did not specify the region. Across the Indian studies, there were considerable differences in the RSV incidence rates reported, ranging from 6.2 episodes per 1000 person-years (PYs) among adults aged ≥ 60 years [29] to 151.1 episodes per 1000 PYs among adults aged ≥ 65 years [27].

Of studies reporting patient-level RSV hospitalization rates among different age groups, four provided estimates in adults aged ≥ 50 years; these ranged from 0 to 160 cases per 100,000 PYs in adults aged ≥ 50 years [32–35]. The highest estimate (160/100,000 PYs) was reported in a prospective cohort study from the Philippines published in 2017 [34]. Additionally, five studies included estimates for adults aged ≥ 65 years; these ranged from 0 to 130 cases per 100,000 PYs [32, 33, 35–37]. The highest end of this range was from a Thai prospective cohort study published in 2013 [36].

Patient-level RSV-associated outpatient visits among different age groups were reported in three studies, with estimates ranging from 0 to 15 cases per 100,000 PYs among individuals aged ≥ 50 years [32–34]. A prospective cohort study from South Africa, published in 2022, reported episode-level data among different age groups, with estimates of 1.79 and 7.34 per 1000 PYs for RSV-associated hospitalization rates and RSV-associated outpatient infection incidence rates, respectively, among ≥ 65-year-old adults [38].

18–59-Year-Old High-Risk Adults

ARI

Overall, three articles reported data on RSV positivity among 18–59-year-old high-risk adults with ARI; all were from Latin America. RSV positivity was found to vary between studies [33, 39, 40]. For example, a prospective cohort study in Guatemala, published in 2019, reported that, among adult patients with ARI with any comorbidity (see Table S7 for details), 6.5% were RSV-positive [39], while a retrospective cohort study in Brazil, published in 2023, reported that, among ARI patients with heart disease or immunosuppression, 31.9% and 14.7% were RSV-positive, respectively [40].

ILI

Three articles reported data on RSV positivity among 18–59-year-old high-risk adults with ILI, specifically among patients with HIV, all of which were from South Africa. Overall, RSV positivity among adults aged ~18–44 years with ILI and HIV varied between 2.0% and 3.4% across studies [38, 41, 42].

SARI

RSV positivity among high-risk adults with SARI was reported in eight articles across Africa, Latin America, and Asia. Estimates for RSV positivity varied from 1.5% to 11.4% across studies [26, 38, 41–46]. The highest rates were reported in a retrospective cohort study of adults with any comorbidity in Brazil (see Table S7 for details), published in 2024 [46].

≥ 50-Year-Old Adults

ARI

RSV positivity among ≥ 50-year-old adults with ARI was reported in 13 articles. Most were from Latin America or Asia. Across these studies, 0‒2.0% of adults aged ≥ 50 years [32, 47], 0‒0.6% aged ≥ 60 years [10, 48, 49], and 1.8‒50.0% aged ≥ 65 years were positive for RSV [10, 33, 40, 50–53].

ILI

RSV positivity among ≥ 50-year-old adults with ILI was reported in 13 articles. Across these, 0‒9.1% [32, 34, 54–56], 0‒18.9% [10, 57–59], and 0‒11.4% of adults aged ≥ 50 years, ≥ 60 years, and ≥ 65 years with ILI were RSV-positive, respectively [11, 38, 42, 55, 60, 61]. The highest estimates were reported among adults aged ≥ 60 years in Brazil [10].

SARI

Nine articles reported RSV positivity among ≥ 50-year-old adults with SARI. Ranges for RSV positivity among patients with SARI were 3.3‒5.6% in adults aged ≥ 50 years [34], 1.0‒8.8% in adults aged ≥ 60 years [46, 48, 62], and 3.1–9.1% in adults aged ≥ 65 years [38, 42–44, 55, 63].

Distribution of RSV Subtypes

In total, seven studies reported data on the distribution of RSV patients by subtype (RSV-A and RSV-B). Of these, three were from Latin America, two from Asia, and one each from Africa, and the Middle East and Eastern Mediterranean region [25, 43, 46, 53, 54, 64, 65].

An overview of the data reported in these articles is shown in Fig. S3. Distribution of subtypes varied across different countries within a particular geographic region, and across regions. Some studies reported data across multiple seasons, with one finding RSV-A to predominate between 2010 and 2012 in Mexico, while both subtypes circulated simultaneously between 2012 and 2014 [54].

Seasonality of RSV

Overall, 12 articles reported data on the seasonality of RSV (Fig. S4). Among these, eight articles reported distribution of annual RSV cases by month, while four reported RSV positivity separately for different months. Across these articles, the seasonal pattern of RSV was generally reported to align with the rainy or winter seasons of their respective countries, with similar patterns reported in 18–59-year-old high-risk and ≥ 50-year-old adults [25, 26, 28, 29, 45, 65–71].

RSV Disease Severity

Four articles provided data on disease severity among 18–59-year-old high-risk and ≥ 50-year-old adults with RSV (Table s8); two were from the Middle East and Eastern Mediterranean region, and one each from Asia and Latin America.

18–59-Year-Old High-Risk Adults

A cross-sectional study in Türkiye, published in 2023, found that 26.1% of adult patients with RSV and comorbidities experienced moderate-to-severe disease, as assessed by lung parenchymal infiltration status and oxygen levels [72]. Older age, as well as presence of diabetes, heart failure, hypertension, and bacterial co-infection, were significantly associated with moderate-to-severe disease. Similarly, a prospective cohort study in Chile, published in 2012, reported that 31.3% of comorbid adults with CAP due to RSV had class 4 or 5 severity of pneumonia, as assessed by the pneumonia severity index (PSI) [68].

≥ 50-Year-Old Adults

In Thailand, a retrospective cohort study, published in 2022, reported that, among adults with a mean age of 76 years, 44.6% of patients with RSV met the diagnostic criteria for severe CAP on the basis of Infectious Diseases Society of America/American Thoracic Society (IDSA/ATS) criteria [66]. A retrospective cohort study from Saudi Arabia, published in 2023, reported a median PSI score of 120.5 among patients with RSV and a median age of 69 years, indicating class 4 severity of pneumonia [71].

18–59-Year-Old High-Risk Adults

Across studies among 18–59-year-old high-risk adults with RSV, the proportion of patients experiencing complications varied. For example, a Thai study published in 2019 and conducted among hospitalized patients with comorbidities reported that pneumonia was the most common respiratory complication at the time of admission (65.2%) and hypoxemia was the most common during hospitalization (53.6%) [70]. Additionally, 24.6% of patients experienced cardiovascular complications during hospitalization, with preexisting coronary artery disease being a significant predictor of this [70]. An older study from Guatemala reported that, among adults with pulmonary and cardiovascular diseases, 27.3%, 24.2%, and 21.2% of patients with RSV had hyperaeration, pneumonia, and hypoxemia upon hospital admission, respectively [33].

≥ 50-Year-Old Adults

Estimates for the burden of complications with RSV among ≥ 50-year-old adults also varied largely between studies. For example, across studies, estimates for the proportion of adults aged ≥ 65 years with RSV who experienced pneumonia ranged from 31% to 78.0% [36, 37, 53, 69, 73], while estimates for bronchitis ranged from 4.8% to 18.4% at the time of admission [53, 69, 73]. Statistical comparisons for the association of respiratory complications with age were not reported. Additionally, a retrospective cohort study in Thailand, published in 2021, reported that, among hospitalized patients with RSV (mean age: 72), 21.3% experienced any cardiovascular complication at the time of admission [69].

Mortality Associated with RSV

A total of 23 studies reported data on mortality in patients with RSV (Fig. S5 and Table S10). Most were from Africa (n = 7), Latin America (n = 7), or Asia (n = 5). Mortality outcomes were reported as case fatality rates, mortality rates, or RSV-associated excess deaths (as defined in Table S5). Five publications reported data on factors associated with mortality in patients with RSV [66, 70, 71, 74, 75].

18–59-Year-Old High-Risk Adults

Among the 11 articles reporting on mortality in 18–59-year-old high-risk adults with RSV, case fatality rates ranged from 8% to 18.4% [26, 33, 39, 41, 46]. Lowest rates were reported among adults aged 20–40 years with SARI and comorbidities in Brazil [46]. Highest rates were reported in this same study, among adults aged 40–60 years [46]. Mortality rates ranged from 0% to 34.8% [43, 68, 70, 76, 77], with the lowest and highest rates reported among adults with a range of comorbidities in Egypt and Brazil, respectively [76, 77]. Finally, a retrospective cohort study from South Africa, published in 2015, reported RSV-associated excess deaths among adults aged 20–44 years with HIV to be 13.1 per 100,000 PYs [78].

One article investigated factors associated with mortality among high-risk adults with RSV, with no significant difference being found between the mortality rates of patients who did or did not receive ribavirin during the study period [70].

≥ 50-Year-Old Adults

Among the 15 articles reporting mortality in ≥ 50-year-old adults with RSV, case fatality rates ranged from 2.5% among adults aged 50–64 years with acute lower respiratory tract infections in Thailand [36] to 27.0% among adults aged > 60 years with SARI in Brazil [46].

Reported mortality rates also varied between articles [10, 58, 66, 69, 71, 75, 77, 79]. For example, a SLR published in 2020 reported that, among hospitalized adults with RSV aged ≥ 60 years in Mexico, the mortality rate was 25.0% [10]. In contrast, an older prospective cohort study from Chile reported a mortality rate of 4.8% among hospitalized adults (mean age 62.6 years) with RSV, ILI, and comorbidities [58]. A retrospective cohort study from South Africa published in 2018 also provided data on RSV-associated deaths among ≥ 50-year-old adults with SARI, reporting annual percentages of 0.6% and 0.8% among adults aged 65–74 years and ≥ 75 years, respectively [80].

Finally, among the four articles reporting on factors associated with mortality in ≥ 50-year-old adults with RSV, identified factors included steroid and vasopressor treatment, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, severe RSV at hospitalization, bacterial superinfection, and lower estimated glomerular filtration rate [66, 71, 74, 75].

Discussion

Despite the significant global health challenge that RSV poses [6, 8], there is a lack of comprehensive evidence on RSV epidemiology in adults, particularly in low, lower-middle, and upper-middle income countries [6, 12, 13, 27]. This review aimed to provide a comprehensive summary of the epidemiology and clinical burden of RSV among 18–59-year-old high-risk and ≥ 50-year-old adults in these countries.

Included articles were critically appraised in terms of the quality of selection, comparability, and outcomes [20, 21]. Most articles identified in the SLR were of moderate–high quality, indicating a moderate–low risk of bias. However, significant heterogeneity observed between studies across all parameters (e.g., design, population, diagnostic methods) made comparisons between studies difficult. Nevertheless, this moderate–low risk of bias supports the use of the included articles for deriving descriptive outcomes and conclusions.

There was a substantial lack of articles on the prevalence and incidence of RSV among the two study populations. This aligns with established scarcity of RSV research in adults globally, with a lack of available RSV surveillance and research data preventing accurate estimation of RSV burden in many previous studies [10, 11, 81]. When RSV testing is conducted, RSV cases are often underreported due to the suboptimal sensitivity of diagnostic tests [82]. For example, a recent study in high-income countries estimated the true rates of RSV infection among hospitalized ≥ 65-year-old adults to be over two times greater than reported in previous studies in these countries [82].

More data were available for RSV positivity, although estimates varied largely between studies. The large variation is generally consistent with those observed in a previous SLR of developed countries [6], and may be in part due to the heterogeneity observed between studies with respect to the study type, case definitions considered, and the diagnostic methods used. The highest estimates for RSV positivity in 18–59-year-old high-risk adults in the current study exceed those reported in a previous SLR [11]. As the current SLR included more recent studies, RSV testing techniques and sensitivity improving over time, along with increased attention to and better understanding of RSV, may provide one reason for the higher RSV positivity rates observed.

Data were also limited for the distribution of RSV subtypes, with the evidence-base being further restricted by the low proportion of patients who were subtyped in some of the included articles. Predominance of RSV subtypes among the two populations varied across countries and regions, with this regional variation being previously reported in a previous systematic review of global RSV genetic data [83]. However, as some included studies do not provide data on subtype distribution across multiple seasons, conclusions are potentially limited due to RSV subtype distribution being known to vary season-by-season [84].

Adequate data were available on the seasonality of RSV, although some studies were conducted at a single center [25, 65, 66, 69–71], limiting generalizability. RSV prevalence was generally greatest in the autumn and winter seasons in temperate regions, and rainy seasons in tropical and subtropical regions, aligning with previous SLRs of global seasonality of RSV and with studies conducted in Europe and the USA, where more data are available [85–87]. The seasonality of RSV was similar between the two populations, and generally aligns with other respiratory viruses, such as influenza and, possibly, coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) [88, 89], potentially leading to concerns of “triple epidemics” that result in increased burden on healthcare systems and poor patient outcomes [90, 91]. This highlights the need for improved preventative measures for respiratory infections, particularly for populations that are at increased risk of severe disease [91], and in lower income countries, where healthcare infrastructure is often underfunded [92].

Data on RSV disease severity were scarce, with heterogeneity in severity assessments between articles (e.g., lung parenchymal infiltration status and oxygen level, PSI, IDSA/ATS criteria) making generalizability across studies difficult. This emphasizes the need for validated clinical severity scores for RSV, and although efforts have been made toward this in children, there remains a lack of consensus on the most clinically useful score [93].

Sufficient data were available on RSV-associated complications and mortality outcomes, although there was wide variation in how these outcomes were assessed. For example, for complications resulting in pneumonia, some studies assessed this via ventilators, while others used chest X-rays. Similarly, different mortality outcomes were reported across studies, with some studies also not clarifying the follow-up period over which deaths were reported. Nevertheless, across articles included in the SLR, RSV was associated with significant mortality, with case fatality rates that were generally consistent with, or slightly higher than, those reported in previous SLRs for high-risk and aging adults across multiple countries, including high-income countries [6, 81].

The results of this SLR must be interpreted within the context of its limitations. Due to the prioritization of publications, some potentially relevant data may have been excluded. Nevertheless, as the most pertinent and high-quality articles were prioritized, the excluded publications are not anticipated to have substantially affected the findings. Additionally, the inclusion of review articles may have led to the occasional overlap of studies. However, this overlap was taken into consideration throughout the selection, analysis, and reporting of the SLR, minimizing its impact. Furthermore, the partial overlap between the two study populations in some articles (i.e., some included ≥ 50-year-old adults with comorbidities) may have resulted in data for some outcomes being more similar than if the two populations had been truly isolated.

Drawing definitive conclusions in this SLR is limited by the significant data gaps identified. High levels of heterogeneity across articles included, in terms of both study design and data reported, also make the results of this SLR difficult to interpret, and difficult to compare and generalize across the wide geography included. Additionally, availability of RSV testing is likely to have varied significantly across studies, with differences in testing practices leading to potential patient selection bias. For instance, in several studies, only samples of patients with suspected respiratory infection were sent for RSV testing, which may have led to bias in the prevalence and clinical presentations reported. Particularly in the context of lower-income countries, viral pathogen testing may not be commonplace, and patients with RSV may only visit healthcare settings infrequently, if at all. Additionally, polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based testing, which is a particularly sensitive method of detecting RSV positivity [94], is more commonly available in inpatient versus outpatient settings [95]. As PCR is typically more expensive than more traditional testing techniques [96], this may particularly be the case in lower-income geographies where resources and access to molecular testing may be limited. Therefore, less severe cases of RSV infection may not be detected. These limitations are likely to lead to an underestimation of the burden of RSV disease. It was also unclear how the underlying conditions of high-risk adults were treated prior to their RSV episode in many studies, potentially impacting RSV-related outcomes in these patients.

In low- to middle-income countries, a lack of funding for healthcare and surveillance [97] or difficulties for patients in accessing and affording healthcare [98] can also represent significant limitations. However, disparities in healthcare access and disease-related outcomes occur all over the world, even within high-income countries such as the USA [99], and disparities in healthcare and surveillance can also be present within countries, for instance, between rural and urban areas [100]. Conversely, countries in Latin America, including Mexico and Brazil, have well-established and effective surveillance systems for respiratory viruses [100–102]. Therefore, in comparing RSV epidemiology across such broad, disparate geographical regions in this SLR, variations in healthcare systems represent a considerable source of heterogeneity. Since the results of this SLR rely entirely on previously published data, any limitations inherent to said data also have an impact on the present analysis.

In addition to the heterogeneity across articles, many of the individual articles included had several inherent limitations associated with their study design. For example, several prospective and retrospective studies were conducted at a single center, creating vulnerabilities to biases and incomplete data, while the results of observational studies may be limited by residual confounders. Several studies included in the SLR also had small sample sizes, limiting generalizability. Most included articles had study populations comprising of aging adults with many underlying medical conditions, which may have had unmeasured direct and indirect effects on the outcomes reported.

Finally, exploration of the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic was not intended as part of this SLR and has therefore not been investigated in this analysis. However, the search strategy employed encompassed relevant studies published between 2000 and 2023, and as a result, data collection in some of the more recent studies may have been conducted in or impacted by the pandemic. An in-depth exploration of the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic is an area of interest for future work, considering the growing body of literature on this topic [103–106].

Strengths of this SLR included its comprehensive scope and ability to address a variety of research questions pertinent to RSV. The large number of studies included enabled identification of some trends that are consistent with previously published global literature, with most important findings highlighted throughout the article and supported by detailed study tables. AI-enabled title/abstract screening provided time-efficiency given the wide scope of the SLR, whilst ensuring sufficient sensitivity and specificity of the results. Additionally, the hybrid approach to screening, whereby a human reviewer validated the AI-screened articles, ensured consistency in accuracy and quality.

Despite the gaps in the evidence described above, the articles identified in this SLR generally indicate a large clinical burden associated with RSV among high-risk and aging adults in low, lower-middle, upper-middle, and selected high-income countries. This significant burden is largely consistent with numerous previous SLRs conducted in similar regions, including Africa, the Middle East, Latin America, and Asia [6, 10, 11, 27, 81, 107–112]. Future studies may focus on prospective burden of disease or surveillance studies, using clear case-definitions for RSV, high-sensitivity diagnostic tests (e.g., nucleic acid amplification tests) [113], and consistent study settings, to address the issue of underreporting of RSV. When generating evidence in the future, longitudinal cohort or cross-sectional studies among adults with RSV may help to address specific gaps in primary data (e.g., incidence and prevalence data) and help inform future strategies for improved awareness and prevention of RSV in these countries.

Conclusions

The literature assessed in this SLR demonstrates that RSV has a substantial negative health impact on 18–59-year-old high-risk and ≥ 50-year-old adults in low, lower-middle, upper-middle, and selected high-income countries. The wide scope of this SLR, both in terms of the outcomes reported and the geography included, was facilitated by an AI-enabled screening approach that maximized time-efficiency without sacrificing sensitivity. Heterogeneity across studies and significant gaps in the literature highlight the need for further data generation to more comprehensively characterize the epidemiology and clinical burden of RSV among these patient groups and inform strategies for improved awareness and prevention of RSV.
