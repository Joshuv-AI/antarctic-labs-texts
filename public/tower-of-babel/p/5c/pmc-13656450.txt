Endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy versus sub-endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy for acute appendicitis: a retrospective observational cohort study
Authors: Shouli Cao, Song Li, Jinming Yan, Dongyun Xue, Fanlu Meng, Junshan Li, Xiaopei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute appendicitis is a common acute abdominal emergency. Laparoscopic appendectomy (LA) carries risks such as bleeding, infection, and negative procedures. Endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy (ERAT) is minimally invasive but cannot directly visualize the appendiceal cavity. The novel eyeMax disposable biliary imaging system enables direct observation of the appendix cavity during sub-endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy (SERAT), overcoming key limitations of ERAT. The aim of this study is to compare the effectiveness and clinical outcomes of ERAT with SERAT in treating acute appendicitis.MethodThis retrospective study analyzed 60 patients undergoing ERAT and 97 undergoing SERAT for acute appendicitis at Shandong Provincial Third Hospital, China, from November 2021 to September 2024. Propensity score matching (PSM, 1:1) was performed to balance baseline characteristics, yielding 55 pairs of patients. Baseline data, procedural indicators, short-term recovery, and long-term recurrence were compared.ResultsBaseline characteristics were generally comparable between the two groups before and after PSM. After matching, SERAT was associated with significantly shorter operation time and intubation time than ERAT (24.69 vs. 42.65 min, P < 0.001; 96.38 vs. 241.38 s, P < 0.001). The technical success rate was significantly higher in the SERAT group than in the ERAT group (53/55 vs. 47/55, P = 0.047). No significant differences were found in fecalith removal, 6-hour VAS score, hospital stay, or hospitalization cost. Direct visualization in the SERAT group showed inner-wall congestion and edema in 100% of cases, intracavitary fecaliths in 54.4%, and intraluminal pus in 89%. During follow-up, recurrence-free survival was significantly better in the SERAT group ( P = 0.036). Among patients with follow-up data, recurrence occurred in 3/55 (5.4%) patients in the SERAT group and 11/55(20%) patients in the ERAT group. No long-term adverse events were observed in either group.ConclusionSERAT is an effective and safe approach for the diagnosis and treatment of acute appendicitis, offering shorter operation and intubation times, a lower recurrence rate, and minimally invasive treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05030-x.Keywords: Acute appendicitis, Endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy, Sub-endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy, Minimally invasive, eyeMax

Background

Acute appendicitis is one of the most common acute abdominal conditions, typically arises from appendix obstruction, often due to fecalith blockage [1]. However, tumors or lymphoid hyperplasia can also frequently cause appendix obstruction. Surgical appendectomy, the standard treatment for over a century, often employs laparoscopic appendectomy (LA) [2, 3]. However, comorbidities like bleeding, wound infection, and intestinal obstruction are common [4–7]. Additionally, the risk of a negative appendectomy ranges from 8–15% [8, 9].

Recent advancements in minimally invasive endoscopic technology have led to the emergence of endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy (ERAT), a novel, nonoperative approach for treating acute appendicitis [10, 11]. ERAT combines endoscopy with interventional therapy to intubate the appendix lumen, relieving obstruction through appendix irrigation, fecalith extraction, and stent placement [10, 12–14]. However, a limitation of ERAT is its inability to directly view images within the appendiceal cavity, fully exclude tumors, or precisely remove appendiceal fecalith [15–17].

eyeMax(Micro-Tech, Nanjing, China), a novel disposable biliary imaging system, enables direct observation of the appendix cavity in sub-endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy (SERAT). This system facilitates directional irrigation and, when required, lithotripsy.

To date, no studies have compared the efficacy of ERAT and SERAT in treating acute appendicitis.This study aims to evaluate the feasibility of SERAT and ERAT in diagnosing and treating acute appendicitis.

Methods

Patient selection

This retrospective study analyzed 157 patients diagnosed with acute appendicitis admitted to the Third Hospital of Shandong Province between November 2021 and September 2024. The diagnosis of acute appendicitis was comprehensively confirmed by a combination of clinical manifestations (right lower abdominal tenderness, fever, nausea/vomiting, etc.), laboratory examinations (elevated white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, C-reactive protein), and imaging examinations (abdominal CT/B-ultrasound showing appendiceal thickening, edema, fecalith obstruction, or periappendiceal inflammation). All diagnoses were confirmed by at least two senior gastroenterologists and one radiologist to ensure diagnostic accuracy. Inclusion criteria were: (1) Acute appendicitis confirmed or suspected based on CT or B-ultrasonography, with patients opting out of appendectomy; (2) Patients with an Alvarado score of ≥ 5. Exclusion criteria included: (1) Appendix perforation or other forms of complicated appendicitis; (2) Contraindications to colonoscopy; (3) Hemorrhagic, neurological, mental disorders, or other severe diseases; (4) Impaired cognitive function. A STROBE participant flow chart that depicts screened, excluded (with reasons), included, and final numbers by group is shown in Fig. 1. This retrospective cohort study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the the Third Hospital of Shandong Province (Approval No. KYLL-2024065; Approval Date: 05/08/2024). All procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and institutional guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines to ensure transparency and completeness in reporting observational research.

Description of technique

Patients were assigned to the ERAT or SERAT group based on the clinical suggestions of attending physicians and the informed choice of patients and their families. In the ERAT group, patients received either 2 L of polyethylene glycol electrolyte solution or 300-500 ml of clean enema, administered five times. The ERAT procedure included: (1) Inserting a colonoscope (CF-H290AI, Olympus, Japan) into the ileocecal area with a conical transparent cap to assess the appendix opening and surrounding mucosa for coprolite incarceration and abscess discharge; (2) Using a conical transparent cap to push the Gerlach valve and insert a wire-guided catheter into the appendiceal cavity; (3) Conducting appendiceal angiography with iohexol contrast medium to examine the appendiceal cavity’s morphology and diameter, assess contrast medium leakage or filling defects, and identify appendiceal stones or perforations. Fecaliths were removed using a net basket or balloon as needed. (4) Repeatedly rinsing the appendiceal cavity with 50–100 mL of normal saline; (5) In cases with significant pus or a narrow appendiceal cavity, a 7–8.5 F plastic stent (SPSOF7-7, Cook, USA) was inserted for continuous drainage and rinsing; The endoscopic image of the ERAT procedure is depicted in Fig. 2.

Procedures of endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy (ERAT). A Appendiceal edema. B Pus extraction. C The endoscopic retrograde appendicography (ERA) fluoroscopy show the appendix lumen. D Remove the appendix fecalith

In the SERAT group, preoperative preparations were identical to those in the ERAT group. The procedure involved pushing the Gerlach valve with a conical transparent cap and inserting a wire guide into the appendiceal cavity. Subsequently, the eyeMax was introduced along the guide wire to examine the cavity and inner wall of the appendix for stones or intraluminal pus. Treatments like stone removal, washing, and biopsy were performed as required. The endoscopic image of the SERAT procedure is depicted in Fig. 3.

Procedures of sub-endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy (SERAT). A Congestion and edema around the mucosa of appendix orifice. B Spyglass insertion. C Edema of the inner wall of the appendix. D Appendix fecalith

Antibiotics were administered to all patients for a maximum of three days post-surgery. Once abdominal pain subsided, patients were put on a liquid diet and discharged upon symptom relief and normalization of laboratory tests. All endoscopic procedures were performed by three senior gastroenterologists with more than 5 years of experience in digestive endoscopy, and the operational processes were standardized through unified training. Postoperative temperature was monitored every 4 h, VAS score was evaluated at 6 h and 24 h after surgery, and white blood cell count was rechecked at 1 day and 3 days after surgery. However, the assessors were not blinded in this study.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data collected included age, sex, comorbidities, history of abdominal surgery, clinical symptoms (abdominal pain, fever, tenderness, rebound pain), and laboratory/imaging results (white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, CRP, CT or ultrasound).

Short-term surgical success was defined as effective appendicitis treatment without perforation or intraoperative comorbidities like bleeding. Operation time, intubation time, stent placement, and fecalith extraction were compared between the two groups. Postoperative recovery metrics evaluated included VAS score for abdominal pain 6 h post-treatment, length of hospital stay, and hospitalization costs. Endoscopic findings encompassed congestion and edema at the appendix opening and surrounding mucosa, presence of fecaliths, and pus discharge. eyeMax microscopic observations included intracavitary fecaliths, pus, pus moss attachment, hyperemia, edema on the appendix’s inner wall, and lumen distortion, tortuosity, dilation, or stenosis. Recurrence rates and long-term postoperative outcomes, including abdominal pain, perforation, and diarrhea, were recorded and compared between the two groups. There were no missing data in the clinical information, laboratory examinations, imaging results and follow-up data of all enrolled patients, and the full analysis set was used for statistical analysis.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed using SPSS version 23 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA), GraphPad Prism version 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), and the MatchIt and survival packages of R software version 4.1.0. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Time-to-event analysis for recurrence was performed using the Kaplan–Meier method with log-rank test. A p-value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. The sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the results based on 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) with a 0.2 caliper used to form the control group, with adjustment for age, sex, comorbidities, history of abdominal surgery, Alvarado score, laboratory indices, and imaging features.

Results

Patient characteristics

Before propensity score matching (PSM), a total of 60 patients in the ERAT group and 97 patients in the SERAT group were included. Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, comorbidities, history of abdominal surgery, clinical symptoms, signs, laboratory and imaging findings, Alvarado score, and VAS score, were comparable between the two groups (P > 0.05). To minimize confounding and balance baseline covariates, 1:1 PSM was performed with a caliper of 0.2, adjusting for age, sex, comorbidities, history of abdominal surgery, Alvarado score, laboratory indices, and imaging features. After matching, 55 patients were retained in each group. Baseline characteristics remained well balanced between groups, with no significant differences observed in all variables (P > 0.05). Comparison of short-term efficacy

Before PSM, SERAT showed clear procedural advantages over ERAT. The operation time was significantly shorter in the SERAT group than in the ERAT group (20.8 vs. 44.8 min, P < 0.001), and the intubation time was also significantly shorter (103 vs. 258 s, P < 0.001). In addition, the technical success rate was significantly higher in the SERAT group (94/97) than in the ERAT group (52/60, P = 0.015), whereas failed intubation occurred less frequently in the SERAT group. Stent implantation was less common in the SERAT group than in the ERAT group (P = 0.046). After PSM, compared to the ERAT group, the SERAT group had significantly shorter operative and intubation times (24.69 vs. 42.65 min, P < 0.001; 96.38 vs. 241.38 s, P < 0.001) in Tables 3 and 4.

Comparison of short-term efficacy after propensity score matching

No significant differences were observed between the groups in stent implantation, fecalith removal rate, VAS score at 6 h post-treatment and white blood cell count, length of hospital stay, and hospitalization costs (P > 0.05). No perioperative complications occurred in either group. Direct visualization with eyeMax in the SERAT group revealed appendiceal inner-wall congestion and edema in all cases (100%), intracavitary fecaliths in 54.4% of patients, and intraluminal pus in 89% of patients, demonstrating the ability of SERAT to provide detailed intraluminal information and guide targeted treatment.

Comparison of long-term efficacy

During follow-up, recurrence occurred in 8 patients in the ERAT group and in 3 patients in the SERAT group after PSM. Among patients with follow-up data, recurrence-free survival was significantly better in the SERAT group (3/55, 5.4% vs. 11/55, 20%). Kaplan–Meier analysis further showed that recurrence-free survival was significantly better in the SERAT group than in the ERAT group (log-rank P = 0.036) (Fig. 4). Of the recurrent cases in the ERAT group, 5 underwent surgery, 3 received follow-up conservative management, and 3 improved after subsequent SERAT treatment. The only recurrent patient in the SERAT group underwent surgery. No long-term adverse events, including abdominal pain, perforation, or diarrhea, were observed in either group.

Discussion

Acute appendicitis is one of the most common acute abdominal emergencies requiring urgent intervention [1, 18, 19]. Although appendectomy has long been the standard treatment, it is associated with surgical risks, negative resection, and loss of an immune-functional organ.Furthermore, the appendix plays a role in immunity, hosting beneficial microorganisms and contributing to the maintenance of intestinal bacterial homeostasis [20]. Additionally, a study has indicated a correlation between appendectomy and an increased risk of Crohn’s disease [21]. Minimally invasive organ-preserving endoscopic strategies have therefore become increasingly preferred for uncomplicated acute appendicitis.

Endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy (ERAT) has been widely applied as a safe and effective option [22–24].However, conventional ERAT lacks direct visualization of the appendiceal lumen, which may restrict the completeness of fecalith removal and limit intraluminal evaluation. Sub-endoscopic retrograde appendicitis therapy (SERAT) using the eyeMax imaging system enables direct observation of the appendix cavity, potentially overcoming these limitations and improving therapeutic precision [16, 24–26].

In our retrospective cohort study, we compared ERAT and SERAT for the treatment of acute appendicitis and found that SERAT showed several clinically meaningful advantages after propensity score matching. Compared with ERAT, SERAT was associated with significantly shorter operation time and intubation time, as well as a higher technical success rate. In addition, recurrence-free survival during follow-up was significantly better in the SERAT group, and recurrence occurred less frequently. By contrast, several short-term recovery indicators, including fecalith removal rate, postoperative pain score, hospital stay, and hospitalization cost, were comparable between the two groups after matching. These findings suggest that the main advantage of SERAT lies in increasing procedural efficiency and technical success while reducing subsequent recurrence.

SERAT significantly shortened both operation time and intubation time compared with ERAT, and this advantage remained evident after matching. Conventional ERAT mainly relies on indirect access to the appendiceal lumen and appendicographic guidance, which can be challenging in the setting of mucosal edema, luminal narrowing, or anatomical distortion. In contrast, SERAT allows direct intraluminal visualization, enabling the operator to identify the appendiceal cavity more precisely and perform targeted irrigation and intervention under direct vision. This direct-view characteristic likely explains the significantly shorter intubation and overall procedural times observed in the SERAT group. In the present study, SERAT achieved a significantly higher successful treatment rate than ERAT, suggesting that eyeMax-assisted direct visualization may help overcome some of the anatomical and inflammatory obstacles that limit conventional ERAT. Similar to the findings of Kong LJ et al. [16] and Tao L et al. [26] who used digital single-operator cholangioscope for appendiceal diseases, our study confirmed that the application of ultrathin endoscopy systems in the treatment of acute appendicitis can achieve direct visualization of the appendiceal cavity, which is conducive to targeted treatment.

The direct endoscopic findings in the SERAT group further support the mechanistic basis for these advantages. In our cohort, eyeMax-assisted examination revealed inner-wall congestion and edema in all cases, intracavitary fecaliths in 54.4% of patients, and intraluminal pus in 89% of patients. These findings demonstrate that SERAT can provide detailed intraluminal information that is difficult to obtain with ERAT alone. Under conventional ERAT, assessment of appendiceal pathology is mainly indirect, relying on the appendiceal orifice and appendicography. By contrast, SERAT allows direct inspection of the disease focus itself, which may improve the precision of irrigation, drainage, and intraluminal clearance. This more accurate evaluation and treatment of appendiceal obstruction and inflammation may partly explain the better procedural performance and long-term outcomes observed in the SERAT group.

An additional important finding of this study is the better long-term efficacy of SERAT. After PSM, recurrence occurred less frequently in the SERAT group than in the ERAT group, and Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated significantly better recurrence-free survival. Recurrence is one of the major concerns associated with appendix-preserving treatment strategies, and therefore this result is of particular clinical significance. A plausible explanation is that direct visualization allows more complete treatment of residual intraluminal disease, including fecaliths, pus, and localized stenotic or inflammatory changes, thereby reducing the likelihood of persistent obstruction or recurrent inflammation. In this context, the superior recurrence-free survival observed after SERAT is likely not merely a statistical finding, but a clinically meaningful extension of its procedural advantages.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that need to be acknowledged. First, this is a retrospective single-center study, which has inherent selection bias. Patients were assigned to the two groups based on clinical physician recommendations and patient choices, which may lead to allocation bias. Second, the sample size of the study is relatively small, and the number of endpoint events (recurrence) is low, which undermines the statistical reliability and leads to limited statistical power. The interpretation of the results needs to be cautious. Third, the follow-up period of the study is relatively short, and the long-term efficacy and safety of SERAT need to be verified by long-term follow-up of a large number of patients. Fourth, the assessors were not blinded in the study, and the operator experience, although standardized, may still have individual differences, which may lead to performance/measurement bias.

Conclusions

SERAT is a safe and effective minimally invasive treatment method for acute appendicitis, which has significant advantages over ERAT in shortening operation time and intubation time, and reducing the recurrence rate of acute appendicitis. The application of eyeMax enables direct visualization of the appendiceal cavity, which can realize targeted irrigation and precise removal of fecaliths, and overcome the shortcomings of ERAT that cannot directly observe the appendiceal cavity.

Both ERAT and SERAT can preserve the appendix, avoid the risks of surgical resection, and have the advantages of high short-term success rate and short hospital stay. SERAT is more suitable for clinical promotion and application in the treatment of uncomplicated acute appendicitis, especially for special populations such as pregnant women, children and patients who refuse radiological examinations. However, the long-term efficacy and safety of SERAT still need to be verified by large-sample and long-term follow-up studies.
