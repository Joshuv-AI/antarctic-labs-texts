Dampening of positive affect as an emotional contrast avoidance strategy: An experimental test of the contrast avoidance model
Authors: Myriam Gérardy, Liesbeth Bogaert, Michelle G Newman, David J Hallford, Filip Raes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDampening of positive affect is a transdiagnostic vulnerability factor for various mental health problems, yet the underlying mechanism driving this process remains unclear. The Contrast Avoidance Model (CAM) offers a potential explanation, proposing that individuals may intentionally downregulate positive emotions to prevent distressing negative emotional contrasts (NECs). The present preregistered experimental study examined the applicability of the CAM to dampening. In a randomized controlled design with an unselected community sample, participants first underwent a happiness induction and were then randomly assigned to either a 2-min dampening induction (n = 50) or a relaxation induction (n = 52) prior to a sadness induction, to test whether the dampening induction reduced the strength of a downward shift from high to low positive affect (i.e., NEC). Positive and negative affect were assessed before and after each induction. Multilevel analyses indicated that the negative emotional contrast from before versus after the sadness induction was less pronounced in the dampening condition compared to the relaxation condition. These findings provide initial experimental support for the applicability of the CAM to dampening as an emotional contrast avoidance strategy. Clinical implications and directions for future research are discussed.Keywords: Anhedonia, Dampening, Emotion regulation, Negative emotional contrasts, Positive affect

Dampening refers to the tendency to respond to a positive affective state with mental strategies that reduce its intensity and duration (Feldman et al., 2008). Typical dampening thoughts might include “This is too good to be true”, “People will think I am bragging”, and “I’m not worthy of feeling good” (Feldman et al., 2008). Research on dampening has been primarily conducted in the context of depression, where it has been associated consistently with depressive symptoms in both cross-sectional and longitudinal studies (Bean et al., 2022; Dunn & Roberts, 2016; Feldman et al., 2008). Dampening has been conceptualized as an emotion regulation mechanism (i.e., processes that influence the frequency, intensity and duration of, in this case, positive emotions; Gross, 1998) that contributes to the development and maintenance of depression (Joormann & Vanderlind, 2014; Vanderlind et al., 2022). More specifically, dampening has been proposed as a key contributor to deficits in positive affect (PA) along with heightened anhedonia (Dunn et al., 2018). Anhedonia, defined as a markedly diminished interest or pleasure in activities, is one of the two cardinal symptoms of Major Depressive Disorder (MDD; American Psychiatric Association, 2022).

Apart from its strong bidirectional relationship with depressive symptoms, dampening appears to represent a transdiagnostic vulnerability and emotion regulation factor contributing to a range of other forms of psychopathology. Elevated levels of dampening have been linked to symptoms of anxiety disorders, posttraumatic stress disorder, eating difficulties, and bipolar disorder, as well as an elevated risk for psychosis and substance use (Bean et al., 2022; Damme et al., 2022; Dworschak et al., 2023; Edge et al., 2013; Eisner et al., 2009; Malivoire et al., 2022; Peckham et al., 2020; Wolkenstein et al., 2022). Furthermore, experimental studies with community samples demonstrated that induced dampening caused decreased happiness and increased sadness in university students (Burr et al., 2017; Dunn et al., 2018) and adolescents (Yilmaz et al., 2019).

As a transdiagnostic process that reduces PA, dampening may contribute to clinically relevant impairments, given that reduced PA and anhedonia have been associated with motivational and functional deficits. Moreover, a meta-analysis of Wong et al. (2024) reported that anhedonia was associated with greater functional impairment and poorer health-related quality of life. Reduced PA may also diminish the broader health advantages linked to PA, including better physical health such as lower risk of developing upper respiratory illness after exposure to a rhinovirus or influenza (Cohen et al., 2006) and better sleep quality (Sin et al., 2017). The associations between positive emotions and health benefits align with Fredrickson’s (1998) broaden-and-build theory that states that positive emotions serve an adaptive function by expanding individuals’ momentary thought-action repertoires and, over time, building enduring cognitive, social, and physical resources. Positive emotions were also associated with greater relationship satisfaction (on a cross-sectional level; Watson et al., 2000) and overall job satisfaction (on a prospective level; Watson & Slack, 1993). Beyond its appetitive effects, anhedonia has also been linked to reduced threat learning, potentially driven by a diminished experience of relief (Leng et al., 2024).

Accordingly, it is essential that treatments address diminished positive affect. However, conventional treatments of MDD have primarily focused on reducing negative affect (NA) and paid comparatively little attention to strategies for cultivating and sustaining PA (Dunn, 2012). Yet, the mechanisms underlying PA are partially independent from the mechanisms underlying NA (Dunn & Roberts, 2016; Watson et al., 1999). Thus, treatment of depressive symptoms should target both decreasing the elevated NA and enhancing the low PA (Dunn, 2012; Dunn & Roberts, 2016; Watson et al., 1999). The latter also holds high clinical relevance. Interventions that aim at increased PA are consistent with the views of patients with major depression that restoring PA is of high importance in recovery from depression (Demyttenaere et al., 2015). Reflecting this growing recognition, interventions have been developed that explicitly target deficits in positive affect and reward processing, including Positive Affect Treatment (PAT; Craske et al., 2019; Craske et al., 2016), Augmented Depression Therapy (ADepT; Dunn et al., 2019), and Behavioral Activation for Anhedonia (BATA; Nagy et al., 2020). Emerging evidence suggests that such interventions can improve positive affect, anhedonia, and broader depressive outcomes (Craske et al., 2024; Sandman & Craske, 2022). Similarly, LaFreniere and Newman (2023b, 2024) found that individuals with generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) who received a savoring treatment had decreased worry, increased positive emotions and prioritizing positivity, as well as reduced “kill-joy thinking” (i.e., a specific form of dampening; Bryant et al., 2011) compared to a self-monitoring control condition.

In particular, understanding why individuals engage in dampening is key to developing interventions that address this maladaptive emotion regulation strategy. Yet, to date, the underlying mechanism that drives dampening has not been established. Interventions that do not consider interfering underlying mechanisms such as maladaptive emotion regulation strategies, run the risk of being counterproductive. For instance, when individuals engaged in dampening appraisals during pleasant activity scheduling (i.e., a strategy used in behavioral activation), there was an increase in NA and a decrease in PA compared to individuals who were in the control condition (Burr et al., 2017). Recent interventions aiming to increase PA, such as ADepT, have explicitly targeted barriers to positive affect including dampening (Dunn et al., 2019, 2023). However, although these interventions have acknowledged dampening as a relevant barrier to positive affect, the motivation underlying dampening remains insufficiently understood. A clearer understanding may help further refine and improve interventions targeting positive affect.

Insights into potential motivational mechanisms behind dampening can be drawn from research on the emotion regulation strategy of worry. In the context of generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), Newman and Llera (2011) developed the Contrast Avoidance Model (CAM). This theoretical framework conceptualizes worry as an experiential avoidance strategy. Rather than the avoidance of specific negative emotional states, the CAM posits that individuals try to avoid a sharp increase in negative emotion, referred to as a negative emotional contrast (NEC). As part of CAM theory, engaging in perseverative negative thoughts is a strategy to maintain emotional homeostasis. In other words, individuals engage in worrying to increase and sustain negative emotion and to decrease positive emotion in anticipation of future negative events to preserve emotional stability and avoid the instability arising from NECs.

The CAM has been evidenced as having transdiagnostic relevance, such as in GAD, depression, panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, bipolar disorder, and obsessive compulsive disorder (Gerdan, 2025; Kim et al., 2024; Newman et al., 2023; Swisher & Newman, 2025). In experimental (Jamil & Llera, 2021; Kim & Newman, 2019, 2022, 2023; Llera & Newman, 2010, 2014) and naturalistic momentary assessment studies (Baik & Newman, 2023, 2025; Newman et al., 2019, 2022) both worry and rumination were more concurrently associated with decreased positive emotion and increased negative emotion relative to non-worry states.

Moreover, these studies found that high (vs. low) worry and/or rumination at a prior timepoint led to less of an increase in sadness and/or anxiety at a later timepoint and/or when individuals encountered a subsequent negative event. Also, compared to those without these disorders, individuals with GAD and/or depression were more likely to report using these perseverative thought styles to avoid NECs (Baik & Newman, 2025). Importantly, concerning positive affective states, participants with GAD and/or depression also reported greater vulnerability to NECs when feeling positive (Baik & Newman, 2025). Thus, downregulating PA may serve to soften the downward shift in positive emotions that inevitably occurs once a positive affective state ends. In other words, individuals with a dampening tendency may prefer actively downregulating positive emotions in the moment to avoid experiencing the end of the positive affective state.

The CAM might also clarify the mechanisms that drive dampening. A recent cross-sectional study found that avoidance of NECs predicted concurrent levels of dampening above and beyond age, gender, and engagement in repetitive negative thinking (Bogaert et al., 2026). Moreover, qualitative findings from Bogaert et al. (2023) suggested that a key reason for avoiding positive emotions was the anticipation of feeling worse after positive emotions had faded. Thus, dampening may serve as a defensive strategy to prevent NECs that may emerge as a result of exposure to a negative experience. Nonetheless, the applicability of CAM to dampening has not been directly examined in experimental studies.

The aim of the current experimental study was to test the applicability of the CAM to dampening. Two hypotheses were formulated to examine whether a dampening induction reduced the strength of NECs. Hypothesis 1 states that following a happiness induction, an experimental manipulation of induced dampening (vs. relaxation) would result in greater decreases in PA (H1a) and increases in NA (H1b). To this end, a dampening induction similar to that used by Dunn et al. (2018) was employed, enabling the first independent replication outside of the original Exeter laboratory as well as the first application of this paradigm in an online experimental format. The central hypothesis (H2) was that a subsequent sadness induction would result in a smaller decrease in PA (H2a) and a smaller increase in NA (H2b) for participants who previously engaged in dampening, compared to relaxation.

Additionally, as stated in the preregistration, the study explored whether baseline levels of anhedonia, depression, dampening, and avoidance of NECs moderated affective responses to the manipulation and the sadness induction. We expected that these baseline traits would predict sharper decreases in PA and increases in NA from the happiness induction to the dampening (vs. relaxation) manipulation, as well as smaller shifts in PA and NA from the dampening (vs. relaxation) manipulation to the sadness induction. These expectations were based on previous work demonstrating cross-sectional associations between dampening and anhedonia/depression (e. g., Dunn et al., 2018; Feldman et al., 2008), and between dampening and NEC avoidance (Bogaert et al., 2026). As these traits are associated with dampening, they are expected to magnify the effects of the experimentally induced dampening.

Method

Design

The present study employed a randomized controlled experimental repeated measures design with one between-subject factor (condition: dampening vs. relaxation as experimental manipulation) and one within-subject factor (time). Participants were randomly assigned to one of the two conditions and completed four measurement points: baseline (T0), after the happiness induction (T1), after the experimental manipulation (T2), and after the sadness induction (T3).

Participants

An a priori power analysis using RMASS II (Gibbons et al., 2015; Wei et al., 2023) indicated a required sample of 100 participants to detect differences of a medium effect size (d = 0.50, α = .05, β = 0.80, r for repeated measures = 0.60) between the experimental condition (dampening) and the control condition (relaxation), accounting for 5% attrition (see LaFreniere & Newman, 2023b). Data collection was performed in June 2025. Recruitment proceeded until useable data was collected from at least 48 participants per condition (Fig. 2 for the flowchart conform the CONSORT 2010 guidelines; Cuschieri, 2019). In total, 125 native English-speaking community adults between 18 and 80 years old, representing a community sample, were recruited via Prolific (2025). Because participant data usability could only be determined after completion, recruitment was reopened in small batches to ensure at least 48 useable cases per condition, resulting in a slight intentional oversampling.

Flowchart representing the participants through the stages of data collection and processing.

Data from 102 participants was used in the analysis (53 women, 46 men, and 3 other; age: M = 39.25, SD = 12.84), with 50 in the dampening condition and 52 in the relaxation condition. The sample included participants from 11 different nationalities, most commonly from the United Kingdom (UK; 50%) and the United States (US; 27%). Of the 23 participants who did not complete the study, 14 withdrew immediately at the start, and data from the remaining nine participants were excluded from the analysis (as preregistered) because they failed at least one attention check or reported poor engagement with the experiment (see Measures for details). All participants gave their informed consent and full study participation was rewarded with £2.70, in line with Prolific’s payment principles (£9.00 per hour). The study was approved by the Social and Societal Ethics Committee of our institution. Prior to data collection, the study was preregistered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/6a954).

Measures

The Dampening Subscale of the Responses to Positive Affect Questionnaire (RPA-d; Feldman et al., 2008).

The RPA-d consists of eight items and measures participants’ general tendency to engage in dampening. Participants were asked to rate each item (e.g., “When you are feeling happy, how often do you think about things that could go wrong?”) from 1 (almost never) to 4 (almost always). In the current sample, the internal consistency of the RPA-d was good (α = .89).

The Leuven Exeter Dampening Scale (LEDS; Bogaert et al., 2025).

Although the RPA-d is traditionally used, the LEDS was developed to provide a more comprehensive assessment of trait dampening by covering a broader range of clinically and cross-culturally informed appraisals (Bogaert et al., 2025). The inclusion of both dampening scales maximized the robustness of the findings on trait dampening. The scale contains 13 items (e.g., “When you start to feel happy, how often do you think “I’m not worthy of feeling good”?”) that are answered on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). The internal consistency of the LEDS in this study was excellent (α = .90).

The Snaith Hamilton Pleasure Scale (SHAPS; Snaith et al., 1995).

The SHAPS consists of 14 items and measures anhedonia. Participants were asked to indicate to which degree each statement (e.g., “I would find pleasure in my hobbies and pastimes”) applied to them, from 0 (strongly disagree) to 3 (strongly agree). The internal consistency of the SHAPS was excellent in this study (α = .90).

The Depressive Symptoms Subscale of the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21; Lovibond & Lovibond, 1995).

The Depressive Symptoms subscale of the DASS-21 contains seven items and measures the emotional state of depression. Participants were asked to indicate how much each item (e.g., “I felt down-hearted and blue.”) applied to them over the past week, from 0 (does not apply to me at all) to 3 (applies to me very much or most of the time”). The internal consistency of the Depressive Symptoms subscale of the DASS-21 in this study was excellent (α = .92). For brevity, from here onwards the subscale will be referred to as the DASS-D.

The Discomfort with Emotional Shifts Subscale of the Contrast Avoidance Questionnaire (CAQ-GE; Llera & Newman, 2017).

The Discomfort with Emotional Shifts subscale of the CAQ-GE contains seven items and was used to measure participants’ discomfort with emotional shifts. Participants were asked to indicate the extent to which each item (e.g., “I feel uneasy with emotional changes.”) was true for them, from 1 (not at all true) to 5 (absolutely true). The Discomfort with Emotional Shifts subscale of the CAQ-GE had an excellent internal consistency in this sample (α = .94). For brevity, from here onwards the subscale will be referred to as the CAQ-GE-DES.

An Eight-Item Version of the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS; Watson et al., 1988).

In line with Burr et al. (2017), who modified the PANAS to capture both high and low arousal affect, we used their eight-item version of the PANAS to assess participants’ current PA and NA, given that the present task was not expected to elicit high arousal. Participants were presented with four PA (i.e., relaxed, excited, happy, and contented) and four NA items (i.e., tense, sad, upset, and bored), for which they were asked to report the extent to which they experienced it in that moment, from 1 (very slightly or not at all) to 5 (extremely). However, visual inspection of pilot data collected using the same experimental protocol (Table S1 and Table S2 in the Supplementary Materials) suggested that the PA item ‘excited’ on average yielded slightly lower (and the NA item ‘bored’ slightly higher) scores than the other PA items (and NA items, respectively). Moreover, conceptually the item ‘excited’ is associated with high arousal which was not specifically intended to be elicited in the current study. Therefore, as preregistered, those two items were excluded from the main analyses. However, all analyses including those two items were run as well and results are reported in the Supplementary Materials. Overall, unless stated otherwise, conclusions drawn from these analyses including these two items were identical to the ones reported in the article. The internal consistency of the PANAS subscales at baseline was good (α reduced PA scale = 0.88; α full PA scale = 0.90; α reduced NA scale = 0.82; α full NA scale = 0.83). The PANAS was, apart from the baseline measurement (i.e., T0), also administered at T1, T2, and T3 to measure changes in PA and NA throughout the experiment (see Procedure).

Manipulation Checks.

Participants in the dampening condition also completed a state-adapted version of the RPA-d at T2 and T3 (see Procedure). Following Dunn et al. (2018), who modified the instructions of the RPA to assess state appraisals during a positive event memory recall task, the instructions in the current study were adapted to “To what extent while watching the video did you …”. This modification aimed to capture the extent to which participants in the dampening condition engaged in dampening during the dampening induction (measured at T2) and during the sadness induction (measured at T3). Additionally, after the experiment, all participants were asked to indicate the extent to which they had followed the dampening or relaxation instructions, with the assurance that their responses would not affect remuneration.

Attention Checks and Control Questions.

To ensure data quality, three attention checks were embedded within the baseline measures in the form of instructed-response items (e.g., “This is a control question. Please indicate: Strongly disagree”). Participants in the dampening condition were also asked whether they had experienced any alternative thoughts during the manipulation that might have either strengthened or weakened the effect of the manipulation. Finally, at the end of the experiment, an item was included to explore whether participants believed that the type of manipulation played a role in the magnitude of the NEC during the sadness induction (i.e., Hypothesis 2; see Table S3 in the Supplementary Materials for the items).

Experimental manipulation

After the happiness induction was completed and the PANAS was administered for the second time, the script randomly assigned participants (based on Morys-Carter, 2024) to one of two conditions with different instructions about how to process the elicited positive feelings (Table 1 for an overview). In the dampening condition (i.e., the experimental condition), participants were asked to engage in dampening according to instructions based on Dunn et al. (2018) and to “think about why the feelings of positivity experienced” … were too good to last, why you don’t deserve these positive feelings and what things could go wrong as a result of these positive feelings”). In the relaxation condition, which functioned as control condition, participants were instructed to engage in relaxation through diaphragmatic breathing following the instructions from Zainal and Newman (2018; similar to the relaxation induction in Kim & Newman, 2022). In both conditions, participants engaged in the assigned response for a fixed duration of 2 min. To ensure a fixed duration of 2 min across participants, a timer was started after participants indicated they read the instructions. A progress bar was displayed throughout the 2-min interval, after which the task ended automatically and participants could proceed to the next phase.

Emotion induction

Emotions were induced using facial expression picture sets, an approach shown to effectively elicit discrete emotions such as happiness or sadness in the meta-analysis of Joseph et al. (2020). The happiness-inducing and sadness-inducing (Fig. 1) phases were shown to effectively elicit the intended emotions in an online pilot study conducted in our lab (see Table S4 in the Supplementary Materials). Pictures with the highest ratings for valence and arousal from the basic-emotion subset (i.e., happiness, anger, fear, disgust, sadness, and surprise) were selected from the Nencki Affective Picture System (NAPS-BE; Marchewka et al., 2014; Riegel et al., 2016; see Table S5 in the Supplementary Materials). Before watching the picture sets, participants were instructed to pay close attention to the portrayed emotions, to take note of the facial expression and body language of the people depicted, and to think about moments in their own lives when they may have experienced similar feelings.

Procedure

Participants enrolled via Prolific, an online participant recruitment platform for behavioral research. The study was hosted and run on Pavlovia, an online platform for surveys and behavioral experiments, with a custom HTML script using the jsPsych library (de Leeuw, 2015). After providing informed consent, they reported their age, gender (male, female, non-binary, or prefer not to disclose), and nationality (selected from a standardized list of countries included in the demographics module from jsPsych; de Leeuw, 2015). At baseline (T0), participants completed the RPA-d, LEDS, SHAPS, the DASS-D, the CAQ-GE-DES, and the first administration of the PANAS (see Fig. 1). Next, all participants watched a happiness-inducing set of pictures and then completed a second PANAS assessment (T1). They were then randomized to one of two conditions: half of the participants received the dampening instructions, the other half received the relaxation instructions (see Table 1). At T2, all of them again completed the PANAS. Following this, participants watched a sadness-inducing set of pictures and completed a final PANAS at T3. In addition, contrary to several prior studies (Burr et al., 2017; Dunn et al., 2018; Yilmaz et al., 2019), only the participants in the dampening condition completed the modified (state-adapted) RPA-d at T2 and T3. This approach prevented the risk that those in the relaxation condition would be unintentionally cued to engage in dampening through repeated exposure to dampening-focused items. Finally, all participants watched a set of pictures intended to improve their mood and answered control questions. The total duration of the experiment was approximately 25 min.

Statistical analyses

Manipulation Checks.

As preregistered, for the happiness induction a paired-sample t-test was performed on the sum scores of the PA items before versus after the happiness induction. No manipulation check could be conducted for the impact of the sadness induction, as it could not be disentangled from the dampening versus relaxation induction. To check whether the experimental manipulation of dampening versus relaxation had the intended effect, two types of analyses were performed. First, average levels of PA and NA sum scores following the induction were compared via independent-sample t-tests. Second, within both conditions, paired-sample t-tests compared PA (and NA) levels at baseline versus after the manipulation (i.e., after the dampening induction vs. after the relaxation induction). In addition, self-reported levels of state RPA-d in the dampening group were assessed.

Main Analyses.

As preregistered, the primary outcome variables were PA and NA. To test the two main hypotheses, piecewise (two-level) multilevel analyses were conducted with PA as the primary outcome for Hypotheses 1a and 2a and NA for Hypotheses 1b and 2b to examine the main and interaction effects of the conditions and time (in line with Kim & Newman, 2022). Time points (Level 1: T0, T1, T2, T3) were nested within participants (Level 2). The models included (a) dummy-coded time points at Level 1, (b) condition (relaxation versus dampening) at Level 2, and (c) their cross-level interactions as predictors of PA and NA. Age and gender were entered as covariates to adjust for potential confounding effects. As decided in a post-hoc way, to adjust for potential confounding effects when studying the effect of the manipulations on PA and NA, levels of PA (/NA) at T1 (i.e., after the happiness induction) were included as Level-2 covariates. This controlled for residual individual differences in affect following the happiness induction, which was intended to produce uniformly high PA and low NA across participants. Because adjusting for age, gender, and pre-manipulation PA/NA may constitute a conservative control strategy, additional analyses were conducted without these covariates to examine the robustness of the findings. For each effect, Cohen’s f2 was computed, where f2 ≥ 0.15 is considered a medium, and f2 ≥ 0.35 a large effect size (Cohen, 1988; Selya et al., 2012). Consistent with recent methodological recommendations (Rubin, 2024), no correction for multiple testing was applied because the statistical inferences concerned individual hypotheses rather than a family-level inference based on the presence of at least one significant effect (Rubin, 2024).

Exploratory Analyses.

As preregistered, exploratory moderator analyses were conducted to examine whether individual differences in anhedonia, depressive symptoms, dampening, and discomfort with NECs influenced the magnitude of the expected emotional shifts from T1 (happiness induction) to T2 (manipulation) and from T2 to T3 (sadness induction). Three-way interactions between time, condition, and each moderator (T2 × Condition × Moderator; T3 × Condition × Moderator) were calculated to examine whether the effect of the manipulation (at T2) or the size of the NEC (at T3) varied as a function of these individual differences. For the exploratory analyses as well, sensitivity analyses excluding age, gender, and pre-manipulation PA/NA scores as covariates are performed to control for potential distortion of effects.

Specifically, the dampening instructions were expected to have stronger effects among participants reporting higher levels of anhedonia, depressive symptoms, trait dampening, and discomfort with NECs. A similar moderating effect was hypothesized to be at play in the affective changes during the sadness induction, reflected in smaller NECs for participants scoring high on dampening and on discomfort with NECs compared to those with lower scores. Significant three-way interactions were followed up with simple slope analyses.

Results

Demographics and baseline scores

No significant differences were observed between the conditions in age or nationality (Table 2). Chi-square tests indicated that the gender distribution did not significantly deviate from general population proportions of 49% women, 49% men, and 2% other (non-binary and/or non-disclosure of gender) in the total sample or within either condition (all p > .31). One-Way ANOVAs indicated no significant differences between the conditions in any of the baseline assessments of mood and emotion regulation (Table 2) and average scores fell within the norms of the scales (see the Supplementary Materials for a more detailed description). Trait dampening was significantly associated with discomfort with NECs (see Table S6 in the Supplementary Materials for the full baseline measures correlation matrix). Manipulation Checks

Mood Inductions.

The happiness induction had the intended effect of increasing PA (b = 0.69, SE = 0.29, t(302.26) = 2.34, p = .020, f2 = 0.01) and decreasing NA (b = −0.91, SE = 0.27, t(302.22) = −3.40, p < .001, f2 = 0.03) across all participants before the start of the experimental manipulation, with no significant differences in affective change between the conditions (T0 to T1; both p > .42) or level at T1 (i.e., after the happiness induction; both p > .60). Affective changes pre-to post the sadness induction are studied in more detail via the main analyses.

Experimental Manipulation Instructions.

The results suggest that the experimental manipulation was effective. Independent-samples t-tests on PA and NA levels at T2 indicated that participants in the dampening condition reported lower PA (t(94.26) = −4.55, p < .001, d = 0.91) and higher NA (t(83.11) = 3.79, p < .001, d = 0.76) compared to those in the relaxation condition. Within the dampening condition, paired-sample t-tests comparing scores at T0 vs. T2 (i.e., baseline vs. after the dampening induction) indicated a significant decrease in PA (t(49) = 2.11, p = .040, d = 0.30) but no significant change in NA (p = .161). Conversely, within the relaxation condition, from baseline to after the relaxation induction there was a significant increase in PA (t(51) = −2.54, p = .014, d = 0.35) and decrease in NA (t(51) = 3.61, p = <0.001, d = 0.50). In addition, in the dampening condition average state dampening was 14.24 (SD RPA-d = 5.02) at T2 (i.e., after the dampening induction) and 14.53 (SD RPA-d = 4.70) at T3 (i.e., after the sadness induction). Both were comparable to scores on the dampening subscale of the state-modified RPA reported by Dunn et al. (2018) following their dampening induction.

Experimental manipulation

In line with Hypothesis 1, there was a significant Time × Condition interaction from T1 to T2 (i.e., before versus after the manipulation) for both PA (b T2 × condition = −2.15, SE = 0.57, t(299.32) = −3.74, p < .001, f2 = 0.11) and NA (b T2 × condition = 1.27, SE = 0.53, t(299.32) = 2.38, p = .018, f2 = 0.07; Table 4; see Table S8 in the Supplementary Materials for analyses with inclusion of covariate PA/NA at T0 instead of T1). From T1 to T2, for PA, participants in the dampening condition showed a decrease in PA (b = −1.82, SE = 0.41, t(299.32) = −4.44, p < .001, f2 = 0.11) whereas participants in the relaxation condition showed no significant change (p = .417, Table 4). For NA, participants in the dampening condition showed an increase (b = 1.40, SE = 0.38, t(299.32) = 3.69, p < .001, f2 = 0.06) whereas participants in the relaxation condition showed no significant change (p = .717, Table 4). Results were comparable across models with and without covariate adjustment.

Effect of dampening versus relaxation on a negative emotional contrast

Consistent with Hypothesis 2, there was a significant Time × Condition interaction from T2 to T3 (i.e., before versus after the sadness induction) for both PA (b T3 × condition = 1.54, SE = 0.58, t(300.02) = 2.67, p = .008, f2 = 0.02) and NA (b T3 × condition = −1.42, SE = 0.53, t(299.92) = −2.67, p = .008, f2 = 0.02; Table 4 and Fig. 3; see Table S8 in the Supplementary Materials for analyses including PA/NA at T0 as covariate instead of at T1). For PA, both groups showed decreases from T2 to T3, but the magnitude of the decrease in PA from T2 to T3 was significantly smaller in the dampening condition compared to the relaxation condition (b dampening = −3.21, SE = 0.41, t(300.69) = −7.79, p < .001, f2 = 0.33; relaxation: b relaxation = −4.75, SE = 0.40, t(299.32) = −11.81, p < .001, f2 = 0.62). For NA, both groups showed increases from T2 to T3, but the magnitude of the increase in NA from T2 to T3 was significantly smaller in the dampening condition than in the relaxation condition (b dampening = 2.75, SE = 0.38, t(300.50) = 7.22, p < .001, f2 = 0.23; b relaxation = 4.17, SE = 0.37, t(299.32) = 11.23, p < .001, f2 = 0.60). In sum, both groups showed decreases in PA and increases in NA following the sadness induction, with the changes being less pronounced in the dampening condition compared to the relaxation condition (Table 4; see Table S9 in the Supplementary Materials for the full PA and NA item sets). Results were comparable across models with and without covariate adjustment. Of note, in support of the findings, 34 out of 50 participants in the dampening condition (68%) indicated in the exploratory question that they thought the dampening induction made the sadness induction feel less intense.

Exploratory moderation analyses

From T1 to T2 (i.e., before versus after the manipulation), for PA, there was a significant three-way interaction between time, condition, and trait dampening (b T2 × condition × RPA-d score = 0.23, SE = 0.10, t(293.31) = 2.23, p = .026, f2 = 0.08; b T2 × condition × LEDS score = 0.17, SE = 0.06, t(293.31) = 2.86, p = .005, f2 = 0.16; Table 5). Simple slope analyses indicated that within the dampening condition, those with lower trait dampening scores exhibited a steeper decrease in PA pre-to post the dampening induction (b RPA-d = 0.21, SE = 0.07, t(143.31) = 2.86, p = .005, f2 = 0.08; b LEDS = 0.16, SE = 0.04, t(143.31) = 3.83, p < .001, f2 = 0.17). At T2, within the dampening condition no significant differences between participants with lower versus higher trait dampening remained (for both RPA-d and LEDS scores, p > .670). In contrast, no significant pre-to post-manipulation moderation effects were observed in the relaxation condition for either RPA-d or LEDS scores (both p > .730). No other significant three-way interactions were observed from T1 to T2 (all p ≥ .06).

Discussion

The current study aimed to experimentally test the applicability of the CAM (Newman & Llera, 2011) to the domain of dampening of positive affect, as dampening may function as a NEC avoidance strategy (Bogaert et al., 2026). Specifically, we investigated whether engaging in dampening (versus relaxation) effectively lowered positive affect and increased negative affect (H1), and whether this low PA and high NA decreased the size of the negative emotional contrast that resulted from a subsequent sadness induction (H2). Participants completed a 2-min induction of happiness followed by an experimental manipulation (i.e., dampening vs. relaxation) and a 2-min sadness induction. They rated their positive and negative affect before and after the happiness induction, after the experimental manipulation, and after the sadness induction, to capture affective changes throughout the experiment.

Results supported the first hypothesis. Following a happiness induction, an experimental manipulation of induced dampening resulted in a greater decrease in PA and a greater increase in NA compared to those in the relaxation condition. Participants in the relaxation condition showed no significant affective changes, and maintained high PA and low NA. These findings provide the first independent replication outside the original Exeter laboratory and demonstrate that the dampening manipulation can be successfully implemented in an online experimental format.

The results also supported the central hypothesis. When exposed to the sadness induction, participants who had previously engaged in a dampening induction exhibited smaller shifts in PA (H2a) and NA (H2b) relative to those in the relaxation condition. The dampening induction may have rendered participants to have a lower positive affective state, thereby leaving less room for a sharp decline in PA during the subsequent sadness induction. Conversely, participants in the relaxation condition likely entered the sadness induction with higher PA and lower NA, potentially increasing their vulnerability to the NEC. Although these interaction effects were small in size, the pattern mirrored findings by Kim and Newman (2022) who observed that relaxation, compared to an experimental induction of worry or rumination, produced a sharper surge in negative affect after a sadness exposure. Thus, in line with our second hypothesis, participants in the dampening condition experienced significantly smaller NECs compared to those in the relaxation condition, as they started the sadness induction (i.e., at T2) with lowered PA and increased NA because of the prior dampening induction. In other words, responding to PA by engaging in dampening may have had a protective function against sharp NECs, whereas engaging in relaxation may have left participants vulnerable to be exposed to experiencing a sharper NEC during the sadness induction. This ability to control and reduce this NEC could possibly explain the process of dampening in daily life. That is, for individuals who excessively engage in dampening in daily life (or, who exhibit high trait dampening), dampening of PA could function as a means to prevent experiencing the short-term discomfort associated with sharp downward positive affective shifts (i.e., from high to low PA) once the positive affective state ends. However, as discussed above, positive affect is essential to mental health, and frequent suppression or dismissal of positive affective experiences forms a transdiagnostic vulnerability factor for many mental health problems (e.g., (Bean et al., 2022; Dworschak et al., 2023; Eisner et al., 2009; Malivoire et al., 2022).

Exploratory moderation analyses suggested that before versus after the manipulation, trait dampening moderated changes in PA within the dampening condition. Participants lower in trait dampening showed a larger decrease in PA pre-to post the dampening induction compared to those higher in trait dampening. No differences were found using trait dampening level as a moderator after the dampening induction. One possible interpretation of this within condition pattern is that the manipulation of negative mood leveled the playing field between those who spontaneously tend to use dampening and those who do not. Given the exploratory nature of these analyses and the small number of participants with elevated trait dampening (n = 9 for RPA-d scores ≥ + 1 SD; n = 7 for LEDS scores ≥ + 1 SD), these findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating pending replication in larger samples.

Exploratory analyses further suggested that, in the relaxation condition only, trait discomfort with NECs moderated changes in NA (including boredom) following the sadness induction. Specifically, participants higher in trait discomfort with NECs showed a greater increase in NA from relaxation to sadness compared to those lower in discomfort with NECs. Within the CAM framework, these exploratory findings may have reflected that those with high trait discomfort with NECs are typically motivated to sustain negative states in order to decrease vulnerability to subsequent negative affect. When instructed to engage in relaxation after a happiness induction, however, they may have followed the instructions despite this tendency, resulting in a temporarily decreased NA state. Consequently, the subsequent shift to sadness may have constituted a particularly salient and aversive negative affective contrast, thereby amplifying the increase in NA. In the dampening condition, this affective contrast may have been reduced due to the prior dampening induction, which may explain why the effect occurred uniquely in the relaxation condition. Alternatively, individuals higher in negative emotional sensitivity may not only experience stronger emotional responses (Hisler et al., 2020; Mettler et al., 2025), but may also be more aware of this heightened responsiveness. This could foster more negative appraisals of their own emotional sensitivity and, in turn, amplify reported negative affect following the sadness induction.

Clinical Implications.

The current findings have multiple potential clinical implications. A clinical intervention aimed at lowering dampening and its NEC avoidance function could help reinstall healthy responses to positive affective states. Consistent with clinical recommendations for excessive rumination and worry (Kim & Newman, 2022), similar intervention strategies may be applicable to individuals who frequently engage in dampening. Given its avoidance function, treatments addressing dampening of positive affect should rely on exposure-based approaches. Accordingly, interventions focused solely on increasing positive affect or enhancing positive emotional engagement may not fully address the contrast-avoidance function of dampening. In line with Kim and Newman (2022) who proposed exposure-based techniques for excessive rumination and worry, behavioral interventions targeting exposure to NECs may help disrupt the cycle of negative reinforcement by promoting experiential learning that NECs are tolerable. The NEC exposure would consist of repeated exposure to transitions from happiness to sadness to cause multiple sharp decreases in positive affect. Such approaches could be combined with response substitution strategies, such as training savoring techniques as adaptive responses to positive emotions. For instance, the smartphone-delivered ecological momentary intervention SkillJoy (LaFreniere & Newman, 2023b) which led to reduced contrast avoidance in (LaFreniere & Newman, 2023a), could be used to strengthen skills in savoring positive emotions in daily life. In addition, psychoeducation may help increase awareness of the long-term costs of reduced positive affect, including transdiagnostic vulnerability to psychopathology (e.g., Bean et al., 2022; Eisner et al., 2009). Relevant to this, a worry outcome monitoring momentary intervention led to greater reduced contrast avoidance relative to a thought monitoring control group (Schwob & Newman, 2026). This was likely because tracking their worries and subsequent outcomes showed them that a large majority of feared worry outcomes had not happened (LaFreniere & Newman, 2016; Schwob & Newman, 2026). Thus, this intervention may have helped them realize the long-term cost of contrast avoidance. Also, incorporating self-compassion (e.g., Desmond, 2016) may further facilitate adaptive reappraisal of dampening by helping individuals acknowledge its short-term protective intent (e.g., reducing anticipated emotional discomfort), while also recognizing that repeated dampening may ultimately interfere with psychological wellbeing. Through the perspective of Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), NEC avoidance could be viewed as a form of reduced psychological flexibility in response to emotional shifts, characterized by avoidance of negative emotional contrasts and reduced engagement in valued action when positive emotions arise (A-Tjak & Hayes, 2022; Ong & Eustis, 2022). Therefore, elements from ACT such as present-moment awareness, openness to sustained positive emotion, and values-consistent engagement in positive activities may also be applicable. Finally, experiential (rather than analytical) processing approaches may be promising for reducing dampening and enhancing positive affect (Sandman & Craske, 2024). In Positive Affect Treatment (PAT), for instance, imaginal recounting encourages individuals to recall pleasant autobiographical experiences with attention to sensory, emotional, and contextual details (Craske et al., 2019; Sandman & Craske, 2024), thereby promoting embodied engagement with positive experience rather than abstract or evaluative processing.

Limitations and Future Directions.

Our study methods slightly deviated from prior studies that experimentally manipulated dampening during a positive experience or memory recall procedure (Burr et al., 2017; Dunn et al., 2018; Yilmaz et al., 2019). In our study, we first induced positive affect and subsequently induced dampening thoughts. This approach ensures that a positive affective state was prior to the manipulation of dampening. Future research could examine whether combining a positive affect induction with concurrent dampening (versus control) instructions, in line with previous work, yields comparable effects on negative emotional contrasts. The present study also has some limitations to be considered. First, the somatic focus of the relaxation induction in contrast with the cognitive and emotional focus of the dampening induction may have contributed to condition differences independent of the intended manipulation (e.g., somatic relaxation can enhance positive affect (e.g., Jain et al., 2007). Future studies may benefit from using more comparable manipulation formats. Second, it was not possible to conduct a valid manipulation check because the state-modified RPA was administered exclusively in the dampening condition and there was no state dampening assessment at baseline. Future studies should assess state dampening and relaxation in both conditions, at baseline and after the manipulation. Third, the picture-based affect inducing procedures (AIPs) may not fully reflect how emotions emerge in daily life. Although more personally relevant inductions (such as imagery for positive future events; Dunn et al., 2018) may enhance ecological validity, the meta-analysis of Joseph et al. (2020) recommended facial expression pictures over imagery or autobiographical alternatives for inducing both happiness and sadness. Fourth, conducting the study online via Prolific may have introduced selection effects, attracting participants motivated by financial incentives or with particular interest in mental health. Despite attention checks for data quality, engagement and adherence could not be monitored as closely as in a laboratory setting. Fifth, the study relied solely on self-report. Future research could include physiological measures such as skin conductance for sympathetic activity (Kim & Newman, 2022) and facial electromyography for emotion-related muscle activity. Sixth, data on participants’ ethnicity were not collected, limiting conclusions regarding the generalizability of the findings across ethnicities. Finally, the present study was conducted in a community sample. Future research could examine whether the present findings extend to clinical populations. In line with findings that pathological worriers (versus controls) view worry as a beneficial coping strategy for avoiding NECs (Kim & Newman, 2022), clinical populations may be more likely than non-clinical groups to perceive dampening as protective against NECs.

Conclusion

The findings support the hypothesis that dampening serves as a cognitive strategy to sustain low positive affect and thereby can be used to avoid sharp shifts from high to low positive affect during exposure to sadness. This might have a soothing effect on the short-term and may therefore act as negative reinforcement, which could explain the motivation to frequently engage in dampening despite its negative mental health outcomes. This research could contribute to a deepened understanding of the mechanism driving individuals to engage in dampening and to inform more effective interventions for those caught in the cycle of avoidance of negative emotional contrasts.
