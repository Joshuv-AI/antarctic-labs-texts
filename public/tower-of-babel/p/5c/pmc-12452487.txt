Regulatory roles of intestinal CD4+ T cells in inflammation and their modulation by the intestinal microbiota
Authors: Ziyu Ma, Zixu Wang, Jing Cao, Yulan Dong, Yaoxing Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntestinal inflammation is a major global health challenge, driving the pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and imposing a significant socioeconomic burden. Dysregulated homeostasis of intestinal CD4+ T cell subsets, particularly the imbalance between pro-inflammatory T helper cells (Th1, Th2, Th17, Th9) and regulatory T cells (Tregs), is a key trigger of intestinal inflammation. These immune cells orchestrate immune responses through distinct cytokine profiles (IFN-γ/IL-4/IL-17/IL-9 vs. IL-10/TGF-β). Recent studies highlight the pivotal role of the intestinal microbiota in regulating this immune axis via three primary mechanisms: 1, Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) induce epigenetic reprogramming by inhibiting HDACs, promoting Treg differentiation and inhibit the differentiation of Th17/Th9 cells; 2, Secondary bile acids (BAs) suppress Th17 polarization through FXR/TGR5/PXR signaling; 3, Tryptophan metabolites (indole, kynurenine) balance Th17/Treg ratios via AhR-IL-22 signaling. Microbial dysbiosis disrupts this network by secreting pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), such as Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and peptidoglycan (PG), which activate pathogen pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), such as TLR4/NOD2/NF-κb signaling, driving Th1/Th17 differentiation. This review summarizes recent advances in the microbiota-CD4+ T cell interaction and discusses therapeutic strategies to modulate the intestinal microbiota, aiming to enhance understanding of IBD pathogenesis and identify potential clinical interventions.KEYWORDS: Intestinal CD4+ T cells, inflammation, intestinal microbiota, microbiota metabolites

Introduction

Intestinal inflammation has emerged as a critical global health challenge, driving the increasing prevalence of IBD, particularly Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), while imposing significant economic burdens on medical facilities. The annual global healthcare expenditure for IBD management exceeds $12 billion, with disease-associated productivity losses emphasizing the urgent need for innovative therapeutic strategies.1,2 The immunological core of this pathology involves dysregulated CD4+ T cell homeostasis. Naïve CD4+ T lymphocytes undergo lineage-specific differentiation upon antigenic stimulation, giving rise to Pro-inflammatory subsets: T helper cells (Th1/Th2/Th17/Th9).3 Immunosuppressive subsets: regulatory T (Treg) cells.4 This delicate balance is governed by cytokine networks: Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha(TNF-α)/Interleukin(IL)-6 vs. IL-10/Transforming Growth Factor-β (TGF-β).5 Consequently, the identification of effective regulatory mechanisms that can modulate the balance between these different subtypes of T cells is essential for the management of intestinal inflammation.

The intestinal microbiota, an ecosystem comprising over 100 trillion microorganisms encoding 3 million unique genes, has been identified as a crucial regulator of host immunity.6 It influences 70% of intestinal immune responses and is considered the body’s largest endocrine network. Its symbiotic relationship with mucosal immune cells is essential for intestinal homeostasis, and its dysregulation is implicated in 80% of inflammatory IBD cases and 40% of immune-mediated disorders globally.7,8 The immunomodulatory mechanisms of intestinal microbiota regulate immune cell function through three core metabolites: Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), Secondary bile acids (BAs), and Tryptophan. Consequences of microbial dysbiosis of this intricate network trigger a pathogenic cascade. Therefore, it is crucial to comprehend the mechanisms through which these metabolites function and to explore methods of modulating the composition of the intestinal microbiota, with a subsequent effect on their metabolites, thereby aiming to alleviate intestinal inflammation. This review thus focuses on the mechanisms of different subtypes of T cell differentiation and their influence on intestinal inflammation, as well as the influence of intestinal microbiota on these mechanisms. Additionally, it explores methodologies for the improvement of intestinal microbiota.

Imbalanced CD4+ T-cell subsets cause intestinal inflammation

Inflammation is a fundamental defense mechanism initiated by the innate immune system in response to pathogenic invasion. Under physiological conditions, antigen-presenting cells (APCs), particularly dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages, recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) via pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), thereby initiating predominantly via the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB)-mediated production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6).9,10 This cascade then recruits phagocytes, natural killer cells, and lymphocytes to eliminate pathogens while initiating adaptive immune responses through major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-II mediated antigen presentation, but dysregulation of this otherwise finely tuned system leads to chronic inflammation and autoimmunity. Persistent activation of the PRRs induces pro-inflammatory signaling and drives the pathological T-cell responses observed in IBD.11,12 Counterregulatory mechanisms, including IL-10-producing Treg cells pathways, normally limit excessive inflammation. CD4+ T lymphocytes are key to this immunopathological process, constituting 60–70% of lamina propria lymphocytes in the human intestinal13 (Figure 1).

Differentiation and functional subsets of intestinal CD4+ T cells.

In the intestinal tract, naïve CD4+ T cells undergo a differentiation process, resulting in the formation of distinct effector subtypes. This differentiation occurs in response to antigen presentation by antigen-presenting cells (APCs). These subtypes include Th1 cells expressing the transcription factor T-bet and Th2 cells expressing GATA-binding protein 3 (GATA3). Th1 cells predominantly secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), whereas Th2 cells produce interleukins IL-4, IL-5, IL-9, and IL-13, driving inflammatory or allergic responses when overrepresented, respectively. Additionally, CD4+ T cells possess the capacity to differentiate into three distinct types of cells: Th17 cells are distinguished by the expression of retinoid-related orphan receptor gamma t (RORγt), and regulatory T cells (Tregs) are characterized by the expression of forkhead box P3 (Foxp3). Th17 cells secrete the cytokine IL-17, which mediate intestinal inflammation when excessively activated. Conversely, Tregs secrete the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), thereby counterbalancing the pro-inflammatory effects. Furthermore, Th9 cells are characterized by the expression of PU.1, which secretes IL-9 and promotes inflammatory responses. Treg cells, however, also have an inhibitory effect on Th9 cells. A carefully regulated balance among these subsets is necessary to maintain intestinal immune homeostasis; disruptions in this balance can result in immune dysregulation and disease. Th1/Th2 cells

CD4+ T lymphocytes are originated from thymic development and persist as naïve cells in peripheral lymphoid organs until antigen encounter. Upon recognition of microbial antigens presented by dendritic cells in mesenteric lymph nodes, these cells undergo lineage-specific differentiation into effector subsets that are critical for intestinal mucosal immunity.14 Th1/Th2 cells have been observed to exhibit antigen-specific homing to inflammatory loci through CXC-chemokine receptor-3 and chemokine receptor-4 (CXCR3/CCR4)-mediated chemotaxis, where they execute recall responses upon re-exposure to cognate antigens.15 Th1 lymphocytes, distinguished by TBX21 (T-bet) expression, have been shown to orchestrate immunity through multifaceted mechanisms.16 T-bet facilitates sustained granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) and Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) production, and IFN-γ amplifies signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) signaling, inducing IL-12 expression. IFN-γ upregulates the expression of MHC-II and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase.17 GM-CSF has been shown to promote monocyte differentiation into the M1-like macrophage phenotype, which in turn induces the production of TNF-α.18,19 The process of Th2 cell differentiation is initiated by antigens derived from parasites and microbiota, thereby priming chromatin accessibility at loci associated with Th2 cells. This process is further amplified by IL-4-mediated Janus kinase 1 (JAK1)/STAT6 activation, where phosphorylated STAT6 (pSTAT6) induces GATA binding protein 3 (GATA3) subsequently orchestrates Th2 differentiation.20 Mature Th2 cells execute humoral immunity by driving B cell differentiation into IgE-secreting plasma cells, while IL-5 and C-C motif chemokine ligand 11(CCL11) signaling has been observed to recruit CC chemokine receptor 3 (CCR3) positive eosinophils.21 The coordination of these responses leads to an inflammatory response, which has been demonstrated to reduce the parasite burden in preclinical models and can also trigger allergic diseases.

The function of Th1 and Th2 cells differs significantly. Th1 cells have a tendency to promote inflammation, while Th2 cells promote allergies. The balance between these two is crucial for antiviral and antitumour effects. For instance, Calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) has the capacity to activate the Th1 cells transcription factor STAT by binding to receptor activity-modifying protein 3 (RAMP3), thereby enhancing its antiviral effect of IFN-γ. In addition, CGRP has been demonstrated to inhibit the differentiation of Th2 cells.22 The balance between these two is also related to inflammatory bowel diseases.23 The aforementioned studies have demonstrated that these two types of cells exert antagonistic effects, with diseases able to be treated by regulating their balance (Figure 1).

Th17/Treg/Th9

The immune system is responsible for maintaining homeostasis, and it achieves this through specialized CD4+ T cell subsets exhibiting counter regulatory functional dichotomy. Another such example of this is the Th17/Treg axis. Th17 cells, a specialized subset of CD4+ helper T cells, are characterized by their secretion of IL-17 and potent pro-inflammatory capabilities. Their differentiation is tightly regulated by transcription factors, in particular the retinoid-related orphan receptor gamma t (RORγt), encoded by RoRc, and RORα, both of which synergistically stabilize the Th17 cells’ transcriptional programme. Cytokine signaling critically shapes Th17 cells differentiation; in particular, IL-6 and IL-21 activate STAT3 pathways to enhance RORγt expression, thereby promoting Th17 cells lineage commitment, Furthermore, IL-23 sustains its proliferative capacity and pathogenic potential in a persistent inflammatory environment.24,25 Functionally, Th17 cells exert a protective role by recruiting neutrophils and promoting mucosal barrier defense against extracellular pathogens through the secretion of cytokines such as IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-22, and TNF-α. However, uncontrolled or dysregulated Th17 cell responses can drive chronic inflammation and autoimmune diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis, systemic lupus erythematosus, and asthma, via persistent IL-17-driven tissue injury.26,27 Treg cells, a distinct subset of CD4+ T cells, are critically defined by the master transcription factor forkhead box P3 (Foxp3), which controls both their differentiation and immunosuppressive functionality. Upon exposure to cytokines such as TGF-β or certain microbiota-derived metabolites in the intestinal lamina propria, naive CD4+ T cells upregulate Foxp3 expression and differentiate into functional Treg cells.28,29 These cells exert their immunoregulatory effects primarily through the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines, including TGF-β and IL-10, effectively suppressing excessive inflammatory responses driven by Th17 cells. As a result, Treg cells are central to immune homeostasis and play a pivotal role in the regulation of autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus, multiple sclerosis, and inflammatory bowel disease. Beyond immune modulation, Treg cells facilitate tissue repair, attenuate inflammation-associated organ damage in obesity, and promote recovery from neuroinflammatory conditions. Deficiencies in the abundance or function of Treg cells, resulting from either genetic mutations in Foxp3 or adverse environmental factors, are sufficient to precipitate severe autoimmune pathology. This observation underscores the indispensable role of Treg cells in the maintenance of immune homeostasis and tissue integrity.30,31 The balance between Th17 and Treg cells exhibits remarkable plasticity, allowing dynamic shifts between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory phenotypes in response to environmental stimuli. This cellular plasticity has been implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of numerous diseases, including intestinal inflammation, organ transplant rejection, and cancer. Due to their opposing roles in immune modulation, precise modulation of the Th17/Treg cells balance represents a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of inflammatory diseases. For instance, using recombinant humanized type I collagen (rhCOLI) can improve the function of Treg cells, balance the ratio of Th17/Treg cells, enhance maternal reproductive function, and decrease the likelihood of miscarriage.32 In addition, drugs that inhibit IL-17A secretion by Th17 cells in the colon and promote IL-10 secretion by Treg cells via the gut-liver axis have the potential to improve liver metabolic diseases triggered by excessive alcohol consumption.33 In particular, resolving the delicate balance between Th17-driven inflammation and Treg-mediated immunosuppression is critical for effectively alleviating intestinal inflammation and restoring mucosal immune homeostasis34 (Figure 1).

Th9 cells constitute a discrete lineage of helper T cells derived from naive CD4+ T cells precursors, primarily distinguished by their capacity to produce elevated levels of IL-9. The differentiation of Th9 cells is a meticulously regulated process contingent on a distinct cytokine milieu and the orchestration of pivotal lineage-defining transcription factors. The differentiation cascade is initiated by the synergistic action of TGF-β and IL-4. Mechanistically, these cytokines activate their respective downstream signaling pathways, namely the SMAD and STAT6 pathways. The convergence of these signals results in the synergistic upregulation of two crucial transcription factors: PU.1 and Interferon Regulatory Factor 4 (IRF4). PU.1 has been identified as the primary regulator of the Th9 lineage, as evidenced by its direct binding to the promoter region of the Il-9 gene, thereby inducing robust transcription. Concurrently, IRF4 cooperates with PU.1, ensuring robust and stable commitment to the Th9 cell fate. This intricate molecular network culminates in the generation of mature Th9 cells capable of copious IL-9 secretion.35 The developmental trajectory of Th9 cells is intricately linked with that of Treg cells, as both subsets can arise from naive CD4+ T cells under the influence of TGF-β.36 This developmental plasticity is governed by the cytokine environment. It has been demonstrated that TGF-β plays a pivotal role in the differentiation of induced Treg cells. In addition, the concomitant presence of IL-4 has been shown to impede Treg cells development and redirect the differentiation program toward the Th9 phenotype. This finding underscores a critical checkpoint in CD4+ T cell fate determination. In addition, a direct antagonistic relationship has been identified at the molecular level. FOXP3, the master transcription factor of Treg cells, has been demonstrated to bind to the Il-9 gene locus, thereby actively repressing IL-9 production.37,38 This provides a clear molecular basis for the mutual regulation between the Treg and Th9 cells lineages. Functionally, Th9 cells are potent mediators of inflammation. The primary effector function of these cells is driven by IL-9, which promotes the recruitment, survival, and activation of key innate immune cells, particularly mast cells and eosinophils. The pathogenic role of this axis has been thoroughly documented in the context of inflammatory conditions. For instance, in preclinical models of IBD, such as UC, the inhibition of IL-9 signaling has been shown to effectively ameliorate disease severity,39,40 underscoring the therapeutic potential of targeting the Th9/IL-9 axis in certain inflammatory disorders (Figure 1).

Intestinal microbiota has a profound effect on intestinal CD4+ T cells differentiation

The intestinal microbiota exerts a profound influence on the structure and functionality of the intestinal barrier, fostering a symbiotic relationship that is mutually beneficial to both the host and the microbiota. The host provides nutrients and a conducive environment for microbial colonization, while the microbiota-derived components and metabolites play a crucial role in regulating and modulating intestinal immunity. Specifically, intestinal microbiota activate the host immune system through interactions between microbial molecules and PRRs expressed on intestinal immune cells, Such processes include the differentiation of CD4+ T cells.41–43 Notably, these immune signals and microbial metabolites are not confined to the intestine but can traverse the intestinal barrier, influencing systemic immune responses across multiple organs via the circulation. Studies utilizing germ-free mouse models have demonstrated significant immune deficiencies, including reductions in neutrophil numbers, impaired bone marrow progenitor cell populations, defective innate lymphoid cell (ILC) function, decreased microglia differentiation and consequent immunoglobulin A (IgA) deficiency, In addition to the presence of abnormal immune checkpoints, as has been demonstrated, this results in a reduction in the ability to resist cancer, ultimately rendering these mice highly susceptible to pathogenic infections.44–46 Furthermore, recent studies have indicated a correlation between the intestinal microbiota and the host genome, along with an association with rheumatoid arthritis and ulcerative colitis in host subjects. and genetic linkage has been demonstrated, suggesting a potential underlying genetic basis for these correlations.47,48 Thereby emphasizing the importance of intestinal microbiota. Collectively, these findings underscore the critical and reciprocal relationship between intestinal microbiota and host immune function, highlighting the microbiota as a key modulator of intestinal and systemic immunity.

The homeostasis of the intestinal microbiota is critically important for the development, maturation, and regulation of host intestinal immunity. Under physiological conditions, the microbiota maintains a balanced inflammatory state that is advantageous to the host. This controlled inflammation is an example of a healthy immune response in the intestines, thereby facilitating rapid clearance of potential pathogens.49 The recognition of microbes and subsequent activation of the immune system are primarily mediated through microbial-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs), including lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from Gram-negative bacteria and peptidoglycan (PG) from Gram-positive bacteria. These components are specifically detected by host PRRs, such as TLRs and nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain (NOD) like receptors. Engagement of these PRRs initiates intracellular signaling cascades, NF-κB and other pathways, resulting in the activation and differentiation of innate immune cells, including macrophages, DC cells, and neutrophils,50–52 Specifically, IL-22 promotes the production of antimicrobial peptides, notably regenerating islet-derived protein III gamma (RegIIIγ) and α-defensins from Paneth cells, significantly enhances intestinal mucosal immune function, thereby helping to eliminate pathogenic bacteria.53 Concurrently, the activation of intracellular receptors such as NOD1 and NOD2 by PG fragments further contributes to the maintenance of intestinal immune homeostasis by facilitating immune surveillance and pathogen clearance.54 In addition to innate immunity, the intestinal microbiota exerts a profound influence on adaptive immune responses. It has been demonstrated that LPS and PG can directly promote the differentiation and proliferation of Treg cells and Th cells, resulting in increased secretion of adaptive immune key cytokines, including sIgAs, IgM, and some IgG. Collectively, these cytokines orchestrate balanced adaptive immune responses.55,56 Recent research has highlighted NOD2 ligand purified from Lactiplantibacillus plantarum enhances peripheral tissue sensitivity to growth hormone (GH), subsequently increasing circulating insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) levels and improving growth outcomes in chronically malnourished animal models, thus providing clear evidence of microbiota-host interactions extending beyond immune modulation.57 Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that exogenous administration of segmented filamentous bacteria (SFB) can enhance complement generation and phagocytosis by APC cells in mice, thereby inhibiting influenza virus infection58 (Figure 2).

Regulatory effects of intestinal microbiota on immune cell function.

Under homeostatic conditions, intestinal microbiota-derived lipopolysaccharide (LPS) has been demonstrated to induce a moderate activation of Toll-like receptors (TLRs). These receptors subsequently signal through the adaptor molecule myeloid differentiation factor 88 (MyD88), thereby triggering the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) pathway. In a similar manner, bacterial peptidoglycan (PG) activates nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain (NOD)-like receptors, which in turn activate transforming growth factor-β activated kinase-1 (TAK1) and the NF-κB pathway. These signaling cascades induce controlled secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, promoting immune development and pathogen resistance. Conversely, microbiota dysbiosis has been demonstrated to result in excessive production of LPS and PG, leading to hyperactivation of TLR/NOD receptors and sustained NF-κB signaling. This heightened inflammatory environment has been demonstrated to promote the differentiation of CD4+ T cells toward pro-inflammatory Th17, Th9, and Th1 phenotypes. However, when the intestinal microbiota is disrupted, the imbalance in microbial composition leads to persistent stimulation of PRRs, resulting in the overactivation of the innate immune system. This sustained activation drives an imbalance in immune cell populations, with excessive proliferation of inflammation-promoting immune cells, which in turn induces severe chronic inflammatory responses. This imbalance is a key driver of the pathogenesis of diverse inflammatory diseases.59 Citrobacter rodentium has been observed to increase the enrichment of pro-inflammatory pathogens in the colon. This creates an environment that is prone to inflammation and exacerbates the progression of colitis.60 As demonstrated in the relevant literature, there is a growing body of evidence to suggest that certain types of microbiota, including Escherichia-Shigella, Helicobacter, and Staphylococcus, have the capacity to promote the differentiation of macrophages toward the M1 type. This, in turn, has been shown to exacerbate UC. Akkermansia, Lactobacillus, Clostridia_UCG-014, and Bifidobacterium have been demonstrated to attenuate this phenomenon.61 Research has identified a number of microbiota that are responsible for the production of LPS, including E. faecalis, E. coli, B. fragilis, S. bovis, F. nucleatum, and H. pylori. These microbiotas have the capacity to produce harmful substances, including endotoxins, and have been demonstrated to play a pathogenic role when combined with the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway. Consequently, this results in an increased risk of Th cell differentiation toward an inflammatory phenotype, as well as the development of inflammatory bowel disease and cancer.62 Furthermore, Porphyromonas gingivalis has been demonstrated to exacerbate the progression of colitis by increasing the abundance of Bacteroides, decreasing the abundance of Firmicutes, Verrucomicrobia, and Actinobacteria, promoting the differentiation of Th17 cells, and inhibiting the abundance of Treg cells.63 As demonstrated, the suppression of the IL-6/STAT3 pathway is an efficacious method of preventing the differentiation of Th17 cells specific to intestinal microbiota. This provides a potential therapeutic approach for the management of CD.64 It has been demonstrated that Streptococcus suis is capable of inducing intestinal tissue damage and inflammation subsequent to a lung infection. This phenomenon is driven by the induction of intestinal dysbiosis, which precipitates an imbalance in the Th1/Th2 axis, ultimately exacerbating infection-associated lung pathology.65 Similarly, the reduced abundance of Bacteroides spp. and the increased abundance of Lactobacillus spp. contribute to the increase in Treg cells and the reduction in Th9 cells, thereby alleviating inflammatory asthma.66 In summary, these findings suggest that a stable intestinal microbiota may improve the immune microenvironment by balancing CD4+ T cells subpopulations, thereby helping to alleviate inflammatory diseases. Conversely, the inflammatory cytokine storm engendered by intestinal microbiota dysbiosis has been demonstrated to promote the differentiation of CD4+ T cells toward Th9/Th1/Th2/Th17, thereby exacerbating the progression of inflammatory diseases (Figure 2).

Modulation of CD4+ T cells differentiation by microbial metabolites

In addition to direct interaction with immune receptors via their structural components, the intestinal microbiota, including genera such as Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Akkermansia muciniphila, also exert immunomodulatory effects through the fermentation of undigested dietary components, particularly complex carbohydrates such as glucose. This metabolic activity produces several bioactive metabolites, in particular SCFAs, BAs, and tryptophan-derived compounds. These metabolites act as important signaling molecules by binding to specific receptors on local immune and epithelial cells within the intestinal microenvironment. In addition, they have the ability to cross the intestinal epithelial barrier, subsequently entering the systemic circulation and exerting regulatory influences on immune responses in distal organs. The following sections review the mechanisms and functional roles of these three major classes of microbiota-derived metabolites.

SCFA

Dietary fibers and other indigestible carbohydrates that are not absorbed in the upper gastrointestinal tract are fermented by the intestinal microbiota, resulting in the production of SCFAs as important metabolic by-products. These SCFAs, primarily acetate, propionate, and butyrate, play an essential role in maintaining intestinal health and host metabolism. The fermentation process of dietary fibers by specific microbiota groups, notably anaerobic microbiota such as Clostridia species, Akkermansia muciniphila, Bifidobacterium spp, Coprococcus catus are crucial for the production of these metabolites (Table 1).67,68 A diet abundant in fiber has been shown to significantly enhance the production of SCFAs, which are vital for numerous physiological functions. Notably, the intestinal microbiota has been observed to metabolize these indigestible cellulose polysaccharides, producing SCFAs. These, in turn, influence immune cells, thereby affecting intestinal immune function and structural integrity. This microbial process is a fundamental way in which the intestinal microbiota contributes to the host’s overall health.69

SCFAs have been demonstrated to exert a variety of effects through binding to specific G-protein-coupled receptors on host cells. The most thoroughly characterized SCFA-sensing receptors are GPR43 (also known as FFAR2), GPR41 (FFAR3), and GPR109A (HCA2).70 These receptors exhibit distinct ligand specificity, with GPR43 being most potently activated by acetate and propionate,71 whereas GPR109A is most strongly activated by longer SCFAs such as butyrate.72 GPR41 and GPR43 are widely expressed on intestinal epithelial cells and various immune cells, allowing SCFAs to modulate diverse cell types. Upon binding of SCFAs, GPR43/41 typically couples to Gi/Go and/or Gq proteins, leading to a reduction in intracellular cAMP levels and the release of calcium. This, in turn, activates downstream signal cascades. SCFAs receptors mediate the effects of microbial metabolites on host immune cells, including T cell subsets, by engaging these specific signaling pathways.73

Butyrate is distinguished by its multifaceted role, beginning with its function as the primary energy substrate for colonocytes, supplying approximately 60–70% of their metabolic requirements.74 Beyond this vital energetic role, butyrate is integral to maintaining intestinal barrier integrity. As demonstrated in the extant literature, butyrate has been shown to enhance barrier function by promoting the assembly of tight junction proteins and stimulating the expression of MUC2, the principal gel-forming mucin.75–77 Consequently, butyrate has been shown to fortify the protective mucus layer against pathogenic invasion. Furthermore, butyrate has been demonstrated to be a potent immunomodulatory agent. Its primary anti-inflammatory mechanism involves the inhibition of histone deacetylases (HDACs). As a potent HDACs inhibitor, butyrate prevents the deacetylation of histones, thereby suppressing the NF-κB signaling pathway and consequently inhibiting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, even in the presence of potent inflammatory stimuli like LPS. Concurrently, butyrate facilitates the differentiation and function of both colonic and peripheral Treg cells, which are crucial for suppressing aberrant immune responses.78–80 In addition, butyrate have been shown to modulate pro-inflammatory CD4+ T helper subsets by limiting Th17 cells differentiation through inhibition of HDACs and IL-17 production.81,82 Butyrate has also been reported to inhibit Th9 cells proliferation by promoting Treg cells proliferation.66 Propionate contributes to intestinal homeostasis and host defense through distinct mechanisms. At the local level, the propionate under investigation has been demonstrated to induce contraction of the intestinal smooth muscle and to augment mucus secretion. This collective action serves to fortify the body’s defenses against pathogen colonization and subsequent inflammatory insults.83 On a systemic level, propionate modulates immunity primarily through promote the differentiation of Treg cells while attenuating the activity of pro-inflammatory helper T cells.84 Consequently, this results in a decrease in systemic inflammatory responses and contributes to overall immune tolerance. Acetate, the most prevalent SCFA in the colon, plays a role in both host metabolism and immunomodulation. Acetate has been observed to enter the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle in various peripheral tissues following its absorption into the circulation.85 This signaling cascade exerts a regulatory effect on the expression of key pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and TNF-α, thereby preventing excessive inflammatory responses to endotoxins such as LPS.86 Furthermore, SCFAs enhances mucosal defense by promoting intestinal barrier repair, reducing bacterial translocation, and regulating the binding of secretory IgA (sIgA) to the microbiota.87 This multifaceted process plays a pivotal role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis. Recent studies have demonstrated that SCFAs metabolized by Acidaminococcaceae impede Th9 cells differentiation and enhance the progression of UC.88 Therefore, it can be hypothesized that SCFAs metabolized by probiotics play a pivotal role in maintaining immune health by influencing CD4+ T cell balance, reducing intestinal inflammation, and preventing excessive microbial translocation and unnecessary immune activation.

Despite their apparent benefits, SCFAs have been observed to exert detrimental, context-dependent effects on host defense mechanisms. These substances have the capacity to compromise immune system function by impeding the migration of innate immune cells and by suppressing protective Th17 cells, which may, in turn, lead to an elevated risk of infection.89 Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that SCFAs exhibit a biphasic, concentration-dependent effect on the endothelial barrier, enhancing it at low concentrations but disrupting it at high levels. This disruption can lead to an increase in vascular permeability and tissue damage.90 These dual actions underscore the necessity of meticulous consideration of dose and the specific immunological context for the therapeutic use of SCFAs (Figure 3).

Signaling pathways mediating the effects of intestinal microbiota derived metabolites on immune cells.

The influence of intestinal microbiota on immune homeostasis in its host is primarily exerted through the production of metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), bile acids (BAs), and tryptophan derivatives. SCFAs, serving as the primary energy substrate for intestinal epithelial cells, have been demonstrated to promote epithelial proliferation and the expression of tight junction proteins, thereby enhancing barrier integrity. In addition, studies have demonstrated that SCFAs bind to G protein-coupled receptors (GPRs) and inhibit HDACs activity. This, in turn, has been demonstrated to promote regulatory T cell (Treg) differentiation and immune regulation. The primary bile acids that enter the intestine are subsequently metabolized by bacteria into secondary bile acids. These secondary bile acids subsequently modulate immunity via receptors such as the farnesoid X receptor (FXR), Takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5), and pregnane X receptor (PXR). The activation of these receptors has been demonstrated to suppress the NF-κB pathway, thereby inhibiting pro-inflammatory Th17 cell responses and promoting Treg expansion, thus maintaining immune equilibrium. Tryptophan is metabolized via three primary pathways: kynurenine by indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO1), serotonin by tryptophan hydroxylase 1 (TPH1), and indole derivatives by intestinal microbiota. These pathways have been shown to activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signaling pathway, inducing downstream cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYP1A1, CYP1A2) and IL-22 secretion to limit inflammation and preserve intestinal immune balance. BA

BAs are cholesterol-derived molecules that are produced in the liver. In hepatocytes, cholesterol is converted into primary BAs such as cholic acid (CA) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA) via cytochrome P450 (CYP)-mediated oxidation. Subsequently, the liver secretes BAs into bile, which is then stored in the gallbladder. The ingestion of food leads to gallbladder contraction and bile release into the duodenum. In the small intestine, BAs emulsify dietary fats to facilitate absorption, and the majority are reabsorbed in the ileum and transported back to the liver through the enterohepatic circulation. BAs that have not been reabsorbed and reach the distal ileum and colon undergo metabolic transformation by intestinal microorganisms, including the genera Bacteroides, Eubacterium, and Clostridium (Table 1). These microorganisms secrete the bile salt hydrolase BSH, which causes the primary BAs to dissociate into secondary BAs, including deoxycholic acid (DCA), lithocholic acid (LCA), and ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA).91 Beyond their well-established function in lipid digestion, BAs serve as signaling molecules that regulate a variety of metabolic processes, including glucose homeostasis and energy expenditure.92,93 Recent studies have also demonstrated that intestinal microbiota-driven BAs transformations can modulate intestinal immune responses and help alleviate inflammation.94 Consequently, BAs have been identified as pivotal mediators at the nexus of metabolism and immunity.

It has been demonstrated that BAs exert a multitude of effects through the activation of specific host receptors. The major BAs-sensing receptors comprise the nuclear farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and pregnane X receptor (PXR), the G protein-coupled BAs receptor (TGR5), and the vitamin D receptor (VDR).95 The activation of these pathways has been demonstrated to influence immune cell functions. For instance, BA-induced FXR activation in macrophages has been shown to inhibit the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby reducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-2, TNF-α, and IFN-γ.96 In a mouse model, suppression of intestinal microbiota has been shown to exacerbate intestinal inflammation. Furthermore, PXR expression has been observed to be associated with Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis in human subjects.97 Recent findings have validated the capacity of the secondary BA 3β-hydroxydeoxycholic acid (isoDCA) to curtail the immunostimulatory function of DC cells. This effect is achieved through the stimulation of the FXR receptor within DC cells, consequently amplifying Foxp3 induction and regulatory T cell transcription.98 In addition, the administration of DCA and LCA has been demonstrated to decrease the population of Th17 cells within the intestinal tract, thereby ameliorating inflammatory diseases.91 Taurocholic acid (THDCA) has demonstrated the capacity to reduce Th1 cell-associated transcription factors and cytokines, including T-bet, IFN-γ, and TNF-α. This reduction leads to a balanced state between Th1 and Th2, resulting in the alleviation of intestinal inflammation.99 In summary, these findings emphasize the critical function of BAs, which are metabolized by the intestinal microbiota into secondary BAs, in maintaining intestinal immune homeostasis. Secondary BAs have been demonstrated to modulate the recruitment, differentiation, and functional activities of intestinal CD4+ T cells, thereby contributing significantly to the balance between pro-inflammatory and regulatory responses. This intricate interplay underscores the importance of BAs in regulating intestinal immune-mediated diseases and offers potential therapeutic targets for intervention (Figure 3).

Tryptophan

Tryptophan is an essential aromatic amino acid that must be obtained exclusively through dietary intake. Beyond its primary role in protein biosynthesis, tryptophan serves as a key metabolic precursor for numerous biologically active molecules produced by both host cells and intestinal microbiota. Accumulating evidence underscores the profound impact of tryptophan on diverse physiological processes, including neuronal signaling, immune regulation, oxidative stress responses, and intestinal barrier integrity. Extensive research has been conducted on the intricate relationship between tryptophan metabolism and the intestinal microbiota. The mounting evidence indicates that these two factors establish a reciprocal metabolic-immune regulatory network that is essential for mucosal homeostasis and immunological tolerance.100–102 Consequently, disruptions in tryptophan metabolism are frequently associated with intestinal inflammatory diseases and immune dysregulation.

Tryptophan metabolism in the gastrointestinal tract occurs primarily through three major pathways: the indole pathway, the serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT) synthesis pathway, and the kynurenine (Kyn) pathway. It has been documented that approximately 90% of dietary tryptophan is metabolized via the kynurenine pathway by indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO1/IDO2) or tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase (TDO), resulting in metabolites such as kynurenine, kynurenic acid, and quinolinic acid.103 A smaller fraction (~5%) is metabolized by the intestinal microbiota to indole and related derivatives, including indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and indole-3-propionic acid (IPA), which have distinct immunomodulatory properties.104 Finally, the serotonin pathway accounts for a minor proportion of tryptophan metabolism, predominantly catalyzed by tryptophan hydroxylase 1 (TPH1) in intestinal enterochromaffin cells (ECs) to yield serotonin.105 The influence of the intestinal microbiota on host tryptophan metabolism and the shaping of local immune responses through direct enzymatic activity is profound. For instance, certain microbiota species, such as Clostridium sporogenes have been observed to efficiently convert dietary tryptophan into indole derivatives.106 These indole derivatives have been demonstrated to exert a substantial influence on mucosal immunity via aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) activation. Probiotic Lactobacillus species metabolize excess dietary tryptophan to indole-3-lactic acid (ILA), exerting beneficial anti-inflammatory effects.107 The significance of the microbiota in regulating tryptophan availability and metabolic pathways has been highlighted in germ-free animal models, underscoring its pivotal role in modulating host tryptophan metabolism. This dysregulation of serotonin and kynurenine metabolism in the absence of microbial colonization has been demonstrated.108

The kynurenine pathway is the predominant route of tryptophan catabolism, and it is critically regulated by the enzymes IDO1/IDO2 and TDO. Recent studies have demonstrated that Clostridium innocuum and Dubosiella newyorkensis can induce immunotolerance in DC cells and promote the proliferation of Treg cells. This process involves the activation of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO1) and the enhancement of tryptophan catabolism to Kyn via the AhR-dependent pathway. This process has been shown to alleviate intestinal inflammation.109 IDO1 has also been demonstrated to facilitate the binding of STAT1 to the promoter and to modulate the expression of IL-6, thereby impeding the differentiation of CD4+ T cells into the Th17 phenotype.110 However, it is important to note that chronic upregulation of the kynurenine pathway has been associated with pathological outcomes. Elevated kynurenine levels have been observed to correlate with persistent immune suppression and, paradoxically, increased inflammation in chronic intestinal diseases, thereby underscoring the complexity of its immunoregulatory function.111,112

Indole derivatives generated by microbial enzymes act primarily as potent ligands for the AhR, a key transcription factor expressed by various immune cell subsets, including intestinal CD4+ T cells, innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), and macrophages. In its quiescent state, the AhR is localized within the cytoplasm. Upon stimulation by specific ligands, AhR translocates to the nucleus and heterodimerizes with the aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator (ARNT). The AhR-ARNT complex is activated, subsequently binding to xenobiotic response elements (XREs) in the promoter regions of target genes, thereby initiating the transcription of CYP1A1 and CYP1A2.113 The activation of AhR is contingent upon a diverse spectrum of ligands, including exogenous environmental pollutants and, more critically for immune homeostasis, endogenous molecules. It is imperative to acknowledge the pivotal role of the intestinal microbiota in modulating immune responses at mucosal interfaces. The microbiota serves as a primary source of potent endogenous AhR ligands, producing a rich array of tryptophan-derived metabolites. This microbiota-driven AhR activation is a crucial mechanism that regulates immune responses in these specific anatomical environments.114 In dendritic cells, for instance, AhR signaling, activated by ligands, results in the repression of MHC class II and co-stimulatory molecules (CD80/CD86). It is imperative to note that this interaction directly orchestrates a tolerogenic environment by promoting the expression of anti-inflammatory and tissue-protective cytokines, most notably IL-22 and IL-10.115 The induction of the AhR-IL-22 axis facilitates the differentiation of Tregs while suppressing the proliferation of pro-inflammatory Th17 cells.116 Collectively, this cascade of events is instrumental in maintaining intestinal immune homeostasis and culminates in the amelioration of inflammation, such as in colitis. Indole metabolism is associated with bifidobacteria and Lactobacillus.117,118 Upon activation, AhR signaling drives the secretion of IL-22, which enhances intestinal barrier integrity, epithelial regeneration, and mucus secretion. Consistently, the lack of microbiota-derived indole metabolites observed in germ-free mice correlates with reduced AhR signaling, decreased IL-22 production, and increased susceptibility to intestinal inflammation.119,120 IPA has demonstrated efficacy in regulating the Th1/Th17 cells balance, reducing inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-17A, and ameliorating colitis in murine models.121

Serotonin (5-HT), predominantly synthesized in enterochromaffin cells of the intestine, is another critical mediator regulating intestinal immune homeostasis. Serotonin exerts its effects via multiple 5-HT receptors expressed by innate and adaptive immune cells. These receptors influence intestinal motility, permeability, and inflammatory responses. Recent studies have revealed that intestinal resident bacteria, including certain strains of Escherichia coli, Lactobacillus, and Enterococcus, can directly produce or stimulate host synthesis of serotonin (Table 1).122,123 This, in turn, enhances Treg cell populations and promotes immune tolerance.124 However, the immunomodulatory effects of serotonin are dose and context-dependent. Dysregulated serotonin signaling has been associated with exacerbated intestinal inflammation in conditions such as IBD, indicating a complex role for serotonin in intestinal immunity125 (Figure 3).

A growing body of evidence underscores the intricate metabolic interplay among dietary tryptophan, intestinal microbiota, and host immunity. Dysregulation of these metabolic pathways – indole and AhR, serotonin, and kynurenine can lead to severe intestinal inflammation and impaired immune tolerance. Consequently, therapeutic strategies that target the manipulation of intestinal microbiota composition, the intake of dietary tryptophan, or the targeting of specific metabolic pathways have emerged as promising approaches for restoring intestinal immune balance and treating inflammatory disorders.

Modulation of host immunity by reconstructing intestinal microbiota

As previous studies have shown, the intestinal microbiota can influence immune cells by producing various metabolites, thereby regulating the immune balance and helping to alleviate various types of inflammatory diseases. This underscores the significance of regulating the intestinal microbiota within the body. The microbiota and the host coexist and influence each other in a dynamic relationship. Presently, there is an exploration of several approaches to intestinal microbiota reconstruction, including the direct alteration of its composition through methods such as probiotic supplementation and fecal microbiota transplantation. Alternatively, the intestinal environment can be modified to support probiotic colonization, which can be achieved through dietary changes and the use of prebiotics or probiotics.

Directly reconstructing the intestinal microbiota

Direct interventions aimed at modifying the intestinal microbiota have garnered significant attention as potential therapeutic strategies for a wide range of gastrointestinal and immune-related disorders. Among these, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has emerged as one of the relatively mature methods for directly altering the microbiota composition. FMT is a procedure in which a fecal suspension from a healthy donor is transferred to a recipient. The administration of this transfer can be accomplished either by oral intake or enema. The objective of this process is to restore a balanced, diverse microbiota in the recipient, which may contribute to the resolution of dysbiosis and the alleviation of disease symptoms.126

FMT has been demonstrated to markedly reduce intestinal inflammation and promote immune homeostasis by enhancing the intestinal microbiota.127 A recent study has demonstrated the efficacy of FMT in promoting a healthy composition of the intestinal microbiota. This intervention has been demonstrated to augment the proportion of CD4+ Foxp3+ Treg cells, thereby engendering a more robust immune regulatory environment. Furthermore, FMT has been observed to reduce the recurrence rate of Clostridium difficile infection, underscoring its potential to mitigate the severity of gastrointestinal disorders.128 Furthermore, research findings have indicated that FMT can enhance the metabolism of BAs within the gastrointestinal tract.129 In accordance with the findings of the previously mentioned microbiota studies, FMT has been demonstrated to enhance intestinal barrier function and immune system function by regulating short-chain fatty acid, BAs, and tryptophan metabolism, thereby alleviating intestinal inflammation.130–132 FMTs’ clinical efficacy now robustly established for the management of recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection (rCDI). Furthermore, the study’s findings have indicated the promising potential of this approach to induce remission in patients diagnosed with UC, underscoring its broader therapeutic applicability.133,134 The efficacy of FMT is significantly influenced by recipient-specific factors and pre-procedural conditioning. For instance, pretreatment of the recipient with antibiotics is a critical strategy to deplete the endogenous microbiota. This results in the formation of a receptive ecological niche, thereby facilitating superior engraftment of donor-derived beneficial taxa, such as Bifidobacterium, and thus enhancing therapeutic outcomes.135 Consequently, the selection of recipients is of the utmost importance. Patients with compromised intestinal barrier integrity may experience an elevated risk of adverse events,136 while the intervention of early intestinal microbiota may also influence the potential to influence therapeutic efficacy.137 This finding indicates that the baseline microbial composition of the recipient significantly influences the efficacy of the procedure. The transplantation of disparate microbial communities from donors can elicit divergent effects on recipients. Research has demonstrated that the feces of mice treated with vancomycin can induce a Th1/Th17 polarization in recipient CD4+ T cells, while metronidazole has been shown to promote IL-10 secretion in murine models. This finding indicates that the processing and preparation of donated fecal matter is of critical importance.138 Rigorous screening of donors and advanced sample processing are critical components of a safe FMT protocol. In order to mitigate the risk of pathogen transmission, donor selection involves not only comprehensive clinical evaluation but also high-throughput sequencing modalities. Metagenomic and 16S rRNA gene sequencing are routinely employed to profile the donor’s fecal microbiome, enabling the exclusion of potential pathogens and characterization of microbial diversity. Furthermore, advancements in bioprocessing are transitioning the field from rudimentary fecal material formulations to more standardized and safer alternatives. These include lyophilized preparations (“freeze-dried powder”) and refined microbial consortia, such as encapsulated formulations or extracellular vesicle-based products, which aim to enhance safety, stability, and dose standardization.139 In addition to FMT, recent advancements in bacterial engineering technologies have enabled the development of bacteria-based particles that can be targeted to specific anatomical locations for the prevention and treatment of various diseases, including IBD.140 This encompasses the modification of Escherichia coli through synthetic biology methodologies, thereby facilitating its capacity to target tumor cells and augment T cell immune function by enhancing arginine metabolism.141 The transplantation of extracellular vesicles (EVs) extracted from microbial communities holds promise as a future therapeutic approach for intestinal inflammation. EVs preserve microbial function, reduce irritability.142 Studies have shown that this method is more effective than FMT for treating colitis in mice.143 However, there is currently a lack of human clinical studies. These studies underscore the potential of microbiota transplantation not only to restore microbial diversity but also to modulate immune responses, making it a promising tool for combating chronic intestinal inflammation and other intestine-related disorders (Figure 4).

Strategies for modulating the intestinal microbiota.

The modulation of intestinal microbiota can be approached in two broad classifications: direct and indirect methods. Direct interventions include fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), a process in which fecal suspensions from healthy donors are introduced into the recipient’s gut via oral administration or enema to restore microbial balance. Alternatively, targeted probiotic supplementation has been demonstrated to directly enhance beneficial microbial populations. Indirect approaches entail the modification of the intestinal microenvironment, thereby favoring the colonization and proliferation of beneficial bacteria. Such strategies include dietary modifications and stress reduction, such as the reduction of high-fat and high-sugar foods, and the incorporation of prebiotic-rich nutrients and dietary fibers, including fish, dairy products, and whole grains. reduce stress through exercise and sleep. Indirectly reconstructing the intestinal microbiota

While direct interventions, such as FMT, can have immediate impacts on intestinal microbiota composition, indirect approaches also play a critical role in shaping the microbial environment. The composition of microbiota and its functional properties are influenced by several factors, including diet, host genetics, and the intestinal physiological conditions.144–146 The colonization of the intestinal microbiota is contingent upon the presence of a conducive environment within the gastrointestinal tract, as distinct regions of the intestines exhibit a predilection for particular microbiota taxa. For instance, the small intestine, with its relatively high pH and active digestive enzymes, is more conducive to the growth of facultative anaerobes. Conversely, the large intestine, characterized by a lower pH and diminished enzyme activity, fosters the proliferation of strict anaerobes and a heightened abundance of microbiota that thrive on fermentation.147 This underscores the significance of modifying the intestinal environment to promote the proliferation of beneficial microbiota.

It has been established that diet constitutes a primary factor in the indirect modulation of the intestinal microbiota.148 A diet abundant in dietary fiber, for instance, has been associated with the production of SCFAs, which have been demonstrated to facilitate immune responses and maintain intestinal barrier function. A substantial body of research has demonstrated that the ingestion of a diet abundant in fiber can serve as a preventative measure against the development of chronic diseases, including obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases.149–151 In experimental models utilizing animals, the ingestion of a diet with a high fiber content has been demonstrated to augment the production of SCFAs within the intestine. This, in turn, has been demonstrated to promote the function of Treg cells, thereby alleviating symptoms of inflammatory diseases.152 Similarly, foods abundant in prebiotics (pectin, guar gum, xylan, and fructans) have been demonstrated to encourage the growth of beneficial microbiota that generate SCFAs. These SCFAs have been demonstrated to play a pivotal role in reducing intestinal inflammation and preserving intestinal health.153–155

In addition, dietary habits that promote the metabolism of tryptophan by the intestinal microbiota, such as diets rich in fish, poultry, and dairy, have been shown to activate the AhR signaling pathway, thereby improving immune homeostasis in the intestinal.156 Moreover, diets high in fats and sugars, common in individuals with obesity, have been linked to reduced SCFAs and BAs metabolism, decreased beneficial microbiota abundance, and increased intestinal permeability, which can lead to inflammation.157 Conversely, the consumption of unhealthy Western diets, characterized by elevated levels of fat and sugar, has been demonstrated to disrupt the composition of the intestinal microbiota, thereby exacerbating inflammatory responses. For instance, obese individuals exhibit an increased abundance of Enterobacteriaceae in their intestinal microbiota, resulting in a disruption of intestinal microbiota equilibrium and the subsequent activation of the TLR inflammatory pathway, thereby leading to exacerbated intestinal inflammation.158 Furthermore, there is a possibility that this obese intestinal microbiota may be hereditary.159 Functional food, also represents a cornerstone of this strategy. A growing body of research has demonstrated the selective promotion of beneficial genera, such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, by plant-derived polysaccharides functioning as prebiotic substrates. This selective promotion has been shown to stimulate the production of health-promoting SCFAs.160 Certain dietary combinations of prebiotics can increase the abundance of Clostridia and Bacteroides in the intestine. These bacteria are significantly reduced in patients with IBD.161 A significant technological challenge, however, is ensuring the stability and targeted delivery of these and other sensitive bioactive compounds (essential oils, unsaturated fatty acids). To address this challenge, advanced encapsulation and delivery systems are employed, incorporating nanotechnology-based formulations, lyophilization (freeze-drying), and spray drying. The primary function of these systems is to protect the molecules from gastrointestinal degradation and to enable their release within the intestinal lumen.162 Consequently, dietary modifications have been demonstrated to exert a pivotal influence on the composition of intestinal microbiota and immune function, with the potential to offer benefits in the management of intestine-related diseases.

In addition, the gut-brain axis serves as a pivotal pathway for microbial modulation through lifestyle factors. A substantial body of research has demonstrated that chronic psychological stress is a contributing factor to the development of intestinal dysbiosis.163 Conversely, lifestyle modifications, including regular physical exercise and sufficient sleep, have been demonstrated to counteract these detrimental effects and restore microbial homeostasis.164 Evidence supports this association; for instance, studies in murine models deprived of sleep have shown that melatonin supplementation not only elevates intestinal SCFA concentrations but also ameliorates systemic health parameters.165 Collectively, these dietary and lifestyle-based approaches represent powerful, non-pharmacological strategies for indirectly shaping the intestinal microbiome, offering promising avenues for maintaining host health and preventing disease (Figure 4).

Conclusion

In summary, intestinal inflammation and immune homeostasis are intricately regulated by CD4+ T cells, whose functions are significantly influenced by metabolites derived from the intestinal microbiota. Dysbiosis, defined as a disruption in the composition of microbial communities and the resulting alteration in metabolite production, has been observed to shift immune balance toward excessive inflammatory responses, as evidenced in IBD. The restoration of healthy microbiota via interventions such as FMT, probiotics, and dietary modifications has proven effective in recalibrating immune responses and reducing intestinal inflammation. In the future, a more profound comprehension of the interactions between microbiota and CD4+ T cells may result in new opportunities for the development of preventative and therapeutic interventions. Early-life microbial colonization is of particular significance, as initial microbiota exposures have been shown to exert a profound influence on lifelong immune health. For instance, disparities in infant microbiota colonization are influenced by mode of delivery and breastfeeding, which have a substantial impact on the probability of developing immune-mediated diseases later in life.166–168 Consequently, future research should prioritize the development of strategies to enhance early microbial colonization through targeted probiotic or prebiotic interventions. Furthermore, the potential of microbial-based therapies in adults suggests the necessity for further investigation. The advent of personalized microbiome treatments, meticulously designed diets, and cutting-edge probiotics engineered to selectively augment beneficial microbial metabolites signifies a paradigm shift in the management of chronic intestinal inflammation and associated pathologies. Beyond the realm of human health, analogous principles find application in the domain of livestock management. The modulation of microbes in the early life stages of animals such as pigs, cattle, chickens, and sheep has been demonstrated to enhance intestinal health, improve immune function, and increase production efficiency. In summary, the targeted modulation of the microbiota CD4+ T cell axis demonstrates considerable potential for the management of intestinal inflammation. It is imperative that future research endeavors place a premium on the implementation of preventive strategies during early life stages, in conjunction with the development of therapeutic approaches for adults. This integrated approach has the potential to yield positive outcomes across diverse health domains, encompassing both human and animal health.
