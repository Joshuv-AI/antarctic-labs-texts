Evaluating the Innovator Training Program: Strengthening Evidence‐Based Practice and Building Implementation Capability in the Australian Aged Care Workforce
Authors: Stephanie Champion, Rebecca Bilton, Susan Gordon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study evaluated the outcomes for individuals who completed an Innovator Training Program (ITP) funded by the Commonwealth Government of Australia and delivered by Aged Care Research & Industry Innovation Australia (ARIIA). The program was designed to build best‐practice implementation and change management capability among the Australian aged care workforce. The intent was to drive sustainable innovation in aged care settings by using evidence‐based knowledge and best‐practice implementation methods to address work challenges in real time.MethodsParticipants enrolled in the ITP, between April 2022 and April 2023, participated in a multi‐methods program evaluation. Quantitative data were collected via pre‐ and post‐training surveys based on validated tools. Qualitative data were gathered through immediate post‐program interviews and follow‐up interviews at 6‐ and 12‐month post‐training. Descriptive statistics and McNemar–Bowker Exact Tests were used for quantitative analysis; thematic analysis was applied to qualitative data.ResultsSignificant improvements were observed in participants' confidence to adopt evidence into practice (p < 0.001), research skills (p < 0.001) and mentoring capacity (p < 0.001). Participants reported increased ability to integrate research into practice, develop project plans and influence organisational change. Qualitative findings highlighted enhanced leadership and collaboration skills, and career development, with some participants assuming new roles post‐training.ConclusionsThe ITP was associated with improved confidence, skills and capacity among participants to use evidence, and to plan sustainable implementation projects, providing the aged care workforce with skills to contribute to aged‐care reform. With recognition of the small sample and reliance on self‐report, further research is needed to examine longer term impacts on practice and organisational outcomes.Keywords: education, health services for the aged, implementation science, organisational innovation

Introduction

The Australian aged care sector is experiencing rapid and unprecedented change [1]. An ageing population, combined with an increased proportion of older Australians living with complex chronic health issues, has led to a strained aged care system that needs to adapt rapidly to meet demand [2]. The needs and preferences of older Australians are also evolving [3]. The national reform agenda emphasises the importance of improving access to person‐centred care to meet the increasing and changing needs of older Australians, while remaining economically viable and sustainable [4].

Alongside significant policy reforms, greater competition from for‐profit aged care service providers [5] and increasing demand for services, aged care providers are facing major workforce challenges related to the attraction and retention of skilled staff [3]. To ensure Australians continue to receive quality care as they age, it is essential to develop the skills of the aged care workforce to innovate and implement evidence‐based best practices into care [3] that align with the new rights‐based Australian Aged Care Act (launched on 1 November 2025) and the new aged care standards.

Innovation in aged care refers to practices or approaches that are ‘new in a given service setting’, rather than novel in absolute terms [5, p. 62]. Sustainable innovation requires effective implementation with careful attention to leadership, resourcing and context. Across Australian aged care and related services, the majority of workers have vocational or clinically focussed qualifications that do not explicitly include training in evidence‐based innovation or implementation skills [6]. As a result, opportunities to develop practical implementation and contextualised change management capabilities are limited for the Australian aged care workforce, and particularly for healthcare managers [7, 8, 9]. Building workforce capacity to adapt, innovate and apply evidence‐based solutions tailored to the local context is therefore critical to support ongoing aged care reform.

In response to these capability gaps, Aged Care Research and Industry Innovation Australia (ARIIA) was funded by the Australian Commonwealth Government in 2020 to consult with the aged care sector to determine priorities and methods to address these shortfalls. The ARIIA Innovator Training Program (ITP) was one approach identified to support the sector, designed to build implementation capacity by supporting participants to develop and plan innovative, evidence‐based solutions to real‐world workplace challenges. The ITP was open to any member of the Australian aged care workforce employed in an approved home, residential or flexible care services, with organisational support to participate. To ensure equitable access, organisations were supported to participate with a reimbursement of A$2000 per enrolment to cover the cost of staff backfill.

The ITP participants are supported to develop a robust and fit‐for‐purpose project plan and pitch to address a challenge or opportunity in their organisation. Participants build skills and knowledge in how to define challenges using research and clinical evidence, translate evidence to identify potential solutions, co‐design feasible implementation plans with diverse stakeholders for sustainable evidence‐based innovation, and identify indicators of success, risks, outcomes and impacts. The program logic for the design of the ITP modules can be broadly mapped to widely used public‐sector logic models, including the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [10] and the Knowledge‐to‐Action (KTA) Framework [11].

The ITP is delivered through a blended online learning model, with executive sponsorship to assist participants to navigate organisational priorities. This paper presents the outcomes for participants in the ITP during 2022–2023 with respect to skills, confidence, capability, and personal and professional growth.

Methods

All participants who enrolled in the ITP between April 2022 and April 2023 were invited to participate in a multi‐methods program evaluation. Ethics approval was granted by Flinders University Human Research Ethics Committee (project number 5306).

A longitudinal evaluation undertaken in parallel aimed to understand participants' experiences and perceptions of the ITP from training delivery, pre‐implementation through to project implementation at 12‐month post‐ITP completion. The evaluation was informed by Proctor's implementation outcomes taxonomy [12], which guided the selection of survey domains and development of interview questions to capture acceptability, adoption, feasibility and sustainability‐related outcomes relevant to workforce capacity building.

Participant demographic information was drawn from the initial application. Participant updates were collected at 6‐ and 12‐month post‐ITP completion via online and telephone interviews, email communications or proxy reports from organisational colleagues.

Quantitative Surveys—Baseline and Follow‐Up

All enrolled participants were invited to complete an online baseline survey prior to commencing the ITP, and a follow‐up survey 1 week after program completion. The 51 question, multiple‐choice survey assessed confidence, skills and practices related to finding, evaluating and adapting evidence, project management and knowledge translation skills.

Survey items were drawn from validated surveys previously used to assess research capacity and evidence use, including the Research Capacity and Culture (RCC) [13], the Foundations in Knowledge Translation [14] and Evidence Use (SIEU) [15], as they each measure core constructs that align with the intended short‐ and long‐term outcomes for ITP participants. Selected scales were used to ensure a wide range of outcomes were assessed, while minimising the burden on participants. The use of individual scales in place of using validated instruments in their entirety can affect the internal consistency, scale reliability and construct validity of these tools; thus, outcomes were used to detect patterns in responses, to be interpreted within the context of the qualitative data rather than to generate scale scores.

Interviews Post‐ITP, 6‐ and 12‐Month Post‐ITP

Qualitative data were collected through semi‐structured interviews conducted immediately post‐training and at 6‐ and 12‐month follow‐up. Post‐training interviews focussed on participants' perceived changes in knowledge and confidence. Interviews at 6 and 12 months explored individuals' experiences with implementation, barriers and enablers to implementation, and application of the knowledge and skills gained from the ITP. All interviews were conducted online or by phone, audio‐recorded and transcribed for analysis.

Analysis Methods

The matched survey data were analysed using the McNemar‐Bowker Exact Test to assess changes in categorical responses [16]. A significant change was indicated by a p‐value of < 0.05.

Sample size calculations for the planned analysis were conducted [17]. To detect moderate effects using the McNemar–Bowker Exact Test with 80% power and an alpha of 0.05, a conservative estimate indicated that 200–300 matched pairs would be required. As this was not achievable with the available participants in the ITP, the researchers approached the quantitative analysis pragmatically, opting to strengthen the quantitative analysis with qualitative data to provide richer insight into participants' experiences, corroborate the interpretation of quantitative trends and acknowledge the limited paired sample size.

The qualitative interviews were analysed using an inductive thematic approach informed by Braun and Clarke's six‐step framework [18]. Inductive coding was initially completed by one researcher, with codes applied to meaningful statements to capture the essence of the categories and identify patterns. Initial codes were independently reviewed by two additional researchers to ensure credibility in codes and themes. The three researchers contributing to the qualitative analysis have backgrounds in aged care research, implementation science and qualitative research methods, and maintained reflexivity through team discussions and memo‐writing to acknowledge researcher assumptions and positionality. Qualitative data analysis software package NVivo (version 14) was used to manage data and support systematic coding [19]. Illustrative quotes were selected to exemplify each theme. Pseudonyms and role categories were used for all participant quotes.

Data Management

To support analysis, the response scales for selected survey items were collapsed where distributions were highly skewed. Seven‐point and 10‐point scales were grouped into broader categories to improve interpretability for paired data.

In interviews, participants indicated the training translated into readiness to initiate, plan and implement evidence‐based change to address organisational challenges:

I think it's [the ITP] definitely given me like a base knowledge so that if something else was to arise, I'd feel comfortable to start my own little project and know sort of the processes to make and ensure that its evidence‐based and that I was on the right track with it. … And I think developed me as a person as well, to feel more confident to do these things. The project Leila spearheaded went on to receive grant funding, and Leila is now leading a key element of the project's implementation.

I think there's a lot of good ideas in aged care, but I just don't think that the general health care assistant, who's working on the floor in residential aged care, is thinking their idea could be translated to something bigger. (Lee, Executive)

Research Skills

Significant improvements were observed in finding relevant literature, in writing research (‘project’) plans and reports, and integrating evidence into practice post‐ITP (Table 3). Combined with the mentoring findings, these responses indicated participants improved in their capacity to communicate their ideas and plans to peers.

Participants reported better understanding of how to find reliable resources, and adapt and apply evidence into practice within their setting. One participant reflected on his organisation's ‘culture of anecdotal evidence’, sharing his experiences in how individual opinions ‘drown out’ research evidence. Participants talked about how they would approach project planning differently in future:

When we're putting together new projects just to make sure that they're really clearly defined before we're presenting them to our board… sometimes I felt like projects are a bit haphazardly put together, and so for me, it's really clarified this is what we need to do in a step‐by‐step process… we'll definitely use that with our projects going forward. (Anika, Executive)

I think the way that I ask questions now is sometimes different. I'm a bit more direct to get the answer that I need rather than fluffing about… What is the actual problem is probably the first question because is the problem really a problem? Yes, 100%, it makes me think differently. (Lee, Direct care)

As many of the ITP participants held executive roles, it was apparent that some participants were already familiar with implementation concepts and research approaches but found value in being refreshed on the ideas:

It wasn't new [to me]. It was something that we learnt at university, so it wasn't new, but it was a good refresher, and it organised my thoughts so that I could get the project going. (Mei, Direct care)

Interview data suggested the ITP helped embed the approach of using evidence to support project planning, change and sustainable implementation:

For me, it was the linkages between research and just the normal project management lifecycles…just embedding that as part of that cycle. I think we've got good project managers here… we probably lack a little bit on the research… So that's where we're looking to fill that gap. … making sure that going to the evidence is included as part of our cycle. Participants described using internal organisational data to promote and align their projects with organisational priorities. They noted a lack of internal data, poor communication and documentation as barriers to project development and implementation. The ITP highlighted gaps in their data processes and the need for better recording of progress.

The ITP process was recognised as an important starting point for project planning, though participants found their projects evolved more post training. The ITP timeframe was not sufficient to complete all stages of planning, including co‐design and piloting. Participants recommended that ITP facilitators clearly communicate this to organisational sponsors to ensure expectations were realistic.

Participants highlighted the value of building peer connections and gathering peer feedback during ITP workshop discussions. Workshops provided real‐world insights into problem‐solving approaches, and barriers and enablers to project implementation from across a broad range of organisations:

The defining of the problem was, there was a huge focus on that with the ITP program, and I think … as much as it felt like I was floundering, and I really wanted to move on to the bit that actually made a difference, I think it was really crucial. I'm questioning people on things all the time now, to try and understand what happened. Are they aware of what's been done in the past and especially in health? There's so much learning to [access] because it's already happened so many places before. (Arjun, Direct care)

The ITP ends with the participants pitching their project plan to their peers. Several participants shared that they used their pitch post‐ITP to socialise their project, using feedback and the skills developed in the ITP to make their pitch more audience focused and comprehensive:

…we've been trying to build the profile [of the project]. That's why it was so useful to have that little recorded 10‐min presentation… it demonstrated to everyone that I sent it to that this is necessary. We should all be doing this at each site and why aren't we? Why isn't this part of the usual expectation of the residents, their families and all staff? I think it's definitely been useful from that point of view to raise the profile, to lift people's expectations. (Mei, Direct care)

New Roles and Opportunities

Several participants reported expanded responsibilities or new roles in the follow‐up period, partly attributing visibility of skills, demonstration of their passion for innovation and the evidence‐based implementation project plans developed during the ITP. Six participants reported moving to more senior roles in the follow‐up period.

As examples, Issac, a direct care worker, was invited to take on an organisational project lead role following the ITP, to use the solutions identified in his project plan to successfully implement improved care across the facility for people living with dementia. Liam (Direct care) was able to lead other staff to deliver his planned project and has since been tasked with identifying scaling opportunities within the broader organisation. Liam was sought out to support the roll‐out of a similar project in another state, an opportunity he attributes to the ITP.

Most participant feedback was positive. The researchers actively looked for neutral or negative perspectives in interview transcripts and found a small number of participants expressed neutral views, reporting limited benefits of the ITP. No participants reported dissatisfaction with the program, though most described challenges with the implementation of projects, such as limited time, resourcing and organisational buy‐in during implementation. Even participants with prior research training, or whose projects stalled or shifted direction post‐ITP, indicated they gained useful skills or insights from the program. The largely positive feedback may reflect self‐selection bias as evaluation participants may have had more positive experiences with the ITP and post‐training implementation than non‐participants. Future analysis will explore challenges and enablers of implementation in aged care in greater depth, providing a more critical analysis of these issues.

Discussion

Australian aged care requires evidence‐based innovation to meet the growing complexity of older people's needs. The ITP was designed to strengthen the workforce's capability to use research evidence and implementation principles when planning change. Participants reported substantial improvements in their confidence, research and implementation skills, and their ability to utilise evidence to address organisational problems. The qualitative findings highlighted how the ITP supported participants to interpret evidence, define problems more rigorously, engage stakeholders and evaluate evidence relevance for local projects.

Participants also demonstrated stronger judgement in considering evidence for contextual fit, and to write feasible, evidence‐based implementation plans. This is an important success of the ITP as, in many clinical settings of residential aged care, adherence to good clinical practice guidelines is concerningly low. A recent investigation of evidence‐based care of Australian long‐term care residents showed adherence to evidence‐based care indicators ranged from 12% to 81% for some conditions [20]. Increasing awareness of the value of evidence‐based practice is critical to improving care outcomes, and skills in contextualisation and adaption of evidence are especially important in Australia's diverse aged care sector, where a one‐size‐fits‐all approach is unlikely to be successful.

The ITP also facilitated cross‐organisational collaboration. Collaboration is critical to support providers to solve common problems and innovate during reform of the sector. Opportunities for collaboration are sparse due to bureaucratic and financial barriers [21], and strategies to support collaboration between care providers are needed as the sector aims to provide more personalised, coordinated evidence‐based practice and optimise resources [22].

Despite the broad eligibility criteria for the ITP, most participants held executive or senior roles, reflecting ongoing workload and rostering challenges preventing personal care workers (PCWs) from participating in extended training. Program adaptations, including online delivery, creating shorter and more flexible versions of the ITP, and tailored content to accommodate varying levels of experience, aim to reduce inequities in access to training. Facilitators also work with organisations to ensure project scope and level of organisation support are proportional to the role of the participant. Well‐supported direct carer ITP participants have successfully implemented well‐defined and manageable projects, effecting small but meaningful change at the care frontline.

The ITP participants have reported utilising their skills beyond the scope of the initial project, mentoring colleagues and contributing to other organisational initiatives. This outcome reflects broader evidence that capacity building programs can promote leadership, enhance organisational readiness for change and support sustainable innovation [7, 9]. The ITP outcomes demonstrated the value to participants to identify problems and opportunities in their environments and develop sustainable, evidence‐based solutions. Empowering the workforce to implement evidence‐based change fosters individual growth and contributes to enhanced care and services. Overstretched and under‐resourced, Australian aged care workers de‐prioritise non‐essential care tasks beyond the scope of their role [23], including seeking opportunities for innovation. Building confidence in staff to recognise challenges within their setting and apply knowledge into practice is critical for implementation success.

The broader outcomes of the ITP are difficult to capture through short‐term evaluation. Designed to build capacity and capability in the aged care workforce, the ITP aligns with international findings that personal growth and career development pathways are key to workforce retention [24, 25]. Retention remains a challenge in Australia, where demand for skilled workers is growing [6], yet 49% of residential aged care workers plan to leave their roles within 5 years [26]. While the current evaluation cannot yet determine the long‐term impact of the ITP on workforce retention, interim evidence suggests meaningful training opportunities improve job satisfaction and enable workplace projects, supporting retention [25].

Globally, implementation science capacity building programs have expanded across research and industry settings, improving practitioners' knowledge and confidence in applying evidence‐based interventions [27, 28]. Recent reviews in aged care highlight the need for implementation leadership training to support successful adoption of and sustainability of innovations [29].

Our findings aligned with this literature, showing that building implementation capacity can strengthen organisational readiness and support sustainable change [7, 9]. Like similar initiatives, the ITP faces challenges engaging frontline staff [8], a key issue in Australia where workforce capability and retention are central to achieving sustainable aged care reform [30]. Embedding co‐design and collaborative implementation, as modelled in the ITP, advocates for active involvement of the aged care workforce at all levels, to ensure innovation in the sector is accepted, relevant and sustainable [31].

The aged care sector urgently needs innovation [1], and technology alone cannot fully offset the shortfall in workforce capacity and capability. Barriers to innovation, including competing priorities, rapidly changing demands, organisational silos, risk aversion and disempowered staff, inhibit innovation [32]. Sustainable innovation requires a workforce adaptable to new technologies and complex care needs [33], and leaders with implementation capability [29]. Future iterations of ARIIA capacity‐building programs will address these barriers, and strengthen content on sharing implementation skills with peers. While not statistically significant, participants reported greater consideration of implementation costs, training needs and organisational fit; areas that future programs could continue to reinforce.

Without the strong implementation planning, innovations risk remaining short‐term and failing to embed into practice [31]. Organisations also often struggle to disseminate outcomes of innovations (successes and failures), limiting broader learning and uptake of innovations [34].

Limitations

A limitation of this study is the skewness of the survey data towards high baseline scores, suggesting participants may enter the ITP with existing confidence and skills, or that response bias influenced the participants. The under‐representation of frontline staff limits insight into the experiences of staff facing the greatest operational pressures. The small sample size also constrains statistical power. The quantitative findings should be interpreted cautiously and alongside the qualitative data. The short follow‐up period between surveys may likely have captured intent rather than actual change in practice, as interview responses suggest that participants continued to develop their skills and evolve their practice beyond the completion of the follow‐up survey.

Some inconsistencies in the survey may indicate difficulty interpreting the questions or the complexity of evidence‐based decision‐making. While the surveys used validated tools, the SIEU scale [15] developed for US contexts, may not have translated cleanly to aged care settings.

Conclusions

The ITP participants reported increased confidence and capability in applying evidence‐based practices, research skills and project planning, with the interviews illustrating some of the ways participants applied these skills in their project and day‐to‐day roles. Participants reported seeking out opportunities for innovation in their organisation beyond the scope of their original projects. Although only a small number of participants reported new roles or responsibilities, early indications suggest the ITP may support career development. Participants valued the opportunity for collaboration with their peers, noting that external perspectives strengthened their implementation strategies.

The ITP offers an adaptable pathway towards workforce development, providing a solution to gaps in implementation knowledge and providing an opportunity for workforce capacity building to support aged care reform. Empowering aged care staff with implementation skills is essential for sustainable innovation and the delivery of rights‐based care in a sector facing workforce and service delivery challenges.
