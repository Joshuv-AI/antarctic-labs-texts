Medicine use during breastfeeding in Uganda: stakeholder perspectives on barriers and facilitators
Authors: Ritah Nakijoba, Ronald Kiguba, Winnie Nakiyingi, Faridah Namitala, Catriona Waitt, Lynn M. Atuyambe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreastfeeding supports infant health, yet medicine use during this period is often influenced by limited safety data, cultural beliefs, and weak regulatory guidance. This study highlights how these factors, along with partner influence, contribute to uncertainty and reliance on informal advice, emphasizing the need for clear guidelines and better communication to support safe medicine use.MethodsThis exploratory qualitative study was conducted between January and April 2024, involving twelve breastfeeding women, seven of their partners, ten healthcare providers, and five representatives from the National Drug Authority in Uganda. Data were collected through in-depth interviews with breastfeeding women and their partners, while key informant interviews were conducted with healthcare providers and regulatory officials. Thematic analysis was employed to analyze the data, identifying recurring codes that were systematically organized into subthemes and overarching themes.ResultsFour key themes were identified concerning perspectives on medicine use during breastfeeding. First, healthcare providers and regulatory personnel showed varying levels of knowledge of medicine use during breastfeeding, relying on external resources due to uncertainty, while women identified healthcare providers as their primary and most trusted source of information regarding medicine use. Facilitators included trust in healthcare providers, support from partners and family, access to reliable safety information, strengthening pharmacovigilance systems, and conducting drug safety studies. The role of partners in offering financial and emotional support and encouraging medicine adherence was acknowledged. Barriers to medication use during breastfeeding included poor communication and inadequate guidance from healthcare providers, stigma, and concerns about risks from the medicines to the infant. Additionally, unclear drug labeling, inaccessibility of information, and ethical concerns about including breastfeeding women in studies hindered informed decision-making. Recommendations emphasized improving health education, using reliable resources, prescribing safer drugs, timing breastfeeding to minimize medicine risks, and considering non-hormonal contraceptive options to prevent reduced breast milk supply.ConclusionThe study highlights the critical need for improved communication, guidance, and access to reliable information regarding medicine use during breastfeeding. It emphasizes the importance of healthcare provider education, partner involvement, and evidence-based guidelines to support informed decision-making.

Background

Breastfeeding is recognized for its significant health benefits, including reducing infant morbidity and mortality, enhancing child development, and improving maternal health outcomes [1]. However, breastfeeding women frequently encounter medical conditions that necessitate pharmacological interventions, creating a dilemma for healthcare providers regarding the safety of medication use during lactation [2].

Breastfeeding women often face challenges when using medicines due to unclear or conflicting information about drug safety during lactation [3, 4]. Many struggle to balance the benefits of breastfeeding with the need for medical treatment, leading to concerns about potential harm to their infants [5]. Misinformation, inconsistent guidance from healthcare providers, and a lack of accessible, evidence-based resources further complicate decision-making. As a result, some women discontinue necessary medicines, while others stop breastfeeding prematurely.

The exclusion of pregnant and breastfeeding women from clinical trials has contributed to a lack of robust evidence on the safety of many medicines during lactation [6]. As a result, healthcare providers often adopt a cautious approach, relying on incomplete or outdated resources, which can hinder optimal care [7]. Studies have also shown that providers face challenges due to inconsistent guidelines, while partners who often influence medicine decisions are shaped by cultural norms and misinformation [8, 9]. This highlights the importance of understanding the broader sociocultural context in which breastfeeding and medicine decisions are made. Additionally, regulatory authorities are constrained in offering clear guidance, as they primarily depend on post-market surveillance rather than clinical trial data [10]. While global literature highlights these concerns, limited research exists on the perspectives of breastfeeding women and their families in Uganda and the broader Sub-Saharan African region. Contextual challenges such as limited healthcare access, low health literacy, and strong cultural influences may further complicate safe medicine use during lactation. The lack of locally relevant evidence and guidance leaves both families and providers without adequate support for informed decision-making, highlighting the need for research to inform policy and practice. Despite growing awareness of these issues, little is known about how they manifest in low-resource settings like Uganda. This study addresses that gap by exploring the perspectives of breastfeeding women, their partners, healthcare providers, and National Drug Authority members to fill critical knowledge gaps and contribute to the development of evidence-based recommendations that promote the safe use of medicines during lactation.

Methods

Study design and setting

This qualitative study was conducted through face-to-face and online interviews, exploring perceived barriers, facilitators, and recommendations for medicines used during breastfeeding. The study was conducted at the Infectious Diseases Institute (IDI) and affiliated Kampala City Council health care sites. The sites were selected due to their high patient volumes and diverse urban populations. These settings provided access to a range of healthcare providers and breastfeeding women, making them ideal for exploring real-world experiences with medicine use during lactation and ensuring findings are relevant for informing practice and policy in similar low-resource urban settings. Also, the healthcare sites provide prevention, care, and treatment for over 200,000 general adult patients from different regions of the country. The study population comprised four key stakeholder groups: healthcare providers involved in the care of breastfeeding women, breastfeeding women themselves, their partners, and representatives from the National Drug Authority (NDA). The NDA serves a pivotal role in Uganda’s health system by regulating and ensuring the availability of essential, effective, and affordable medicines, thereby contributing to the promotion of quality healthcare and the safe use of medications nationwide. Healthcare providers recruited from the study sites included physicians, nurses, clinical officers, and pharmacists, all of whom have substantial experience in delivering care to diverse patient populations, including breastfeeding mothers. Partners of breastfeeding women were identified and recruited through the Sexual and Reproductive Health Unit at the Infectious Diseases Institute (IDI), ensuring the inclusion of individuals actively engaged in decision-making around medicine use during lactation.

Participant recruitment and sampling

Participants were recruited using purposive, expert, and typical case sampling strategies, appropriate for the qualitative design of the study. The typical case sampling method is used to select participants who reflect common experiences relevant to the research topic, it was used to recruit partners of breastfeeding women, given their influential role in decisions surrounding medicine use. Purposive sampling was employed to identify breastfeeding women with firsthand experience of medicine use during lactation. Expert sampling guided the selection of healthcare providers and representatives from the National Drug Authority (NDA) due to their professional expertise in pharmaceutical regulation and clinical care.

Breastfeeding women were eligible if they had breastfed within the past six months and had used or considered using medicines during this period. Healthcare providers were recruited if they were actively involved in prescribing, dispensing, or advising on medicine use during lactation. National Drug Authority (NDA) staff were selected based on their roles in drug regulation and policy, while partners were included if they played a supportive or decision-making role in the care of a breastfeeding woman. Recruitment was done through direct contact at healthcare facilities, phone calls, and emails. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. Initial data analysis began after seven in-depth interviews (IDIs) and five key informant interviews (KIIs) indicated saturation. To ensure saturation, additional interviews were conducted, resulting in a final sample of 34 participants: 12 breastfeeding women, 10 healthcare providers, 5 NDA representatives, and 7 partners. Participants were drawn from multiple diverse healthcare settings to capture varied experiences across healthcare cadres, including medical doctors, clinical officers, pharmacists, and nurses. Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected through in-depth interviews (IDIs) and key informant interviews (KIIs). IDIs were conducted with breastfeeding women and their partners. KIIs included healthcare providers and representatives from the National Drug Authority (NDA), recognized for their expert knowledge in pharmaceutical care and regulation. Both IDIs and KIIs followed pre-designed, semi-structured topic guides developed by the lead investigator and refined through collaborative review by the supervisory and study teams, as documented in the data and materials sources. They were refined through expert review and piloting with representative participants to ensure clarity, cultural relevance, and content validity, following best practices for qualitative tool design and validation as outlined by Braun and Clarke [11]. These guides were assessed for relevance and alignment with the study objectives. Each interview lasted approximately 45 min. Topic guides included open-ended questions and probes to encourage detailed responses. Pilot testing was conducted to refine the tools: interviews were piloted with two healthcare providers, one NDA representative, three breastfeeding women, and two partners. Feedback informed revisions to enhance the clarity, flow, and appropriateness of the guides for diverse participant groups. All interviews were conducted in a one-on-one format to ensure privacy and encourage open dialogue.

Data collection took place between January and April 2024. IDIs with breastfeeding women and their partners were conducted face-to-face, while KIIs with NDA staff were conducted online to accommodate logistical considerations. Interviews with healthcare providers were conducted in person at their respective facilities. The choice of individual interviews over focus group discussions was intentional, to foster a more comfortable environment for participants to share sensitive experiences.

Participants were interviewed in their preferred language, primarily Luganda or English, and scheduled at times convenient to them. If unavailable on the initially proposed date, participants were encouraged to select an alternative date. All interviews were audio recorded and those conducted in Luganda were translated into English before transcription to preserve the authenticity of responses. Each interview lasted approximately 45 min and was guided by the structured topic guide. Participant demographic and professional characteristics, such as age, gender, discipline, number of children, and years of professional experience, were documented to aid in data interpretation. Data saturation, defined as the point at which no new themes were identified, was assessed through ongoing team debriefings. These discussions informed decisions on whether additional interviews were necessary to ensure thematic completeness.

Outcomes

Perceived barriers, knowledge of medicines, facilitators, and recommendations for medicine use during breastfeeding were the core outcomes of this study.

Data analysis

An inductive thematic analysis was conducted using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework to systematically identify, analyze, and interpret patterns within the data. First, two researchers (RN and NF) initially selected three transcripts for open coding. Second, each transcript was read line by line to generate the initial set of codes using Atlas 6.0 software. Third, codes were organized into potential themes based on patterns in participants’ responses. Fourth, themes were reviewed to ensure coherence with coded extracts that included: barriers, the role of partners, facilitators, knowledge, and recommendations related to the study objectives. Fifth, themes were defined and named to capture their essence. Finally, a detailed report was produced with supporting quotations to illustrate each theme. Data analysis was iterative, and findings were regularly discussed among the research team to ensure rigor and reflexivity.

The analysis was informed by a critical realist epistemological stance, which acknowledges that while participants’ accounts are shaped by their contexts and interpretations, there are underlying structures and mechanisms that influence their experiences as reported by Fletcher [12] This perspective supported the move beyond surface-level themes to identify deeper causal explanations and contextual influences underpinning participants'behaviors and beliefs.

Data credibility and reliability

The transcript content was cross-verified with the audio recordings to ensure accuracy by RN, NW, and NF. To enhance the credibility of the analysis, the study team became thoroughly familiar with the data through note-taking and debriefing during data collection, as well as double reading and debriefing during the analysis phase. Consistency in coding was reinforced by following the established coding framework, which further contributed to the trustworthiness of the analysis. Inter-coder reliability was assessed using the first three transcripts, and thematic saturation was employed to ensure a comprehensive interpretation of the results.

Data management and storage

Transcripts and audio files from the IDI and KII were securely stored on a password-protected computer, with access restricted to study personnel only. Transcripts were de-identified by assigning unique codes to participants and removing any personally identifiable information, thereby protecting the anonymity of the respondents. Following the evaluation of the transcripts, all audio recordings were permanently deleted to ensure confidentiality.

Results

Ten key informant interviews were conducted with healthcare providers and five NDA staff, 12 IDIs with breastfeeding women, and seven IDIs were carried out with their partners. The healthcare providers represented a diverse array of professional disciplines across a wide range of specialties, and more than half had over five years of experience working in clinical settings. More than half of the partners had attained some level of formal education. Data structure for perspectives on medicines use during breastfeeding

Guided by a critical realist epistemology, four themes were developed to reflect participants’ perspectives on medicine use during breastfeeding. These themes captured both the reported experiences and the underlying contextual and structural influences shaping them. Themes included: knowledge of medicines during breastfeeding, facilitators of medicine use, barriers to medicine use, and recommendations.

Knowledge of medicines during breastfeeding

This theme highlights that breastfeeding women mainly rely on healthcare providers for medicine information, with limited use of written or digital sources. Informal networks also influence their decisions. While providers understand the need to weigh risks and benefits, uncertainty about specific medicines remains, indicating ongoing gaps in accessible, reliable information as observed in the following subthemes.

Sources of information on medicines

Among breastfeeding women, 70% identified healthcare providers as their primary and most trusted source of information regarding medicine use during breastfeeding. However, the utilization of digital resources, such as internet searches, was minimal due to limited awareness and accessibility. 90% of women reported not consulting drug leaflets or packaging inserts for guidance. Instead, these materials were often repurposed for non-informational uses, such as lighting charcoal stoves. Additionally, informal sources, including peer networks and family members, contributed to shaping women's perceptions and decisions regarding medicines during breastfeeding.

“Whenever I need to take medicine or buy the drug from an outside drug store, I always call the nurse first at the IDI clinic, and that is what I was told by the counselor... I trust their advice more than anything else because they understand my issue, what is safe for me and my baby” (IDI_BFW-07).

Awareness of risk–benefit assessment

More than half of the healthcare providers generally reported the need to balance risks and benefits when prescribing medicines. Uncertainty around specific medicines

Despite general awareness, a quarter of healthcare providers had uncertainty about specific drugs and their safety during breastfeeding.“Like diclofenac is not recommended while you're breastfeeding. Then I also believe some anticonvulsants, I'm not so sure of which one exactly, but some of them are not recommended while you're breastfeeding. I think also some drugs that have caffeine because they almost reduce the milk production and supply, so those are also not so recommended”. (KII_HCP-05).

Facilitators of medicines during breastfeeding

Trust in healthcare providers and adherence support

Over 90 percent of women reported that trust in healthcare providers significantly influenced their adherence to medical advice and treatment. They highlighted the importance of seeking information from health facilities before taking medicines, sharing that support from counselors and other healthcare providers plays a critical role in helping women on long-term medication, such as antiretroviral (ART) used to treat the Human Immune Virus (HIV), and continuous adherence to their treatment.

“For breastfeeding, women to go with their children to the health worker to identify the exact type of medication to be prescribed to them because when you get a problem could be that sometimes you might buy medication which is not supposed to be used when breastfeeding and you end up getting dizzy or medication that you are just not supposed to use, therefore it’s important to ask for advice before self-medication” (IDI_BFW-O4)

“I was educated about the importance of sharing information about the drug I am taking, and that I was not the only one or the first, so slowly by slowly I started to swallow and I feel free to take the medication without hiding it” (IDI_BFW-O7)

Partner and family support

More than half of breastfeeding women reported that partners and family members played a key role in supporting medication adherence, providing emotional, financial, and informational support. While spouses were often consulted and valued for their input, advice from family members, though well-intentioned, sometimes reflected cultural beliefs rather than scientific guidance. Partners themselves recognized their critical role, expressing a strong commitment to supporting their spouses during the postpartum period, particularly in navigating treatment decisions.

“My husband plays a big role in my healthcare decisions because he is the one who buys the medicine. If he is unsure about a drug, he may refuse to buy it until we confirm with a doctor. He wants to be sure that it is safe for both me and our baby. Sometimes, he even accompanies me to the clinic to get the treatment” (IDI_BFW-O5).

Inclusion in clinical studies

Three healthcare providers emphasized the importance of including breastfeeding women in clinical trials to address the lack of safety data for some medicines. Doing so would generate reliable evidence, helping healthcare providers make informed decisions and offer safer treatment options. They advocated for careful study designs to protect both mothers and infants while improving health outcomes.

“.... how would you know that this drug is either safe or not safe in breastfeeding? we need breastfeeding women to be included in the studies so that you also get that information. I'll take an example of DTG, I think the first studies didn't include pregnant and breastfeeding mothers, but later included them... now the drug is prescribed to breastfeeding women” (KII_HCP-06).

Strengthening systems for reporting adverse events related to drug use

Over ninety percent of healthcare providers and regulatory personnel emphasized the critical role of strengthening monitoring and reporting adverse events related to medicines. They emphasized that active pharmacovigilance systems are essential for detecting any potential side effects or risks that may develop after medications are prescribed to breastfeeding women. By tracking these adverse events in real-time, healthcare providers and researchers can identify patterns of harm, assess the extent of drug safety, and take timely action to address any issues. Participants also noted that reporting mechanisms should be easy to use, encouraging both healthcare professionals and breastfeeding women to actively use them.“I think, you know, detailed research is involved, finding out...pharmacovigilance, so if we follow up with lactating women and find out how they are doing with the drugs, how are the children being affected by those drugs, I think that can help us to assess more” (KII_NDA-01).

“I think, you know, detailed research is involved, finding out...pharmacovigilance, so if we follow up with lactating women and find out how they are doing with the drugs, how are the children being affected by those drugs, I think that can help us to assess more” (KII_NDA-01).

Conduct studies on drug safety

Two regulators emphasized the importance of conducting studies to understand how medications transfer from the breastfeeding mother to her infant through breast milk. They highlighted that many current prescribing guidelines lack detailed data on the extent and rate at which drugs are passed to the infant, making it difficult to accurately assess the risks associated with medication use during breastfeeding. This would enable healthcare providers to make more precise decisions about which medications are safe for breastfeeding women, reducing unnecessary medicine avoidance.“...watching out for when the drug is taken and the breastfeeding time. It could be, according to the pharmacodynamics of the drug, or rather the pharmacokinetics, how the drug moves in the body. If I am aware of the amount of time, and pharmacokinetics of this product, maybe I would advise them how they should space breastfeeding” (KII_NDA-04).

Barriers to medicines use during breastfeeding

Communication challenges

Two healthcare providers highlighted the need to pay extra attention to effectively communicate the risks and benefits of medicines, as breastfeeding mothers may opt to avoid necessary treatment due to concerns about their infant's safety.“We often focus on prescribing the medication, but sometimes don’t fully explain the details to breastfeeding mothers. Then you just have to reassure the mother and you tell them, we are giving you this medicine because it's your health first” (KII_HCP-01).

“We often focus on prescribing the medication, but sometimes don’t fully explain the details to breastfeeding mothers. Then you just have to reassure the mother and you tell them, we are giving you this medicine because it's your health first” (KII_HCP-01).

Inadequate guidance from healthcare providers

At least five breastfeeding women reported struggling with inadequate information from healthcare providers regarding medicines safety during lactation. Some women reported that they were only asked about their breastfeeding status after experiencing adverse effects from medicines. Others emphasized the need for proactive guidance from healthcare providers to prevent unintended medicine-related complications.

Perceptions of the safety of breastmilk and fear of side effects

One partner and two women voiced apprehension about the potential transfer of the drug to the baby, raising concerns and fear of reduced breast milk supply. Others were worried that medicines use might compromise the quality of breast milk, further influencing their attitudes toward medicines use during breastfeeding.“But my worries... I don’t trust some researchers, the drugs given to the woman may affect the breastmilk, and then she stops breastfeeding, since I am fully responsible for her, I have to be in the know of what is going on” (IDI_PBFW-07).

“But my worries... I don’t trust some researchers, the drugs given to the woman may affect the breastmilk, and then she stops breastfeeding, since I am fully responsible for her, I have to be in the know of what is going on” (IDI_PBFW-07).

Inadequate information during drug labeling

Two personnel from the National Drug Authority emphasized the tendency of manufacturers to provide ambiguous information regarding the use of medicines during breastfeeding, leaving healthcare providers in a dilemma about whether to prescribe the drug. This lack of clear guidance often leads to uncertainty in prescribing decisions and causes hesitation in recommending certain medications.“Investigator's procurement doesn't give any strong evidence about what happens to the drug when it goes into the mother's body, or at the point of the drug moving into the breast milk. You have a dose that the mother receives, and we don't know whether that's the dose that goes to the infant” (KII_NDA-03).

“Investigator's procurement doesn't give any strong evidence about what happens to the drug when it goes into the mother's body, or at the point of the drug moving into the breast milk. You have a dose that the mother receives, and we don't know whether that's the dose that goes to the infant” (KII_NDA-03).

Inaccessibility of information

Three healthcare providers reported a struggle to find reliable, up-to-date safety information on medications for breastfeeding women. They mentioned that a lack of readily available data delays decision-making and can lead to overly cautious or uninformed prescribing. Furthermore, more than half of breastfeeding women indicated that available information is often too technical for them to comprehend without additional clarification from healthcare providers.

Ethical concerns

About 20 percent of healthcare providers, women, and their partners expressed reservations about including breastfeeding women in clinical studies investigating new drugs due to fear of possible harm to the infant. They were concerned that exposing breastfeeding mothers to experimental drugs could lead to unknown side effects that might negatively affect the baby through breast milk. Participants emphasized that while including breastfeeding women in research could generate crucial safety information, it must be done with caution and under strict ethical guidelines to minimize any risks to both the mother and child.

“It’s a necessary evil: we can’t expose the baby to unknown drugs. So, exposing... of course, if the mother is not well, her health is a priority. okay. But while ensuring that she's well, we cannot also expose the nursing infant to harms that could be avoided” (KII_NDA-05).

Recommendations for medicines use during breastfeeding

Health education talks

Breastfeeding women emphasized the need for increased health education to boost their understanding of medicines during lactation. Many expressed concerns about the lack of accessible and simplified information on medicines. They recommended regular educational sessions at healthcare facilities to address common misconceptions and provide evidence-based guidance. Additionally, they suggested the use of multiple communication methods, such as videos and translated materials in local languages. Women also highlighted the importance of involving partners and family members in these educational efforts, as their support plays a crucial role in adherence to safe medicine practices.

"I was told about not taking any medicines when I was pregnant, No one told me anything about medicines after giving birth. I only found out later that some drugs can be used wisely. If they included this information in antenatal hospital visits, more mothers would know what to do"(IDI_BFW-06).

Discussion

This study explored stakeholder perspectives on medicine use during breastfeeding, including healthcare providers, regulatory personnel, breastfeeding women, and their partners. The findings light critical gaps in knowledge, structural barriers, and opportunities for improving safe medicine use in this population.

Participants demonstrated variable awareness of medicine safety during breastfeeding. Some were able to assess the risks and benefits effectively, while others expressed uncertainty, particularly around specific drug effects on milk production. A concern similarly noted by Sachs, who emphasized the challenges of decision-making in the context of limited safety data and unclear clinical guidance [13]. Structured training programs and tools such as LactMed and the British National Formulary (BNF) are essential in supporting evidence-based prescribing, as suggested by Hale and Krutsch [14]. Our findings align with those of Ito [15], who reported a lack of confidence among providers due to limited formal training on medicine use during Participants demonstrated variable awareness of medicine safety during breastfeeding. Some were able to assess the risks and benefits effectively, while others expressed uncertainty, particularly around specific drug effects on milk production and lactation. Notably, the study affirms the central role of healthcare providers as trusted sources of information, yet highlights how knowledge gaps can lead to unnecessary treatment avoidance or discontinuation.

Decision-making around medicine use in breastfeeding is complex and influenced by multiple factors, including drug characteristics. As outlined by Sachs, appropriate decision-making requires evaluating the clinical need for treatment, drug excretion into breast milk, neonatal oral absorption, potential adverse effects on the infant, and the drug’s impact on lactation. These factors are rarely detailed in standard drug labeling, and our findings support prior work by Tantray et al. [16], indicating that inadequate label information creates ambiguity and hampers informed prescribing.

Partner involvement occurred as a significant facilitator of medicine adherence, extending beyond financial to emotional and practical support. This supports prior research by Zeng et al., which shows that emotional and practical support enhance treatment continuity [17]. However, fears of judgment, stigma, or infant harm can lead to non-disclosure and non-adherence, especially among women living with HIV. Transparent communication among mothers, partners, and healthcare providers is therefore crucial, not only to build trust but also to promote shared decision-making, a finding reinforced by Mey et al. [18].

Barriers to safe medicine use during lactation included unclear or absent labeling, limited access to current guidance, and inadequate pharmacovigilance. Participants emphasized the ethical dilemma of excluding breastfeeding mothers from research due to perceived risks, even as this exclusion contributes to the persistent evidence gap. A stepwise approach to trial inclusion, especially for drugs lacking safety data, is warranted as recommended by Fairlie et al. [19]. These perspectives support growing calls for greater inclusion of breastfeeding women in clinical trials [20], to generate robust pharmacokinetic and safety data needed for informed prescribing.

The study also highlighted the need for ongoing health education tailored to breastfeeding women, including translated materials and audiovisual formats. These preferences align with prior findings that multimedia tools enhance understanding and retention of health information [21]. Involving partners and family members in such interventions is essential, as social support is consistently associated with improved adherence and breastfeeding success [22].

Stakeholders emphasized the need for improved post-marketing surveillance. As noted by Kiguba et al., pharmacovigilance in low-resource settings is often constrained by staffing shortages, limited funding, and low awareness [23]. These systemic challenges must be addressed to monitor adverse drug reactions among breastfeeding dyads more effectively. In addition, participants recommended strategies such as selecting drugs with low milk transfer, timing administration to minimize infant exposure, and temporarily suspending breastfeeding when safer alternatives are unavailable, recommendations echoed by Meek and Silveira [24, 25]. Hormonal contraceptives containing estrogen were specifically flagged for their potential to reduce milk supply, aligning with previous literature [15], though some studies report limited effects on breastfeeding duration, such as the review by Kelsey which reported that while combined oral contraceptives (COCs) may reduce milk volume in some women, they do not consistently shorten the overall duration of breastfeeding [26].

This study contributes to the growing body of evidence advocating for better provider training, improved access to reliable information, enhanced public health education, and greater research inclusion for breastfeeding women. Addressing these gaps requires coordinated efforts among policymakers, researchers, and clinicians. Ensuring safe and effective medicine use during lactation is not only a clinical imperative but a matter of health equity for women and their infants.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first study in Uganda to explore medicine use during breastfeeding from the perspectives of breastfeeding women, their partners, healthcare providers (HCPs), and regulators, offering a multidimensional understanding of balancing maternal treatment needs and infant safety during lactation. It includes input from HCPs across various specialties involved in prescribing and counseling. A key strength lies in amplifying women's voices regarding medicine use while breastfeeding, an often-overlooked area, while also addressing the significant evidence gaps caused by their exclusion from clinical research. By highlighting the need for inclusive research, the study contributes to global conversations on equitable clinical trial representation. In addition, the study emphasizes the importance of pharmacovigilance and pharmacokinetic research, aligning with international efforts to strengthen drug safety monitoring. These findings support the development of evidence-based prescribing guidelines, inform regulatory policies mandating drug safety evaluations for breastfeeding women, and promote awareness among HCPs and the public about monitoring drug effects in vulnerable populations.

However, the study's findings must be interpreted within the context of Uganda, where breastfeeding is often the only viable option due to limited access to safe and affordable alternatives. This may affect the generalizability of the findings to settings where different infant feeding options are more feasible. Future research should examine similar questions in varied cultural and geographical contexts to broaden applicability. Furthermore, the study's cross-sectional design limits the ability to track changes in knowledge, attitudes, or practices over time. Longitudinal studies are needed to assess how awareness of medicine safety evolves, particularly as clinical guidelines change or new educational interventions are introduced.

Conclusion

This study reveals challenges associated with medicine use during breastfeeding, including barriers to access, limited safety data, and variability in healthcare provider knowledge. Although breastfeeding women consider healthcare providers their most trusted source of information, gaps in provider training and inconsistent communication contribute to poor adherence and treatment avoidance. Addressing these issues requires targeted educational programs for healthcare providers focused on lactation pharmacology, integration of breastfeeding safety data into national formularies, and routine access to evidence-based resources such as LactMed.

The inclusion of breastfeeding women in clinical trials is essential to close existing data gaps and inform prescribing practices. Moreover, engaging partners in decision-making and delivering community-based health education through culturally tailored methods such as audiovisual materials in local languages can enhance support and improve both medication adherence and breastfeeding outcomes. These findings call for coordinated policy action to ensure safer medicine use among breastfeeding populations, including the establishment of national guidelines, strengthened pharmacovigilance systems, and investment in inclusive research infrastructure.
