Diagnostic accuracy in BRAF V600E-negative papillary thyroid cancer via 5-gene expression profiling of residual nucleic acids from mutation testing: a retrospective study
Authors: Liang Wang, Wu Mao, Hua Deng, Wei-ping Zhou, Liang Zhang, Jie Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBRAF V600E mutation is insufficient for accurate diagnosis of thyroid neoplasms, although it has been widely used in clinical settings. We aimed to construct a messenger RNA (mRNA)-based model to identify thyroid cancer in indeterminate thyroid nodules in the absence of BRAF mutation.MethodsOur cohort consisted of 88 papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) patients with BRAF V600E substitution, 103 BRAF mutation negative PTC cases, and 96 subjects with benign thyroid nodules. Based on the mRNA levels of MET proto-oncogene, receptor tyrosine kinase (MET), TIMP metallopeptidase inhibitor 1 (TIMP1), transforming growth factor alpha (TGFA), Cbp/p300 interacting transactivator with Glu/Asp rich carboxy-terminal domain 1 (CITED1), and fibronectin 1 (FN1) in thyroid specimens, model building was conducted to develop a gene expression-based classifier to differentiate benign from malignant thyroid nodules. In addition, external validation was performed using the thyroid carcinoma (THCA) dataset from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA).ResultsDataset-based bioinformatic analysis confirmed the elevated expression levels of these five genes in PTC compared to normal thyroid tissue. Overall, the binary classified model correctly identified 84 of 88 BRAF-mutated PTC and 75 of 103 BRAF mutation negative PTC as malignant tumors, respectively. For PTC carrying BRAF V600E mutation, the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and area under the curve (AUC) were 0.955, 0.969, 0.966, 0.959 and 0.987, respectively. More importantly, the diagnostic performance of the approach in determining PTC without BRAF mutation was presented as the sensitivity of 0.728, specificity of 0.969, PPV of 0.962, NPV of 0.769, and AUC of 0.871.ConclusionWe developed and validated a gene expression classifier to assist in the diagnosis of BRAF V600E-negative PTC cases. This tool is poised to become a useful adjunct to individualized management of thyroid cancer in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15818-5.Keywords: Papillary thyroid cancer, Indeterminate thyroid nodules, BRAF mutation, Gene expression classifier

Introduction

In recent decades, thyroid cancer has emerged as the most common endocrine malignancy worldwide. Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) represents the predominant histological subtype, comprising 80%–90% of all new cases [1]. Although PTC is generally indolent and associated with a favorable 10-year survival rate (> 95%) [2], its rapidly increasing incidence has raised significant public health concerns and imposed a growing medical burden [3]. Thyroid nodules are recognized as important precursors of thyroid cancer, and ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration biopsy (FNAB) followed by cytopathological examination remains the gold standard for evaluating thyroid neoplasia [4]. However, in up to 30% of cases, FNAB yields indeterminate results due to insufficient sampling or cytological ambiguity [5]. This diagnostic limitation significantly hampers clinical decision-making in the era of precision medicine.

Conventional molecular diagnostic approaches have primarily focused on detecting gene mutations and rearrangements, such as point mutations in BRAF and NRAS, as well as RET rearrangements. Nevertheless, approximately one-third of thyroid cancers lack these genetic alterations, which may lead to false-negative results [6]. To improve detection sensitivity, high-throughput techniques enabling simultaneous screening of multiple genes have been introduced, albeit with substantially increased costs [7]. In recent years, gene expression profiling–based tools have been applied to PTC diagnosis [8]; a notable example is the Afirma Gene Expression Classifier (GEC; Veracyte), which employs machine learning to analyze the mRNA expression patterns of 167 genes [5] and has become the most widely used commercial molecular test in this setting [9]. More recently, cancer epigenetics—particularly DNA methylation in promoter regions—has gained prominence as a promising research field with strong potential for improving differential diagnosis of human tumors [10, 11]. For instance, hypermethylation of Ras association domain family member 1 (RASSF1A) has been shown to aid in diagnosing follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC) [12]. Genome-wide methylation microarrays have also demonstrated efficacy in distinguishing benign thyroid nodules from malignant ones [13], offering a new avenue for molecular diagnosis. However, these advanced methodologies remain costly and are therefore not yet suitable for routine preoperative screening.

In the present study, we employed quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) to measure mRNA expression levels of candidate genes in thyroid biopsy specimens for the differential diagnosis of thyroid lesions. Nucleic acids remaining after routine BRAF mutation testing of FNAB samples were repurposed for gene expression analysis. This approach may help overcome the limitations of current BRAF-only molecular testing and improve the diagnostic accuracy for indeterminate thyroid nodules.

Materials and methods

Study subjects and sample collection

The overall research framework is shown in Fig. 1. A total of 367 eligible patients who underwent FNAB and thyroid surgery at the People’s Liberation Army General Hospital from September 2024 to February 2025. Retrospective analysis was conducted to collect relevant information such as cytological analysis, postoperative pathological examination, and mutation status of BRAF V600E. Final histopathology upon surgical resection by experienced endocrine pathologists acted as the gold standard to determine benignity or malignancy. For patients with thyroid nodules classified as intermediate to high risk (Chinese TI-RADS IV-V), ultrasound-guided FNAB was performed, which represents a one-to-one correspondence between patients and samples. A dedicated portion of the obtained cells or rinse fluid was used for nucleic acid isolation. Total nucleic acid was extracted with an RNase-free, magnetic bead-based centrifugation column method (AmoyDx Kit, #20160115, Xiamen, China). Subsequently, the extracted nucleic acid was analyzed for the BRAF V600E mutation using the AmoyDx BRAF V600E Mutations Detection Kit (#20143401824, Xiamen, China), a qualitative real-time PCR assay designed to detect V600E mutation in the human BRAF gene. The remaining nucleic acid samples were cryopreserved at -20 °C in the pathology department for 1–2 months. After mutation detection, the remnant nucleic acid was used for mRNA analysis. This study was approved by the PLA General Hospital Human Ethics Committee (No. S2025-398-01), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before surgical resection.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

The remaining nucleic acid that had previously been extracted from FNAB tissues used for BRAF V660E mutation detection was subjected to qRT-PCR testing (Guangdong Bright-Innovation BioMed Co., Ltd., Shunde, China). Gene selection was performed through a literature review and comprehensive analyses, resulting in an initial panel of 9 genes. Four of these genes—serpin family A member 1 (SERPINA1), glutaminyl-peptide cyclotransferase (QPCT), keratin 19 (KRT19), and dual specificity phosphatase 3 (DUSP3)—were subsequently excluded after evaluation revealed their poor accuracy in discriminating between histologically confirmed malignant PTC specimens and benign biopsy samples. The final 5-gene signature, comprising MET, TIMP1, TGFA, CITED1, and FN1, served as the focus for the ensuing analyses. In this assay, primers and probes designed to quantify the mRNA expression levels of these five genes were obtained from a commercial kit (Bright-Innovation BioMed Co., Ltd, China). To prevent genomic DNA amplification, all primers were designed to span introns. It was confirmed by control experiments showing no amplification under Taq-only conditions (without reverse transcriptase), while successful amplification occurred in reactions containing reverse transcriptase. In brief, the primers and probes of each target gene, as well as the internal reference gene, HPRT1, were mixed in a tube for the amplification reaction. All assays were run on a SLAN-96P quantitative instrument (Shanghai Hongshi Medical Technology Co., Ltd, China) and the experiments were conducted in duplicate.

Model building

Nowadays, effectively distinguishing BRAF-unmutated PTC from benign thyroid lesions in clinical practice remains challenging. Given that BRAF V600E positivity offers robust molecular evidence for diagnosing PTC with high specificity and diagnostic accuracy, we selected BRAF V600E-positive PTC cases for model construction, with benign lesions serving as the comparator group. We constructed a binary algorithm based on the 5-gene expression panel including MET, TIMP1, TGFA, CITED1, and FN1. First, around 70% of the data was divided into the training set (62T vs. 68 N) and the remaining 30% (26T vs. 28 N) as the testing set using the Caret package for R. Based on the samples of confirmed thyroid cancer carrying BRAF mutation and benign thyroid nodules in training dataset, logistic regression was conducted using the generalized linear model (GLM) package in R, meanwhile, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed via R pROC, the area under the curve (AUC) and Youden’s index were calculated to generate an Expression Score [14]. Afterwards, we used the selected algorithmic classification to check its performance in the testing dataset as well as in 103 PTC samples that were negative for BRAF V600E mutation. In addition, 5-fold cross-validation, calibration analysis, and decision curve analysis were conducted to assess the potential clinical net benefit. For performance evaluation, logistic regression was compared with other established methods, including penalized regression, random forest, and gradient boosting.

Dataset-based external validation

An external validation was conducted using the thyroid carcinoma (THCA) RNA-Seq database from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). This cohort comprises 513 tumor samples (including 8 metastatic and 505 primary tumors, of which 417 were diagnosed as papillary adenocarcinoma) and 59 normal samples. Genomics data commons (GDC) transcript-per-million (TPM) expression data along with phenotype annotations were downloaded via the Xena platform [15]. Expression values were provided as log₂(TPM+1). For each sample, the relative expression of each target gene was calculated by subtracting the expression value of the reference gene HPRT1 from that of the target gene. Sample group assignments (thyroid cancer vs. normal control) were based on the phenotype metadata. A Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to determine whether the mean expression of each gene differed significantly between the two groups.

Statistical analysis

All continuous variables were initially assessed for normality of distribution. Student’s t test, Mann-Whitney U test, and Pearson χ2 test or Fisher exact test were used for normally distributed variables, variables with a skewed distribution, and categorical variables, as appropriate. The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated to evaluate the performance of the algorithm in diagnosing PTC. R software and GraphPad Prism (version 9.5) software were used to construct the classifier and plot the ROC/AUC. All data analyses were 2-tailed, and a P value of less than 0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of the included subjects

Of the initial 367 subjects, 80 were excluded because their samples (recycled nucleic acids) yielded insufficient RNA quantity for gene expression profiling, as defined by a Ct value > 34 for the internal reference gene HPRT1. Consequently, the final analysis included 287 patients. Patient features and lesion characteristics of the cohort, including sex, age, lesion size, TI-RADS stage, and presence or absence of BRAF V600E mutation, are outlined in Table 1. Based on medical documents including cytology, postoperative histopathology, and genotyping for BRAF V600E, 96 subjects with benign nodules and 191 patients with pathologically confirmed classical PTC were selected for further analysis. Among the 191 cases of PTC, 88 were identified as harboring the BRAF V600E mutation, while the remaining 103 cases were wild-type for the BRAF mutation. Compared to males, more females suffered from thyroid diseases, especially for PTC (ratios of female/male: 68/28 = 2.43, 61/27 = 2.26, 79/26 = 3.04). Generally, the subjects were in their forties. Notably, the youngest PTC patient was only 14 years old. In the TI-RADS classification, TI-RADS category V constituted the predominant type, with nearly 70% (196/287, 68.3%) of patients having thyroid nodules measuring less than 1 cm in diameter.

Elevated expression of target genes in PTC relative to normal thyroid tissue

To mitigate potential overfitting of the internally developed model and to assess its biological relevance and clinical applicability, an independent validation was performed utilizing the RNA-seq dataset from the TCGA-THCA project. Bioinformatic analysis confirmed the significant upregulation of all five signature genes in PTC samples compared to normal thyroid tissues (Fig. 2A). This distinct expression pattern, combined with an AUC of 0.950 (Fig. 2B), underscores the panel’s robust potential for discriminating between malignant and benign thyroid lesions. Together, these external validation results substantiate the real-world generalizability and reinforce the potential clinical utility of the 5-gene panel-based classifier.

Furthermore, we conducted 5-fold cross-validation, in which the logistic regression model exhibited robust performance on the test dataset, attaining an AUC of 0.97 (sFig. 1). Calibration analysis (sFig. 2) and decision curve analysis (sFig. 3) were employed to assess its potential clinical net benefit. Comparisons between logistic regression and other approaches—such as penalized regression, random forest, and gradient boosting—confirmed that the observed performance was not attributable to model-specific artifacts.

Expression pattern algorithm-based identification of PTC with and without BRAF V600E mutation

Furthermore, the aggregate cohort of PTC carrying BRAF mutation (88T) and benign thyroid nodules (96 N) was subjected to the analysis using the same diagnostic algorithm (Table 2). The gene expression classifier correctly identified 84 of the 88 nodules as malignant tumors. The sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and AUC were 0.955, 0.969, 0.966, 0.959, and 0.987 (95% CI: 0.975–0.999, P < 0.0001), respectively (Fig. 3A).

Diagnostic performance of the 5-mRNA-based model.​ Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that the classifier achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.987 (A) and 0.871 (B) for identifying papillary thyroid cancer with and without BRAF V600E mutation, respectively

Eventually, the model was applied to identify PTC that did not harbor V600E mutation in the BRAF gene (Table 2). It correctly identified 75 of the 103 nodules as malignant lesions. Accordingly, the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and AUC were 0.728, 0.969, 0.962, 0.769, and 0.871 (95% CI: 0.816–0.925, P < 0.0001), respectively (Fig. 3B). As expected, a similar, albeit slightly lower, model performance was observed in BRAF mutation-free PTC cases that are definitely in the minority.

Discussion

Despite PTC usually exhibiting favorable outcomes and satisfactory prognosis, excessive therapeutic intervention remains a pressing issue in clinical settings. To balance therapeutic efficacy with the minimization of unnecessary adverse effects, de-escalation of overtreatment in thyroid neoplasms is imperative. Whereas, it highly relies on the accurate diagnosis of PTC. Although BRAF V600E mutation, the most common and prominent genetic mutation in PTC, has now become a routine tool in molecular diagnosis of thyroid neoplasms, approximately 35% of thyroid cancer cases do not possess any known cancer-associated genetic alterations [12]. The diagnostic limitation of mutation analysis presents a dilemma for healthcare professionals. In this study, we developed a 5-mRNA expression-based classifier capable of discriminating nonmalignant samples from BRAF-mutated PTC and BRAF-unmutated PTC in 95% and 73% of cases, respectively. Our data indicate that this classifier can effectively make up for the limitations of BRAF mutation testing and cover a more comprehensive spectrum of PTC in the diagnosis of PTC via improving diagnostic accuracy and reducing false-negative results. Among BRAF V600E mutation-negative PTC cases, cytology alone can provide a definitive diagnosis for approximately 30% of samples, while the remaining 70% are categorized as cytologically suspicious for PTC. This latter group, however, can be identified with high accuracy (AUC = 0.871, exceeding 80%) using the 5-mRNA-based model. Therefore, for patients with cytologically indeterminate and concurrently tested negative for BRAF mutation, this classifier can guide individualized management and avoid unwarranted diagnostic surgery or overtreatment, thereby optimizing clinical decision-making and eventually promoting precise diagnosis in healthcare.

Of 103 PTC samples tested negative for BRAF V600E substitution, up to 72.8% (75/103) of these undetermined cases were defined as malignant lesions using the 5-gene expression-based model alone, which provides a robust tool in the auxiliary diagnosis of BRAF-unmutated PTC. Intriguingly, two subsets of PTC share similar changes in the expression pattern of selected genes, regardless of BRAF mutation or not. As is well known, thyroid carcinogenesis is a complicated process that involves multiple genes and evolutionary steps. The selected candidates (MET, TIMP1, TGFA, CITED1, and FN1) cover multiple oncogenes and signaling pathways with a driving effect on thyroid tumorigenesis. As described in a review [16], which screened the top 12 targets that exhibited altered gene expression and may be valuable thyroid cancer markers. Among them, MET, TIMP1, and FN1 were on the list. Prior reports revealed that changes in MET expression not only act as diagnostic and prognostic tools for PTC but also correlate with a high risk of metastasis and recurrence for PTC patients [17–19]. For FN1, it plays an essential role in modulating thyroid cell adhesiveness and the growth of cancer cells [20].Whereas, activating mutation in BRAF just represents a single point mutation in a single gene, providing relatively limited information for informed decision-making. In addition to BRAF V600E mutation analysis, other GEC-based approaches have been developed to distinguish benign from malignant nodules. Compared to the gene panels containing dozens or hundreds of candidates, our 5-gene expression classifier possesses the advantages of cost-effectiveness, simplicity, convenience, and accessibility.

Since the primers/probes were designed across introns during mRNA detection in the qRT-PCR, genomic DNA did not affect the detection of mRNA, as confirmed by using the Taq-only method. Therefore, there is no need to use extra biopsy samples to measure gene expression, which would greatly facilitate its application in clinical practice. In terms of cost, the 5-gene expression assay is comparable to that of two RNA virus detection kits. In addition, gene-specific primers were used in this qRT-PCR assay to amplify short amplicons (typically < 120 bp), the amplification efficiency of the endogenous reference genes served as a reliable indicator of initial RNA template integrity, thereby obviating the need for separate prior RNA quality assessment. Notably, 28 of 103 (27.2%) BRAF mutation-free PTC patients were incorrectly diagnosed as benign nodules using this diagnostic algorithm. On the one hand, a small amount of tissues and cells were captured in FNAB samples. On the other hand, the interference of non-cancerous cells, including lymphocytes and inflammatory cells, at least in part, affected the diagnostic efficiency of the model. It is needed to optimize its capacity and improve its performance in clinical settings.

Notably, the majority of patients enrolled in this study were classified as TI-RADS category IV or V. According to the 2025 Chinese Expert Consensus and Practical Guideline for Ultrasound-guided Fine Needle Aspiration Biopsy of Thyroid Nodules and Cervical Lymph Nodes [21], FNAB is recommended for Chinese TI-RADS category V nodules prior to surgery. Moreover, molecular testing is advised for lesions with indeterminate cytology (e.g., Bethesda III–IV) or borderline features to allow a more accurate malignancy risk assessment. Current commercially available assays, such as Afirma and ThyroSeq, have been primarily validated in studies focusing on cytologically indeterminate nodules, especially those categorized as Bethesda III [22]. In contrast, our cohort mainly comprised Bethesda II (benign) and Bethesda VI (malignant) cases, with a particular focus on BRAF-negative PTC, which often pose diagnostic challenges due to their lower cytological discrimination. Thus, a direct comparison of specific performance metrics between our model and existing commercial tests is not feasible.

Limitations in the present study should be acknowledged. First, a relatively small sample size, along with this retrospective study conducted at a single center or location in the Chinese population, may restrict its generalization in practical settings before further validation. Second, over 20% of samples were excluded due to insufficient RNA quantity for gene expression profiling from the recovered nucleic acids. In this study, gene expression analysis was performed using residual nucleic acids extracted for prior BRAF mutation testing. The magnetic bead-based extraction protocol optimized for DNA-based mutation detection lacked pH adjustment in the liquid-phase system to specifically enrich and recover RNA, leading to inadequate RNA yield for mRNA-based assays. To reduce the high sample failure rate, we plan to optimize the total nucleic acid extraction protocol to improve RNA recovery efficiency, thereby enabling nucleic acids from a single biopsy specimen to fulfill both mutation analysis and gene expression profiling requirements simultaneously. Third, as the nucleic acids used herein were derived from residual FNAB samples originally collected for BRAF mutation testing, the enrolled nodules were predominantly ultrasound categories IV–V. Future studies will aim to include a greater proportion of cytologically indeterminate thyroid lesions (e.g., Bethesda III–IV categories).In addition, we are unsure whether the 5-gene pattern presents an optimal combination, especially for various subtypes of PTC, because diverse combinations of selected genes may have different power in classifying specific thyroid neoplasms. Notably, various platforms, for instance, qRT-PCR and RNA-seq, for multiplex mRNA analysis, may impact the performance. Another factor should be considered since usually only FNAB specimens are available in clinical settings. In addition, PTC constitutes 80–90% of all thyroid cancers, while FTC and medullary thyroid cancer (MTC) account for 5–15% and 1–2% of cases, respectively. Compared with PTC, FTC and MTC are associated with a less favorable prognosis. Furthermore, FNAB cytology exhibits limited accuracy in differentiating benign lesions from malignant FTC or MTC. Given that the current classifier demonstrates high efficacy in distinguishing PTC from benign nodules, further studies will focus on improving the diagnostic assessment of FTC and MTC. Here, our pilot study only involved accurate diagnosis; future research in prospective multi-center studies will address other key issues such as the prediction of invasion/metastasis, treatment response, and recurrence hazard of thyroid cancers [23].

Conclusion

In summary, our findings indicate that the 5-gene expression classifier may serve as a useful adjunct in the molecular diagnosis of thyroid neoplasms. The assay provided diagnostic value complementary to BRAF mutation analysis, particularly for nodules with indeterminate cytology. This strategy should be of clinical significance and translational potential in facilitating the preoperative diagnosis of PTC and reducing unnecessary surgeries.
