Variations and Evolution in Balloon-Based Leaflet Modification
Authors: Kwong-Yue Eric Chan, Ho-On Alston Conrad Chiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Coronary obstruction is a well-documented life-threatening complication during valve-in-valve transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), with a higher reported incidence when compared to TAVR in native valves (2.5% vs 0.4%).1 This increased risk primarily stems from the anatomical configuration of the surgical valve, which is frequently sewn in a supra-annular position, placing the prosthetic valve closer to the coronary ostium. Displaced leaflets may block the coronary ostia once a new transcatheter heart valve (THV) is implanted or when the frame of the new THV sequesters the aortic sinuses. The risk of sinus sequestration is often higher during TAV-in-TAV procedures, due to a higher “neoskirt” consequent to the supra-annular leaflet position and tall stent frames.2

With increasing use of leaflet-modification procedures, including bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA) and undermining iatrogenic coronary obstruction with radiofrequency needle (UNICORN), globally, variations exist regarding the technical steps.3,4 In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Ruberti et al5 reported a case of bilateral leaflet modification during valve-in-valve TAVR using double balloon-based leaflet modification technique with over-sized balloons, followed by deployment of a self-expanding valve (SEV) platform, resulting in procedural success. As techniques advance and new variations emerge, one should understand the foundational steps and subtleties of the classic UNICORN.3 As with all procedural techniques, there is always room for refinement to balance safety, efficiency, and generalizability.

The UNICORN Is Here, How Do We Evolve?

Recrossing technique in self-expanding THVs

In contrast to balloon-expandable valves (BEVs), SEVs do not generate sufficient opening or radial force to achieve complete leaflet laceration upon benchtop testing. This may result in valve underexpansion and potential hemodynamic compromise prior to definitive postdilatation. The “recrossing” technique, which entails traversing the leaflet fenestration with a guidewire followed by the advancement of a peripheral balloon via a contralateral 7F femoral (or radial) access, has been described.6

Differing from upfront leaflet laceration with an oversized balloon, the “recrossing technique,” that adopts a side-by-side approach, involves simultaneous delivery of both the SEV catheter and a peripheral balloon into the leaflet fenestration (Figure 1A). Operators typically deploy the SEV often on a short-frame index THV or surgical prosthetic valve that aligns with the inflow of the index valve. Once the metal frame of the SEV inflow reaches early annular contact, the peripheral balloon is inflated under controlled pacing to effectuate leaflet laceration. The deflated peripheral balloon, or alternatively the guidewire alone, serves as a barrier to prevent leaflet recoil during SEV deployment or repositioning and can be readily withdrawn following full annular contact at the inflow prior to complete THV release. Through simultaneous laceration and valve deployment, the lacerated leaflet is displaced laterally and away from the jeopardized coronary ostium. In addition, the “recrossing” technique potentially mitigates intraprocedural AR severity by occupying the leaflet fenestration with both the SEV delivery catheter and a peripheral balloon.

UNICORN with ultra-low-profile balloon-expandable THVs

During the classic UNICORN procedure, a 10-mm peripheral balloon is often used to facilitate passage of a BEV through the leaflet fenestration during the UNICORN procedure.3,7,8 Nonetheless, dilatation of fenestration created on a degenerated leaflet may cause complete leaflet tear, which attenuates the intended benefits of the classic UNICORN approach—namely, simultaneous leaflet laceration, controlled valve deployment, and effective leaflet entrapment. Conversely, if the leaflet recoils after balloon inflation, it may be difficult to advance the BEV across the recoiled fenestration. As such, additional balloon dilatation or leaflet laceration with a larger balloon may be necessary.

Benchtop studies that determine the optimal balloon size during UNICORN procedures in redo TAVR are ongoing. However, the ideal choice is affected by a wide range of variables, including the site of leaflet traversal, the extent of leaflet degeneration, as well as the specific sizes and combinations of TAVR devices involved. In principle, a smaller THV profile enables easier intraleaflet positioning and lowers the risk of full leaflet laceration. Ultra-low-profile BEVs with an 8F delivery system have been evaluated and reported in early feasibility study.9 In that regard, should such BEV platforms be used for UNICORN procedures, a much smaller peripheral balloon, potentially in the 6- to 8-mm range, could be used for the initial leaflet modification theoretically (Figure 1B). This improvement may help minimize aortic regurgitation severity and premature leaflet tear.

Intravascular ultrasound evaluation and orthotopic stenting in case of upfront leaflet laceration

Despite successful leaflet modification, leaflet prolapse may still potentially result in delayed coronary artery obstruction.10,11 Nevertheless, subsequent orthotopic stenting, deployed across the aortic root and coronary ostium through the THV frame, enables physiological stent positioning and is considered a marker of procedural success in BASILICA (Figure 1C).10,11 Following successful leaflet modification, the mechanism underlying coronary artery obstruction transitions from complete sinus sequestration to residual leaflet prolapse into the coronary ostium. The latter scenario permits coronary blood flow, allowing time for coronary wiring through the THV frame, intravascular ultrasound evaluation, and orthotopic stenting, when indicated, to mitigate delayed ischemia. Reports indicate favorable outcomes with this approach.10,11 Understandably, in certain variations involving modified UNICORN techniques, some operators have opted to lacerate the leaflet upfront to enhance procedural efficiency.12 However, as with BASILICA, maximal sinus clearance may not be achievable compared with the classic UNICORN when such approach is adopted. Therefore, comprehensive assessment with multimodality imaging, including intravascular ultrasound, and orthotopic stenting when necessary are critical to abolish the residual risk of coronary obstruction.13

Conclusions

The field of balloon-based leaflet modification continues to evolve, prompting important considerations about how to achieve consistent procedural success. Rather than adopting a fundamentalist stance, we focus on innovative methods to improve safety and broader applicability as we pursue “the perfect UNICORN.”
