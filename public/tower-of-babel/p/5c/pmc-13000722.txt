An alkaline nanosized platelet vesicle-based hydrogel for the treatment of osteoporotic bone defects
Authors: Ke Yang, Yang Yang, Chen Xia, Yiyu Chen, Zhanqiu Dai, Zijie Wang, Lintao Hu, Yijun Li, Yongcheng Wang, Yining Wang, Wenshuai Li, Qingqing Wang, Na Li, Shuqi Jiang, Shouyan Zu, Feng Hao, Qichen Shen, Shunwu Fan, Pengfei Chen, Liqing Shangguan, Lei Ning
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteoporosis is a systemic bone disease in which excessive differentiation of osteoclasts leads to reduced bone mass, weakened bone microstructure, and increased fragility. These overactive osteoclasts require low lysosomal pH to function. Therefore, alkalinizing lysosome represents a potential therapeutic strategy for osteoporotic bone defects. Recently, platelet-derived nanovesicles have shown promise in tissue repair and drug delivery. Here, we developed nanosized platelet vesicles (NPVs) loaded with sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3@NPVs) for the repair of osteoporotic bone defects. These vesicles were encapsulated in an extracellular matrix hydrogel (NaHCO3@NPVs-EH) made from fibrinogen and thrombin. When applied to bone defects, the hydrogel releases NaHCO3@NPVs at the defect site. After being internalized by bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs), NaHCO3@NPVs can inhibit osteoclast differentiation and activity through a “biochemical dual-action” mechanism. On the one hand, NaHCO3 can effectively alkalinize lysosomes, promote lysosomal calcium depletion, eliminate receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL)-induced intracellular calcium oscillations. On the other hand, the bioactive factors contained in NPVs can directly or indirectly exert an inhibitory effect on osteoclasts. In vivo experiments in a rat osteoporotic bone defect model revealed that the NaHCO3@NPVs-EH reduced the number of overactive osteoclasts and enhanced bone repair. These findings suggest that NaHCO3@NPVs-EH could be a promising tool for the treatment of osteoporotic bone defects. This study also provides a new perspective on acid-base neutralization for the treatment of osteoporosis.

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a systemic metabolic bone disease caused by an imbalanced bone remodeling, in which osteoclast-mediated bone resorption outpaced osteoblast-mediated bone formation [1,2] and results in reduced bone mass, a weakened bone structure, and increased bone fragility [3]. These changes increase the risk of fractures in osteoporosis patients and impair their bone healing ability [4]. As reported, the global prevalence of osteoporosis reached 19.7% in 2021, while osteopenia — the osteoporosis condition — affects up to 40.4% of the population [5]. The primary clinical feature of osteoporosis is fragility fractures, which are leading causes of disability and mortality among elderly people. Notably, following osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs), approximately 15% to 35% of patients develop persistent nonunion (e.g., Kummell's disease) or pseudarthrosis, resulting in intractable pain and kyphotic deformity [6]. Currently, most treatments including traditional drugs and novel biomaterials for osteoporotic bone defects are still confined to the preclinical phase of research with the limitations such as drug toxicity, poor biocompatibility, and immune rejection [7,8]. Therefore, it is urgent to develop new therapies for osteoporotic bone defects to overcome existing challenges [9].

Osteoclasts play a vital role in bone healing. After the inflammatory phase of fracture, osteoclasts are recruited to smaller bone fragments to remove burrs, and help absorb the cartilaginous callus, forming the woven bone. Subsequently, osteoblasts replace the callus with hard bone and remodel woven bone into lamellar bone [10]. Under osteoporotic conditions, oestrogen deficiency leads to overactivation of osteoclasts. This overactivation impacts bone healing in several ways. First, increased bone resorption occurs due to excessive differentiation and activation of osteoclasts, which may overwhelm the bone formation capacity of osteoblasts, thereby delaying the bone healing process [11]. Second, bone formation reduces as the osteoblast function and differentiation are suppressed by specific factors such as semaphorin 4D (Sema4D) and sclerostin secreted from osteoclasts [12,13]. Third, decreased secretion of pro-osteogenic factors, such as platelet-derived growth factor-BB (PDGF-BB) by osteoclast precursors impairs angiogenesis and bone formation [14]. Fourth, the pH of the bone microenvironment lowers by excessive osteoclast activation negatively affects osteoblast differentiation and bone healing [[15], [16], [17]].

Lysosomes play critical biological roles in cells with the function of degradation of intracellular and extracellular substrates, regulation of growth in response to nutrients, and participation in secretion processes, and immune functions [18,19]. Furthermore, osteoclast lysosomes are indispensable throughout the bone resorption process. On the one hand, vacuolar-type ATPase (V-ATPase) is transported to the osteoclast membrane, where it secretes acidic substances into the extracellular space, dissolving inorganic minerals in the bone matrix. On the other hand, lysosomes secrete functional enzymes, such as cathepsin K (CTSK), into the extracellular space, where they become active in the acidic environment and degrade the organic components of the bone matrix [[20], [21], [22]]. These processes, which are essential for bone matrix degradation, depend on the acidic lysosomal pH. Additionally, a mutation (R740S) in the V-ATPase a3 subunit of osteoclasts increases lysosomal pH, leading to impaired nuclear factor of activated T-cells, cytoplasmic 1 (NFATc1) signalling and nuclear translocation, which inhibits osteoclast differentiation in vitro [23]. In conclusion, we believe that lysosomal pH is crucial for osteoclast differentiation and function. Alkalinization of lysosomes has the potential to inhibit osteoclast activity and promote bone healing in osteoporosis.

Recent studies have shown that platelet-derived nanovesicles behave like platelets but without the excessive release of inflammatory factors [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. These nanovesicles have potential for promoting angiogenesis, supporting tissue repair, and mediating drug delivery [[28], [29], [30]]. Some synthetic nanoparticles administered in vivo can be disseminated throughout the body via the circulatory system and accumulate in major organs, potentially leading to toxicity, as previously reported [[31], [32], [33]]. However, platelet-derived nanovesicles can be derived from autologous sources, ensuring favourable biocompatibility and safety. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that platelet-derived nanovesicles could be used as vehicles for the delivery of alkaline substances, thereby inhibiting overactive osteoclasts and promoting osteoporotic bone defect repair.

In our study, we constructed nanosized platelet vesicles (NPVs) loaded with sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3@NPVs). We encapsulated NaHCO3@NPVs in an extracellular matrix (ECM) hydrogel (NaHCO3@NPVs-EH) made of fibrinogen and thrombin, both of which are FDA-approved [24], and applied the resulting hydrogel for the repair of osteoporotic bone defects. Materials and methods

Preparation and characterization of NaHCO3@NPVs

The collection and use of blood samples in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital at Zhejiang University School of Medicine, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Whole blood was collected from healthy donors and combined with acid citrate dextrose solution A (ACD-A) as an anticoagulant at a ratio of 1 mL ACD-A to 9 mL of blood. Following centrifugation at 100×g for 15 min, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) was isolated. The PRP was then subjected to a second centrifugation step at 800×g for 20 min. The supernatant was discarded, leaving behind the platelet pellet. 12 mL of whole blood yielded approximately 6 mL of plasma. The platelet concentration, determined using a haemocytometer, was approximately (6.20 ± 0.12) × 1011 platelets/L. To generate NaHCO3@NPVs, platelets were resuspended in 2 mL isotonic solution of sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3, 150 mM) containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA, 5 mM) and prostaglandin E1 (PGE1, 1 μM). This mixture was subjected to noncontact sonication at 30% power for 15 min, with a cycle of 3 s on and 2 s off. Following sonication, the solution was filtered sequentially through a 450 nm filter and a 220 nm filter to yield NaHCO3@NPVs. Polyethersulfone (PES) filters were used to ensure low protein-binding affinity and high recovery rates. To remove unencapsulated NaHCO3, the NaHCO3@NPVs were dialyzed in an 8 kDa‒14 kDa dialysis bag, with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) as the dialysate for 24 h. After dialysis, we obtained approximately 1.5 mL of NaHCO3@NPV solution at a protein concentration of 1 mg/mL. The concentration of protein was quantified using a Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) assay kit. Nanoflow cytometry revealed a vesicle concentration of approximately (5.67 ± 0.30) × 1012 particles/L. Excluding losses during the preparation process, one platelet yields approximately 9–10 NaHCO3@NPVs, with the entire process taking approximately 30 h.

The morphology and size of both the platelets and NaHCO3@NPVs were examined via cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM) at 200 kV (FEI Tecnai G2 F20, USA). Particle size and zeta potential measurements were conducted via dynamic light scattering (DLS). The stability of the NaHCO3@NPVs was assessed via DLS by tracking changes in particle size at 0, 2, 4, and 6 days. To measure the internal pH of the NaHCO3@NPVs, fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), the absorbance at 495 nm of which is stronger in alkaline environments, was used. Both NaCl-FITC@NPVs and NaHCO3-FITC@NPVs were also prepared following the same procedure for comparison. Equal amounts of vesicle protein were used for calibration in both groups. The absorbance at 495 nm was measured using a Varioskan LUX microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

For the determination of NaHCO3 loading efficiency and loading yield, NaHCO3@NPVs were prepared according to the previously described method. The NaHCO3@NPVs in PBS solution were subjected to ultracentrifugation at 100,000×g for 2 h at 4 °C. After that, the NaHCO3@NPVs precipitation was collected in saline solution. Isotonic solutions with varying pH values were prepared using KH2PO4, KCl, and K2HPO4 for assessing the stability of NaHCO3@NPVs at different pH. Released NaHCO3 were measured at specific time points (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h) using a bicarbonate content detection kit (Jonln Bio, China) and a sodium (Na+) colorimetric assay kit (Elabscience, China). The unreleased NaHCO3 loaded in the NPVs was quantified after disruption with acetonitrile (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent) and subsequent removal of precipitated protein by centrifugation. The loading efficacy was defined as (mass of loaded NaHCO3/total NaHCO3 input) × 100%. The loading yield was defined as (mass of loaded NaHCO3/mass of NPVs protein) × 100%.

Preparation and characterization of the NaHCO3@NPVs-ECM hydrogel

To create NaHCO3@NPVs-rich plasma, NaHCO3@NPVs were mixed with plasma at a concentration of 50 μg/mL. In vitro, a NaHCO3@NPVs-ECM hydrogel (NaHCO3@NPVs-EH) was prepared by activating fibrinogen with thrombin. Specifically, 50 μL of thrombin (1 mg/mL) was added to 0.5 mL of NaHCO3@NPVs-rich plasma in 24-well plates. After allowing the mixture to stand for 5 min, fibrinogen was activated, resulting in the formation of a gel-like clot. The rheological properties of the NaHCO3@NPVs-EH were assessed using an Advanced Rheological Expansion System (TA Instruments, USA). Stable shear scan analysis was performed to determine the viscosity of the hydrogel, and dynamic frequency sweep analysis was conducted to measure the frequency-dependent storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) of the gel. The mechanical properties of ECM hydrogels were measured using a universal testing machine (Zwick/Roell Z020). For compression testing, the parameters were set as follows: load cell, 500 N; test speed, 1 mm/s; preload, 0.1 N. Cylindrical samples (diameter 36.8 mm, thickness 5 mm) were placed between the compression platens. The upper platen was lowered until it contacted the sample surface, the load was reset to zero, and the compressive modulus was determined according to the standard procedure. For tensile testing, the parameters were set as follows: load cell, 500 N; test speed, 1 mm/s; preload, 0.1 N. Rectangular strip samples (length 10 mm, width and height 5 mm) were mounted on the tensile grips. The load was reset to zero, and the tensile modulus was determined according to the standard procedure. To assess the release profile of NaHCO3@NPVs from the NaHCO3@NPVs-EH, NaHCO3@NPVs-EH were first labelled with DiI and then mixed with plasma, followed by thrombin activation to obtain the NaHCO3@NPVs-EH. The release was monitored over 72 h at 37 °C in PBS by measuring the fluorescence intensity of DiI in the supernatant.

Labelfree proteomics analysis

Protein from the NaHCO3@NPVs or NaCl@NPVs was analysed according to the manufacturer's instructions. Samples were separated on a Vanquish Neo UHPLC nano-LC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile), with a trap-and-elute setup consisting of a PepMap Neo Trap Cartridge (300 × 5 mm, 5 μm) and an Easy-Spray™ PepMap™ Neo UHPLC column (150 × 0.15 mm, 2 μm). The column temperature was maintained at 55 °C, with 200 ng injected at a flow rate of 2.5 μL/min (6.9 min gradient, 8 min run time). DIA analysis was performed on an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) operated in positive ion mode (380-980 m/z, resolution 240,000 at 200 m/z, AGC target 500%, maximum IT 5 ms); MS2 was acquired in DIA mode with 299 windows (2 Th each), HCD collision energy 25%, AGC target 500%, and maximum IT 3 ms. The extraction, detection, and quantitative analysis of protein profiling in the samples were performed by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All proteins identified in the NPVs were employed in subsequent analytical procedures.

In vivo biodegradation assay

All animal studies were performed according to ethical regulations and protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Zhejiang Center of Laboratory Animals. C57BL/6J mice were anaesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg), and the dorsal skin was shaved and disinfected with povidone-iodine. NaHCO3@NPVs-EH was prepared according to the method described above. Sulfo-Cy5.5-NHS (Lumiprobe, Germany) was used to label protein components. We then implanted the NaHCO3@NPVs-EH into the mice's backs and sutured the incisions. Live animal fluorescent imaging (IVIS) was used to track fluorescent signals at 0,1,3,5,7days.

Cell culture

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were extracted using a method previously described in the literature [34]. Fresh bone marrow stromal cells were isolated from 6–8-week-old C57BL/6J mice. Briefly, bone marrow was flushed from the femurs and tibias of the mice. The cells were then cultured in α-modified minimum essential medium (α-MEM, Cienry, China) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, Cellmax, China) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (complete α-MEM). To obtain pure bone marrow stromal cells, nonadherent cells were collected and cultured in complete α-MEM containing 25 ng/mL Macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF, R&D Systems, USA). After 5 days of culture, the adherent cells were used for further experiments.

Bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were isolated from the bone marrow of the femurs and tibiae of 4-week-old C57BL/6 mice. Briefly, the mice were euthanized, and the lower limb bones were harvested. The bone marrow cavity was flushed with a 1 mL syringe to obtain a cell suspension, which was subsequently filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer to remove debris and cell aggregates. The collected cells were cultured in complete DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Non-adherent cells were removed by medium replacement after the initial 3 h and 8 h of incubation. Thereafter, the culture medium was refreshed every two days.

Cell viability assay

The viability of the BMDMs was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) kit (MCE, USA). A total of 1 × 104 BMDMs were seeded into 96-well plates and pretreated with NaCl@NPVs or NaHCO3@NPVs at various concentrations in complete α-MEM for 24 h. Afterwards, 100 μL of serum-free medium supplemented with 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well. The cells were incubated for 3 h at 37 °C. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a Varioskan LUX microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

In vitro osteoclastogenesis assay

BMDMs were seeded into either conventional 96-well plates or specialized plates (1 × 104 cells/well). The cells were stimulated with M-CSF and 50 ng/mL RANKL (R&D Systems, USA), and NaHCO3@NPVs were added to promote osteoclast formation. After 5 days, the culture mixture was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. Osteoclast formation was evaluated by measuring tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) enzyme activity. Each osteoclast must exhibit both TRAP staining and multinuclearity (3 or more nuclei). TRAP activity was quantified using an acid phosphatase cell kit (JoyTech, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

BMDMs were cultured in six-well plates (3 × 105 cells/well) and stimulated with RANKL and either NaHCO3@NPVs or NaCl@NPVs for 5 days. Total RNA was extracted from the cells using an RNA kit (Chigan City, Valencia, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using reverse transcription reagents (Promega, Madison, USA), which converted the RNA into cDNA. RT‒qPCR was carried out using a SYBR Master Mix qPCR kit (Takara). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as an internal reference gene. All procedures followed the manufacturer's instructions. The primers used in the experiment are listed in Table S1.

Western blot analysis

Western blotting was used to evaluate the protein expression of NFATc1 (sc-7294, Santa Cruz), c-Fos (sc-166940, Santa Cruz), CTSK (11239-1-AP, ProteinTech), and β-actin (20536-1-AP, ProteinTech). Cells or cell supernatants were treated with radioimmunoprecipitation (RIPA) lysis buffer to extract proteins. The proteins were separated via sodium dodecyl sulfate‒polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‒PAGE). After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were then blocked with skim milk. After blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight with the primary antibody with gentle shaking. The membranes were then incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) for 1 h at room temperature. Chemiluminescence signals were detected using a chemiluminescent substrate (Beyotime Institute of Biotech, China). Images were captured using an ImageQuant LAS 4000 (GE Healthcare, Silverwater, Australia) and analysed with ImageJ software.

Bone slice resorption assay

To mimic bone resorption in vivo, lyophilized bovine bone slices (Φ6 mm × 0.5 mm) were placed at the bottom of a 96-well plate. The bone slices (JoyTech Bio, 2-0001-10) used in this study were derived from the cortical portion of fresh bovine bone. BMDMs (1 × 104 per well) were seeded onto the surface of the bone slices and incubated for seven days. The cells were treated with or without NaHCO3@NPVs in medium containing RANKL. At the end of the incubation period, the bone slices were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min.

Next, EDTA-trypsin was used to digest the cells. The surface cells were scraped off with a brush. The bone fragments were then sprayed with a gold‒palladium (Au‒Pd) coating and observed using a scanning electron microscope (TM-1000, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunofluoresence staining

BMDMs were seeded in confocal imaging dishes and incubated with DiO-labelled NaHCO3@NPVs for 2 h at 37 °C. Following incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min at room temperature. For immunofluorescence characterization, the BMDMs were stained with an anti-F4/80 antibody (ab6640, Abcam) to delineate the macrophage membrane, the F-actin was stained with phalloidin (Yeason, China), followed by nuclear counterstaining with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). The intracellular localization of the nanoparticles was subsequently observed and captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM).

ALP staining in vitro

BMSCs were seeded into the lower chambers of 12-well Transwell plates (Corning, USA) at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. To establish the co-culture system, ECM hydrogel, NaCl@NPVs-EH, or NaHCO3@NPVs-EH were placed into the corresponding upper inserts. After 24 h of incubation, the culture medium was replaced with an osteogenic induction medium containing 10−8 M dexamethasone, 10 mM β-glycerol phosphate, and 50 mg/L ascorbic acid (all from Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Following 7 days of induction, the cells were fixed and stained using a BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Color Development Kit (CWBIO, China) for 10 min. For quantification, ALP activity was measured using an ALP assay kit (Beyotime, China), and the optical density (OD) was recorded at 405 nm.

Alizarin red S (ARS) staining in vitro

BMSCs were seeded and co-cultured with the respective materials under the same conditions as described for the ALP assay. After 24 h, the medium was transitioned to osteogenic induction medium. Following 14 days of culture, the cells were fixed and stained with a 1% ARS solution (Beyotime, China) at room temperature. To quantify the mineralized nodules, the stain was eluted with 10% cetylpyridinium chloride (CPC, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 30 min. The absorbance was subsequently measured at 562 nm.

Transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the BMDMs, BMDMs + RANKL, and BMDMs + RANKL + NaHCO3@NPVs groups (1 × 106 cells per well) using TRIzol reagent (Accurate Bio, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The RNA purity and concentration were measured using a Nanodrop2000 microspectrophotometer. RNA integrity was assessed with the LabChip GX touch system, and eukaryotic mRNA was enriched with oligo(dT) magnetic beads. The mRNA was fragmented, and cDNA synthesis was carried out using random primers and DNA polymerase I. After purification, the cDNA was repaired, poly(A) tails were added, and ligation with sequencing adapters was performed, followed by PCR amplification to generate libraries. The library concentration was quantified with the Qubit 3.0 system, and sequencing was performed via the SBS method to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Genes with P values < 0.05 and absolute log2 (fold change) ≥ 1 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Colocalization experiment

BMDMs were seeded into confocal dishes (1 × 105 cells per dish) and cultured for 24 h to allow cell adherence. The cells were then incubated with DiI-labelled NaHCO3@NPVs (Beyotime, China) for 2 h. After the medium was removed, the cells were washed three times with PBS. Lysosomes were labelled with LysoTracker Green DND-26 (L7526, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (Beyotime, China) for 30 min at 37 °C. The cells were washed three times with PBS and observed using a highly sensitive live-cell super-resolution microscope (HIS-SIM, CSR Biotech, China). Colocalization analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Measurement of lysosomal pH using LysoSensor green DND-189

BMDMs were seeded in 6-well plates at 3 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 24 h to allow the cells to adhere. The cells were then stimulated with RANKL and treated with or without NaHCO3@NPVs for 5 days. After digestion with EDTA-trypsin, the cells were collected by centrifugation at 300×g for 5 min and were stained with a 1 μM concentration of the LysoSensor green DND-189 fluorescent probe (L7535, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 20 min at 37 °C. The mean fluorescence intensity was measured using the B525-FITC channel on a flow cytometer, and the data were analysed with FlowJo software.

Measurement of lysosomal pH using LysoSensor yellow/blue DND-160

BMDMs were seeded in light-protected 96-well plates (1 × 106 cells per well) and cultured for 24 h to ensure cell adherence. The cells were then stimulated with RANKL and treated with or without NaHCO3@NPVs for 5 days. Before the lysosomal pH was measured, calibration buffers were prepared. These buffers contained 20 mM 2-(N-morpholino) ethanesulfonic acid (MES), 110 mM KCl, and 20 mM NaCl were adjusted to pH values of 4.5, 5.0, 5.5, and 6.0 using HCl/NaOH. The buffers were also supplemented with 10 μM monensin and 10 μM nigericin, which were diluted 1:500 from their 5 mM stock solutions into the pH-adjusted buffers. Next, the culture medium was replaced with 50 μL of prewarmed (37 °C) medium containing 1 μM LysoSensor Yellow/Blue DND-16 (L7545, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and the cells were incubated for 5 min. For the standard curve group, after the cells were washed twice with PBS, they were washed once with the calibration buffer with the appropriate pH and incubated with 50 μL of the same buffer for at least 5 min. For the experimental group, the cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with 50 μL of PBS. The fluorescence intensities were measured using a microplate reader with excitation wavelengths of 329 nm and 384 nm and emission wavelengths of 440 nm and 540 nm. A standard curve was generated on the basis of the obtained data (Fig. S4B), and the lysosomal pH of the target cells was calculated.

Calcium oscillations

BMDMs were seeded in confocal dishes (1 × 105 cells per dish) and cultured for 24 h to allow the cells to adhere. The cells were then incubated with or without NaHCO3@NPVs for 2 h. BafA1 (1 μM) was used as a positive control. The cells were incubated with Fluo-4 AM (Beyotime, China) at 37 °C for 30 min. Afterwards, the cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with assay buffer for 15 min at room temperature. Fluorescence confocal microscopy was used to observe the cells. The fluorescence intensity of Fluo-4 in individual cells was measured at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm over 5 min, with a 5-s intermeasurement interval. The average fluorescence intensity over the first 30 s was recorded as the baseline. Then, RANKL diluted in assay buffer was added at a final concentration of 50 ng/mL, and the fluorescence intensity was measured post-stimulation. Peak analysis was performed using ImageJ to calculate relevant parameters. All procedures followed the manufacturer's instructions.

Construction of a rat osteoporotic bone defect model

All animal procedures followed the guidelines of the Ministry of Science and Technology of China for laboratory animal care and were approved by the Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of the Zhejiang Laboratory Animal Center. Twelve-week-old female Sprague‒Dawley rats (250–300 g) were anaesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (40 mg/kg) and underwent ovariectomy (OVX). The rats were maintained for 12 weeks to establish an osteoporotic model. Afterwards, all the rats were anaesthetized again, and the distal femur was exposed. Using continuous saline irrigation, a bone defect was created by drilling a cylindrical defect (3 mm in diameter and 3 mm in depth) perpendicular to the bone surface from the inside out at the medial condyle of the distal femur with a low-speed spherical burr. The rats were randomly assigned to four groups: (1) defects treated with PBS (control group), (2) defects filled with ECM hydrogel (ECM hydrogel group), (3) defects filled with NaCl@NPVs-EH (NaCl@NPVs-EH group), and (4) defects filled with NaHCO3@NPVs-EH (NaHCO3@NPVs-EH group). After surgery, the incisions were sutured layer by layer under sterile conditions. The defects were injected twice weekly with the following: platelet-poor plasma for the ECM hydrogel group, 50 mg/mL NaCl@NPVs for the NaCl@NPVs-EH group, and 50 μg/mL NaHCO3@NPVs for the NaHCO3@NPVs-EH group. At 4 and 8 weeks, the rats were euthanized, and the femurs were collected to assess the in vivo therapeutic effects of NaHCO3@NPVs-EH.

Micro-CT analysis

At 4 and 8 weeks, the rats were euthanized, and their femurs were collected. The femurs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. They were then scanned using a micro-CT scanner (Skyscan 1176, Bruker, USA) with a pixel size of 11 μm and a pixel metre of 25 mm. The images obtained were reconstructed using NRecon software (Bruker micro-CT, Kontich, Belgium). A 3 mm region of interest (ROI) was generated at the defect site on the bone surface. A constant threshold of 80-255 was applied. Parameters such as bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular number (Tb.N) and lower trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) were analysed using the program CTAn (Bruker micro-CT, Kontich, Belgium).

Bone histomorphometry analysis

After micro-CT analysis, the bone tissues were decalcified in 14% EDTA (Sigma‒Aldrich, Germany) at 37 °C for 30 days. The samples were then embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson's trichrome, and TRAP. After TRAP staining, the sections were washed/eluted and then counterstained with DAPI to label cell nuclei. Both TRAP staining and multinuclearity (3 or more nuclei) need to be present for each osteoclast. Immunofluorescence staining for Type I Collagen (B03043R, Haokebio) and Osteocalcin (ab93876, Abcam) was performed on bone tissue sections to evaluate osteogenic capacity. Additionally, CD68 (B03014R, HaoKebio) and LAMP1 (DF4806, Affinity) were co-stained to assess the lysosomal mass of macrophages.

In vivo toxicity assay

Venous blood was collected from the eyes of the rats before they were sacrificed. The serum was then separated by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 10 min. Liver and kidney function were assessed using serum indicators. After the mice were sacrificed, the major organs (the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. The organs were then paraffin-embedded and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Pathological changes in these tissues were observed to assess systemic toxicity.

Statistical analysis

The quantitative data are presented as the means ± standard deviations. For comparisons between two unpaired groups, Student's t-test was used. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. Dunnett's test was applied when groups were compared against a single control, whereas Tukey's test was used for multiple group comparisons. Statistical significance was set as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.005, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.001.

Preparation and characterization of the NaHCO3@NPVs-ECM hydrogel

After nanonization, the NaHCO3@NPVs were approximately 100–200 nm in size (Fig. 1B). We used dynamic light scattering (DLS) to measure the particle size and zeta potential of the platelets and NaHCO3@NPVs. We used fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), a pH-sensitive fluorescent reagent that shows stronger absorbance under alkaline conditions [35], to assess the alkaline environment within the NaHCO3@NPVs. We encapsulated NaCl-FITC and NaHCO3-FITC in NPVs. The absorbance of the NaHCO3-FITC@NPVs was significantly higher than that of the NaCl-FITC@NPVs, confirming the alkaline environment within the NaHCO3@NPVs (Fig. 1F). The loading efficacy and release curve under various pH conditions were determined via colorimetry, and as shown in Fig. S1A, the loading efficacy was calculated to be 9.15 ± 0.42%, the loading yield was 13.27 ± 0.08%, and the release rate of NaHCO3 was highest in an acidic environment (pH 5.0), with more than 60% of the cargo released within 12 h. At neutral pH (7.0), the release kinetics followed a trend similar to that of the acidic condition, reaching a cumulative release of more than 60% after approximately 24 h. In contrast, the NaHCO3@NPVs exhibited the greatest stability and slowest release rate under alkaline conditions (pH 9.0), where more than 50% of the NaHCO3 remained encapsulated after 72 h (Fig. 1G). This phenomenon could be attributed to the combined action of two underlying mechanisms. First, in acidic environments, protons react with NaHCO3 to produce CO2 bubbles, causing physical rupture of the vesicles and subsequent burst release [15]. Additionally, the pH-dependent denaturation of surface proteins on the NPVs may compromise membrane stability, further influencing the NaHCO3 release rate [36].

We next examined the additional contents of NaCl@NPVs and NaHCO3@NPVs. First, Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining was performed on proteins from platelets, NaCl@NPVs, and NaHCO3@NPVs, which showed that both vesicle types retained some platelet-derived proteins (Fig. 1H). We then performed proteomic analysis. In total, 4990 proteins were identified in NaCl@NPVs, whereas 5027 proteins were identified in NaHCO3@NPVs. Of these, 4923 proteins were common to both groups, demonstrating a high degree of homogeneity between the two types of vesicles (Fig. 1I). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of cell component of the 5094 proteins identified in both groups revealed that most of the proteins in NPVs originated from the cytoplasm and membranes, with some derived from exosomes and mitochondria (Fig. 1J).

Next, we investigated how different concentrations of NaCl@NPVs and NaHCO3@NPVs affected the activity of BMDMs using the CCK8 assay. The results revealed that 200 μg/mL NaCl@NPVs and 100 and 200 μg/mL NaHCO3@NPVs significantly promoted BMDMs proliferation (Fig. 1K). To avoid any inhibitory effects of the drug on cell proliferation, we used 50 μg/mL NPVs in the following cell experiments.

We then characterized the properties of the NaHCO3@NPVs-ECM hydrogel. After thrombin was added, the plasma containing NaHCO3@NPVs quickly solidified into a gel (Fig. 1L). We also measured the rheological properties of the NaHCO3@NPVs-ECM hydrogel. The results revealed that the rheological behaviour of the hydrogel containing NaHCO3@NPVs was similar to that of the hydrogel without NaHCO3@NPVs. Both hydrogels exhibited shear-thinning behaviour, as their viscosity decreased with increasing shear rate (Fig. 1M). In assessing the rheological performance of the materials, the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) provide critical insights into their viscoelastic behaviour. To evaluate these properties, the G'/G″ ratios were compared for the ECM hydrogel and the NaHCO3@NPVs-ECM hydrogel across a frequency range of 10−1 to 101 rad/s (Fig. S1B). The results indicated that the pure ECM hydrogel consistently maintained a G'/G″ ratio above 1, confirming its stable elastic nature throughout the tested range. In contrast, while the NaHCO3@NPVs-ECM hydrogel exhibited elastic behaviour at lower frequencies, the G'/G″ ratio decreased below 1 at shear frequencies exceeding 3.59 rad/s, suggesting a transition towards viscosity-dominant behaviour at higher frequencies (Fig. S1B). We attribute this shift to the introduction of the NaHCO3@NPVs suspension, which reduced the portion of fibrinogen in the autologous plasma, thereby impairing the elastic network formation of the resulting hydrogel. We then evaluated the mechanical properties of the ECM hydrogel. The compressive modulus was approximately 0.61 kPa, and the tensile strength was approximately 8.07 kPa (Fig. S1C and D). Finally, we examined the release profile of NaHCO3@NPVs from the ECM hydrogel. We found that NaHCO3@NPVs were continuously released over a period of 72 h at 37 °C (Fig. 1N). In addition, we conducted a subcutaneous degradation test of the hydrogel, demonstrating that the ECM hydrogel was almost completely degraded in vivo within approximately one week, regardless of whether it was encapsulated with NaHCO3@NPVs (Fig. S1E and F). These results indicate that the ECM hydrogel possesses a sustained-release capacity for vesicles within 72 h and may serve as a promising delivery system for the treatment of bone defects.

NaHCO3@NPVs inhibit osteoclast differentiation and function in vitro

To determine whether NaHCO3@NPVs can inhibit osteoclast differentiation and function in vitro, we first utilized confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) to evaluate the internalization of NaHCO3@NPVs. The macrophages (F4/80+, red) were incubated with DiO-labelled NaHCO3@NPVs (green) for 2 h. Confocal imaging demonstrated substantial uptake of NaHCO3@NPVs by the macrophages within this timeframe (Fig. S2A). Then BMDMs were stimulated with RANKL and simultaneously treated with NaCl@NPVs or NaHCO3@NPVs. After 5 days of stimulation, TRAP staining revealed that the OCs (osteoclasts, BMDMs stimulated with RANKL for 5 days) group displayed many multinucleated, TRAP-positive osteoclasts (Fig. 2A and B). The addition of NaCl@NPVs increased the number of osteoclasts but significantly reduced the TRAP-staining area in the field of view. These results suggested that NaCl@NPVs may inhibit osteoclast fusion. In contrast, the addition of NaHCO3@NPVs reduced both the number and the area of multinucleated TRAP-positive osteoclasts, which indicated that NaHCO3@NPVs inhibited both osteoclast differentiation and function. To clarify the inhibitory effect of NaCl@NPVs on osteoclasts, we reanalysed our previous proteomic data. The analysis of biological process and molecular function categories indicated enrichment in processes such as cell migration, positive regulation of apoptosis, calcium ion binding, and ATP hydrolysis activity, which are related to osteoclast differentiation (Fig. S2B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment further revealed the significant involvement of NaCl@NPV-related proteins in pathways associated with osteoclast differentiation, including the osteoclast differentiation pathway and the NF-κB signalling pathway (Fig. S2C). We screened for proteins enriched in the KEGG pathway related to osteoclast differentiation and identified several proteins with confirmed or potential inhibitory effects on this process, including cylindromatosis (CYLD), interferon gamma (IFN-γ), superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2), protein kinase amp-activated catalytic subunit alpha 1 (PRKAA1), and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) [[37], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44]]. These proteins were detected in both NaCl@NPVs and NaHCO3@NPVs (Fig. S2D). We then analysed osteoclast-related gene expression and found that the expression of genes related to osteoclast differentiation and function (Nfatc1, C-fos, Atp6v0d2, Ctsk, Acp-5, and Mmp9) was significantly upregulated in the OCs group (Fig. 2C). However, the expression of these genes was downregulated in the NaHCO3@NPVs groups, which suggested that NaHCO3@NPVs had a stronger inhibitory effect on osteoclast differentiation and function. Bone resorption assays further supported these findings. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that the NaHCO3@NPVs group had a significantly reduced area of bone resorption (Fig. 2D and E). In comparison, the osteoclast and NaCl@NPVs groups presented more acid-etched defects and enzymatic digestion of the bone tissue. Western blot analysis also confirmed these results (Fig. 2F and G). Compared with that in the osteoclast group, the abundance of osteoclast-related proteins (NFATc1, c-Fos, and CTSK) in the NaHCO3@NPVs group was significantly lowered. Furthermore, we visualized the organization of the F-actin cytoskeleton via phalloidin staining (Fig. 2H). Upon RANKL induction, the control (OCs) group developed large, multinucleated cells featuring well-defined peripheral actin rings—a definitive morphological hallmark of mature, functional osteoclasts. In contrast, the NaHCO3@NPVs-treated group exhibited disrupted actin ring formation, with cells failing to assemble intact circumferential structures. Overall, these results demonstrate that NaHCO3@NPVs can significantly inhibit osteoclast differentiation and function in vitro.

To clarify the role of the ECM hydrogel and ensure that the observed effects were driven by the nanoparticles, we performed a Transwell-based rescue experiment. BMDMs were seeded in the lower chamber, while either the ECM hydrogel alone or the NaHCO3@NPVs-EH was placed in the upper chamber. TRAP staining revealed that BMDMs cocultured with the ECM hydrogel alone underwent robust osteoclastogenesis upon RANKL induction, as evidenced by the presence of numerous TRAP-positive multinucleated cells (Fig. S2E and F) and the elevated expression of osteoclast-specific markers (Fig. S2G). In contrast, the NaHCO3@NPVs-EH group exhibited a significant reduction in TRAP-positive cells and suppressed marker gene expression. These findings demonstrate that the ECM hydrogel itself lacks intrinsic inhibitory effects on osteoclast differentiation; rather, the therapeutic efficacy of NaHCO3@NPVs-EH is mediated primarily by the sustained release of NaHCO3@NPVs from the ECM hydrogel.

Considering the vital role of osteoblasts (OBs) in bone healing, we evaluated the effects of NaHCO3@NPVs-EH on their differentiation. After 7 days of osteogenic induction, all the groups exhibited robust and comparable ALP staining, similar to that of the control group (Fig. S3A). While ALP activity was slightly greater in the NaHCO3@NPVs and NaHCO3@NPVs-EH groups, the differences lacked statistical significance (Fig. S3B). This subtle increase may stem from the mildly alkaline microenvironment created by extracellularly released NaHCO3, which is known to favour osteogenesis [45,46]. However, the limited release rendered this effect statistically negligible. On day 21, Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining and subsequent CPC quantification revealed extensive but similar mineralization levels across all cohorts (Fig. S3C and D). These findings demonstrate that NaHCO3@NPVs-EH has no significant effect on osteoblast differentiation, confirming its therapeutic selectivity towards hyperactivated osteoclasts under osteoporotic conditions.

We then used LysoSensor Green to detect the lysosomal pH of BMDMs and osteoclasts [47]. By measuring the lysosomal pH at various stages of osteoclast differentiation (days 0, 1, 3, and 5), we found that the lysosomal pH decreases as BMDMs differentiate into osteoclasts, reaching its lowest point on day 3 and slightly increasing by day 5, although it is still greater than that of BMDMs on day 0 (Fig. S4A). We then tested whether NaHCO3@NPVs could alkalinize lysosomes. The results revealed that the addition of NaHCO3@NPVs significantly increased the lysosomal pH, whereas the addition of NaCl@NPVs did not significantly change the lysosomal pH (Fig. 4B). To further confirm this effect, we quantified the lysosomal pH using the LysoSensor yellow/blue probe. In BMDMs, the lysosomal pH was approximately 5.60. After differentiation into osteoclasts, this value decreased to 5.28. The addition of NaCl@NPVs slightly increased the pH to 5.37, but there was no significant change. However, adding NaHCO3@NPVs significantly increased the lysosomal pH to 5.65 (Fig. 4C). These results confirmed that NaHCO3@NPVs effectively alkalize the lysosomes.

Transcriptome sequencing suggested that NaHCO3@NPVs might regulate lysosomal acidity and intracellular calcium signalling in osteoclasts (Fig. 3F). A study revealed that increased lysosomal pH could induce abnormal lysosomal calcium efflux and increase cytoplasmic calcium [48]. Osteoclast differentiation depends on RANKL, which binds to RANK expressed on the surface of osteoclast precursors and triggers intracellular calcium oscillations. These oscillations activate NFATc1, leading to the expression of downstream transcription factors and proteins [[49], [50], [51]]. Depletion of lysosomal calcium prevents RANKL-induced calcium oscillations and the formation of mature osteoclasts [52]. We further examined whether NaHCO3@NPVs interfered with the formation of mature osteoclasts by eliminating calcium oscillations. The Fluo-4 AM probe was used to measure intracellular calcium levels in BMDMs. Changes in real-time fluorescence intensity were observed under a confocal microscope. First, we recorded the baseline fluorescence intensity over 30 s. At the 30-s mark, we added RANKL and monitored changes in fluorescence to assess its impact on calcium oscillations [50]. Control BMDMs showed strong calcium oscillations after RANKL stimulation. Adding NaCl@NPVs displayed negligible impact. However, the addition of NaHCO3@NPVs or bafilomycin A1 (BafA1, a v-ATPase inhibitor) significantly reduced calcium oscillations (Fig. 4D and E). Fluorescence images of individual cells confirmed this effect (Fig. 4F). Overall, these findings confirm that NaHCO3@NPVs alkalize lysosomes and inhibit intracellular calcium oscillations, which ultimately affects osteoclast differentiation and function (Fig. 4G).

In vivo therapeutic effects of NaHCO3@NPVs-EH

To evaluate the in vivo effects of NaHCO3@NPVs, we established an osteoporosis model via ovariectomy in female Sprague‒Dawley rats. After 12 weeks, we evaluated the BMD and trabecular parameters of rats in the sham-operated group as well as the OVX group using micro-CT to verify the success of the osteoporosis model construction (Fig. S5A and B). Next, we created a bone defect in the medial condyle of the distal femur, which served as an osteoporotic bone defect model and was subjected to different treatments (Fig. 5A). Micro-CT scans at 4 weeks postsurgery revealed that the NaHCO3@NPVs-EH treatment group had more new bone formation than the control group did (Fig. 5B). Compared with the control group, the NaHCO3@NPVs-EH group presented greater bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular number (Tb.N) and lower trabecular separation (Tb.Sp). In contrast, the ECM hydrogel and NaCl@NPVs-EH groups presented weaker bone repair ability (Fig. 5C). We then performed histological analysis. Masson staining revealed increased collagen fibre formation at the defect site, with a well-organized and dense arrangement (Fig. 5D). To verify whether the suppression of osteoclast differentiation by NaHCO3@NPVs-EH in vivo was mediated by lysosomal alkalinization, we observed and characterized the lysosomal abundance of bone marrow macrophages from a rat model. Previous studies have shown that lysosomal alkalinization disrupts the transmembrane proton gradient, thereby inhibiting the activation of mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase complex 1 (mTORC1). This inhibition prevents transcription factor EB (TFEB) phosphorylation and promotes its nuclear translocation, ultimately driving lysosomal biogenesis [[53], [54], [55], [56]]. On the basis of this mechanism, we assessed lysosomal abundance within macrophages surrounding the bone defect using CD68 (macrophage marker) and lysosomal-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1, lysosomal marker) immunofluorescence staining. The results revealed a significant increase in the lysosomal content of macrophages following NaHCO3@NPVs-EH treatment (Fig. 5G and H). These findings indirectly suggest that upon internalization by macrophages, NaHCO3@NPVs releases NaHCO3, which increases the lysosomal pH and consequently triggers intracellular lysosomal biogenesis and results in the inhibition of osteoclast differentiation.

We further characterized osteogenic maturation using specific markers. Collagen Type I (Col1), the major component of the bone matrix, was abundantly detected in the NaHCO3@NPVs-EH group and organized into well-defined structures (Fig. S6A and B). Furthermore, the expression of osteocalcin (OCN), a late-stage marker of osteoblastic differentiation, was significantly upregulated in the treatment group compared with that in the control group (Fig. S6C and D). This concomitant upregulation of Col1 and OCN suggests that NaHCO3@NPVs-EH accelerated the entire osteogenic process, from matrix deposition to mineralization.

We then analysed femur samples at 8 weeks post-surgery to investigate the efficacy of NaHCO3@NPVs-EH in promoting osteoporotic bone regeneration. Micro-CT and quantitative analysis revealed that the differences between the groups were smaller than they were at 4 weeks. Discussion

In healthy physiological conditions, osteoclasts and osteoblasts work together to maintain bone homeostasis during the healing process. During bone healing, osteoclasts resorb the cartilaginous callus and woven bone. Moreover, osteoblasts gradually replace the callus with hard bone and remodel woven bone into more structured lamellar bone, completing the healing process [57]. However, under osteoporotic conditions, osteoclasts become overactivated. This disrupts the balance between osteoclasts and osteoblasts, leading to excessive bone resorption and insufficient bone formation. Thus, promoting bone healing under osteoporotic conditions requires the restoration of this balance [[58], [59], [60]].

Current clinical strategies for managing osteoporosis primarily rely on antiresorptive agents, such as bisphosphonates and anti-RANK ligand (RANKL) monoclonal antibodies (e.g., denosumab) [61]. However, these drugs may induce systemic side effects such as gastrointestinal toxicity or osteonecrosis of the jaw [62,63]. In recent years, given that acid secretion is indispensable for osteoclastic function, numerous studies have explored local extracellular alkalization as a strategy to suppress hyperactive osteoclasts and mitigate osteoporosis [15,64,65]. However, the extracellular delivery of alkaline agents presents significant limitations. First, to effectively counteract the complex extracellular milieu, high concentrations of alkaline substances (e.g., 300 mM or 1 M NaHCO3) are typically needed, which can trigger localized pH spikes that jeopardize the viability of surrounding cells. Second, extracellular alkalization has a minimal influence on intracellular signalling cascades, potentially allowing for osteoclasts to maintain basal activity or initiate early-stage differentiation. In contrast, NaHCO3@NPVs bypass these issues through a specialized intracellular release mechanism, offering a safer and more effective approach to osteoclast inhibition.

Considering that osteoclast differentiation and function highly rely on lysosomal activity [66], lysosomal alkalinization represents an effective way to inhibit osteoclast function. In this study, we propose a strategy to treat osteoporotic bone defects by alkalinizing lysosomes to inhibit overactivated osteoclasts. We used NPVs to deliver NaHCO3 to lysosomes, with the ECM hydrogel serving as a scaffold for local application. The source materials for NPVs and ECM hydrogel—platelet-rich plasma (PRP)—can be entirely autologous, which reduces the risk of immune rejection and aids in clinical translation [67]. The NaHCO3@NPVs-EH developed here could release NaHCO3@NPVs locally at the defect site. After being taken up, NaHCO3@NPVs entered lysosomes. Then, NaHCO3 was released into the lysosomal lumen as the NPV membrane was digested by lysosomal enzymes, increasing the lysosomal pH. Alkalinization of the lysosomes depleted the calcium in the lysosomal lumen and prevented calcium oscillations in response to RANKL stimulation [48]. These processes then suppressed NFATc1 expression and inhibited the differentiation and function of osteoclasts under osteoporotic conditions.

Furthermore, during our study, we observed that NaHCO3@NPVs suppressed osteoclast differentiation and function through a “biochemical dual-action” mechanism. Specifically, NaHCO3 was delivered into the cell via NPVs and entered the lysosome. NaHCO3-mediated lysosomal alkalinization significantly inhibited osteoclastogenesis. This represents the chemical aspect of the “biochemical dual-action”. Furthermore, we discovered that NPVs without NaHCO3 loading (NaCl@NPVs) also exhibited an inhibitory effect on osteoclasts. Proteomic profiling revealed that NPVs were enriched in various bioactive factors, including CYLD, IFN-γ, SOD2, PRKAA1, and TGF-β, which are known or potential inhibitors of osteoclast differentiation [[37], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43], [44]], which represents the biological aspect of the “biochemical dual-action”. Notably, after NaHCO3 loading, NaHCO3@NPVs had a more pronounced inhibitory effect on osteoclasts. This dual action of NaHCO3@NPVs allows for the efficient suppression of osteoclasts.

Previous studies on platelet-derived nanovesicles have focused mainly on natural platelet-derived extracellular vesicles (PEVs) [30,68] or platelet membranes [69,70], research on NPVs obtained via artificial extrusion is limited. Compared to PEVs, NPVs offer distinct advantages in terms of higher production yield and a broader spectrum of drug-loading capacities. Furthermore, unlike simple platelet membranes, NPVs are enriched with a diverse array of bioactive factors, which possess the potential to enhance cellular proliferation and promote endothelial cell differentiation [71,72]. We used NPVs to encapsulate NaHCO3 and to validate their effects on osteoclasts, opening the door for future research on NPVs as drug delivery vehicles.

While this study demonstrated the therapeutic potential of NaHCO3@NPVs, it has several limitations. This study focused mainly on the ability of lysosomal alkalinization to reduce lysosomal calcium levels. However, the underlying mechanism was not explored in detail. Previous research has shown that transient receptor potential mucolipin 1 (TRPML1) is a key calcium channel on lysosomes. Knocking out this protein blocks RANKL-induced calcium oscillations in osteoclasts [52]. Therefore, lysosomal alkalinization may affect the function of TRPML1 or alter Ca2+-H+ exchange, leading to increased calcium transport.

Our findings demonstrate that NaHCO3@NPVs-EH exerts no significant stimulatory or inhibitory effects on osteoblasts. While the bone defect microenvironment is highly complex and encompasses a diverse array of cell types beyond the osteoblast‒osteoclast axis, other essential populations, such as chondrocytes and endothelial cells, also play pivotal roles in orchestrating the regenerative process. This study did not evaluate the effect of NaHCO3@NPVs on these cells. Since NPVs encapsulate a diverse repertoire of bioactive factors, they may also potentiate endothelial cell differentiation, thereby indirectly increasing osteogenesis through the promotion of angiogenesis [[71], [72], [73]].

Besides, this study was conducted in vivo using a rat model of osteoporotic bone defects, as the ECM hydrogel scaffold employed in our experiments exhibits some limitations. First, the mechanical properties of the ECM hydrogel are relatively poor, restricting its use to nonload-bearing bone defects. While its malleability is advantageous for irregular geometries, future research will focus on enhancing its mechanical properties through mineralization or the incorporation of advanced nanofillers. Second, NaHCO3@NPVs were rapidly released from the ECM hydrogel, which may narrow the therapeutic window. To achieve more precise temporal control and sustained-release capacity, we aim to optimize the release kinetics by increasing the cross-linking density or integrating stimuli-responsive motifs. These advancements are expected to enhance the system's clinical utility and facilitate the translational potential of the material.

Additionally, our 8-week rat model demonstrated accelerated local bone healing, it may not fully capture the long-term systemic complexity of osteoporosis. Despite localized suppression of osteoclastic overactivity, the persistent osteoporotic state remains a risk for chronic bone loss or reinjury in the later stages. Thus, while this study provides a promising strategy for accelerating fracture repair in osteoporotic patients, addressing systemic bone mass recovery remains an ongoing challenge. Future research should prioritize the development of systemic therapeutic enhancements and the use of large animal models (e.g., sheep or pigs) with extended observation periods (6 – 12 months) to rigorously validate the long-term stability and systemic safety of this platform prior to clinical translation.

Although there are still several limitations, these issues point to important directions for future research. They also provide a foundation for advancing the clinical application of autologous-derived biomaterials.

Conclusion

In this study, we confirmed that osteoclast differentiation is associated with a decrease in lysosomal pH. The NaHCO3@NPVs effectively alkalized lysosomes, eliminated RANKL-induced intracellular calcium oscillations, and inhibited osteoclast differentiation and activity. The NaHCO3@NPVs-based hydrogel accelerated the healing of osteoporotic bone defects in in vivo models. In summary, we propose a potential strategy for treating osteoporotic bone defects and provide new insights into acid‒base neutralization for the treatment of osteoporosis.
