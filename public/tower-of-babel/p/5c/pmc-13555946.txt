Lead exposure and risk of ADHD and conduct disorders in children: evidence from a retrospective cohort in Philadelphia, PA
Authors: Xuewen Pei, Katarzyna Kordas, Meng Wang, Muwu Xu, Jing Nie, Amy Hequembourg, Adil I Khan, John Turella, Marina Oktapodas Feiler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood lead exposure remains a significant public health concern, particularly in urban settings with persistent socioeconomic disadvantage. Although prior studies have linked lead exposure to adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes, evidence using clinically diagnosed neurobehavioral disorders at contemporary low blood lead levels (BLLs) is limited.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of children aged 11 years or younger who received care through the Temple University Health System in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, between 2010 and 2020. BLLs were obtained from electronic health records and analyzed as continuous measures and dichotomized at 3.5 µg/dL. Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and conduct disorders were identified using international classification diagnoses (ICD) codes, and only incident diagnoses occurring after blood lead measurement were considered. Multivariable logistic regression models were fit adjusted for individual- and neighborhood-level sociodemographic factors selected using a directed acyclic graph framework. Sex-stratified analyses were conducted to assess effect modification.ResultsAmong 13,989 children included in the ADHD analysis, 751 (5.4%) were diagnosed with ADHD. Among 14,098 children included in the conduct disorder analysis, 247 (1.8%) were diagnosed with a conduct disorder. Higher log10-transformed continuous BLLs were associated with increased odds of ADHD diagnosis (adjusted OR 1.51; 95% CI 1.37, 1.67). Children with BLLs ≥ 3.5 µg/dL had higher odds of ADHD (adjusted OR 1.84; 95% CI 1.56, 2.16) compared with those with BLLs < 3.5 µg/dL, with stronger associations observed among males than females. Associations between log10-transformed continuous BLLs and conduct disorders were positive but not statistically significant (adjusted OR 1.12; 95% CI 0.95, 1.31), and no sex-specific associations were observed.ConclusionChildhood lead exposure was associated with increased risk of ADHD in this urban clinical population, including at blood lead levels at or above the current reference value set by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, emphasizing the importance of continued prevention efforts. These findings underscore the ongoing neurodevelopmental risks of low-level lead exposure and highlight the disproportionate burden placed on children residing in socioeconomically disadvantaged urban communities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12940-026-01318-3.Keywords: Blood lead level, ADHD, Conduct disorders, Sex-differences, Children

Introduction

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and conduct disorders are among the most common neurobehavioral conditions of childhood, affecting learning, social functioning, and long-term health trajectories [1, 2]. ADHD is more frequently diagnosed in males, with a recent large-scale meta-analysis estimating the global prevalence of ADHD in children and adolescents at approximately 8%, with rates nearly twice as high in boys (10%) compared with girls (5%) [3]. For conduct disorders, an umbrella review of meta-analyses reported a pooled global prevalence of 3.0% (95% CI: 2.0, 5.0%) in children and adolescents, based on random-effects models [4]. Stratified analyses further indicated that the prevalence of conduct disorders was approximately 2.6 times higher in boys than in girls [4]. Beyond differences by sex, a range of biological, social, and environmental factors contribute to the development of ADHD and conduct disorders. Genetic susceptibility plays a major role in ADHD, but genes interact closely with early-life environmental influences [5, 6]. Prenatal and perinatal factors, such as maternal smoking and alcohol consumption during pregnancy, have been consistently associated with a higher risk of ADHD and associated behavioral outcomes among children [7, 8]. In addition, socioeconomic disadvantage can exacerbate or trigger behavioral dysregulation in vulnerable children [9].

Increasingly, environmental toxicants have been recognized as important contributors to the etiology of neurodevelopmental disorders [10, 11]. Lead, a pervasive neurotoxicant with no known safe blood lead level (BLL) in children, has long been associated with adverse cognitive and behavioral outcomes [2, 12, 13]. Even at low BLL, exposure can disrupt brain development and affect neurobehavioral functioning later in life [14]. Such exposures are linked to developmental delays, learning difficulties, and behavioral problems [12]. Children are particularly vulnerable because they absorb lead more efficiently than adults, have ongoing brain development, and engage in frequent hand-to-mouth behaviors that increase ingestion of contaminated dust or paint [15, 16]. In 2021, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) lowered the Blood Lead Reference Value (BLRV) from 5 µg/dL to 3.5 µg/dL, reflecting the distribution of blood lead levels in U.S. children rather than a biological threshold for safety [16]. This change underscores a critical point that no biologic threshold exists below which lead exposure is considered harmless, and even minimal exposure may contribute to the burden of neurobehavioral disorders in children.

Philadelphia, Pennsylvania (PA), provides a critical setting for examining pediatric lead exposure and neurobehavioral outcomes, given its long history of industrial activity, dense urban environment, extreme poverty, older housing stock, and persistent environmental health disparities [17–20]. These conditions contribute to multiple pathways of lead exposure among children, including deteriorating lead-based paint in homes built before the 1978 federal ban, lead-contaminated household dust and soil, drinking water conveyed through aging lead-containing plumbing infrastructure, and take-home exposures associated with parental occupations or hobbies involving lead-containing materials [17, 21, 22]. These exposure sources disproportionately affect socioeconomically disadvantaged communities and contribute to persistent disparities in childhood lead burden [19, 21]. Consequently, Philadelphia represents a particularly relevant setting for investigating the role of lead exposure in the etiology of ADHD and conduct disorder in urban pediatric populations. Importantly, although national surveillance data demonstrate substantial declines in childhood BLLs over recent decades, children living in many U.S. cities continue to face elevated risks of lead exposure and poisoning [21].

The aim of this study was to investigate the association of childhood BLL and subsequent risk of ADHD and conduct disorders in an urban, clinical cohort of children aged 11 years or less who received health care through the Temple University Health System (TUHS) in Philadelphia, PA, between 2010 and 2020. This age range was selected because ADHD typically manifests in early to middle childhood, and conduct problems often emerge before adolescence and co-occur with ADHD [23, 24]. In addition, early and middle childhood represent a critical developmental window of heightened neurotoxic vulnerability [10, 15, 25]. By leveraging electronic health record data linked with laboratory measures, this study seeks to provide new evidence on the role of childhood lead exposure in the development of neurobehavioral disorders in an urban pediatric population.

Methods

Study population and health data

We utilized data from a pediatric clinical cohort established through the TUHS in Philadelphia, PA. Eligible participants were children aged 11 years or less who had at least one outpatient health care encounter at TUHS between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2020, and at least one laboratory-measured BLL recorded in their electronic health records (EHRs), approximately 92% of the total TUHS patient pediatric population had a measured lead value (N = 14,217). The EHR dataset included child-level demographic characteristics (age, sex, race, and ethnicity), insurance type, International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) diagnosis codes, and residential address information recorded at the time of clinical encounters, which was used to derive neighborhood characteristics. Although the study period spanned 2010–2020, all ICD-9 diagnosis codes recorded prior to the U.S. transition to ICD-10-CM in 2015 were converted to ICD-10 codes within the TUHS EHR system. Therefore, ICD-10 codes were available and used consistently throughout the entire study period.

ADHD and conduct disorder diagnoses were identified using ICD-10 codes (F90.0, F90.1, F90.2, F90.8, F90.9 for ADHD; F91.1, F91.2, F91.3, F91.8, F91.9 for conduct disorders). The age at first diagnosis was determined using the earliest relevant ICD-10 code recorded, first diagnosis must have occurred after a blood lead value in the EHR to ensure temporality. Children without diagnostic codes for these conditions were considered non-cases. ADHD and conduct disorder diagnoses were not required to occur in isolation, and co-occurring neurodevelopmental, behavioral, or psychiatric diagnoses were not considered exclusion criteria. Accordingly, ADHD and conduct disorder diagnoses were not mutually exclusive.

Exposure assessment

Children in this sample had their blood drawn at TUHS and sent for blood lead screening to either one of the following locations: Quest Diagnostics, or LabCorp. Details about these laboratory processes have been previously described [26].

In brief, Quest Diagnostics and LabCorp utilize the venous whole blood lead test measured using inductively coupled plasma/mass spectrometry (ICP/MS) or atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS). The minimum sample volume for this test is 0.5 mL collected in a lead-free tan-top, EDTA specimen collection tube. These tests are categorized as lab developed tests, are not FDA approved, but meet Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) requirements and are permitted for use in clinical settings. Both laboratories had a reportable range of 1–100 µg/dL and a limit of quantification (LOQ) of 1 µg/dL.

If the blood lead concentration was below the LOQ, it was reported by the lab as < 1.0 µg/dL. Blood lead values were parameterized as continuous and categorical variables, with categorization based on the 3.5 µg/dL cut-off defined by the CDC to reflect clinical and policy significance. If a child’s medical file listed multiple blood lead values from different testing dates, the highest or peak lead value was used in the analyses [26].

Covariates

Based on the theoretical framework linking lead exposure, sociodemographic factors, and child neurodevelopment, we identified individual- and neighborhood-level covariates that could confound associations between BLL, and neurobehavioral outcomes. Covariates were selected as minimally sufficient adjustment variables under a directed acyclic graph (DAG) framework (Fig. 1) [27]. Individual-level variables, including child age at blood lead testing (months), sex (male and female), race (White, Black, and Other), ethnicity (Hispanic, Non-Hispanic, and Other), and health-insurance type (private and public or self-pay), were obtained from TUHS EHR. The Social Deprivation Index (SDI) at the address recorded in EHR was calculated for each patient, ranging from 0 (least deprivation) to 100 (most deprivation), to represent neighborhood-level socioeconomic disadvantage [26]. The SDI includes seven components representing percent: living in poverty, less than 12 years of education, single-parent households, living in rented housing units, living in the overcrowded housing unit, households without a car, and non-employed adults under 65 years of age [28].

Study design and statistical analysis

A complete-case analysis approach was applied, restricting the analytic sample to participants with non-missing data for exposures, outcomes, and main covariates. We excluded participants whose BLL measurements could not be linked to ADHD or conduct-disorder diagnosis (ICD) code, and those lacking age, race/ethnicity, sex, and insurance type information. The final analytic samples included 13,989 children for ADHD and 14,098 for conduct disorders (see Figure S1).

Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize participant characteristics by ADHD and conduct-disorder status. Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations (SD) and compared using t-tests; categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared using chi-square tests.

Separate logistic regression models were used to estimate crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between BLL and having a diagnosis of either ADHD or conduct-disorder. Because BLLs exhibited a right-skewed distribution, continuous BLLs were log10-transformed to reduce the influence of extreme values and improve model fit. BLLs were modeled as [1] continuous log10-transformed variables and [2] binary variables (< 3.5 µg/dL vs. ≥ 3.5 µg/dL). Adjusted models controlled for child age at lead testing, sex, race, ethnicity, and insurance type. Analyses were also stratified by sex to explore potential effect modification. In addition, multiplicative interaction terms between BLL and sex were included in the regression models to formally assess effect modification by sex.

To evaluate the robustness of findings, sensitivity analyses were performed by restricting the analytic sample to participants with complete data on neighborhood-level socioeconomic (SDI) and full covariate information (N = 10,196 for ADHD; N = 10,233 for conduct-disorder analyses; see Figure S2) and re-estimating adjusted logistic regression models that additionally included SDI.

An additional sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding children with autism spectrum disorder (ICD-10 code F84.0) and other mental, behavioral, and neurodevelopmental disorders (ICD-10 codes F01–F99, excluding ADHD and conduct disorder) from the analytic cohort and re-estimating the logistic regression models.

All statistical analyses were two-sided with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Descriptive statistics

Compared with children without these diagnoses (i.e., non-cases; Tables 2 and 2), children with ADHD or conduct-disorder were more often male (71.6% vs. 64.0%), Black (56.3% vs. 80.2%), and publicly insured (97.9% vs. 98.8%). Mean BLLs were higher among those with ADHD diagnosis than non-cases (3.95 ± 4.03 vs. 3.19 ± 3.61 µg/dL; p < 0.0001) and among those with diagnosed conduct disorders compared with non-cases (3.74 ± 3.77 vs. 3.21 ± 3.61 µg/dL; p = 0.0217). Mean age at BLL testing was approximately 38 months among ADHD cases and 36 months among those with diagnosed conduct disorders. The mean age at diagnosis was approximately 80 months for ADHD and 70 months for conduct disorders.

Logistic regression

In the crude models, higher BLLs were associated with increased odds of both ADHD and conduct disorder diagnosis. Specifically, each log10-unit increase in BLL was related to 45% higher likelihood of ADHD (95% CI 1.33, 1.59) and 35% higher likelihood of conduct disorders (95% CI 1.16, 1.57) (Table 3). After adjustment for covariates (age at lead testing, race, ethnicity, sex, health insurance), the association remained significant for ADHD (adjusted OR 1.51; 95% CI 1.37, 1.67) but attenuated for conduct disorders (adjusted OR 1.12; 95% CI 0.95, 1.31). Similarly, children with BLL ≥ 3.5 µg/dL had 1.84-fold higher odds of ADHD in multivariable models (95% CI 1.56, 2.16) compared with those < 3.5 µg/dL, whereas the corresponding association for conduct disorders was not statistically significant (adjusted OR 0.90; 95% CI 0.69, 1.19).

Sex-stratified analysis

Table 4 revealed stronger associations between each log10-unit increase in BLL and ADHD among males (adjusted OR 1.59; 95% CI 1.42, 1.78) than females (adjusted OR 1.34; 95% CI 1.11, 1.61). Males with BLL ≥ 3.5 µg/dL also higher risk (adjusted OR 1.97; 95% CI 1.63, 2.39) relative to females (adjusted OR 1.54; 95% CI 1.14, 2.09). However, formal tests for interaction between BLL and sex were not statistically significant. For conduct disorders, adjusted associations were not statistically significant in either sex (male: adjusted OR 1.17; 95% CI 0.95, 1.43; female: adjusted OR 1.03; 95% CI 0.78, 1.36).

Sensitivity analysis

After additional adjustment for neighborhood-level socioeconomic status using the SDI, the associations between BLLs and ADHD remained statistically significant and of similar magnitude to the primary analyses (Table 5). In the covariate-adjusted model, each log10-unit increase in BLL was associated with a 45% higher odds of ADHD (95% CI 1.27, 1.65), consistent with the main model. Similarly, children with BLLs ≥ 3.5 µg/dL had 1.58 times the odds of ADHD (95% CI 1.25, 1.99) compared with those below 3.5 µg/dL, representing a modest attenuation relative to the primary analysis. For conduct disorders, the associations remained non-significant in the multivariable models.

Discussion

In this large urban clinical cohort, higher BLLs were associated with an increased risk of ADHD. Children with BLLs ≥ 3.5 µg/dL had higher odds of ADHD compared with those with BLLs < 3.5 µg/dL, and associations between BLLs and ADHD were stronger among males than females. These findings are consistent with prior epidemiologic studies demonstrating associations between low-level childhood lead exposure and ADHD diagnoses, symptoms of inattention and impulsivity, and deficits in executive function [29–31]. In contrast, although higher BLLs were associated with increased odds of conduct disorders, these associations did not reach statistical significance. Notably, children with BLLs ≥ 3.5 µg/dL did not have higher odds of conduct disorders compared with those with BLLs < 3.5 µg/dL. These findings differ from prior longitudinal studies that have linked childhood lead exposure to conduct problems, aggression, and antisocial behavior later in life [32, 33], as well as population-based studies demonstrating associations between higher BLLs and conduct disorder diagnoses and symptom severity in U.S. children [34].

Several biological mechanisms may underlie the observed associations between lead exposure and ADHD. Lead is a well-established neurotoxicant that disrupts synaptogenesis, neurotransmitter signaling, and myelination, particularly within dopaminergic pathways critical for attention regulation, impulse control, and executive functioning [35, 36], providing biological plausibility for its role in ADHD etiology. Social and structural factors likely also play an important role. Lead exposure is strongly patterned by socioeconomic disadvantage, housing quality, and neighborhood disinvestment, and these same factors influence access to health care, diagnostic practices, and developmental supports [37, 38]. The public health implications of these findings may be particularly relevant in urban communities such as Philadelphia, where historical and ongoing environmental inequities contribute to persistent lead exposure risks among children. Interventions targeting environmental sources of lead exposure and addressing structural determinants of health may help reduce the burden of ADHD and other adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in vulnerable populations.

Several factors may explain the weaker and non-significant associations observed for conduct disorders in the present study. First, the relatively young age of the cohort may be important, as conduct disorder and more severe antisocial behaviors often emerge later in childhood or adolescence rather than in early childhood [39]. Second, reliance on clinically diagnosed conduct disorders documented in electronic health records may underestimate subclinical or emerging behavioral problems that are more readily captured using symptom-based instruments [40]. Third, the relatively small number of conduct disorder cases compared with ADHD cases may have limited statistical power to detect modest associations. Together, these factors may contribute to attenuation of observed associations in this clinical pediatric population.

The observed sex differences align with previous evidence suggesting greater susceptibility to neurotoxic effects of lead among males, potentially due to sex-specific neurodevelopmental trajectories and hormonal influences [41]. Lead preferentially disrupts dopaminergic signaling and prefrontal cortical development, pathways critical for attention regulation and impulse control that exhibit sexually dimorphic maturation patterns [35, 36]. Estrogen has demonstrated neuroprotective effects that may partially buffer neurotoxic insults in females, whereas testosterone may enhance oxidative stress and dopamine dysregulation [41]. Although most children in this cohort were prepubertal, early hormonal changes typically begin in late childhood, particularly among older participants, and may contribute to emerging sex-specific vulnerability. Additionally, evidence suggests that ADHD in girls is more likely to be underdiagnosed because symptoms often present differently than in boys, with fewer overt hyperactive or disruptive behaviors, leading to reduced clinical recognition and differential outcome ascertainment [3]. Moreover, sex differences in ADHD prevalence and symptom expression may be socially patterned, with some populations reporting comparable or higher ADHD symptom levels among girls, potentially reflecting cultural expectations regarding behavior [42].

This study has several important strengths. We leveraged a large, urban clinical cohort with laboratory-measured BLLs and clinically diagnosed neurobehavioral outcomes captured through EHRs. Use of peak BLLs allowed us to capture the highest documented exposure during a critical developmental window. Importantly, the study population represents a predominantly socioeconomically disadvantaged and historically underrepresented pediatric population, a group for which evidence on lead-related neurobehavioral outcomes remains limited. The availability of detailed individual-level demographic information and linkage to neighborhood-level social deprivation indices enabled adjustment for key confounding factors. Finally, examining sex-specific associations provided insight into potential differential vulnerability.

Several limitations should be considered. First, although we adjusted for multiple individual- and neighborhood-level indicators of socioeconomic context, residual confounding by unmeasured social and environmental factors cannot be fully excluded. For example, unmeasured housing characteristics (e.g., building age, lead-based paint hazards, and water infrastructure) [43–46], and co-exposures to other neurotoxicants (e.g., secondhand tobacco smoke or other environmental contaminants) [34] may confound associations between lead exposure and neurobehavioral outcomes. However, the study population is relatively homogeneous with respect to socioeconomic disadvantage, with generally high levels of SDI and a high proportion of racially and ethnically minoritized children. As a result, some unmeasured social and environmental factors may be similarly distributed across the cohort, potentially limiting their impact on observed exposure–outcome associations and reducing the likelihood of substantial residual confounding. Second, outcome misclassification is possible for both ADHD and conduct disorders, as diagnoses identified through EHR rely on provider documentation rather than standardized behavioral assessments and may underestimate true prevalence in the population. Additionally, ADHD diagnosis rates have increased over time, likely reflecting changes in awareness, screening practices, and clinical recognition. Because ADHD outcomes were identified using EHR-documented diagnoses, temporal changes in diagnostic practices during the study period may have influenced case ascertainment and contributed to outcome misclassification. Third, although ICD-10 codes permitted identification of ADHD subtypes, the present analysis evaluated ADHD as a single outcome and did not examine subtype-specific associations. Most ADHD diagnoses in the source data were classified as unspecified type, and stratification by subtype would have substantially reduced the number of cases available for analysis in individual subtype groups. Consequently, subtype-specific analyses would have been limited in interpretability and statistical precision. Although lead exposure may differentially influence specific ADHD symptom domains, existing evidence suggests that lead-related neurotoxicity affects dopaminergic neurotransmission, executive functioning, attention regulation, and impulse control, mechanisms that are relevant to both inattentive and hyperactive-impulsive ADHD presentations [35, 36]. Future studies with more complete subtype information are warranted to determine whether lead exposure differentially influences inattentive, hyperactive, and combined ADHD presentations. Fourth, findings from this single urban health system may not be fully generalizable to other populations or settings.

Conclusion

In this large urban pediatric cohort, childhood lead exposure was associated with increased risk of ADHD, including at BLLs at or above the current CDC reference value of 3.5 µg/dL, with stronger associations observed among males. Although associations with conduct disorders were not statistically significant, differences in developmental timing, outcome ascertainment, and statistical power may partly explain these findings. Continued efforts to reduce lead exposure, particularly in socioeconomically disadvantaged urban communities, remain critical for protecting children’s neurodevelopment and long-term behavioral health.
