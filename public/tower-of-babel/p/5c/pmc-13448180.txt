Physics-informed neural network with adaptive loss balancing for real-time radiotherapy dose prediction and verification
Authors: Kunyi Zhang, Yunfei Li, JunMing Zhang, Bangcai Wang, Zhanlin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate and efficient dose computation lies at the heart of modern radiotherapy planning, yet practitioners are still pulled in two opposing directions: high-fidelity solvers — Monte Carlo simulation and deterministic linear Boltzmann transport equation (LBTE) solvers such as Acuros XB — deliver the physical accuracy that adaptive workflows demand, but the runtime they impose remains hard to reconcile with on-couch decision-making. The present study introduces a physics-informed neural network (PINN) framework that embeds radiation-transport-derived constraints into a 3D encoder-decoder backbone for rapid voxel-wise dose prediction and verification. The network ingests preprocessed CT volumes together with plan-specific information — gantry, collimator and couch angles, projected MLC fluence maps, and per-beam monitor units — and outputs the full 3D dose distribution. We formulate a physics residual loss inspired by the LBTE under simplifying assumptions, applied as a soft regularizer rather than as a replacement for the supervised target, so that training data calculated by the Anisotropic Analytical Algorithm (AAA, Eclipse v15.6) remains the primary supervisory signal while physical plausibility is encouraged across the volume. To overcome the gradient competition between data-driven and physics-driven terms, a momentum-based adaptive weighting strategy continuously rebalances their contributions through training. An end-to-end verification pipeline integrating gamma analysis, dose-volume histogram (DVH) evaluation, and energy-conservation checks completes prediction-plus-quality assurance in under 15 s per patient. On a held-out cohort of 27 head-and-neck and thoracic patients, the proposed system achieved mean global gamma passing rates of 97.8% at 3%/3 mm and 93.7% at 2%/2 mm (10% low-dose threshold) against the AAA reference, outperforming both a conventional 3D U-Net and a static-weight PINN; cross-checking against a subset recomputed with Acuros XB and EGSnrc-based Monte Carlo confirmed the robustness of the gain. Notably, the physics-constrained model trained on only half of the available cases matched the data-only baseline using the full training corpus — an indicator of meaningful sample efficiency. Taken together, the results suggest that combining transport-inspired regularization with adaptive training dynamics offers a practical route toward clinically deployable real-time dose verification in adaptive radiotherapy.

Introduction

Radiation therapy continues to be one of the most widely used modalities in the clinical management of malignant tumors, with roughly half of all cancer patients receiving some form of radiotherapy during their treatment course1. The fundamental aim of any plan is to deliver a prescribed dose to the target volume while sparing surrounding healthy tissues and organs at risk. Achieving that aim depends, to a non-trivial degree, on the accuracy of the dose-distribution calculations that underpin treatment planning2. Even modest deviations on the order of a few percent can translate into measurable differences in tumor control probability or normal-tissue complication rates — a fact that has driven decades of effort toward more precise computational approaches3.

Two distinct families of dose engines currently sit at the high-accuracy end of the spectrum, and they are worth distinguishing carefully. Monte Carlo (MC) simulation tracks individual particle histories through stochastic sampling and is widely regarded as the gold standard for radiation transport in heterogeneous patient anatomy4; its main drawback is the prohibitive runtime — single-plan IMRT calculations may take hours even on multi-core or GPU-accelerated hardware5. Deterministic LBTE solvers such as Acuros XB, by contrast, discretize the transport equation in space, angle and energy and reach Monte-Carlo-class accuracy in inhomogeneous media at considerably reduced cost, although they are still appreciably slower than convolution-superposition methods, especially in adaptive workflows where re-planning latency matters6,7. Pencil-beam and collapsed-cone algorithms, developed as faster analytical alternatives, cut computation time substantially but lose fidelity in regions of pronounced tissue heterogeneity such as air-tissue interfaces in the thorax or head-and-neck region8. Neither class fully satisfies the emerging clinical demand for dose computations that are simultaneously accurate and available within seconds — a demand that grows as adaptive radiotherapy moves on-couch9.

Against this backdrop, deep learning has attracted considerable attention as a possible route to real-time dose prediction. Existing methods, however, fall into two distinguishable categories that warrant clearer separation. The first relies on patient anatomy alone — typically the CT volume, sometimes augmented with the structure set — and learns a mapping from anatomy to a “typical” dose distribution; convolutional encoder-decoder networks of this kind have demonstrated voxel-level prediction within seconds for prostate, head-and-neck, and pelvic sites10–12. The second category is plan-specific: beam parameters such as gantry angles, MLC sequences, and per-beam monitor units are encoded explicitly so that the predicted dose responds to changes in delivery technique13. Approaches in the first category, while elegant, fail predictably whenever a new case deviates from the technique distribution seen during training, because the input does not contain the information needed to adapt. The plan-specific paradigm is therefore the more appropriate setting for verification tasks, and it is the one we adopt here. More recently, attention-based and transformer-style models have been explored to capture longer-range spatial dependencies in dose deposition14. These purely data-driven approaches, regardless of category, share a common limitation: they treat dose prediction as image regression and impose no explicit constraint that the output obey radiation transport. Predicted maps may therefore violate energy conservation, exhibit non-physical gradients near heterogeneous boundaries, or produce values inconsistent with the LBTE in regions underrepresented in training15. Such violations erode clinical confidence and complicate integration into quality assurance pipelines.

Several lines of work have therefore sought to inject physical knowledge into the learning process, and they do so in different ways. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), first formalized for solving partial differential equations by embedding the governing equations directly into the loss function, place the physical knowledge in the training objective16. A complementary line of work places the physical knowledge in the input itself: DeepSMCP, for example, uses a 3D U-Net to denoise high-uncertainty Monte Carlo dose distributions within the Swiss Monte Carlo Plan, so that the network learns from a noisy MC dose channel rather than from anatomy alone, and produces low-uncertainty MC-quality dose in roughly 35 s17. Both philosophies move beyond the purely data-driven baseline; they differ in where the physics enters the system. The PINN paradigm is especially attractive for radiotherapy because the underlying physics — although complex — is mathematically well characterized and amenable to soft-constraint formulation18, and because the dual supervision mechanism it provides guides the network toward solutions that are not only consistent with observed data but also respect physical principles, which empirically improves generalization to anatomical configurations underrepresented in the training corpus.

Our preliminary investigation, in line with reports from other groups, suggests that naively combining physics-based losses with data losses often runs into optimization trouble; the disparate magnitudes and convergence rates of the two terms can cause training instability or premature convergence to suboptimal solutions19. Addressing this difficulty calls for careful attention to loss-function architecture and to dynamic balancing strategies — an area that, as of this writing, remains insufficiently explored within the radiotherapy domain.

The present study is motivated by two intertwined needs. First, there is a clear clinical demand for a dose prediction framework capable of producing physically consistent results at interactive speed, particularly for adaptive and online replanning scenarios. Second, the methodological gap between generic PINN formulations and the specific requirements of radiotherapy dose verification warrants targeted investigation. We therefore set out to develop a PINN-based system for real-time dose distribution prediction and verification, with the following principal contributions. We design a physics-constrained loss function that takes its inspiration from the LBTE under simplifying assumptions appropriate for megavoltage photon transport in heterogeneous media, and that encodes a depth-attenuation residual together with dose-energy deposition relationships as soft penalty terms, ensuring that network outputs conform to the governing physics across the solution domain6. Building on this, we propose an adaptive weight-adjustment strategy that dynamically re-balances data-driven and physics-driven loss components during training; the mechanism monitors gradient magnitudes across loss terms and rescales their relative contributions at each optimization step, mitigating the well-known issue of loss-term competition. Furthermore, we construct an online verification framework that couples the trained PINN model with a rapid consistency-checking module, enabling real-time comparison between predicted and reference dose during treatment delivery. The framework incorporates gamma analysis and energy-conservation metrics to flag clinically significant discrepancies before they can propagate into delivered dose20.

By integrating these three components — physics-constrained loss design, adaptive training dynamics, and an online verification pipeline — into a unified system, this work aims to bridge the gap between the speed of deep learning inference and the rigor expected of clinical dose calculations. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews the theoretical foundation, including the governing equations and the PINN formulation adopted here. Section III details the network architecture, loss function design, and adaptive weighting scheme. Section IV describes the online verification framework. Section V presents experimental results on clinical datasets, and Section VI discusses implications, limitations, and directions for future research.

Radiation transport physics and dose calculation principles

When a clinical photon beam traverses biological tissue, three interaction mechanisms dominate across the therapeutic energy range. The photoelectric effect prevails at low photon energies, where the incident photon is fully absorbed by a bound electron. Compton scattering becomes the principal mode at megavoltage energies typical of linear accelerators, producing a scattered photon together with a recoil electron that deposits energy locally. At energies exceeding 1.022 MeV, pair production opens an additional channel through electron-positron creation in the nuclear Coulomb field21. The relative weight of each process depends on both photon energy and the effective atomic number of the traversed medium — a dependence that makes dose calculation in heterogeneous patient anatomy inherently challenging.

Basic framework of physics-informed neural networks

The central premise of physics-informed neural networks is straightforward yet powerful: a neural network approximates the solution to a physical system, and the governing partial differential equations (PDEs) are enforced not through mesh-based discretization but through residual penalties evaluated at collocation points scattered across the domain26. Consider a general PDE of the form F[u(x)] = 0, x ∈ Ω, where F is a differential operator and u is the unknown field variable defined over domain Ω. A PINN parameterizes u by a deep neural network u_θ(x) with trainable parameters θ, and the PDE residual F[u_θ] is computed at a set of interior collocation points via automatic differentiation — a technique that yields exact symbolic derivatives of the network output with respect to its inputs at machine precision, without resorting to finite differences27.

Training proceeds by minimizing a composite loss function that couples observational data with physical constraints. The data-driven component penalizes discrepancies between network predictions and available measurements at a finite set of labeled points, the physics residual loss enforces the governing equation at a separate set of collocation points, and boundary or initial conditions are incorporated through an analogous penalty term. The overall training objective therefore becomes a weighted sum:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\cal L}_{total}} = {\lambda _d}{{\cal L}_{data}} + {\lambda _r}{{\cal L}_{pde}} + {\lambda _b}{{\cal L}_{bc}}$$\end{document}

where λ_d, λ_r, and λ_b control the relative influence of each term16. This formulation is applicable to both forward problems, where boundary data are known and the interior solution is sought, and inverse problems, where unknown physical parameters must be inferred from sparse observations28. In either scenario, the multi-objective nature of Eq. (4) introduces a well-documented optimization difficulty: gradient magnitudes can differ by orders of magnitude across loss components, allowing one term to dominate training at the expense of others. Several gradient-balancing strategies have been proposed in response. Among them, the adaptive scheme that rescales each weight according to the inverse of the corresponding gradient statistics has shown particular promise29, and we adopt a momentum-smoothed variant of this idea (Eq. (10) below). The capacity to embed domain-specific equations — such as the LBTE-inspired residual and dose-energy relations introduced in Sect. 2.1 — directly into the training objective is what makes the PINN paradigm well-suited to dose prediction under physical-consistency requirements.

Research progress of deep learning in radiotherapy dose prediction

The past several years have witnessed a rapid proliferation of deep learning models tailored to three-dimensional dose prediction. U-Net-based encoder-decoder architectures were among the earliest to demonstrate that a convolutional network, trained on CT volumes paired with planned dose distributions, could reproduce voxel-level dose maps within seconds for head-and-neck and pelvic sites12. Subsequent work extended these ideas to ResNet and DenseNet backbones, where skip connections and dense feature reuse improved gradient flow and allowed deeper networks to capture finer dosimetric details across prostate, lung, and cervical cases30. A common training objective in these studies minimizes the voxel-wise mean squared error between predicted and reference dose, written compactly as L_MSE = (1/N) Σi (D_θ(xi) − D_ref(xi))², where D_θ is the network prediction and D_ref is the ground-truth dose computed by a clinical treatment-planning system.

Despite encouraging accuracy on in-distribution test sets, purely data-driven models reveal notable fragility when training corpora are limited or when anatomical configurations deviate from those seen during training. Generalization degrades because the network has no intrinsic mechanism to enforce physical plausibility — nothing in the MSE objective prevents the predicted dose from violating conservation principles or producing negative values in low-dose regions31. This concern is more than academic; clinically, a predicted dose map that appears numerically close to the reference yet violates underlying transport physics may mislead quality-assurance procedures. A second source of fragility, sometimes underappreciated, is the input itself: anatomy-only models cannot respond to changes in beam configuration, whereas plan-specific models that ingest gantry, MLC and MU information adapt naturally to delivery technique13. Mixing these two paradigms in literature comparisons has, in our reading, contributed to inconsistent reports of how much “physics” deep learning truly captures.

Recognizing these gaps, recent studies have begun embedding physics-based terms into neural network training for radiation applications, and they do so along two complementary axes. One axis augments the loss with a residual derived from the dose-energy relationship or a simplified transport equation, penalizing deviations from expected physical behavior at collocation points32. The other axis injects physics through the input channel: DeepSMCP feeds a noisy MC dose distribution into a denoising 3D U-Net so that the network operates on data that already encodes transport physics, achieving low-uncertainty MC-quality dose in roughly 35 s17. Early results from both directions suggest that physical knowledge — whether expressed as a soft constraint or as a richer input — improves dose prediction in heterogeneous regions, especially near air-tissue interfaces, while reducing the volume of training data required for acceptable performance. Most exploratory loss-based efforts, however, have relied on static weighting between data and physics losses, have not addressed the gradient competition problem discussed in Sect. 2.2, and have not incorporated real-time verification mechanisms33.

What remains conspicuously absent is a unified framework that couples a rigorously formulated physics-constrained loss — rooted in the LBTE rather than in simplified heuristics — with adaptive training dynamics and an online verification pipeline that closes the loop on plan-specific inputs. This is precisely the gap our work targets.

Clinical data acquisition and preprocessing

The dataset assembled for this study comprises 180 patients who received intensity-modulated radiotherapy for head-and-neck or thoracic malignancies at a single institution between 2019 and 2024. For each case, the following data were collected: planning CT volumes acquired at 512 × 512 in-plane resolution with 2.5 mm slice thickness, physician-delineated contours of the planning target volume and organs at risk, beam configuration parameters, and reference dose distributions. Reference dose distributions were generated by the Eclipse treatment planning system (Varian Medical Systems, version 15.6) using the Anisotropic Analytical Algorithm (AAA), with a calculation grid of 2.5 mm. Because AAA — although clinically validated and widely deployed — diverges from LBTE-based solvers in heterogeneous regions, we further recomputed a randomly selected subset of 30 cases (15 head-and-neck, 15 thoracic) using both Acuros XB version 15.6 (also a deterministic LBTE solver, used here in dose-to-medium mode) and an EGSnrc/BEAMnrc-DOSXYZnrc Monte Carlo simulation, the latter run with a 6 MV phase-space file taken from the IAEA repository for our Varian TrueBeam linac and a per-voxel statistical uncertainty below 1% within the high-dose region34. The MC implementation was first benchmarked against ionization-chamber measurements in a 30 × 30 × 30 cm³ water phantom for a 6 MV, 10 × 10 cm² reference field; percentage depth dose and lateral profiles agreed with measurement to within 1.5% globally and 1 mm in the penumbra (Fig. 1). Acuros XB was likewise benchmarked against the same measurements and against MC, with deviations not exceeding 2% in lung-equivalent media. These cross-checks let us treat AAA as the primary supervisory signal for training while using Acuros XB and MC as independent references for selected verification analyses (Sect. 4.1–4.3). Institutional review board approval was obtained prior to data extraction, and all records were de-identified in compliance with patient privacy regulations.

Preprocessing followed a sequential pipeline designed to standardize inputs across the heterogeneous acquisition conditions inevitably present in a multi-year clinical dataset. CT Hounsfield unit values were first converted to relative electron density maps using a scanner-specific calibration curve, since electron density rather than raw CT number is the physically meaningful quantity for dose transport35. All volumes were then rigidly registered to a common coordinate frame and resampled to a uniform voxel spacing of 2.5 × 2.5 × 2.5 mm³ via trilinear interpolation. Reference dose arrays underwent min-max normalization to the prescribed dose so that network outputs reside in the [0,1] range, a step that empirically stabilizes gradient magnitudes during training. A region of interest encompassing the target and a 3 cm expansion margin was cropped from each volume to reduce memory footprint while retaining the dosimetrically relevant anatomy.

A non-trivial part of the preprocessing is the conversion of plan-specific delivery parameters — which are intrinsically discrete and sequence-based — into volumetric inputs the 3D network can ingest. For each beam, we project the gantry, collimator and couch angles into the patient coordinate system and ray-trace from the source through every voxel; for each control point along the MLC sequence we compute the open fraction at that voxel and weight it by the corresponding fractional monitor unit. Aggregated over all control points and beams, this procedure produces a per-beam fluence-deposition volume that carries directional information implicitly through the ray geometry. Six channels enter the network per case: (i) electron density, (ii) target binary mask, (iii) sum of beam fluence-deposition volumes (cumulative incident-fluence proxy), (iv–vi) three orthogonal projections of beam direction encoded as cosine maps. This encoding is illustrated schematically in Fig. 2. Because all channels are voxelized at the same grid as the dose, the convolutional encoder operates on geometrically aligned tensors throughout. We verified empirically that ablating channels (iii)–(vi) and keeping only anatomy reduced gamma passing rates by 4–6% points at 2%/2 mm, consistent with the literature on plan-specific versus anatomy-only paradigms13.

PINN dose prediction model construction

The backbone of our prediction model adopts a three-dimensional encoder-decoder architecture loosely inspired by the 3D U-Net, modified to accommodate the physics-constraint branch that distinguishes this work from conventional deep learning approaches. The encoder consists of five resolution stages, each containing two convolutional blocks with 3 × 3 × 3 kernels followed by LeakyReLU activations (α = 0.01) and instance normalization. Channel depths double at each downsampling step — from 32 at the input to 512 at the bottleneck. The decoder mirrors this structure with transposed convolutions for upsampling and long-range skip connections that concatenate encoder features to preserve spatial detail38. A final 1 × 1 × 1 convolution with sigmoid activation maps the output to normalized dose values. As depicted in Fig. 3, a dedicated physics-constraint branch operates in parallel with the data-driven decoder. This module receives the predicted dose volume D_θ together with the electron density map ρₑ and computes the residual of a simplified transport relation. We do not enforce the full LBTE — that would be computationally intractable at every training iteration. Instead, we adopt a depth-attenuation residual derived directly from the equations of Sect. 2.1, and the derivation is given here in three steps so that the move from Eqs. Step (ii): the right-hand side must include a source term S(x) that accounts for scatter contributions which Eqs. (2)–(3) treat only implicitly; this term is absent from the homogeneous-medium form (Eq. (3)) but is required as soon as one writes a residual that is expected to vanish in a clinical, heterogeneous, finite-field setting. Each term in Eq. (5) deserves a comment. The radiological depth d is not a primary input to the network; instead, it is constructed from the electron-density input by accumulating ρₑ along the beam direction with the same ray-tracing routine used in Sect. 3.1. Because d is therefore a differentiable function of the input tensor, automatic differentiation propagates ∂D_θ/∂d through the chain rule: the network produces ∂D_θ/∂x via standard backpropagation through the convolutional layers, and the geometric derivative ∂x/∂d follows from the ray-tracing geometry computed once per case. The two are multiplied at every collocation voxel and summed over the relevant beam axes, which keeps the operation entirely within the computational graph and avoids any finite-difference approximation39. The effective attenuation coefficient µ_eff is parameterized by the local electron density at the voxel of evaluation; we acknowledge that this formulation does not explicitly include the cumulative path-integrated heterogeneity upstream of the voxel, and we treat that simplification as deliberate — the resulting loss acts as a soft, voxel-local regularizer rather than as a substitute for a full path-aware solver. The cumulative effect of upstream heterogeneity is, in practice, captured by the data-driven component of the loss, since the AAA reference itself accounts for the full beam path; the physics term complements rather than duplicates this information. Section V revisits this choice in the limitations discussion.

The scatter term S(x) is estimated through a pre-computed kernel lookup. We generated a set of single-energy point-spread kernels for a 6 MV beam by Monte Carlo simulation in a uniform water phantom (5 × 10⁸ particle histories, EGSnrc/DOSXYZnrc, 2 mm radial bin, 5 mm depth bin), parameterizing scatter dose deposition as a function of distance from the primary interaction site. For heterogeneous patient anatomy, we apply the standard density-scaling theorem (O’Connor) so that scatter ranges and amplitudes are rescaled by the local electron-density ratio relative to water, and we convolve the resulting kernel with the per-beam fluence-deposition volume already constructed for the network input (Sect. 3.1). The product is queried by trilinear interpolation at each collocation point to give S(x) at run time. This procedure is admittedly an approximation; it omits the energy spectrum and assumes axial symmetry, but it captures the dominant first-scatter contribution and is consistent with the analytical scatter handling used in convolution-superposition algorithms25.

Collocation points are sampled at every training iteration to evaluate Eq. (5). We use stratified sampling across three spatial zones: (a) within the planning target volume and a 1 cm margin, (b) within identified high-gradient regions detected by thresholding the gradient magnitude of the AAA reference, and (c) uniformly over the remainder of the cropped volume. The numbers per iteration are 4096, 4096, and 8192 respectively, for a total of N_r = 16,384 collocation points per batch element; the collocation set is resampled at every iteration so that the loss surface is not biased toward any fixed subset. Boundary points (the surface of the patient outline plus a 5 mm shell into air) contribute an additional 2048 points at which the dose is constrained toward zero. The corresponding physics loss aggregates Eq. Real-time verification system framework design

The verification system we propose is organized into three functionally distinct yet tightly coupled modules: prediction, verification, and feedback. Figure 4 presents the end-to-end data flow. The prediction module ingests preprocessed CT volumes and beam parameters, executes the trained PINN model described in Sect. 3.2, and outputs a three-dimensional dose distribution. This prediction is immediately passed to the verification module, which compares it against the reference dose from the treatment planning system across multiple quality metrics. Should any metric breach its predefined tolerance, the feedback module triggers an alert and generates a structured deviation report for clinical review, in keeping with the broader trend of using deep learning to support individualized quality assurance in radiotherapy42.

Fig. 4Workflow of the real-time dose verification system, illustrating data flow among the prediction, verification, and feedback modules within the adaptive radiotherapy loop.

Workflow of the real-time dose verification system, illustrating data flow among the prediction, verification, and feedback modules within the adaptive radiotherapy loop.

As shown in Fig. 4, the verification module operates along two complementary evaluation axes. The first is a voxel-level gamma analysis, a widely accepted standard in clinical dose verification43. The gamma index Γ(x) for each voxel is computed as the minimum over neighbouring reference voxels of the joint distance-to-agreement and dose-difference metric, with criteria Δd and ΔD; a voxel passes when Γ(x) ≤ 1. Throughout this study we report global gamma passing rates with a 10% low-dose threshold (i.e., voxels whose reference dose is below 10% of the maximum reference dose are excluded from the calculation), normalizing the dose-difference term to the global maximum reference dose. We adopt the clinically standard 3%/3 mm and a stricter 2%/2 mm criterion for sensitivity testing. Unless explicitly stated otherwise — and we make this explicit again in every figure caption and table heading — the reference dose against which Γ is computed is the AAA dose used during training; for the cross-validation subset of 30 cases described in Sect. 3.1, we additionally report Γ against Acuros XB and against MC, so that the AAA-versus-LBTE comparison made repeatedly in our discussion can be read off directly.

The second evaluation axis quantifies deviations in dose-volume histogram (DVH) metrics for each delineated structure. We define a normalized DVH deviation score as ΔDVH = (1/M) Σ_m |V_m^pred(D_x) − V_m^ref(D_x)|/V_m^ref(D_x), where V_m^pred and V_m^ref are the predicted and reference volumes receiving at least dose D_x for structure m, and M is the total number of evaluated structures44. Achieving real-time throughput — defined here as full prediction and verification within 15 s per patient — demands deliberate engineering. We convert the trained PINN model to TensorRT format with FP16 mixed precision, reducing single-case inference to under 3 s on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. The gamma computation, which is inherently parallelizable, is implemented as a custom CUDA kernel that evaluates the gamma index across all voxels simultaneously, completing in approximately 4 s for a typical 128 × 128 × 96 volume. A pipelined architecture overlaps data loading for the next case with verification of the current one, keeping the GPU fully occupied45.

As presented in Table 6, removing the physics constraint (variant C) degrades the gamma passing rate at 2%/2 mm by 6.4% points relative to the full model, confirming that the transport-based residual penalty is the single most impactful component. Replacing adaptive weighting with static weights (variant B) recovers much of this loss but still trails by 3.2 points at the stricter criterion, which aligns with our earlier observation that gradient competition between data and physics terms limits the effectiveness of fixed coefficients51. The shallow network (variant D) sacrifices roughly 2 points at 3%/3 mm while halving inference time and memory footprint — a trade-off that could prove worthwhile for resource-constrained deployment scenarios.

From an efficiency standpoint, all neural network variants complete inference three orders of magnitude faster than Monte Carlo simulation. The physics-constraint branch adds only 0.2 s to the pure U-Net inference because the automatic differentiation graph is precompiled during TensorRT conversion and the collocation-point evaluation runs in parallel with the decoder forward pass52. The full prediction-plus-verification pipeline, including gamma computation via the CUDA kernel described in Sect. 3.3, finishes within 11 s per case — well within the 15-second threshold we set for real-time clinical applicability.

Perhaps the most revealing finding concerns sample efficiency. When we retrained all variants on progressively smaller subsets of the training data (25%, 50%, 75%, and 100%), the PINN-Adaptive model trained on only 50% of the data (63 cases) matched the gamma passing rate that the U-Net achieved with the full 126-case corpus. At the 25% level, the U-Net’s 2%/2 mm passing rate dropped to 79.1%, while PINN-Adaptive retained 88.4% — a gap of over 9 points53. This resilience under data scarcity is arguably the most practically significant advantage of the physics-informed approach, given that curating large, high-quality radiotherapy datasets remains expensive and institutionally constrained. The physics residual effectively acts as a domain-specific regularizer that compensates for limited observational supervision, anchoring the network to physically plausible solutions even when the training distribution is sparse.

Discussion

The experimental results presented in Section IV reveal a consistent pattern: embedding radiation transport physics into the neural network training loop yields measurable improvements in dose prediction accuracy, and these improvements are most pronounced precisely where they matter most clinically — in regions of steep dose gradients and pronounced tissue heterogeneity. It is worth examining why this is the case, what practical limitations remain, and where this line of research might lead.

A point we should address head-on, because it shaped many of our design choices, is the apparent mismatch between training reference (AAA) and physics inspiration (LBTE). On the surface this looks like an inconsistency: AAA is a convolution-superposition algorithm and is not the most accurate engine available, whereas the physics residual draws its motivation from the LBTE that underlies Acuros XB and MC. Why, then, train on AAA at all? Two reasons. First, the residual loss in Eq. (5) is deliberately formulated as a soft, voxel-local regularizer rather than as a substitute for the supervisory target — it constrains internal consistency (a physically plausible attenuation behavior at every voxel) without prescribing the absolute dose value, leaving that to the data term. The role of physics is therefore complementary, not competitive: the network is supervised by AAA where AAA is reliable (most of the volume) and is nudged toward physical consistency where AAA may stumble (heterogeneous interfaces). Second, AAA remains the most widely deployed clinical engine and supplied by far the largest dataset to which we had institutional access; treating it as the primary supervisory signal aligned the study with conditions a typical clinic could reproduce. We tested whether this choice biased our conclusions by recomputing 30 cases with both Acuros XB and EGSnrc MC: the relative ranking of methods, the gain of PINN-Adaptive over the baselines, and the qualitative pattern of residuals near tissue interfaces all persist when the reference is changed (Sect. 4.1– 4.3). A cleaner study would re-train the entire pipeline on Acuros-recomputed dose distributions; this remains a worthwhile direction for follow-up work. What we report here is best understood as a first demonstration that the methodology generalizes across reference engines, rather than as a claim that AAA-trained models reproduce LBTE-class accuracy in every region.

The physical mechanism behind the observed accuracy gains is, in retrospect, fairly intuitive. A purely data-driven network learns dose distributions as spatial patterns correlated with anatomical features, but it has no internal representation of why dose attenuates differently in lung versus bone. When such a network encounters a tissue-interface configuration underrepresented in its training set, it interpolates between learned patterns in a way that may be statistically reasonable but physically implausible — producing, for instance, dose values that imply photon fluence increasing with depth, or energy appearing from nowhere at a boundary. The physics residual penalty in our formulation directly addresses this by requiring the predicted dose field to satisfy an attenuation relationship at thousands of collocation points sampled throughout the volume. Near a soft-tissue-to-lung transition, the effective attenuation coefficient drops sharply, and the network is forced to produce a dose gradient consistent with this change. The constraint does not dictate the exact dose value at any point; rather, it restricts the space of admissible solutions to those that are physically coherent, and within that restricted space, the data loss guides the network toward the correct answer. This explains why the improvement is largest in thoracic cases, where electron density varies by nearly a factor of three across the field, and more modest in head-and-neck anatomy, where soft tissue dominates and the data-driven baseline already performs reasonably well.

The role of adaptive weight adjustment deserves separate discussion, because it addresses a subtlety that is easy to underestimate. During early training, when the network has not yet learned basic dose topology, the physics residual is large everywhere and its gradients can overwhelm the data loss signal, pulling the optimizer toward trivially smooth solutions that satisfy the transport equation but bear little resemblance to actual clinical dose distributions. Our two-phase training strategy — data-only warm-up followed by gradual physics activation — mitigates the worst of this problem, but even after the physics loss is introduced, the relative magnitudes of the two gradient signals shift continuously as the network refines its predictions. The momentum-based adaptive scheme of Eq. (7) tracks these shifts and rebalances accordingly. We observed during training that the physics weight typically peaks around epoch 200, when the network has learned coarse dose features and the physics loss begins to correct fine-scale inconsistencies, then gradually decreases as both losses approach convergence. Without this dynamic adjustment, the static-weight variant sometimes exhibited oscillatory behaviour in the validation loss during mid-training — a sign of competing gradient directions that the fixed weighting could not resolve. The adaptive strategy eliminated these oscillations in all runs, producing smoother convergence curves and lower final loss values.

We should be candid about the limitations of the current system. First, our formulation uses a simplified attenuation-based physics constraint rather than the full LBTE; the residual is voxel-local and does not propagate cumulative path-integrated heterogeneity upstream of each evaluation point. This simplification keeps the computational overhead of the physics branch manageable, but it does not capture all scattering effects, particularly lateral scatter in large fields and backscatter near high-density implants, and cases involving metallic prostheses or dental fillings would likely require either a more sophisticated physics model or explicit artifact correction in the preprocessing pipeline. Second, the dataset in this study encompasses only head-and-neck and thoracic sites treated with conventional photon IMRT; we have not validated the system on stereotactic body radiotherapy with very small fields, volumetric modulated arc therapy with continuous gantry rotation, or electron beam treatments — each of which introduces distinct physical characteristics that our current physics branch may not fully capture. Extending the framework to these modalities would require re-deriving the residual terms to match the relevant transport conditions, a non-trivial but conceptually straightforward task. Third, the supervisory reference was AAA-based; while we cross-checked against Acuros XB and MC on a 30-case subset, a fully re-trained Acuros-based pipeline would constitute a cleaner test and is on our near-term agenda.

There is also the practical question of clinical deployment. Our system currently operates as a standalone research pipeline; integrating it into an existing treatment planning or adaptive replanning workflow would require DICOM-RT interoperability testing, regulatory validation under medical device standards, and institutional credentialing of the verification thresholds we have proposed in Table 2. The 11-second end-to-end latency is encouraging, but real-world network overhead, data transfer between systems, and quality assurance logging could push total turnaround beyond the interactive threshold in some clinical environments.

Looking forward, we see two particularly promising directions. The first is extension to particle therapy. Proton and heavy-ion beams exhibit Bragg peak behaviour that is exquisitely sensitive to tissue composition along the beam path — arguably an even stronger case for physics-informed prediction than photon therapy, since small density errors translate into large range shifts with potentially severe clinical consequences. The Bethe-Bloch stopping power equation and the associated range-energy relationship could serve as natural candidates for physics residual terms in a particle-therapy PINN. The second direction involves deeper integration with online adaptive workflows, where the patient is imaged on the treatment couch and the plan is adjusted in real time. In such scenarios, speed is paramount, and the ability of our system to produce a verified dose distribution in under 15 s could eliminate the current bottleneck of waiting for a full recalculation. Coupling the PINN predictor with deformable image registration and automated re-optimization would bring the field closer to truly closed-loop adaptive radiotherapy — a goal that has been discussed for over a decade but remains elusive in routine clinical practice.

Conclusion

We have presented a physics-informed neural network system for real-time radiotherapy dose distribution prediction and verification, addressing the long-standing trade-off between the rigor of MC/LBTE engines and the speed required by adaptive workflows. The system makes three contributions: a physics residual loss derived from the LBTE under simplifying assumptions and acting as a soft regularizer on a 3D encoder-decoder backbone; a momentum-based adaptive weighting strategy that resolves gradient competition between data-driven and physics-driven terms; and an end-to-end verification framework completing prediction plus gamma- and DVH-based quality assurance in under 15 s per patient.

On a 27-case test cohort spanning head-and-neck and thoracic sites, the system achieved mean global gamma passing rates of 97.8% (3%/3 mm) and 93.7% (2%/2 mm) against the AAA training reference, with the gain over a 3D U-Net and a static-weight PINN preserved when the reference was switched to Acuros XB or Monte Carlo on a 30-case cross-check subset. A physics-constrained model trained on half the data matched the data-only baseline using the full corpus, indicating useful sample efficiency for institutions with limited annotated datasets. Limitations include the use of a simplified, voxel-local physics constraint, evaluation on only two treatment sites with conventional IMRT, and reliance on AAA as the primary supervisory signal; multi-institutional validation under regulatory-grade conditions remains future work. Further details on extensions to particle therapy and on integration with online adaptive workflows are discussed in Section V.
