Evaluation of trauma-related mortality in Peshawar, Pakistan: a study at Khyber Teaching Hospital
Authors: Rafi Ullah, Laiba Ali, Farooq Ahmad, Lubna Kashif, Asif Khan, Faheem Ullah, Irfan Khan, Adnan Khan, Saeed Ahmad, Asraf Hussain, Adil Rehman, Javed Iqbal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTrauma is a leading cause of death and disability in low-income and middle-income countries. In Pakistan, limited reporting and absence of trauma registries restrict understanding of outcomes in Peshawar.ObjectiveTo describe the patterns, severity, and in-hospital mortality of trauma at Khyber Teaching Hospital (KTH), Peshawar, during 5 years.MethodsThis retrospective cohort (2018–2022) included all trauma admissions at KTH. Data on demographics, mechanism, Injury Severity Score (ISS), Revised Trauma Score (RTS), and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) Score were extracted. Logistic regression identified predictors of in-hospital death; annual trends were analyzed.ResultsAmong 10 542 patients (mean age 32±19 years; 72% male), blunt trauma comprised 58%, penetrating injuries 23%, and burns 8%. Road traffic accidents (RTAs) caused half of all injuries, followed by falls (16%), and violence (15%). Overall mortality was 9.4% (n=995), decreasing from 10.8% in 2018 to 8.7% in 2022. Fatalities had higher median ISS (25 vs 9; p<0.001) and more severe traumatic brain injury (56% vs 18%; p<0.001). Independent predictors of death were age >60 years (OR 4.3; 95% CI 3.5 to 5.4), ISS >25 (OR 5.6; 4.5–6.9), systolic BP <90 mm Hg (OR 4.9; 3.9–6.2), and GCS Score <8 (OR 6.1; 4.9–7.7). RTS and Trauma and Injury Severity Score showed strong discrimination (Area under the curve (AUC)=0.90).ConclusionTrauma mainly from RTAs remains a major cause of in-hospital death at KTH despite modest decline. High ISS, hypotension, and low GCS Score were strongest mortality predictors. Strengthened prehospital care, dedicated trauma centers, and a provincial registry are key to reducing preventable deaths in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa.

Introduction

Trauma is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, accounting for an estimated 4.4 million deaths annually.1 Injuries are particularly devastating among young populations, with trauma ranking as one of the top causes of death in individuals aged 5 years to 29 years.2 Globally, low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) bear nearly 90% of injury-related deaths—a disparity attributed to rapid urbanization, increased motorization, and limited access to advanced trauma care systems.3 4

In Pakistan, the burden of trauma is especially pronounced. National studies have shown that injuries contribute significantly to disability-adjusted life years lost, with road traffic accidents (RTAs), falls, and interpersonal violence emerging as the primary mechanisms of injury.5 6 The city of Peshawar, as the capital of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) province, is particularly affected due to its dense population, heavy traffic, and periodic regional conflict, all of which contribute to a high incidence of traumatic injuries.7 8 Khyber Teaching Hospital (KTH) serves as a critical tertiary care center in this region, managing a substantial volume of severe trauma patients.9

Historically, trauma data in Peshawar have been sparse and fragmented, often limited to isolated studies on specific injury types such as penetrating chest trauma or burns.10 11 A pilot trauma registry implemented in 2018 at a neighboring hospital demonstrated that even short-term systematic data collection can yield valuable insights into local injury patterns and outcomes.12 13 However, the absence of a comprehensive, long-term trauma surveillance system has impeded the development of effective trauma networks and quality-improvement initiatives.14 15

Distinct from prior LMIC descriptive reports, this study provides 5-year, single-center, all-mechanism coverage with standardized Injury Severity Score (ISS)/Revised Trauma Score (RTS)/Trauma and Injury Severity Score (TRISS) modeling from Peshawar, a conflict-adjacent urban hub. Against this backdrop, our study aims to conduct a comprehensive 5-year retrospective analysis (2018–2022) of all trauma admissions at KTH. Our objectives are to evaluate overall and mechanism-specific trauma mortality, quantify injury severity using standardized scoring systems (such as the ISS, RTS, and TRISS), and identify independent predictors of in-hospital death.16 17 We hypothesize that despite incremental improvements in hospital care, trauma mortality remains elevated in Peshawar due to delayed presentations, limited prehospital services, and resource constraint challenges that are common in LMIC settings.18 19 Furthermore, the findings of this study are expected to provide robust, location-specific data that can serve as a foundation for establishing an organized trauma system in the region and guiding targeted prevention strategies.20

Methods

We performed a retrospective observational cohort study at KTH, Peshawar, a tertiary academic center with 24-hour emergency and full surgical services. KTH serves Peshawar District and surrounding KPK catchment via mixed access pathways, with patients arriving both self-conveyed and by ambulance referral; the emergency department functions as a regional hub receiving direct walk-ins and interfacility transfers. All trauma patients of any age or sex presenting to the emergency department between January 1, 2018 and December 31, 2022 were eligible for inclusion. The hospital’s ethics review board granted a waiver of informed consent in light of the study’s retrospective nature.

Trauma patients were identified via emergency logbooks, hospital admission registers, and surgical department archives. We defined trauma as any physical injury resulting from external forces, including blunt (eg, road traffic collisions, falls, assault), penetrating (eg, gunshot, stab wounds, blast injuries), and thermal injuries (burns). Patients pronounced dead on arrival were included in the mortality analysis only if resuscitation was attempted; those brought in without any resuscitation effort were excluded from further analysis.

Trained medical officers abstracted information using a standardized case report form. Collected variables comprised:

Presentation: date and time of arrival; direct versus referred transfer; documented injury-to-arrival interval when available.

Severity scoring

Each injury was coded by body region according to the Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) (2005 edition), and the ISS was calculated as the sum of squares of the three highest AIS region scores. Major trauma was defined as ISS >15. Physiological derangement on arrival was quantified using the RTS, incorporating GCS Score, systolic blood pressure, and respiratory rate. We also computed each patient’s probability of survival using the TRISS methodology, which combines ISS, RTS, age, and injury mechanism (blunt vs penetrating) with standard coefficients from the Major Trauma Outcome Study.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, defined as death prior to discharge (including in the emergency department, operating theater, or wards). We further categorized timing of death as early (≤24 hours), intermediate (2 days to 7 days), or late (> 7 days). Secondary outcomes included hospital length of stay, discharge disposition among survivors (home vs rehabilitation referral), and record of urgent surgical procedures (eg, laparotomy, craniotomy, thoracotomy).

Data quality and reliability

All data were double-entered into a dedicated database and cross-validated for accuracy. To assess inter-rater reliability, two senior trauma surgeons independently reviewed a random 10% sample of charts for AIS coding; agreement exceeded 90%.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics characterized the cohort: categorical variables as counts and percentages, continuous variables as mean±SD or median (IQR) depending on distribution. We stratified analyses by trauma type and admission year to examine trends. Group comparisons used χ2 tests for proportions and Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous measures.

For mortality risk modeling, variables with p<0.10 in univariate analysis (including age categories, sex, injury mechanism, severe TBI, hypotension (SBP <90 mm Hg), major torso injury, high ISS, RTS) were entered into a multivariable logistic regression with backward stepwise elimination. We checked for multicollinearity among ISS, GCS, and RTS, assessed model fit with the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and evaluated discrimination via the area under the receiver operating characteristics curve. Adjusted ORs with 95% CIs and two-sided values of p (p<0.05 deemed significant) are reported for final predictors.

To compare predictive performance of ISS, RTS, and TRISS, we calculated sensitivity, specificity, and AUC for established thresholds (ISS >15, RTS <4, TRISS survival probability <0.5). All analyses were conducted using SPSS V.26 (IBM Corp.) and Stata V.16 (StataCorp).

Results

Patient volume and demographics

Between January 2018 and December 2022, 10 542 trauma patients met inclusion criteria. Annual admissions rose from 1950 in 2018 to 2311 in 2022, representing an average yearly increase of 4.3% (figure 1). We did not observe a pronounced COVID-era nadir in total admissions; however, periodic movement restrictions and changes in care-seeking may have influenced mechanism mix and non-admission triage in 2020–2021, consistent with regional reports. Trauma accounted for 18% to 22% of all emergency presentations each year. The cohort was 72.0% male (n=7590) and 28.0% female (n=2952). Patients ranged from 1 years to 85 years old (median 30 years, IQR 22–45), with children and adolescents (<18 years) comprising 14% of patients, adults (18–60 years) 76%, and seniors (>60 years) 10%.

Mechanisms and types of injury

RTAs were the predominant mechanism (49.8%), followed by falls (15.6%), firearm injuries (8.7%), stab/cut wounds (6.1%), blunt assault (3.5%), burns (8.1%), blast injuries (1.9%), and miscellaneous causes (9.8%) (table 1). Blunt trauma (eg, RTAs, falls, blunt assaults) accounted for 58.3% of patients, penetrating injuries for 22.5%, and burns for 8.1%.

Injury severity and clinical characteristics

The overall median ISS was 10 (IQR 4–17). Major trauma (ISS >15) occurred in 29.8% of patients, most commonly in blunt injuries (33% vs 15% in penetrating injuries). On arrival, 12% of patients were hypotensive (SBP <90 mm Hg), 28% had moderate to severe altered consciousness (GCS Score <13), and 5% required emergent intubation. The mean RTS at admission was 7.11±1.4. Burn patients (n=856) exhibited a mean total body surface area burned of 35% (IQR 15% to 40%), with inhalation injury noted in 2.4%.

Management and hospital course

Major surgical intervention was required in 36.1% of patients (n=3 804), including orthopedic fixation (1120), neurosurgical procedures (980), abdominal operations (740), and thoracic operations (366). A further 56.4% were admitted for inpatient care, whereas 7.8% were observed and discharged from the emergency department within 24 hours. Median hospital length of stay was 4 days (IQR 2–9 days), with some complex cases extending up to 4 months.

Mortality outcomes

Overall in-hospital mortality was 9.4% (995 deaths). Annual death rates declined modestly from 10.8% in 2018 to 8.7% in 2022 (Cochran-Armitage trend p=0.12) (figure 1). Blunt trauma mortality was higher than penetrating trauma (10.6% vs 6.8%, p<0.001). RTAs carried an 11% mortality rate, falls 7%, firearm injuries 12%, and stab wounds 4%. These rates are within the range reported by the Peshawar pilot registry, supporting local external validity. Burn mortality was 15.3%, predominantly among patients with >40% Total Body Surface Area (TBSA) burns. Blast injuries resulted in 14% mortality. 58% of deaths occurred within 24 hours of arrival, 25% between days 2 and 7, and 17% after 1 week; severe TBI and hemorrhagic shock were the leading causes of early deaths.

Severity scores and predictive performance

Non-survivors had a mean ISS of 27.8±13 vs 8.9±7 in survivors (p<0.001). Mortality rose from <1% in ISS 1–9 to 42.7% in ISS >25, with virtually no survivors above ISS 50 (figure 2). The mean RTS in non-survivors was 4.1 vs 7.5 in survivors (p<0.001). TRISS demonstrated excellent calibration (predicted survival 92% vs observed 90.6%) and discrimination (AUC=0.91), outperforming ISS (AUC=0.85) and RTS (AUC=0.83) (figure 3).

Independent predictors of mortality

In multivariable logistic regression, advanced age (OR 1.45 per decade; age >60 years, OR 4.3, 95% CI 3.5 to 5.4), high ISS (OR 1.10 per 5-point increase; ISS >25, OR 5.6, 95% CI 4.5 to 6.9), hypotension on arrival (SBP <90 mm Hg; OR 4.9, 95% CI 3.9 to 6.2), severe TBI (GCS <8; OR 6.1, 95% CI 4.9 to 7.7), and large burns (≥40% TBSA; OR 3.5, 95% CI 2.2 to 5.6) were all independently associated with higher odds of in-hospital death (table 2). Penetrating mechanism, sex, and transfer status were not significant after adjustment.

Discussion

Our study provides a comprehensive evaluation of trauma-related mortality at KTH during a recent 5-year period. The overall in-hospital mortality rate of 9.4% is consistent with rates reported in comparable LMIC settings.13 RTAs accounted for nearly half of all trauma patients, underscoring their dominant contribution to the urban injury burden in Peshawar.4 5 The high incidence of RTAs likely reflects rapid urbanization, growing motorization, and inadequate enforcement of traffic safety regulation factors repeatedly highlighted in regional literature.6 7 Importantly, our mortality rate (9.4%) and RTA predominance (~50%) closely mirror findings from the Peshawar pilot trauma registry, which also identified road traffic injuries as the leading mechanism and demonstrated a comparable case-fatality profile.3

Blunt trauma, predominantly due to RTAs and falls, was the most common mechanism, and penetrating injuries and burns, although less frequent, were associated with higher injury severity. In subgroup analyses, firearm-related penetrating trauma carried higher case fatality than stab wounds, but the overall penetrating category had lower crude mortality than blunt trauma due to its smaller share of total cases. These findings align with prior studies in Pakistan, which report that blunt trauma constitutes the majority of injury mechanisms, whereas penetrating injuries, particularly firearm and stab wounds, often result in more critical presentations.8 9 In our analysis, severe TBI and hemorrhagic shock emerged as the primary causes of early mortality, mirroring international data where these conditions are recognized as leading contributors to trauma deaths.10 11

The incorporation of standardized scoring systems, such as the ISS, RTS, and TRISS, allowed for precise risk stratification. Our multivariate analysis identified advanced age, high ISS, hypotension on arrival, and low GCS Scores as independent predictors of mortality.1214 These predictors are consistent with global findings, reinforcing that injury severity and initial physiological compromise are critical determinants of outcome.

The modest decline in mortality observed from 10.8% in 2018 to 8.7% in 2022, although not statistically significant after adjustment for severity, may reflect gradual improvements in trauma care at KTH. Enhancements in prehospital services, damage control resuscitation protocols, and rapid surgical interventions could be contributing to this trend.15 16 Nevertheless, persistent delays in patient presentation and limitations in prehospital care remain significant challenges that adversely affect outcomes.17 18

Our findings also underscore the urgent need for a structured trauma system in Peshawar. The establishment of a regional trauma registry would enable continuous monitoring of injury patterns and outcomes, facilitating quality improvement initiatives and benchmarking against international standards.19 Moreover, the implementation of dedicated trauma teams and optimized prehospital care protocols is critical for reducing preventable deaths, particularly among patients with severe hemorrhage and TBI.20

Despite these strengths, our study has several limitations. As a retrospective analysis, it relies on the accuracy and completeness of existing records, and prehospital data were often incomplete. Additionally, our findings are derived from a single tertiary care center, which may limit their generalizability to other regions in Pakistan. Nonetheless, the consistency of our results with international trauma literature supports the validity of our conclusions.

In summary, our study provides valuable, location-specific data that highlight the significant burden of trauma in Peshawar, predominantly driven by RTAs and severe injuries. The findings reinforce the need for investments in trauma care infrastructure, including enhanced prehospital services, organized trauma teams, and the establishment of a trauma registry. Such measures are essential for reducing trauma-related mortality and aligning local practices with global best standards.

Conclusion

During the 5-year period from 2018 to 2022, trauma remained a major contributor to in-hospital mortality at KTH, with an overall death rate of 9.4%. RTAs accounted for nearly half of all injuries and are the leading cause of fatal outcomes. Key independent predictors of mortality include advanced age, high injury severity (ISS >25), hypotension on arrival (SBP <90 mm Hg), and severe impairment of consciousness (GCS Score <8). Although there was a modest, non-significant decline in mortality rates over time, the persistence of early deaths, particularly from TBI and hemorrhagic shock, underscores ongoing gaps in both prehospital care and definitive in-hospital management.

To reduce trauma-related deaths in Peshawar and the wider KPK region, we recommend the following strategic measures (Haddon Matrix framing: pre-event, event, postevent):

Pre-event: road-safety enforcement, first-responder training networks (extends #2).

Event: standardized damage-control resuscitation and rapid hemorrhage/TBI pathways (#3–4).

Postevent: regional trauma registry and feedback loops (#1) to drive continuous quality improvement.

Future work should include prospective, multicenter registry linkage (prehospital → Emergency department → Intensive Care Unit/Operating Room) and mechanism-specific audits (eg, firearm injuries, high-ISS RTAs) to test targeted pathway improvements.

By aligning local trauma systems with international best practices and addressing the identified predictors of mortality, these efforts hold promise for further reducing avoidable deaths and improving outcomes for injured patients in this resource-limited setting.
