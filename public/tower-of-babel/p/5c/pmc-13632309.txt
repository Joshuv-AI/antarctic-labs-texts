Pelvic floor muscle training for pelvic organ prolapse: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Zhicheng Cong, Zhao Wang, Junyi Sun, Zhengyan Tang, Wenjie Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPelvic organ prolapse (POP) substantially affects women’s health and quality of life. Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) is a promising conservative treatment for POP, but evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) remains diversity. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of PFMT for POP.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review by using the PICOS framework, including RCTs that aimed to compare PFMT with other conservative treatments for POP. Outcomes included POP Symptom Score (POP-SS), POP Quantification (POP-Q), self-reported symptom improvement, and pelvic floor muscle function (sEMG-MVC, endurance, Modified Oxford Grading).ResultsA total of 16 studies were included in this meta-analysis. Compared with the control group, PFMT group was associated with lower POP-SS, higher sEMG-MVC, and greater pelvic floor muscle endurance. PFMT also improved self-reported symptom improvement, overall POP-Q stage, and anterior vaginal wall prolapse. No clear benefit was seen for posterior vaginal wall prolapse or Oxford grade. Short-term PFMT (≤ 6 months) showed beneficial effects, whereas long-term efficacy (> 6 months) and effects in individuals aged ≥ 55 years were not statistically significant.ConclusionsPFMT may improve short-term symptoms and pelvic floor muscle function in women with POP. However, long-term efficacy and effects in older women remain uncertain and require further investigation.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12894-026-02230-1.Keywords: Pelvic organ prolapse, Pelvic floor muscle training, Systematic review, Meta-analysis, Randomized controlled trial, Conservative treatment.

Background

Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) is the descent of one or more of the pelvic organs (uterus, vagina, bladder, or bowel) [1]. POP is a common clinical condition in women, the pelvic organs descend due to weakness or damage to the muscles and connective tissues which normally support them [2, 3]. POP can have a significant negative impact on patients’ quality of life, and produce symptoms secondary to weakened pelvic floor support structures, vaginal bulge, urinary disorder, intestinal and sexual dysfunction [4–6].

At present, there are many treatment schemes for POP, which can be roughly divided into conservative treatments and surgical treatments, both of which have distinctive advantages and disadvantages [7]. Conservative treatments usually include: pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT), pessary, electro-stimulation and so on. Among them, PFMT occupies an undeniable position [8, 9]. PFMT is usually recommended as a conservative treatment of POP or an adjuvant treatment in POP surgical management [10–13]. PFMT was initially proposed by researcher Arnold Kegel, in the beginning, it is used to treat urinary and gynecological dysfunction by strengthening pelvic floor muscles(PFM) [14]. At the same time, it can also help patients to enhance sexual function, prevent the relaxation of PFM and provide treatment for POP [15].

To date, there is no reliable evidence that other exercise programs such as hypopressive exercise, yoga, etc. are more effective than PFMT for POP [16]. Although PFMT has been established as the first-line treatment for stress urinary incontinence (SUI), it has not yet reached the same level of evidence for POP treatment [17, 18]. Furthermore, the conclusions of existing RCTs in this field are divergent, the therapeutic effect of PFMT on POP remains undetermined. Moreover, there are differences in the measuring strategy of outcomes selected by different RCTs, which also brings difficulties to systematic assessment of the therapeutic effect of PFMT on POP.

The primary objective of this study is to provide a relatively systematic evaluation and review of existing RCTs, to evaluate the therapeutic effect of PFMT on POP comprehensively. Secondary, we expect to obtain updated evidence on the efficacy of PFMT for women with POP and to offer insights for designing future RCTs in this area.

Methods

This systematic review was designed and reported in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) 2020 guidelines to ensure transparent, complete, and reproducible reporting of the review process. A PRISMA flow diagram and checklist are provided to document the study selection process and reporting completeness. And prospectively registered our protocol on PROSPERO (Registration number: CRD42024618189).

Data extraction was performed independently by two independent reviewers and disagreements were resolved by a third senior reviewer when necessary. The following information was extracted from each selected article: (1) author and year of publication; (2) Sample characteristics: age, disease type and BMI; (3) Study design; (4) Intervention measures: control group and experimental group; (5) Training characteristics: specific scheme and details of PFMT; (6) Follow up time and related information of different outcomes. All data items are listed and defined in a specific template. The reviewers used standardized templates for data extraction and included data from each study or group. For all outcomes, we reported the number of participants actually analyzed rather than intention-to-treat populations, as some studies had substantial dropout rates affecting specific measures.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Embase, and Web of Science from inception to 16 November 2024. The search was restricted to English-language literature, and the search strategy combined medical subject headings and free text terms, with publication type restricted to RCTs. The search terms included: “pelvic organ prolapse”, “pelvic floor”, “pelvis”, “uterine prolapse”, “POP”, “pelvic organ”, “physical therapy modalities”, “PFMT”, “exercise”, “physical fitness”, “exercise training” and so on. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants performed by any of the authors. The complete search strategy for each database is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Two independent reviewers screened titles/abstracts and performed full-text assessments. Data extraction and risk-of-bias assessments were conducted independently by two paired reviewers. Disagreements of all stages were resolved through consensus discussion or adjudication by a third senior reviewer. Authors of trials were contacted for additional information when necessary.

Risk of bias assessment

Two independent reviewers used the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool to assess the methodological quality of included RCTs [19]. Disagreements were resolved by a third senior reviewer. The following domains were assessed: random sequence generation and allocation concealment (selection bias); blinding of participants and personnel (performance bias); blinding of assessment for POP symptoms, POP stage and other outcomes (detection bias); handling of incomplete outcome data (attrition bias); selective reporting (reporting bias); and other bias (including contamination with alternate PFMT regimen in intervention and control groups and adherence to structured PFMT in intervention group). Risk of bias judgment for each domain was classified as “low risk”, “high risk”, or “unclear risk” in accordance with the criteria for judgment in the “Risk of Bias” assessment tool. The quality of evidence was evaluated using the GRADE framework with five considerations (study limitations, inconsistency, imprecision, indirectness, publication bias).

Assessment of the quality of evidence

We used the five GRADE considerations (study limitations, inconsistency of effect, imprecision, indirectness and publication bias) to assess the quality of evidence. We used the methods and recommendations described in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions using GRADEpro GDT software [19]. Two independent reviewers working independently assessed the quality of the evidence and reached a consensus on the downgrading decisions.

Statistical analysis

Only final assessment time points from each study were pooled in the primary meta-analysis to avoid multiple comparisons from the same participant cohort and reduce dependency between effect estimates. However, this approach does not account for substantial variation in follow-up durations across included studies, which may introduce time-lag bias and limit comparability between short-term and long-term outcomes.

For dichotomous outcomes, we reported treatment effects as risk ratios (RRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). For continuous outcomes, we reported treatment effects as weighted mean difference (WMD) and their 95% CI. Means and standard deviations before and after intervention were used to calculate the WMDs and 95% CI [20–22]. Level of significance was set at P = 0.05 for all outcomes. Heterogeneity between pooled RCTs was achieved by χ2 test for heterogeneity and the I2 statistic. According to the Cochrane Handbook, I2 > 75% indicates considerable heterogeneity.

Publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plots for all main outcomes. But the number of studies included in each funnel plot ranged from 3 to 7. Funnel plots should be interpreted with caution because fewer than 10 studies were included for all outcomes and substantial heterogeneity was present in several analyses; thus, these figures do not represent a formal assessment of publication bias. Funnel plots are presented as Supplemental Fig. 1.

In some cases, we applied subgroup analyses with pre designed criteria (The specific subgroup analysis methods will be shown in detail in the following sections). Pre-specified subgroup analyses included intervention duration (≤ 6 vs. > 6 months), age (≥ 55 years), and prolapse site (anterior vs. posterior vaginal wall). All other subgroup analyses were exploratory. Several subgroup analyses were based on a small number of studies, resulting in limited statistical power; these analyses are hypothesis-generating only and not confirmatory.

Intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses were not available in most included RCTs; therefore, per-protocol (completer) analyses were used for all meta-analyses. Differential dropout across studies may introduce attrition bias. Sensitivity analyses based on ITT estimates were not feasible because insufficient ITT data were reported in the primary studies. All data were analyzed by using RevMan 5.3 (Cochrane Community, London, United Kingdom, June 2014).

Results

Characteristics of the studies included

After removing 1852 duplicate records, we initially screened a total of 4697 records based on their titles and abstracts, and excluded 4619 articles for various reasons. For the 78 articles retained, we assessed their eligibility for inclusion through full-text review. Through this process, 16 articles were ultimately included in our study [23–38]. The process of screening and selection are detailed in Fig. 1. The study by Page et al. did not explicitly mention PFMT, but it provided a relatively structured and reasonable pelvic floor exercise (PFE) protocol and course, with the treatment process supervised one-on-one by a pelvic floor physiotherapist, and thus was included in our meta-analysis [38]. The studies by Stüpp et al. and Resende et al. involved the same research population and provided identical data suitable for meta-analysis, complementing each other in terms of study design and execution [25, 26]. Caagbay et al. instructed the experimental group on PFMT and lifestyle advice through an informational flipchart [37]. Two studies by Due et al. involving the same study population but with different follow-up times and reported different results [31, 32]. Three studies limited their research population to women with POP aged at least 55 years old [29, 33, 35]. Among them, the study by Panman et al. reported the 2-year effects of the same population as in the study by Wiegersma et al. [29, 35] However, both studies provided analyses of relevant outcome indicators that could be used for subgroup analyses, and therefore they were both retained. In addition, one study did not report the method of random sequence generation, and one study explicitly recorded that blinding was not used during the study process [24, 27]. Assessment of the quality of evidence

The quality of evidence for each key outcome was rated using the GRADE approach, with detailed ratings provided in Supplemental Table 3. For most outcomes, evidence was downgraded due to serious inconsistency (substantial statistical heterogeneity) across studies, serious imprecision (small sample sizes, wide confidence intervals or low event numbers), and potential publication bias. Only the overall POP-Q stage improvement was rated as high-quality evidence, as it showed no significant heterogeneity and relatively precise estimates. All other key outcomes were rated as moderate, low, or very low certainty. Notably, long-term outcomes (> 6 months) and results in patients aged ≥ 55 years were downgraded to very low certainty owing to extreme heterogeneity, wide confidence intervals, and small numbers of studies. Overall, the certainty of evidence for the main outcomes of this meta-analysis was low to moderate.

Outcomes

The included 16 RCTs comprised a total of 2063 women who received PFMT or other conservative treatments. Information from each study is summarized in Supplemental Table 2. The Actual numbers of participants per group for each analysis were provided detailly in Supplemental materials 5. There are some differences in measured outcomes and follow-up time reported in those RCTs.

POP-SS

The Pelvic Organ Prolapse Symptom Score (POP-SS) is a tool used to assess symptoms of pelvic organ prolapse, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms [39]. Three studies reported changes in POP-SS scores that were suitable for meta-analysis [23, 28, 34]. All three studies provided improvements in POP-SS scores at different time points, ranging from 20 weeks to 2 years. By pooling the results from all studies at the final assessment time point, the results showed that PFMT had a significant effect on improving POP-SS scores (WMD − 2.43, 95% CI − 3.70, − 1.15, P = 0.04; I2 = 69%; Fig. 3).

Self-reported change in prolapse symptoms

Self-reported change in prolapse symptoms is reported by the patients themselves, can directly reflect their subjective perception of symptom changes. The reported outcomes of Self-reported change in prolapse symptoms include better, no change, or worse. After receiving those interventions in each group, if participants reported “better”, we consider the treatment to have achieved relatively effective outcomes.

Seven studies presented Self-reported change in prolapse symptoms that were suitable for meta-analysis [23, 27, 28, 31, 33, 35, 38]. Four of these studies provided improvements in Self-reported change in prolapse symptoms at different time points [23, 28, 31, 38]. By pooling the results from all studies at the final assessment time point, the results showed that PFMT had a positive effect on improving Self-reported change in prolapse symptoms, but this result should be interpreted cautiously with substantial heterogeneity. (RR 2.43, 95% CI 1.35, 4.38, P < 0.00001; I2 = 89%; Fig. 4).

Subgroup analysis was conducted by using six months as the threshold to distinguish between short-term and long-term PFMT treatment. The results indicated that short-term PFMT provided benefits for the improvement of Self-reported change in prolapse symptoms (≤ 6 months: RR 3.00, 95% CI 1.96, 4.59, P = 0.73; I2 = 0%), while long-term PFMT showed inconclusive results with substantial heterogeneity precluding definitive conclusions (> 6 months: RR 1.37, 95% CI 0.84–2.24; P = 0.001; I²=81%; Fig. 5). POP-Q

The Pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantification System (POP-Q) is a standardized system for assessing POP in women. It involves the quantitative measurement of six specific anatomical points on the anterior and posterior vaginal walls and the apex to describe and stage pelvic organ prolapse [40]. According to the POP-Q system, participants who have improved at least one stage will be considered to have received effective treatment. Six studies reported data on changes in POP-Q stage suitable for meta-analysis, with three studies involving prolapse of anterior vaginal wall, posterior vaginal wall, and apical vaginal wall [23, 24, 28, 33, 35, 37]. Improvement of POP-Q stage was defined as a reduction of at least one stage in POP-Q stage from pre-treatment to post-treatment. Analysis showed that the positive effect of PFMT on improving POP-Q stage is clinically suggestive but statistically inconclusive (RR 1.46, 95% CI 1.12, 1.91, P = 0.34; I2 = 12%; Fig. 7).

POP-Q improvement between PFMT and control group. Values are presented as relative risk (RR) with 95% CI. Improvement was defined as ≥ 1-stage reduction in POP-Q stage

Subgroup analysis was conducted using six months as the threshold to distinguish between short-term and long-term PFMT treatment. Compared with other conservative treatments, short-term PFMT showed improvement in POP-Q stage (RR 1.51, 95% CI 1.06, 2.14, P = 0.46; I2 = 0%), while long-term PFMT did not demonstrate significant statistical differences (RR 1.13, 95% CI 0.50, 2.55, P = 0.09; I2 = 65%; Fig. 8). The two studies involving long-term PFMT focused on patients over 55 years of age, suggesting that the evaluation of long-term PFMT’s effect on POP-Q improvement may be influenced by this demographic factor.

PFMT demonstrated a clinically relevant but statistically non-significant improvement on improving anterior wall prolapse (RR 1.98, 95% CI 1.38, 2.85, P = 0.57; I2 = 0%), but it did not show statistical differences for posterior wall prolapse (RR 1.26, 95% CI 0.59, 2.66, P = 0.28; I2 = 21%; Fig. 9). Only one study reported observations on changes in apical prolapse symptoms, indicating that PFMT did not produce significant improvement in apical prolapse [37].

The changes of PFM

The strength of PFM can be assessed through various methods. The Modified Oxford Grading (MOG) is a quantification standard for assessing PFM strength [41]. Surface electromyography (sEMG) can evaluate the resting tone, maximum voluntary contraction (MVC) and endurance of PFM. The sEMG-MVC is a widely used indicator for assessing muscle function and can be utilized to evaluate the function and dysfunction of PFM [42, 43]. Using MOG, sEMG-MVC and endurance of PFM as analytical criteria, there are four studies reporting changes in PFM function suitable for meta-analysis [25, 26, 30, 36]. PFMT did not show statistical difference in its effect on MOG between the two groups (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.62, 1.18, P = 0.59; I2 = 0%; Fig. 10a). However, PFMT demonstrated a numerical increase of MVC (sEMG-MVC: WMD 5.10, 95% CI 3.88, 6.32, P = 0.59; I2 = 0%; Fig. 10b) and endurance of PFM (WMD 3.37, 95% CI 1.91, 4.83, P = 0.04; I2 = 76%; Fig. 10c). But those results should be interpreted cautiously given the potential for ecological bias and heterogeneity.

Discussion

This meta-analysis aims to analyze the therapeutic effects of PFMT on POP, provide clinical recommendations for healthcare professionals, and inform the direction of future designs of RCTs in this field. By meticulously selecting outcome measures from included studies, we had analyzed the therapeutic effects of PFMT on POP systematically. In comparison with previous studies, this meta-analysis was designed to update the existing evidence, ameliorate the limitations of current systematic reviews in this field, and rectify the analytical strategies in prior meta-analyses that we deem to be flawed.

Overall, this meta-analysis revealed that PFMT can improve several important outcomes in patients with POP, but the evidence was moderate, low or even very low-quality (see Supplemental Table 3). The results of meta-analysis suggested that PFMT was associated with improvement in POP-SS, POP-Q stage, anterior vaginal wall prolapse, self-reported change in prolapse symptoms, sEMG-MVC and Endurance of PFM. However, PFMT did not demonstrate significant benefits for posterior vaginal wall prolapse or the MOG of PFM.

This meta-analysis classified outcomes into three categories: symptom-based (POP-SS, self-reported symptoms), anatomical (POP-Q stage, anterior/posterior wall prolapse), and pelvic floor muscle function (MOG, sEMG-MVC, endurance). These three types of outcomes are conceptually distinct and should not be treated as equivalent measures of treatment effect. Improvements in muscle function do not necessarily mean anatomical improvement of prolapse. In addition, pooling highly heterogeneous outcomes may overestimate the consistency of observed effects, which represents a potential limitation.

In comparison with previous studies, this meta-analysis was designed to update the existing evidence, ameliorate the limitations of current systematic reviews in this field, and rectify the analytical strategies in prior meta-analyses that we deem to be flawed. The selection of relevant evaluation metrics was updated at the same time as the study and the improvement of said metrics was assessed. For example, we utilize MOG, sEMG-MVC and endurance of PFM as outcome measures in our study. As the manners recommended by the International Continence Society (ICS), the function of PFM can be evaluated by visual inspection, digital palpation, ultrasound, MRI, manometer, and electromyography [44]. Although MOG, sEMG-MVC and endurance of PFM are correlated, each of them reflects distinct aspect of PFM function. For RCTs focusing on the treatment of POP with PFMT, incorporating these metrics allows for a quantitative assessment of pelvic anatomical restoration and improvements in PFM function.

The limitations of this study are mainly the limited number of studies and the quality of evidence. The differences highlighted by the subgroup analysis between short-term and long-term PFMT treatment effects are generally consistent with the results reported in extant studies [2, 45]. Short-term PFMT (≤ 6 months) showed positive improvements in self-reported change in prolapse symptoms and POP-Q stage. In contrast, long-term PFMT (> 6 months) did not show statistical differences in treatment effects, particularly among individuals aged ≥ 55 years. To provide new high-quality evidences that support clinical decision-making, there is an urgent need for additional high-quality RCTs, particularly those with follow-up periods exceeding 6 months.

Among the studies included in our review, only two focused on women aged at least 55 years old. Factors such as declining hormone levels, comorbidities, age-related loss of muscle mass, and aging of the PFM may have a more negative impact on older women with POP [46, 47]. These factors may also increase complexity due to potential confounding effects related to aging and underlying conditions. Moreover, the perioperative tolerance, compliance, and acceptance of surgical treatment in elderly female POP participants are sometimes worse than those in other age groups. For elderly POP female participants, conducting more RCTs focusing on PFMT treatment plans for perimenopausal women with POP will help better guide clinical practice and address related clinical issues.

POP has a significantly negative impact on women’s quality of life in aspects of biological, physical, psychological and social [48, 49]. Pelvic Floor Impact Questionnaire-7 (PFIQ-7) and the Pelvic Floor Distress Inventory-20 (PFDI-20) are both questionnaires designed to assess the impact of pelvic floor health problems on women’s quality of life [50]. In the studies included in this review, four studies reported the changes in PFDI-20 scores [31–33, 35], and three studies reported the changes in PFIQ-7 scores [31, 32, 35]. However, only the results reported by U. Due et al. could be used for meta-analysis [32].

In the studies by U. Due et al., the 6-month follow-up results showed no significant difference in PFDI-20 score changes between the Lifestyle advice and Lifestyle advice with PFMT groups [32]. The Lifestyle advice group showed better improvement in the bowel subscale (CRAIQ-7) of the PFIQ-7 scores. The 12-month follow-up results suggested that both groups showed improvements in PFDI-20 and PFIQ-7 scores [31, 32]. In the study by C. M. C. R. Panman et al., the PFDI-20 scores in the PFMT group decreased at 3 months but slowly increased thereafter, and by 24 months, it was almost equal to the baseline score. In contrast, the improvement in PFDI-20 scores in the pessary group remained until the end of the study [33]. In another study by C. M. C. R. Panman et al., the 24-month follow-up results indicated that, for both PFMT and Watchful waiting groups, analyses of the PFDI-20 subscales indicated improvements in the PFMT group in terms of prolapse (POPDI-6), anorectal (CRADI-8), and urinary (UDI-6) symptoms [35]. However, there was no statistical difference in changes in PFIQ-7 scores between the two groups. In summary, current RCTs are unable to definitively establish the effectiveness of PFMT in improving PFDI-20 and PFIQ-7 scores. There is an urgent need for more high-quality RCTs in this field that focus on quality of life.

During the meta-analysis process, we excluded 12 studies that used PFMT as an adjunctive treatment for POP surgery. The high heterogeneity of these studies making it impossible to present as the method of meta-analysis. Results from nine of these studies indicated that compare to surgery alone, the addition of PFMT during surgery did not show obvious benefits [51–59]. Regardless of whether PFMT was used preoperatively, postoperatively, or both preoperatively and postoperatively, patients who underwent surgery alone and those who received PFMT as an adjunct to surgery both showed improvements in POP symptoms, urogenital symptoms, PFM function, and quality of life, with no obvious differences between groups.

The study by D. McClurg et al. suggested that patients who received PFMT both preoperatively and postoperatively reported no significant differences in symptoms compared to the control group at 6 months, but reported fewer symptoms at 12 months. However, the researchers also cautioned that these results should be interpreted with care, as they are primarily based on responses from one site [13]. The study by C. Brandt et al. concluded that PFMT after pelvic floor reconstructive surgery has not a beneficial impact on POP symptoms and quality of life, but have positive effect on pelvic and abdominal muscle function [60]. S. K. Jarvis et al. found that for incontinence and/or prolapse surgery, preoperative and postoperative PFMT can help improve surgical outcomes and quality of life for women, but this study did not report specific surgical procedures and did not strictly distinguish between incontinence and POP [61]. To sum up, there is currently no explicit evidence that PFMT as an adjunctive treatment for POP surgery can provide benefits to patients, and this area also requires more high-quality RCTs to provide relative data. Funnel plots for the main outcomes were generated to assess potential publication bias. Given the limited number of eligible studies per analysis, relevant statistical tests were not conducted.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis. Regarding the limitations of the included studies, most trials were associated with a high risk of performance bias due to the nature of pelvic floor muscle training, which cannot be fully blinded. Additionally, many included studies had small sample sizes, insufficient reporting of outcome indicators, and substantial clinical and statistical heterogeneity. Intervention protocols, outcome measurement tools, and follow-up durations also varied considerably across the randomized controlled trials.

Clinical heterogeneity was evident across included studies with regard to PFMT protocols, supervision intensity, training frequency, and patient adherence, which may have contributed to variability in treatment effects. Adverse events, potential harms, and patient burden associated with PFMT were not systematically evaluated or synthesized in this review. Therefore, the safety and tolerability of PFMT remain unclear. The conclusions of this meta-analysis are limited to the study populations and interventions included and cannot be broadly generalized beyond the available evidence.

For the limitations of the review process, this study was restricted to English-language publications of four major electronic databases (PubMed, CENTRAL, Embase, and Web of Science). Grey literature was not included, which may have resulted in potential missing studies. Potential reviewer subjectivity could not be fully eliminated despite dual independent review and third-party arbitration.

Only final follow-up time points were pooled across studies with widely varying follow-up durations, which may introduce time-lag bias and limit the comparability of short-term and long-term treatment effects. Time-stratified subgroup analyses were performed for several outcomes but were limited by the small number of studies in each stratum. This represents a major limitation of the present meta-analysis.

Regarding the meta-analysis, high statistical heterogeneity was observed in several outcome analyses. Some pooled results were based on a limited number of included studies, which reduced the reliability of the effect estimates. The use of per-protocol analyses rather than intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses may bias effect estimates owing to differential dropout in PFMT trials, which further reduces the certainty of evidence.

According to GRADE ratings, most outcomes were downgraded due to high heterogeneity, imprecision (small samples and wide confidence intervals), and potential publication bias. Long-term efficacy (> 6 months) and effects in patients aged ≥ 55 years were rated as very low certainty, indicating substantial uncertainty. No definitive conclusions can be drawn due to the overall low-to-moderate certainty of evidence.

Funnel plots were generated for exploratory purposes but cannot be considered a reliable assessment of publication bias, as all outcomes included fewer than 10 studies and several showed substantial heterogeneity. Many subgroup analyses were based on only a small number of studies, leading to low statistical power. Accordingly, these results should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Conclusions regarding intervention duration and age effects were interpreted cautiously to avoid overstatement of findings from non-significant or highly heterogeneous results.

Finally, these findings are applicable to adult women with stage I–III pelvic organ prolapse, excluding women in the early postpartum period and those with stage IV prolapse. The long-term efficacy beyond 6 months and effects in elderly patients aged 55 years and older remain unclear due to limited evidence. Owing to the low-to-moderate quality of evidence, the conclusions of this review should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

Based on low-to-moderate certainty evidence, PFMT may provide improvements in pelvic organ prolapse symptoms, POP-SS, POP-Q stage, and some pelvic floor muscle function outcomes among women with stage I–III POP. Compared to other conservative treatment options, PFMT has a certain degree of therapeutic effectiveness in improving POP-SS, POP-Q stage, anterior vaginal wall prolapse, Self-reported change in prolapse symptoms, sEMG-MVC and endurance of PFM for POP patients. But PFMT does not present clear improvement for posterior vaginal wall prolapse and MOG of PFM. Subgroup analysis results indicate that compared to other conservative treatment options, short-term PFMT (≤ 6 months) has a positive effect on improving Self-reported change in prolapse symptoms and POP-Q stage. However, long-term PFMT (> 6 months) and long-term PFMT specifically targeting individuals aged ≥ 55 years do not show statistical effects on improving Self-reported change in prolapse symptoms and POP-Q stage. In addition, our systematic review was limited by the quality of the original data. However, the magnitude of benefit remains uncertain due to high risk of bias, substantial heterogeneity, and imprecision across included studies. Long-term efficacy beyond 6 months and effects in older women aged ≥ 55 years are particularly uncertain and cannot be confirmed due to very low–quality evidence. Future large, well-blinded, long-term RCTs with standardized protocols are needed to establish the efficacy, optimal duration, and long-term outcomes of PFMT for POP.
