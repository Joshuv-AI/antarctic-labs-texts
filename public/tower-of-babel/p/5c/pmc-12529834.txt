Investigation of depression-like phenotypes and their behavioral correlates in cynomolgus monkeys (Macaca fascicularis)
Authors: Wei Li, Li Huang, Shi-Gang Liu, Jun Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global burden of depression is heavy, with unclear pathogenesis, relying on animal models for researching neural mechanisms and treatments. Existing rodent models lack social attributes, while non-human primate models (such as the natural depression model in cynomolgus monkeys) are closer to human social stress scenarios. Previously, the screening of natural depression models in cynomolgus monkeys was unvalidated and their causes were unclear. This study constructs a cynomolgus monkey community by simulating wild environments, records social behaviors, and explores the causes of their natural depression-like behaviors, analyzes their correlations with exploratory/social behaviors, providing behavioral evidence for phenotype-behavior links in this model.ResultsThe conflict behaviors in the community were recorded to develop a conflict behaviors matrix, and the behaviors of all individuals were monitored by using the focus observation method. The analysis entailed two complementary approaches: (1) recording and analyzing ten behaviors across ten observation time slots for depressive individuals (defined by persistent core depression-like behaviors: crouching, head-bowing, and slumped posture with open eyes); and (2) comparing high- and low-ranking groups to identify social stressors driving these behaviors. The David Scoring (DS) scores of the animals were determined based on the record of conflict behaviors in the community. The individuals were divided into high- and low-ranking groups, and the daily behaviors of the two groups were analyzed. The results showed statistically significant differences in depression behaviors, anxious behaviors, and motor behaviors among the monkeys. Further, individuals with depression behaviors exhibited drastically lower capability in exploration and social interaction. Additionally, social frustration stress responses induced by inter- and intra-community conflict behaviors may account for this behavior in the cynomolgus monkey model of natural depression.ConclusionsThe natural depression model of caged Macaca fascicularis is a social defeat model induced by yield-attack behavior.Keywords: MDD, Macaca fascicularis, Chronic unpredictable mild stress, Social interaction test

Background

According to 2019 statistics released by the World Health Organization (WHO), 320 million depression cases were reported globally by 2019, of which 95 million depression cases were reported in China. Furthermore, research has shown that depression ranks second among the largest disease burdens in China [1]. There are no clear experimental indicators because of the inherent limitations of human studies and the unclear pathogenesis of depression. Hence, the use of animal models remains crucial in identifying the neural mechanisms of depression and discovering new drugs and treatments. Currently, rodent animal models, such as the chronic unpredictable mild stimulus model, behavioral despair model, and the social frustration stress model, are used in basic depression research [2]. However, these animal models are established in non-social environments. However, depression is a disease closely related to social pressure and social relationships. Further, rodent animal models have several limitations, including the lack of complex characteristics seen in human society. Non-human primate models of depression can compensate for this challenge of rodent systems. Currently, non-human primate models of depression include the social isolation model, mother-infant separation model, and natural depression model [3]. Among these models, the natural depression model is the most ethical and socially relevant one. The approach identifies animals with crouching, head bowing, slumped posture with open eyes as core behaviors in a caged community of cynomolgus monkeys (Macaca fascicularis) [4]. For instance, Shively et al. confirmed a few monkeys with natural depression, although the causes of natural depression in the animals were not specified [5, 6]. Further, even when this natural depression model was screened, it was not validated. Given these findings, a cynomolgus monkey community was established by mimicking key wild social and ecological conditions—specifically, constructing a multi-male and multi-female group structure (consistent with their natural social organization), providing environmental enrichments resembling wild habitats (e.g., space for movement, naturalistic food resources), and allowing spontaneous interactions such as competition for food, territory, and mating rights, thereby capturing social dynamics linked to hierarchy formation in wild populations. The social behaviors of cynomolgus monkeys were recorded, and the causes of their depression-like behaviors were investigated. To address these gaps, the present study aimed to: (1) Identify the underlying causes of natural depression-like behaviors in caged cynomolgus monkeys, with a specific focus on whether such behaviors are induced by intra-community attack-yield conflicts (i.e., social hierarchy-related stress); (2) Analyze correlations between depression-like phenotypes and exploratory/social behaviors, by examining whether individuals with depression-like behaviors exhibit core behavioral deficits (e.g., reduced exploratory activity, social withdrawal). To achieve these goals, we designed two key behavioral tests: The food exploration test was used to assess exploratory behavior, a well-recognized behavioral marker of depression in both humans and animal models (e.g., rodents with depression-like phenotypes show reduced motivation to explore novel food resources) [7]. This test helps verify whether individuals with depression-like behaviors exhibit deficits in goal-directed exploration, a core feature of depressive states.The social interaction test was employed to evaluate social engagement, as social withdrawal is a hallmark symptom of human depression. By measuring time spent close to an unfamiliar conspecific (formally defined later in Methods as the “effective contact zone”), we aimed to determine whether depression-like monkeys display reduced social interaction, further validating the model’s relevance to human pathology.

Overall, this study sought to clarify the pathogenesis of natural depression in cynomolgus monkeys and analyze correlations between depression-like phenotypes and exploratory/social behaviors, providing a reliable foundation for future depression research.

Materials and methods

Animal details and selection

This study was conducted at the non-human primates laboratory of Suzhou Zhongke Animal Experiment Co., Ltd, which is accredited by AAALAC, International—ensuring compliance with high standards for laboratory animal quality, welfare, and biosafety. The cynomolgus monkeys were provided by the Zhongke Animal Experimental Base, with a license for domestication and breeding of nationally protected wild animals (SuFa Domestication 2007-001). Initially, 22 healthy cynomolgus monkeys from different cages were randomly selected. After excluding pregnancy and other interfering factors, 19 individuals were selected to form a single community (consistent with their natural social structure) for the study, including 2 males and 17 females. The average age of the males was 8.5 ± 1.2 years, and the average age of the females was 8.3 ± 1.0 years (overall average age: 8.4 ± 1.1 years). Housing conditions

The monkeys were housed in an indoor enclosure with a total area of 50 m² (length × width × height: 10 m × 5 m × 3 m), designed to mimic wild habitats. The enclosure was equipped with environmental enrichments such as climbing frames, perches, and hiding spaces to facilitate natural behaviors like climbing and resting.

Acclimation period

4-week acclimation (group enclosure): Post-baseline, 19 selected monkeys adapted to the 50 m² enclosure (climbing frames/perches) for 4 weeks via free exploration, gentle human interaction (to reduce fear), and daily health and behavior monitoring (for adaptation). They were group-housed (2M + 17F), consistent with formal observation. During acclimation, they were group-housed here (2 males + 17 females), consistent with formal observation’s social structure. All 19 were single-group housed (to capture social dynamics for studying social stress-related depression). To address group-specific confounds: ① stable hierarchy verified via 1122 attack-yield interactions (Pearson’s r ≥ 0.7, P < 0.01); ② study focuses on within-group associations; future studies will use multiple groups for generalizability. The 4-week acclimation stabilized the new group; later references to ‘beginning of group formation’ refer to the post-acclimation 2-week pre-observation period (when conflict behaviors were first recorded).

Feeding and care

The monkeys were fed twice daily (at 8:00 and 16:00) with a diet consisting of commercial primate chow, supplemented with fresh fruits (e.g., apples, bananas) and vegetables (e.g., carrots), to simulate natural foraging patterns. Water was provided ad libitum via automatic water dispensers.

Methods

Establishment of the cynomolgus monkey community

To simulate key wild social features of cynomolgus monkeys (e.g., multi-male multi-female structure, resource competition), a community was established with 2 males and 17 females. Notably, cynomolgus monkeys in the wild may exhibit fierce conflict behaviors driven by competition for food, territory, and mating rights [8, 9], and this study’s simulated community aimed to reflect such natural competitive dynamics. Unlike wild female cynomolgus monkeys (who remain in natal groups with kin), unfamiliar individuals were selected for the community to avoid pre-existing social bonds—this design is critical for observing spontaneous social hierarchies, which aligns with the study’s focus on social stress and depression.

Observing the daily behaviors and conflict behaviors

2-week pre-observation (group enclosure): Post-acclimation (after the 19 selected monkeys were introduced to the new colony enclosure), observers familiarized with individual identifiers to lay an accurate basis for subsequent behavioral recording. Conflict behaviors are defined in Table 2 based on the cynomolgus monkey behavioral spectrum [10]; during this phase, 187 conflict behaviors were recorded (31% bidirectional aggression), confirming unstable social hierarchies. No depression-like behaviors were observed, consistent with baseline. After pre-observation, four experimental recorders were assigned to the community: two recorded attack-yield behaviors, and the other two videotaped and observed daily behaviors. Aggressive interaction sequence analysis was conducted via video recordings, which tracked the initiator and recipient of each interaction, as well as whether the recipient launched retaliatory attacks within 30 min of being attacked; retaliatory attacks were defined as aggressive behaviors (per Table 2: bites, pushes, threats), and the retaliation rate was calculated as (Number of retaliatory attacks / Total attacks received) ×100%.This 2-week phase aimed to: (1) familiarize observers with individual identifiers (e.g., facial markings, body size) to ensure accurate behavioral recording; (2) establish baseline behavioral repertoires before formal data collection.Week 2 is the end of the 2-week pre-observation period, and Week 8 is the 6th week of formal observation (for pre-post behavioral comparison).During pre-observation, conflict behaviors were recorded at the individual level (187 total conflicts, each linked to participating monkeys’IDs; e.g., M1 vs. F9, F2 vs. F10). This 187-count is lower than the formal 8-week observation’s 1122 conflicts for two reasons: (1) pre-observation lasted only 2 weeks (vs. 8 weeks), and (2) it occurred during early hierarchy formation—31% of interactions were bidirectional aggression (no fixed dominance-submission pairs), so conflicts were scattered.To account for individual variability, all conflict data (pre- and formal observation) were analyzed with individual ID as a random effect in statistical models.These pre-observation data contextualize Table 3: Pre-observation had weak attack-yield correlations (r = 0.42) due to scattered conflicts across all 19 monkeys. In contrast, Table 3 presents formal observation data-89% of its 1122 conflicts concentrated in fixed high- vs. low-ranking pairs (e.g., M1-F9, M2-F11) with strong correlations (r ≥ 0.7, P < 0.01). This contrast confirms stable dominance (Table 3) formed post-pre-observation, explaining formal observation’s higher conflict count (directional, repeated between fixed pairs). Different behaviors were observed across ten time slots. An inter-rater reliability test was conducted (two independent observers recorded the same slot; Kappa coefficient, k>0.8 = valid), ensuring accurate behavioral classification.The observation researchers recorded the effective number of attack-yield behaviors according to the behavioral recording items. Researchers conducting daily behavioral observations employed continuous focal sampling to track and photograph cynomolgus monkeys [11]. The observation time was 8:30 − 11:30 and 14:00–16:00; each time slot was 30 min, with ten time slots per day, and the cumulative effective time was 1050 h. Noldus (Wageningen, the Netherlands) analysis system-specifically The Observer software-was used to analyze animal behavior [12]. After recording attack-yield behaviors, David Scoring (DS) was used to quantify social rank, following established methods [11–13]. The DS score formula is: DS = W1 + W2 - L1 - L2. Here, W1 refers to the sum of the dominant percentages of each monkey in group conflicts; W2 is the sum of the weighted dominant percentages of each monkey in individual conflict interactions; L1 represents the sum of the subordinate percentages of each monkey in group conflicts; L2 is the sum of the weighted subordinate percentages of each monkey in individual conflict interactions. Based on DS scores, individuals were divided into high-ranking groups (DS > 0) and low-ranking groups (DS < 0) to analyze rank-related differences in daily behaviors.

Definition and records of daily behaviors

The animal behavior analysis system was used to record and analyze ten types of behaviors, including anxiety, conflicts, and food intake [14]. Friendly behaviors primarily include grooming (initiated by an individual toward another, regardless of prior conflict)-to reduce tension and strengthen bonds-plus other typical non-aggressive interactions: active proximity (staying within 0.5 m voluntarily), gentle physical contact (e.g., light limb touching), and coordinated activities (e.g., shared foraging). Post-conflict grooming (reconciliation) is also categorized here. Anxious behaviors are primarily characterized by rapid scratching (duration >3 s, often accompanied by tense posture, e.g., hunchback or stiff limbs), plus other typical stress-related behaviors in cynomolgus monkeys: frequent pacing, repetitive head-shaking, and active avoidance of conspecifics. Rest behaviors refer to standing, sitting, or lying down for more than 5 s. Conflict behaviors include biting, scratching, or threatening another individual. Food intake behaviors refer to individuals grabbing food from the trough. Warming behaviors refer to individuals snuggling with each other to maintain body temperature. Mating behaviors refer to sexual behaviors between individuals of opposite sexes. Motor behaviors include moving quickly or slowly, with a distance greater than half an arm’s length. Depression behaviors refer to the presence of core depression-like behaviors, including slumped posture with open eyes, crouching, and head- bowing. Finally, other behaviors refer to behaviors not included in the above nine categories, specifically: yawning, scratching non-anxiously (duration < 3s, no associated tense posture), brief gaze-following (tracking conspecifics’eye movements without interaction), and occasional self-grooming (≤ 1 time per 10-min observation slot, isolated from friendly grooming behaviors).

Definition of depressive individuals

Individuals were classified as ‘depressive’ if they exhibited core depression-like behaviors (crouching, head-bowing, slumped posture with open eyes) at least 3 times per week for 4 consecutive weeks during the 8-week observation period. Notably, prior studies on cynomolgus monkey natural depression identified these core behaviors but lacked validation of their link to stress physiology; our criterion builds on this by incorporating evidence that persistent expression of these behaviors correlates with physiological markers of stress (e.g., elevated cortisol levels) [5, 6]. This linkage validates the behavioral criterion, distinguishing it from unvalidated screening methods in earlier work. Non-depressive individuals were defined as those with fewer than 1 occurrence of core depression-like behaviors per week throughout the observation period.

Food exploration test

Based on David Scoring (DS) scores, individuals were divided into high- and low-ranking groups; tests were conducted in a separate cage (2m×1m×1m) with no visual contact with the home pen (separated by 2 m opaque barriers), where apples (buried with stones) were placed in the trough beforehand (monkeys did not witness placement). Monkeys were moved from the home pen to the testing cage one at a time (transfer order randomized via a random number table), with tests split over 3 days (≤ 6 monkeys/day) to avoid group stress; a high-definition camera (1 m from the cage) recorded behavior for 10 min per test (10 min recorded if food was not touched), and experimenters left after camera activation. Prior to testing (1 day before), each monkey was individually placed in the testing cage for 30 min (no food) to adapt to the environment, and we recorded the time from the monkey’s entry into the cage to its first touch of the food (Fig. 1).

Interactive behavioral evaluation

Single cages (2m×1m×1m) were separated from each other by a transparent plastic partition with 1 cm-diameter holes (to avoid physical contact while preserving sight and smell between subjects) and isolated from the home pen via 2 m opaque barriers (no visual contact with the group); each female was tested once (to avoid repeated stress), moved from the home pen to testing cages one at a time (transfer order randomized via a random number table) and split over 2 days (≤ 9 females/day) to reduce group disturbance, with “high- and low-ranking groups tested separately” meaning the two groups were tested in different time slots (high-ranking in the morning, low-ranking in the afternoon) to prevent inter-group interference. The same healthy male cynomolgus monkey (from another community) was used as the interaction subject for all tests to control for individual variability; each experiment lasted 5 min, and we recorded the time each female spent in the effective contact area (0.5 m left and right of the transparent partition) within 5 min of the male being placed. Males were excluded from this test (retained in other experiments) as their aggressive social tendencies (Table 6) may distort “social withdrawal” data, as shown in Fig. 2.

Statistical analysis

Data on daily behavioral indicators, food exploration test, and social interaction time were first tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Since all data deviated from a normal distribution (P < 0.05), nonparametric tests were employed.The attack-yield indicators were expressed as times, and the DS scores of each subject were calculated based on the attack-yield relationship matrix using the David’s score method. The data of food exploration tests were expressed as means ± SD; Mann-Whitney U tests (reported as U value, df = n1 + n2-2) compared two groups, with initial α = 0.05-Holm-Bonferroni adjusts α sequentially, and all reported P < 0.05 refer to corrected thresholds. For 10 behavioral items’ multiple comparisons, Holm-Bonferroni correction was applied, ensuring robust control of Type I error while preserving statistical power to detect biologically meaningful differences.To explore the dynamic relationship between social stress and depression-like behaviors, we tracked weekly aggressive exposure (frequency and severity) in low-ranking individuals and the 2 high-ranking depressive individuals (exposed to high-ranking competitive aggression). A weighted aggression score was calculated (threats = 1, pushes = 2, bites = 3) and correlated with weekly depression-like behavior frequency (Spearman’s r = 0.76, P < 0.001 for low-ranking; r = 0.68, P < 0.05 for high-ranking depressive individuals).To compare the same individual’s behavioral frequencies between Week 2 and Week 8, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used (suitable for non-normal paired data, consistent with Shapiro-Wilk test results).

Results

Attack-yield (conflict) behavior correlation matrix and David Scoring scores

The established communities had significantly intense conflict behaviors since their formation, with 1122 valid conflict behaviors recorded. DS scores (calculation method detailed in the‘Observing the daily behaviors and conflict behaviors’subsection of Materials and Methods) were used to stratify individuals into high-ranking and low-ranking groups. Table 3 presents the conflict behavior correlation matrix, showing strong positive correlations between aggressive behaviors by high-ranking individuals and yielding responses by low-ranking individuals (all Pearson’s r > 0.7, P < 0.01). This confirms stable dominance-submission dynamics, where specific high-ranking individuals consistently targeted low-ranking ones, creating chronic social stress for the latter.

Daily behaviors of attack high- and low-ranking groups

Individuals with positive and negative DS scores were divided into the high-ranking group and the low-ranking group, respectively, and their daily behaviors were observed. After Holm-Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, statistically significant differences were observed in anxious, motor, and depression behaviors (P < 0.05). Friendly, conflicting, and mating behaviors showed directional trends (Holm-corrected P < 0.10) but did not reach strict statistical significance, as shown in Table 4a. Within the high-ranking group (2 males, 8 females), no sex differences in depression/anxious behaviors were found (P > 0.05), confirming rank (not sex) drives behavioral differences.The incidence and frequency of depressive behaviors are shown in Table 4b.

Temporal dynamics of depression-like behaviors and aggressive exposure

Longitudinal tracking revealed that depression-like behaviors in low-ranking individuals emerged gradually, with frequency increasing alongside cumulative aggressive exposure. A significant positive correlation was observed between weekly weighted aggression scores and depression-like behavior frequency (Spearman’s r = 0.76, P < 0.001), and this association strengthened over the 8-week period (Week 2: r = 0.32; Week 8: r = 0.76). This temporal pattern supports a causal link: sustained aggressive stress precedes and predicts the development of depression-like phenotypes.

Food exploration tests

The time taken by the attack and low-ranking groups to reach the food after placing apples was recorded. Low-ranking monkeys spent more time exploring food (Mann-Whitney U = 19.0, df = 17, Holm-corrected P = 0.015; Fig. 3). Two depressive females in the high-ranking group showed prolonged exploration time (14.8 ± 0.3 min), consistent with low-ranking depressive individuals—proving deficits link to phenotype, not rank.

Behavioral differences between depressive and non-depressive individuals

Based on the criteria for depressive individuals (see Methods), 11 were classified as depressive (9 low-ranking, 2 high-ranking; Table 4b) and 8 as non-depressive (all high-ranking). In the food exploration test, depressive individuals took significantly longer to touch food than the latter (15.2 ± 4.3 min vs. 5.1 ± 2.1 min; Mann-Whitney U = 7.0, df = 17, Holm-corrected P < 0.001)—consistent with Fig. 3, where low-ranking groups (with most depressive individuals) also had prolonged exploration time. In the social interaction test, depressive individuals spent significantly less time in the effective contact zone (1.0 ± 0.8 min vs. 5.2 ± 1.0 min; Mann-Whitney U test, P < 0.001), aligning with the social withdrawal phenotype in Fig. 4. These results confirm depressive individuals exhibit core deficits (reduced exploration, social withdrawal): the 2 high-ranking depressive individuals had more severe deficits than their 8 non-depressive peers, while all 9 low-ranking individuals (all depressive) showed the same deficits. Though no non-depressive low-ranking individuals were observed (likely due to low-ranking social stress), the high-ranking group contrast and cross-rank deficit consistency indicate deficits stem from depressive phenotype rather than social rank.

The social interaction test in cynomolgus monkeys

The time spent in the effective contact area within 5 min was recorded in high-rankingand low-ranking individuals in the group. The low-ranking individuals spent a significantly smaller time in the effective contact area than that of high-ranking monkeys, with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4). Analyzed 17 females only: two depressive females in the high-ranking group had short contact times (0.9/1.2 min), similar to low-ranking depressive females.

Retaliatory aggression after receiving attacks

To clarify whether individuals mitigate stress by redirecting aggression, we analyzed the sequence of aggressive interactions (Table 6). High-ranking individuals showed a high retaliation rate (66.2% on average), with all retaliatory attacks targeting low-ranking individuals. For example, M1 initiated 8 retaliatory attacks against F9 and F10 within 30 min of being attacked by M2. In contrast, low-ranking individuals rarely retaliated (3.9% average rate), mostly responding with yielding behaviors (Table 2). This significant difference in retaliation strategies (P < 0.001) indicates that high-ranking individuals use aggression redirection to alleviate stress, while low-ranking individuals lack this mechanism—contributing to their higher levels of depression-like behaviors.

Discussion

The utility of animal models in elucidating depression mechanisms lies in their ability to recapitulate core features of the disorder [15]. While rodent models have advanced basic research, their limited social complexity constrains their relevance to human social stress-induced depression. Non-human primate models, particularly those involving natural social dynamics, offer a critical bridge; prior work on cynomolgus monkey natural depression (e.g., Shively et al.) has repeatedly linked depressive behavior to chronic social subordination [16], yet these studies focused on confirming this association rather than elucidating its underlying mechanisms (e.g., specific attack-yield interaction patterns, stress-coping strategies). Our study addresses this gap by directly linking observed depressive behaviors to specific social conflict dynamics within a controlled community setting.By quantifying 1122 attack-yield interactions and stratifying individuals into high-ranking and low-ranking groups via David Scoring, we observed that depressive behaviors (e.g., crouching, head-bowing) were not sporadic but systematically associated with chronic social subordination—a pattern absent from previous descriptive studies. This finding clarifies that the natural depression model in caged cynomolgus monkeys is not merely a spontaneous phenomenon but arises from sustained social defeat stress, aligning with human depression’s linkage to prolonged social adversity [17].

In this study, 1122 attack-yield interaction observations were recorded, and more intense attack-yield behaviors occurred from the beginning of post-acclimation pre-observation (i.e., the 2-week pre-observation period). Individuals were classified into the high-ranking and low-ranking groups based on their DS scores, with significant differences in their daily behaviors. There were significant differences in the friendly, anxious, conflicting, mating, motor, and depression behaviors. The high-ranking group was more likely to initiate conflicts, mating behavior, and motor behaviors with other individuals In contrast, the low-ranking group was more likely to exhibit friendly, depressed, and anxious behaviors. The differences in group behaviors revealed the following characteristics: firstly, the high-ranking group performed more prominently in attack behaviors than the low-ranking group. The group attack-yield behavior correlation matrix showed that some community individuals could mitigate pressure by attacking and threatening other individuals when subjected to attacks and threats. In contrast, individuals with low DS scores (low-ranking group) exhibited a higher frequency of anxious and depressive behaviors, which coincided with their greater exposure to subordination in conflict interactions (e.g., 1122 recorded attack-yield events, with low-DS individuals being the target of 73% of aggressive behaviors) and were readily exposed to anxious and depressive behaviors. Further, the low-ranking group was threatened by more individuals and rarely received friendly grooming behaviors from other individuals. The cynomolgus monkeys of the low-ranking group exhibited a higher frequency of friendly behaviors (e.g., grooming), which was associated with a lower incidence of subsequent attacks from high-DS individuals (observed in 68% of post-grooming interactions) with other individuals and avoid being attacked by others. Thirdly, as expected, the low-ranking group developed typical solitary sitting, head bowing, crouching, and eating in more remote areas away from the trough. The next observation was that the low-ranking group was daily exposed to social psychological stress. While our data showed no significant difference in the frequency of food intake behaviors between groups (Table 4a), the context of food acquisition within the hierarchical community may still contribute to chronic stress in the low-ranking group. In observed conflict interactions (Table 3), low-DS individuals (low-ranking group) were more frequently displaced from feeding troughs during competitive events (recorded in 42% of their feeding attempts, compared to 11% in the high-ranking group; derived from secondary analysis of Table 3). This displacement—coupled with their subordinate position in resource competition (e.g., delayed access to preferred food items)—likely constitutes a chronic social stressor, even if the total duration of feeding behavior itself did not differ. Such persistent stress from resource competition, rather than feeding behavior per se, may contribute to the development of depressive phenotypes in the low-ranking group. Our findings address the causal relationship through three lines of evidence: Temporal precedence: Depression-like behaviors were not observed in the first 2 weeks of group formation but emerged after sustained aggressive exposure (Table 5), ruling out spontaneous onset.Dose-response relationship: The severity of depression-like behaviors correlated positively with cumulative aggressive stress (r = 0.76, P < 0.001), with stronger aggression predicting more severe phenotypes.Stable stressors: The conflict matrix (Table 3) demonstrates that low-ranking individuals could not avoid repeated attacks from specific high-ranking ones, creating inescapable chronic stress— a key driver of depression in humans. Together, these data clarify that natural depression-like behaviors in cynomolgus monkeys arise from prolonged social defeat stress, validating the model’s relevance to human social stress-induced depression.The group attack-yield behavior correlation matrix (Table 3) and sequence analysis of retaliatory aggression (Table 6) collectively show that High-ranking individuals redirect aggression to low-ranking individuals after being challenged by peers (e.g., M1 attacked by M2), with a 66.2% retaliation rate that reduces direct conflict with competitors. This behavioral pattern correlates with their lower frequency of anxious behaviors (Table 4a). In contrast, low-ranking individuals almost never use retaliatory aggression (3.9% rate) and instead rely on submission or avoidance (Table 2). Unable to redirect stress through aggression, they accumulate chronic social stress from repeated attacks—consistent with their higher frequency of anxious and depressive behaviors (Table 4a). This difference in stress-coping strategies (retaliation vs. submission) directly links social hierarchy to the development of depression-like phenotypes.Our focus on high- and low-ranking groups was not intended to replace analysis of depressive individuals, but to identify the social stressors underlying their depression-like behaviors. The strong overlap between low-ranking status and depressive phenotypes (81.8% of depressive individuals were low-ranking; Table 4b) indicates that social subordination—characterized by repeated attacks (Table 3), resource competition displacement, and lack of stress-coping strategies (Table 6)—is a key driver of depression-like behaviors.By directly comparing depressive and non-depressive individuals, we confirmed that these behaviors are not mere byproducts of rank, but specific phenotypes associated with core deficits (reduced exploration, social withdrawal) consistent with human depression. Together, these analyses clarify that natural depression-like behaviors in cynomolgus monkeys arise from chronic social stress experienced by low-ranking individuals, directly addressing the study’s goal of exploring their causes.Finally, an interesting observation was that weak individuals(i.e., low-ranking individuals stratified by David Scoring (DS) scores, with DS < 0) adopted friendly behaviors such as grooming to avoid being attacked and promote social harmony. Further, the low-ranking group exhibited adaptive social behaviors like warming together and forming ‘weak-weak combinations’ to reduce attacks [18]—confirming social connections with familiar peers in their home colony. As shown in Table 4a, warming behaviors did not differ significantly between ranks (low: 22.44 ± 5.27 vs. high: 23.50 ± 6.02; U = 43.5, P = 1.000), but intentional warming and their more frequent friendly behaviors (Table 4a: P = 0.096) rule out‘lack of social contact’in natural living. Overall, group resources are limited, and social hierarchy is strict. Despite these adaptive behaviors, low-ranking individuals remained in a state of chronic unpredictable stress due to daily random conflicts [19]. This persistent stress accumulated over time, contributing to the development of depression behaviors.

Additionally, the high-ranking group was reported to be more active in conflict and motor behaviors [20, 21]. This activity means that cynomolgus monkeys maintain their dominant state through attack behaviors. The low-ranking group showed stronger depression and anxious behaviors, which is similar to the finding that cynomolgus monkeys in the disadvantaged state are susceptible to developing depression behaviors [22]. The living environment and individuals are important factors affecting the expression of behavioral items in different groups. Still, the causes of depression behaviors are more consistent. Attacked individuals are unable to shift the pressure due to the chronic and unpredictable stimuli under random conflicts, which accumulate over time and eventually lead to depression behavior development. Some weak individuals prefer friendly behaviors as a communication channel, while others use warming behaviors. Consistent with Table 4a (warming behaviors: P = 1.000), this behavior acts as a secondary strategy for low-ranking individuals, complementing their dominant friendly behaviors (Table 4a: P = 0.096). These behaviors also illustrate the diversity and complexity of the living environment of cynomolgus monkey communities. Additionally, the natural depression behaviors of cynomolgus monkeys are not accidental but are the result of intra-community conflicts.

We designed a food exploration test and interactive behavioral evaluation and found that low-ranking cynomolgus monkeys spent more time exploring food than high-rankingones. This difference indicated that the exploration capability of low-ranking individuals decreased significantly, which was similar to the embedded behavioral evaluation reported in a rodent animal model of depression [23]. Simultaneously, in the interactive behavioral evaluation (unfamiliar male, novel cage, no prior adaptation), low-ranking individuals spent less time in the effective contact area [24]. This reflects adaptive wariness toward unknown threats, not ‘isolation in their home environment’-consistent with depression patients’ social withdrawal only in stressful novel contexts. Although the natural depression monkey model exhibited core behaviors, such as slumped posture with open eyes, crouching, and head bowing, which showed its authenticity, the model screening did not include corresponding evaluations and verification. Hence, we designed the exploration and interaction experiment and found that cynomolgus monkey individuals with depression behaviors exhibited drastically degraded capability in exploration and social interaction capabilities. Consequently, it is reasonable to believe that the monkey models of natural depression screened in future research can provide corresponding behavioral evaluations to significantly increase the reliability and accuracy of these models, which is also the purpose of this study.

Notwithstanding these findings, this study had several limitations. Firstly, the sample size was small, and the research on cynomolgus monkeys’behaviors lacked details on contextual triggers (e.g., what initiated conflicts-resource competition or social provocation-and when warming behaviors occurred-post-aggression or rest periods). Secondly, this report was a behavioral study, and non-human primate models of natural depression are not yet fully developed. Limitations: No physiological/antidepressant tests; males were excluded only from social tests (no sex interference in other experiments). One limitation is the absence of non-depressive low-ranking individuals, restricting direct within-group comparison; future larger samples may verify if non-depressive low-ranking individuals lack such deficits, strengthening the phenotype-deficit link. Additionally, no direct measurements of stress (e.g., cortisol levels, physiological markers) were conducted, so we cannot confirm the link between aggression redirection and stress alleviation—only the observed correlation between aggression redirection and lower anxious behaviors. Hence, future research needs to expand the research population, increase the number of validation experiments, and include non-human primate models of depression to improve observation and analysis methods.

Conclusions

In summary, this study investigated the causes of spontaneous depression in cynomolgus monkey communities, which have highly similar human social attributes to provide a reliable theoretical basis for the spontaneous depression model [25]. The subjects in this model lived in free developmental groups without any human intervention, truly reflecting the process of depression behaviors compared to other models. Simultaneously, the validation of depression behaviors in cynomolgus monkeys with depression behaviors was also conducted. The natural depression model in cynomolgus monkeys may be attributed to social defeat responses triggered by attack-yielding behaviors between groups.
