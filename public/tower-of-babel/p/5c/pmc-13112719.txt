Provu video stylet-assisted intubation under direct laryngoscopy and video laryngoscopy in high Arne score patients: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Mirco Leo, Christian Zanza, Aman Mahajan, Raymond Planinsic, Fabio Piccolella, Domenico Ruvolo, Benedetta Savarese, Gabriele Savioli, Dana Shiffer, Antonio Voza, Giulia Racca, Marco Panasyuk, Roberto Leo, Alessandro Girombelli, Francesco Saglietti, Gloria Nuzzolo, Sabino Mosca, Fabrizio Racca, Yaroslava Longhitano

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDifficult tracheal intubation remains a critical issue in airway management. While videolaryngoscopy (VL), particularly using hyperangulated blades such as the C-MAC D-Blade, has become the gold standard due to superior glottic visualization compared to direct laryngoscopy (DL), intubation challenges persist. The ProVu™ Video Stylet (PVS) is a novel device that integrates real-time visualization with a guided endotracheal tube system.MethodsThis is an open-label, randomized, three-arm parallel-group, we evaluated whether the use of PVS in combination with either DL or VL could improve tracheal intubation outcomes in anesthetized patients with anticipated difficult airways, defined by an Arné score ≥ 11. The primary outcome was first-pass intubation success. Secondary outcomes included laryngoscopy and intubation times, number of attempts, need for external airway manipulation, and complications related to difficult intubation.ResultsOne hundred fourteen patients undergoing elective surgery were randomized into three groups: VL using C-MAC D-Blade alone, PVS with DL, and PVS with VL. First-pass intubation success was significantly higher in both PVS-assisted groups compared to the VL-only group (100% vs. 86.8%, P = 0.04). Median intubation times were also shorter in the PVS groups (32.7 and 32.0 s) compared to the control group (38.9 s). No episodes of oxygen desaturation below 90%, need for rescue techniques, or severe complications were observed. Principally, minor complications were observed only in the videolaryngoscope-alone group.ConclusionThe integration of the ProVu™ with either direct or video laryngoscopy significantly improves first-attempt intubation success and reduces intubation time compared to videolaryngoscopy alone in patients with anticipated difficult airways.Trial registrationRegistered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05902858) on 23th january 2023.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03754-7.

Introduction

Repeated laryngoscopy attempts and delays in tracheal intubation are associated with an increased risk of complications, including hypoxemia, cardiac arrest, and death [1–3]. Current guidelines for the management of difficult airways emphasize the importance of maximizing first-attempt intubation success [4–6]. Recent studies have demonstrated that videolaryngoscopy (VL) provides superior performance compared with the traditional Macintosh laryngoscope, including improved laryngeal visualization, higher first-pass success rates, and shorter intubation times [7–10].

Randomized controlled trials comparing hyperangulated VL to direct laryngoscopy (DL) in patients with predicted difficult airways have shown that VL provides better glottic visualization, requires fewer additional maneuvers, and achieves higher overall success rates [11–13]. A recent meta-analysis by Hansel et al. further confirmed that VL improves laryngeal exposure and reduces both failed intubation attempts and complications such as hypoxemia [14, 15].

However, hyperangulated VL introduces unique challenges, shifting the difficulty from visualizing of the larynx to advancement of the endotracheal tube (ETT). Despite optimal glottic visualization, the angulated blade design can hinder passage of the ETT through the vocal cords [16]. Although the use of a conventional rigid stylet in conjunction with videolaryngoscopy is well established and recommended in current difficult airway guidelines, the present study does not evaluate a standard non-articulating stylet. Instead, we investigated a dynamic, articulating video stylet that provides continuous distal tip visualization and real-time bidirectional angulation control during endotracheal tube advancement. This distinction is clinically relevant, as conventional rigid stylets do not permit active adjustment of the tube trajectory once glottic visualization has been achieved. The novelty of our investigation lies in assessing an articulating video stylet rather than a standard rigid introducer, comparing its performance when combined with both direct and indirect laryngoscopy, and evaluating its potential to mitigate the well-described “visual paradox” of hyperangulated videolaryngoscopy, where excellent glottic exposure does not necessarily translate into straightforward endotracheal tube delivery.

A video stylet has recently been introduced for insertion into a standard ETT. It integrates a visualization system at the stylet tip with a built-in light source, similar to a fiberoptic device. Additionally, it features a tube-guidance mechanism controlled by a proximal thumbwheel (ProVu), allowing bidirectional rotation and enabling both flexion and retroflexion of the stylet built-in ETT within a 95° range of motion. The device can also assist in confirming correct ETT placement (Figs. 1 and 2; Supplemental Video 1).

This trial aims to test the hypothesis that the use of a video stylet, combined with either direct or indirect laryngoscopy, in anesthetized patients with a predicted difficult airway may improve intubation performance by allowing precise alignment of the distal tip and Murphy’s eye of the ETT with the glottis and trachea via proximal thumbwheel control operated by the anesthesiologist (Fig. 1). This technique is expected to improve first-pass intubation success compared with the use of a hyperangulated C-MAC videolaryngoscope blade or standard direct laryngoscopy.

The secondary objectives of the study are to evaluate: (i) Time required for laryngoscopy; (ii) Time required for intubation; (iii) number of intubation attempts; (iv) Type and incidence of complications; (v) need for adjunctive maneuvers; (vi) need for assistance from another anesthesiologist; (vii) Need to change the intubation strategy; and Viii) post intubation complications (hoarseness, airway injury, sore throat).

Methods

Study design

This open-label, randomized, three-arm parallel-group trial was conducted at a tertiary university hospital in South-West Piedmont (Italy) following approval by the local Ethics Committee on Clinical Investigations (protocol No. 557) and registration on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05902858). The study was conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines [17]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Consecutive adult patients scheduled for elective surgery requiring tracheal intubation and presenting with one or more predictors of a difficult airway were included in the study.

Five anesthesiologists, all co-authors of this study and experienced difficult airways management, participated in the trial as intubators. None had prior clinical experience with the ProVu video stylet (PVS). Before initiating clinical use, each anesthesiologist underwent targeted training, including at least 70 tracheal intubations performed using the combined techniques on a Laerdal Airway Management Trainer® (Laerdal Medical, Stavanger, Norway).

The medical history and physical examination records of all patients scheduled for surgery were screened for predictors of difficult airway management according to institutional practice, including neck circumference and the simplified Arnè score (range 0–48) [18]. Demographic, comorbidities and type of surgery

We selected to induce anesthesia in these patients because current Difficult Airway Society (DAS) guidelines lack clear high-level evidence (Grade D) supporting the routine use of awake intubation. However, we excluded all patients who, according to DAS guidelines, require mandatory awake intubation, including those with planned awake or nasal intubation; Increased risk of gastric aspiration; or predicted difficult face mask and laryngeal mask ventilation [19–24].

Furthermore, some studies have shown no significant differences in first-attempt success rates between awake intubation and intubation performed after induction of general anesthesia. However, awake intubation has occasionally been associated with an increased number of attempts, episodes of hypoxemia, and cardiovascular instability, although no deaths were reported [19–22].

In our study, we used videolaryngoscopy, which is currently recommended as the device of choice for all tracheal intubations (predicted and unpredicted), particularly in the presence of predictors of difficult airway [1–5, 19–21, 24].

Iin the group that underwent direct laryngoscopy, the ProVu stylet was used in combination with direct laryngoscopy, effectively transforming it into a form of videolaryngoscopy. No patients enrolled in the trial experienced life-threatening complications at any stage, consistent with the risk mitigation strategies described above [19–22].

The ProVu stylet features a thumb-controlled tube-guidance system located at the proximal end of the device. This mechanism allows bidirectional rotation of the distal ETT tip, enabling both flexion and extension within a total 95° range motion. This device can also be used to confirm correct ETT placement [5]. (Figures 1 and 2, Supplemental video 1). The ProVu stylet is supplied with a dedicated intubating tube available in four internal diameters (6.5, 7.0, 7.5, and 8.0 mm).

Selection criteria and randomization

Eligible patients were adults aged ≥ 18 years scheduled for tracheal intubation and presenting with a simplified Arné score ≥ 11.

Patients were exluded if they had a mouth opening ≤ 2 cm, a planned awake or nasal intubation, an increased risk of gastric aspiration, predicted difficult facemask and laryngeal mask ventilation [6, 21–24], or if they refused to provide informed consent.

Patients were randomly assigned to the study groups using a web-based system (Sealed Envelope; https://www.sealedenvelope.com). Randomization was conducted using a minimization-biased coin algorithm, balancing to balance body mass index (BMI) and the Arné risk index across groups. An independent statistician supervised the randomization process to minimize potential allocation bias.

Procedures

All patients were connected to standard monitoring equipment. Patients with a BMI ≤ 30 kg/m² were positioned in the sniffing position, whereas those with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² were placed in the ramped position. Preoxygenation was performed using 100% oxygen administered via facemask for 3 min. Additionally, patients with a BMI > 30 kg/m² received 10 cm H₂O of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) for 5 min before induction of anesthesia.

Anesthesia was induced with propofol (2–3 mg/kg) and fentanyl (1–2 µg/kg). Neuromuscular blockade was achieved using rocuronium (0.6 mg/kg, or 1 mg/kg for rapid sequence induction), and tracheal intubation was performed when the train-of-four (TOF) count reached zero.

In the control group, the C-MAC videolaryngoscope (D Blade; Karl Storz, Tuttlingen, Germany) was used in combination with a rigid stylet pre-shaped to match the blade curvature and inserted into a standard ETT [5] (Figs. 1 and 2).

In the two intervention groups, the standard stylet was replaced with the ProVu Stylet (PVS). In the first group, the PVS was used in combination with a direct laryngoscope (Macintosh blade size 3 or 4) (Fig. 3c). In the second study group, the PVS was used with a videolaryngoscope (C-MAC D-Blade) (Fig. 3b). After obtaining laryngeal visualization, intubation was performed using the PVS under direct glottic visualization through the device itself [25]. In the third study group, only C-MAC D-Blade Videolaringoscopy was used (Fig. 3a).

Primary and secondary endpoints

The primary endpoint was first-attempt intubation success, defined as successful tracheal tube placement during a single maneuver. An attempt was considered unsuccessful if the laryngoscope blade was withdrawn from the mouth without achieving intubation. Each attempt was immediately interrupted if peripheral oxygen saturation dropped below 90% during airway instrumentation. In the event of a failed attempt, mask ventilation was provided. After three unsuccessful attempts, the technique was considered failed and the standard intubation protocol was initiated.

Secondary endpoints included procedural time, divided into two components. Laryngoscopy time was defined as the interval between insertion of the laryngoscope blade into the mouth and the passage of the ETT beyond the teeth.

Intubation time was defined as the interval between the passage of the ETT beyond the teeth and cuff inflation. Total intubation time was calculated as the sum of laryngoscopy time and intubation time.

Additional data collected included the the device used for successful intubation; the number of intubation attempts, defined as the number of times the ETT was inserted into the mouth (a value > 1 indicated tube removal and reinsertion); the need for external laryngeal manipulation; the use of additional equipment; the need for assistance from the supervising anesthesiologist; and the occurrence of complications (hoarseness, airway injury, sore throat, oxygen desaturation SpO2 < 90%, and esophageal intubation).

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculation was based on detecting a clinically meaningful absolute difference of 7% in first-pass intubation success between groups, with a two-sided alpha of 0.05 and 80% power. In the absence of device-specific randomized comparative data for articulating video stylets, effect size estimation was informed by published studies evaluating advanced dynamic tube-guidance techniques, including videolaryngoscopy combined with flexible endoscopy. In particular, reference was made to a comparable study evaluating intubation success rates using a videolaryngoscope versus a flexible endoscope to guide the orotracheal tube tip [25]. Although these techniques are not identical to the ProVu™, they share the principle of real-time distal tip visualization and active trajectory control. Based on these assumptions, 38 patients per group were required. Given the exploratory nature of this study and the limited comparative data available, the trial was designed to detect a clinically relevant difference rather than to establish superiority.

Data analysis was performed using R software (version 3.6.1). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were reported as absolute numbers and percentages. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Fisher test. Data with normal distribution were analyzed using Student’s t-test, while non-normally distributed data were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test, as appropriate. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 1,020 patients were assessed for eligibility, and 114 patients were enrolled in the study between August 2023 and December 2024 after application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria. All enrolled patients completed the study, and none were excluded from the final analysis in any of the three study groups (see Fig. 5).

Primary and secondary outcome results are shown in Table 3. First-pass intubation success achieved in 100% of patients in both PVS groups, compared with 87% in the C-MAC D Blade VL group (P = 0.04). All failures in the C-MAC D Blade VL group (n = 5) were due to the need to reshape the styleted ETT because of a more anterior airway. No attempts were interrupted due to oxygen desaturation (SpO2, < 90%), and no cases required bag-mask ventilation. Laryngoscopy time was comparable among the three groups. However, both median intubation time and median total intubation time were significantly longer in the C-MAC D Blade VL group (Table 3).

No cases of severe airway injury (uvular injury, inhalation, lingual, hypoglossal, laryngeal nerves damage) were observed. There were no episodes of oxygen desaturation < 90% and no instances of oesophageal intubation. Difficult bag-mask ventilation, the need for external laryngeal manipulation, the use of alternative technique, or intervention by the supervising anesthesiologist were not reported. No postoperative complications such as hoarseness were observed in any group.

Discussion

This study found that the use of the ProVu™ video stylet in conjunction with either videolaryngoscopy (VL) or direct laryngoscopy (DL) represents a viable strategy for managing predicted difficult airways. Specifically, the ProVu stylet improved first-pass intubation success and reduced intubation time compared with the use of the C-MAC D Blade VL alone.

Recent research underscores the role of VL in improving airway management safety and outcomes. However, unchanneled hyperangulated VL blades may complicate ETT advancement [1–5, 15].

The relative effectiveness of bougies versus stylets remains a topic of debate, as both have limitations in allowing real-time tip adjustment during intubation.

A randomized trial reported improved first-attempt success rates when VL was combined with a flexible bronchoscope compared with VL alone (91% vs. 67%) [25]. Similarly, in our study. first-attempt success rate was higher in the ProVu stylet (100% vs. 87%). This improvement is likely due to the enhanced maneuverability and real-time tip control provided by the ProVu [25]. Importantly, the ProVu technique requires only a single provider, unlike the fiberoptic-laryngoscope technique described by Mazzinari et al., which requires two providers [25].

In our study, poor direct laryngoscopy (DL) view (Cormack-Lehane grade III or higher) were observed in 21% of cases. In contrast, hyperangulated VL provided optimal glottic visualization in nearly all patients. Despite these visualization challenges with DL, first-attempt intubation success was 100% with the ProVu stylet (PVS), whereas five patients in the C-MAC D-Blade group required a second attempt. These findings align with those reported by Teoh et al. and Al Qasim et al., who reported high rates of glottic visualization (up to 87%) but also documented greater number of multiple intubations attempts in patients intubated using the C-MAC videolaryngoscope [26–28].

Intubation times were significantly longer in the C-MAC D-Blade group, likely reflecting the difficulty associated with ETT advancement despite satisfactory glottic views. The superior performance of the ProVu stylet may be attributed to its ability to dynamically adjust tube angulation in real time, facilitating smoother and more effective intubation. This evidence aligns with the higher rate of complications observed in the C-MAC D-Blade group, where longer intubation times are associated with more attempts and frequent blade adjustments. These factors result in increased movement within the oral cavity, which can contribute to airway injuries and sore throat, due to multiple attempts to locate the tracheal opening and correctly position the endotracheal tube.

In this trial, the C-MAC D-Blade group showed a higher incidence of sore throat, soft tissue injury, and dental trauma, although these individual complications did not reach statistical significance. However, when combined, the overall airway injury rate (dental trauma + soft tissue injury) was significantly higher in the C-MAC D-Blade group, with a p-value of 0.025.

Notably, there were no reports of severe airway injuries—such as uvular injury, inhalation, or damage to the lingual, hypoglossal, or laryngeal nerves, in any of the three groups. Additionally, no cases of desaturation (SpO₂ < 90%) or esophageal intubation were reported.

This study also reinforces the concept of the “visual paradox” associated with videolaryngoscopy (VL), wherein improved laryngeal visualization does not always translate to easier or more successful intubation. Previous studies have reported VL success rates ranging from 85% to 100%, despite excellent glottic views [20, 22–29]. Our findings suggest that the ProVu™ video stylet (PVS) mitigates this paradox by enabling real-time ETT tip adjustments, thereby enhancing success rates, even in anatomically difficult cases.

Notably, the DL group achieved intubation success comparable to the VL group, despite poorer laryngeal visualization. This outcome is likely attributabed to the video-assisted and maneuverability features of the ProVu stylet, which may compensate for suboptimal views by allowing more precise ETT guidance.

The existing literature remains inconsistent regarding the relative effectiveness of VL when used with bougies versus stylets. However, Rios et al. advocate for the use of a dynamic or articulating introducer, such as a flex-tip stylet, ProVu™, or a fiberoptic bronchoscope, over a conventional rigid stylet for tracheal intubation in patients with difficult airways (Level of Evidence: Low; Grade of Recommendation: Strong) [5, 24].

We hypothesize that operator experience heavily influences intubation success rates, while PVS may reduce dependence on expertise by providing a direct view and adjustable tip control [28–31]. This may explain why DL with ProVu stylet performed comparably or better than VL with a standard bougie or stylet. In our study patients underwent various non-cardiothoracic surgery including ENT and maxillofacial surgeries, which are associated with a higher incidence of difficult airway management [31, 32].

Notably, despite the presence of predictive difficult airway factors, only 8 patients (20%) in our study exhibited a Cormack-Lehane grade 3 view with DL, and none had grade 3 views with VL. Although some patients did not demonstrate a high-grade Cormack–Lehane view during laryngoscopy, it is important to distinguish between a predicted difficult airway and a difficult laryngoscopic view. All enrolled patients had a Simplified Arné score ≥ 11 and presented with at least two predictors of difficult airway management, a threshold associated with an increased likelihood of difficult intubation. Predictive airway scores are preoperative risk stratification tools, whereas the Cormack–Lehane classification reflects intraoperative glottic exposure under specific conditions. These represent related but distinct constructs. Therefore, our cohort appropriately reflects patients with anticipated difficult airways, even if not all ultimately exhibited high-grade laryngoscopic views. This underscores both the improved visualization provided by VL and the limitations of current airway prediction tools. We compared ProVuTM to C-MAC D-Blade VL as it was the device available at our insititution. However, similar results would likely be expected with other hyperangulated VLs that operate on the “look around the corner” principle rather than the direct line-of-sight approach of DL [28–31].A potential limitation of PVS is cost, as the disposable video stylet is more expensive than standard stylets. Nevertheless, because the device is preloaded into the ETT and operated by a thumb-controlled mechanism, it does not require multiple operators. Future studies should evaluate its cost-effectiveness. The ProVu Stylet may be advantageous in both predicted and unpredicted difficult airways due to its ability to provide a clear view of the glottis. This visual advantage helps guide the endotracheal tube more accurately, resulting in a high first-pass success rate [28–31].

It may also be particularly useful in patients with limited cervical spine mobility, as it does not require significant head or neck repositioning to obtain a clear view. This makes it ideal for patients with potential cervical spine injuries, as it minimizes the risk of secondary spinal cord damage during intubation [14, 30].

Thanks to its slim design, the ProVu Stylet is compatible with any adult ETT and may serve as a suitable alternative in patients with restricted mouth opening (temporomandibular joint dysfunction, facial edema, following certain maxillofacial surgeries or irradiation) as well as in cases of morbid obesity where the narrowing is due to the excess soft tissue in the upper airway, both of which complicate intubation [31].

For awake intubations performed after topical anesthesia, the ProVu Stylet has also been recognized as a device that improves patient comfort while reducing oropharyngeal trauma [32–37].

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center design and involved a limited number of anaesthesiologists, potentially limiting generalizability. Second, all participating anaesthesiologists had extensive experience with flexible bronchoscopy, which may have influenced the results. The ProVu™ stylet may require a skill set more closely aligned with bronchoscopic techniques than with conventional laryngoscopy; therefore, prior experience may have contributed to the high success rates observed. Future studies should evaluate its performance among less experienced providers.

Blinding was not feasible, introducing the possibility of performance or observer bias. To mitigate subjective bias in airway assessment, airway predictor grading was independently reviewed. We used the Cormack–Lehane classification rather than POGO or Fremantle scores because our study included both direct and video laryngoscopy. However, future research should consider incorporating alternative scoring systems that may better capture glottic exposure across different intubation modalities.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the combination of direct or indirect laryngoscopy with ProVu™ video stylet in anesthetized patients with predicted difficult airway may increase first-attempt intubation success and reduce intubation time compared with hyperangulated VL using a styleted ETT alone. Hoever, before recommending the routine incorporation of this combined technique into the difficult airway management armamentarium, further evidence from larger, multicentre randomized clinical trials involving a broader range of patients and anesthesiologists is needed. The recently published “Guidelines on Strategies for the Universal Implementation of Videolaryngoscopy” (published June 2025) aim to provide a standardized algorithm to facilitate the widespread adoption of videolaryngoscopy (VL). These guidelines are structured as comprehensive cognitive aids grounded in human factors principles and are designed to support a smoother transition toward VL as the standard of care.

The guidelines outline four key components: (1) structured pre-Intubation Preparation, including thorough patient and airway assessment, equipment verification, and team briefings; (2) a Standardized Videolaryngoscopy Sequence, emphasizing optimal patient positioning and technique selection for successful intubations; (3) systematic management of technical and physiological challenges, including rescue strategies for failed intubation, troubleshooting recommendations, and management of hypoxemia or hemodynamic instability; and (4) defined team communication protocols, including role assignment, shared visualization via the VL monitor, and cognitive support tools to enhance coordination and support less experienced operators.
