Manipulation of interpersonal synchrony in psychotherapy using extended reality: Mapping future directions
Authors: Tal Krasovsky, Zvi Tubul-Lavy, Sigal Zilcha-Mano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interpersonal synchrony is the dynamic coordination of two individuals over time, reflected in the alignment of their actions, emotions, thoughts, and physiological processes. Interpersonal synchrony is a core mechanism associated with therapeutic alliance and treatment outcomes in psychotherapy. However, given the largely correlational nature of current literature, manipulation of synchrony is essential in order to reveal its potential causal relationships with therapeutic relationships in psychotherapy. Despite growing recognition of its clinical significance, synchrony remains difficult to manipulate because it often emerges implicitly, shaped by relatively stable individual and interpersonal characteristics. This paper first introduces an Extended Reality (XR)-based framework for understanding interpersonal synchrony, drawing from Milgram and Kishino’s Reality–Virtuality continuum. We then use this framework to map options for synchrony manipulation during therapeutic encounters involving real and digital entities. We then explore the different challenges of implementing these synchrony manipulations, including conceptual, technological, and ethical issues. This outlines a future-oriented perspective on the potential of XR to provide an infrastructure for synchrony manipulation. This may provide new opportunities for tailoring therapy to individual synchrony profiles, with potential applications in treating disorders marked by interpersonal dysfunction, such as social anxiety or autism spectrum disorder. Ultimately, XR manipulation of synchrony may transform both research and clinical practice by bridging the gap between static trait-like tendencies and the facilitation of adaptive therapeutic flexibility.

Introduction

Interpersonal synchrony is the dynamic coordination of two individuals over time, reflected in the alignment of their actions, emotions, thoughts, and physiological processes (Ackerman & Bargh, 2010; Palumbo et al., 2017; Scheidt et al., 2021). A foundational mechanism in human social interaction and mental health, interpersonal synchrony appears to support adaptive emotion regulation. Emotion dysregulation is further implicated across many common forms of psychopathology, including anxiety and mood disorders (Koole & Tschacher, 2016). Interpersonal synchrony has been linked to psychotherapy outcomes, empathy, and therapeutic alliance, such that movement synchrony is most often associated with outcomes, physiological synchrony with empathy, and language or vocal coordination with alliance (Wiltshire et al., 2020). More recent meta-analyses suggest that the association is more consistent for patient-perceived alliance or connection than for therapeutic outcome, and may depend on the modality being measured (Gregorini et al., 2025; Jennissen et al., 2025). Despite its clinical relevance, synchrony is not easily amenable to change because it often emerges implicitly and may reflect relatively stable tendencies across settings, including therapeutic contexts (Zilcha-Mano, 2025b). Since modifying interpersonal synchrony may have the potential to affect therapeutic outcomes, this work proposes a framework for the manipulation of synchrony using Extended Reality technology.

Empirical findings accumulating across the sciences, from neuroscience and physiology to behavioral and social interaction research, indicate that interpersonal synchrony plays a central role in supporting coordination, social bonding, and adaptive regulation (Koole & Tschacher, 2016; Zilcha-Mano, 2025b), whereas lower levels of synchrony are associated with psychopathology (Shaul-Tsoran & Aderka, 2025). Focusing specifically on psychotherapy, converging evidence links synchrony in movement, physiology, speech, and facial expressions with core therapeutic processes such as empathy, alliance, and symptom improvement (Wiltshire et al., 2020; Zilcha-Mano, 2025b). Taken together, these findings may indicate that synchrony has the potential to operate as a curative mechanism that facilitates co-regulation, emotional attunement, and interpersonal plasticity during treatment. Yet, the literature is characterized by mixed findings: synchrony in some studies predicts positive outcomes, in others negative outcomes, and yet in others shows no association at all (Zilcha-Mano, 2025b), and its outcomes seems to be context-related (Gordon et al., 2025). Thus, it has been proposed that synchrony can index either adaptive collaboration or maladaptive interpersonal entanglement, depending on the individual’s trait-like synchrony signatures, activation tendencies, and contextual demands within the therapeutic dyad (Zilcha-Mano, 2025b). This complex pattern underscores both the promise of conceptualizing synchrony as a potential mechanism of therapeutic change and the need for greater precision in identifying which components of synchrony matter, under what conditions, and for whom. At the same time, it highlights a critical gap: existing correlational findings cannot determine whether synchrony is merely a marker of interpersonal functioning or a causal contributor to therapeutic change, emphasizing the critical need for experimental methodologies capable of manipulating synchrony systematically and ethically.

In order to manipulate interpersonal synchrony, it is essential to understand the different facets encompassing synchrony and how they are demonstrated in the therapeutic encounter. DaSilva and Wood (2024) provide a terminology map for the different facets along which synchrony can vary. They define six dimensions along which interpersonal synchrony can be positioned: periodicity, discreteness, spatial similarity, directionality, leader–follower dynamics, and observability. In psychotherapy, each of these six dimensions has the potential to play a crucial role in shaping the therapeutic process. Periodicity, or the regularity of behaviors, can reflect the therapeutic progress; for instance, as a client learns to regulate emotions more effectively, their verbal and nonverbal rhythms might become more stable and predictable. In contrast, a shift to less periodic behavior could signal a deterioration in the client's mental state (Mundt et al., 2012; Stassen et al., 1998). Discreteness can be observed in turn-taking during dialogue, where transitions between speaking and listening reflect attunement of the therapist to the therapeutic session (López-Florit et al., 2021). Spatial similarity might manifest in matched body language, which can reflect but also enhance rapport and signal empathy as treatment progresses (Maurer & Tindall, 1983). Directionality can manifest in psychotherapy either as in-phase (positive) synchrony, as in movement (Cohen et al., 2021) or as anti-phase (negative) synchrony, as in physiological synchrony (Peysachov et al., 2025). Leader–follower dynamics can be demonstrated where therapists may alternate between leading (e.g., in directive therapies) and following (e.g., in supportive therapies). Patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) tend to exhibit follower dynamics, mirroring the therapist’s cues more passively (Shaul-Tsoran & Aderka, 2025). In supportive-expressive therapy, for example, therapists initially gather information (following the client’s lead) and then shift to a more directive mode, providing interpretations (Book, 1998). Lastly, observability is central to therapy, as both verbal and nonverbal synchrony may provide cues about the client’s internal states and the quality and strength of the therapeutic alliance. For instance, a therapist's ability to observe, be responsive, and adapt to subtle changes in a client’s posture or tone of voice can influence the therapeutic relationship as well as treatment outcomes.

In the therapeutic encounter, each member of the therapeutic dyad exhibits a trait-like synchrony signature for each parameter of behavioral synchrony (Zilcha-Mano, 2025b), which may have some unconscious elements and may not be easily modified. Modulation of synchrony is essential in order to reveal the potential causal relationships between interpersonal synchrony and therapeutic relationships in psychotherapy. The effect of synchrony modulation is not necessarily uniform. In some cases, improving synchrony can promote the therapeutic relationship, or decreasing synchrony can “perturb” the encounter, both promoting necessary change – depending on the individual’s tendencies to activate/deactivate the synchrony system (Zilcha-Mano et al., 2025). However, in order to examine this effect one needs to experimentally manipulate synchrony characteristics, in one (or more) of the ways described below. The difficulty with manipulating interpersonal synchrony lies in the fact that trait-like tendencies to synchronize may not be easily amenable to change in humans (Zilcha-Mano, 2021, 2025a; Zilcha-Mano & Fisher, 2022). To this end, we propose a novel view of the potential ways in which synchrony may be manipulated, using Extended Reality (XR).

Extended Reality (XR) is an umbrella term, encompassing Virtual Reality (VR), Augmented Reality (AR), and Mixed Reality (MR) – which are environments where real and digital entities are merged to different levels (Holopainen et al., 2023). XR is an increasingly important player in clinical interactions (Krasovsky et al., 2020), and particularly in mental health (Pons et al., 2022; Tabassum et al., 2025). Within this framework, XR offers powerful platforms for exploring and manipulating interpersonal synchrony. XR technologies have the potential to facilitate experimentally controlled and dynamic social interactions, essential for investigating synchrony mechanisms in therapeutic contexts. Although rigorous experimental work is still lacking (Bouguettaya & Aboujaoude, 2024), dyadic and group therapy is increasingly using XR technologies, suggesting its non-inferiority compared to traditional therapy for populations such as patients with psychotic disorders (Dellazizzo et al., 2021; Spark et al., 2025), MDD (Lee et al., 2025), PTSD (Dai et al., 2025) and anxiety disorders (Loenen et al., 2022). While XR-based therapy is typically positively regarded by patients and therapists (Dhunnoo et al., 2024; Dilgul et al., 2021), additional work refining the interaction between patients and therapists within XR-based settings is essential in order to understand the mechanism of operation of XR-based therapy.

Importantly, as synchrony is conceptualized as a relational process that emerges from the joint dynamics of patient and therapist, the manipulation is acting not on the patient as an object, but on the digital representation of the dyad. Manipulation of the environment

Physical environments are known to impact health (Ulrich, 1984), emotions and well-being (Yao et al., 2021) as well as on opinions and judgment of personality of others (Gosling et al., 2002). For therapeutic encounters in psychotherapy, environments may be manipulated when the setting of the therapeutic encounter is digital, rather than real. Manipulation of digital representation of the environment is something that we are all familiar with in our remote interactions (e.g. “Zoom”), when we can select a beach house or a fancy office as the backdrop during conversation (similar to option B in Fig. 1). In fact, recent work is demonstrating that the digital environment (background in a zoom call) affects perceptions of competence and trust in online conversations (Cook et al., 2023) and student learning in an online class (Kelly et al., 2024). In a digital psychotherapy session, manipulation of the environment could potentially serve two main purposes:1.Anxiety/Arousal Regulation: Adjusting the environment can help regulate arousal levels, such as using a stress-inducing setting for exposure therapy in flight phobia or a calming setting for emotion regulation training.2.Generalization: Changing the environment can also facilitate the generalization of synchrony developed in therapy to other contexts. For example, exposing patients to different settings during therapy may enhance their ability to transfer self-regulation and synchrony skills to real-world situations.

Manipulation of the patient and/or therapist’s digital representation

Manipulation of the patient and/or therapist’s digital representation can also play a critical role in assessing the generalization of skills learned in therapy. For instance, adjusting the digital representation's characteristics—such as gender, age, or socio-economic and ethnic background of the therapist—allows for the examination of how well patients can maintain a balance between self-regulation and co-regulation with different types of interaction partners. While there are no studies addressing this manipulation in psychotherapy, previous research has demonstrated that avatar appearance in conversations affects the level of emotional disclosure (Bradbury et al., 2022). In addition, the manipulation of one’s own representation in digital space provides anonymity which enables closer emotional interactions (Jang et al., 2025). By evaluating the impact of different digital identities for the therapist and/or the patient during the sessions, it may be possible to facilitate the understanding of what factors underlie the therapeutic alliance in XR-based psychotherapy.

Manipulation of the therapist’s movement in digital space

A third factor associated with interpersonal synchrony which can be manipulated is movement. Movement is key in our perception of others (Fraser et al., 2025), and the level of movement expressiveness, manipulated in VR interpersonal interactions, has been demonstrated to affect the quality of social interactions (Fraser et al., 2022). Synchronization of movement within psychotherapy has been demonstrated to be associated with improved outcomes (Ramseyer & Tschacher, 2014), suggesting that potential manipulation of movement synchrony may improve outcomes. Manipulation of the therapist movement characteristics, in order to increase or decrease synchrony with the patient, is possible when the therapist (real or computerized) is displayed digitally – since if the real therapist is presented, manipulation of their movement would be impossible (given the trait characteristic nature of interpersonal synchrony). There are several options for XR-based psychotherapy where this type of manipulation is possible (Fig. 1, panels C,D,E). However, there are several challenges which need to be overcome in order to manipulate interpersonal synchrony in XR. These will be described in the following section.

Technical challenges in using extended reality for synchrony manipulation

Interpersonal synchrony is multi-modal in nature and can be manifested by evaluating emotional states, physiological signals, brain activation patterns, or behavioral patterns such as facial expressions, speech, posture, or movement. This work will focus mostly on the manipulation of interpersonal synchrony via body movement patterns and facial expressions, but the principles of manipulation of interpersonal synchrony presented here may be extended to other manifestations of synchrony.

Measuring interpersonal synchrony in movement. Interpersonal synchrony in movement can be measured in several ways, depending on the methodology used for motion capture (Cornejo et al., 2017). Effective manipulation of interpersonal synchrony relies on precise and real-time tracking of behavioral synchrony (e.g., body movements and expressions). Several methods exist, each with distinct advantages and limitations.1.Laser-based Tracking (e.g., SteamVR Lighthouse): This method involves external base stations emitting lasers detected by body-worn sensors like HTC Vive trackers. This approach offers high accuracy, precision, and minimal latency, closely approximating expensive laboratory-based motion tracking systems (Lubetzky et al., 2019; Merker et al., 2023). However, it requires dedicated physical setup, sensor calibration, and clear line-of-sight, limiting mobility and scalability (Obukhov et al., 2023).2.Inertial Measurement Units (IMU): IMU systems, composed of accelerometers and gyroscopes, track limb orientation and position via inverse kinematics, independent of external visibility. IMU suits allow freedom of movement and wireless operation. Nevertheless, frequent calibration is necessary, and environmental noise can cause drift over time (García-de-Villa et al., 2023)3.Camera-based Tracking (RGB and Depth Cameras): Systems like Microsoft's Kinect or software solutions such as OpenPose and MediaPipe enable markerless, multi-person motion tracking. Camera-based methods are easy to deploy but suffer from lower accuracy, especially with rapid movements or occlusion (body parts hidden from camera view). These systems demand significant computational resources, leading to latency and reduced sampling rates (Nakano et al., 2020).4.Standalone VR Consoles: Devices using basic inside-out tracking primarily track head and hand movements, typically excluding accurate lower-body tracking. Although algorithms can predict leg movements, these remain insufficient for complex synchronization tasks requiring nuanced bodily coordination (Holzwarth et al., 2021; Lubetzky et al., 2019).

Measuring eye and facial expressions. Eye and facial expressions remain a significant challenge in the design of virtual reality platforms (Tanenbaum et al., 2020). However, recent advancements incorporate eye and facial expression tracking, substantially enhancing avatar realism and emotional synchrony in XR. Eye-tracking capabilities in modern VR headsets facilitate authentic eye contact and gaze-following behaviors. Facial tracking captures nuanced expressions, projecting them onto avatars in real-time. Full-face tracking enhances non-verbal communication fidelity, significantly enriching emotional and interpersonal synchrony (Grewe et al., 2021). However, these technologies remain experimental, facing current challenges in widespread implementation due to hardware limitations and computational demands (Moreno-Arjonilla et al., 2024).

Computational challenges of real-time synchrony manipulation. Aside from accurate measurement, manipulating interpersonal synchrony in XR requires real-time data acquisition and immediate processing, which may present additional challenges. Such processing can burden CPUs/GPUs, causing performance limitations, heat generation, or delays. Cloud-based processing could alleviate device constraints but introduce latency from network communication, negatively affecting real-time interpersonal synchronization. Additional computational challenges are associated with cross-platform compatibility. Current XR environments are diverse, each employing distinct tracking protocols. Applications using Apple's ARKit or Google's ARCore for smartphone-based AR differ substantially from PC VR platforms using proprietary tracking solutions. Thus, it may be difficult to adapt the XR solution to other environments and devices. Future scenarios involving cross-platform XR interactions, such as between AR glasses and VR headsets, would require innovative solutions for bridging gaps in tracking fidelity and ensuring the generalization of the computational solution.

Ethical considerations in XR-based manipulation of synchrony

We assert here that manipulating interpersonal synchrony experimentally may potentially be associated in modification of treatment outcomes. However, a necessary first step is to address ethical considerations of this manipulation in a research setting. To do this, we draw on a comprehensive ethical framework from an adjacent domain - AI-based speech analysis in a clinical context (Petti et al., 2023), which articulates five key ethical dimensions relevant to our work: autonomy, privacy, welfare, transparency, and fairness. Autonomy asserts that all studies will include: explicit informed consent explaining what components of synchrony may be manipulated, plain-language explanations of why manipulation is used, the client’s right to pause, withdraw, or decline feedback at any point, and collaborative goal-setting regarding whether synchrony enhancement or reduction aligns with their comfort and values. This mirrors the best-practice recommendations for vulnerable populations and technically opaque interventions. Importantly, real therapists are never manipulated. Only their digital representation (avatar/digitally controlled agent) may be modified, and only in contexts where both therapist and client have explicitly agreed. Privacy is critical since acquisition of movement trajectories, gaze, and micro-gesture patterns can uniquely identify individuals, similarly to voiceprint data. Movement patterns can uniquely identify individuals, serving as a "behavioral fingerprint" (Nair et al., 2023). Coupled biometric data (facial expressions, gaze, heart rate) further intensifies these risks, potentially exposing emotional states or medical conditions. (Aziz & Komogortsev, 2025). Such vulnerabilities highlight the importance of robust data encryption, local data processing policies, and strict measures for anonymizing or deleting sensitive user data. Furthermore, it is essential that synchrony data will not be used for diagnosis, prediction, or classification, without human involvement in the process and synchrony signatures will not be shared with third parties. Welfare of participants indicates that in order to prevent increased distress during manipulation, the manipulation will be performed only in safe, monitored and reversible ways: the client can stop immediately if discomfort increases, therapists receive training in detecting signs of emotional overload during synchrony shifts, and, lastly, manipulations are never allowed to increase anxiety beyond clinically appropriate exposure contexts. Synchrony manipulation should always support, not replace, therapist empathy. Manipulation is not intended to simulate fake attunement, and real relational cues remain primary, while XR cues are augmentative only. In addition, the technical application of manipulation needs to take into account practical considerations of comfort and user fatigue, as cumbersome equipment can limit interaction duration and impair synchrony quality. Therefore, ergonomically designed hardware and lightweight wearable sensors (Souchet et al., 2023) are crucial for prolonged and realistic interactions conducive to therapeutic applications. Transparency implies that clients receive easy-to-understand explanations of synchrony, what is being adjusted, and how it may feel. Therapists must understand how manipulation is carried out; “black-box synchrony shifts” are prohibited. Finally, Fairness asserts that synchrony signatures are not diagnostic, they cannot be used to assess suitability, compliance, or personality, and also that clients retain full control over whether synchrony profiles are saved or discarded.

By maintaining these pillars of ethical conduct, manipulation of interpersonal synchrony can be examined safely within the sensitive infrastructure of the therapeutic encounter.

How to manipulate interpersonal synchrony: identifying the “right” components to manipulate

Once all technological and ethical challenges are considered, one must evaluate which facet(s) of interpersonal synchrony are to be manipulated. Along the framework of DaSilva and Wood (2024), five of the six facets considered can be manipulated (not necessarily in isolation) - periodicity, discreteness, spatial similarity, directionality and leader–follower dynamics (observability is assumed here to be more difficult to manipulate). For example, spatial similarity can be manipulated by modifying the movement patterns of the virtual therapist to be more or less similar to those of the patient. This would involve real-time capture of the patient’s movement and adaptation of the therapist’s movement to adapt to it in real-time. Leader-follower dynamics may be manipulated by changing temporal characteristics of the therapist’s movement patterns such that the amount of movement, for example, would lead or follow that of the patient across different delays. Indeed, recent work is beginning to explore the potential of manipulating temporal characteristics of interpersonal synchrony using mimicry (Tarr et al., 2018; Yekani et al., 2024), but the introduction of this type of manipulation to interpersonal synchrony in the context of psychotherapy is significantly more challenging. Naturally, manipulation can be of more than one element – resulting in a complex pattern of monitoring and change affecting multiple facets of behavior.

The manipulation of interpersonal synchrony may further differ according to time-sensitive, within-individual dynamically changing characteristics. XR technologies can facilitate nuanced manipulations of these dimensions, adapting therapeutic interactions dynamically within-individual. For example, therapists' avatars or digital agents in XR environments can adjust synchrony behaviors, complementing clients' trait-like tendencies toward hyperactivation or deactivation of synchrony mechanisms (Zilcha-Mano, 2025b). Therapists' digitally controlled representations can dynamically modulate movements to optimize therapeutic responsiveness to the patients’ trait-like synchrony signatures (Zilcha-Mano et al., 2025), a manipulation unfeasible with purely human interactions due to inherent trait tendencies of the therapists themselves. XR platforms further allow environmental manipulations affecting synchrony indirectly by modulating client arousal or anxiety. Customized virtual settings can either induce stress or promote relaxation, aiding exposure therapies or emotional regulation strategies. Additionally, varying therapist characteristics digitally (e.g., age, gender, ethnicity) can evaluate and reinforce clients' generalization of synchrony skills across diverse interpersonal scenarios.

Applications for mental health

The integration of XR technologies for manipulating interpersonal synchrony is a long-term translational goal that presupposes a sequence of basic and clinical studies designed to identify which components of synchrony are beneficial, for whom, and under what conditions, before any use with vulnerable mental health populations is ethically justified. We suggest that psychotherapy in an XR context remains psychotherapy in the traditional sense: a structured, relational, collaborative process between a therapist and a client, guided by evidence-based clinical principles, but augmented through digital elements that can temporarily modify sensory, contextual, or interactional parameters. In XR-assisted psychotherapy the therapeutic relationship remains central and unchanged in its foundational role. XR serves as a tool, not as a replacement for the therapist. The XR environment provides controlled real-time manipulations of interpersonal cues which cannot be manipulated reliably in a purely human-to-human encounter. The goal is not to create an artificial therapy, but to expand the therapist’s ability to explore collaboratively with the client relational processes and adjust them as needed, all of which are already part of the treatment (Zilcha-Mano, 2017). Thus, XR is conceptualized as a modulatory layer over traditional therapeutic processes, not as a new form of therapy.

From a basic research perspective, manipulating interpersonal synchrony enables a thorough exploration of synchrony as a mechanism for therapeutic change. By experimentally varying synchronization patterns, researchers can identify individual-specific adjustments needed in each person's trait-like synchrony signatures to optimize mental health outcomes (Zilcha-Mano, 2025b, 2025d). Insights gained from this line of research can facilitate the development of evidence-based practical interventions (Zilcha-Mano, 2025c; Zilcha-Mano & Krasovsky, 2024). Clinically, manipulating synchrony within XR environments can substantially improve interpersonal synchrony skills, particularly among individuals with interpersonal disabilities, social anxiety, or Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). By observing responses to various synchronization manipulations, therapists can precisely define a patient's unique synchrony signature (Zilcha-Mano, 2021, 2025d, 2025a). This knowledge allows tailored therapeutic interventions designed to modify synchrony patterns effectively, thereby enhancing interpersonal interactions and overall mental health. Personalized synchrony profiles thus enable therapists to customize treatments, maximizing intervention efficacy and patient outcomes.

The capability to manipulate interpersonal synchrony through XR environments offers substantial promise for tailoring therapeutic interventions to distinct individual profiles (Zilcha-Mano, 2025b) . Patients characterized by hyperactivation of the synchrony system excessively depend on external cues and often experience difficulties self-regulating their emotional states, becoming overly responsive or reactive to the synchronization attempts of others. In these cases, XR-based interventions can purposefully reduce avatar synchrony responsiveness, encouraging patients to rely more on internal regulatory capacities and thus promoting greater emotional independence and resilience. Gradually reducing the synchrony levels presented by virtual therapists or avatars can assist these individuals in developing adaptive coping strategies, mitigating their dependency on continuous external interpersonal feedback. Conversely, individuals displaying deactivation tendencies typically exhibit diminished responsiveness to interpersonal synchrony cues, often leading to social withdrawal or difficulties forming meaningful close interpersonal interactions (Zilcha-Mano, 2025b). For these individuals, XR environments can deliberately amplify synchrony cues, enhancing the sensitivity and responsiveness of the virtual avatars or therapists to the patient's subtle movements and expressions. By progressively increasing interpersonal synchrony within therapeutic interactions, patients can gradually learn to recognize, respond to, feel less aroused and more comfortable in close intimate interactions, and gradually start to reciprocate these cues, thereby fostering improved social connectedness and emotional engagement.

Critically, these tailored interventions within XR will enable therapists to be dynamically responsive to their patients’ evolving needs within the therapeutic session, and adjust synchrony parameters in real-time, facilitating an individualized, responsive therapeutic experience. Such nuanced modulation of synchrony provides patients with immediate tailored feedback, allowing for incremental adjustments in behavior that aligns closely with their evolving therapeutic needs and capacities, ultimately enhancing overall mental health outcomes (Zilcha-Mano, 2025b).

Conclusion

Extended Reality may provide a significant advancement in researching and applying interpersonal synchrony within therapeutic contexts. Despite existing technological challenges and considerations surrounding ethics, computational demands, platform compatibility, comfort, and data security, XR offers novel opportunities for precise, adaptive modulation of synchrony. With technological advances and positive future findings from research, these innovative capabilities may hold promising implications for enhancing personalized, effective mental health interventions.
