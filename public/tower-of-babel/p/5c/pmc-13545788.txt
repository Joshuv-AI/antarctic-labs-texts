Development and validation of the KAP-arbovirus survey: a new tool for assessing physician preparedness in endemic regions
Authors: Oriana A Regalado-Gutiérrez, Fhabián S Carrión-Nessi, Maya A Ocanto-Ystúriz, Luz M Ríos-Di Ciaccio, Andreína H Rondón-Pérez, David A Forero-Peña
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe co-circulation of arboviruses like dengue, Zika, and chikungunya presents a remarkable diagnostic and management challenge in the Americas, particularly in countries with compromised health systems like Venezuela. Physicians are central to an effective response, yet no validated instrument exists to assess their preparedness. This study aimed to develop and psychometrically assess the first comprehensive survey to identify knowledge gaps and factors influencing the clinical management of arboviruses among Venezuelan physicians.MethodsA cross-sectional validation study was conducted from April to May 2025 using a 47-item online survey administered to physicians in Venezuela. Psychometric properties were evaluated through item analysis, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with Promax rotation, and two-step cluster analysis to establish performance categories. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and the Kuder-Richardson 20 (KR-20) coefficient.ResultsThe instrument demonstrated preliminary evidence supporting construct validity. EFA of the attitudes and practices scales revealed two-factor structures, distinguishing between “Proactive Stance” and “Risk Perception” (attitudes), and between “Patient-Centered Education” and “Systemic Public Health Engagement” (practices). Internal consistency coefficients for the multi-dimensional knowledge (KR-20 = 0.52) and attitudes (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.523) scales reflected these construct’s heterogenicity, while the practices scale showed acceptable consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.691). Illustrative application of the instrument revealed that 43.8% of physicians had low knowledge, mainly among non-frontline physicians (55% vs. 37.3%, p = 0.028). A significant attitude-practice gap was identified, driven by low performance on system-dependent behaviors like disease notification.ConclusionsThis study presents a newly developed instrument with preliminary psychometric support for assessing physician preparedness for arboviral diseases in endemic, resource-limited settings. The tool aims to differentiate between individual clinical competencies and system-dependent practices, offering a structured approach to designing targeted medical education strategies and strengthening public health infrastructure in similar contexts. Further studies with larger samples are warranted to improve its psychometric properties and identify KAP gaps among physicians.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15078-w.Keywords: Arboviruses; Health knowledge, attitudes, practice; Physicians; Surveys and questionnaires; Validation study; Venezuela; Medical education

Background

Arboviral diseases, transmitted by arthropod vectors, represent a major public health threat worldwide, particularly in the tropical and sub-tropical regions of the Americas [1]. The recent increase in dengue cases, together with the ongoing circulation of Zika and chikungunya viruses, has created a complex syndromic challenge for clinicians [2–5]. Because these pathogens are often transmitted by the same vector, Aedes aegypti, and present with overlapping clinical features, accurate diagnosis and appropriate management require a high degree of clinical expertise [6–8]. In 2024, the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) reported the highest incidence of dengue ever recorded [2], prompting a regional health alert [9, 10] and highlighting the need for adequate clinical preparedness and effective public health responses [11].

These challenges are particularly evident in Venezuela, where a prolonged humanitarian crisis has precipitated the collapse of the national health system [12]. The exodus of qualified health professionals [13], disruption of vector control programs [14, 15], and the absence of official epidemiological reporting since 2016 [16] have contributed to the persistence and re-emergence of endemic arboviruses. In this context, physicians on the frontlines are the most critical pillar of disease control, responsible for timely diagnosis, appropriate case management, and patient education to mitigate morbidity, mortality, and onward transmission.

Despite the urgent need for physician’s preparedness, a critical public health gap persists. There is no reliable, validated instrument to diagnose the specific strengths and weaknesses in physician preparedness for co-circulating arboviruses in Latin America. Previous studies have often focused on a single disease, targeted community populations rather than clinicians, or lacked the rigorous psychometric validation necessary for a diagnostic tool [17]. As there is no available tool to systematically assess physicians’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP), it is impossible to determine whether poor outcomes stem from knowledge deficits that can be fixed with training, or from systemic barriers that require infrastructural investment. Consequently, public health authorities and medical educators have limited evidence to guide the development of targeted educational and training strategies.

Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to develop and conduct a preliminary psychometric evaluation of a multidimensional survey instrument designed to assess physicians’ preparedness for co-circulating arboviruses in an endemic, resource-constrained setting. As a secondary objective, the instrument was applied to explore patterns of knowledge, attitudes, and practices among physicians in Venezuela, in order to illustrate its practical utility and provide contextually relevant preliminary data. By generating both methodological and applied evidence, this study seeks to provide a scalable prototype that may support efforts to strengthen health systems responses to arboviral diseases in Venezuela and other at-risk countries.

Methods

Study design and sample

A cross-sectional study was conducted between April and May 2025 to develop and psychometrically assess an online survey instrument. The target population comprised physicians practicing in Venezuela, including general practitioners, specialists, postgraduate residents, and community-based integrated physicians. Due to the lack of an official, up-to-date national registry of physicians, a non-probabilistic convenience sampling method was employed. This approach is considered appropriate for pilot studies focused on instrument validation, where the primary goal is to assess psychometric properties rather than population-level prevalence [18, 19]. Participants were recruited via a shareable link to a survey created on the Google Forms® platform (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA), which was distributed through professional medical networks and social media platforms (WhatsApp®, Instagram®, Threads®, X®).

For comparative analysis, participants were stratified into two groups based on their clinical role. “Frontline” physicians (n = 102, 63% ) were defined as those whose primary professional activity involves the initial evaluation, diagnosis, and management of patients with suspected or confirmed arboviral infections. All other physicians were classified as “non-frontline” (n = 60, 37%). Participation was voluntary, anonymous, and confidential, with informed consent obtained electronically at the beginning of the survey.

Data collection instrument

A novel 47-item questionnaire was designed based on the KAP framework [20]. The initial item pool was generated through a comprehensive review of current clinical practice guidelines for arbovirus management published by the PAHO and the World Health Organization (WHO) [6–8]. The instrument is organized into five sections (Supplementary Data 1):

Knowledge: there were 23 dichotomous-response questions covering arbovirus epidemiology, transmission, clinical presentation, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention. Correct answers were scored as 1 and incorrect answers (including a “don’t know” option to minimize guessing) as 0, for a total score ranging from 0 to 23.

Knowledge: there were 23 dichotomous-response questions covering arbovirus epidemiology, transmission, clinical presentation, diagnosis, treatment, and prevention. Correct answers were scored as 1 and incorrect answers (including a “don’t know” option to minimize guessing) as 0, for a total score ranging from 0 to 23.

Attitudes: included 6 items assessing physician perceptions of arbovirus risk, confidence in management, and stance on prevention. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with a total score from 6 to 30.

Higher scores in each KAP dimension indicate more comprehensive knowledge, more positive attitudes, or more appropriate practices. A total composite score was not calculated, as each domain measures a distinct construct.

Instrument validation process

A multi-step process was employed to establish the instrument’s psychometric properties.

Content validity

Content validity was established by grounding the instrument’s items directly in evidence based WHO/PAHO guidelines [1, 7, 21, 22]. An initial draft was also reviewed by a panel of three infectious disease specialists and two epidemiologists for clarity, relevance, and comprehensiveness.

Construct validity

Construct validity was assessed using two complementary methods. First, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was planned for the KAP scales to identify underlying latent factors. Principal component analysis with Promax rotation was selected, as the underlying constructs (e.g., different facets of attitudes) were hypothesized to be correlated. The suitability of the data for EFA was evaluated using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Second, a two-step cluster analysis was used to empirically describe performance categories for each KAP dimension (e.g., “low knowledge” vs. “high knowledge”). It was chosen as a descriptive classification method to identify natural groupings within the data due to its ability to handle mixed data types and determine an optimal number of clusters based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and silhouette coefficient. Additionally, item difficulty and discrimination indices were calculated for the knowledge scale.

Reliability analysis

Internal consistency was evaluated for each scale. Based on the understanding that complex clinical domains like medical knowledge are often heterogeneous and multi-dimensional, it was hypothesized a priori that the knowledge and attitudes scales might not represent unidimensional constructs [23, 24]. Consequently, lower reliability coefficients were anticipated, as they would reflect this underlying complexity rather than instrument error. For the dichotomous knowledge scale, the Kuder-Richardson 20 (KR-20) coefficient was calculated. For the Likert-scaled attitudes and practices dimensions, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was used.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS® Statistics version 27. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation [SD], median, interquartile range [IQR], frequencies, percentages [%]) were used to summarize the data. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess normality. Group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to assess relationships between the KAP dimensions. To identify factors associated with low knowledge, a binary logistic regression model was built using a backward stepwise (Wald) method. The final model’s goodness-of-fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and Nagelkerke’s R², with results reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Construct validity: factor analysis

An EFA was conducted on the three scales. For the 23-item knowledge scale, the KMO measure was 0.485, below the recommended threshold of 0.5, and the data were deemed unsuitable for EFA. Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p < 0.001).

For the 6-item attitudes scale, the KMO measure was 0.687 and Bartlett’s test was significant (p < 0.001). A two-factor solution was extracted using principal component analysis with Promax rotation, which explained 54.1% of the total variance. Items A3, A5, and A6 loaded onto the first factor, while items A1, A2, and A4 loaded onto the second factor.

For the 6-item practices scale, the KMO measure was 0.723 and Bartlett’s test was significant (p < 0.001). A two-factor solution was extracted, explaining 58.7% of the total variance. Items P2, P3, and P4 loaded onto the first factor, while items P1, P5, and P6 loaded onto the second factor.

Correlations between dimensions

Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed a weak, positive correlation between total knowledge and attitude scores (rs = 0.216, p = 0.006) and between total attitude and practice scores (rs = 0.219, p = 0.005). No significant correlation was found between total knowledge and practice scores (rs = 0.071, p = 0.366) (Supplementary Data 3).

Factors associated with low knowledge

A binary logistic regression model identified several factors associated with having low knowledge (Fig. 4). Failure to identify warning signs as a hospitalization criterion was the strongest predictor of low overall knowledge (aOR = 11.692, 95% CI = 3.605–37.92). Not knowing the correct transfusion criteria for thrombocytopenic dengue patients (aOR = 3.639, 95% CI = 1.518–8.72) and being unaware that arboviral infections are often asymptomatic (aOR = 3.789, 95% CI = 1.481–9.689) were also significantly associated with low knowledge.

Discussion

The principal contribution of this study is the development and preliminary psychometric evaluation of the KAP-Arbovirus survey, the first comprehensive instrument to assess physician preparedness for the synergistic threat of co-circulating arboviruses in Latin America. The descriptive findings on physicians’ KAP levels are presented primarily to illustrate the application of the instrument in a real-world endemic setting, should not be interpreted as nationally representative estimates. In contexts where arboviral co-circulation intersects with health system fragility, the absence of validated assessment tools limits the ability to design targeted educational and structural interventions. By providing preliminary assessment of a structured and psychometrically examined framework, this study addresses a methodological gap in epidemic preparedness research within Latin America.

Application of the KAP-Arbovirus survey in a Venezuelan endemic setting

A key finding of the instrument application is the significant discordance between physicians’ positive attitudes and their self-reported practices. While the vast majority recognized arboviruses as a major public health problem (92%) and expressed a desire for more training (84%), a substantial proportion (42%) reported engaging in inappropriate practices. This “attitude-practice gap” is not uniform. Our factor analysis provides a structural explanation for this phenomenon, separating practices into two distinct domains: “Patient-Centered Education” and “Systemic Public Health Engagement”. Physicians reported high adherence to practices within their individual control, such as educating patients on warning signs and prevention measures. In contrast, practices that depend on a functional health system—such as participating in formal training, notifying cases to epidemiological surveillance systems, and conducting long-term patient monitoring—were performed far less frequently.

These findings illustrate how the survey may help identify discrepancies between attitudes and self-reported practices, which provides information that may guide future educational and health-system interventions. In a context where official epidemiological data is absent and resources are scarce, physicians may perceive that their notification efforts are futile, leading to a collapse in this critical surveillance function despite their positive attitudes. This implies that interventions focused solely on changing individual attitudes are destined to fail. Effective strategies must be bifurcated: they must continue to support individual clinicians with education while simultaneously advocating for and investing in the rebuilding of essential public health systems.

Instrument validity and the multi-dimensionality of physician preparedness

Methodologically, this study contributes a newly developed instrument with preliminary psychometric evidence to a field where such tools are scarce. Unlike many KAP surveys that rely only on face validity, our approach included multiple statistical and psychometric assessment, which revealed a nuanced structure for each domain. Most notably, the knowledge dimension proved to be a multi-dimensional construct. The inability to derive a stable factor structure for the knowledge domain, together with the observed KR-20 coefficient (0.52), suggests that arbovirus-related knowledge encompasses multiple heterogeneous content areas, which is expected when multidimensional and broad constructs are assessed [23, 24]. While this may partially explain the modest reliability estimate, the coefficient also indicates limited measurement precision and highlights the need for further refinement of the knowledge domain in future iterations of the instrument to improve its psychometric performance.

KAP findings in the Venezuelan context: critical gaps and the “learning by doing” effect

The application of the instrument revealed significant and concerning gaps in physician preparedness. Nearly half of the respondents (43.8%) demonstrated low knowledge, a finding that echoes studies in other resource-limited settings [25]. We identified critical misconceptions with direct clinical consequences. For instance, fewer than a quarter of physicians (22.8%) correctly identified that thrombocytopenia is no longer a formal diagnostic criterion for dengue per current WHO/PAHO guidelines. This likely reflects an ingrained adherence to outdated 1997 guidelines [26] and highlights that passive access to updated information—reported by over 75% of our sample—is insufficient to change entrenched clinical habits.

Encouragingly, our findings suggest that clinical exposure is a powerful educator. Frontline physicians, despite being significantly younger, demonstrated superior knowledge and higher rates of recent training compared to their non-frontline peers. This “learning by doing” effect underscores the importance of practical experience. This suggests that interventions should not be limited to traditional continuing medical education but must also include structured mentorship and clinical rotation opportunities for non-frontline physicians to gain practical experience in arbovirus management.

Limitations of the study

The use of multiple statistical and psychometric analysis provides a comprehensive assessment of physician preparedness. However, the study has limitations. The non-probabilistic convenience sampling, while pragmatic and appropriate for a pilot validation study, limits the generalizability of the descriptive KAP findings to all physicians in Venezuela. The self-reported nature of the online survey introduces a potential for social desirability bias, although anonymity was employed to mitigate this risk. Furthermore, the logistic regression model produced wide confidence intervals for certain predictors due to quasi-complete data separation. This statistical phenomenon occurs when an incorrect answer to a specific question is a very strong predictor of the outcome, which limits the precision of the odds ratio estimate but also underscores the critical diagnostic importance of that knowledge item. Finally, the reliability coefficients observed for the knowledge and attitude domains indicate that additional refinement is required before stronger conclusions regarding measurement performance can be drawn. Although the multidimensional nature of these constructs may partially contribute to these findings, limited measurement precision remains an important limitation of the current version of the instrument. Future studies should explore alternative scale structures, including the development of more homogeneous subdomains. For this matter, future research should involve applying this instrument to a larger sample and conducting longitudinal studies to assess the real-world impact of educational interventions on physician practices and, ultimately, patient outcomes.

Conclusions

This study presents the KAP-Arbovirus survey, a novel instrument with preliminary psychometric evidence for assessing physician preparedness to manage arboviral diseases in endemic, resource-limited settings. The tool aims to differentiate between individual clinical competencies and practices that may depend on broader health system factors, offering a structured approach to designing targeted medical education strategies and strengthening public health infrastructure in similar contexts. The application of the survey in a Venezuelan pilot sample demonstrated its potential to identify educational and preparedness gaps across knowledge, attitudes, and practices domains.

Although further validation studies involving larger and more diverse populations are needed, the survey provides a structured framework that may support continuing medical education initiatives and preparedness assessments in regions affected by arboviral diseases. This instrument offers a scalable model for assessing and improving arbovirus management capacity across other endemic nations facing similar challenges.
