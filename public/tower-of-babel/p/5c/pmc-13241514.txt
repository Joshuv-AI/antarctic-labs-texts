Optimizing single-lead ECG axis for AI-based detection of myocardial diseases
Authors: Masamitsu Nakayama, Ryuichiro Yagi, Kyohei Daigo, Yoshinori Katsumata, Rahul C. Deo, Calum A. MacRae, Shinichi Goto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wearable devices enable electrocardiograms (ECGs) outside traditional healthcare settings. While these devices are usually equipped with single-lead ECGs to monitor rhythm abnormalities, recent studies show utility in detecting myocardial diseases when combined with artificial intelligence (AI). However, the optimal single-lead axis for detecting myocardial diseases beyond rhythm abnormality remains unclear. To address this knowledge gap, we trained models to detect 3 myocardial diseases: left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVSD), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), and cardiac amyloidosis (CA) using single-lead ECGs, systematically synthesized at 10-degree intervals, leveraging the limb leads from 12-lead ECG data. For all 3 diseases, the highest discriminations were observed near the aVR axis and its inverse. The optimal angles were 20° for LVSD (AUROC 0.884 [95% CI: 0.877–0.891]) and HCM (0.911 [0.900–0.922]), and 210° for CA (0.893 [0.888–0.899]). These findings may inform the development of wearable ECG devices with utility beyond rhythm abnormality detection.

Introduction

Wearable electrocardiogram (ECG) devices have enabled cardiac monitoring outside traditional healthcare settings, eliminating the need for visiting healthcare providers for some conditions1,2. Currently, wearable ECGs are primarily used for detecting rhythm abnormalities using a single lead3,4. However, recent studies have demonstrated that single-lead ECGs, when combined with artificial intelligence (AI), have the potential to detect left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVSD)5–7, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM)8,9, and cardiac amyloidosis (CA)10,11. Indeed, previous studies have suggested that each lead in a standard 12-lead ECG carries unique diagnostic information for AI-based detection12,13. Furthermore, our previous study demonstrated that models trained with a common single-lead achieve promising performance in detecting LVSD, HCM, and CA14. The standard 12-lead ECG configuration was historically determined arbitrarily and has remained unchanged for over a century15. Theoretically, any frontal plane lead axis can be synthesized from Lead I and Lead II based on the mathematical framework of Einthoven’s triangle. From this perspective, we hypothesized that universal single-lead ECG axes may exist for structurally similar cardiac diseases, such as myocardial diseases that mainly affect the left ventricle. However, to our knowledge, there are no studies that systematically explored the optimal-lead axis beyond the conventional leads.

In this study, we aimed to identify the optimal single-lead axis for AI-based detection of myocardial diseases by systematically synthesizing leads at 10-degree intervals, leveraging the limb leads of 12-lead ECGs. We evaluated their performance in detecting myocardial diseases that affect the function of the left ventricle, such as LVSD, HCM, and CA. Detection of atrial fibrillation (AF) was used as a reference task to ensure that the ability to detect rhythm abnormality was preserved.

Results

Patient population

As the data source to generate single-lead ECGs with various axes, standard 12-lead ECGs were collected from Brigham and Women’s Hospital (BWH) in the United States. The BWH cohort comprised a total of 75,033, 23,592, and 24,331 ECGs for the detection of LVSD, HCM, and CA, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 1–3), derived from cohorts described in our previous studies16–18. The test cohort comprised 3928 patients for LVSD (475 cases), 3693 for HCM (124 cases), and 4860 for CA (127 cases) (Table 1). The mean ages were 66.8 (SD 14.3) for LVSD cases, 51.5 (SD 14.9) for HCM cases, and 71.6 (SD 9.3) for CA cases. The proportions of male patients were 70.1% for LVSD, 64.5% for HCM, and 81.9% for CA. Highest diagnostic performance was observed near the aVR axis and its inverse

To investigate axis-specific AI model performance, we synthesized single-lead signals from 0° to 350° at 10-degree intervals according to the Einthoven and Goldberger formulas19 and standard vector synthesis (Fig. 1), using Leads I (0°) and II (60°) extracted from the 12-lead ECG. These synthesized leads were then used to train disease detection models. The training/testing processes for each model were repeated five times to account for potential variations caused by the initialization vector. The performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) in the test set with 95% confidence interval (CI) calculated from five AUROC scores obtained from independent runs. Since the primary focus of the wearable ECGs in the current settings is the detection of rhythm abnormalities, we first trained models to detect AF. Their performance varied minimally across angles, with AUROC ranging from 0.979 to 0.986, suggesting that the detection of irregular rhythm is not dependent on specific lead axis (Supplementary Fig. 7). For LVSD, HCM, and CA, the models consistently showed the highest AUROC near the aVR (210°) and its inverse (30°) axes. In detecting LVSD, the best-performing angles were 20°, 210°, and 30°, with AUROCs of 0.884 [95% CI: 0.877–0.891], 0.881 [0.873–0.889], and 0.880 [0.876–0.885], respectively (Fig. 2A). Similarly, for HCM, the top-performing angles were 20°, 200° and 210°, with AUROCs of 0.911 [0.900–0.922], 0.903 [0.889–0.917], and 0.902 [0.894–0.909] (Fig. 2B). For CA, the best results were observed at 210°, 30° and 20°, with AUROCs of 0.893 [0.888–0.899], 0.891 [0.877–0.905] and 0.888 [0.881–0.895] (Fig. 2C). In contrast, the model trained on Lead I (0°) achieved high but reduced performance compared with these synthesized leads with AUROCs of 0.865 [0.859–0.870] for LVSD, 0.885 [0.871–0.900] for HCM, and 0.881 [0.874–0.888] for CA. The best-performing lead axis was consistent across patient characteristics

To assess the robustness of the patterns of angle-specific performance across patient characteristics, subgroup analyses were performed stratified by sex, race, age, heart rate, and AF groups. The pattern showing the highest AUROC in axis 20° to 30° (and its inverse) was preserved across the subgroups (Fig. 3). Although slight performance fluctuations were observed in specific subgroups, such as age and race for HCM, these differences were not statistically significant. Integrated models exhibited higher sensitivity at an optimal axis than Lead I

To understand the performance of the models on deployment, we integrated the outputs of the five models produced by five independent training runs by averaging the predictions. The optimal axes identified from these integrated models were consistent with those observed in the analysis of individual runs (20° for LVSD, 20° for HCM, and 210° for CA) but with a slightly higher AUROC of 0.902 [0.889–0.916], 0.934 [0.912–0.954], and 0.917 [0.892–0.940], respectively for LVSD, HCM and CA (Fig. 4). Compared with the integrated model using Lead I, the optimal-lead models showed significantly higher AUROC for LVSD (Lead I: 0.887 [0.873–0.900], P < 0.05) and HCM (Lead I: 0.917 [0.892–0.940], P < 0.05), whereas the difference was not significant for CA (Lead I: 0.905 [0.876–0.929], P = 0.259). Further evaluation examined performance within subgroups defined by ECG abnormalities, such as premature atrial contractions (PACs) and premature ventricular contractions (PVCs). The integrated models were robust to real-world wearable noise

To evaluate model performance with real-world wearable noise, we added the noise signals derived from the MIT-BIH Noise Stress Test Database21 onto the test set ECGs. Notably, the addition of real-world noise did not result in any meaningful change in model performance, which supports the robustness of our model to real-world noise encountered in wearable devices (Supplementary Fig. 12). For the optimal-axis models, AUROCs without noise were 0.914 [0.895–0.931] for LVSD (20°), 0.914 [0.858–0.958] for HCM (20°), and 0.956 [0.906–0.991] for CA (210°), and remained largely unchanged under noisy conditions at 0.901 [0.879–0.920], 0.905 [0.853–0.948], and 0.950 [0.896–0.985], respectively. Similarly, for the Lead I models, AUROCs without noise were 0.905 [0.885–0.924] for LVSD, 0.876 [0.800–0.934] for HCM, and 0.936 [0.833–0.975] for CA, and remained stable under noisy conditions at 0.891 [0.870–0.911], 0.869 [0.824–0.907], and 0.910 [0.835–0.970], respectively.

Discussion

In our systematic evaluation of single-lead ECG of synthesized leads, axes near the aVR (and its inverse) consistently achieved the highest AUROC for detecting LVSD, HCM, and CA. The models trained with single-lead ECGs to detect LVSD and HCM from these optimal axes showed higher sensitivities compared to Lead I when the specificities were matched. These results support the existence of a common optimal-lead axis with the highest information content for detecting this range of diseases affecting the left ventricular myocardium.

A key finding of this study is that the optimal-lead axes were consistently located near aVR and its inverse (−aVR) across all three diseases and two cohorts. Notably, the top-performing angles for LVSD (20°, 210°, and 30°), HCM (20°, 200°, and 210°), and CA (210°, 30°, and 20°) all clustered within the same directional region. From a deep learning perspective, aVR and its inverse (−aVR) are mathematically equivalent, and AI models are expected to extract equivalent information from both orientations, which may explain why axes near both 30° and 210° consistently appeared among the top-performing leads. Furthermore, the differences in AUROC among these top-performing angles were not statistically significant. These results suggest that the clustering of optimal axes in the aVR directional region is more meaningful than the ranking of individual angles. It may imply that the superiority of this axis reflects a shared electrophysiological mechanism rather than a disease-specific phenomenon. The aVR and its inverse (−aVR) lie along the same axis, aligned with the main left ventricular depolarization and repolarization vectors. This directional alignment allows the aVR axis to capture the largest projection of left ventricular electrical activity, maximizing the signal content relevant to myocardial disease detection. Given that LVSD, HCM, and CA all primarily affect the left ventricular myocardium, it is physiologically conceivable that a lead axis oriented along the dominant left ventricular vector would provide the greatest diagnostic information. This interpretation is consistent with prior clinical observations demonstrating that aVR provides a unique direct view into the left ventricle and carries strong prognostic value for underlying myocardial disease22–24. Interestingly, the presence or absence of AF did not significantly affect myocardial disease detection, suggesting that rhythm irregularity plays a limited role in the model’s decision-making. This is consistent with our previous finding that deep learning models primarily rely on morphological features of the ECG waveform rather than rhythm-based characteristics25.

Previous studies have generally assessed single-lead performance within the conventional 12-lead ECG configurations13,26. Although most studies have reported good performance with limb-lead models, some have suggested that chest leads may offer superior performance for detecting myocardial diseases9,10. However, chest leads may be less suitable for wearable devices, as they require electrodes to be placed on specific thoracic positions, making consistent placement challenging for patients in daily use27. In practice, certain single-lead ECG devices can be rotated or repositioned to acquire signals along a preferred axis28,29. Our findings offer valuable insights to guide the future design of wearable ECG systems capable of achieving high diagnostic accuracy not only for the detection of abnormal rhythms but also for the detection of other myocardial diseases. In practice, multi-lead wearable platforms, such as garment-based devices and adhesive patch recorders, are already available in clinical and consumer settings30–32. These devices are capable of constructing the target lead through physical placement or mathematical calculation.

Another important finding from our analysis is the relatively high performance of AI models trained with the Lead I ECG. While we demonstrate that other axes achieve higher AUROC for the detection of LVSD and HCM, the difference was relatively small. Along with the high absolute AUROC values, the results support the development of AI models for detecting myocardial diseases using currently available wearable devices that record Lead I ECGs.

When evaluating the performance of the model, it is always important to think in the context of how the model will be used in deployment. We assessed the model performance based on sensitivity at multiple target specificities. While these metrics reflect intrinsic performance, PPV is jointly determined by the specificity and the disease prevalence. It is important to recognize that even if intrinsic model performance remains stable, PPV is expected to be higher in settings with higher real-world prevalence. At the individual patient level, ECG abnormalities present in real-world settings may also influence model performance. The model showed lower performance for detecting LVSD in the subpopulation with PVCs, although the performance remained at a clinically acceptable level. Importantly, in practice, such abnormalities are already flagged within routine clinical workflows, and these patients typically undergo further evaluation. The clinical value of our models lies precisely in identifying asymptomatic patients who might otherwise be overlooked.

In the current analyses, we focused on the lead axis optimization strategy primarily for chronic cardiac diseases. The same concept could be applied to various other settings, from acute care to long-term cardiac monitoring. For example, acute events such as detecting ST-elevation myocardial infarction33, and certain emergency settings such as predicting urgent revascularization34, may also benefit from systematic discovery of the optimal axes, potentially as part of a comprehensive real-time battery of diagnostic tools. Furthermore, AI-derived ECG features have been shown to correlate with conventional biomarkers, including NT-proBNP35, potassium36, and troponin37. With further research, exploration of alternative single-lead may reveal optimal axes for various clinical contexts.

There are several limitations to this study. First, the analyses were done using synthesized leads derived from standard 12-lead ECGs rather than directly acquired from wearable devices. While the noise robustness analyses using signals derived from the MIT-BIH Noise Stress Test Database demonstrated robust model performance under real-world noise, we were not able to test the model on the actual data derived from wearable devices. Validation using actual wearable device data remains an important future direction to further confirm the generalizability of these findings to wearable devices. Second, the data used in the current analyses were from hospital settings. Although subgroup analyses suggested stable performance across patient demographics and ECG abnormalities, further studies are warranted to evaluate generalizability to unselected populations using wearable devices. Additionally, the potential influence of targeted disease-modifying therapies on ECG-based model performance remains an important avenue for future investigation.

In conclusion, lead axes around the aVR and its inverse axes consistently achieved higher diagnostic performance for AI-based detection of LVSD, HCM, and CA using single-lead ECGs. These findings may support the design of the wearable ECG devices optimized for early detection of myocardial diseases.

Methods

ECG voltage data collection

All 12-lead ECGs recorded at BWH were acquired using Marquette ECG machines (GE Healthcare) and stored digitally in the MUSE Cardiology Information System. Another independent dataset was collected from Keio University Hospital (KEIO), a Japanese hospital using Nihon Kohden ECG machines. All ECG data were analyzed as digital standard 12-lead vectorized signals and utilized for the model development in the form of a Numpy array as a matrix of the dimension of 12 (representing twelve leads) × 2500 (representing 250 Hz × 10 s)38. Patients’ background information (age, sex, and race) was extracted from the electronic health record systems. Heart rate information was obtained from the ECG machines. ECG abnormalities, such as AF, left bundle branch block (LBBB), PAC, and PVC, were identified from ECG diagnoses overread by cardiologists.

Model training cohort

Patient datasets for LVSD16, HCM17, and CA18 were prepared in accordance with previously published protocols among the BWH and KEIO cohorts. In brief, the LVSD dataset comprised ECGs recorded within 14 days of an echocardiogram, with cases defined as those demonstrating an ejection fraction (EF) of less than 40% (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 4)16. HCM cases were defined as patients with maximum left ventricular wall thickness exceeding 15 mm without any other explanation for ventricular hypertrophy, confirmed by chart review17. CA cases were confirmed by tissue biopsy, nuclear medicine scan, cardiac MRI, or genetic testing for ATTR, and by biopsy with evidence of cardiac involvement for AL amyloidosis, all verified by chart review18. The identified cases of HCM and CA were defined after excluding ECGs showing pacing rhythm or LBBB. These case ECGs were then matched to control ECGs in a 1:5 ratio based on age and sex, excluding such rhythms from the controls in the same manner (Supplementary Figs. 2, 3, 5 and 6). The datasets were randomly divided into derivation, validation, and test sets in a 5:2:3 ratio without patient overlap. While all available ECGs in the derivation and validation sets were used, only one ECG per patient was selected (the ECG closest to the echocardiogram for LVSD and the first available ECG after diagnosis for HCM and CA) for the test sets. To train the AF detection models for reference using the BWH cohort, patients without pacemakers who were diagnosed with AF on ECG were included as cases, and those without AF were included as controls (Supplementary Fig. 13, Supplementary Table 3).

Model architecture and development

All models were trained using the same architecture and procedure across each synthesized lead angle and disease. The details of the model architecture were published previously18, and the code is provided in https://github.com/obi-ml-public/Optimized-SingleLead-ECG-Axis. In brief, the model consisted of a layer of 2D-CNN followed by 20 layers of a multi-2D-CNN module and a fully-connected layer. The model was trained using the training set to minimize the binary cross entropy between the model prediction scores and the labels using the RMSprop optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0001. Each model was iteratively trained for 50 epochs, and the model with the highest AUROC for detecting the cardiac diseases on the validation cohort across the epochs was chosen as the final model. To account for variance due to random initialization, the training procedure was repeated five times for each synthesized lead. The mean AUROC across the five models was used to identify the optimal-lead axis. This approach was designed to ensure that the observed difference in AUROC across the axes was not unduly influenced by randomness inherent in the training process, including weight initialization, random sampling, and stochastic components of the optimizer39. The 95% CIs of the AUROCs were calculated based on the performance in the test set across these five models.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess model performance across patient characteristics, such as sex (male vs. female), race (White vs. non-White; unknown excluded), age, heart rate, and AF. Age and heart rate quartiles were defined separately for each test cohort based on the respective population distribution. The age cutoffs were: 53, 66, and 75 years for LVSD; 47, 57, and 67 years for HCM; and 67, 73, and 79 years for CA. Heart rate cutoffs were: 64, 75, and 89 bpm for LVSD; 65, 76, and 91 bpm for HCM; and 63, 75, and 89 bpm for CA.

Development of integrated models

To develop the integrated models, the prediction scores from the five independently trained models for each lead axis were averaged to create a single integrated model. The 95% CIs for the AUROCs were calculated using 2000 bootstrap resamples of the test set. The subgroup analysis for ECG abnormalities, such as, presence/absence of PAC and PVC were performed to assess the robustness of integrated models. The integrated KEIO models were constructed using the same procedure.

Sensitivities were calculated for both the optimal and the Lead I integrated models. For each target specificity set at 0.70, 0.80, 0.837, and 0.90, the cutoff value corresponding to the specificity closest to but not lower than the target was selected. The value of 0.837 was chosen because it corresponds to the threshold used in a randomized controlled trial of smartwatch-based ECG screening5. An additional analysis was conducted using the cutoff with the overall closest specificity, irrespective of whether it was higher or lower than the target.

Evaluation of the robustness of the integrated models to real-world noise

To evaluate model robustness to real-world noise, noise signals were derived from the MIT-BIH Noise Stress Test Database21. Noise segments were randomly sampled from these recordings and added onto the ECG signals from the KEIO test set (noisy test set ECGs). To assess the impact of the real-world noise on model performance, prediction scores were calculated using the integrated model, inputting the noisy test set ECGs. The resulting AUROCs under noisy conditions were compared against those obtained from clean test set ECGs. Performance was evaluated for the optimal synthesized lead axis and Lead I, across all three conditions (LVSD, HCM, and CA).

Statistical analysis

Model performance was assessed using the AUROC. For each synthesized lead, five models were independently trained with different random seeds, resulting in different initialization vectors, and their AUROC values were averaged to identify the optimal-lead axis. This approach was designed to capture variance due to random initialization rather than to assess the utility of a fixed model. Therefore, the 95% CIs were calculated across these five runs rather than using bootstrap resampling of the test dataset39. In contrast, the utility of the fixed combination of the models is of vital importance for the integrated models. Thus, bootstrap-based CI estimation using 2000 resamples of the test set was applied. The averaged prediction scores from the five models provide a stable basis for resampling and appropriately reflect the utility on deployment. A two-sided P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
