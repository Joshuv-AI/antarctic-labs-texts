Genome-wide identification, characterization, and expression profiling of TIFY family members in Panax notoginseng
Authors: Yun Yang, Yonghan Qu, Xueping Li, Quandong Nong, Qingsong Yang, Xianmei Chen, Chuanjiang Nong, Chunqing Li, Ruihong Ru, Yonghong Tao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn plants, TIFY proteins regulate growth, development, and stress response. Although the chromosome-level Panax notoginseng genome has been published, the PnTIFY gene family remains uncharacterized. Here, we conducted a comprehensive bioinformatic analysis of the PnTIFY gene family, including phylogenetic, gene structure, chromosome location, as well as tissue-specific and Fusarium solani-induced gene expression analyses.ResultsA total of 26 PnTIFY genes were identified and distributed on all twelve chromosomes. The TIFY genes of P. notoginseng, Arabidopsis thaliana, and Oryza sativa were divided among the JAZ (further divided into three subgroups), TIFY, ZIM/ZML, and PPD subfamilies. PnTIFY genes belonging to the same subfamily tended to have similar gene structures and conserved domains. Collinearity analysis showed that segmental duplication events played a key role in the expansion of the PnTIFY gene family. Cis-elements related to phytohormones, stress, and light were identified in the PnTIFY gene promoters, suggesting roles in growth and stress adaptation. JAZ I subgroup genes were highly expressed in flowers and leaves, while JAZ II/III and ZIM/ZML members exhibited stable, tissue-wide expression. JAZ II/III genes were prominently upregulated in older roots, implicating their roles in jasmonic acid signaling and secondary metabolism. Finally, qRT-PCR analysis revealed that PnJAZ2/4/6/16/17 were strongly induced in response to F. solani infection.ConclusionsThe results of this study clarify the molecular regulatory network underlying the response of P. notoginseng to soil-borne fungal diseases and will aid in the development of disease-resistant varieties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12140-5.Keywords: Panax notoginseng, TIFY gene family, Root rot, Fusarium solani, Disease resistance

Background

Panax notoginseng (Burk.) F.H. Chen is commonly used to treat blood stasis syndromes in traditional Chinese medicine [1]. P. notoginseng, as well as the related species P. ginseng and P. quinquefolius, contain a wide array of bioactive compounds, including ginsenosides, polysaccharides, volatile oils, proteins, amino acids, organic acids, flavonoids, vitamins, and trace elements, among others. Because of this, the pharmacological activities and medicinal efficacies of the three species are highly similar [2]. P. notoginseng must be cultivated in shady, humid conditions for at least three years prior to harvest for medicinal purposes. During this time, the crop is highly susceptible to root rot caused by the ubiquitous soil-borne necrotrophic fungus Fusarium solani [3]. F. solani initiates infection by germinating its conidia in the soil and subsequently invading the plant host via the root tip or wounds. After proliferating in the root tissues, the fungus can further disseminate throughout the plant, significantly reducing both crop yield and quality [4]. Therefore, it is essential to understand the response of P. notoginseng to F. solani infection as well as to identify key disease-resistance genes to aid in the development of disease-resistant ginseng.

The TIFY gene family is a group of plant-specific transcriptional regulators named for its core domain, the highly-conserved TIFY motif [5]. TIFY family members are categorized into four subfamilies: JAZ (jasmonate ZIM-domain), ZML (ZIM-like), PPD (PEAPOD), and TIFY. JAZ proteins act as core repressors of the jasmonic acid (JA) pathway and thereby regulate plant stress resistance, development, and secondary metabolism [6]. Specifically, JAZ subfamily members regulate downstream defense gene expression by interacting with transcription factors (TFs) such as MYC2. This mechanism has been found to underly fungal disease resistance in plants such as Arabidopsis thaliana and Oryza sativa [7]. In Populus trichocarpa, JAZ subfamily members exhibit significant differential expression in response to leaf rust infection, highlighting their important functions in pathogen stress responses [8]. In P. notoginseng, JA signaling regulates the expression of the resistance gene PnOLP1, which encodes an osmotin-like protein capable of inhibiting F. solani mycelial growth [9].

TIFY family members have been extensively studied in both model species such as A. thaliana and O. sativa, as well as non-model species such as Salvia miltiorrhiza and Ganoderma lucidum [10]. However, it is unknown whether and how TIFY gene family members participate in JA-mediated disease resistance in P. notoginseng. Furthermore, previous research suggests that the WRKY15 TF positively regulates resistance to F. solani in P. notoginseng, although whether this mechanism involves TIFY TFs remains an open question [3]. Here, we utilized P. notoginseng whole-genome data to identify TIFY gene family members and analyze their sequence characteristics, evolutionary relationships, tissue-specific expression patterns, and expression dynamics during F. solani infection and root development via transcriptomic sequencing and qRT-PCR. The results of this study provide a theoretical basis for elucidating the molecular regulatory network underlying the response of P. notoginseng to soil-borne fungal diseases. In addition, these findings will enable the screening of candidate disease-resistance genes for molecular breeding schemes aimed at improving the disease resistance, yield, and quality of P. notoginseng.

Results

Identification of TIFY family members in the P. notoginseng genome

BLAST and HMMER searches revealed 26 TIFY family genes in the P. notoginseng genome (Supplementary Table S1). Among them, PnJAZ15 encoded the shortest protein (133 amino acids). In general, shorter amino acid sequences yield proteins with lower molecular weights. The molecular weights of the 26 PnTIFY proteins ranged from 15.21 KDa (PnJAZ15) to 176.59 KDa (PnJAZ12), with isoelectric points ranging from 4.98 (PnJAZ10) to 9.74 (PnJAZ6) and aliphatic indices ranging from 51.21 (PnJAZ10) to 78.71 (PnZIM1). Prediction of subcellular localization revealed that all of the PnTIFY proteins are localized to the nucleus.

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of PnTIFY proteins

To explore evolutionary relationships among JAZ, TIFY, ZML/ZIM, and PPD subfamily members in P. notoginseng, O. sativa, and A. thaliana, a phylogenetic tree containing 18 AtTIFY, 20 OsTIFY, and 26 PnTIFY proteins was created. Across all three species, TIFY members were categorized into one of four subfamilies (JAZ, TIFY, ZML/ZIM, and PPD), and JAZ subfamily members were further subdivided into three subgroups (JAZ I-III; Fig. 1). The 26 PnTIFY genes included 17 JAZ members, three TIFY members, five ZML/ZIM members, and one PPD member. Among JAZ subfamily members, JAZ I and III contained six members while JAZ II contained five. Notably, only P. notoginseng and A. thaliana contained PPD subfamily members.

Phylogenetic tree of TIFY domain-containing proteins from Panax notoginseng, Arabidopsis thaliana, and Oryza sativa

Gene structure and conserved motif analysis of the PnTIFY gene family

The structural features and conserved motifs of the PnTIFY family members were analyzed using the MEME Suite. In all, 10 conserved motifs were detected, with individual PnTIFY proteins containing between one and five motifs (Fig. 2B). One motif was found in the majority of PnTIFY members, with the exception of PnJAZ3, PnJAZ6, PnJAZ11, PnJAZ13, PnJAZ15, PnJAZ17, PnPPD, PnTIFY1, PnTIFY2, and PnTIFY3. Other motifs were distributed only in specific subfamilies or groups. Specifically, motifs 3, 5, and 8 were unique to JAZ II, while motifs 4 and 6 were unique to JAZ III, with the exception of PnJAZ5 and PnJAZ10. Similar motif distributions within a particular subfamily or group indicate a conserved protein structure. The PnTIFY genes contained between three and 14 exons, with JAZ III containing the fewest and JAZ II containing the most (Fig. 2C). The majority of PnTIFY genes harbored three to 14 exons, with the exception of a handful of genes containing only two exons (PnJAZ1, PnJAZ4, PnJAZ5, PnJAZ8, PnJAZ10, and PnJAZ16).

Blue boxes represent exons and black lines represent introns. The upstream/downstream untranslated regions (UTR) are indicated by red boxes

PnTIFY chromosomal distributions and duplication analysis

The 26 TIFY genes were unevenly distributed among twelve P. notoginseng chromosomes. Chromosome 1 contained the most genes (four), followed by chromosomes 2, 6, and 10 (three each), chromosomes 3, 4, 5, 7, and 8 (two each), and chromosomes 9, 11, and 12 (one each) (Fig. 3A). According to MCScanX analysis, several gene duplication events appear to have occurred throughout the PnTIFY family. Syntenic analysis revealed that the following gene pairs represent segmental duplications: PnJAZ2/PnJAZ14, PnZIM1/PnZML3, PnJAZ3/PnJAZ6, PnJAZ3/PnJAZ11, PnJAZ4/PnJAZ8, PnJAZ6/PnJAZ11, PnJAZ7/PnJAZ12, PnZML1/PnZML2, and PnTIFY1/PnTIFY2 (Fig. 3B). Ka/Ks ratios were calculated to identify potential selective pressures acting on these duplicated gene pairs. All Ka/Ks ratios values were < 1 (Supplementary Table S2), suggesting that these genes have been constrained by intense purification selection pressure.

Synteny and evolutionary analysis of PnTIFY genes

To explore the evolutionary and duplication history of the PnTIFY gene family, a collinearity analysis was conducted among P. notoginseng, P. ginseng, A. thaliana, Daucus carota, and Capsicum annuum (Fig. 4). P. notoginseng shared 87 orthologous gene pairs with P. ginseng, 25 with A. thaliana, 38 with D. carota, and 23 with C. annuum. While P. notoginseng shared significant homology with the closely-related P. ginseng, it also shared considerable homology with its Apiales (syn. Umbellales) cousin D. carota, as well as the more distantly-related A. thaliana and C. annuum.

PnTIFY promoter cis-element analysis

Cis-acting elements drive gene expression and thereby regulate downstream biological functions. We extracted the 2000 bp upstream regions of the 26 PnTIFYs to identify cis-acting elements in their promoters. The 26 PnTIFY genes were found to contain a diverse set of motifs associated with photoreception and phytohormone response as well as growth-responsive and stress-related elements (Fig. 5). Notably, all 26 PnTIFY genes contained at least one photoreaction elements. Cis-elements related to abscisic acid (ABA), methyl jasmonate (MeJA), gibberellic acid (GA), and salicylic acid (SA) were identified in 23, 17, 16, 11, and six PnTIFY genes, respectively. In addition, cis-elements involved in drought, low temperature, defense and stress response were identified in 14, 12, and seven PnTIFY genes. These findings suggest that PnTIFYs may play roles in photosensing and hormone regulation, as well as stress and defense.

Cis-acting elements in the PnTIFY gene promoters. The upstream 2000 bp sequences of 26 PnTIFY genes were analyzed using the PlantCare database

Tissue-specific PnTIFY gene expression

Tissue-specific PnTIFY gene expression was evaluated via transcriptomic analysis (Fig. 6A). Genes in the JAZ I subgroup (PnJAZ13, PnJAZ15, PnJAZ17, PnJAZ11, PnJAZ6, and PnJAZ3) were highly expressed in all tissues, particularly PnJAZ1, PnJAZ6, and PnJAZ11 (which were highly expressed in flowers and leaves). Genes in the JAZ II subgroup were minimally expressed in all tissues, particularly in flowers and leaves (e.g., PnJAZ2, PnJAZ7, and PnJAZ12), suggesting that their expression was not significantly tissue specific. In the JAZ III subgroup, PnJAZ5, PnJAZ8, and PnJAZ16 were most highly expressed in flowers and leaves, followed by roots. PnJAZ10 was most highly expressed in stems and exhibited minimal expression in other tissues. ZML/ZIM subfamily (e.g., PnZIM1, PnZIM2, PnZML1, PnZML2, and PnZML3) expression was relatively stable across all tissues, with no significant tissue specificity observed. PPD subfamily members were most highly expressed in flowers and stems. Finally, TIFY subfamily (PnTIFY1, PnTIFY2, and PnTIFY3) expression was relatively stable across all tissues, suggesting that these genes may support fundamental cellular functions.

Expression patterns of PnTIFY genes in different tissues (A) and at different root growth stages (B). Gene expression was quantified via RNA-Seq. The heatmap was developed using Log2 FPKM values

Changes in PnTIFY family gene expression during root development

Next, we explored changes in PnTIFY gene expression during the course of root development (Fig. 6B). Two TIFY subfamily genes (PnTIFY1 and PnTIFY3) were downregulated in three-year-old roots, whereas PnTIFY2 was upregulated. Most ZML/ZIM subgroup genes (e.g., PnZIM1, PnZIM2, PnZML2, and PnZML3) were highly expressed in one-year-old roots, although PnZML1 was upregulated in three-year-old roots. In the PPD subfamily, PnPPD was upregulated in one-year-old roots. Among JAZ I subgroup genes, PnJAZ3 was highly expressed in three-year-old roots while PnJAZ17, PnJAZ11, and PnJAZ6 were upregulated in one-year-old roots. The JAZ II subgroup genes PnJAZ7, PnJAZ9, PnJAZ12, and PnJAZ14 were upregulated in three-year-old roots, while PnJAZ2 was downregulated in one-year-old roots. Among JAZ III subgroup genes, PnJAZ4, PnJAZ5, and PnJAZ8 were upregulated in one-year-old roots, whereas PnJAZ1, PnJAZ10, and PnJAZ16 were upregulated in three-year-old roots. Overall, the JAZ II and JAZ III subgroups contained the most upregulated members, highlighting their central roles in the JA signaling pathway.

Changes in PnTIFY family gene expression in response to F. solani infection

Finally, stepwise changes in PnTIFY family gene expression in response to F. solani infection were evaluated via qRT-PCR. Six differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified (Fig. 7). Among the five upregulated genes, PnJAZ2/6/16/17 were stably enhanced and exhibited maximum expression after 24 h, while PnJAZ4 expression peaked after 48 h. On the other hand, PnJAZ11 was suppressed by F. solani infection.

Error bars indicate standard deviations and asterisks indicate significant differences between the control (CK, 0 h) and F. solani treatment at 24 h or 48 h (* p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01)

Discussion

We identified 26 PnTIFY genes unevenly distributed across twelve chromosomes. Consistent with classifications in Brassica napus [11], Hordeum vulgarev [12], and Eremochloa ophiuroides [13], the PnTIFY proteins were divided into four subfamilies (JAZ, TIFY, ZIM/ZML, and PPD). The JAZ subfamily was the largest, with 17 members, reflecting its central role in JA signaling, defense response, and secondary metabolism [14]. For example, expression profiling under JA induction in tobacco revealed JAZ proteins as central mediators of JA signaling and metabolic reprogramming. In addition, the JAZ subfamily also regulates ABA signaling and physiological processes such as photosynthesis [15]. On the other hand, the PPD subfamily was found to be present exclusively in P. notoginseng and A. thaliana, suggesting it emerged following the divergence of monocotyledonous plants [5, 16].

Tandem and segmental duplications are key mechanisms in plant evolution, gene family expansion, and functional differentiation, particularly in response to abiotic stress [17]. Studies suggest that segmentally duplicated genes commonly exhibit similar functions and expression patterns [18]. We identified 26 PnTIFY genes in P. notoginseng, exceeding the number found in A. thaliana (18) and O. sativa (20), suggesting gene family expansion occurred in this species. Indeed, we uncovered several segmental duplication events which may have contributed to such expansion, including those involving PnJAZ2/PnJAZ14 and PnZIM1/PnZML3. These results are generally in line with previous studies in Brassica rapa [19], Eriobotrya japonica [20], soybean [21], and other species. Notably, the Ka/Ks ratios of all PnTIFYs were less than 1, indicating strong purifying selection pressure [22]. This pattern aligns with observations in A. thaliana and O. sativa, wherein TIFY family expansion was also driven by segmental duplications under purifying selection [10, 14]. The strong negative selection pressure experienced by the PnTIFY genes likely preserved their functions, resulting in minimal functional divergence [23].

TIFY gene family members regulate diverse biological processes, including development, stress response, and phytohormone signaling. Previous studies have shown that TIFY genes are typically responsive to JA and ABA [24, 25]. In this study, we identified light-responsive cis-acting elements in 26 PnTIFY genes, while hormone-responsive cis-acting elements were detected in 23 of these genes. Light-responsive elements were the most abundant, suggesting that many of the PnTIFY genes are transcriptionally regulated via light signals [26]. In addition, 23 PnTIFY gene promoters contained ABA-responsive cis-elements, which are implicated in seed dormancy, germination, growth, and biotic/abiotic stress response. Similar results have been observed in E. japonica [20], tobacco [27], and Helianthus annuus [16]. Finally, flavonoid biosynthesis-related elements were also detected, linking PnJAZ15 and PnTIFY3 to bioactive secondary metabolite regulation in P. notoginseng [28]. Together, these results suggest that PnTIFYs play active roles in ABA signaling, stress response, and secondary metabolism. However, these results should be confirmed through experimental studies utilizing exogenous hormone treatments.

Tissue-specific expression profiling can uncover clues about the biological roles of individual genes. We observed high expression of JAZ I genes (PnJAZ1/6/11) in flowers and leaves, aligning with their roles in reproductive development and leaf senescence [29]. Conversely, the root-specific downregulation of JAZ III genes (PnJAZ5/8/16) may reflect their involvement in JA-driven root growth suppression, a mechanism conserved in A. thaliana [30]. The stable expression of ZIM/ZML genes across tissues suggests that these genes play housekeeping roles, potentially in maintaining cellular homeostasis or coordinating growth-defense trade-offs [14, 31]. The upregulation of PnPPD in flowers and stems aligns with its importance to growth and developmental processes [20]. In addition, the PnTIFY genes exhibited differential expression between one-year-old and three-year-old roots, underscoring their roles in maturation and secondary metabolite accumulation. JAZ II/III genes (PnJAZ7/9/12/14 and PnJAZ1/10/16) were upregulated in older roots, correlating with enhanced production of bioactive triterpenoid saponins [32]. JA signaling is known to stimulate secondary metabolism in medicinal plants, suggesting that PnJAZ proteins may act as repressors which are degraded upon JA induction, thereby releasing TFs to activate biosynthetic genes [33].

JAZ-mediated herbivory and pathogen resistance mechanisms are modulated by a number of phytohormones, including JA, SA, and ethylene (ET) [19]. Here, qRT-PCR analysis revealed that five of six tested PnTIFY genes were upregulated following F. solani infection, with PnJAZ2, PnJAZ6, PnJAZ16, and PnJAZ17 expression peaking at 24 h post-inoculation. JA signaling is central to plant immunity against necrotrophic fungi [34], and JAZ proteins typically repress JA responses until pathogen attack triggers their degradation [35]. The rapid upregulation of PnJAZ2, PnJAZ6, PnJAZ16, and PnJAZ17 24 h after infection suggests a transient JA response. On the other hand, PnJAZ4 expression peaked 48 h after infection, likely reflecting a delayed feedback mechanism to restore homeostasis. This dynamic regulation mirrors that of A. thaliana JAZ12, which is induced by Botrytis cinerea to fine-tune the defense response [36]. Conversely, the downregulation of PnTIFY1 and PnTIFY3 in older roots during infection may indicate a trade-off between growth and defense, as chronic JA activation can impair plant growth [37]. However, specific functional studies still need to be conducted to verify these results.

Conclusions

Here, we conducted the first genome-wide analysis of the evolutionary history, structural diversity, and functional potential of the TIFY gene family in P. notoginseng. The expanded JAZ subfamily exhibited dynamic expression under stress, revealing this subfamily’s central role in JA-mediated defense and secondary metabolism. Future research should explore the interplay between PnTIFY proteins and TFs regulating saponin biosynthesis, which could inform genetic strategies to enhance the medicinal value and stress resilience of P. notoginseng.

Materials and methods

Identification and characterization of the TIFY gene family in P. notoginseng

The P. notoginseng genome and annotation data were obtained from our previous study [38]. TIFY family members were identified using two methods. First, 18 AtTIFY [39] and 20 OsTIFY [24] proteins were used as BLASTP (E-value < 1 × 10−5) queries to identify PnTIFY proteins [13]. Second, Hidden Markov model profiles of the TIFY (PF06200), Jas (CCT-2) (PF09425), and CCT (PF06203) domains were used as queries to identify PnTIFY gene family members. The two sets of candidates were intersected, and the conserved domains of the candidate proteins were further identified using the Pfam database (http://Pfam.sanger.ac.uk/) and the SMART tool (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/) [40]. Expasy ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) was utilized to estimate protein molecular weights and isoelectric points [41]. WoLF PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp) was used to predict the subcellular localizations of PnTIFY proteins.

Phylogenetic analysis of TIFY family members

Multiple sequence alignment of PnTIFY proteins was performed using MUSCLE implemented in MEGA11 with default parameters. A neighbor-joining (N-J) phylogenetic tree was constructed in MEGA11 with 1000 bootstrap replications and visualized using the iTOL website [42].

Conserved domain and gene structure analysis

To investigate the intron-exon organization of the PnTIFY genes, their coding sequences (CDS) were aligned with corresponding genomic sequences. Results were obtained using the Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS; http://gsds.cbi.pku.edu.cn/index.php) [43]. Conserved motifs were identified using MEME (http://meme.ncbr.net/meme/cgi-bin/meme.cgi), with the maximum number of conserved motifs set to 10 and all other parameters set to default [44].

Chromosomal location and collinearity analysis

The chromosomal location of each PnTIFY gene was obtained from the GFF genome annotation for P. notoginseng. Chromosome maps were drawn using TBtools (v.2.142) [45]. One Step MCScanX from TBtools was used to identify tandem and duplicated genes [13]. Synteny analysis was conducted using TBtools (one step MCScanX) and Dual Synteny Plot [46]. Panax genomic data and annotation files were retrieved from the National Genomics Data Center (CNCB-NGDC; https://download.cncb.ac.cn/gwh/Plants/) [47]. Genomic data and annotations for D. carota and C. annuum were acquired from the Ensembl Plants database (https://plants.ensembl.org/index.html). Synonymous (Ka) to non-synonymous (Ks) ratios (Ka/Ks) for duplicated gene pairs were calculated based on CDS alignments using the Ka/Ks calculator in TBtools [48].

Cis-regulatory element prediction

TBtools (v2.142) was used to extract the 2000 bp sequences upstream of each PnTIFY gene transcription start sites (TSS). Each extracted sequence was submitted to PlantCARE for promoter analysis (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent/webtools/plantcare/html/) [49]. TBtools was used to visualize the cis-element distribution of each promoter [27].

RNA-seq data analysis

RNA-Seq data for roots, stems, leaves, and flowers of P. notoginseng were downloaded from the NCBI public database (Accession Number: SRR7427741-SRR7427758) [50]. Raw RNA-Seq data from one-year-old and three-year-old P. notoginseng roots were downloaded from the Genome Sequence Archive (Genomics, Proteomics & Bioinformatics 2017) of the BIG Data Center (Nucleic Acids Res 2018), Beijing Institute of Genomics (BIG), Chinese Academy of Sciences, under project number PRJCA001065 (accession numbers: CRX030750-CRX030755) [51]. The heatmap was generated using TBtools based on log2 FPKM values, which were calculated as described in previous studies [52].

RNA isolation and qRT-PCR analysis

F. solani was cultured on potato-dextrose agar (PDA) at 28 °C in darkness. One-year-old P. notoginseng plants exhibiting consistent growth were transplanted into Hoagland nutrient solution and grown hydroponically for two weeks. Plants were then placed into 250 mL of F. solani spore suspension (1 × 106 CFU mL−1) for 30 min, and then replaced in the hydroponic environment. Root samples were collected 0, 24, and 48 h after inoculation and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen (−80 ℃), with three biological replicates per treatment [38]. Total RNA was extracted from P. notoginseng roots using a rapid RNA extraction Kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). A TransScript One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix Kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) was used to synthesize first-strand cDNA. All primers are listed in Supplementary Table S3. The NCBI website was used to design primers, and the actin gene was used as an internal reference gene. Thermal cycling was performed under the following conditions: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Gene expression levels were quantified using the 2−△△Ct method [53].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel 2016, Origin 2018, and SPSS 24.0. Data are shown as the means ± standard deviations (SD) of three independent biological replicates. Two-tailed Student’s t-tests and one-way ANOVA were used to evaluate statistical significance at the *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.001 levels.

The datasets generated and/or analyzed during the current study are available in the NCBI SRA database under the accession numbers SRR7427741, SRR7427742, SRR7427743, SRR7427744, SRR7427745, SRR7427746, SRR7427747, SRR7427748, SRR742742749, SRR7427750, SRR7427751, SRR7427752, SRR7427753, SRR7427754, SRR7427755, SRR7427756, SRR7427757, and SRR7427758. The raw RNA-Seq data from one-year-old and three-year-old *P. notoginseng* roots were downloaded from the Genome Sequence Archive (Genomics, Proteomics & Bioinformatics 2017) of the BIG Data Center (Nucleic Acids Res 2018), Beijing Institute of Genomics (BIG), Chinese Academy of Sciences, under project number PRJCA001065 (accession numbers: CRX030750, CRX030751, CRX030752, CRX030753, CRX030754, and CRX030755). *TIFY* gene domain architecture was obtained from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/). The ExPASy proteomics server (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) was used to estimate protein lengths, molecular weights, and theoretical isoelectric points (PI). The MEME tool (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) was chosen to identify conserved motifs. WoLF PSORT (https://www.genscript.com/wolf-psort.html) was used to analyze subcellular localization. PlantCARE (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) was used to predict *cis* -acting elements. The aforementioned *P. notoginseng* plant materials were collected from the cultivation base owned by Wenshan Academy of Agricultural Sciences.
