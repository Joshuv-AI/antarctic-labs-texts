Virulence diversity among Porphyromonas gingivalis strains: a review of genetic factors
Authors: Hanie Ahmadi, Jacob Burks, Katherine A. Innamorati, Garth D. Ehrlich, Ann Progulske-Fox
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract BackgroundPorphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) is a Gram-negative anaerobic bacterium and a keystone pathogen in chronic periodontitis. Beyond oral disease, it has been implicated in systemic conditions, including cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, and neurodegeneration. Marked strain-to-strain differences in tissue invasion, immune evasion, and pathogenicity suggest that virulence is shaped by complex genetic and regulatory interactions rather than a single determinant.ObjectiveTo summarize current knowledge of the genetic diversity of P. gingivalis virulence determinants and their contribution to strain-specific pathogenicity.DesignThis narrative review synthesizes current evidence on the diversity of major virulence determinants, including fimbriae (FimA and Mfa1), gingipains (RgpA/RgpB/Kgp), Hag-family hemagglutinins, capsular polysaccharide loci, lipopolysaccharide variants, the RagAB nutrient acquisition system, and the type IX secretion system. It also examines the roles of allelic variation, domain rearrangements, phase variation, and horizontal gene transfer in shaping strain-specific virulence repertoires.ConclusionsThe reviewed evidence indicates that genetic variation across multiple virulence-associated loci contributes to substantial phenotypic diversity among P. gingivalis strains, influencing host interaction, tissue tropism, immune modulation, and pathogenic potential. Integrating comparative genomics with functional phenotyping provides a framework for predicting strain-specific virulence and may facilitate the development of improved diagnostic tools and targeted therapeutic strategies.

Background

Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) is classified as a non-motile, Gram-negative, rod-shaped, anaerobic bacterium that forms black colonies on blood agar plates, is associated with chronic periodontitis, and is considered a keystone pathogen in periodontal dysbiosis [1]. Research increasingly links P. gingivalis to systemic diseases beyond the oral cavity, such as Alzheimer's disease, rheumatoid arthritis, cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes and certain cancers [2,3]. Patients with periodontitis consistently show higher circulating levels of several systemic inflammatory markers, most notably C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and fibrinogen, compared with periodontally healthy controls [4–6]. In a sequencing-based study of coronary and femoral artery bypass specimens, P. gingivalis accounted for nearly 80% of the oral bacterial reads detected, although the reported abundance varies substantially across patient populations, sample types and analytical methods [7]. Clinical and experimental studies also confirm its presence and pathogenic activity in pancreatic tissues, especially in diabetes and pancreatic cancer [8].

For the purpose of this review, we define a virulence factor as any molecule or structure that enhances the bacterium's ability to colonize, survive, or damage host tissues beyond that of a non-pathogenic commensal. These factors support functions such as tissue adhesion, nutrient acquisition, immune modulation and tissue invasion [9]. As a keystone pathogen, P. gingivalis carries a wide range of virulence genes involved in nutrient scavenging, invasion, immune activation and evasion and gene transfer. For example, adhesion to host surfaces, which is critical for invasion, is mediated by the major and minor fimbriae (fimA, mfa1) and hemagglutinins (hagA, B and C). Interestingly, the hemagglutinin/adhesin domains of RgpA and Kgp share related adhesive domains with hagA. Iron acquisition occurs via trypsin-like gingipains that harvest heme-derived iron [1,10]. P. gingivlais encodes an atypical lipopolysaccharide (LPS) that promotes elevated levels of inflammatory cytokine production, which contributes to periodontal tissue destruction [11].

Comparative in vitro and in vivo data including invasion, intracellular survival, immune evasion and lethality in animal models reveal the degree of widely differing pathogenic potential among strains [12–14]. For example, the ability of strains to invade host cells can vary due to genetic differences in key virulence factors such as the capsule, fimbriae, gingipain proteases, hemagglutinin adhesins, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and their related genes. Cellular invasiveness and virulence have been evaluated for P. gingivalis strains using both in vitro and in vivo models. These studies demonstrated that strains show significant differences in their ability to invade host cells. Highly virulent strains have an enhanced ability to invade tissue, evade immune defenses and survive intracellularly for extended periods. Strains such as W83, W50, A7436 and ATCC 49417 are classified as highly virulent while the type strains ATCC 33277 and 381 are less virulent [14]. These strains may still be present in the oral cavity but are more common in individuals with healthy or mild periodontal conditions and likely cannot survive dissemination to other tissues [15].

To facilitate comparison among strains, Dorn et al. grouped P. gingivalis isolates into four invasion categories based on the number of internalized bacteria recovered in their in vitro infection model: highly invasive strains internalized approximately 105 CFU, moderately invasive strains approximately 104 CFU, low-invasive strains approximately 103 CFU and non-invasive strains yielded ≤102 CFU [16]. While this classification provides a useful framework for comparing invasion phenotypes, it represents an operational definition derived from a specific experimental system and it is not a universal measure of virulence.

Advances in sequencing technology and bioinformatic methods have substantially improved the resolution with which P. gingivalis strains can be defined, classified and compared. Whole-genome sequencing combined with average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis now provides a robust framework for delineating strains and confirming species boundaries, with ANI values above 95% conventionally defining members of the same species [17]. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST), based on sequence variation in seven conserved housekeeping genes, has been widely applied to P. gingivalis clinical isolates to investigate population structure and epidemiological relationships across geographically diverse cohorts. More recently, pan-genomic approaches using tools such as Roary and PIRATE have enabled large-scale comparisons of core and accessory genome content across expanding collections of sequenced strains, revealing that the P. gingivalis distributed genome is considerably larger and more variable than previously appreciated. Long-read sequencing platforms, including PacBio and Oxford Nanopore, have further facilitated the generation of complete, closed genome assemblies that resolve repetitive regions, genomic inversions and mobile genetic elements that are poorly captured by short-read methods. These features are particularly relevant for a species whose virulence gene repertoire is shaped by recombination and horizontal gene transfer [18].

Comparative genomic analyzes of P. gingivalis have also been facilitated by the growing number of publicly available genomic sequences. To date, 71 P. gingivalis genome assemblies have been sequenced enabling increasingly detailed investigations of strain diversity, phylogenetic relationships and virulence-associated genetic variation [19].

A phylogenomic comparison of 19 P. gingivalis strains revealed 1,037 core proteins and uncovered two primary phylogenetic clades: one containing ATCC 33277, 381 and HG66, and another including W83, W50 and A7436, with other strains such as TDC60 branching separately. Early comparative genomics between the highly virulent W83 strain and the less virulent ATCC 33277 strain identified hundreds of strain-specific differences in coding sequences (CDSs) and extensive genome rearrangements [20].

Recently, to further characterize the phylogenetic relationships among P. gingivalis strains and their associated virulence phenotypes our group conducted a pan-genome analysis of 71 P. gingivalis genome including 18 that were PacBio sequenced for this project at Drexel University College of Medicine and 53 that were available as downloads from NCBI. A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using RAxML and overlaid with previously published virulence data from a mouse abscess model (MAM) [21–25], categorizing strains into four groups: 1) no lesion, 2) non-spreading lesion, 3) spreading lesion and 4) spreading lesion with death. Phylogenetic analysis revealed two broad groups: a clade containing highly virulent strains capable of causing spreading infections and death, and a larger clade encompassing avirulent and non-lethal strains, including the well-characterized reference strains ATCC_33277, 381 and HG66 (Figure 1) [12,13,19]. These phenotypic differences likely reflect the unique combination of virulence factors carried by each strain and may determine the ability of strains to contribute to oral and systemic diseases.

These pan-genomic analyzes reveal that P. gingivalis possesses a conserved core genome while the distributed genome includes a large repertoire of genes, many of which are involved in pathogenicity or environmental adaptation. The core genome encodes essential cellular functions and remains stable across strains, while the distributed genome is fluid, providing genetic plasticity that underlies strain-specific differences, including variations in virulence and the ability to adapt to different environments. The distributed gene sets vary substantially among strains due to horizontal gene transfer processes such as transposon activity, chromosomal inversions and allelic exchange [26]. Comparative analyzes and phylogenetic studies have revealed that this genomic diversity contributes to the broad spectrum of pathogenic behaviors observed among P. gingivalis strains [27].

The broad and multifaceted set of virulence factors possessed by P. gingivalis, and their cognate phenotypes are unlikely to arise from one gene alone; instead, they are likely to result from the combined action of multiple genes. Search strategy

A structured literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science and Google Scholar for articles published through February 2026. Search terms included Porphyromonas gingivalis, strain diversity, virulence factors, fimbriae, gingipains, capsule, lipopolysaccharide, type IX secretion system, RagAB, genomics, phylogenomics and horizontal gene transfer. Only peer-reviewed original research articles, comparative genomic studies and review articles in English relevant to strain-specific virulence were considered. Reference lists of eligible articles were also screened to identify additional relevant publications. Preprints were excluded unless they provided genome sequences not yet available in peer-reviewed literature.

Classical virulence factors

Virulence factors are microbial components that enhance colonization, survival, persistence, immune evasion, or tissue damage in the host [30]. In P. gingivalis, a subset of virulence determinants has been characterized across diverse strains and experimental models and is consistently linked to core pathogenic functions; these are designated here as classical virulence factors. These factors include fimbriae (FimA and Mfa1), gingipains (RgpA, RgpB and Kgp) and their hemagglutinin/adhesin domains, Hag-family hemagglutinins (HagA, HagB, HagC), capsular polysaccharide (CPS), lipopolysaccharide (O-LPS and A-LPS), the RagAB outer membrane transporter complex and the type IX secretion system (T9SS) [30,31]. Despite broad conservation, these determinants exhibit allelic variation, domain remodeling and differential expression among strains, resulting in highly divergent phenotypes in adhesion, invasion, immune modulation and nutrient acquisition [30]. Figure 2 illustrates the spatial organization of major virulence determinants within the P. gingivalis cell envelope. From the outside inward, the capsular polysaccharide (CPS; K1–K6 serotypes) and O-LPS/A-LPS layers mediate immune evasion and surface antigenicity. FimA major and Mfa1 minor fimbriae project from the outer membrane and facilitate adhesion and biofilm formation. Gingipain cysteine proteases (RgpA/B, Kgp) with their hemagglutinin/adhesin (HA) domains are surface-anchored or secreted via the type IX secretion system (T9SS). The RagAB complex mediates peptide and nutrient uptake. Strain-specific genetic variation in each of these components underlies the differences in pathogenic potential discussed throughout this review.

Fimbriae (major and minor)

Fimbrial gene polymorphisms, differences in expression levels, and post-transcriptional regulation combine to create strain-specific phenotypes, influencing disease progression and host interaction [32]. P. gingivalis strains commonly express two main types of fimbriae: fimA and mfa1. While the genetic diversity of fimA has been extensively characterized, the genes encoding the mfa1 (minor fimbriae) components (mfa1 to mfa5) have only recently begun to be systematically studied [32]. Fimbriae are classified into six genotypes (I–V and Ib) based on gene sequence [33]. Epidemiological studies have reported that fimA genotypes II and IV are predominantly found in deep pockets and in patients with severe periodontitis, whereas genotype I is more prevalent in healthy periodontal tissue or mild disease subjects [32]. Type III and Type V are detected less frequently; Type V is rarely found or absent in many clinical studies [34].

The virulent strains W83 and W50 carry a type IV fimA allele consistent with its association with disease, whereas the less-virulent ATCC 33277, a typical reference strain from health or mild disease conditions, carries genotype I fimA. However, some studies have revealed exceptions to the type associated with disease or health and so there is likely no absolute virulence-genotype correlation [32] (Table 2). Fimbrial structures also underline P. gingivalis strain differences in biofilm formation [21,35,36]. ATCC 33277 and 381 are highly fimbriated strains which form large, robust and cohesive biofilms in vitro. This characteristic is attributed to the abundance of fimA and mfa1 fimbriae, which promote adhesion and structural integrity [27], whereas virulent encapsulated strains such as W83 or A7436 produce reduced biofilms which are poorly fimbriated due to low fimA expression and phase variation [14]. Although W83 carries genes encoding the subunits of both major fimA and minor mfa1 fimbriae, it produces very low levels of these fimbrial structures, likely due to weak or inactive fimbrial expression in this strain. In contrast, fimA type IV is detected in clinical isolates of P. gingivalis, but its surface expression varies among strains and does not necessarily correlate directly with fimA genotype alone [22,23].

Functionally, the presence of both major and minor fimbriae is important and strains lacking either fimA or mfa1 are attenuated in biofilm formation and host cell attachment. The mfa1 fimbriae displays considerable variation with respect to fimA variation. The mfa operon (mfa1–5) encodes the short fimbrial filament, with mfa1 alleles grouped into two main size-based genotypes: ~53 kDa and ~70 kDa. The 70 kDa form is further divided into 70 A and 70B alleles, reflecting additional sequence diversity. Most gingivalis strains carry the 70 kDa genotype, while the less common 53 kDa variant appears to confer distinct surface characteristics including altered fimbrial architecture, adherence patterns and likely differences in co-aggregation and immune recognition, although these phenotypes remain less comprehensively characterized than the 70 kDa forms. [24,32]. Allelic variation in mfa2–mfa4 generally parallels mfa1, suggesting coordinated evolution, while mfa5 is more diverse, with five genotypes (A–E) independent of mfa1 [32]. Some strains even carry two mfa5 alleles, potentially altering fimbrial assembly [32]. Although mfa1–mfa4 proteins are structurally conserved, sequence polymorphisms can affect adhesion, immunogenicity and biofilm formation [24,25]. For example, the mfa1 fimbriae in ATCC 33277, a less virulent strain, belongs to the typical mfa1 genotype (~70 kDa group), consistent with many other P. gingivalis strains [32]. Interestingly, some strains carry duplicated mfa genes: W83, For instance, has two copies of mfa5 (a tip adhesin protein) of distinct sequence types, a reflection of genomic rearrangements [32]. P. gingivalis W83 produces mfa1 minor fimbriae encoded by the mfa1-5 operon, structurally typical but less abundant than in strains such as ATCC 33277, reflecting a phenotype associated with enhanced virulence and capsule presence [37].

Gingipains

Proteolytic enzymes are central to both metabolism and virulence of P. gingivalis, with gingipains being the most prominent. These cysteine proteases not only degrade host proteins but also modulate immune responses [38]. All sequenced P. gingivalis strains carry genes encoding the arginine-specific gingipains (rgpA and rgpB) as well as the lysine-specific gingipain (kgp) [39,40]. Notably, the catalytic domains of gingipains are highly conserved among strains, showing minimal sequence divergence [39,40]. At least two distinct catalytic domain variants of kgp, known as kgp_catI and kgp_catII, have been identified among P. gingivalis strains. Additionally, three variants of the 4th adhesin domain (the CAD or K4 domain) have also been reported among these strains. These protein domain differences affect activity and potential interaction with host proteins, reflecting notable genetic diversity in this key virulence determinant [41,42].

In contrast to the gingipain active sites (or catalytic domains), the adhesion/hemagglutinin (HA) domains of rgpA exhibit significant sequence divergence and structural variation among P. gingivalis strains, including truncations and rearrangements [42]. The diversity in these domains is generated by domain shuffling and allelic exchange, allowing polyclonal populations to rapidly alter their phenotype and antigenic profile [43,44]. This variability in adhesin domains is a non-random adaptive mechanism that helps P. gingivalis evade host immunity while maintaining its virulence functions, such as colonization and immune modulation [44]. Subclones with deletions or rearrangements in these domains often show reduced virulence, indicating a trade-off between immune evasion and pathogenic capacity [42].

Apart from being released as soluble enzymes, gingipains are also localized on the bacterial surface or in outer membrane vesicles, where they can interact with host cells. Notably, the retention of gingipains on the cell surface versus their release differs by strain. Strain HG66 of P. gingivalis is an extreme example in gingipain secretion phenotype: it does not retain gingipains on the cell surface but instead secretes almost all gingipains and other C-terminal domain (CTD)-bearing proteins into the surrounding medium [45,46]. In contrast, the gingipains in W83 are largely surface-bound, promoting localized proteolytic activity important for tissue [47]. ATCC 33277 secretes gingipains and expresses gingipain-associated hemagglutinins, but compared to W83, it shows greater differences in gingipain glycosylation and activity which may affect virulence and host interactions [14]. A7436 is considered more virulent than ATC 33277, with enhanced gingipain enzymatic activity and different regulation of iron/heme acquisition systems, contributing to greater tissue damage potential [14]. W50 exhibits the highest combined Arg- and Lys-gingipain activity on whole cells among several laboratory strains, indicating robust proteolytic potential likely linked to virulence [48]. Strain 381 has relatively lower gingipain activity but an intact regulatory system that impacts its immune-stimulatory capacity, contrasting with ATCC 33277 which has higher gingipain expression that dampens host immune activation [49].

Hemagglutinins

P. gingivalis also produces several non-gingipain hemagglutinins and adhesins, including hagA, hagB and hagC, which play key roles in pathogenicity and host interaction. hagB and hagC are homologs, varying only by a few amino acids, but are differentially expressed [50]. HagA is especially notable as a large hemagglutinin protein encoded by the hagA gene, characterized by repetitive adhesin domains contributing to its function as an adhesin and virulence factor [51]. HagA is one of the largest bacterial proteins known, encoded by a gene of about 7.9 kb long and producing a 2,628 amino-acid polypeptide with molecular weight of over 283 kDa [51]. Its structure includes several large, continuous repeat regions, usually two to four per molecule, each about 1.3 kb long [51]. These repeats form hemagglutinin/adhesin domains that resemble those found in the adhesin portions of rgpA and kgp gingipains [52]. While the number of repeats varies among P. gingivalis strains, creating allelic and size diversity, the number remains stable within an individual strain during lab culture or infection experiments [53].

HagA plays a key role in P. gingivalis pathogenicity by promoting attachment to host epithelial and endothelial cells, enabling red blood cell clumping to obtain heme and enhancing overall virulence in both laboratory and animal models [53,54]. Strains that produce HagA show stronger hemagglutination and greater survival within host tissues, with the number of its domain repeats linked to virulence intensity [55]. Conversely, mutants lacking hagA exhibits weaker hemagglutination, reduced heme-binding capacity and diminished biofilm formation [54,56]. Although HagA, HagB and HagC all support adhesion and hemagglutination, HagA is especially critical for red blood cell binding and heme-based nutrient acquisition [57].

Highly virulent P. gingivalis strains such as W83 and A7436 typically maintain full-length adhesins and intact gingipain domains, supporting stronger host cell adhesion and persistence in infection models. In contrast, less virulent strains such as ATCC 33277 and HG66 have been reported to carry alterations in adhesin and gingipain loci, which are associated with reduced hemagglutination and lower invasion efficiency [14,43,58]. These mutations can occur within the C-terminal cleaved adhesin domains of kgp or rgpA gingipains or within hemagglutinin/adhesin genes such as hagA and hagB, which share repeating adhesin motifs prone to recombination [42].

Comparative genomic and phylogenetic studies show that P. gingivalis has a highly mosaic pattern of surface virulence genes, especially in gingipain and hemagglutinin regions. Different forms of Lys-gingipain (kgp_catI and kgp_catII) and their adhesin domains, along with various fimbrial gene alleles such as fimA, mfa1, ragAB, tpr and prtT, appear across strains without following their core genome relationships. Comparative genomic analyzes of P. gingivalis isolates from around the world reveal extensive horizontal gene transfer and recombination, creating complex, interconnected phylogenetic patterns in which virulence gene variations rather than core genome differences account for most of the species' diversity [42,59].

The extensive domain rearrangement and allele swapping observed within P. gingivalis indicate that selective pressures from the host immune system promote continuous diversification of its surface virulence genes as predicted by the distributed genome hypothesis [21,60,61]. Consequently, the bacterium's virulence profile likely evolves not through simple clonal inheritance but through adaptive recombination and the exchange of adhesin and gingipain components. This genetic flexibility allows P. gingivalis to refine its strategies for host colonization and effectively evade immune defenses [42,59].

Peptidylarginine deiminase (PPAD)

The P. gingivalis peptidylarginine deiminase (PPAD), encoded by ppad, is a distinctive virulence factor that catalyzes the conversion of arginine residues to citrulline. Unlike mammalian peptidylarginine deiminases, PPAD is calcium-independent and preferentially deiminates C-terminal arginines, many of which are generated after proteolysis by arginine-specific gingipains. This functional coupling between gingipains and PPAD links bacterial proteolysis with post-translational modification of both bacterial and host proteins [62,63].

PPAD has received particular attention because of its proposed role in the association between periodontal disease and rheumatoid arthritis. P. gingivalis is unusual among oral bacteria in its ability to generate citrullinated peptides, and PPAD-mediated citrullination may contribute to the formation of neoepitopes that stimulate anti-citrullinated protein antibody responses in genetically susceptible individuals. Experimental studies have supported a mechanistic link between PPAD and arthritis-related autoimmunity, strengthening the view that PPAD is relevant not only to periodontal inflammation but also to systemic inflammatory disease [63–65].

PPAD activity can also vary among P. gingivalis strains. Recent work has shown that ppad genetic variants differ in enzyme activity, and mutations near functionally important regions of the protein may alter catalytic efficiency. This strain-level variation suggests that PPAD should be considered a variable virulence determinant rather than a uniform species trait [62,66].

In addition to its role in citrullination and rheumatoid arthritis-associated mechanisms, PPAD also contributes directly to host‒pathogen signaling. Recent studies showed that PPAD activity, together with fimbriae, is required for P. gingivalis-induced TLR2-dependent host cell signaling. These findings indicate that PPAD is not only a metabolic or modification enzyme, but also part of the signaling machinery by which P. gingivalis engages host innate immune responses [67,68].

Capsule (capsular polysaccharide)

The capsule of P. gingivalis is characterized by a thick polysaccharide layer external to the bacterial cell wall that acts as a major virulence factor aiding immune evasion [28]. Encapsulated strains demonstrate markedly increased resistance to phagocytosis by macrophages and dendritic cells, as well as to complement-mediated killing, compared with non-encapsulated strain [28]. Experimental subcutaneous infection models show that encapsulated strains induce invasive, spreading phlegmonous infections, whereas non-encapsulated counterparts cause only localized abscesses, confirming the capsule's crucial role in systemic virulence [1,28].

The capsule of P. gingivalis is also known for its dampening effect on host immune activation. Encapsulated strains reduce dendritic cell maturation and suppress cytokine expression, including interleukin-1 (IL-1), IL-6 and IL-8, thereby downregulating the host inflammatory response and attenuating activation of the complement alternative pathway [1]. The capsule's anti-inflammatory properties promote bacterial persistence and limit immune clearance by inhibiting recognition pathways tied to toll-like receptors and complement proteins [28].

Early studies divided P. gingivalis into six main capsule types (K1–K6), each characterized by different surface sugars, plus a non-capsulated type called K− [1,69]. A possible seventh serotype (K7) has since been suggested [70]. The capsule types correlate with certain strains: for example, W83 and its close relative W50 belong to serotype K1 [71]; other reference strains for K-types include HG184 (K2), ATCC 53977 (K3), ATCC 49417 (K4), HG1690 (K5), HG1691 (K6) and an experimental strain 34-4 (K7) [70]. In contrast, ATCC 33277 is non-encapsulated (K−) [71]. Table 3 summarizes the capsule status for various strains.

Genomic comparisons show that capsule biosynthesis genes cluster in the capsular polysaccharide synthesis (CPS) locus and vary by K-serotype. A comparative genomic hybridization study (K1–K7) found most strain differences localized to this region. Each serotype has a distinct capsular gene cluster, while non-encapsulated strains like ATCC 33277 and 381 typically carry deletions or mutations in the CPS locus [71]. However, capsule alone does not fully determine virulence: For instance, W83 and W50 are both K1, yet W83 is slightly more virulent, likely due to additional factors. Likewise, strain 381, though largely non-encapsulated, can still cause disease under certain conditions. However, encapsulated strains (e.g. W83, A7436, W50) tend to be more virulent, whereas non-encapsulated strains (e.g. ATCC 33277, HG66, 381) generally show reduced virulence, consistent with the broader role of capsule in enhancing invasive potential [29].

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)

P. gingivalis produces two forms of LPS: O-LPS and A-LPS. O-LPS carries an O-antigen made of repeating sugar units that vary by strain and define O-serotypes (e.g. O1–O3 in strains 381, HG1691, W50). The O-LPS core can appear either ‘uncapped’ (without O-antigen) or ‘capped’ (with O-antigen). The core is linked to lipid A, the main immunoactive component and contains mannose-rich structures that can be further modified (e.g. with phosphoethanolamine), influencing antigenicity and immune interactions [72]. In contrast, A-LPS is an anionic polysaccharide characterized by a phosphorylated mannan backbone with branching α-mannose side chains linked through phosphodiester bonds. A-LPS in P. gingivalis has been proposed to form a surface-associated layer involved in surface protection and host interaction, but its structural organization and biological significance remain incompletely defined [29,73]. Since A-LPS shares features with capsular polysaccharides, it can partially compensate when the capsule is absent, helping the bacterium evade immunity. These surface adaptations shape how P. gingivalis modulates host responses and promotes persistent infection [73].

Strain-specific differences in P. gingivalis LPS stem from variation in the 15 biosynthetic genes that control polysaccharide length, composition and lipid A modification [72]. For instance, W50 produces two LPS forms with different O-antigen chain lengths, seen as a ‘ladder’ pattern on gels. The ratios of these forms differ by strain due to variations in glycosyltransferases and related enzymes [72]. Comparative genomic data shows that LPS and capsule biosynthesis genes are among the most variable across strains, contributing to differences in surface structure, immune evasion and virulence [72,73]. In one comparative genomic study, four hemagglutinin genes and multiple LPS/capsule genes were among the 25 most variable genes indicating frequent changes in these surface polysaccharides [70]. Overall, these genomic variations generate distinct LPS chemotypes that shape each strain's pathogenic potential. As a result, LPS heterogeneity among strains significantly influences immune evasion and host‒pathogen interactions. Different strains produce LPS molecules with varying proportions of tetra-acylated and penta-acylated lipid A isoforms, which distinctly modulate innate immune signaling [70]. Strain-specific LPS differences also involve variation in O-antigen and A-LPS.

It is clear that P. gingivalis strain differences in LPS lipid A acylation patterns and polysaccharide antigen structures generate a spectrum of immune-modulating capabilities, thereby influencing strain virulence, host immune recognition and bacterial persistence [74].

RagA and RagB protein

P. gingivalis survival during infection relies on scavenging limited nutrients such as heme, iron and B12 [75]. SusC_1/RagA, a TonB-linked outer membrane protein, plays a key role in heme transport [76]. The P. gingivalis rag locus encodes two outer membrane proteins, RagA and RagB, forming a TonB-dependent receptor complex involved in nutrient uptake and linked to virulence. RagA is a 115 kDa TonB-dependent 22-stranded β-barrel transporter and surface levels of both protein a 55 kDa substrate-binding lipoprotein that together form a mechanism resembling a ‘pedal bin’, opening to take up peptides cleaved by gingipains [77]. ragA and ragB are expressed together and rely on each other for proper folding and surface localization; disrupting both gene greatly reduces surface levels of both cognate proteins and decreases virulence [77]. The rag alleles differ substantially, with only ~30%–50% amino acid identity among variants. Consistent with this divergence, monoclonal antibodies against one RagB type fail to bind others, showing that each variant has a distinct antigenic profile [78].

Four highly divergent rag alleles (rag-1 to rag-4) have been identified across clinical strains, with significant functional and antigenic variation among them [78]. The rag-1 allele, found in the highly virulent strains W83 and W50, is strongly associated with increased virulence in animal models of infection, including soft tissue destruction and enhanced survival within the host [79]. Epidemiological studies indicate rag-2 and rag-4 are also widely present in clinical isolates but are generally associated with lower virulence. Geographical differences in allele prevalence have also been reported [77]. Functional implications of rag allelic variation may include differences in substrate specificity, kinetics of the transport cycle and regulation, potentially granting strains unique nutrient acquisition profiles that influence fitness and pathogenicity [77,80].

The type IX secretion system (T9SS)

The type IX secretion system (T9SS) in P. gingivalis is a specialized export pathway that transports many key virulence factors, including gingipains and other conserved C-terminal domain (CTD)-bearing proteins, across the outer membrane to the cell surface or extracellular milieu [81]. The system is composed of multiple core components, including PorK, PorL, PorM, PorN, PorP, PorQ, PorT, PorV, PorU and PorZ, which assemble into large membrane-associated complexes. Several of these genes are co-transcribed in operons such as porKLMN, forming a functional secretion nanomachine. Expression of T9SS genes is tightly regulated by the PorX–PorY two-component system and the ECF sigma factor SigP, and disruption of essential T9SS components or regulators results in loss of colony pigmentation, impaired gingipain activity, defects in heme acquisition and markedly reduced virulence. Beyond the T9SS, numerous additional genes contribute to virulence and fitness, including those involved in iron acquisition, outer membrane adhesion and porin function, outer membrane vesicle production, proteolysis and stress-response pathways such as oxidative, nitrosative, heat-shock, DNA repair and stringent responses [82–89]. Furthermore, two-component regulators, global transcriptional factors, quorum-sensing systems, small regulatory RNAs and capsule/EPS biosynthesis genes modulate virulence expression and host adaptation [83,90–96]. Collectively, the accessory genes complement the core virulence machinery and broaden the organism's pathogenic potential (Table 4).

Conclusion

The virulence of P. gingivalis is highly strain dependent and is driven by genetic variation in key virulence determinants, including fimbriae, gingipains, hemagglutinins, capsular polysaccharides, lipopolysaccharides, nutrient acquisition systems and the type IX secretion system. Comparative genomic and phenotypic studies of strains such as W83, ATCC 33277, HG66, A7436 and others have demonstrated that relatively small genetic differences can result in substantial variation in colonization, invasion, immune modulation and overall pathogenic potential. Highly virulent strains often possess a combination of characteristics, including enhanced capsule production, increased gingipain activity, specific fimbrial genotypes and other virulence-associated alleles, whereas less virulent strains such as ATCC 33277 frequently lack capsule production or express lower-virulence fimbrial genotypes.

Advances in comparative genomics, phylogenomics and strain-level characterization have greatly improved our understanding of the remarkable diversity that exists within the species. At the same time, horizontal gene transfer, genome rearrangements and variation in regulatory networks continue to generate new genetic combinations that blur traditional strain classifications and complicate the prediction of virulence based solely on lineage. Although significant progress has been made in identifying strain-specific virulence factors, many questions remain regarding how these determinants interact and contribute to pathogenicity in different host environments.

Population-based studies and experimental models suggest that certain phylogenetic groups are more frequently associated with severe periodontitis and exhibit enhanced pathogenic potential. However, the goal of future research should extend beyond cataloging strains to identify the specific genetic, regulatory and functional signatures that drive virulence and tissue tropism. Integrating comparative genomics with transcriptomic, proteomic and functional analyzes across a broader collection of clinical isolates will be essential for defining these molecular signatures. Such efforts may ultimately improve risk assessment, facilitate precision diagnostics and support the development of targeted therapeutic strategies for periodontal disease.
