Dynamic Source Distribution and Emission Inventory of a Persistent, Mobile, and Toxic (PMT) Substance, Melamine, in China
Authors: Shaoxuan Zhang, Jiazhe Chen, Zhanyun Wang, Chengkang Chen, Anna Chen, Qiaonan Jing, Jianguo Liu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Persistent, mobile, and toxic (PMT) substances are affecting the safety of drinking water and are threatening the environment and human health. Many PMT substances are used in industrial processing or consumer products, but their sources and emissions mostly remain unclear. This study presents a long-term source distribution and emission estimation of melamine, a high-production-volume PMT substance of emerging global concern. The results indicate that in China, approximately 1858.7 kilotonnes (kt) of melamine were released into the water (∼58.9%), air (∼27.0%), and soil systems (∼14.1%) between 1995 and 2020, mainly from its production and use in the decorative panels, textiles, and paper industries. The textile and paper industries have the highest emission-to-consumption ratios, with more than 90% emissions per unit consumption. Sewage treatment plants are the largest source of melamine in the environment for the time being, but in-use products and their wastes will serve as significant melamine sources in the future. The study prompts priority action to control the risk of PMT substances internationally.

Introduction

Persistent, mobile, and toxic (PMT) substances are growingly identified as ubiquitous water contaminants of emerging concern in recent years, posing an equivalent level of concern to persistent, bioaccumulation, and toxic (PBT) substances.1−5 Over 200 substances are suggested as PMT or very persistent and very mobile (vPvM).6 PMT substances such as melamine are now receiving increasing attention both domestically and internationally.7 Still, critical data gaps persist, hampering sound management of them.6,8−10 Among others, while many PMT substances are used in industrial processing or consumer products, little is known about their specific sources and emissions.11−13

Melamine was among the first substances suggested as a PMT/vPvM substance1,6 and, therefore, has recently been identified as a substance of very high concern (SVHC)14 under the European Union’s Chemicals Regulation, REACH.15,16 Melamine has previously made headlines worldwide due to its unauthorized addition to pet food and infant formula, which led to severe adverse health impacts on animals and young children.17 Epidemiological studies further show that it is closely associated with kidney damage and reproductive interference,18−20 and it may have low toxicity to algae, fish, shrimp, and other aquatic organisms.21,22

Numerous pieces of evidence together indicate that melamine is adversely affecting the ecosystem and human health. In recent years, the occurrence of melamine has been reported in various environmental media,23−33 plants,34 animals,35−37 and humans.38−40 Moreover, the highest concentration of melamine in sewage treatment plants (STPs) ever recorded in the world was reported in China.26,34 Due to its high detection rates (33–91%) and concentrations (>1 μg/L) in the water environment, which exceed those of most other PMT/vPvM substances, melamine is increasingly recognized as a critical pollutant of drinking water sources.30−32

The ubiquitous environmental contamination of melamine comes from its extensive production (more than one million tonnes per annum41,42) and use in industrial processing and commercial products.7 Melamine is mostly employed in commercial products in the form of resins.43 For a series of excellent characteristics including flame retardancy, moisture-proof, and insulation, melamine resins are widely used in various consumer products, such as surface decorative panels, adhesives, coatings, and molding compounds.33,44 Melamine is also widely used as a wet-strength agent for paper, a flame retardant for textiles, and a high-efficiency water reducing agent for concrete.43,45 It has been detected in building products,46 tableware,44 childcare facilities,47 and infant clothing.45,48 Available studies show that melamine could hardly be removed in the wastewater treatment systems, and a large fraction emitted via water would be released into the environment.49 However, the current research on the emissions of melamine and other PMT substances is still in its infancy,50−52 with limited to no quantitative understandings.8

In 2020, the global production of melamine reached 1.6 million tonnes, of which about 75% occurred in China.38 Not only being the world’s largest producer, China is also the world’s largest consumer of melamine.53,54 This study investigates the specific source distribution and quantifies the major emissions of melamine in China between 1995 and 2020. Thus, this study aims to put forward an important piece of the puzzle of the sources and emissions of high-production-volume PMT substances and facilitate future research on the fate, exposure, and risk of melamine.

Materials and Methods

Data Analysis of the Melamine Industry in China

In China, the extensive production and use of melamine started in the 1990s using well-established domestic technology,55−57 and by the mid-1990s, its annual production capacity had reached 40 kt.55 The most recent data on the production and use of melamine come from the year 2020.58−60 Therefore, this study covers the time period 1995–2020. The spatial scope is limited to mainland China, as reliable statistics for Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan are not available.

219 melamine industry data entries were collected from 18 published literature and statistics publications, including annual production, import and export, apparent consumption, and proportion of use in major application industries between 1995 and 2020 (Table S1). The retrieved data were processed in the following ways to address data quality and data gaps, using the R programming language where relevant.

First, data from the same source with long-term series characteristics were preferred. From the melamine industrial data, mainly the original data of melamine production and apparent consumption recorded in Huang,55 Yu,61 and Jia59 were selected because they show a good time continuity, covering the years 1995–1997, 1998–2008, and 2010–2020, respectively. Second, the structural time series model in the imputeTS package was utilized for data interpolation,62 filling in the data gaps on melamine production and consumption in 2009, and on the proportion of yearly industrial applications in about half of the time series. Third, data from the same source could not always cover the entire time period. The paucity of existing data on the application proportion of melamine in the industry is particularly obvious.55,56,58,59,61,63 In such cases, the locally weighted regression (LOESS) approach was used to smooth the regression of literature data and interpolation data to reduce the disparity in data reporting quality between different literatures.

Due to a lack of quantitative and detailed information, especially on the emission factors related to waste disposal methods,7 emissions of melamine from the disposal of end-of-life products could not be comprehensively and accurately quantified. Instead, preliminary estimations of such emissions were conducted by integrating the relative proportions of waste disposal methods in China (i.e., incineration, recycling, landfill, and dumping and simple landfill)79,80 with assumed or model-estimated EFs under different scenarios (Tables S7 and S8). Considering that the proportions of end-of-life products undergoing incineration and recycling are low and the emissions from these processes are usually low,7,81 this study assumed that the emission factors of melamine under these two disposal methods are negligible. For the landfill as well as dumping and simple landfill processes, the MOCLA model82 was used to estimate EFs. More specifically, four degradation half-lives of melamine (T1/2) were used as input parameters for the model to estimate four scenarios of EFs from landfills: Scenario I: T1/2 = 37.5 days; Scenario II: T1/2 = 60.0 days; Scenario III: T1/2 = 180.0 days; and Scenario IV: no degradation (more details can be found in Supporting Information Text S2).

Validation of the Emission Estimation

The MAMI III (multimedia activity model applicable to neutral and ionizable molecules)83 model was used to stimulate the environmental concentrations of melamine in China based on the emission estimates above. The MAMI III model, based on the chemical activity, comprehensively considers the effects of advection, diffusion, degradation, dissociation constant, and ionic strength on the transport behavior and fate of chemicals and is suitable for use with nonvolatile and ionizable chemicals84 such as melamine. The simulated concentrations were then compared to the monitored concentrations in Chinese rivers, soils, and sediments (Table S9). A detailed description of the relevant model parameterization can be found in the Supporting Information, Text S3 and Tables S10 and S11.

Sensitivity and Uncertainty Analysis

Sensitivity analysis provides a powerful tool to test the influence of changes in input parameters on changes in model output and to quantify it by the sensitivity coefficient (S).85 To evaluate the relative impact of individual parameters on the estimated emissions, this study varied the input parameters of the material flow analysis by ±10% of their original values, including the production and consumption of melamine, the product import–export trade, the product life span, and the emission factors of different life-cycle stages and industries. This is based on the commonly used method in the literature and has been proven to be reasonable and effective.65,79,86−89 The sensitivity was obtained for the entire time period of 1995–2020 using eq 8; the higher the value, the more significant the impact of the independent variable change on the results8where S is the sensitivity of the estimation results to the independent variables; I0, I+10%, and I–10% are the original value of the independent variable and the value after 10% expansion or reduction; O0, O+10%, and O–10% are the estimates obtained after the original value of the estimated result and the change of the independent variable ±10% (annual emissions were selected here for comparison).

The study further evaluated the uncertainties in melamine emissions (total and to the air, water, and soil systems), melamine in in-use products, and melamine entering the end-of-life stage from 1995 to 2020. It used Monte Carlo simulations for 15,000 iterations, where the coefficient of variation for each input variable was calculated as suggested by Laner et al.90 This method introduces six data quality indicators (reliability, completeness, temporal correlation, geographic correlation, other correlation, and expert estimate) to evaluate the representativeness of the input parameters and scores the indicators under the level of sensitivity of different data defects to the results (Table S12), transformed into an uncertainty range, i.e., the coefficient of variation (Table S13). This analysis was performed in Oracle Crystal Ball software, and all input variables were assumed to be normally distributed. The uncertainty range was the difference between the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles (95% confidence interval).

Results and Discussion

Temporal Distribution of Melamine Use in China

According to our analysis, as shown in Figure 1, in China, melamine has been widely used in surface decorative panels, adhesives, coatings, molding compounds, pulp and paper, textiles, and cement products. Surface decorative panels were the largest melamine consumption industry in most years, with an average contribution of 40.6%. Molding compounds, mainly tableware, consumed about 18.3% of melamine. Coating and adhesive consumption accounted for the same proportion, both of which were 13.1%. Among the other products, melamine was used in a small proportion.

Temporal distribution of melamine uses in various industries in China from 1995 to 2020.

Over the long time period, the proportion of melamine used in decorative panels and molding compounds showed a seesaw effect, which may be related to chemical control policies and public interest in related products.91,92 China officially implemented mandatory standards for controlling free formaldehyde content in building materials in 200192 and improved indoor formaldehyde concentration standards again in 2010.91 These actions have greatly encouraged the consumption of melamine in surface decorative panels because melamine resin can effectively prevent formaldehyde releases from the panels.93 Melamine molding compounds have also seen a transition over the past 20 years, from wide usage to much less due to questions on their safety.44,94 The application ratio of melamine in molding compounds has demonstrated a trend of first an increase and then a decrease. In other industries, such as adhesives, coatings, textiles, and cement products, the volume of use of melamine has increased, but the proportion of use has remained relatively stable in recent years.

Overview of Melamine Flows and Its Stocks in In-Use Products

Figure 2 shows the flows of melamine across life-cycle stages. Between 1995 and 2020, the production of melamine in China increased nearly 40 times, from 34.0 to 1297.0 kt/year (more details in Figure S1), and the cumulative production reached 14212.0 kt. While the import of melamine was relatively minor (about 115.5 kt in total), the export has increased steadily, with a sum export of 3615.0 kt by 2020, i.e., more than 25.0% of the total production. Thus, the apparent consumption of melamine (the amounts entering the products and losses during downstream product manufacturing) has increased significantly, with approximately 10667.0 kt within more than two decades. Meanwhile, about 132.5 kt of melamine was exported abroad with downstream products, and 773.7 kt entered the domestic market through imported products. After the products were used, 26.8% of melamine (∼3812.4 kt) was discarded with the end-of-life products over time.

Flows of melamine in China from 1995 to 2020, with the flow width indicating the scales of mass.

By the end of 2020, the net accumulation of melamine in in-use products had reached 4399.7 kt, accounting for about 31.0% of the total production. Among them, the average proportions of individual in-use products are surface decorative panels (44.4%), coatings (18.0%), adhesives (19.1%), molding compounds (12.1%), cement water reducers (5.9%), pulp and paper (0.5%), and textiles (0.03%; Figure 3 and Table S14). It is worth noting that the combination of high usage and medium-long life span95 leads to the highest melamine stock in surface decorative panels, while the stocks in paper and textiles are low due to low melamine use, high emission factors (Table S4), and short product life span (Table S5). In addition, despite the use of melamine in cement water reducers being equivalent to textiles and paper, the much longer life span of cement products than that of textiles and paper96 and the lower emission factor (Table S4) has led to increases of melamine stock in cement water reducers.

Net accumulation and proportion of melamine in in-use products in China from 1995 to 2020.

Dynamic Emissions of Melamine into the Environment

Differences in the use patterns have led to different emission characteristics (including environmental media) across various industries and life-cycle stages. As shown in Figures 2 and 4, China emitted 1858.7 kt of melamine into the environment from the production of melamine, as well as the manufacture and use of products between 1995 and 2020, with an increase of more than 87 times in annual emissions from 1.7 kt in 1995 (0.09%) to 146.0 kt in 2020 (7.9%).

Temporal evolutions of melamine annual emissions: (a) to different receiving environment media; (b) from different industries; and (c) from various emission processes.

Our estimates indicate that the water system was the principal sink, receiving 58.9% of the cumulative emissions, followed by the air system, accounting for another 27.0%. The remaining 14.1% entered the soil system indirectly through leakage during product manufacturing and use, STP sludge disposal, and other ways (Figures 2 and 4a). While our estimation of the multimedia distribution of melamine emissions is generally consistent with the simulation results by ECCC and HC,97 the current study further presents quantitative and dynamic source distributions of melamine emissions.

Pulp and paper (∼40.1%), surface decorative panels (∼28.1%), and textiles (∼19.6%) are the three application industries that contributed most to the emissions, while the other four industries have had relatively limited emissions (Figure 4b). It has been noted that melamine was far less commonly used in the pulp and paper industry than in surface decorative panels (Figure 1), while the former produced more significant emissions. This means that estimating ultimate emissions based on usage alone is unreliable.

When looking at releases from individual processes (Figure 4c), STP, as the centralized disposal site of most melamine-containing wastewaters,26,98 has emitted more than 1090.0 kt into the water and soil systems, accounting for 58.7% of the total emissions. The second dominant source was the in-use products, with 546.4 kt of melamine flowing directly into the environment, accounting for 29.4% of the total emissions. The melamine released during the production of melamine and manufacturing of downstream products reached 1136.0 kt, but only 221.5 kt (11.9% of the total emissions) directly entered the environment uncollected; its direct contribution to environmental emissions was relatively small; another 914.5 kt went into STP with wastewater, which is also an important factor for STP to become the largest source of melamine environmental emissions.

From 1995 to 2005, the emissions from in-use products were comparable to those from STPs. With improved wastewater collection in China (Table S6), the emissions from the STPs increased rapidly. The emission difference between the two sources peaked in 2012 (54.0 kt). Since then, the gap has slowly decreased to 14.3 kt in 2020 (Figure S2). It is anticipated that even if the production and use of melamine are severely restricted in the future, the stocks in in-use products will continue to be slowly released. At that time, the primary emission source will likely change from STPs to in-use products, resulting in changes in environmental exposure and their implications on the ecosystem and human health.

The emission validation results show that more than half of the predicted concentrations are within an order of magnitude of the environmental monitoring concentrations of melamine reported in the literature, taking into account the range fluctuations in the monitoring concentrations (Table S9 and Figure S3). This good agreement suggests the robustness of our emission estimates.

Priority Analysis of the Melamine Sources

As shown in our results, different patterns exist between the industrial distribution of melamine uses and emissions (Figures 1 and 4b). Thus, concentrating on industries with either high usage or high emission factors alone cannot accurately identify the most “problematic” or “worrisome” sources that need priority attention.

Hence, in our analysis, we introduced the emission-to-consumption ratios (units: kt/kt) for each product industry by dividing the annual melamine emissions by the annual apparent consumption (Figure 5). The emission-to-consumption ratios differ from the EFs for each life-cycle stage as they measure the total emissions per unit amount of melamine consumed by each product industry throughout the life cycle. The emission-to-consumption ratios of individual industries are, in descending order of the average values, textiles (∼94.2%), pulp and paper (∼90.3%), cement water reducers (∼17.5%), surface decorative panels (∼9.5%), coatings (∼7.6%), adhesives (∼2.8%), and molding compounds (∼0.3%).

Emission-to-consumption ratios of melamine in China during 1995–2020: (a) pulp and paper; (b) textiles; (c) surface decorative panels, adhesives, coatings, and cement water reducers; and (d) molding compounds.

The emission-to-consumption ratios can give an insightful picture of individual industries that need priority attention. For example, despite minor usage in textiles (Figure 1), the high solubility of melamine makes it almost entirely emitted into wastewater during the textile washing process,45 resulting in the highest emission-to-consumption ratios from the textile industry. Similarly, about 90% of melamine, the main component of paper wet-strength agents, flows into wastewater during pulp processing (Table S4), resulting in the high emission-to-consumption ratios in this industry.72 Melamine usage in these two industries makes up around 10% of the overall consumption, but the release makes up nearly 60.0% of the total emissions (Figure 4b). Increasing the collection of pulp and textile wastewater and melamine removal in STPs is expected to effectively reduce emissions into the water system.

Surface decorative panels contribute most significantly to melamine consumption and stocks in in-use products. However, as shown in Figure 5, only 9.5% of melamine in surface decorative panels has been released, with most expected to adhere to dust particles in the air system.33,99,100 Substantial amounts of melamine are still present in in-use products and will gradually be released into home environments due to its widespread use in interior design. Such long-term, large potential for human exposure, and possible adverse effects on human health warrant further attention.

In comparison, melamine emissions associated with the cement water reducer, coatings, adhesives, and molding compound industries have a relatively lower priority (Figure 5). For example, although the emission-to-consumption ratios of cement water reducers were moderately high, their low usage resulted in a limited emission contribution. Meanwhile, it is crucial to keep in mind that low emission-to-consumption ratios do not mean that molding compound products are harmless. This is because a significant amount of melamine may be released from the molding compound products or migrate into food at high temperatures and under high radiation conditions, given the popularity of automatic dishwashers and ultraviolet disinfection cabinets in Chinese families.44,101

Scenario-Based Emission Estimation of Melamine in End-of-Life Products

The amounts of melamine that enter the end-of-life stage with waste products continue to increase, from 1.3 kt/year in 1995 to 400.6 kt/year in 2020. Among the end-of-life products, surface decorative panels and molding compounds are the main ones (Figures 6 and S4). Using four scenarios, the current average annual emissions of melamine from end-of-life products were preliminarily estimated to be in the range of 0.1–2.2 kt/year. We also note that from Scenario 1 to Scenario 4, longer melamine half-life results in a higher emission factor for melamine in end-of-life products and longer emission duration (Table S8) and thus larger cumulative emissions (Figure S4). In Scenarios 1–3, the majority of melamine is eventually degraded in landfills. Under the “Worst Emissions Scenario” (i.e., Scenario IV, assuming no degradation of melamine in landfills), the total historical emissions of melamine-containing scrap products reached 57.2 kt, which were far lower than the emission of 1858.7 kt during the production of melamine and the production and use of downstream products; nondegradation also means that the rest of the melamine that was not emitted remains in landfills and is a persistent source of pollutant emissions.

Amount of melamine entering the end-of-life stage with discarded products per year and the annual emissions of melamine from the end-of-life stage under different scenarios.

Note that this is a scenario-based estimate of possible emissions of melamine from end-of-life products in the absence of quantitative and detailed information and with two main limitations. First, we assume that the reported disposal pattern of domestic waste in China reflects the disposal pattern of melamine-associated end-of-life products, which may not be necessarily the case in reality. Second, we focus on melamine emissions from landfill as well as dumping and simple landfill (>50% of end-of-life products; Table S8) and do not consider emissions from incineration and recycling. This omission may result in underestimating the total emissions from the end-of-life phase of the products, which may warrant future studies.

Sensitivity and Uncertainty Analysis

Parameter sensitivity analysis (Figure S5) revealed that most independent variables had little effect on the dMFA-MEL model. Among the various parameters, the apparent consumption of melamine, which was based on high-quality literature research and industry reporting, had the greatest influence on the estimated environmental emissions. Others such as EFs (mainly pulp and paper, surface decorative panels) and product life were also relatively sensitive input parameters. We selected the EFs based on OECD,102 European Chemicals Bureau,68 and research recommended values (Table S4), and the product life span based on international databases95 and public literature (Table S5). More realistic emission parameters required extensive environmental monitoring, and product life span parameters depended on sample size and representativeness,103 which were key parts of improving simulation accuracy.

The Monte Carlo uncertainty analysis results indicated that the output results followed a normal distribution (Figure S6), assuming that all of the input parameters were randomly independent and normally distributed. The simulation results for melamine emissions, melamine in in-use products, and the amount of melamine entering the end-of-life stage were quite stable, and the order-of-magnitude differences between the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the Monte Carlo analysis results were within a reasonable range (Table 1).

While being robust and reliable, it should be noted that the estimations of past melamine emissions in China from this study have additional uncertainties that cannot be captured by the Monte Carlo analysis. A meaningful quantitative assessment of all uncertainty is not possible due to the lack of adequate information available. Instead, the following is a qualitative discussion of the uncertainties.

The study assumed that the EFs of melamine in STP in China were consistent (Table S4), which might differ from the actual operation of different sewage treatment processes. This is also a potential source of uncertainty. However, despite the existence of various wastewater treatment processes, the literature indicated that melamine was hardly biodegraded in STP and was mainly removed by activated sludge adsorption.25,67 Moreover, the latest SimpleTreat model screening showed that nearly 77.5% of melamine in STP entered the environment from effluent.49 Based on these reports, the EFs of melamine in STP selected in this study had good reliability, and the parameter sensitivity analysis also demonstrated that they had little impact on the estimated results.

We acknowledge that this study does not contain a complete picture of melamine emissions to the environment but rather quantifies emissions that were associated with the direct production and use of melamine. Use of cyromazine and cyanamide fertilizers in agriculture may also generate melamine,24,34 but it comes from the transformation of precursors,7 which are beyond the focus of this study.

This study assesses the impact of international trade on melamine mass flow and emissions by assuming equal melamine content in all imported and exported melamine products. This assumption is based on the similarity of the melamine-consuming application industries around the globe.61,76 The actual melamine levels in products from different production regions may vary, which may warrant future studies.

Furthermore, due to the lack of relevant data on the waste disposal processes and emission parameters, this study estimated the possible emissions from end-of-life products under different EF scenarios. The melamine emissions from the “Worst Emissions Scenario” were found to be much smaller than the emissions from the production of melamine and the production and use of downstream products. Even so, there are additional uncertainties in the estimated emissions from waste disposal processes, and therefore, this part of the emissions is not fully captured in the melamine flows in Figure 2.

Implications and Outlook

Although ubiquitous environmental contamination of PMT substances has been detected, the sources and emissions of most PMT substances remain largely unknown, especially for those with large production volumes. This study revealed the diverse emission sources, distributions, and dynamic emission inventory of melamine, a high-production-volume PMT substance of global concern. The results indicated that the mass production and extensive use of melamine caused significant and increasing emissions of this PMT substance to the environment, with more than half of the total emissions entering the water system. In-use products that contain high levels of melamine, such as surface decorative panels and molding compounds, could become important sources of emissions in the future. The continuous emission of melamine from waste disposal due to product scrapping may also have potential impacts on the environment and public health.

As numerous PMT substances are produced and used globally without restriction, the findings of this case study prompt urgent management action against them internationally. The EU has incorporated PMT/vPvM into its CLP regulation (classification, labeling, and packaging) as a new hazard category.104 Meanwhile, North America is strengthening the control of persistent and mobile PFAS substances in drinking water.105 China’s PMT/vPvM regulation is facing a critical opportunity window, with the scientific community urging the Chinese government to include PMT substances in the priority list of controlled toxic and hazardous chemicals.4,42

Building on existing screening and monitoring studies, source identification and emission estimation for broader PMT substances of concern should be undertaken. Consequently, comprehensive assessments of the environmental fate of and exposure to these substances are necessary, including but not limited to studies of traceability, multimedia environmental distribution and transformation, and transport of PMT substances on a larger scale. It is also time to consider the essential use of PMT substances and foster safer alternatives in various applications and industries. The assessment of potential alternatives to PMT substances in cosmetics has taken an important step forward,106 and other industries need to carry out relevant studies as soon as possible. Since most PMT substances, such as melamine, are discharged via STPs into water environments, effective removal technologies for them in STPs are crucial. Adsorption and advanced oxidation processes can remove some PMT substances, but their applicability to other pollutants needs further investigation.107−109 In the future, policies, codes, and technologies for environmentally sound management and disposal of melamine and other PMT-containing products and waste will be essential to control long-term environmental emission of these substances of very high concern.
