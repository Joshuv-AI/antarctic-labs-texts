An engineered hirudin-albumin prodrug enables thrombus-targeted long-acting anticoagulation with reduced bleeding risk
Authors: Zhiyou Wang, Zepeng Huang, Keyu Lv, Linan Lin, Yinping Hu, Yang Zhou, Cai Yuan, Chao Fang, Mingdong Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHirudin is a potent thrombin inhibitor limited by short circulation half-life and bleeding complications. We engineered an FXa-activatable hirudin thrombus-targeted prodrug, yet the N-terminal IEGR peptide introduced unwanted basal activity. After further screening, we found this residual function was fully silenced by a P-selectin-binding peptide upstream of IEGR, with no compromise to FXa-mediated cleavage. Our final construct PXHV2 incorporates both peptides, C-terminal human serum albumin to extend half-life, and an albumin-embedded cyclic RGD sequence for constitutive platelet recruitment. PXHV2 remains catalytically inert until FXa proteolysis restores thrombin-suppressive capacity. In murine electrical and laser-induced thrombosis models, PXHV2 delayed arterial and microvascular occlusion and maintained 120 min pre-injury antithrombotic protection, unlike rapidly ineffective free hirudin. PXHV2 elicited no increase in tail bleeding relative to saline controls. Our data identify PXHV2 as a long-lived, thrombus-selective prodrug with durable efficacy and minimal bleeding risk.Subject areas: drug delivery system, biological sciences, protein, bioengineering, biomaterials

Introduction

Thrombin, the pivotal end-product in the coagulation cascade, facilitates thrombus formation by converting fibrinogen to fibrin and activating platelets.1,2 However, excessive thrombin generation can lead to pathological clotting and serious conditions such as deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and stroke.3,4,5 For this reason, inhibiting thrombin remains a key therapeutic strategy for preventing thrombotic diseases.6,7

Hirudin is a potent and specific thrombin inhibitor from the medicinal leech (Hirudo medicinalis), and has long been recognized as a powerful anticoagulant.8,9 It potently shuts down the pro-coagulant activity of thrombin by irreversibly binding to both the active site and exosite 1 (fibrinogen-binding exosite) of the enzyme.10,11 However, native hirudin’s clinical use is hampered by two issues: short plasma half-life of only about an hour due to quick clearance, and significant risk of bleeding.12,13,14 As a result, native hirudin usually requires frequent dosing, which makes precise dose control a persistent challenge. Strategies such as PEGylation, human serum albumin (HSA) fusion, albumin-binding domains, and depot-forming elastin-like polypeptides have been developed to prolong exposure, but systemic thrombin inhibition still raises safety concerns.15,16,17,18,19

To overcome these limitations, we developed a hirudin prodrug that remains inert in circulation but is selectively activated at sites of pathological clotting.

Our approach is based on the proteolytic reaction by coagulation factor Xa (FXa), a key enzyme enriched in thrombi during coagulation.18,19,20,21 Fusion with an IEGR peptide, a sequence cleavable by FXa, at the N-terminal of hirudin masks the thrombin-inhibitory activity, while proteolytic cleavage of this peptide by FXa enables the localized release of functional hirudin. However, our initial constructs, with or without a C-terminal HSA fusion, unexpectedly retained significant residual thrombin-inhibitory activity. Further inclusion of a P-selectin-binding peptide (PBP), which targets the clot where extensive P-selectin exposure is present,22 before the FXa cleavable sequence, achieved complete masking of hirudin’s inhibitory activity to thrombin.

While effective in maintaining hirudin in a quiescent state, the PBP targeting motif would be excised upon FXa activation, abolishing its clot-targeting capability precisely when needed and therefore introducing a limitation. To create a construct that maintains the clot-targeting capability post-activation, we developed a strategy by inserting a cyclic RGD (cRGD) peptide, which binds to the activated epitope of integrin, into a surface-exposed loop of the HSA domain (residues 363–364). This insertion site was previously shown by us23 and others24 to accommodate functional motifs without disrupting albumin folding.23,24 The final construct, namely PXHV2, exhibited robust antithrombotic efficacy across large-vessel and microvascular thrombosis models. Unlike hirudin’s short-acting activity, the antithrombotic effect of PXHV2 was sustained for at least 2 h post-administration. Importantly, PXHV2 showed reduced bleeding risk compared to native hirudin. This work established a model for designing the next-generation anticoagulants, which are characterized by combined advantages of improved pharmacokinetics, precise spatial targeting, and reduced bleeding risks.

Results

The effects of different versions of hirudin fusion proteins on thrombin inhibitory activity

Hirudin is a bivalent inhibitor that potently inhibits thrombin by simultaneously binding its N-terminal domain to the active site and its C-terminal domain to exosite 1 of thrombin. Thus, we hypothesized that the fusion of additional amino acids at the N-terminal of hirudin could reduce its thrombin-inhibitory activity, generating an inactive prodrug of hirudin, and hopefully, increasing its safety profile. To test this hypothesis, we generated several recombinant hirudin variants using a mammalian expression system.

As anticipated, the TGHV construct, where HV denotes hirudin and TG denotes a dipeptide sequence Thr-Gly derived from an AgeI restriction site introduced during gene manipulation at the N-terminal of hirudin (Figure 1A), completely lost its ability to inhibit thrombin, as determined by a chromogenic substrate assay (Figure 1B). In contrast, the prodrug construct XHV, where X denotes an FXa-cleavable tetrapeptide sequence (Ile-Glu-Gly-Arg or IEGR) fused at the N-terminus, unexpectedly retained partial but significant thrombin inhibitory activity prior to activation (Figure 1C). A similar result was observed in a construct that additionally included a C-terminal HSA fusion (denoted as XHV1) intended to extend plasma half-life. Subsequent enzymatic cleavage of XHV and XHV1 by FXa generated the products (denoted as HV and XHV1a, respectively) that exhibited robust thrombin-inhibitory activity (Figure 1C), confirming the efficient release of the active hirudin core.

Together, these results indicated that, in spite of the reduced hirudin activity in the presence of the N-terminal insertion, the uncleaved forms of FXa-responsive prodrugs XHV and XHV1 are not completely inert. Comparison of the residual activity of XHV and XHV1 and complete inactivation of TGHV suggests that the inhibitory effect may not only depend on the presence of an N-terminal block but also on the specific amino acid sequence of the fused peptide.

Development of a hirudin prodrug with complete activity masking and a permanent targeting moiety

We next included PBP peptide (DAEWVDVS)22 upstream of the IEGR sequence, resulting in the fusion proteins PXHV and PXHV1 (Figure 2A), where P denotes the PBP moiety. Both PXHV and PXHV1 showed no thrombin-inhibitory activity in the absence of FXa activation (Figure 2B). Upon FXa activation (producing HV and PXHV1a), significant thrombin inhibition was observed (Figure 2B). We next evaluated the platelet-binding capacity of either PXHV1 or PXHV1a in vitro on activated platelets (Figure 2C). Quantitative analysis demonstrated significantly lower fluorescence intensity for FXa-activated PXHV1a compared to PXHV1 (p < 0.05), indicating that FXa-mediated activation attenuated PXHV1’s platelet-targeting capability. Although effective in masking thrombin-inhibitory activity, the PBP sequence, which was positioned upstream of the FXa cleavage site, was excised following proteolytic activation, thereby abolishing its thrombus-targeting functionality and rendering PXHV1 a potential limitation.

To improve the clot-targeting ability of the activated protein following FXa cleavage, we further incorporated a cRGD peptide. The cRGD sequence selectively binds to the activated epitope of integrin β3, which is highly expressed on activated platelets at clot sites. To facilitate the cyclic conformation of RGD, we inserted the peptide into a unique surface-protruding loop of HSA (between residues 363 and 364), a location we23 and others24 previously identified as permissive for functional motif integration without disrupting HSA folding or activity. Based on this finding, we engineered two variants by inserting cRGD at the 363–364 locus: PXHV2, in which cRGD was directly inserted (Figure 2A) to take advantage of its spatial proximity to stabilize the cyclic peptide conformation, and PXHV3, which included flanking G4S linkers surrounding the cRGD sequence (Figure 2A) to enhance ligand exposure and maximize integrin-binding efficiency.

Both cRGD-containing protein-XHV2 and PXHV3) retained susceptibility to FXa cleavage, as confirmed by SDS-PAGE analysis (Figure S1A). PXHV2 remained uncleaved in the presence of the FXa inhibitor rivaroxaban, as shown by SDS-PAGE analysis (Figure S1B). Importantly, such as PXHV1, neither protein exhibited thrombin-inhibitory activity prior to FXa activation, indicating effective N-terminal masking (Figure S2). Upon FXa-mediated cleavage (yielding PXHV2a and PXHV3a), both exhibited significant thrombin-inhibitory activities in chromogenic assays (Figure 2D). We then evaluated the platelet-binding capacity of these two cRGD-containing proteins in vitro using activated platelets (Figure 2E). Quantitative analysis revealed significantly higher fluorescence intensity for both RGD-containing constructs (PXHV2 and PXHV3) compared to the non-RGD-containing construct PXHV1 (p < 0.05), confirming the critical role of the RGD motif. Interestingly, PXHV2 (direct insertion) exhibited much stronger binding than PXHV3 (with flanking G4S linkers). This counterintuitive result suggested that the structural constraints introduced by the flexible linkers may compromise rather than enhance the interaction of RGD with integrin. Consequently, PXHV2 was selected for further investigation. PXHV2a was further examined for its specificity in binding to activated platelets. Upon the addition of the αIIbβ3 integrin antagonist tirofiban, PXHV2a showed a complete loss of binding to activated platelets as revealed by flow cytometric analysis (Figure 2F; Figure S3). To further verify these findings in a more physiologically relevant context, we assessed the pharmacological activity of PXHV2 and PXHV2a using the thrombin generation assay (TGA) in platelet-poor plasma (Figure 2G). PXHV2a significantly suppressed thrombin activity compared to the saline control (p < 0.05), whereas the unactivated PXHV2 showed no statistically significant effect. These TGA results demonstrate that FXa-mediated cleavage is essential for the thrombin-inhibitory function and confirm the functional inertness of the masked construct in plasma. In addition, we determined the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of PXHV2a against thrombin. The results (Figure S4) showed that the IC50 of PXHV2a for thrombin inhibition was 18.59 ± 5.11 nM, which was not significantly different from that of hirudin.

PXHV2 demonstrated potent anticoagulating activity in vitro

To evaluate the in vitro efficacy of our anticoagulants, we performed a thrombin-induced coagulation assay using murine whole blood, and quantified clot formation by measuring the wet weight of the resulting clots (Figures 3A and 3B). As expected, the TGHV construct, which lacks inhibitory activity, failed to prevent coagulation, resulting in a clot mass comparable to that of the saline control group (53.79 ± 0.61 mg). In stark contrast, both the FXa-sensitive prodrugs (PXHV2 and PXHV) and their pre-activated forms (PXHV2a and HV, respectively) demonstrated potent anticoagulant effects, as shown by the reduction of final clot mass to a similar extent (37.06 ± 1.52 mg; 40.45 ± 1.00 mg; 37.65 ± 0.64 mg; and 38.98 ± 1.27 mg, respectively). Notably, no statistically significant differences were detected between PXHV2 and PXHV2a or between PXHV and HV, indicating that the FXa-sensitive constructs released full thrombin-inhibitory activity in whole blood in spite of their N-terminal masking. The HV construct has a protein sequence identical to hirudin, and thus was used in the following experiments as a control.

PXHV2 exhibits potent in vivo antithrombotic efficacy in both arterial and microvascular thrombosis models

To establish the antithrombotic potency of PXHV2, we first employed the widely used ferric chloride (FeCl3)-induced thrombosis model. We began by assessing efficacy in a large-vessel, high-shear environment—the murine carotid artery—which mimics arterial thrombosis (Figures 4A–4C). As a critical control, a sham surgery group underwent identical surgical procedures without FeCl3 application, and no vessel occlusion was observed in this group throughout the monitoring period, indicating that the observed occlusion in treatment groups was specifically due to FeCl3-induced thrombosis rather than non-specific surgical effects. The hirudin protein (HV) prolonged the occlusion time (468.2 ± 33.4 s) by approximately 1.8-fold compared to the saline control (259.3 ± 21.0 s, p < 0.05) when the variants were administered through the tail vein at the dose of 0.143 μmol/kg. Notably, the protein PXHV2 demonstrated superior efficacy in prolonging occlusion time (611.3 ± 37.4 s) by approximately 1.3-fold compared to the HV (p < 0.05).

Having established efficacy in a major artery, we next sought to determine if this protection extended to the microvasculature, a distinct low-flow, low-shear hemodynamic environment. For this, we utilized a dorsal skin-fold chamber model with blood vessels 30–50 μm in diameter (Figures 4D–4F). In this model, treatment with the hirudin HV prolonged the FeCl3-induced occlusion time (1697 ± 39.3 s) by approximately 3.2-fold compared to the saline control (532.8 ± 80.7 s, p < 0.05), while PXHV2 showed markedly greater efficacy, completely preventing occlusion for the entire 1800 s observation period (p < 0.05 vs. HV).

PXHV2 demonstrates a sustained antithrombotic effect

Besides potency, a critical determinant of a drug’s clinical utility is its duration of action. To assess this key parameter and to ensure our findings were not model-dependent, we transitioned to a model with a different injury mechanism: electrical injury-induced carotid artery thrombosis. When the agents were administered through the tail vein 10 min before injury, HV and PXHV2 significantly prolonged the time (226.7 ± 10.6 s and 350.0 ± 22.7 s, respectively) to occlusion, by approximately 3.5- and 5.4-fold, respectively, compared to the saline control (65.0 ± 9.0 s; Figure 5A), confirming their potent immediate anticoagulant efficacy. However, when the agents were infused 2 h prior to vessel injury, the protective effect of HV was completely abolished, with occlusion times reverting to control levels (Figure 5B). In contrast, PXHV2 retained its full antithrombotic activity at the 2-h time point, maintaining a significantly prolonged occlusion time (305.8 ± 45.3 s) by approximately 4.6-fold compared to the saline control (p < 0.05). This prolonged duration of action is attributed to the extended plasma half-life conferred by the albumin fusion.

To provide direct visual and mechanistic confirmation of this sustained efficacy at the microvascular level, we next employed a laser-induced cremaster muscle injury model coupled with real-time intravital microscopy. This approach allowed us to directly visualize and quantify the dynamics of fibrin accumulation using a fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated anti-fibrin monoclonal antibody 59D8 (Figures 6A–6C). To evaluate the immediate effects, the agents were administered through the tail vein 10 min prior to laser-induced injury. In this cohort, saline-treated mice exhibited rapid fibrin generation (visualized in green), which began at 30 s following injury and plateaued at ∼90 s. In contrast, both PXHV2 and HV-treated mice showed a delayed onset of fibrin accumulation (starting at ∼60 s) and attenuated clot growth. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity and the area under the curve (AUC) confirmed that both agents significantly inhibited thrombus formation compared to saline. To evaluate the antithrombotic effect at later time points, we then repeated the experiment with the agents administered 120 min prior to vessel injury (Figures 6D–6F). In this cohort, mice treated with HV showed rapid and robust fibrin accumulation, similar to the saline group, indicating that the therapeutic effect of HV was lost at these time points. In contrast, fibrin formation in PXHV2-treated mice remained consistently suppressed throughout the observation window (Figures 6D and 6E). This sustained efficacy was confirmed by the quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity and AUC from multiple injury sites (Figure 6F). These results demonstrate that PXHV2 exhibits durable antithrombotic effects, as shown by reduced thrombus formation 120 min after infusion.

Pharmacokinetics and tissue distribution of PXHV2 in mice

We further evaluated the in vivo distribution, retention, and clearance kinetics of PXHV2 using live animal fluorescence imaging and plasma concentration measurements. We found that PXHV2 had high concentration from 10 min to 10 h, followed by a pronounced decrease after 24 h. Such overall kinetics was similar to the HV group but the concentration decrease was much slower (Figure 7). Plasma concentration-time profiles (Figure 7B) further demonstrated that PXHV2 exhibited a markedly prolonged circulation profile, with substantial signal remaining at 8 h, whereas HV was cleared much more rapidly following administration.

PXHV2 is associated with reduced bleeding risk compared to hirudin

To assess in vivo hemostatic functions, we measured tail bleeding time in mice and hemoglobin absorbance as a measurement of blood loss. As previously reported,25,26 hirudin markedly prolonged the bleeding time (1399.0 ± 142.0 s) by approximately 2.7-fold compared to saline-treated controls (519.8 ± 52.1 s, p < 0.05) (Figure 8A). In contrast, PXHV2 showed no difference compared to the saline control group (bleeding time of 507.3 ± 54.1 s). Similar results were obtained with hemoglobin absorbance measurements (Figure 8B). Both assays demonstrated that PXHV2 is associated with reduced risk of bleeding compared to hirudin.

Discussion

Hirudin is the most potent and very specific inhibitor of thrombin, a central enzyme in coagulation. Following the determination of its crystal structure in complex with thrombin,27 recombinant hirudin was developed and approved for clinical use in the late 1990s.28 However, its therapeutic potential has always been constrained by two major drawbacks: a very short plasma half-life requiring frequent administration, and a high risk of systemic bleeding. A notable early success in hirudin engineering was bivalirudin, a smaller (20 amino acid) synthetic peptide that acts as a reversible thrombin inhibitor with reduced bleeding risk, making it suitable for patients with heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). Nevertheless, such as native hirudin, it still possesses a short plasma half-life of only 25 min in patients with normal renal function.29 This persistent challenge of a short therapeutic window spurred extensive protein engineering efforts to create next-generation hirudin variants.

In this study, our protein engineering efforts revealed two key insights that represent significant advancements in the design of anticoagulant prodrugs. First, we demonstrated that the common strategy of N-terminal masking with only an FXa-cleavable linker is insufficient to completely abolish the potent inhibitory activity of hirudin. Complete shielding required the inclusion of an upstream PBP motif, highlighting a previously underappreciated complexity in achieving full bioactivity masking. We do not yet fully understand the structural basis for this incomplete masking by the IEGR sequence alone, despite our computational efforts using molecular dynamics. The observation that the N-terminal IEGR extension of hirudin does not completely abolish its thrombin-inhibitory potency is unexpected, at least at first glance. However, a closer look suggests that a residual level of inhibition is not entirely implausible. While isoleucine at the P1 position (I1) is an optimal residue for hirudin, glutamic acid at P2 (E2)—though far from ideal—does not entirely prevent binding to the catalytic center, as previously indicated.30 Secondly, and more importantly, we engineered a solution to the critical problem of activation-induced loss of targeting. By inserting the cRGD targeting motif directly into a surface-exposed loop of the HSA scaffold itself, we created a permanent, activation-resistant targeting mechanism. This ensures that even after FXa has cleaved the N-terminal mask, the activated hirudin remains physically tethered to the clot via the HSA-RGD module, maximizing local efficacy and minimizing systemic escape.

The bleeding time and total blood loss for PXHV2-treated animals were indistinguishable from those of saline controls (Figure 8), in contrast to the significant hemorrhage induced by the HV. This remarkable safety is the direct functional readout of our successful N-terminal masking strategy, confirming the prodrug remains fully inert in systemic circulation. In short-term evaluations (10 min post-administration), a time frame intentionally chosen to isolate the impact of targeting from the long-term benefits of half-life extension, PXHV2 showed superior antithrombotic efficacy to the non-targeted HV in the high-flow carotid artery thrombosis model (Figure 5A). At this early time point, where both reagents are still fully available in circulation, the immediate enhancement in potency may be attributed to our second key design principle: the permanent, activation-resistant cRGD targeting module, which effectively concentrates the reagents at the thrombotic site. Conversely, in the low-flow microvasculature models during this short-term evaluation, the non-targeted HV was itself as highly effective as PXHV2 (Figure 6C). This observation suggests that in low-shear environments, the non-targeted potent inhibitor can achieve sufficient local concentration and residence time to exert a strong effect.

Our PXHV2 uses HSA as an entity to extend its plasma half-life, and may have the advantage of low immunogenicity compared to, e.g., albumin-binding domain (ABD). HSA fusion technology has successfully transitioned to the clinic, with notable examples including Albiglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 fusion protein for type 2 diabetes, and a coagulation factor IX fusion for hemophilia B, which enable reduced dosing frequency and improved patient compliance. It should be pointed out that, although fusion with albumin significantly extended the half-life of our expressed hirudin variant, our PXHV2 showed a somewhat reduced thrombin-inhibitory activity. It is likely that the large size of albumin, when fused to the C-terminus of hirudin, may interfere with hirudin binding to the exosite of thrombin.

Limitations of the study

Firstly, the antithrombotic efficacy and bleeding safety of PXHV2 were evaluated mainly in acute mouse thrombosis models, which may not fully recapitulate the complexity of human thrombotic diseases or chronic anticoagulant therapy. Secondly, although PXHV2 showed prolonged circulation and sustained antithrombotic activity, more detailed pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, toxicology, and repeated-dose studies are needed before clinical translation. Thirdly, the structural basis by which the IEGR linker alone incompletely masks hirudin activity, as well as the potential influence of albumin fusion on thrombin binding, remains to be further defined. Finally, immunogenicity, manufacturability, and efficacy in larger animal models or disease-relevant settings were not assessed in this study.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants

Human venous blood samples were collected from healthy adult donors for platelet-rich plasma preparation, platelet binding assays, competitive inhibition assays, thrombin generation assays, and related ex vivo coagulation experiments. Blood was collected into sodium citrate anticoagulant tubes after donors had been informed of the study objectives and had provided written informed consent. Donors were screened based on self-reported medical history and were eligible only if they had no known hematological disorders and had not used anticoagulant or antiplatelet medications before blood collection. A total of six healthy adult donors aged 22–30 years were included, comprising three male and three female participants. Sex was recorded based on donor self-report at the time of blood collection. Gender identity, ancestry, race, and ethnicity were not collected or analyzed because the samples were de-identified and the study was not designed to assess population-associated effects.

Human samples were not assigned to clinical intervention groups. Instead, platelet-rich plasma, platelet-poor plasma, or isolated platelets prepared from donor blood were allocated ex vivo to the indicated experimental conditions, including saline, HV, PXHV1, PXHV1a, PXHV2, PXHV2a, PXHV3, PXHV3a, tirofiban, or other controls, as specified in the corresponding method sections. All human samples were de-identified before experimental use. All procedures involving human samples were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines for biomedical research involving human participants. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Fuzhou University under approval number K2025-03-045.

Sex-associated differences were not specifically evaluated because the human blood samples were used only for ex vivo platelet and coagulation assays, and the study was not designed or powered to evaluate sex-associated effects. Gender-associated effects could not be evaluated because gender identity was not collected. This should be considered a limitation of the study.

Mouse models

Male ICR mice (Mus musculus; outbred, wild-type background) were used for all in vivo thrombosis, pharmacokinetic, fluorescence imaging, and bleeding studies. Mice were 6-8 weeks old and weighed 18-22 g at the time of experiments. Animals were obtained from SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and acclimated for at least one week before experimentation. Mice were housed under specific-pathogen-free conditions at 22 ± 2°C, and 40%–70% relative humidity, with a 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle, and were provided standard chow and water ad libitum.

For in vivo studies, mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups, including saline, HV, PXHV2, and sham surgery groups where applicable, with n = 6 mice per group unless otherwise indicated. Male mice were selected to reduce experimental variability associated with the estrous cycle. Therefore, the influence of biological sex on pharmacokinetics, antithrombotic efficacy, and bleeding risk was not evaluated in this study which represents a limitation.

All animal procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals established by Fuzhou University and complied with relevant institutional and national regulations for animal research. The animal experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Fuzhou University under approval number 2019-SG-006.

Cell lines

HEK293F cells were used for recombinant protein expression. HEK293F cells are a suspension-adapted derivative of the human embryonic kidney HEK293 cell line and were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. The sex and genotype of the specific HEK293F derivative used in this study were not independently determined. HEK293F cells were maintained in OPM-293 CD05 medium at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and shaken at 125 rpm.

Cells were passaged according to standard mammalian cell culture procedures and were used during the logarithmic growth phase for transient transfection and recombinant protein production. Cell viability and density were monitored before transfection. HEK293F cells were not authenticated by STR profiling during this study; their identity was verified based on the documented cell source and expected suspension growth and recombinant protein expression characteristics. The cells were tested for mycoplasma contamination using a PCR-based mycoplasma detection assay before protein expression experiments, and the results were negative.

Microbial strains

Escherichia coli DH5α, a standard laboratory K-12-derived cloning strain, was maintained as an in-house laboratory stock and used for plasmid amplification and cloning. The strain genotype was not independently confirmed in this study beyond vendor strain identity. Transformed bacteria were cultured in LB medium containing ampicillin at 37°C. Recombinant colonies were screened by colony PCR, and final plasmid constructs were confirmed by DNA sequencing. Bacterial cultures were maintained according to standard microbiological procedures. No plants or primary cell cultures were used in this study.

Method details

Recombinant plasmid construction

All recombinant plasmids were constructed based on the pHLsec vector, which was first linearized by double enzymatic digestion with XhoI and AgeI. The corresponding gene fragments of interest were then inserted into the prepared vector backbone utilizing the Gibson assembly method. The required inserts were generated via either standard PCR or overlap extension PCR strategies as outlined in Figures S5–S7, using primers detailed in Table S1. Specifically, the XHV fragment was created by amplifying hirudin from the pHLsec-TGHV template to incorporate an N-terminal FXa cleavage site (IEGR). The XHV1 fragment was generated by fusing the XHV gene with the HSA gene, amplified from pPICZαA-HSA, through overlap extension PCR. Subsequently, a P-selectin-binding peptide (DAEWVDVS) was fused to the N-terminus of XHV and XHV1 to yield the PXHV and PXHV1 fragments, respectively. The PXHV2 and PXHV3 fragments were similarly generated via overlap extension PCR, using pHLsec-PXHV1 as the template, by inserting a cRGD peptide (CNARGDMHC)—either directly (for PXHV2) or flanked by flexible GGGGS linkers (for PXHV3)—between residues 363 and 364 of the HSA domain. Following transformation into E. coli DH5α and selection on ampicillin-containing media, all final constructs (pHLsec-XHV, pHLsec-PXHV, pHLsec-XHV1, pHLsec-PXHV1, pHLsec-PXHV2, and pHLsec-PXHV3) were initially screened by colony PCR and subsequently confirmed by DNA sequencing.

Recombinant proteins preparation

HEK 293F cells were cultured in OPM-293 CD05 medium at 37°C, oscillating (125 rpm) in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator. For transfection, the cells were divided into 3-4 × 106 cells/mL. Transfection was performed according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, PEI-Max transfection reagent and plasmid DNA were independently diluted to 1/20 of the total transfection volume. Typically, the amount of DNA transfected was 1 μg DNA per 1 mL of culture, and a 5:1 PEI-Max to DNA ratio (w/w) was used. The PEI-Max and DNA mixtures were gently mixed and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. The PEI-Max DNA mixture was then gradually added to the cells. 5% (v/v) OPM-293 Profeed was added 24 hours after transfection and every 48 hours. The cells were harvested when cell viability was below 60% which typically takes five to seven days. The cell culture supernatant was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 20 min and filtered using a 0.45 μm vacuum filter membrane. The filtered supernatant was thoroughly mixed with a Ni Sepharose Excel (GE Healthcare) column and equilibrated with 20 mM Tris-HCl, 20 mM NaCl, pH 7.4. The column was then washed with equilibration buffer containing 30 mM imidazole, and the target protein was eluted with 300 mM imidazole in the same buffer. The eluates containing the protein of interest were pooled and further purified by anion-exchange chromatography on a Resource 15Q column. The final purified protein was buffer-exchanged by dialysis against 20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4, and stored for subsequent experiments.

FXa-mediated activation and thrombin inhibition analysis of the fusion protein

The enzymatic cleavage of the fusion proteins by FXa was performed according to a previously established procedure.18 Briefly, the protein substrate (PXHV2 or PXHV3) was dissolved in cleavage buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM CaCl2, pH 8.0) to a final concentration of 2.5 μM. The reaction was initiated by adding FXa to a final concentration of 0.04 μg/μL within a total reaction volume of 100 μL. The mixture was then incubated at 25°C for 180 minutes. Cleavage specificity and completion were verified by analyzing the reaction products using SDS-PAGE.

Following cleavage, residual FXa was removed from the reaction mixture using a heparin affinity column to yield the activated proteins, designated PXHV2a and HV. To assess the thrombin inhibitory activity of these activated constructs, a chromogenic assay was performed. PXHV2a or HV was diluted to a final concentration of 1.8 μM in assay buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% BSA, pH 8.0) and mixed with thrombin (final concentration: 0.1 U/mL). The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 5 minutes. The reaction was initiated by the addition of 40 μL of the chromogenic substrate S-2238 to a final concentration of 125 μM. The rate of substrate cleavage was determined by monitoring the change in absorbance at 405 nm over 30 minutes at 25°C using a microplate reader (SpectraMax i3x, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). The absorbance at 405 nm (OD405) over time was exported. Data points from the linear phase (600 seconds) of the reaction were selected. Time (seconds) was plotted on the x-axis and OD405 on the y-axis. Linear regression fitting was performed, and the slope obtained represents the initial reaction rate V0 for that well (unit: ΔOD405/sec). The V0 was calculated for all sample wells and saline control wells. Thrombin Relative Activity (%) = (V0 of sample well/V0 of saline control well) ×100%.

Determination of IC50 for thrombin inhibition by the fusion proteins

The protein samples (HV2 and PXHV2a) were subjected to five-fold serial dilution in cleavage buffer, generating a series of eight concentration gradients. The experimental procedure for assessing the inhibitory activity of HV2 and PXHV2a against thrombin followed the methods detailed in the section “FXa-Mediated Activation and Thrombin Inhibition Analysis of the Fusion Proteins.” Relative Inhibitory Activity (%)=[1–(V0 of sample well/V0 of saline control well)] × 100%.

Rivaroxaban inhibition assay for FXa-Mediated proteolysis

Rivaroxaban is a highly selective, oral anticoagulant that directly targets the active site of FXa. In this assay, it was introduced into the proteolytic reaction system comprising FXa and the prodrug (PXHV2) to verify the FXa dependency of the activation mechanism. By competitively inhibiting the catalytic activity of FXa, rivaroxaban was expected to block the cleavage of the IEGR sequence at the N-terminus of PXHV2.

A stock solution of rivaroxaban (1 mM) was prepared in DMSO. The stock was further diluted with reaction buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM CaCl2, pH 8.0) to obtain a 100 μM working solution. For each reaction, 1 μL of the 100 μM rivaroxaban working solution was first added to the reaction tube. This was followed by the addition of other components, including the protein substrate (PXHV2 or HV, final concentration 2.5 μM) and cleavage buffer. The mixture was pre-incubated at 25°C for 5 minutes to allow the inhibitor to interact with FXa. The reaction was then initiated by adding FXa to a final concentration of 0.04 μg/μL, resulting in a total reaction volume of 100 μL. A solvent control was established in parallel by adding an equivalent volume of pure cleavage buffer instead of the inhibitor working solution, to observe the normal cleavage efficiency in the absence of inhibition. The complete reaction mixture was incubated at 25°C for 180 minutes. Following incubation, the reaction products were analyzed by SDS-PAGE to verify the specificity and completeness of the cleavage.

Platelet Adhesion Assay of the fusion proteins

FITC-labelling of the fusion proteins: The fusion protein, at a concentration of 1 mg/mL in a 1 mL volume, was first dialyzed against a labeling buffer (90 mM NaHCO3, 10 mM Na2CO3, 126 mM NaCl, pH 9.0). This dialysis step was repeated three times, with each round lasting 6 hours, to effectively remove any residual Tris-HCl buffer prior to the labeling reaction. A stock solution of fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) was prepared by dissolving it in DMSO to a final concentration of 1 mg/mL and was subsequently stored in the dark at 4°C. The dialyzed protein solution was then incubated with the FITC stock solution at a ratio of 25 μL of FITC per 1 mg of protein. The FITC solution was added dropwise while stirring continuously, and the conjugation reaction was allowed to proceed for 8 hours at 4°C in the dark. To quench the reaction, NH4Cl was added to a final concentration of 50 mM, followed by a 2-hour incubation at 4°C. Excess, unconjugated FITC was removed using a G25 desalting column. The purified, FITC-labeled fusion proteins were then stored at -80°C and protected from light until further use.

Platelet Binding Assay: The binding affinity of the fusion protein to activated platelets was assessed using an assay adapted from a previously reported procedure.32 Specifically, a black 96-well plate was coated by adding 50 μL of either type II collagen (1 mg/mL) or HSA to each well, followed by an overnight incubation at 37°C. The following day, the coating solution was discarded, and the wells were washed with PBST to remove any unbound protein. Next, 50 μL of human platelet-rich plasma, pre-activated with 10 μM ADP, was added to each well. The plate was then incubated for 120 minutes at 37°C to facilitate the adhesion of activated platelets to the immobilized collagen or HSA. Following this incubation, non-adherent platelets were removed by washing the wells with PBS. Subsequently, 100 μL of the FITC-labeled fusion protein (200 μg/mL, equivalent to 2.5 μM) was added to each well, and the plate was incubated for an additional 120 minutes at 37°C in the dark. The fluorescence intensity in each well was then quantified using a microplate reader, with excitation and emission wavelengths set to 488 nm and 520 nm, respectively. All assays were performed in triplicate.

Competitive inhibition of cRGD-Mediated platelet binding by tirofiban

To verify whether the cRGD motif serves as the critical functional element for platelet targeting in PXHV2a, we conducted an in vitro platelet binding assay. This assay aimed to assess if tirofiban, a high-selectivity, reversible αIIbβ3 integrin antagonist, could competitively inhibit the specific binding of PXHV2a fusion protein to activated platelets. Tirofiban specifically binds to the αIIbβ3 receptor on activated platelets. In this experimental setup, it competes with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled PXHV2a for the cRGD-binding site on αIIbβ3. By measuring the fluorescence intensity of platelet-bound PXHV2a in the presence of tirofiban, we evaluated the specificity of this interaction for the cRGD-αIIbβ3 pathway.

For platelet preparation, venous blood was collected in sodium citrate anticoagulant tubes. To obtain platelet-rich plasma (PRP), the blood was centrifuged at 150 × g for 10 minutes. It is important to note that platelets were freshly prepared from PRP to ensure optimal activity; platelets isolated from PRP that had been stored frozen exhibited reduced viability.

Next, 8 mL of PRP was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 minutes to obtain a platelet pellet. The pellet was washed twice using a platelet washing buffer (pH 6.5) and finally resuspended in a balanced salt solution (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM CaCl2) to prepare a 4 mL platelet suspension. During preparation, the suspension was passed through a 300-mesh sieve to remove platelet aggregates.

The resuspended platelets were randomly aliquoted into four tubes (1 mL per tube). Tirofiban working solution was added to one tube to achieve a final concentration of 100 μM, followed by a 5-minute pre-incubation at room temperature. All tubes were supplemented with ADP to a final concentration of 10 μM to activate platelets. The mixtures were gently mixed and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. The corresponding FITC-labeled protein working solution was added to each tube to a final concentration of 1 μM. After thorough mixing, the tubes were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 minutes. Samples were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 minutes. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was washed once with PBST, then resuspended in 1 mL PBS for immediate analysis by flow cytometry.

Thrombin generation assay

The potential inhibitory effect of the test proteins on thrombin generation was evaluated using a chromogenic substrate assay. First, human platelet-poor plasma was diluted to a final concentration of 30% (v/v) using a Tris-HCl buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, 5% glycerol, pH 7.4). This diluted plasma was then pre-incubated for 30 minutes at 37°C with gentle rotation in the presence of either PXHV2, PXHV, PXHV2a, or HV at a final protein concentration of 1.8 μM. Thrombin generation was subsequently initiated by the addition of 5 mM Gly-Pro-Arg-Pro (GPRP) peptide and 6 mM CaCl2. To measure thrombin activity, a 20 μL aliquot of the reaction mixture was quenched by diluting it to a final volume of 100 μL with PBS containing 12.5 mM EDTA. This stop solution also contained 100 μM of the chromogenic thrombin substrate S-2238. The rate of substrate cleavage was monitored kinetically by measuring the change in optical absorbance at 405 nm for 30 minutes at 37°C using a SpectraMax i3x microplate reader. Initial reaction velocities (V0) were determined by applying a linear regression to the absorbance data collected during the first 10 minutes of the reaction.

In vitro anticoagulant activity assay

The anticoagulant efficacy of the engineered proteins was assessed using an in vitro clot formation assay. Murine whole blood was pipetted into a 96-well plate (145 μL per well). The specified anticoagulant proteins (PXHV2, PXHV, PXHV2a, HV, or TGHV) were then added to a final concentration of 2.5 μM. Clotting was initiated by the sequential addition of thrombin (to a final concentration of 3 U/mL) and CaCl2 (to a final concentration of 6 mM). The total reaction volume was adjusted to 200 μL using saline. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 3 hours to allow for complete clot formation. Following incubation, non-clotted components were carefully aspirated, and the wet weight of the resulting clots was measured to quantify the extent of coagulation.

Ferric chloride (FeCl3)-Induced carotid artery thrombosis model

The antithrombotic efficacy of the engineered proteins was first evaluated in a ferric chloride-induced arterial injury model. Male ICR mice (18-22 g) were randomly assigned to three treatment groups and a sham surgery control group (n = 6 per group). Animals received a single intravenous injection via the tail vein with either saline (vehicle control), HV (0.143 μmol/kg), or PXHV2 (0.143 μmol/kg). The sham surgery control group received vehicle injection and underwent identical surgical exposure of the carotid artery without the application of FeCl3 for injury induction. At 10 minutes post-injection, mice were anesthetized with tribromoethanol. The common carotid artery was surgically exposed, and vascular injury was induced by the topical application of a filter paper saturated with 30 μL of 20% FeCl3 solution. Immediately following injury induction, blood flow in the affected artery was monitored continuously using a Laser Speckle Contrast Imaging System. The percent occlusion was recorded every 5 seconds, and the time to occlusion (defined as the time to reach a 95% occlusive rate) was determined as the primary efficacy endpoint.

Ferric chloride (FeCl3)-Induced microvascular thrombosis model

To investigate the compound effects on microcirculation, a dorsal skinfold chamber model was utilized. Following the same animal grouping and dosing protocol described above, mice were anesthetized 10 minutes post-injection. A titanium dorsal skinfold chamber was surgically implanted to isolate a section of skin. The dermal and subcutaneous tissue on one side within the chamber's circular window was carefully removed to expose the microvasculature. Thrombosis was induced by applying 1 μL of a 35% FeCl3 solution directly onto a selected arteriole (40–60 μm in diameter). Blood flow within the vessel was subsequently monitored for 30 minutes using laser speckle contrast imaging, with readings recorded every 5 seconds to assess thrombus formation and vessel patency.

Direct current-induced carotid artery thrombosis model

A direct current-induced injury model was used as an alternative method to assess in vivo antithrombotic efficacy. Male ICR mice (18–22 g) were randomized into the same three treatment groups (saline, HV, or PXHV2; n = 6 per group) and administered the compounds intravenously. To evaluate both immediate and sustained effects, mice were anesthetized at either 10 minutes or 120 minutes post-administration. The common carotid artery was surgically exposed, and thrombosis was initiated by applying a 0.5 mA direct current to the vessel wall. Blood flow was monitored continuously using a YLS-14B thrombosis monitoring instrument. The percent occlusion of the artery was recorded every 5 seconds, and the time to occlusion (95% blockage) was recorded as the primary endpoint.

Intravital microscopy of laser-induced injury thrombosis

The in vivo efficacy of the engineered proteins was further assessed using a laser-induced arteriole thrombosis model in the mouse cremaster vasculature, as previously described.33,34,35 Briefly, mice were anesthetized, and the cremaster muscle was surgically exteriorized and positioned on the stage of an Olympus BX51WI microscope. The exposed tissue was kept moist and physiologically stable by continuous immersion in bicarbonate-buffered saline (37°C, pH 7.4). A cannula was placed in the jugular vein for intravenous administration of antibodies and test agents. To visualize thrombus formation, a FITC-conjugated anti-fibrin monoclonal antibody (59D8; 1 mg/kg) was injected via the jugular cannula. Vascular injury was then induced by nitrogen laser pulses at the endothelial wall of the selected arteriole.

To evaluate immediate antithrombotic effects, animals received an intravenous injection of either saline (vehicle control), HV (0.143 μmol/kg), or PXHV2 (0.143 μmol/kg) 10 min prior to laser injury. To assess sustained effects, a separate cohort of mice was treated by intravenous injection with the same agents via the tail vein 120 minutes before the procedure. Fluorescent images of fibrin accumulation at the injury site were captured using a high-speed camera. Image analysis comprised of data points of ≥26 thrombi from 3–4 mice per experiment group using SlideBook 6.0 software. The total amount of fibrin deposition was quantified by calculating the area under the curve (AUC) of the median fluorescence intensity over the course of thrombus formation.

Cy5-labeling and fluorescence pharmacokinetics of fusion proteins in vivo

The fusion proteins PXHV2 and HV were covalently labeled with the near-infrared fluorophore Cy5 following the protocol detailed in the section “Platelet Adhesion Assay of the Fusion Proteins.” Healthy male ICR mice (6–8 weeks old) were randomly allocated into three experimental cohorts (n = 6 per group). Mice received a single tail vein injection of either Cy5-labeled PXHV2, Cy5-labeled HV, or sterile saline (vehicle control) at an equimolar dose of 0.143 μmol/kg.

Serial blood samples (∼30 μL) were obtained via tail vein puncture at predetermined time points after injection: 2, 10, and 30 minutes, and 1, 5, 10, and 24 hours. Each sample was collected into anticoagulant tubes and promptly centrifuged (1000 × g, 10 minutes, 4 °C) to obtain plasma. Subsequently, 5 μL of plasma was mixed thoroughly with 95 μL of 1% (w/v) sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) solution to denature proteins and dissociate non-specifically bound fluorophore.

Fluorescence intensity of the processed samples was measured using a multimode microplate reader. Cy5 was detected with excitation/emission wavelengths set to 649/670 nm. Parallel measurement of plasma from saline-injected mice served as a background control, and its signal was subtracted from sample readings.

Imaging of mice treated with Cy5-Labeled PXHV2 in vivo

The pharmacokinetic examination of PXHV2 was investigated via in vivo imaging. Male ICR mice (18–22 g) were randomized into three treatment groups (n = 6 per group) and received a single intravenous tail vein injection of either saline (vehicle control), Cy5-labeled PXHV2 (0.143 μmol/kg), or Cy5-labeled HV (0.143 μmol/kg). Fluorescence images were acquired at 10 minutes, 30 minutes, 1 hour, 5 hours, 10 hours, 24 hours and 48 hours post-injection using an IVIS® Spectrum In Vivo Imaging System (PerkinElmer, MA, USA). All images were normalized and analyzed with Living Image 4.7.2 software.

Mouse tail transection bleeding assay

The effect of the engineered proteins on hemostasis was evaluated using a tail transection bleeding model, performed as previously described.25,26 Male ICR mice (18–22 g) were randomized into three treatment groups (n = 6 per group) and received a single intravenous tail vein injection of either saline (vehicle control), PXHV2 (0.143 μmol/kg), or HV (0.143 μmol/kg).

Ten minutes post-injection, the animals were anesthetized. A 10-mm segment of the distal tail was transected with a scalpel, and the tail was immediately immersed in 10 mL of pre-warmed saline (37°C) contained within a 15-mL conical tube. The tail was positioned such that the transected tip was approximately 2–3 cm below the body's horizontal plane. The cessation of bleeding was defined as the absence of blood flow for a continuous 30-second period.

Total blood loss was quantified by measuring hemoglobin content. The saline containing the shed blood was centrifuged (3,000 rpm for 10 min), and the resulting erythrocyte pellet was resuspended in 2 mL of erythrocyte lysis buffer (155.2 mM NH4Cl, 10.0 mM KHCO3, 126.6 μM EDTA). Following a 10 minutes incubation at room temperature to ensure complete lysis, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 575 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax i3x, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± SEM unless otherwise stated. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8. The statistical test used, exact n values, definition of n, and significance levels are indicated in the corresponding figure legends and/or STAR Methods sections. For in vitro assays, n represents independent experiments unless otherwise specified; experiments performed in technical triplicate are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. For animal experiments, n represents the number of mice per group. For laser-induced thrombosis experiments, n represents the number of mice per group, and at least 26 thrombi were analyzed per group as described in the STAR Methods.

For comparisons between two groups, two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed data. For comparisons among three or more groups, ordinary one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used when data met parametric assumptions. For non-parametric comparisons among three or more groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical significance is indicated in the figures and figure legends using the following notation unless otherwise stated: ns, not significant; ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
