Integrated relative humidity as a layer-resolved indicator for moisture-precipitation interactions
Authors: Moufeng Wan, Hui Su, Chengxing Zhai, Mengxi Wu, Pak Wai Chan, Shuangchen Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe introduce Integrated Relative Humidity (IRH), a layer-resolved diagnostic of tropospheric saturation, to investigate moisture-precipitation interactions in the subtropical coastal environment (Hong Kong). Using 16 years (2005–2020) of co-located radiosonde and rain gauge data, we classify observations into four precipitation lifecycle stages: No Rain (NR), Before Rain (BR), During Rain (DR), and After Rain (AR). IRH is computed from the surface to nine standard pressure levels (1000–150 hPa), enabling layer-specific saturation diagnostics across these stages. IRH probability density functions (PDFs) reveal stage-specific vertical structures with saturation peaks consistently near 850 hPa. BR shows increasing lower-tropospheric saturation, DR exhibits the highest deep-column saturation, and AR displays reduced mid-level saturation, reflecting dynamic redistribution across the precipitation lifecycle. Bimodal PDF structures in BR, DR, and AR stages are consistent with transitions between shallow and deep convective regimes. Reversed cumulative distribution function (RCDF) for IRH = 0.90 captures low/mid-level buildup in BR and DR, while RCDF for IRH = 0.80 highlights upper-level dissipation in AR. Crucially, IRH thresholds of 0.82 at 700 hPa and 0.70 at 150 hPa correspond to 84% and 82% of DR events, respectively, while both exclude 76% of NR cases. Furthermore, the threshold of 0.70 at 150 hPa is also exceeded in 72% of BR events with the same NR exclusion. These findings demonstrate IRH as a layer-resolved metric for moisture-precipitation interactions, providing a quantitative diagnostic framework that may inform the development of nowcasting tools and aid in model evaluation in subtropical environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47087-4.Keywords: Tropospheric Moisture, Integrated Relative Humidity, Moist Convection, Precipitation, Subtropical Climate, Rainfall NowcastingSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Hydrology

Introduction

Tropospheric moisture and precipitation are closely linked through thermodynamic-dynamical interactions, forming a coupled system throughout precipitation events’ lifecycle. Moisture fuels convection through latent heat release, while convection redistributes moisture and drives precipitation formation, development, and dissipation1–4. This moisture-convection interplay initiates, sustains, or terminates precipitation, including shallow convection moistening the lower troposphere, deep convection moistening the entire column, and stratiform precipitation drying the upper troposphere5–8.

Convective activity and precipitation are closely related to the degree of tropospheric saturation, i.e., the saturation deficit9, which is determined by the profiles of moisture content and temperature. Tropospheric saturation can be synthesized into column relative humidity (CRH) or column saturation fraction (CSF)10,11. Statistical mean precipitation exhibits an exponential relationship with CRH, with a sharp increase after a critical CRH threshold ~ 0.7–0.810,12–14. This exponential behavior is consistent with critical transition theory, where small perturbations in column water vapor (CWV) can lead to abrupt shifts in convective activity15, critical for predicting extreme events under climate change16. The nonlinear relationship between precipitation and CWV has long been recognized15,17, with normalization by a critical threshold revealing universal patterns18. Furthermore, a critical normalized precipitable water threshold distinguishes local evaporation from large-scale moisture convergence regimes in climate models19. The evolution of tropical convective systems is closely associated with large-scale CRH variations, with a nonlinear precipitation pickup above a CRH threshold of ~ 0.7 driven primarily by stratiform area expansion rather than intensified convective rain rates11. Wolding et al. (2020) formalized a cyclic moisture-precipitation lifecycle—shallow convection moistens the column, deep convection triggers intense rainfall, and stratiform processes then dry the column20. While Chen et al. (2022) confirmed that distinct convective modes dominate at low, intermediate, and high CSF levels over tropical oceans7.

The moisture-precipitation relationship is governed by layer-specific humidity dynamics, with mid- and lower-tropospheric moisture driving convective initiation and intensity via entrainment, stability, and radiative-dynamical feedback21–25. However, CRH—as a bulk metric that condenses the entire tropospheric moisture profile into a single value—obscures the vertical gradients critical for distinguishing precipitation phases and convective types (shallow, deep, stratiform) in complex settings like subtropical, mid-latitude, or coastal areas21,26–28. Studies show that mid-tropospheric humidity and integrated buoyancy across the boundary layer, lower, and mid-troposphere exert strong control over deep convection22,24, including the role of deep-inflow mixing in convective organization and transition23. While the sharp precipitation increase above CRH thresholds (~ 0.7–0.8) is attributed to boundary-layer moisture exceeding stability thresholds, modulated by free-tropospheric humidity29,30. Even at high CRH, rain rates vary widely due to thermodynamic controls like 900–700 hPa lapse rates25, with lower-tropospheric moistening often preceding convection and upper-tropospheric moistening following it, embedded in large-scale wave disturbances31,32. Region-specific challenges—including coastal rainfall27, the Madden-Julian Oscillation26, South Asian monsoon biases33, and fundamental differences in convective dynamics over land versus ocean34—highlight the limitations of CRH and necessitate advanced layer-specific diagnostics to capture vertical moisture structures and improve climate model representations of convective transitions35–37.

This study introduces Integrated Relative Humidity (IRH), a novel, vertically resolved metric to overcome the limitations of bulk metrics. While discrete, layer-averaged humidity is a valuable tool for isolating specific vertical segments, IRH captures a fundamentally different physical concept, representing the cumulative moisture environment from the surface upward to a given pressure level. We propose that this integrated measure is more physically meaningful for understanding convective development because it quantifies the total moisture burden that an ascending air parcel must overcome. A deep, moist layer (high IRH) suggests that a rising parcel will remain buoyant and is less likely to be inhibited by dry air entrainment. In this sense, IRH serves as a measure of the pre-conditioned state of the troposphere, indicating whether the environment is favorable for deep convection throughout the entire column a developing cloud must penetrate—not just within a single, isolated layer. Using 16 years of radiosonde and rain gauge data from a subtropical coastal environment, we apply IRH to four distinct precipitation lifecycle stages—No Rain (NR), Before Rain (BR), During Rain (DR), and After Rain (AR)—to characterize how lower- and mid-tropospheric saturation evolve with rainfall onset, intensity, and dissipation21,22,27,38,39. By examining probability density functions (PDFs) and reversed cumulative distribution functions (RCDFs) of IRH at nine vertical levels, we identify layer-specific diagnostic patterns and thresholds that distinguish convective phases and moisture transitions40. This approach offers a physically grounded, quantitative framework for diagnosing moisture-precipitation interactions with greater vertical detail than previously possible with bulk metrics. The IRH framework holds potential for improving the representation of convective processes in climate models under a warming climate41,42.

This paper is organized as follows: Sect. 2 describes the data and methodology for calculating IRH and categorizing precipitation stages. Sect. 3 presents the results of our layer-resolved analysis, and Sect. 4 discusses the conclusions and implications for forecasting and modeling.

Data and methodology

Data

As a case study, we focus on Hong Kong—a subtropical coastal environment characterized by persistently high humidity, a distinct wet season driven by the East Asian monsoon, and occasional typhoon activity—to evaluate IRH’s ability to characterize moisture–precipitation interactions. This climatological setting highlights the importance of layer-resolved moisture diagnostics for understanding precipitation lifecycle transitions and vertical saturation dynamics.

Two observational datasets were utilized: sounding data from the Integrated Global Radiosonde Archive (IGRA) and local rain gauge measurements from the Hong Kong Observatory. Together, these datasets span 16 years from 2005 to 2020, providing essential information for analyzing moisture structure and precipitation behavior. The IGRA dataset provides detailed meteorological profiles, including temperature, vapor pressure, saturation vapor pressure, winds, etc., collected twice daily at 00 and 12 UTC from the King’s Park radiosonde station in Kowloon, Hong Kong. Profiles were quality‑controlled using IGRA flags. Soundings with missing humidity in any target layer were excluded from layer‑specific IRH statistics. These high-resolution vertical soundings capture diurnal variations and are essential for assessing moisture distribution throughout the troposphere. Complementing the radiosonde data, hourly rain gauge measurements are collected from the station collocated with the radiosonde site, ensuring spatial consistency. Precipitating hours were defined as ≥ 0.1 mm h⁻¹—selected to include light rain and ensure comprehensive classification of precipitation occurrences.

Precipitation lifecycle categories

To investigate tropospheric moisture-precipitation interactions, the datasets were categorized into four scenarios based on a 12-hour window, which was chosen to represent the typical duration of mesoscale convective systems (MCS) in the region7,43,44. The 12-hour window is centered on the radiosonde sounding time, spanning from 6 h before to 6 h after the 00/12 UTC observation. These scenarios represent distinct stages in the precipitation lifecycle: NR, no precipitation throughout the entire 12-hour window; BR, no precipitation before and at the sounding, but precipitation occurs afterward; DR, no precipitation before the sounding, but precipitation occurs at the sounding time, regardless of whether it continues afterward; AR, precipitation occurs before the sounding, with no rain at and after the sounding. This categorization enables a systematic analysis of tropospheric moisture conditions relative to the timing of precipitation events.

By resolving the vertical structure of the saturation level, IRH provides a more detailed diagnosis of moisture-precipitation interactions than CRH alone.

Results

IRH distributions across NR, BR, DR, and AR stages were analyzed using kernel density estimation (KDE) to derive PDFs and CDFs (RCDFs = 1-CDF). PDFs were estimated via Gaussian KDE with bandwidth = 0.025; sensitivity checked 0.015–0.03. These statistics were compared quantitatively to identify discrepancies in moisture levels, determine critical thresholds, and investigate the underlying physical mechanisms in precipitation regimes. It should be noted that IRH thresholds reported hereafter are derived from a 16-year climatological dataset and may carry inherent sampling uncertainty. Future studies with longer or independent records could help assess their robustness.

Layer-specific IRH PDFs and convective transitions

IRH PDFs show pronounced vertical moisture contrasts below 500 hPa, with similar distributions above 400 hPa (peak differences less than 0.05). For each stage, the mean rain rate was computed as the total precipitation in the 12-h window divided by the number of precipitating hours within it. This binning is employed to quantify the IRH-rain rate relationships.

For all layers, BR, DR, and AR PDFs are right-skewed with higher saturation than NR, peaking in DR. NR PDFs are unimodal throughout all layers. In contrast, BR, DR, and AR shift between unimodal and bimodal shapes by layer, indicating layer-specific IRH tied to precipitation evolution.

At 1000 hPa, BR and AR PDFs are unimodal, while DR exhibits a clear bimodal structure with a lower peak (~ 0.85 IRH) associated with ~ 0.93 mm h⁻¹ and a higher peak (~ 0.92 IRH) associated with ~ 1.74 mm h⁻¹. The lower peak aligns with shallow/stratiform rain from low‑topped convective clouds or decaying convective systems with stratiform characteristics45,46, while the higher peak indicates deep convection. The transition from unimodal (BR) to bimodal (DR) IRH distributions highlights the sensitivity of IRH to convective initiation and depth. AR shows stronger right-skewness than BR, suggesting enhanced low-level moisture replenishment by surface evaporation from wet surfaces following precipitation47–49. This sustains near‑surface humidity in AR, potentially influencing the timing and intensity of subsequent convective development.

At 850–700 hPa, the distributions for all rain-related stages present to unimodal forms and concentrated at high IRH values, indicating a consistently sustained moisture accumulation and vertical redistribution. Above 700 hPa (500–150 hPa), bimodality reappears in all rain-related stages. For example, at 400 hPa, the PDFs for BR, DR, and AR are with dual peaks of 0.78/0.84, 0.76/0.88, and 0.78/0.86. Lower peaks align with shallow/stratiform convection (~ 0.64 mm h⁻¹ for BR, ~ 0.53 mm h⁻¹ for DR), while higher peaks reflect deep convective development and upper-tropospheric penetration (~ 1.22 mm h⁻¹ for BR, ~ 1.16 mm h⁻¹ for DR). AR exhibits subtler bimodality with flatter and broader PDFs, indicating more diffuse humidity and reduced upper-level saturation post-rain. Non-overlapping BR–AR PDFs, with AR consistently lower than BR across the IRH range of 0.77 to 0.88, reflect moistened pre‑convective upper‑tropospheric conditions of BR to the drier post-rain environment of AR. Notably, the mean rain rate of ~ 0.79 mm h⁻¹ associated with AR in this range signals upper-level desaturation following intense precipitation.

Overall, the BR-to-DR shift in PDF structures, along with dual peaks indicating shallow-deep convective coexistence, highlights IRH at 500/400 hPa as a sensitive indicator of convective development, depth, and maturity. The unimodal-to-bimodal transition from 700 hPa to 500/400 hPa demonstrates the diagnostic power of mid-to-upper-tropospheric IRH in distinguishing convective regimes.

RCDF values are summarized in Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Fig. S1. IRH RCDFs (1-CDF) for thresholds of 0.70, 0.80, and 0.90 (denoted as RCDF70, RCDF80, and RCDF90) for NR, BR, DR, and AR from 1000–150 hPa during the 2005–2020 period. The 0.70 and 0.80 thresholds are applied to all layers, the 0.90 threshold is applied to the layers below 500 hPa, and the 0.82 threshold is applied to 700 hPa.

From 1000 to 850 hPa, NR RCDFs increase modestly (e.g., RCDF80 + 0.14, RCDF90 + 0.08 to 925 hPa), then stabilize or dip slightly (RCDF80 − 0.03 to 850 hPa), suggesting a subtle shift toward drier conditions in the 0.80–0.90 IRH range. Other stages exhibit consistent RCDF increases, strongest in RCDF90 for DR (+ 0.25 total), then BR/AR (+ 0.29/+0.16). AR exceeds BR at low levels (e.g., RCDF90 at 1000 hPa + 0.10), consistent with post-rain enhanced saturation in Sect. 3.2.

From 850 to 500 hPa, NR declines sharply (RCDF80 − 0.40 total; RCDF90 − 0.15), BR/AR decline moderately (RCDF90 − 0.36/–0.33), DR peaks in RCDF70 (~ 0.99 at 850/700 hPa) before declining (RCDF90 − 0.36). These stratified saturation patterns underscore the diagnostic importance of the 700/500 hPa layers as transitional zones for mid-level humidity modulation. From 500 to 150 hPa, NR remains driest, while BR, DR, and AR exhibit steeper RCDF80 declines (− 0.16, − 0.16, and − 0.15, respectively) compared to RCDF70 (− 0.11, − 0.10, and − 0.13). These gradients are sharpest at 500–400 hPa, then 400–300 hPa, emphasizing these levels as pivotal for detecting desaturation and convective outflows. Above 300 hPa, RCDFs stabilize with low variability. Nevertheless, the precipitation lifecycle remains evident—RCDF80 at 150 hPa increases from 0.06 (NR) to 0.34 (BR), peaks at 0.50 (DR), and declines to 0.31 (AR), capturing dynamic moisture buildup, peak, and dissipation.

Layer-specific thresholds highlight the discriminatory power of IRH. At 700 hPa, an IRH threshold of 0.82 identifies 84% of DR events (RCDF82 = 0.84) while excluding 76% of NR cases (RCDF82 = 0.24). Similarly, at 150 hPa, a threshold of 0.70 captures 82% of DR events (RCDF70 = 0.82) and excludes 76% of NR cases (RCDF70 = 0.24). Notably, the same 150 hPa threshold identifies 72% of BR events (RCDF70 = 0.72), reinforcing its utility as a standalone diagnostic indicator of impending rainfall. These results highlight the diagnostic value of mid- and upper-tropospheric IRH in distinguishing both active and pre-convective stages from no-rain conditions.

Low-troposphere differences primarily occur under high-humidity conditions: e.g., RCDF90 at 850 hPa separates DR–NR by more than 0.40, indicating strong saturation during rain. In the mid-troposphere, RCDF90 captures the bulk of variability, with 700 hPa and 500 hPa emerging as key layers for diagnosing vertical moisture transport. In the upper troposphere, RCDF80 predominates, delineating stratification and post-precipitation desaturation. Collectively, these RCDF patterns affirm the sensitivity of IRH to vertical moisture gradients and its effectiveness in identifying precipitation lifecycle transitions.

Conclusions

This study introduces IRH as a layer-resolved metric for diagnosing moisture-precipitation interactions in a subtropical coastal environment. By categorizing radiosonde observations into four precipitation stages—NR, BR, DR, and AR—and computing IRH across nine standard tropospheric layers, the analysis reveals how vertical humidity saturation evolves across the precipitation lifecycle, highlighting the moisture-precipitation interactions. The key findings are as follows:

IRH shows consistent vertical variations in all stages, peaking near 850 hPa in the lower troposphere before declining aloft. DR exhibits the highest low-level saturation and shallowest gradient, marking the lifecycle’s peak saturation phase.

As a layer-resolved metric, IRH enhances our ability to diagnose and analyze precipitation processes by providing a refined view of vertical moisture structure that bulk CRH obscures. This new diagnostic holds significant potential utility for rainfall nowcasting, evaluating and mitigating biases in model representations of convection, and improving predictions of extreme precipitation in a warming climate. Although IRH proves valuable here, the findings are derived from a single subtropical coastal site. Specific IRH thresholds reflect Hong Kong’s monsoon-coastal influences and may vary regionally (e.g., lower over oceans with higher baseline humidity, higher over land with a drier free troposphere34. In addition, radiosonde profiles are available only twice daily (00 and 12 UTC), which may introduce a diurnal sampling bias and limit the ability to fully capture sub‑daily variability in moisture-precipitation interactions.

Future work should therefore include multi‑site testing of IRH thresholds across diverse climates (e.g., tropical ocean and mid-latitude land) to refine its operational thresholds; employing higher‑frequency and vertically resolved observations (e.g., microwave radiometers, satellite humidity profiles, radar echo tops, satellite microphysics retrievals, and reanalysis products) to corroborate distinct convective regimes; integrating IRH diagnostics into operational nowcasting systems at the Hong Kong Observatory to quantitatively assess improvements in short-term rainfall prediction skill; and embedding IRH into high-resolution weather or climate models to enhance forecast accuracy.
