A multifunctional biomimetic nanoplatform combined with immune checkpoint blockade for triple-negative breast cancer immunotherapy through inhibiting polarization of M2 macrophages
Authors: Qianqian Zhou, Zongfang Jia, Yang Mu, Ya Xu, Fang Gao, Ruirui Wang, Liangliang Gu, Feifei Liu, Sheng Zhang, Weidong Chen, Yunna Chen, Lei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy has become a hopeful treatment for triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). However, most patients exhibit a low immune response. Tumor-associated fibroblasts (TAFs) suppress anti-tumor immune responses by encouraging the polarization of M2 macrophages, which diminishes the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs. Inhibiting TAFs can reduce the levels of M2 macrophages in the tumor microenvironment, thereby stimulating anti-tumor immune responses. Here, we developed a hybrid membrane-encapsulated biomimetic nanoparticle for inhibiting M2 macrophage polarization. Salvianolic acid B (SAB) was encapsulated in poly(L-lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA) nanoparticles and coated with a mixed membrane of red blood cells (RBCs) and TAFs on its surface. Nanoparticles coated with RBC membrane possess an “invisible” function that allows them to evade immune clearance and prolong circulation time. When encapsulated by TAF cell membranes, these nanoparticles can precisely target TAFs. By inhibiting TAFs, the released SAB reduced the secretion of CXCL12, thereby interfering with M2 macrophage polarization. In addition, biomimetic nanoparticles increased the levels of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells within tumors, while reducing the recruitment of myeloid-derived inhibitory cells (MDSCs), ultimately triggering an immune response. When combined with ICIs, biomimetic nanoparticles can extend the survival of mice and dramatically slow the growth of tumors. Our research findings suggest that biomimetic nanoparticles coated with mixed membranes represent an optimal strategy for enhancing the immune response to ICIs.Keywords: Triple-negative breast cancer, Salvianolic acid b, Tumor-associated fibroblasts, Tumor-associated macrophages, Immune checkpoint inhibitors

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a subtype of breast cancer with the worst prognosis, lacking the expression of estrogen receptors (ER), progesterone receptors (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER-2) [1]. TNBC is known for its aggressive invasiveness, propensity for metastasis, and limited treatment options [2]. Currently, the primary treatment modalities for TNBC include surgery, adjuvant chemotherapy, and radiotherapy [3, 4]. However, for some patients who miss the surgical window, effective treatment alternatives are lacking. Immunotherapy has become a promising strategy in recent years, showing an improvement in the overall survival rate of TNBC patients [5]. Its mechanism of action involves activating the body’s immune system, relying on its immune functions to recognize and eliminate tumor cells [6]. However, previous studies have demonstrated that current immunotherapies based on immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) exhibit limited clinical efficacy in most patients, with some showing no response at all. Additionally, there are significant variations in treatment outcomes among individuals [7, 8]. Lymph nodes are the primary sites of immune cells and play a crucial role in tumor immunotherapy. In cancer patients, the immune response within lymph nodes is disrupted, rendering it impossible to activate the anti-tumor immune response [9]. In addition, multiple inhibitory cell populations have been identified in the tumor microenvironment (TME) that prevent immunotherapy from being effective [10, 11]. Among these, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) play a crucial role in tumor initiation, progression, and resistance to therapy by establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Consequently, they have emerged as novel targets for cancer immunotherapy [12, 13].

TAMs are the most prevalent inflammatory cells within the tumor immune-suppressive microenvironment of tumors, exhibiting high plasticity [14, 15]. Various stimuli can polarize macrophages into two distinct phenotypes: M1 and M2, which exhibit opposing characteristics and functions [16]. In tumor tissue, M1 macrophages secrete pro-inflammatory factors, such as IL-1β and IL-12, which can inhibit or even kill tumor cells [17]. In contrast, M2 macrophages exhibit pro-tumor properties and contribute to the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment [18], which is associated with malignant disease progression, drug resistance, and a poor prognosis. In most tumor types, macrophages exhibiting an M2-like phenotype are predominant [19]. The polarization state of macrophages can be reversed entirely [20]. Tumor-associated fibroblasts (TAFs) can exert immunosuppressive activity by secreting various chemokines to regulate the polarization of TAMs into the M2 phenotype within the TME [21, 22]. CXCL12 primarily originates from TAF in tumors and is crucial for the polarization of M2 macrophages [23]. TAFs are effective in recruiting monocytes and promoting their conversion to M2 macrophages through the CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway [24]. Therefore, the CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway is a crucial mechanism through which TAFs mediate the polarization of M2 macrophages.

Salvianolic acid B (SAB) is an active component extracted from the roots of Salvia miltiorrhiza. Previous studies have found that SAB inhibition of TAF activation can effectively suppress M2 macrophage polarization and promote an anti-tumor immune response [25]. However, the short half-life and poor targeting of SAB prevent effective aggregation in tumors. Biomimetic nanoparticles enveloped in functional cell membranes have seen extensive applications in drug delivery [26]. Biomimetic nanoparticles can acquire special abilities, including immune escape, extended circulation times, and targeted delivery, after being encapsulated by cell membranes [27–29]. Nanoparticles wrapped in red blood cell membranes (RBCM) can be recognized by phagocytic cells, thereby bypassing phagocytic uptake and gaining invisibility [30, 31]. The surface proteins of tumor cell membranes can endow nanoparticles with specific homing and targeting properties, enabling the precise accumulation of drugs in tumors [32, 33]. In addition, biomimetic nanoparticles exhibit natural biocompatibility and are compatible with other organs and the blood system [34]. Therefore, encapsulating nanoparticles with different types of cell membranes has become a promising approach for developing drug nanocarrier systems.

In this study, we utilized a mixed membrane of RBCM and TAFs membranes (TAFsM) to encapsulate poly(L-lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA) nanoparticles (SAB-PLGA@RTM) as a delivery system for treating TNBC (Scheme 1). Based on the camouflage ability of hybrid membranes, SAB-PLGA@RTM can evade macrophage phagocytosis and acquire extended circulation capability. Meanwhile, TAFsM endowed SAB-PLGA@RTM with homologous targeting ability, which can increase the aggregation of SAB in tumor regions. SAB-PLGA@RTM selectively targeted TAFs to inhibit M2 macrophage polarization and enhance anti-tumor immune response. The combination therapy of biomimetic nanoparticles and ICIs showed excellent synergistic therapeutic effects on TNBC.

Methods

Materials

SAB was provided by Chengdu Philips Biotechnology Co., Ltd. PLGA was obtained from Daigangsheng Degradation Material Co., Ltd. Dichloromethane was provided by Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. TGF-β1 was acquired from Shenzhen Xinbosheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Coomassie Brilliant Blue Rapid Staining Solution was provided by Beyotime Biotechnology. The anti-CD206 antibody, anti-CXCR4 antibody, and anti-alpha SMA antibody were all supplied by Boster Biological Technology Co., Ltd. Zen-Bioscience Co., Ltd, offered the mouse anti-GAPDH antibody. The CXCL12 ELISA kit was acquired from Geneme Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). The TMR (Red) Tunnel Cell Apoptosis Detection kit was provided by Servicebio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). Penicillin-streptomycin was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology Company.

Cell culture

NIH3T3 mouse fibroblasts, the RAW 264.7 mouse monocyte macrophage line, and 4T1 mouse breast cancer cells were all obtained from the Shanghai Institute of Life Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences. NIH3T3 and RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in DMEM medium, while 4T1 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium. NIH3T3 cells were cultured for 24 h with transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) at a concentration of 10 ng/mL to induce the formation of TAFs. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Clinical sample collection and analysis

The gene expression data of 113 normal and 1098 cancer breast samples were downloaded from the TCGA database. The survival curve was obtained from the OncoLnc website.

Extraction of RBCM and TAFsM

The whole blood from mice was transferred into centrifuge tubes containing sodium heparin. After centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, the plasma, white blood cells, and platelets were separated. The obtained RBCs were washed with 1× PBS until the supernatant was colorless. The precipitate was then resuspended in a low-permeability solution of 0.25× PBS at a volume ratio of 1:50 and lysed on a shaker at 4 °C for 1 h to ensure complete rupture of the blood cells. Then, the ruptured blood cells were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C to remove the released hemoglobin. Finally, the precipitate was washed repeatedly with 1× PBS to obtain a pale pink RBCM. Store the extracted cell membranes at −80 °C.

The collected TAFs were crushed using an ultrasonic cell pulverizer for 2 min under conditions of 150 W (5 s on, 5 s off). The obtained mixture was centrifuged at 3200 g and 4 °C for 5 min to eliminate unbroken cells and nuclei. Finally, the supernatant was centrifuged at 100,000 g and 4 °C for 45 min to obtain a membrane precipitate, which was refrigerated at −80 °C.

Preparation of SAB-PLGA@RTM

To prepare SAB-PLGA@RTM, RBCM and TAFsM were initially fused. The RBCM and TAFsM were mixed in a PBS (pH 7.4) solution at a 1:1 ratio. Ultrasonic treatment was subsequently conducted in an ice water bath at a power of 150 W for 10 min to prepare a mixed membrane. SAB-PLGA was prepared using the ultrasonic double emulsion method. PBS (1 mL) containing 10 mg of SAB was used as the internal aqueous phase, and dichloromethane (5 mL) containing 100 mg PLGA was used as the oil phase. The internal water phase was incorporated into the oil phase, and ultrasonic treatment was performed for 1 min with a duty cycle set at 50% (sonication for 4 s followed by a 2 s interval). Next, the colostrum obtained was added to 15 mL of the external aqueous phase containing 1% (w/v) sodium cholate and was ultrasonically treated for 4 min to form a double emulsion (W/O/W). Finally, the double lotion was added to 22.5 mL aqueous phase containing 1% (w/v) sodium cholate and evaporated at 450 rpm at room temperature for 4 h to obtain a blue, milky-white nanoparticle solution. The mixed membrane and SAB-PLGA solution were mixed by ultrasound at a mass ratio of 2:1 for 10 min to obtain SAB-PLGA@RTM.

Characterization of SAB-PLGA@RTM

To verify whether the two membranes can be successfully fused after ultrasound treatment, varying proportions of TAFsM were added to RBCM labeled with both DiO and DiL fluorescence. Fusion was detected by Föster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET). Additionally, to more intuitively characterize double membrane fusion, DiO and DiL were employed to tag RBCM and TAFsM, respectively. A fluorescence microscope (Leica, DMi8) was utilized to examine the co-localization of fluorescence after both physical and ultrasound fusion. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-1400plus, Japan) was used to investigate the morphological structure of PLGA nanoparticles, SAB-PLGA, and SAB-PLGA@RTM. The Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern, UK) was utilized to measure the particle size, dispersion coefficient (PDI), and zeta potential of PLGA nanoparticles, SAB-PLGA, and SAB-PLGA@RTM. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was employed to assess the encapsulation efficiency and drug loading of SAB-PLGA@RTM. The size and zeta potential of SAB-PLGA@RTM suspended in PBS (pH 7.4) were monitored to determine its stability. The protein profiles of various samples were examined using SDS-PAGE, followed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining. Western blotting was employed to detect the expression of CD47, AE1 (a marker for RBCM), and FAP-α (a marker for TAFsM). To investigate drug release behavior, free SAB and SAB-PLGA@RTM were placed in dialysis bags (MwCO: 8000–14000 D). Subsequently, the dialysis bags were immersed in different release media and incubated at 37℃. HPLC was employed to measure the cumulative release of SAB over various time intervals.

Cytotoxicity test

To assess the cytotoxicity and biocompatibility of different nanoparticles on cells, we performed the CCK-8 assays. Initially, NIH3T3 and 4T1 cells were seeded into 96-well plates, and the drug was administered after the cells reached a specific density. After 48 h, CCK-8 solution was added to every well and incubated for an additional 2 h. The optical density (OD) value was then taken at 450 nm.

Cellular uptake of SAB-PLGA@RTM

To evaluate the immune escape ability of various formulations, including SAB-PLGA@RM, SAB-PLGA@TM, and SAB-PLGA@RTM, all were labeled with DiL. After co-incubating RAW 264.7 cells with DiL-labeled nanoparticles for 0.5 h, the residual dyes were removed using precooled PBS (pH 7.4). The RAW 264.7 cells were subsequently fixed and stained for 20 min with Hoechst 33,258. Fluorescence microscopy (FM) was used to observe the phagocytosis of nanoparticles by RAW 264.7 cells. Meanwhile, DiL-labeled nanoparticles were incubated with RAW 264.7 cells for 0.5 h, after which cellular uptake was investigated by flow cytometry (FCM). To study the affinity of SAB-PLGA@RTM for TAFs, NIH3T3 and 4T1 cells were selected as control groups. FM and FCM were employed to observe and quantify the absorption of SAB-PLGA@RTM by different cells, respectively. The experimental steps are the same as above.

In vitro macrophage polarization

In TME, TAFs are essential for promoting the polarization of M2 macrophages. To clarify whether nanoparticles can inhibit M2 macrophage polarization, we first examined the inhibitory effects of nanoparticles on TAFs. TAFs were treated with various formulations (SAB: 100 µmol/mL) for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were collected to assess the expression of α-SMA. The conditioned medium from TAFs exposed to various formulations for 24 h was also collected. An ELISA kit was employed to determine CXCL12 secretion in the conditioned medium from TAFs. The collected conditioned medium was introduced to RAW 264.7 cells and incubated for 24 h. Untreated M0 macrophages served as controls. Western blotting was employed to analyze the expression levels of CD206 and CXCR4 in the macrophages.

Additionally, the treated macrophages were collected and incubated with APC-labeled CD86 antibody at 4 °C for 30 min. Macrophages were subjected to fixation and permeabilization according to the Fix&PERM Kit protocol. The sample was then incubated with FITC-conjugated CD206 antibody at 4 °C for 0.5 h. Lastly, the fluorescence expression in the samples was analyzed using FCM.

Animal

The BALB/c mice were sourced from the Chengdu Dashuo Biological Research Institute. 1 × 106 cells were implanted into the left fourth breast region of BALB/c mice to establish a mouse model of TNBC. All animal experiments adhere to the regulations established by the Experimental Animal Management Committee of Anhui University of Chinese Medicine (AHUCM-mouse-2023170).

Pharmacokinetics and biodistribution of SAB-PLGA@RTM

To facilitate the observation of nanoparticle distribution in vivo, DIR-labeled nanoparticles were prepared. Free DIR, DIR-PLGA, and DIR-PLGA@RTM (DIR: 2 mg/kg) were administered to tumor-implanted mice through the tail vein. Plasma samples were collected at different time points for imaging. The imaging system (IVIS Spectrum, USA) was used to observe the targeting intensity of nanoparticles at the tumor site in mice at various intervals. Finally, the tumor and main organs were collected, and the fluorescence intensity was evaluated in vitro.

In vivo anti-tumor therapy

One week after establishing the TNBC model, mice implanted with 4T1 tumors were randomly assigned to six distinct groups: NS, PLGA@RTM, SAB, SAB-PLGA@RTM, BMS-1, and SAB-PLGA@RTM + BMS-1 groups. Different treatments were administered via the tail vein at a uniform dosage of 20 mg/kg of SAB. Tumor-bearing mice were administered once every other day for eight consecutive doses. Tumor size and the weight of the mice were monitored throughout the treatment, and tumor volume was determined via the equation: volume = length × (width²/2). After the therapy, whole blood, tumors, and major organs were collected for further analysis.

Masson’s trichrome staining

After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, tumor tissues from each group were paraffin-embedded and sectioned using a microtome. The tumor sections were dyed using a Masson kit and imaged under a microscope (Leica, DMi8). ImageJ software was employed for the quantitative assessment of the images.

TUNEL staining

The tumor tissues in each group were sliced after dehydration and fixation. Next, the tumor sections were stained using the TUNEL kit. The apoptosis of tumor cells was analyzed following microscopic examination.

Microvascular imaging of the tumor in mice

To evaluate the tumor blood vessels in mice, the super-resolution small animal ultrasound imaging system (Feiyinuo Technology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) was used to image the tumor site. First, the treated mice were anesthetized with isoflurane. Then, ultrasound gel was applied, the ultrasound probe was positioned in the tumor area, and the contrast agent was administered via the tail vein. Finally, the data was collected on the acquisition tool.

Western blot analysis

After extracting and quantifying tumor tissue proteins, the proteins were separated using 10% SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred to activated PVDF membranes. Membranes were immersed in 5% skim milk for 2 h. They were then co-incubated overnight with antibodies, including α-SMA (1:1000), CXCR4 (1:500), CD206 (1:500), and GAPDH (1:1000). Following this, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h. Finally, an ECL kit was used to detect protein signals.

Flow cytometry analysis

Tumor tissue was cut, milled, and enzymatically digested before filtering through a 100 μm cell sieve to prepare a single-cell suspension. RBC lysis buffer was used to eliminate RBCs. The fluorescein-conjugated antibodies were used for cell staining after blocking Fc receptors. Anti-CD3, anti-CD4, and anti-CD8 antibodies were used to analyze T cells. The anti-CD45 and anti-CD11b were used to identify myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). To detect macrophages, an intracellular fixation/permeation buffer kit (Elabscience, China) was used for cell permeation and intracellular staining. Finally, FCM was used to examine the cells after they were washed with PBS.

Safety evaluation

The blood of healthy rabbits was used to collect RBCs. The collected RBCs were suspended in 0.9% NaCl, and the final concentration was 2% (v/v). After mixing different doses of SAB-PLGA@RTM with an equivalent volume of the 2% RBC suspension, the mixture was incubated for 3 h at 37 °C. Physiological saline acted as the negative control, while deionized water functioned as the positive control group. After centrifugation, an enzyme-labeled instrument was used to measure the absorbance of the supernatant at 540 nm, allowing for the calculation of the hemolysis rate for each sample.

Hemolysis rate = (ODSample group-ODNegative control group)/(ODPositive control group-ODNegative control group)×100%

Main organs and blood samples were collected from tumor-bearing mice for safety assessment. The levels of AST/GOT, BUN, ALT/GPT, and creatinine in serum were assessed according to the kit’s instructions. The main section was prepared for H&E staining. The stained sections were imaged under an optical microscope to determine damage to the major organs.

Statistical analysis

The experimental results were represented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses, including one-way ANOVA and a nonparametric test, were conducted using GraphPad Prism and SPSS Statistics 26. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Analysis of gene expression in clinical samples of breast cancer

The various components present in TME, including immunosuppressive cells, extracellular matrix (ECM), and cytokines, collectively contribute to immune evasion (Fig. 1A). The gene expression profiles of breast samples in the TCGA database were analyzed. As shown in Fig. 1B, the expression levels of cytokines such as CCL2, CCL3, and CXCL9, which are responsible for recruiting natural killer (NK) cells, were significantly reduced in tumor samples. However, the expression of CXCL10 and CXCL11 was found to be upregulated in these samples. In addition to their role in recruiting NK cells, CCL2 and CCL3 also facilitate the recruitment of TAMs, which exert immunosuppressive effects. The CXCL9/10/11-CXCR3 axis enhances the infiltration of T cells and NK cells, playing a pivotal role in anti-tumor immunity [35]. The expression of CCL4, CCL5, CCL8, CCL17, CCL20, and CCL22 is intricately associated with the recruitment of regulatory T (Treg) cells. Notably, CCL5, CCL17, CCL20, and CCL22 exhibit significantly elevated expression in tumors, while CCL4 shows reduced levels. Compared to the control group, the tumor group exhibited significantly elevated levels of cytokines associated with TAM recruitment, specifically CCL20 and CCL11. The primary role of CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL5, and CXCL8 is to recruit MDSCs, thereby promoting tumor growth. As expected, the downregulation of CXCL1, CXCL2, and CXCL5 expression in tumors is advantageous for the development of anti-tumor immunity. The findings reveal a significant recruitment of TAMs into the TME of breast cancer patients, which facilitates immune evasion. Consequently, strategies to inhibit the recruitment of TAMs have become a primary focus of immunotherapy for cancer patients. TAFs play a crucial role in promoting immune escape. Activated TAFs recruit immunosuppressive cells by secreting various cytokines and chemokines, thereby enhancing tumor growth and invasion. Notably, TAFs primarily recruit TAMs through the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis, which contributes to immune evasion. However, the expression of CXCL12 was reduced in breast cancer patients. Although the expression of CXCL12 is reduced in breast cancer patients, the change in CXCL12 secretion by TAFs in breast cancer patients is still unclear. Numerous studies have demonstrated that CXCL12, secreted by TAFs, plays a crucial role in tumor growth, angiogenesis, and immune suppression. Therefore, reducing CXCL12 secretion by TAFs remains a strategy to enhance the therapeutic effect of TNBC. The marker protein FAP representing TAFs activation in breast samples was further analyzed. Compared to normal samples, the expression of FAP was significantly increased in tumor samples, indicating that a large number of TAFs were activated in tumors (Fig. 1C). Additionally, we analyzed the impact of FAP expression on the survival of patients with breast cancer. The survival rate of breast cancer patients with high expression of FAP in the early stage of disease is lower than that of patients with low expression of FAP. In contrast, the expression of FAP in the late stage has no significant impact on the patient’s survival rate. Therefore, inhibiting the activation of TAFs is essential for enhancing the effectiveness of immunotherapy and improving the early survival rate of patients.

Preparation and characterization of SAB-PLGA@RTM

To prepare SAB-PLGA@RTM, RBCM and TAFsM were fused to create a mixed membrane (Fig. 2A). FRET was used to verify whether the two membrane components could successfully fuse. RBCM was labeled with the fluorescent dyes DiO and DiL, and then fused with TAFsM at various mass ratios. As shown in Fig. 2B, an increase in TAFsM resulted in a weakening of the FRET effect between DiO and DiL. The fluorescence intensity increased at 540 nm and decreased at 675 nm, indicating the successful incorporation of RBCM into TAFsM. Furthermore, RBCM was labeled with DiL (red signal) and TAFsM was labeled with DiO (green signal) to confirm successful fusion. As shown in Fig. 2C, when RBCM and TAFsM were fused at a 1:1 mass ratio, the ultrasound-fused mixed membrane exhibited co-localization of red and green fluorescence signals. On the contrary, the physically mixed samples displayed distinct red and green fluorescence signals. The fluorescence co-location curves of DiO and DiL are shown in Fig. 2D. These results provided compelling evidence for the successful fusion of TAFsM and RBCM.

After successfully fusing RBCM and TAFsM, SAB-PLGA@RTM was synthesized. The TEM results demonstrate that PLGA and SAB-PLGA possess a distinct core-shell structure, which is characteristic of the W/O/W configuration (Fig. 2E). SAB-PLGA@RTM exhibited a distinct outer membrane structure, indicating the effective encapsulation of the mixed membrane. The particle sizes of PLGA, SAB-PLGA, and SAB-PLGA@RTM are 164.3 ± 2.53 nm, 166.3 ± 2.03 nm, and 177.6 ± 1.37 nm, respectively (Fig. 2F). Compared to SAB-PLGA, the sizes of SAB-PLGA@RTM have increased by approximately 10 nm, which aligns closely with the cell membrane thickness reported in the literature [36]. This result indicated that RBCM and TAFsM have successfully covered the surface of the PLGA nanoparticles. Moreover, the size distribution of SAB-PLGA and SAB-PLGA@RTM is uniform, with a PDI of less than 0.3. The measured zeta potential for PLGA was − 58.7 ± 1.02 mV, while that for SAB-PLGA was − 58.1 ± 0.74 mV (Fig. 2G). After coating with the cell membrane, the final zeta potential of SAB-PLGA@RTM was − 27.8 ± 0.67 mV. The encapsulation efficiency of SAB-PLGA@RTM was 79.72 ± 0.95% and the drug loading was 2.05 ± 0.22% as determined by HPLC. The size, PDI, and zeta potential of SAB-PLGA@RTM were continuously monitored over a one-week period to evaluate its stability during storage at 4 °C. As shown in Figs. 2H and I, SAB-PLGA@RTM remained stable for a week. In vitro release studies indicated that free SAB accumulated and released 90.40% within 4 h under normal physiological conditions (pH 7.4). In contrast, SAB in SAB-PLGA@RTM was released more slowly, with only 58.47% released within 10 h (Fig. 2J). At the same time, this study simulated the release behaviors of free SAB and SAB-PLGA@RTM under slightly acidic TME (pH 6.5) and lysosomal conditions (pH 5.0). The results indicated that the release of SAB from SAB-PLGA@RTM remained slow. This study demonstrated the sustained release capability of SAB-PLGA@RTM. Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining was employed to evaluate whether the prepared SAB-PLGA@RTM retained the characteristic proteins of RBCM and TAFsM. As shown in Fig. 2K, SAB-PLGA@RTM contained both RBCM and TAFsM characteristic proteins. Additionally, western blotting was performed to evaluate the expression of these characteristic proteins in SAB-PLGA@RTM derived from RBCM and TAFsM. The results showed that AE1 was expressed in SAB-PLGA@RM while FAP-α was expressed in SAB-PLGA@TM. After preparing SAB-PLGA@RTM, the characteristic protein AE1 on RBCM and the characteristic protein FAP-α on TAFsM were both retained (Fig. 2L). CD47 was expressed in both SAB-PLGA@RM and SAB-PLGA@RTM, indicating that nanoparticles can evade macrophage phagocytosis. This finding suggested that SAB-PLGA@RTM may have the functional properties of both RBCM and TAFsM.

Cytotoxicity test in vitro

CCK-8 assay was employed to assess the cytotoxicity of different nanoparticles on NIH3T3 and 4T1 cells. As shown in Fig. 3, within the experimental concentration range of 0-200 µmol/L, the cell survival rate following PLGA@RTM treatment exceeded 90%, indicating that this carrier is safe and effective. In addition, the effects of SAB, SAB-PLGA, and SAB-PLGA@RTM on the survival rate of NIH3T3 cells are illustrated in Fig. 3A. The results indicated that SAB, SAB-PLGA, and SAB-PLGA@RTM were non-toxic to NIH3T3 cells within the concentration range of 0-100 µmol/L. However, when the concentration of SAB reached 200 µmol/L, the activity of NIH3T3 cells decreased significantly. Therefore, a concentration of 100 µmol/L SAB was selected for the subsequent experiments. The cytotoxic effects on 4T1 cells are illustrated in Fig. 3B. As the concentration of SAB increased, the activity of 4T1 cells was progressively inhibited, indicating that SAB, SAB-PLGA, and SAB-PLGA@RTM exhibit cytotoxic effects on 4T1 cells. These results indicated that nanomaterials themselves do not exhibit significant cytotoxicity; instead, their effects were attributed to SAB.

Immune escape and homologous targeting ability of SAB-PLGA@RTM

PLGA nanoparticles inherit immune escape and homologous targeting abilities after being simultaneously encapsulated by RBCM and TAFsM (Fig. 4A). To evaluate whether hybrid membrane camouflage can endow nanoparticles with immune escape ability, RAW 264.7 cells were incubated with DiL-labeled SAB-PLGA, SAB-PLGA@RM, SAB-PLGA@TM, and SAB-PLGA@RTM for 0.5 h. The extent of cellular uptake was assessed using FM and FCM. According to the FM results, the uptake of SAB-PLGA by macrophages was higher than that of SAB-PLGA@RM and SAB-PLGA@RTM (Fig. 4B). These results showed that the ability of SAB-PLGA@RTM to escape being swallowed by the immune system was enhanced after coating RBCM or a mixed membrane. The phagocytosis of RAW 264.7 on various nanoparticles was quantitatively analyzed by FCM (Fig. 4C and D). This result further confirmed that SAB-PLGA@RTM has been successfully endowed with the ability to evade macrophage phagocytosis (***p < 0.001). In addition, to study the homologous targeting ability of SAB-PLGA@RTM in vitro, we incubated DiL-labeled SAB-PLGA@RTM with various cell lines, including 4T1, NIH3T3, and TAF cells. According to CLSM observation, DiL-labeled SAB-PLGA@RTM was primarily concentrated in TAFs, but less absorbed in 4T1 and NIH3T3 cells (Fig. 4E). This result suggested that SAB-PLGA@RTM exhibited a higher affinity for TAFs because of its homologous targeting capability. FCM results showed that TAFs had significantly higher uptake of nanoparticles than NIH3T3 and 4T1 cells (Fig. 4F and G). As expected, SAB-PLGA@RTM possesses both immune escape and homologous targeting abilities.

Inhibition polarization of M2 macrophages by SAB-PLGA@RTM via Inhibition of CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway

Macrophages can undergo phenotypic transformation according to various external signals. Figure 5A is a schematic diagram of the phenotypic transformation of macrophages. TAFs can be obtained by activating NIH3T3 cells. Compared with before administration, a-SMA was significantly down-regulated, and TAF activity was inhibited (Fig. 5B and C). Notably, the blank vector PLGA@RTM does not have a significant effect on α-SMA expression. These results indicated that the inhibitory effect of SAB-PLGA@RTM on TAFs primarily stemmed from the biological activity of SAB, rather than the properties of the carrier material. TAFs can effectively recruit monocytes via the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis, promoting their polarization into M2 macrophages. Therefore, the effect of SAB-PLGA@RTM on the secretion of CXCL12 by TAFs and macrophage surface receptor CXCR4 has been studied. As shown in Fig. 5D, after treating RAW264.7 cells with TAFs’ conditioned medium, the contents of CD206 and CXCR4 were upregulated (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01), while the contents of CD86 did not show significant changes (p > 0.05). This finding suggested that TAFs can upregulate the expression levels of CXCR4, thereby promoting the transformation of macrophages into the M2 phenotype. As illustrated in Fig. 5E and F, the expression of CD206 and CXCR4 was suppressed by SAB and SAB-PLGA@RTM (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001). Compared with SAB, SAB-PLGA@RTM has a more substantial inhibitory effect. The reason for this result may be that TAFs can better take up SAB after being loaded into biomimetic nanoparticles. Furthermore, SAB and SAB-PLGA@RTM had no apparent inhibitory effect on CD86 protein (Fig. 5G). It was worth noting that PLGA@RTM does not affect the expression of CD206, CD86, and CXCR4, indicating that SAB mainly caused the effect of SAB-PLGA@RTM. These results indicated that SAB and SAB-PLGA@RTM can inhibit M2 macrophage polarization, and CXCR4 was involved in this process. To further investigate how CXCR4 participates in regulating macrophage polarization, CXCL12 secreted by TAFs was measured. The CXCL12 secreted by TAFs interacts with CXCR4 on the surface of macrophages, facilitating polarization towards the M2 phenotype. Therefore, CXCL12 secreted by TAFs was determined. As shown in Fig. 5H, CXCL12 was primarily released by TAFs (*p < 0.05). SAB and SAB-PLGA@RTM treatment can significantly reduce the levels of CXCL12 secreted by TAFs. FCM was employed to study the impact of SAB-PLGA@RTM on macrophage polarization. As illustrated in Fig. 5I-M, the conditioned medium of TAFs promoted macrophage polarization into the M2 phenotype. However, following the interventions of SAB and SAB-PLGA@RTM, there was a significant reduction in the percentage of M2 macrophages. Additionally, the rate of M1 macrophages diminished after treatment with SAB and SAB-PLGA@RTM. However, the ratio of M1/M2 increased after the interaction between SAB and SAB-PLGA@RTM, indicating the advantages of SAB and SAB-PLGA@RTM in anti-tumor therapy. To verify whether the effects of SAB and SAB-PLGA@RTM on macrophage polarization are related to the CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway. AMD3100 (CXCL12 inhibitor) was used to study its effect on macrophage polarization. AMD3100 also inhibited the proportion of M2 macrophages. These results indicated that SAB and SAB-PLGA@RTM can inhibit M2 macrophage polarization through the CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway.

Biological distribution of SAB-PLGA@RTM in vivo

DIR is a lipophilic dye that exhibits strong absorption in the near-infrared spectrum and has been widely used as a biological imaging tracer for studying nanoparticle distribution [37]. To visualize the in vivo biological distribution and half-life of biomimetic nanoparticles, DIR was used to label these nanoparticles. The experimental procedure was shown in Fig. 6A, where free DIR, DIR-PLGA, and DIR-PLGA@RTM were administered intravenously to tumor-bearing mice. To investigate the circulation time of biomimetic nanoparticles in vivo, blood samples were collected at various intervals for fluorescence imaging (Fig. 6B). Compared with free DIR, DIR-PLGA@RTM has a longer circulation time and an increased half-life in vivo (Fig. 6C and D). This phenomenon was related to the biomimetic cell membrane on the surface of DIR-PLGA@RTM, indicating that DIR-PLGA@RTM has an “invisible” feature. At specific times, in vivo, fluorescence distribution was monitored using a small-animal imaging setup. As depicted in Fig. 6E and F, the DIR-PLGA@RTM group showed the highest tumor fluorescence intensity at all time points. The retention time of the DIR-PLGA group in tumors was longer than that of DIR. These results indicated that biomimetic nanoparticles can increase drug accumulation at the tumor site and possess homologous targeting ability. The fluorescence intensity of the primary tissue was measured 24 h after drug injection (Fig. 6G and H). Compared with the DIR and DIR-PLGA groups, the DIR-PLGA@RTM group exhibited the highest fluorescence intensity in tumors (*p < 0.05). In addition, DIR-PLGA@RTM accumulated in the liver and spleen, while DIR was mainly distributed in the lungs. These results show that the prepared biomimetic nanoparticles exhibit a longer blood retention time and enhanced targeted enrichment capability compared to free SAB.

SAB-PLGA@RTM enhanced the anti-tumor efficacy of BMS-1

ICIs activate T lymphocytes by targeting PD-1 or PD-L1, thereby inducing anti-tumor immune responses. However, immunotherapy has a low response rate in most patients. To verify whether SAB-PLGA@RTM can induce anti-tumor immune responses, its combination with BMS-1 (an ICI) was evaluated against TNBC. The combination therapy is shown in Fig. 7A; the tumor volume and mouse weight were recorded every 48 h. Compared with the untreated group, the tumor size and weight were significantly reduced in the SAB-PLGA@RTM, BMS-1, and combination therapy groups (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01). At the same time, the effects of PLGA@RTM and SAB were limited (Fig. 7B and C). SAB-PLGA@RTM can inhibit tumor growth while SAB cannot exert its effect. The reason for this phenomenon may be that SAB-PLGA@RTM can evade macrophage clearance and target tumors. A significant extension of survival duration in tumor-bearing mice was achieved through the combination of SAB-PLGA@RTM and BMS-1 therapy (Fig. 7D). After the drug treatment, the weights of the mice exhibited minimal variation across the groups, indicating that there were no significant side effects of each treatment group (Fig. 7E). The trend of tumor volume changes was monitored during the treatment period. In comparison to the untreated group, the SAB-PLGA@RTM, BMS-1, and combination therapy groups all demonstrated the ability to inhibit tumor growth (Fig. 7F). The combination therapy group, consisting of SAB-PLGA@RTM and BMS-1, showed the most pronounced suppression of tumor growth. As shown in Fig. 7G, the cell morphology of NS and PLGA@RTM groups was intact. After SAB treatment, there was a slight disruption in cell morphology. The cells in the SAB-PLGA@RTM and BMS-1 groups showed apoptosis. The combination therapy group of SAB-PLGA@RTM and BMS-1 showed the most severe cell apoptosis, which is due to the enhanced anti-tumor immune response induced by SAB-PLGA@RTM.

TAFs not only participate in the formation and progression of tumors but also prevent drugs and immune cells from entering the tumor by producing extracellular matrix (ECM) [38]. As shown in Fig. 8A and B, both the NS group and the PLGA@RTM group showed severe ECM deposition. Compared to the NS group, ECM deposition was markedly reduced in the SAB-PLGA@RTM, BMS-1, and combination groups (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001). Although SAB can significantly reduce matrix deposition in tumors, its effect was significantly lower than that of SAB-PLGA@RTM (**p < 0.01). This result was attributed to the targeted accumulation of SAB-PLGA@RTM in TAFs, which better inhibited the production of ECM. The co-administration of BMS-1 with SAB-PLGA@RTM resulted in minimal matrix deposition in tumor tissue, indicating the advantages of combination therapy. Apoptotic cell assessment in tumor tissue following therapy was conducted using the TUNEL staining (Fig. 8C and D). Compared to the NS group, tumor cell apoptosis was significantly upregulated after treatment with SAB-PLGA@RTM, BSM-1, and BSM-1 + SAB-PLGA@RTM groups (**p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001). In contrast to treatments utilizing BMS-1 or SAB-PLGA@RTM individually, the combined therapy significantly elevated the rate of tumor cell apoptosis (**p < 0.01). Notably, tumor cell apoptosis in the BMS-1 group was mainly manifested in the marginal area of the tumor. After the combined treatment of BMS-1 and SAB-PLGA@RTM, severe cell apoptosis occurred deep within the tumor. This phenomenon may be due to the physical barrier formed by the ECM hindering the penetration of BMS-1 into the tumor and preventing the killing of tumor cells. SAB-PLGA@RTM can break down physical barriers by reducing matrix deposition, thereby promoting the infiltration of BMS-1 into tumors and killing tumor cells. The formation of neovascularization has a profound impact on the proliferation and metastasis of malignant tumors. TAFs promote angiogenesis in tumors by secreting pro-angiogenic factors [39]. Therefore, angiogenesis was observed after inhibiting TAFs. As shown in Fig. 8E and F, the NS and PLGA@RTM groups exhibited higher vascular density, whereas the SAB, SAB-PLGA@RTM, BMS-1, and combination therapy groups showed significantly reduced vascular density. The combination treatment group of BMS-1 and SAB-PLGA@RTM showed the most significant inhibition of angiogenesis. These results indicated that the blend therapy of biomimetic nanoparticles and ICIs markedly decreased ECM and angiogenesis, while significantly increasing tumor cell apoptosis.

SAB-PLGA@RTM inhibited polarization of M2 macrophages via CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway in vivo

α-SMA expression in tumor tissues was analyzed using western blotting (Fig. 9A and B). The simultaneous administration markedly reduced the expression of α-SMA relative to the single treatment group (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01). These findings demonstrated that BMS-1 + SAB-PLGA@RTM can effectively suppress the activity of TAFs. Due to the biomimetic membrane on the surface of SAB-PLGA@RTM targeting TAFs, the inhibitory effect of SAB-PLGA@RTM on α-SMA was superior to SAB (**p < 0.01). The immunofluorescence findings were consistent with the western blot results (Fig. 9C and D). Upon validating the inhibitory effect of SAB-PLGA@RTM on TAFs, the levels of the M2 macrophage marker protein CD206 were then assessed. Comparatively, the expression of CD206 was notably decreased in the SAB-PLGA@RTM, BMS-1, and combined treatment groups (Fig. 9A and E). Similarly, the fluorescence intensity of CD206 in SAB-PLGA@RTM, BMS-1, and combination therapy groups was significantly reduced (Fig. 9F and G). It is worth mentioning that the combination therapy group showed significantly more potent inhibition of CD206 expression than the SAB-PLGA@RTM and BMS-1 treatment groups alone. The findings indicated that SAB-PLGA@RTM + BMS-1 therapy can inhibit M2 macrophage polarization by suppressing TAFs. To investigate the potential association between the inhibition of M2 macrophage polarization and the CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway, the expressions of CXCR4 and CXCL12 were measured. As shown in Fig. 9A, H, and I, the SAB-PLGA@RTM + BMS-1 group had the most potent inhibitory effect on the expression of CXCR4 and CXCL12, indicating that combination therapy has a synergistic effect. These findings suggested that the inhibitory effect of biomimetic nanoparticles and ICIs on M2 macrophage polarization was related to the CXCL12/CXCR4 pathway.

Remodeling of immunosuppressive TME by SAB-PLGA@RTM

TAFs are considered a key factor in inducing an immunosuppressive microenvironment and promoting tumor progression [40, 41]. CXCL12, secreted by TAFs, facilitates the infiltration of immunosuppressive cells. In addition, TAFs, M2 macrophages, and MDSCs are barriers that hinder T cells from initiating anti-tumor immune responses. Therefore, the infiltration of immune cells has been researched after inhibiting TAFs. As TAFs were inhibited, the proportion of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the tumor significantly increased. In contrast, the proportion of MDSCs and M2 macrophages decreased, which was the result of the interaction between SAB-PLGA@RTM and BMS-1 (Fig. 10A). As expected, after combination therapy, the infiltration of immune cells in the tumor increased. In contrast, the number of immunosuppressive cells decreased, and the effect was better than that of SAB-PLGA@RTM or BMS-1 treatment alone. These results indicated that inhibiting TAFs can promote immune cell infiltration and enhance anti-tumor immune response.

The regulatory effect of tumor immune suppressive microenvironment in vivo. (A) FCM and corresponding quantitative analysis of CD4+ T, CD8+ T cells, MDSCs, and M2 macrophages (n = 3). (B) RT-qPCR analysis of IL-6, CCL5, CCL2, CXCL1, TGF-β and IFNγ expression in tumors after different treatments (n = 5, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001)

Cytokines play a crucial role in intercellular communication during tumor development t [23, 42]. Among them, chemokines regulate the phenotype, recruitment, and function of immune cells [43]. IL-6, as a pro-inflammatory cytokine, exerts tumor immune suppressive ability by promoting the infiltration of MDSCs [44]. CCL5 is an inflammatory mediator that supports the proliferation and spread of cancer [45–47]. CCL2 is responsible for recruiting immunosuppressive cells, such as M2 macrophages, to participate in the formation of the immunosuppressive microenvironment [48, 49]. CXCL1 is a chemokine secreted by M2 macrophages and can promote the metastasis of breast cancer [50]. TGF-β, secreted by tumor and stromal cells within the TME, weakens the efficacy of immunotherapy by inhibiting the anti-tumor activity of immune cells. IFN-γ mainly induces an anti-tumor immune response in TME [51]. Therefore, after observing changes in the infiltration of immune cells and immunosuppressive cells in tumors, the effect of combination therapy on cytokines was studied. Compared to the NS group, the expressions of IL-6, CXCL1, and CCL2 were inhibited in the SAB-PLGA@RTM, BMS-1, and SAB-PLGA@RTM + BMS-1 groups. In contrast, the expression of IFN-γ was increased (Fig. 10B). BMS-1 did not significantly alter the expression of CCL5 and TGF-β. At the same time, both the SAB-PLGA@RTM and combination therapy groups significantly inhibited their expression. Compared to the SAB or PLGA@RTM groups, the SAB-PLGA@RTM group has a more substantial impact on cytokines. The effect of PLGA@RTM on cytokines was not different from that of the NS group. These results suggested that the “invisibility” and homologous targeting ability of SAB-PLGA@RTM enable SAB to target tumors and induce anti-tumor immune responses accurately. In summary, SAB-PLGA@RTM can enhance the anti-tumor immune response of BMS-1 therapy, highlighting the benefits of combination therapy in anti-tumor treatment.

Biosafety evaluation

Evaluating biosafety is crucial for the development of nanoparticles. The biocompatibility of SAB-PLGA@RTM was evaluated by hemolysis experiment (Fig. 11A and B). When the concentration of SAB-PLGA@RTM does not exceed 160 µg/mL, the hemolysis rate of each group is less than 5%. These results confirmed that SAB-PLGA@RTM has reliable biosafety in a specific concentration range. H&E staining was conducted on key organ sections from each mouse group following treatment. As shown in Fig. 11C, different preparations had no noticeable histological changes or functional damage to normal organs. This result suggested that SAB-PLGA@RTM had no apparent toxicity. Additionally, liver and kidney function indicators in the serum were evaluated using ELISA kits. The contents of ALT and BUN in each group have no significant difference within the normal reference range (Fig. 11D). In comparison to normal mice, the NS and PLGA@RTM groups exhibited observably increased levels of AST and CRE, indicating specific toxicity to the liver and kidneys. After multiple treatments, the level of CRE in the SAB group increased. On the contrary, SAB-PLGA@RTM, BMS-1, and combination therapy normalized levels of AST and CRE. In summary, the combination therapy of biomimetic nanoparticles and ICIs reduced the toxicity of SAB, which may be due to the reduction of SAB accumulation in normal tissues by nanoparticles.

Conclusion

In conclusion, PLGA nanoparticles coated with a mixture of RBCM and TAFsM have been developed for delivering SAB. SAB-PLGA@RTM can evade macrophage phagocytosis and precisely target TAFs. SAB released by SAB-PLGA@RTM in TAFs can inhibit TAF activation and CXCL12 secretion. Subsequently, the binding of CXCL12 to CXCR4 on the surface of macrophages was blocked, which suppressed M2 polarization. The combination therapy of biomimetic nanoparticles and BMS-1 can significantly inhibit tumor growth and induce anti-tumor immune responses, as evidenced by an increase in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and a decrease in M2 macrophages and MDSCs. In addition, the levels of immunosuppressive cytokines, including IL-6, CCL5, CCL2, CXCL1, and TGF-β, were significantly downregulated following combination therapy. It was worth noting that the prepared SAB-PLGA@RTM exhibited biocompatibility. In summary, the combination therapy of biomimetic nanoparticles and ICIs demonstrated the potential to enhance anti-tumor effects by inducing immune responses.
