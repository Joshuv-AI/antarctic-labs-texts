Assessing the relationship between event-free and overall survival in randomized controlled trials of perioperative chemoimmunotherapy in gastroesophageal cancers
Authors: SM Durbin, N Teeyapun, MR Strickland, J Prince, A Gupta, H Park, I Sahin, H Zheng, SJ Klempner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerioperative chemoimmunotherapy is emerging as a global standard for nonmetastatic gastroesophageal cancers. Event-free survival (EFS) is a common endpoint in perioperative trials; however, its validity as a surrogate for overall survival (OS) in randomized controlled trials in the era of chemoimmunotherapy is not well established.Materials and methodsWe carried out a systematic review to identify randomized controlled trials evaluating neoadjuvant or perioperative therapy for gastroesophageal adenocarcinomas. Weighted linear regression of the published hazard ratios (HRs) was used to evaluate the correlation between EFS and OS. Criteria from the Institute for Quality and Efficiency in Health Care defined surrogacy strength. Sensitivity analyses evaluating heterogeneous tumor locations, perioperative systemic therapy only, and trials with clearly defined EFS were carried out.ResultsThirty trials comprising 11 453 participants were included in the main analysis. Trial-level analysis identified a positive relationship between EFS and OS (P < 0.001), corresponding to a correlation coefficient (R) of 0.83. Surrogate threshold effect analysis identified an HR for EFS of 1.18 or lower as a threshold at which the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval for OS HR excluded 1.0. EFS correlation with OS was preserved across sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsEFS is a valid surrogate for OS at the trial level in randomized controlled perioperative trials in modern nonmetastatic gastroesophageal trials, supporting the validity of intermediate endpoints for use in development of novel treatment strategies and regulatory considerations in gastroesophageal adenocarcinomas.Key words: event-free survival, gastroesophageal cancer, meta-analysis, overall survival, perioperative chemoimmunotherapy, surrogate endpoint

Introduction

Gastric, gastroesophageal junction, and esophageal adenocarcinomas (together GEA) are aggressive malignancies with high morbidity and mortality. Surgical resection of localized disease is a cornerstone of curative-intent treatment, but rates of recurrence remain high, with up to 60% of patients diagnosed with recurrent disease within 5 years after curative-intent surgery.1, 2, 3, 4 Perioperative chemotherapy and radiation-containing strategies have improved outcomes compared with surgery alone, but overall survival (OS) remains poor, with 5-year OS rates of 40% to 50%.5, 6, 7, 8

In advanced disease, the addition of biomarker-directed treatment, including immunotherapy and HER2-directed therapies, has improved outcomes substantially for patients.9,10 Recent trials have begun to move these therapeutics into the management of early-stage disease.11,12 Recently, the MATTERHORN phase III trial demonstrated a significant improvement in event-free survival (EFS) and OS when the anti-PD-L1 agent durvalumab was added to standard perioperative chemotherapy with 5-fluorouracil, docetaxel, and oxaliplatin (FLOT), in patients with resectable gastric cancer (GC) or gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) cancers.12

OS is a commonly accepted endpoint to definitively establish efficacy in resectable GEA but requires large trials with prolonged follow-up time. As the field advances rapidly, there has been increasing interest in the validity of intermediate endpoints as a surrogate measure for OS, particularly trial surrogacy level in randomized controlled trials, including EFS and disease-free survival, which may allow for accelerated evaluation and approval of novel treatment regimens.13,14 Wainberg et al.15 published an analysis of 15 trials evaluating neoadjuvant or perioperative systemic therapy for GC/GEJ adenocarcinoma. In this dataset, which included trials published up to 2020, EFS was found to be an adequate surrogate for OS. Importantly, this analysis did not include studies with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). ICIs have a distinct mechanism of action and distinct kinetics of tumor response, leading to questions regarding the validity of surrogate endpoints that have been designed to evaluate traditional cytotoxic chemotherapy. In fact, one meta-analysis of immunotherapy trials in advanced solid tumors found overall response rate and progression-free survival to be poor surrogates for OS, raising concern that alternative intermediate endpoints may be needed.16

We sought to analyze trial-level surrogacy between EFS and OS for resectable GEA using data from modern randomized controlled trials (RCTs), including those evaluating perioperative immunotherapy, to better evaluate the validity of intermediate endpoints in modern GEA trials.

Materials and methods

Search strategy and study selection criteria

A systematic literature review was conducted in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines to identify RCTs reporting treatment effects [hazard ratios (HRs)] for both EFS and OS outcomes of neoadjuvant or perioperative treatment of gastric adenocarcinoma, GEJ adenocarcinoma, and esophageal adenocarcinoma.17 Literature searches were carried out from database inception until November 2025 using Medline (via Pubmed) and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), including abstracts from clinicaltrials.gov, American Society of Clinical Oncology, and European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) between January 2018 and November 2025. The Medical Subject Headings and free-text keywords relevant to, but not limited to ‘gastric cancer’, ‘gastroesophageal junction cancer’, ‘esophageal adenocarcinoma’, ‘neoadjuvant treatment’, ‘perioperative treatment’, and ‘randomized controlled trial’ were used as search keywords.

Study endpoints

Trials reporting both EFS and OS, along with corresponding HRs, were included to evaluate the trial-level surrogacy relationship between EFS and OS based on treatment effects (HRs). Both earlier and more recent trials were considered to encompass the range of EFS definitions used across studies. A primary analysis was prespecified to incorporate the largest number of eligible studies to ensure a robust assessment of the EFS–OS association. This approach is consistent with established methods for evaluating surrogate endpoints at the trial level, which require randomized comparisons to estimate relative treatment effects. In addition, prespecified sensitivity analyses were additionally carried out according to EFS definition, trial phase, and treatment strategy to assess the robustness and consistency of the trial-level association between EFS and OS.

Statistical analyses

The trial-level surrogacy relationship between EFS and OS was evaluated using HRs extracted from RCTs meeting the inclusion criteria. Weighted linear regression on log-transformed HRs, with weights proportional to study sample size, was used to account for the log-scale distribution of HRs and heterogeneity across trials. The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the regression line were computed using standard error estimates from the weighted regression model. The coefficient of determination (R2) and its 95% CI were estimated using bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations. All analyses were carried out using R (version 4.5.1) with the boot and ggplot2 packages. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 (two-sided).

Assessment of trial-level surrogacy

Multiple thresholds have been proposed to define the strength of surrogate endpoints based on correlation coefficients (R) or coefficients of determination (R2). Prior studies have suggested that higher values of R or R2 indicate stronger surrogacy.18, 19, 20 To provide a conservative and CI-based interpretation of surrogacy strength, we adopted the criteria proposed by the Institute for Quality and Efficiency in Health Care (IQWiG) for the evaluation of surrogacy. In the IQWiG framework, high surrogacy is defined as a lower bound of the 95% CI of the correlation coefficient (R) ≥0.85, low surrogacy as an upper bound ≤0.70, and intermediate surrogacy as values between these thresholds.21 In our analysis, trial-level surrogacy between EFS and OS was assessed based on the strength of association between treatment effects on the surrogate endpoint (log HR for EFS) and the true endpoint (log HR for OS) using weighted linear regression of treatment effects on EFS [log(HR_EFS)] and OS [log(HR_OS)]. The following are metrics prespecified to characterize the trial-level association. This analytical framework is consistent with established methods for evaluating surrogate endpoints at the trial level, which require randomized comparisons to estimate relative treatment effects.

(i)Slope: the slope reflects the change in log(HR_OS) per unit change of log(HR_EFS). A slope >1 suggests that OS changes proportionally and is strongly correlated with EFS; however, an excessively high slope may indicate an outlier. A slope <1 implies weaker association between EFS and OS within the dataset.

Results

Search strategy and included trials

We identified a total of 623 manuscripts and 29 conference abstracts. After abstract and title screening and full-text review in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement, all RCTs meeting the predefined eligibility criteria were selected (Figure 1).17

PRISMA flow diagram of the study selection.

A total of 30 RCTs comprising 11 453 participants were included in the main analysis (Figure 1). The included RCTs were published between 2010 and 2025. Most studies were phase III trials (19 of 30, 63%), while two were phase II-III trials and nine were phase II trials. The trials enrolled patients from Asian and Western countries, reflecting global clinical practice. Sample sizes ranged from 43 to 1063 participants. Chemotherapy backbones predominantly consisted of fluoropyrimidine- and platinum-based regimens, and more recent trials incorporated novel therapeutic agents, including five trials evaluating ICIs and three trials evaluating targeted therapies (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100368). Median reported follow-up durations ranged from 9.6 months to 12 years. Twenty-eight of the 30 trials (93%) reported a median follow-up of at least 3 years, and 11 trials reported a median follow-up exceeding 5 years. All included trials reported HRs for both OS and EFS (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100368).

Definitions of EFS varied across studies, with most defined from randomization, with some trials defining EFS from initiation of neoadjuvant therapy; a small minority used alternative starting timepoints or did not report a definition (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100368).

EFS is an adequate trial-level surrogate for OS in gastroesophageal cancers

Trial-level analysis of RCTs demonstrated a statistically significant association between treatment effects on EFS and OS in primary analysis (Figure 2). Weighted linear regression showed a positive association between HRs for EFS and OS, with a slope of 0.68 (95% CI 0.50-0.85, P < 0.001). The intercept was −0.04 (95% CI −0.09 to 0.01, P = 0.09). The model explained 69% of the variability in log(HR_OS) (R2 = 0.69, 95% CI 0.44-0.88), corresponding to a trial-level correlation coefficient (R) of 0.83. Surrogate threshold effect (STE) analysis identified an HR for EFS of 1.18 or lower as a threshold at which the lower bound of the 95% CI for HR_OS excluded 1.0.

EFS and OS retain surrogacy across study designs, populations, and endpoint definitions

Sensitivity analyses across multiple subsets of trials with varying designs, populations, and endpoint definitions were carried out to validate the consistency of EFS as a surrogate endpoint for OS.

Heterogeneous tumor location group

This sensitivity analysis included 19 more heterogeneous trials, not limited to a single anatomic location (GC compared with GEJ cancer compared with esophageal adenocarcinoma) (Figure 3; Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100368). In weighted linear regression, the slope relating log(HR_EFS) to log(HR_OS) was 0.8096 (95% CI 0.6362-0.9829, P < 0.0001). The intercept was −0.003 (95% CI −0.052 to 0.046, P = 0.899). The coefficient of determination was R2 = 0.8425 (95% CI 0.699-0.9347), corresponding to a trial-level correlation coefficient of R = 0.92. An STE identified an HR_EFS value of 1.08 as the lower threshold at which the lower bound of the 95% CI for HR_OS crossed 1.0.

Strictly perioperative design group

This sensitivity analysis included 16 trials specifically evaluating perioperative chemotherapy, excluding designs that allowed either pre- or post-operative treatment (Figure 4; Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100368). In weighted linear regression, the slope relating log(HR_EFS) to log(HR_OS) was 0.6528 (95% CI 0.419-0.8866, P < 0.0001). The intercept was −0.0538 (95% CI −0.1121 to 0.0044, P = 0.06784). The coefficient of determination was R2 = 0.6865 (95% CI 0.2647-0.9181), corresponding to a trial-level correlation coefficient of R = 0.83. The STE analysis identified an HR-EFS value of 1.272 or lower as the threshold at which the lower bound of the 95% CI for HR_OS crossed 1.0.

EFS defined from randomization group

This sensitivity analysis included 20 studies that defined EFS as beginning from randomization (Figure 5). In weighted linear regression, the slope relating log(HR_EFS) to log(HR_OS) was 0.6536 (95% CI 0.4551-0.8521, P < 0.0001). The intercept was −0.049 (95% CI −0.1071 to 0.009, P = 0.093). The coefficient of determination was R2 = 0.6691 (95% CI 0.32-0.9064). The STE analysis identified an HR_EFS value of 1.272.

Overall, the results of sensitivity analysis of three groups were consistent with those of the primary analysis, supporting EFS as a valid surrogate for OS among a heterogeneous trial population, a more modern perioperative treatment paradigm, and when a stringent definition of EFS was applied.

To further assess the robustness and consistency of treatment effects, we conducted three additional subgroup analyses, including trials defining EFS from treatment initiation (Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100368), phase III RCTs (Supplementary Figure S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100368), and immunotherapy-based trials (Supplementary Figure S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmogo.2026.100368). Strong associations between HR_EFS and HR_OS were maintained in studies defining EFS from initiation of neoadjuvant therapy (slope 0.83, 95% CI 0.59-1.08; R2 = 0.92, 95% CI 0.53-1.00; STE = 1.145) and in phase III trials (slope 0.79, 95% CI 0.59-0.99; R2 = 0.79, 95% CI 0.58-0.93; STE = 1.113). In contrast, the immunotherapy-based subgroup demonstrated a more moderate association (slope 0.49, 95% CI −0.21 to 1.18; R2 = 0.62, 95% CI 0.02-1.00), although this analysis was limited by the small number of included studies (n = 5), and the STE could not be estimated because no crossing point was identified within the evaluable range.

Discussion

Since the MATTERHORN publication and subsequent Food and Drug Administration approval of perioperative durvalumab with FLOT, the incorporation of ICIs represents a new standard of care for operable gastroesophageal patients.12,22 The majority of future studies are likely to build on the MATTERHORN regimen of durvalumab in combination with FLOT chemotherapy, and most ongoing nonmetastatic GEA trials such as ASTRUM-006 utilize EFS as a primary and/or coprimary endpoint.23,24 This underscores the need to confirm trial-level associations between EFS and OS in RCTs, particularly given the differing kinetics of ICIs. Leveraging the most modern trials, we add to the literature and demonstrate the validity of EFS as an intermediate and surrogate endpoint at the trial level in RCTs in the evaluation of immunotherapy-containing strategies. Among 30 included trials, comprising over 11 000 patients, we found a statistically significant association between trial-specific EFS and OS, with a corresponding correlation coefficient (R) of 0.83.

Although other meta-analyses have used variable criteria to assess surrogacy, we selected the IQWiG definition of proof of validity of surrogate endpoints and found a medium-high correlation between EFS and OS. In sensitivity analyses evaluating trials permitting heterogeneous tumor location, perioperative systemic therapy only, and clearly defined EFS, this correlation was preserved. Importantly, high correlation of surrogate endpoints with OS is relatively rare in oncology clinical trials, with the majority of published meta-analyses of RCTs reporting low and medium correlation.25 However, recent work by Wainberg et al.15 found EFS to be a reasonable surrogate for OS in gastric and GEJ adenocarcinoma, with a correlation coefficient of 0.72. Our analysis incorporated practice-changing studies that have redefined the standard of care for GEA since this prior work. This includes ESOPEC, which established perioperative FLOT as the optimal chemotherapy backbone, as well as MATTERHORN, which has led to the adoption of durvalumab in combination with chemotherapy.8,12 Here, we show that EFS remains a reasonable intermediate outcome, with medium-high correlation with OS, even with the inclusion of the addition of targeted biologics. Importantly, even when restricted to the available immunotherapy-containing trials (n = 5) the trial-level surrogacy is retained.

This study has several important limitations. Firstly, the studies included variable definitions of EFS and variable schedules of follow-up assessments. In contemporary trials evaluating neoadjuvant or perioperative treatment of nonadvanced GC, the starting point of EFS is most commonly defined as the time from randomization.12,26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33 In contrast, earlier trials, particularly those conducted before 2020, often defined EFS as beginning at the initiation of neoadjuvant therapy. This heterogeneity may limit the ability to make cross-trial comparisons and best define the relationship between EFS and OS. However, we included a sensitivity analysis of only those trials with EFS defined as beginning from neoadjuvant therapy, which showed that the correlation between EFS and OS was preserved in this more modern definition. Additionally, the sample sizes, especially among some of the recent smaller trials incorporating immunotherapies and anti-VEGF therapy, limit the ability to include stratification by patient-level characteristics. In addition, follow-up maturity varied across included studies. However, most RCTs reported relatively mature follow-up durations (∼≥3 years), which are likely sufficient to capture the majority of OS events in this setting. Only a small number of studies had shorter median follow-up durations, and their relatively limited contribution to the overall dataset makes a substantial impact on the observed trial-level association between EFS and OS less likely. Nevertheless, longer follow-up from more recent trials will be important to further validate these findings. Further stratification may allow better understanding regarding the limitations of EFS as a surrogate measure in specific populations and better refine the individual-level surrogacy of EFS and OS. Further refinement of this analysis will be needed as the field of perioperative management continues to grow and additional targeted therapies, including anti-HER-2 and anti-Claudin-18.2 therapies, are evaluated in this setting.34 Importantly, our analysis was restricted to RCTs, as evaluation of surrogate endpoints at the trial level typically requires randomized comparisons to estimate relative treatment effects. Therefore, our findings may not be directly generalizable to single-arm studies, for which alternative approaches to surrogate endpoint evaluation may be required. Future research is warranted to evaluate surrogate endpoints in single-arm study designs to avoid overgeneralization of these findings to nonrandomized settings.

In summary, this study builds upon the existing literature evaluating the correlation between EFS and OS in the perioperative management of GEA. We demonstrate that EFS remains a reasonable surrogate measure for OS at the trial level in RCTs, supporting the validity of intermediate endpoints for use in development of novel treatment strategies and regulatory considerations in gastroesophageal adenocarcinomas. Further analysis will be needed as the management of resectable GEA continues to evolve, but it remains important to bring new agents with encouraging activity to this difficult to treat disease.
