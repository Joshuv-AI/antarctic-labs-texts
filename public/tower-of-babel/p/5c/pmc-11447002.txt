Nanoporous Metatitanic acid on γ-Al2O3 aerogel for higher CO2 adsorption capacity and lower energy consumption
Authors: Abolfazl Shokri, Shahrokh Shahhosseini, Amin Bazyari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarbon dioxide capture has become an important issue in reducing atmospheric heat these days. In this study, adsorption of carbon dioxide by aerogel Gamma Alumina-Metatitanic Acid has been investigated and optimized. Morphological and structural analyses such as BET, FESEM, FT-IR, and XRD have also been conducted. In addition, Response surface methodology has been applied in order to achieve the optimal conditions, using a five-level Central composite design. The highest amount of adsorption, 12.874 (mmol/g), was recorded at a temperature of 20 (°C), pressure of 7 (bar), and 25 (%wt) of Metatitanic Acid. This was approximately 11.46% and 4.84% higher than those of mesoporous MgO and 4Azeolite, respectively. Regeneration of the adsorbent was also studied at different temperatures and process durations. Metatitanic acid, as a catalyst, reduces the temperature and regeneration time of the adsorbent by creating active sites and surface hydroxyl groups. It also lowers the required activation energy and enhances the thermal conductivity of the composite material. The optimal result was achieved at a temperature of 100 (°C) and a duration of 30 (min). Finally, isothermal and thermodynamic experiments were conducted to establish the most accurate predictive model and conditions, including Enthalpy, Entropy, and Gibbs free energy. The results indicate that the Freundlich model aligned well with the laboratory findings. Additionally, the negative values of Enthalpy, Entropy, and Gibbs free energy suggested that the adsorption process was physical, exothermic, and spontaneous.Keywords: Aerogel Gamma Alumina, Metatitanic acid, Adsorbent regeneration, CO2 adsorption, RSM, OptimizationSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Engineering

Introduction

In recent years, carbon dioxide emissions have been a primary contributor to the global warming1. Today, due to the use of fossil fuels in industry, factories, and power plants, the concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere has been significantly increased2. There are various methods of post-combustion separation, such as adsorption, cryogenic separation, membrane separation, and absorption. Each mentioned method of attraction has its own advantages and disadvantages3.

The advancements in nanomaterial science in recent years have led to significant progress in CO2adsorption and reduced heat capacity4. Nanoporous materials have garnered significant attention due to their ability to adjust surface, physical, and chemical properties for various applications5. Some of these nanoporous materials, such as Metal-Organic Frameworks (MOFs)6,7, Covalent Organic Frameworks (COFs)8, metal nanoparticles9, and metal oxides10, have been used for the adsorption process11. Nanomaterials generally exhibit superior CO2absorption performance compared to conventional absorbents, but they are hindered by lower selectivity12. Adding nanomaterials to the base solvent can not only enhance stability and reduce the solvent vapor pressure, but also lead to a significant increase in mass and heat transfer13. In light of this, various types of nanoparticles, including SiO2, Al2O3, and TiO2 particles, were employed to enhance the mass transfer of CO2absorption by dispersing them in aqueous solutions14. Synthesis of new adsorbents involved immobilizing DES on nanoparticles like zeolite and silica, as these support materials are cost-effective and readily accessible15. Several researches were conducted to develop CO2adsorbers functionalized with DESs16. In a study, a porous carbon substrate with DES was investigated, resulting in an adsorbent with a CO2adsorption capacity of 2-3.7 (mmol/g)17. In another study, the compatibility of ChCl-Urea for CO2adsorption with a 97.33% recovery rate was reported18. Ghazali et al. improved the CO2adsorption capacity of mesoporous silica by impregnating it with deep eutectic solvent ChCl: U:PEI19.

Some adsorbents, such as nano-sized zeolites, exhibit reduced performance in the presence of water due to the closure of their porous structure20. One way to enhance CO2adsorption capacity is by loading solid adsorbents containing alkali metals onto various supports21. Impregnating a porous matrix with K2CO3/Na2CO3increases the adsorption capacity and reaction speed, but it requires a regeneration temperature of about 300 (°C)13. Although K2CO3 has faster reaction rate and higher capacity for CO2 adsorption than Na2CO3, Na2CO3is more cost-effective and readily available13. Various research studies have been conducted on different types of supports in the CO2adsorption process22,23. Impregnating K2CO3with MgO resulted in a good adsorption capacity, despite the high regeneration temperature of 300 (°C)13. A research was also conducted on the adsorption capacity of K2CO3 on Al2O3, Activated Carbon (AC), and TiO2, which were found to be almost similar24–26. While impregnations on other bases such as CaO, ultrastable zeolite Y (USY), zeolite (NaX) exchanged with cesium (CsNaX), and SiO2were unsuccessful13.

In the synthesis of γ-Al2O3, the sol-gel method is used to control the properties such as pore volume, pore size distribution, and specific surface area at low temperatures. Polyethylene oxide (PEO) has higher solubility in polar solvents due to its lower molecular weight27. In the synthesis of alumina using low molecular weight PEO, it demonstrated excellent results in achieving a high specific surface area and a high pore volume28. Additionally, the use of γ-Al2O3adsorbent as a support was considered because of its abrasion resistance, mechanical strength, and high porosity29. In the research, the effects of Na2CO3/MgO/Al2O3, K2CO3/MgO/Al2O3, and K2CO3/TiO2/Al2O3 were investigated, and they exhibited a high level of effectiveness attributed to the presence of Al2O3porous adsorbent30. Furthermore, new CO2 adsorbents have several drawbacks, such as intricate synthesis, susceptibility to corrosion, high costs, and instability at high pressure and temperature during regeneration processes. Therefore, efforts have been made to identify non-alkaline adsorbents (mineral adsorbents) for CO2adsorption31. Although activated carbons and advanced zeolites have high adsorption capacity, low cost, and fast adsorption kinetics, their CO2adsorption selectivity values are low. Therefore, they need to be modified for specific applications25,32.

Titanium-based materials are considered to be excellent adsorbers in terms of both resistance and price33. In a study, TiO2 was combined with 3-triethoxysilylpropylamine, which increased the CO2adsorption capacity to 6.4 (mmol/g) at 35 (bar) and 25 (°C)34. There is an urgent demand from the industries for adsorption of the polluting gases, and developing an adsorbent that can achieve higher levels of adsorption while requiring lower energy for regeneration and reuse. Synthetic adsorbents require high temperatures for the extended periods of time for regeneration. Therefore, the development of an adsorbent that can address this issue is of great importance. In this study, aerogel γ-Al2O3 was utilized as a support, and TiO(OH)2 was employed as a catalyst with varying support/catalyst ratios to enhance the CO2 adsorption capacity. The mesoporous structure of γ-Al2O3 provides a large surface area that enhances the physical adsorption of CO2 molecules through van der Waals forces. Additionally, TiO(OH)2 contains surface hydroxyl groups that interact with CO2. Consequently, CO2 molecules bond with titanium sites in TiO(OH)2, forming titanium carbonate species that aid in CO2 adsorption. In general, γ-Al2O3 stabilizes the reaction sites of TiO(OH)2 and inhibits aggregation. TiO(OH)2 also determines the minimum temperature and time required for regenerating the adsorbent. Additionally, the Response surface methodology (RSM) software was utilized to identify the suitable model and to optimize the process. The input parameters include temperature, pressure, and the ratio of TiO(OH)2 to γ-Al2O3, while the output parameter is the amount of CO2 adsorption. It was observed that TiO(OH)2 not only increases the number of active sites and CO2 adsorption capacity, but also reduces the regeneration temperature and time, maximizing the adsorbent utilization during multiple consecutive cycles. In this study, the thermodynamic characteristics and CO2 adsorption isotherms were also investigated.

Materials and methods

Materials

γ-Al2O3 mesopores were selected due to their significant surface area and the stabilization of TiO(OH)2 reaction sites, which enhance the physical adsorption of CO2 molecules. Additionally, TiO(OH)2 contains surface hydroxyl groups that interact with CO2. For the synthesis of Gamma Alumina Aerogel support (γ-Al2O3) and Metatitanic Acid (TiO(OH)2), the following materials have been used: Aluminum isopropoxide (purity > 98% from Merck), Ethanol (purity 99.5% from Merck), Polyethylene glycol (purity 99% from Sigma-Aldrich), and Titanium isopropoxide (purity 99% prepared from Merck).

Preparation

Gamma Alumina-Metatitanic acid (Al-MT) adsorbents were prepared in three steps as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Synthesis steps for (a) aerogel γ-Al2O3, (b) TiO(OH)2, and (c) composite.

In the first step, 5.66 (ml) of ethanol is combined with 10.48 (ml) of distilled water, and then 6.60 (g) of alumina precursor (Aluminum isopropoxide) is added to the water-ethanol mixture as a solvent. The solution is dissolved for 30 (min) with stirring at a speed of 450 (rpm) (hydrolysis process). Afterward, 6.46 (g) of Polyethylene glycol were added to the previous solution. In order to achieve a uniform gel in the above solution and reduce the speed of the aluminum isopropoxide reaction, all steps of Alumina aerogel synthesis were performed at 0 (°C) (using a water and ice bath). To form a gel and dry it, the solution is left in the oven for 3 days. The calcination process was carried out to activate the catalyst in two stages: (1) from ambient temperature to 220 (°C) and (2) from 220 to 600 (°C)27.

In the second step, a Titanium isopropoxide precursor was used to prepare TiO(OH)2catalyst. As shown in Fig. 1(b), initially, 6.04941 (ml) of precursor is added to 9.6730 (ml) of deionized water at room temperature and stirred at a speed of 600 (rpm) for 4 (hr). Then, the solution was centrifuged at 15,000 (rpm) for 8 (min) to induce sedimentation. Finally, the precipitate is washed three times, first with ethanol and then with water. The final sediment is then placed in the oven and heated at 100 (°C) for 24 (hr)51.

In the third step, composite samples are formed according to Fig. 1(c) from calcined γ-Al2O3 aerogel (Al) and TiO(OH)2 (MT) powder in different proportions. The experiment involved testing various ratios of catalysts (MT) and (Al) in a solution of 70 (ml) of deionized water at 70 (°C), followed by drying the samples at 600 (rpm). The dried samples were then placed in an oven at 100 (°C) for 12 (hr). The synthesized samples included pure (MT), as well as mixtures containing 75%, 50%, and 25% MT with pure Al. The samples were named Al-MT(100), Al-MT(75), Al-MT(50), Al-MT(25), and Al-MT(0), respectively. Then, the samples from the previous stage are thoroughly crushed and then sieved through a 60-micron mesh.

Characterization

The surface area, volume, and pore size were measured using a BELSORP MINI II JAPAN device through BET analysis. N2 adsorption-desorption isotherm data were collected at 77 (K). The specific surface area and pore volume of the samples were determined using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) and Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) methods, respectively. The chemical functional groups of the synthesized samples were identified through Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopic analysis (FT-IR) conducted using the FT-IR Spectrum1 device. The crystal structure of the pure and composite samples was determined using X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) in the WinXPOW diffractometer. The peaks were recorded in the range of 2θ from 20 to 80 degrees with a step of 0.06 degrees and a counting time of 1 (sec/step). The surface morphology was investigated using a MIRA-TESCAN FESEM. FESEM images were captured using an accelerating voltage of 15.0 (kV).

Results and discussion

Characterization results

Textural properties

BET tests were conducted to examine the physical properties of composite samples. As shown in Figs. 3 and 4, the graphs of N2 adsorption-desorption isotherm and pore size distribution of the samples indicate that all these N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms are of type (IV). This suggests that the samples are mesoporous materials with large gap pores. According to Fig. 3(b and c), N2 adsorption values for two samples, Al-MT(50) and Al-MT(75), were relatively lower, while the N2 adsorption isotherms for Al-MT(25) (Fig. 3a) exhibited much higher N2 adsorption values. The pore size distribution diagrams in Fig. 4(a and b) show that the average pore size of Al-MT(75) and Al-MT(50) falls within the range of 1.21 to 1.54 (nm). In contrast, Al-MT(25) exhibits a relatively wider pore size distribution ranging to 1.64 (nm). In addition, upon examining the values of dVp/drp for Al-MT(25), Al-MT(75), and Al-MT(50), it is evident that the value of Al-MT(25) is significantly higher. According to Table 3, which presents the textural properties of the samples, the BET surface area for the five samples is 443.24, 380.7, 326.52, 298.11, and 290.15 (m2/g), respectively. Additionally, Al-MT(25) and Al-MT(50) exhibit high micropore volumes of 0.5732 and 0.4904 (cm3/g), respectively. The high surface area and pore volume of Al-MT(25) indicate a more favorable activation process for pore expansion, which helps to create more channels and surface active sites for CO2 adsorption. As a result, significant variation in their textural properties affects the performance of CO2adsorption27.

The composite characteristics can be attributed to several factors such as the interaction between components and their formation process54. (1) During the formation of the composite, TiO(OH)2 particles partially block the pores of γ-Al2O3, reducing the total available surface area and effective pore volume. (2) TiO(OH)2 also fills some of the smaller pores of γ-Al2O3, effectively reducing the average pore size55. (3) Mixing and synthesis processes can lead to the accumulation of TiO(OH)2 particles, causing clustering that reduces specific surface area due to less accessible surface area in the larger clusters. (4) Mechanical forces applied during mixing can also densify the materials, reducing pore volume and specific surface area. (5) Strong interactions at the interface between TiO(OH)2 and γ-Al2O3result in more compact structures, leading to smaller pore sizes and surface area42.

XRD analysis

Figure 5shows the X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) pattern for various samples. For the combined samples, the positions of the characteristic peaks correspond to those of the pure samples35. The change in the characteristic peaks of samples made of TiO(OH)2 and γ-Al2O3 can be attributed to: (1) chemical interactions and the formation of new phases, (2) changes in crystallization, including amorphization and phase transformation, (3) strain, defects, and lattice distortions. The analysis of the peaks in the XRD pattern of the composites is linked to the crystal structures of the individual components and any new phases formed due to their interaction. TiO(OH)2 exhibits peaks indicating its amorphous or low crystalline nature. However, the existing crystalline phases are associated with titanium oxide phases like anatase or rutile, depending on the processing conditions. γ-Al2O3 is a type of aluminum oxide with a unique crystal structure. The peaks at 38.13°, 40.55°, 46°, 64.74°, and 66.1° can be attributed to the phases in the composite material. These peaks correspond to transitional alumina phase such as γ-Al2O3, titanium oxide like anatase (TiO2) or rutile (TiO2), TiO2 or γ-Al2O3, and crystalline phases γ-Al2O3, TiO2, or γ-Al2O333. All the samples exhibit more peaks compared to the pure samples.

FT-IR analysis

The FT-IR spectrum in Fig. 6 compares five samples. In the spectra of pure γ-Al2O3 and TiO(OH)2, There are several significant peaks and valleys that can be identified and analyzed56. In the FT-IR γ-Al2O3 analysis, the characteristic peaks in the range of 600.64-801.97 (cm−1) can be attributed to Al-O vibrations. In other words, these peaks are related to the bending and stretching vibrations of aluminum-oxygen bonds in the alumina network. These vibrations are due to the presence of different Al-O bond environments in the octahedral and tetrahedral sites of the alumina structure. This range also includes the bending vibrations of Al-O-Al bonds, which are caused by the bending movements of oxygen atoms that bridge between two aluminum atoms57,58. The peak at about 3390–3460 (cm−1) corresponds to the O-H stretching vibration of adsorbed water molecules, confirming the presence of water in pure samples59. The peak at about 600–800 (cm−1) corresponds to Ti-O, confirming the presence of titanium60. In the spectrum of γ-Al2O3 modified with MT, several additional peaks and troughs can be observed. When comparing the spectrum of modified samples with unmodified samples, the changes in intensity and peak position are evident. Also, the ratio of the contribution of macropores to the total porosity of the sample, as determined by N2 adsorption and desorption isotherms, was 26.83%, falling within the mesopore range.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

FESEM images for pure and the best composite samples are depicted in Fig. 7(a-c). According to the FESEM image of γ-Al2O3 (Fig. 7(c)), it is evident that the macro-holes only marginally contribute to the overall porosity of the γ-Al2O343. This is indicated by the variations in light and dark areas, which reflect changes in the topography and composition of the composite sample surfaces. Bright and dark regions correspond to high and low points or holes in the Al structure, respectively61. One of the important features of the composite adsorbent is the presence of pores in the structure of the sample, as it provides sites for CO2 adsorption. γ-Al2O3 as a base provides a large surface area. Changes in the surface topography of composite sample, such as reduced pore size and blocked pores, indicate the presence of TiO(OH)2 particles in the main structure. TiO(OH)2 can enhance adsorption properties by creating active sites, albeit at the expense of reducing the surface area. The FESEM image reveals a more intricate surface with TiO(OH)2particles. Some pores are blocked or diminished in size, resulting in a decrease in overall porosity62. FESEM analysis of pure and the composite samples provide a new perspective on the surface topography changes caused by the incorporation of TiO(OH)2 into γ-Al2O3. By comparing the images and conducting studies, it can be evaluated that TiO(OH)2 affects the pore structure, surface roughness, and overall porosity. These changes directly affect the performance of the material in adsorbing CO2through the balance between the surface area and the accessibility of the active sites63.

The result of this study is to determine the quantity and impact of the influencing factors on CO2 adsorption capacity.

Co2 adsorption mechanism

The lower specific surface area and pore size of the composite samples compared to their pure counterparts affect the mechanism of CO2 adsorption for γ-Al2O3-TiO(OH)2adsorbent. This mechanism includes66: (1) the mesoporous structure of γ-Al2O3 creating a large surface area that significantly enhances the physical adsorption of CO2 molecules through van der Waals forces, (2) TiO(OH)2 catalyst containing surface hydroxyl groups that can interact with CO2, leading to the formation of bicarbonate or carbonate species on the catalyst surface. (3) Consequently, CO2 molecules interact with titanium sites in TiO(OH)2, potentially forming titanium carbonate species, which facilitates CO2 adsorption and increases accessibility for CO2 molecules. (4) γ-Al2O3 stabilizes the reactive sites of TiO(OH)2and prevents aggregation67.

Therefore, modifying γ-Al2O3 with TiO(OH)2 enhances CO2 adsorption capacity and selectivity, attributed to the aerogel’s high surface area and porosity, as well as the active sites of TiO(OH)2. Additionally, determining the ideal ratio of TiO(OH)2 to γ-Al2O3maximizes synergistic effects and enhances overall performance68.

Cyclic function

In the process of CO2adsorption by sorbents, one of the factors that attracts the attention of all researchers is the speed of regeneration and the ability to reuse the adsorbent multiple times12. Based on the findings from the previous section, it was determined that the Al-MT(25) adsorbent exhibited the highest amount of adsorption under the same conditions compared to all other adsorbents73. After determining the maximum amount of adsorption for the optimal state of Al-MT, various experiments have been conducted on the reconstruction of that optimal adsorbent74. Various factors, such as pressure, temperature, humidity, and regeneration time, affect the regeneration of the adsorbent75,76. In this study, the chosen adsorbent was regenerated after synthesis and adsorption testing under the conditions of 20 (°C) temperature and 5.5 (bar) pressure. This was achieved by subjecting it to 70 (°C) temperature and ambient pressure for 3 (hr) in a conventional oven. It was tested again under the specified conditions. These steps were repeated and tested two additional times at temperatures of 100 (°C) and 130 (°C) under the same ambient pressure and for a duration of 3 (hr). The adsorption results are presented in Table 13. According to Table 13, which aimed to optimize the regeneration temperature of the adsorber, the optimal regeneration temperature mode of 100 (°C) was selected. Although the temperature of 130 (°C) yielded better results, the adsorption did not differ significantly from that at the regeneration temperature of 100 (°C). Therefore, the optimum regeneration temperature of 100 (°C) was chosen.

Metatitanic acid reduces the temperature and regeneration time of the adsorbent through different mechanisms as follows51,77: (1) By creating the sites, TiO(OH)2 enhances CO2 adsorption through chemical interactions, resulting in stronger adsorption. This allows faster regeneration at lower temperatures by reversing these interactions, compared to simpler physical adsorption mechanisms. (2) The surface hydroxyl groups in TiO(OH)2 can form reversible bonds with CO2. During regeneration, these bonds break at lower temperatures, reducing the energy needed for the removal. (3) TiO(OH)2 acts as a catalyst, accelerating the removal process. This catalyst lowers the regeneration temperature and time by decreasing the required activation energy. (4) The inclusion of TiO(OH)2in the sample enhances the thermal conductivity of the composite material. This improvement leads to more uniform and efficient heating during reconstruction, reducing the overall time and necessary temperature78. (5) The pore structure of the composite material with TiO(OH)2 differs from the pure state, creating an optimal pore size distribution. This change results in a quicker release of CO2 during the removal process. (6) TiO(OH)2 helps prevent pore blockage during CO2 adsorption, ensuring the adsorbent remains available during regeneration. This feature reduces the time and temperature needed for complete removal. (7) Composite samples contain active sites that facilitate easier disposal, potentially decreasing the energy required to break the bonds formed with CO279.

Conclusion

The adsorption of CO2 by γ-Al2O3- TiO(OH)2 was studied. γ-Al2O3 was synthesized and combined with MT catalyst. Mesoporous γ-Al2O3 provides a substantial surface area for the physical adsorption of CO2 molecules through van der Waals forces. In contrast, the TiO(OH)2 catalyst, which contains surface hydroxyl groups, can interact with CO2, facilitating the formation of bicarbonate or carbonate species. Additionally, γ-Al2O3 stabilizes the reaction sites of TiO(OH)2 and prevents their accumulation. Consequently, the high surface area and porosity of γ-Al2O3, combined with the active sites of TiO(OH)2, enhance the CO2 adsorption capacity and selectivity. Identifying the optimal ratio of TiO(OH)2 to γ-Al2O3 is crucial for maximizing synergistic effects and improving overall performance. Adsorption tests were conducted using a batch reactor. Structural analyses, such as BET, SEM, FT-IR, and XRD, were performed on various samples. After comparing the results with the references, the accuracy of the synthesis was confirmed. DOE method was used to develop a mathematical equation to model the process and then optimize and determine the effective components. A five-level CCD was used to conduct the experiment, which involved 20 experiments under different input conditions. The optimization operation revealed that the highest adsorption occurred at a temperature of 20 (°C), pressure of 7 (bar), and weight% of 25% for MT, resulting in a value of 12.874 (mmol/g). The factors influencing the response were determined based on three-dimensional grids, including pressure, temperature, and weight% of MT. Additionally, research was conducted to determine the ideal temperature and duration for regeneration the adsorbent. The adsorbent could be regenerated at a temperature of 100 (°C) for 30 (min) in a conventional oven. Resuscitation tests were conducted five times, and the results indicated that the response did not exhibit considerable changes in the adsorption. At the end, thermodynamic and isotherm calculations were performed to determine the constant parameters and the type of adsorption process. Considering the negative values, it could be concluded that the adsorption was spontaneous, exothermic, and physical.
