A Fixed-Dose Solution for a Complex Problem
Authors: Quan M. Bui, Max Jason
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

In the United States, heart failure (HF) with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, with an estimated 5-year survival of 25%.1 Fortunately, guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) has demonstrated clear benefits in reducing mortality and HF hospitalizations.2 These benefits are additive with patients who receive all 4 pillars of GDMT experiencing the most favorable outcomes. However, data from contemporary HFrEF registries reveal persistently low uptake of quadruple therapy at suboptimal doses, a treatment gap that has contributed to the rising HF-related mortality since 2012.1,3,4 Therefore, addressing this implementation gap is an urgent clinical and public health priority.

Among the strategies proposed to improve GDMT uptake in HFrEF is the use of fixed-dose combination medications, or polypills. These formulations aim to improve medication adherence, reduce pill burden, and simplify treatment. Most of the supporting evidence for polypills comes from the secondary prevention of coronary artery disease, where combinations typically include an aspirin, a statin, and an antihypertensive.5,6 For example, the SECURE (Secondary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease in the Elderly) trial showed that in postmyocardial infarction patients, a polypill (aspirin, atorvastatin, ramipril) reduced the composite cardiovascular outcome by 24% at 3 years, with improved medication adherence and fewer discontinuations compared to usual care.6 Thus, the polypill has been extrapolated to HFrEF as a potential tool to overcome patient-level barriers to GDMT uptake. Early findings from an ongoing trial suggest feasibility and acceptability of a polypill strategy in the HFrEF population.7,8

In this issue of JACC: Advances, Chen et al9 present a rigorously designed mixed-methods study that integrates both patient and provider perspectives to inform the development of a multilevel implementation bundle for a GDMT polypill in HFrEF. Through semistructured interviews with 9 patients and 22 providers, the authors identified 4 key themes: 1) the current state of HFrEF care; 2) awareness of new innovations; 3) assessing innovation appropriateness; and 4) building competency in HFrEF care. This study builds on the authors' prior needs assessment work by applying implementation science methods to translate stakeholder-identified barriers into a structured supporting polypill adoption in HFrEF.10 The authors employed the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research-Expert Recommendations for Implementation Change matching tool to develop implementation strategies across domains such as facilitation, staged implementation plan, stakeholder tailoring, quality management, knowledge brokering, and team-based care. They further applied mechanism mapping to outline a structured pathway for polypill introduction, with facilitation identified as the central strategy. Facilitation relied on identification of implementation “champions” responsible for engaging stakeholders and supporting technical integration.

This study makes an important contribution to the field of implementation science in cardiovascular medicine. It integrates proactive implementation planning, a welcomed departure from the traditional model in which implementation is considered only after clinical trial completion. Nonetheless, the study has some limitations. The proposed multilevel implementation bundle lacks specificity in addressing key barriers identified in the authors’ earlier needs assessment, particularly with regard to provider adoption (ie, concerns about rapid GDMT titration) and systemic challenges (ie, insurance coverage, pharmacy availability).10 Most provider participants (12 of 22) were general cardiologists, and only 3 were advanced HF specialists. Moreover, all were from 2 academic institutions, limiting generalizability. Notably, key members of the multidisciplinary care team, such as pharmacists and nurses who are often instrumental in GDMT titration and transitions of care, were not included. Their absence limits insight into real-world operational challenges and opportunities.

Beyond the scope of this study, a more fundamental question remains: Is a fixed-dose combination polypill the solution to the complex challenge of GDMT uptake in HFrEF? While the appeal of a simplified regimen is undeniable, particularly for improving adherence and reducing pill burden for patients, it may also exacerbate provider- and system-level barriers. Unlike secondary prevention of coronary artery disease where fixed-dose combinations can be effective with minimal titration, HFrEF treatment is much more dynamic, requiring frequent, individualized dose adjustments based on blood pressure, renal function, and patient tolerance. Fixed-dose combinations lack the adaptability required to manage side effects, accommodate lab-based contraindications, and account for real-world cost variability. As such, a polypill may be best suited in resource-limited environments or a narrow subset of “goldilocks” patients, those with ample blood pressure and minimal comorbidities.

While polypills may eventually prove beneficial in select settings, large-scale validation in diverse HFrEF populations is still lacking. In the meantime, we should prioritize implementation of proven, multifaceted strategies that are already available and cost-effective.11 Multidisciplinary GDMT clinics, protocol-driven titration algorithms, and patient-centered decision support tools have demonstrated efficacy in increasing GDMT uptake.2 For example, the STRONG-HF (Safety, tolerability and efficacy of up-titration of guideline-directed medical therapies for acute heart failure) trial showed that rapid postdischarge GDMT intensification through frequent in-person clinic visits significantly reduced the composite of HF readmissions or all-cause mortality.12 Furthermore, economic modeling suggests that the STRONG-HF approach is highly cost-effective, with initial startup costs offset by reduced HF hospitalizations.11 Similarly, the TEAM-HF trial demonstrated that embedding a multidisciplinary GDMT clinic embedded within a general cardiology clinic not only improved quadruple therapy uptake but also improved NYHA functional class, N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide levels, and left ventricular ejection fraction.13 This is particularly relevant as the number of HF patients continues to outpace the availability of advanced HF specialists, leading to increase reliance on general cardiology clinics to care for these patients. Other successful implementation strategies include the EPIC-HF (Electronically Delivered Patient-Activation Tool for Intensification of Medications for Chronic Heart Failure with Reduced Ejection Fraction) trial, which demonstrated that empowering patients through a simple previsit video could increase GDMT intensification by reducing provider inertia.14 The success of this tool was rooted in early engagement of stakeholders and user-centered design. In addition, the TURING-HF (Therapy Using Remote Monitoring and Artificial Intelligence in Patients with Heart Failure) trial found that a novel HF-artificial intelligence tool integrated with remote patient monitoring (blood pressure readings) could increase GDMT use and emulate the decision-making process of a cardiologist.15 These trials underscore the fact that we do not lack effective implementation tools.

Until polypills are validated, our focus should remain on implementing the proven multifaceted strategies already available to maximize GDMT uptake and improve outcomes for patients living with HFrEF. For now, the solution to optimizing GDMT is not a magic pill; it is the commitment to implement what we already know works.
