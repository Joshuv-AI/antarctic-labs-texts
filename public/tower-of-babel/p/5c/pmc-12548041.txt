The comparative efficacy of 12-mo of alendronate and raloxifene in maintaining denosumab-induced improvement in peripheral volumetric BMD, microarchitecture, and estimated strength
Authors: Benjamin Z Leder, Joy N Tsai, Sabashini K Ramchand, Mackenzie Jordan, Hang Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhen denosumab is discontinued, antiresorptive therapy is necessary to mitigate high bone turnover bone loss. While bisphosphonates have been investigated for this, alternatives have received less attention. In the Comparison of Alendronate and Raloxifene after Denosumab (CARD) randomized, controlled, open-label study, we previously reported alendronate better suppressed bone remodeling and maintained denosumab-induced areal BMD (aBMD) gains than raloxifene, though raloxifene maintained femoral neck aBMD and partially inhibited loss elsewhere. In this exploratory analysis, we now assess the comparative ability of these drugs to maintain denosumab-induced improvements in peripheral volumetric BMD (vBMD), microarchitecture, and estimated bone strength. CARD randomized 51 postmenopausal women (60–81y) at high fracture risk to receive denosumab 60-mg by subcutaneous injection every 6 mo for 12-mo followed by either 12-mo of oral alendronate (70 mg weekly, n = 26) or oral raloxifene (60 mg daily, n = 25). Total, trabecular, and cortical vBMD and skeletal microarchitecture were measured by high-resolution peripheral quantitative CT of the distal radius and distal tibia. After 12-mo of denosumab, mean total and cortical vBMD at the radius and tibia increased significantly in the cohort as a whole, whereas trabecular vBMD did not change significantly. Bone strength (stiffness and failure load as estimated by finite element analysis) also increased significantly at both sites. After the transition to alternate antiresorptive therapy, the gains observed in total and compartmental vBMD and estimated bone strength were maintained in both treatment groups with no significant between-group differences. No significant changes in cortical porosity or thickness were observed, either during denosumab treatment or after the transition to alternate antiresorptive therapy. We conclude that denosumab improved vBMD and bone strength, and these gains were maintained with both alendronate and raloxifene, supporting raloxifene as an option for those unable or unwilling to take bisphosphonates after short-term denosumab exposure.Keywords: Denosumab, Alendronate, Raloxifene, Osteoporosis, Postmenopausal, Bone Density, High-Resolution Peripheral Quantitative Computed Tomography (HR-pQCT), Bone Remodeling, Bone Strength, Antiresorptive Agents, Randomized Controlled Trial

Introduction

Denosumab, a potent RANK-ligand inhibitor, reduces fracture risk by inhibiting osteoclast differentiation, proliferation, and activity.1 Its effects, however, are rapidly lost upon discontinuation, leading to rebound increases in bone remodeling, rapid BMD loss, and loss of antifracture efficacy.2,3 Thus, patients stopping denosumab require transition to a bisphosphonate, though the optimal agent, dose, and duration remain unclear.4 For those unable or unwilling to take bisphosphonates, options are limited. In the CARD randomized controlled trial, alendronate better maintained denosumab-induced BMD gains at the spine and total hip compared to raloxifene, though raloxifene did maintain femoral neck BMD.5 We now report the comparative efficacy of alendronate and raloxifene in maintaining denosumab-induced improvements in peripheral volumetric BMD (vBMD), microarchitecture, and estimated bone strength assessed by HR-pQCT and micro finite element analysis (μFEA) at the distal radius and tibia.

Methods

The CARD study was a 2-yr open-label randomized controlled trial of 51 postmenopausal women at high fracture risk who received 12 mo of denosumab (60 mg SC every 6 mo) followed by 12 mo of either alendronate (70 mg orally weekly) or raloxifene (60 mg orally daily). High fracture risk was defined as a T-score ≤ −2.5 at the spine or hip, or a T-score between −1.0 and − 2.5 with FRAX 10-yr risk >20% or > 3% for major or hip fracture, respectively. Exclusion criteria included prior IV bisphosphonate use or oral bisphosphonates, teriparatide, abaloparatide, or denosumab within 12 mo. Participants were stratified by previous bisphosphonate use and randomized 1:1. Outcome assessors were blinded to group assignment. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03623633) and approved by the Mass General Brigham IRB. All participants provided written informed consent.

Adherence was assessed by self-reported diaries and pill counts. All were counseled to consume at least 1200 mg of calcium daily through diet and/or supplements and received 400 IU of cholecalciferol daily. Volumetric bone density and microarchitecture at the distal radius and tibia were assessed using HR-pQCT (XCT2) as described in our prior studies.6 Micro-FEA was used to estimate bone biomechanical properties as previously described.7,8

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome of the CARD study was change in biochemical markers of bone resorption; HR-pQCT parameters were secondary endpoints. All participants with at least one post-baseline visit were included. Between-group HR-pQCT changes were analyzed using unpaired t-tests, and within-group 0-12 mo changes with paired t-tests. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Analyses were performed using SAS v9.2.

Results

Fifty-one participants were enrolled, and 48 completed at least one post-baseline visit. Baseline characteristics were balanced (Table 1). As reported previously, areal BMD (aBMD) increased at all sites during the initial 12-mo of denosumab treatment. From months 12-24, BMD remained stable with alendronate, while in the raloxifene group, spine and total hip BMD decreased modestly but stayed above baseline, and femoral neck BMD remained stable.5 Biochemical markers of bone turnover were suppressed during the initial 12-mo of denosumab treatment and then remained suppressed in the alendronate group but gradually reverted to baseline in the raloxifene group.5

Volumetric BMD

In the overall cohort, during 12 mo of denosumab, total vBMD increased by 1.4 ± 1.6% at the tibia (p < .001) and 0.7 ± 1.6% at the radius (p = .01). Cortical vBMD increased by 2.1 ± 1.6% at the tibia and 1.0 ± 1.7% at the radius (both p < .001). Trabecular vBMD showed no significant changes (Figure 1). After transitioning to alternate antiresorptives (mo 12-24), total and cortical vBMD did not change significantly within or between groups. Trabecular vBMD increased by 0.8 ± 1.5% at the tibia (p = .03) in the alendronate group but not the radius. No between-group differences were seen in trabecular vBMD at either site (Figure 2).

Microarchitecture

Cortical porosity and parameters of trabecular microarchitecture (number, spacing, and thickness) did not change significantly during mo 0-12 in the entire cohort, nor were there between-group differences from mo 12-24 at either the radius or tibia.

Estimated bone strength

In the overall cohort, failure load increased by 3.4 ± 5.8% at the tibia and 3.9 ± 6.1% at the radius (both p < .001), while stiffness increased by 3.5 ± 6.3% at the tibia and 3.5 ± 5.7% at the radius (both p < .001) (Figure 1). From mo 12-24, after switching therapies, neither failure load nor stiffness changed significantly within or between groups (Figure 2).

Discussion

This analysis demonstrated that 12-mo of denosumab improved compartmental vBMD and estimated bone strength at the radius and tibia, and these gains were maintained with 12 mo of alendronate or raloxifene. While denosumab’s effects on peripheral vBMD, microarchitecture, and strength have been reported,9,10 data on changes after discontinuation are lacking. Because it is known that aBMD declines and bone remodeling increases rapidly after stopping denosumab, however, similar declines in vBMD and strength seem likely. Supporting this, our group reported that patients who transition from denosumab-to-teriparatide experience deterioration in compartmental vBMD and estimated strength and increases in cortical porosity.11

To our knowledge, prior to CARD, there have been no prior clinical trials assessing the skeletal effects of raloxifene in patients are discontinuing denosumab. Observational studies, however, vary- in a retrospective uncontrolled observational study of 61 patients who received 12-mo of raloxifene therapy after denosumab of various durations, hip and spine aBMD decreased to pre-treatment whereas in a separate propensity-score matched retrospective study, spine aBMD was reported to decrease less in raloxifene treated patients than those not receiving additional therapy.12,13

This study has several limitations. The small sample size limited power to detect within- and between-group differences, so small differences between alendronate and raloxifene may have been missed. Additionally, participants received only 1 yr of denosumab and results may not generalize to those with longer exposure as studies do show that bisphosphonates maintain aBMD after 1-2 yr of denosumab but are less effective after longer-term use.14–16 That said, it is important to note that it is not unusual for patients to discontinue denosumab after 12 mo. For example, in a prospective, cohort study in a real-world setting in the US and Canada, it was reported that 24-mo persistence was only 58% and that 21% of patients received 2 or fewer injections (32% received 3 or fewer).17 Finally, because the antiresorptive treatment period was limited to 1 yr, we do not know if raloxifene would continue to prevent an increase in remodeling beyond that time frame (though it should be noted that in patients discontinuing denosumab, bone turnover generally peaks during the first year).

In summary, this study shows that compartmental vBMD and estimated bone strength were maintained after 12 mo of denosumab followed by 12 mo of either alendronate or raloxifene. These findings, consistent with aBMD changes, support raloxifene as a potential second-line alternative for patients unable or unwilling to take bisphosphonates after short-term denosumab.
