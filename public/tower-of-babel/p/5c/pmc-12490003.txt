Negligible Tritium Accumulation in Japanese Flounder from Treated Water Released from Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant: A Numerical Simulation Study
Authors: Tsubasa Ikenoue, Takashi Tani, Hideyuki Kawamura, Yuhi Satoh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Since 2023, ALPS-treated water containing tritium from the Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant (1F) accident has been released into the ocean. Monitoring of seafood collected near the Fukushima coast has shown negligible increases in the tritium concentration. However, this monitoring has limitations, including delayed data release and a limited sample size. Therefore, a predictive estimation is necessary to assess the potential for high tritium accumulation in seafood. This study estimated tritium concentrations in Japanese flounder near the Fukushima coast using numerical simulations. The estimation combined an oceanic dispersion model for tritiated water (HTO) with a tritium transfer model for the marine food web. Tritium accumulation was evaluated as organically bound tritium (OBT), the long-retaining chemical form of tritium in organisms. First, the dispersion model’s ability to reproduce HTO concentrations in seawater was validated using actual measurement data from the Fukushima coast, showing good agreement. Subsequently, the OBT concentrations in flounder were estimated under the hypothetical maximum release scenario of the treated water. The estimation suggested that even within 100 km of 1F, the maximum concentration of the OBT was comparable with natural levels of tritium in environmental waters. Additionally, the maximum concentration in the flounder remained at a negligible level for internal radiation exposure through consumption.

Introduction

The Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant (1F) accident in 2011 caused various social damages, some of which remain significant concerns. One major concern is the treatment of radioactively contaminated water generated by the 1F accident. To address this, Tokyo Electric Power Company Holdings (TEPCO) has implemented the Advanced Liquid Processing System (ALPS) to remove radionuclides from the contaminated water. Most radionuclides have been successfully removed by this system; however, tritium, a radioisotope of hydrogen, cannot be separated because it is chemically bound to water molecules. Until 2023, TEPCO stored all ALPS-treated water (hereafter referred to as treated water) at 1F, but its storage capacity reached its limit. Consequently, TEPCO planned and initiated the oceanic release of treated water in August 2023. The discharge of tritium from nuclear facilities into the ocean is a globally recognized disposal method. Furthermore, TEPCO’s release plan follows strict safety measures, including an upper limit of 1500 Bq L–1 for tritium concentration, which is significantly lower than the World Health Organization (WHO)’s drinking water guideline of 10,000 Bq L–1. Following strict safety measures, the total HTO releases from 1F were 3.2 and 11.6 TBq in 2023 and 2024, respectively. ,

As of October 2024, the treated water from 1F has been released into the ocean 10 times. , During these releases, TEPCO’s monitoring assessments have not detected tritium concentrations exceeding 50 Bq L–1 in seawater, even near the release outlet, suggesting that the release is proceeding safely. However, evaluating tritium concentrations in seafood, which poses a more direct exposure risk to humans through ingestion than seawater, is crucial for preventing reputational damage and ensuring public confidence. TEPCO has monitored tritium levels in fish and seaweed, measuring both tissue-free water tritium (TFWT) and organically bound tritium (OBT), the primary chemical forms of tritium in organisms. To date, neither TFWT nor OBT concentrations have exceeded 1 Bq L–1, suggesting that tritium accumulation in seafood is negligible.

However, a potential issue with TEPCO’s monitoring of marine organisms is the relatively long time required for tritium measurements, which can take several weeks. This delay is primarily due to pretreatment steps such as vacuum extraction of tissue-free water and the conversion of organic matter (OM) in dried samples into combustion water for measuring TFWT and OBT. As a result, there is a risk that people may consume seafood with high tritium concentrations before such levels are confirmed through monitoring. One effective solution to this problem is the pre-estimation of tritium accumulation potential in marine organisms using numerical simulation models based on appropriate release scenarios for treated water into the coastal seawater near 1F. However, such estimations have only recently become feasible due to the prior lack of predictive models for tritium concentrations in both Fukushima’s coastal waters and marine organisms.

Considering the chemical form of tritium is essential for estimating its accumulation in marine organisms because TFWT and OBT have different accumulation efficiencies. TFWT, or tritiated water (HTO) in tissue-free water, rapidly equilibrates with HTO concentrations in the surrounding seawater of a marine organism. This means TFWT concentrations fluctuate in sync with HTO levels in seawater and decrease as the treated water disperses in the ocean. Conversely, OBT, which is tritium bound to OM, accumulates in heterotrophic organisms through enzymatic hydrogen exchange in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and the ingestion of OBT from food sources. In photosynthetic organisms, OBT also forms through photosynthesis. Because the primary source of OBT in marine organisms is HTO from seawater, the concentrations of OBT typically exhibit time lags relative to HTO fluctuations in seawater. Additionally, OBT has a longer retention time than TFWT due to the slower turnover of OM compared with tissue-free water in organisms. In fact, delayed increases and prolonged elevated levels of tritium in OBT, compared with HTO in seawater, have been observed in fish collected near a nuclear fuel reprocessing plant. Moreover, OBT contributes more significantly to internal radiation exposure in humans than HTO, primarily due to its longer retention in the body. Given these properties, OBT should be the priority when evaluating tritium accumulation in seafood collected near 1F.

This study estimated the concentration of OBT in Japanese flounder (Paralichthys olivaceus) from coastal seawater near Fukushima using numerical simulation models to evaluate the tritium accumulation potential in marine organisms. P. olivaceus, a major edible fish in East Asian countries, was selected due to its sedentary nature in coastal areas, making it suitable for this assessment. The estimation combined an oceanic dispersion model for tritiated water with a tritium transfer model for the marine food web. The results of this study clearly indicate that tritium accumulation in seafood near 1F is minimal.

Materials and Methods

Model Estimation Concept

To estimate OBT concentrations in marine organisms near 1F, accurately predicting HTO concentrations in coastal seawater is essential as HTO serves as the primary determinant of OBT levels. , Previous studies have estimated HTO concentrations using oceanic dispersion simulations of treated water on relatively large scales, such as the open ocean and adjacent seas of Japan. , However, these studies lacked sufficient spatial resolution (i.e., within 1 km) to precisely model coastal seawater dynamics and OBT accumulation in sedentary coastal organisms. Moreover, the HTO concentration in treated water is reported to be approximately 200 Bq L–1, a value significantly higher than the natural background level in Fukushima’s coastal seawater, which averages 0.072 Bq L–1. Given the complex oceanographic conditions in the coastal area near the treated water release point, a high-resolution oceanic dispersion simulation of HTO is necessary for accurately estimating the concentrations of OBT in marine organisms. For the resolution of this problem, we applied the model that was developed for the reconstruction of seawater condition on the Fukushima coast.

In this study, the estimation followed a two-step approach: first, HTO concentrations in seawater were calculated using an oceanic dispersion model, followed by OBT concentration calculations in the fish using a tritium transfer model simulating tritium movement from seawater through the marine food web. Additionally, two simulation cases based on different release conditions were considered: a strict safety assessment (SA) case and a realistic assessment (RA) case. The SA case assumed a continuous release of treated water at TEPCO’s upper limit for HTO concentration (1500 Bq L–1) to evaluate the maximum potential for OBT accumulation. Conversely, the RA case simulated an intermittent release with a more realistic HTO concentration (approximately 200 Bq L–1) to reflect actual conditions.

Estimation of HTO Concentration in Seawater Using an Oceanic Dispersion Model

HTO concentration in coastal seawater near Fukushima was estimated using an oceanic dispersion model developed with the Regional Ocean Modeling System (ROMS) in a double-nested configuration. Because HTO exists solely in the dissolved phase (i.e., water), particle dynamics was not included in the model. HTO concentration was primarily governed by two seawater transport processes: advection and diffusion. Oceanic dispersion simulations for the year 2023 were conducted for two cases with different release conditions: SA and RA. In the SA case, treated water with an HTO concentration of 1500 Bq L–1 was continuously released at a flow rate of 340,000 m3 day–1, a value consistent with TEPCO-reported rates (336,000–360,000 m3 day–1). The HTO concentration used in the SA case represents the upper limit proposed by TEPCO (1500 Bq L–1). In the RA case, treated water with an HTO concentration of approximately 200 Bq L–1 was intermittently released at the same flow rate as that in the SA case. The HTO concentration in the RA case was set based on reported values (160–230 Bq L–1) (Figure S1). In both cases, HTO input from rivers in Fukushima Prefecture was considered in the model. HTO inputs from rivers were calculated based on the HTO concentration in rivers and river discharge. The HTO concentration in rivers was set at 0.41 Bq L–1 based on previous studies. This value is included in the range of HTO concentrations based on actual measurements. This value is within the range of observed HTO concentrations (0.24–0.81 Bq L–1) in rivers in Fukushima Prefecture. The river discharge was set based on daily average from 2011 to 2020 based on the Japan River Association and simulation results. The total HTO releases in the SA and RA cases were 186 and 8.77 TBq, respectively. Additionally, an oceanic dispersion simulation was conducted without HTO from treated water (i.e., considering only natural seawater and riverine HTO inputs) to estimate the background levels for setting initial conditions in the OBT estimation for fish (initial setting IS case). Further details on computational conditions, including double-nested domains and oceanic dispersion model configurations, are provided in Figure S2 and Table S1.

Estimation of the OBT Concentration in P. olivaceus

The OBT concentration in P. olivaceus was estimated using a numerical model of a simple three-level food chain consisting of phytoplankton, small fish, and the target species. OBT refers to tritium that is strongly bound to OM through covalent bonds, specifically termed nonexchangeable OBT (NE-OBT). Conversely, exchangeable OBT (E-OBT) undergoes isotopic exchange with free water in tissue and behaves similarly to HTO. Because the concentration of E-OBT in marine organisms can be assumed to be equivalent to the HTO concentration in surrounding seawater, our estimation focused solely on NE-OBT.

In the model, the NE-OBT concentration in phytoplankton on a given day was calculated using the estimated HTO concentration in seawater for that day (Bq mol-H–1) and a constant ratio representing the daily photosynthetic production of nonexchangeable organically bound hydrogen relative to the total hydrogen content in the dry mass of phytoplankton (mol-H mol-H–1). For small fish (whole body) and P. olivaceus (muscle tissue), NE-OBT concentration was estimated using a single-compartment model that accounts for NE-OBT metabolism through three key transfer pathways: (1) NE-OBT formation from HTO via the TCA cycle, (2) NE-OBT elimination through catabolism, and (3) NE-OBT intake through prey ingestion.

The input data for the model consisted of HTO concentrations in seawater at each analysis site, as estimated by the oceanic dispersion model described above. For P. olivaceus, HTO concentrations from the bottom layer (near the seafloor) were used, whereas for small fish and phytoplankton, the average HTO concentrations from the surface to the bottom layers were used. Initial OBT concentrations in small fish and P. olivaceus were determined based on a 1 year estimation in the IS case of the oceanic dispersion model.

Estimation of the Internal Radiation Dose from Ingesting P. olivaceus

In this study, to assess the radiation dose from consuming fish affected by treated water, we estimated the dose based on our simulation results. The internal radiation dose from consuming P. olivaceus was estimated using the predicted concentrations of TFWT, E-OBT, and NE-OBT in the wet muscle of the fish (Bq kg-wet–1) (Supporting Information). TFWT concentration was estimated based on the assumption of equilibrium between TFWT in muscle and HTO in bottom seawater. This was calculated using the simulated HTO concentration in seawater and a reported relative water content of 0.776 in fish muscle. E-OBT concentration was estimated by assuming that 21% of hydrogen in dried muscle is in an exchangeable form at the T/H ratio in seawater and that the hydrogen content in muscle is 0.073 g-H g-dry–1. NE-OBT concentration (Bq L–1 of combustion water) estimated by the model was converted to Bq kg-wet–1 using the hydrogen content and relative water content values described above. The total annual radiation dose from TFWT and OBT in fish muscle was calculated using ingestion dose coefficients (1.8 × 10–11 Sv Bq1– for TFWT and 4.2 × 10–11 Sv Bq1– for OBT) and an ingestion rate of 0.19 kg-wet person–1 day–1 (Supporting Information). The ingestion rate was referenced from data on populations that consume relatively high amounts of seafood to assess the upper range of potential ingestion-related risks.

Results and Discussion

Estimation of the HTO Concentration in Seawater Derived from Treated Water

First, the oceanic dispersion simulation was validated by using actual water temperature measurements from coastal seawater near Fukushima (Figure ). Validation sites were selected based on coastal to offshore locations to account for specific coastal influences, such as terrestrial water inputs and variations in water depth, including the continental shelf. Water temperature was chosen as the validation parameter as it reflects thermodynamic water processes and is commonly used for similar model validations. , The comparison of temperature profiles from surface to seafloor across a wide area provides a robust validation of the model’s accuracy. For this validation, the simulated oceanic conditions for 2023 were compared with actual measurements, including daily in situ sea surface temperatures (ST and OT in Figure ), monthly in situ seafloor temperatures, and monthly satellite-derived sea surface temperatures. The simulation results showed strong agreement with observed data, with determination coefficients exceeding 0.82 (Figures S3–S5).

The estimated HTO concentrations in seawater in the RA case were compared with those obtained from the actual measurements on the monitoring (Figure S6). In this comparison, the measurement data at the monitoring sites near the analysis sites where the impact of ALPS-treated water release from 1F could be observed were used. The consistency between the estimations and the measurements was evaluated by an index, factor x (x is any positive integer), which is the ratio of the estimated to the measured values expressed as a factor of 1x to x (e.g., ref ). Although factor 10 has been generally used for radiocesium derived from the 1F accident, factor 2, a more strict criterion, was applied in our study for rigorous testing of the estimation. The estimated HTO concentrations were almost (91.4%) within the range of factor 2, which means that model estimation results of HTO are generally accurate. This result, together with the aforementioned verification results using the water temperature, suggests that the estimation of the HTO concentration in seawater was valid. HTO concentration in surface seawater under the SA case (i.e., continuous release of treated water at 1500 Bq L–1) was estimated to examine its spatiotemporal distribution along the coastal waters near Fukushima (Figure ). HTO concentrations exceeding 1 Bq L–1 were largely confined to areas with water depths of up to 20 m, with a predominant expansion in the north–south direction along the coastline. This pattern aligns with previous studies indicating that the dominant oceanic current in this region follows a north–south flow. , Moreover, this flow was particularly pronounced from autumn to winter (October to December), consistent with earlier findings. These results suggest that our HTO concentration estimations reflect the prevailing oceanic current characteristics near Fukushima. Notably, a similar distribution pattern was observed for bottom seawater and for both surface and bottom seawater under the RA case (i.e., intermittent release of treated water at approximately 200 Bq L–1) (Figures S7 and S8).

Level and Trend of the OBT Concentration in P. olivaceus

In the following section, the OBT indicates NE-OBT as explained above (see Section ). The estimated OBT concentrations in P. olivaceus from coastal seawater near Fukushima ranged from 0.017 to 0.27 Bq L–1 under the SA case and from 0.016 to 0.033 Bq L–1 under the RA case (Figure ). Correspondingly, HTO concentrations in seawater at the same sites were estimated to range from 0.072 to 7.0 Bq L–1 under the SA case and from 0.072 to 0.54 Bq L–1 under the RA case (Figure ). A comparison of these estimates shows that OBT concentrations in P. olivaceus did not exceed HTO concentrations in the surrounding seawater. This aligns with previous findings as tritium does not bioconcentrate from seawater into fish. − The highest estimated OBT concentration at sites located 5–60 km from 1F in this study (0.27 Bq L–1 under the SA case) corresponds to only 0.018% of the HTO concentration in the treated water (1500 Bq L–1), suggesting a negligible accumulation of tritium from the treated water in fish inhabiting the coastal waters near Fukushima. Additionally, this maximum OBT concentration represents just 0.00040% of the upper limit for tritium concentration in food (10,000 Bq kg-wet–1) set by the United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization and the WHO (see the Supporting Information). This upper limit was established to prevent radiation exposure from food containing tritium from exceeding 1 mSv y–1. This indicates an extremely low radiation dose to humans from consuming fish affected by treated water.

The maximum estimated OBT concentration in the SA case was, on average, an order of magnitude higher than that in the RA case (Figure ). Meanwhile, the total tritium release in the SA case was 21 times greater than that in the RA case, suggesting that the difference in maximum OBT concentrations was primarily due to the varying tritium release amounts in the treated water. Notably, the difference in OBT concentrations between the SA and RA cases was not uniform across all sites (Figure and Table S2). This variation is likely attributed to differences in release conditions with continuous discharge in the SA case and intermittent discharge in the RA case.

The median OBT concentration tended to decrease with increasing distance from the treated water outlet (Figure ), which aligns with the similar trend observed for HTO concentrations in seawater (Figure ). However, the highest OBT concentration was recorded at a site located 20 km from the outlet rather than at the nearest sites, which are 5 km away. This pattern mirrors that of HTO concentrations, suggesting that the maximum OBT concentration was influenced by HTO distribution. The nonproportional relationship between HTO concentration and distance from the outlet was likely due to the dominant north–south oceanic currents (Figure ). Similarly, the lack of a proportional relationship between the OBT concentration and distance from the outlet suggests that distance alone is not the primary factor governing the OBT levels in fish. Additionally, OBT concentrations in coastal sites (20 m water depth) were consistently higher than those in offshore sites (50 m water depth) (Figure ), a trend that also matched HTO distribution (Figure ). This can be attributed to higher HTO concentrations in shallower waters due to strong horizontal north–south currents (Figures , S7, and S8, Table S2), which limit offshore transport of coastal water. Consequently, the concentrations of the OBT in fish are expected to be lower in deeper offshore areas near Fukushima.

Transfer of Tritium from Seawater and the Food Web to P. olivaceus

To illustrate the transfer of tritium from seawater and the food web to P. olivaceus, we present the estimation results from site S20-20, where the highest OBT concentration in P. olivaceus was recorded under the SA case (Figure ). Results from other stations and cases are summarized in Figures S9 and S10. OBT concentrations in each organism (i.e., phytoplankton, small fish, and P. olivaceus) increased shortly after a rise in the HTO concentration in seawater. A more detailed analysis revealed that phytoplankton responded almost simultaneously to HTO fluctuations, whereas small fish and P. olivaceus exhibited a delay (Figure S11). This lag between phytoplankton and fish is likely due to differences in OM turnover time: phytoplankton has a short turnover time (on a daily scale), whereas fish turnover occurs over a longer period (on a monthly scale), meaning that HTO incorporation into fish OM occurs more gradually. The time lag between OBT increases in small fish and P. olivaceus was minimal. While this might seem unexpected given their different diets (i.e., phytoplankton for small fish and small fish for P. olivaceus), it is reasonable because OBT accumulation via ingestion occurs more slowly than OBT formation through the cellular TCA cycle from HTO in seawater. During phases of decreasing HTO concentration in seawater, OBT concentrations in all organisms also declined accordingly (Figure ), indicating that accumulated tritium is gradually dispersed by the inflow of natural seawater. The rate of the decline OBT followed the order: phytoplankton > small fish > P. olivaceus (Figures and S10), reflecting differences in OM turnover timeorganisms with longer OM turnover (e.g., P. olivaceus) tend to retain higher OBT concentrations for a longer duration.

In the RA case, the OBT concentration increases in each organism were generally slower than those observed in the SA case (Figure ). This is likely due to the intermittent release of treated water in the RA case, which allowed for periods of seawater inflow without treated water, preventing rapid OBT accumulation. These findings suggest that intermittent release may help mitigate tritium accumulation in marine organisms in the coastal waters near Fukushima.

We previously showed that the OBT concentration in P. olivaceus reaches equilibrium with the HTO concentration in seawater over a few years. This suggests that if fish are continuously exposed to treated water with high HTO levels, then the OBT concentrations could gradually increase over time. However, our estimations indicate that the influence of treated water on OBT concentration was neither continuous nor significant, even in the SA case (Figures and ). This is primarily due to the rapid and extensive dilution of treated water by oceanic currents under real environmental conditions. Therefore, the risk of continuous tritium accumulation in fish in coastal waters near Fukushima is minimal.

In the estimation under the RA case, which assumes realistic release conditions of treated water, the maximum OBT concentration in P. olivaceus was 0.033 Bq L–1 (Figure ). This value is an order of magnitude lower than the detection limit (∼0.21 Bq L–1) in TEPCO’s monitoring, suggesting that the increase in the OBT concentration during realistic treated water release is too low to be detected by actual measurements. Even in the SA case, which assumes the maximum possible release of treated water, the estimated OBT concentration reached only 0.27 Bq L–1a level comparable to natural environmental waters such as precipitation (0.25–0.71 Bq L–1) and river water (0.24–0.81 Bq L–1) in Fukushima Prefecture. Since the estimated OBT concentration is comparable to the variation of natural levels, these findings clearly indicate that the potential for tritium accumulation in marine organisms in the coastal waters near Fukushima during treated water release from 1F is negligible.

Radiation Dose from Ingesting Fish Influenced by Treated Water from 1F

To assess the radiation dose from consuming fish affected by treated water, we estimated the dose based on our simulation results. This estimation required two tritium parameters in fish: TFWT and OBT concentrations. Because TFWT concentration is equivalent to the HTO concentration in the surrounding seawater, the maximum dose at each site was calculated using the highest recorded OBT concentration in P. olivaceus and the highest HTO concentration in its surrounding seawater (Tables S2 and S3). The maximum estimated radiation doses were 7.4 nSv y–1 in the SA case and 0.55 nSv y–1 in the RA case. These values are negligibly small compared with the ICRP-recommended public dose limit of 1 mSv y–1, indicating an insignificant radiation risk from fish consumption in the coastal waters near Fukushima.
