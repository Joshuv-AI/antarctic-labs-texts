Dimensional accuracy of clear aligners fabricated using different LCD 3D printer resolutions: an in-vitro study
Authors: Jayaraj Ravi, Mahalakshmi Krishnakumaran, Balaji Krishnan, Annie Shirly Priyanka Gerald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe aim of this study is to compare trueness and precision of dental models from three LCD 3D printers with different horizontal resolutions (2K, 4K, 8K) and to evaluate their clinical suitability for orthodontic use.Materials and MethodsThirty models were printed using Creality Halot One (2K), Phrozen Mighty 4K, and Phrozen Mighty 8K printers, with ten models each using manufacturer-recommended resins. Master digital models from intraoral scans were compared to re-scanned printed models through surface superimposition using Geomagic Control X software. Trueness and precision were evaluated by point deviation and Root-Mean-Square (RMS) analysis, with differences assessed by one-way ANOVA (p = 0.05).ResultsMean RMS values for 2K, 4K, and 8K printers were 0.266 ± 0.015 mm, 0.223 ± 0.017 mm, and 0.121 ± 0.012 mm, respectively (p < 0.001). The 8K printer showed highest trueness and precision, with deviations mainly in occlusal and gingival regions. The 0.25 mm error limit was within acceptable range for 4K and 8K models, while 2K models exceeded this value (p < 0.05).ConclusionPrinter resolution significantly affects dimensional accuracy. The 8K LCD printer demonstrated superior accuracy and reproducibility, proving reliable for orthodontic models and clear aligners.Keywords: 3D printing, dimensional accuracy, LCD printer, trueness, orthodontic models

Introduction

The integration of digital technology and 3D printing has revolutionized dentistry, enabling precise and economical fabrication of complex structures. Advanced technologies such as Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT), 3D imaging, and intraoral scanning have significantly enhanced diagnosis and treatment planning by accurately reconstructing dental and soft tissue anatomy (1, 2). Digital workflows now facilitate the fabrication of aligners, crowns, surgical guides, and implant templates, transforming orthodontic and restorative practices (3, 4).

In clear aligner production, intraoral scans generate 3D surface models, replacing traditional impressions. Additive manufacturing (AM) constructs objects layer by layer, improving material efficiency (5). Among available techniques such as SLA, DLP, and LCD, the LCD printers offer uniform light projection and minimal pixel distortion (6, 7, 8, 9, 10). The smallest feature that the projector can replicate in a single layer is determined by the pixel size. Research indicates that factors such as 3D printing resolution and layer thickness influence the accuracy of 3D printing. Depending on the number of lines and pixels visible on a single screen or projection, these LCD printers are available in 2K, 4K, and 8K resolutions. While higher "K" resolution suggests finer detail, true accuracy is more dependent on pixel size, printer type, optics, resin properties, and build stability. SLA and high-end DLP printers often outperform LCD printers, even when the latter are marketed as 4K or 8K, although most systems still produce clinically acceptable models within the ~0.25 mm error tolerance for aligners. Complex dental anatomy, such as crowding and diastemas, and resin choice can also influence fidelity.

The accuracy of the printed outcome must be assured before utilizing 3D printed dental models for therapeutic applications. Some research has concluded that dental models reconstructed using tested rapid prototyping techniques are considered clinically acceptable in terms of accuracy and reproducibility and may be suitable for selected applications in orthodontics (11). The present study seeks to determine whether variations in LCD printer resolution affect the dimensional accuracy of orthodontic models used in clear aligner treatment. The null hypothesis states that there is no significant difference in the dimensional accuracy of orthodontic dental models fabricated using 2K, 4K, and 8K LCD 3D printers.

Materials and Methods

Ethical statement

An in-vitro study was approved by the Institute Ethics Committee (IEC NO: IEC/TDCH/044/2022), to evaluate the dimensional accuracy of 3D printed models produced by 2k, 4k and 8k LCD printers.

Sample size estimation

Sample size was estimated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.4) for an a priori F-test ANOVA (fixed effects), assuming an effect size f = 1.5, α = 0.05, and power = 80% across three groups. The analysis yielded a total sample size of 10, with an actual achieved power of 0.84.

Study groups and devices

Master STL files (Group 1) were obtained from the Department of Orthodontics. Inclusion criteria included models with mild crowding or spacing, no systemic disease, and no periodontal therapy. Models with skeletal malocclusions or dental anomalies were excluded. Master STL files were optimized using Maestro 3D Dental Studio (AGE Solutions, Italy) and sliced at 100 µm intervals with Chitu Box v1.9.5 (for 2K and 4K) and Lychee Slicer v7.3 (for 8K). Models were oriented at 90° vertically. Three LCD printers were used: 2K (n=10): Creality Halot One (Creality 3D, China), 4K (n=10): Phrozen Mighty 4K (Phrozen Tech, Taiwan), 8K (n=10): Phrozen Mighty 8K (Phrozen Tech, Taiwan) (Figure 1). Resins were selected per manufacturer instructions: Anycubic grey resin for 2K, and Phrozen aqua grey resin for 4K and 8K printers, yielding a total of 30 printed models.Printed models were cleaned in 99% isopropyl alcohol for 10 minutes, air-dried, and post-cured for 15 minutes (385–405 nm). The Anycubic Wash & Cure Machine was used for 2K prints, and Phrozen Cure Mega for 4K and 8K. All models were stored in dark containers to prevent light exposure.

Creality Halot One 2K printer, Phrozen Sonic Mighty 4k printer, Phrozen Sonic Mighty 8k printer

Model scanning

Within 24 hours, printed models were scanned using the Aoralscan 3 intraoral scanner (Shining 3D, China) to create secondary STL files (Group 2). Non-anatomical areas were removed using Maestro 3D software.

Master STL files were superimposed with re-scanned models in Geomagic Control X using the best-fit alignment algorithm, registering up to 10,000 surface points for precise deviation analysis.

Measurement of deviations

Deviations between meshes were measured at three key landmark groups: Incisal/Occlusal landmarks: lateral incisor edges (MI), second premolar grooves (PG), first molar cusp tips (ML). Mid-crown landmarks: central incisor axes (FACC), premolar palatal midpoints (MP), molar buccal pits (BP), Gingival margin landmarks: labial zeniths (GZ), palatal gingival midpoints (HP), and buccal gingival centers (MC). Deviations were visualized via color maps: green indicated minimal discrepancy (<0.01 mm), while yellow/red and turquoise/blue represented positive and negative deviations, respectively.

Reliability and error analysis

To ensure accuracy, the workflow was repeated by a single operator after two weeks and cross-verified by a second operator. Point and whole-deviation assessments were analyzed to determine intra- and inter-operator variability.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM SPSS, Armony, NY, USA). Normality was verified with the Shapiro–Wilk test. ICC assessed inter-observer reliability. One-way ANOVA compared point deviations and RMS values among printer groups, while independent t-tests evaluated upper and lower arch differences, with significance set at p = 0.05.

Results

Systematic errors within observers were remarkably similar. Not one of the ten changes between the first and second replications revealed a statistically significant difference. The error range for the method was 0.000 mm (PG) to 0.07 mm (MI- Mid-incisal edge point of the lateral incisors). The range of results for the intra-class correlation coefficient was 0.864 to 1.000 which showed high correlation and high reproducibility.

The 8K printer also had the second-lowest deviation at the FACC point (-0.0261±0.023). Significant differences were found in several points (MI, ML, FACC, HP, and MC) among the printer groups, with the 8K printer showing the least and the 2K the most overall point deviation. Furthermore, variations between the primary STL mesh and the 3D printed models exhibited positive values at certain points and negative values at others.

In the comparison of point deviations with STL master file and 2k printer, an independent t test revealed that overall, there is no statistically significant difference between upper and lower arches. But there are statistical differences between the arches in MI, PG (Occlusal), FACC (mid crown) and MC (gingival) points. Also, the lowest point deviation was found in ML (occlusal) point in lower arch and highest was found in HP (gingival) point in upper arch. An independent t test was used to compare the point deviations between the higher and lower arches using an STL master file and a 4k printer. The results showed that overall, there is no statistically significant difference. However, the arches in PG, ML (Occlusal), BP (mid crown), and GZ, MC (gingival) points differ statistically. Additionally, the ML (occlusal) point in the lower arch had the lowest point deviation, while the HP (gingival) point in the upper arch had the largest. The independent t test conducted to compare point discrepancies between the STL master file and the 8k printer showed that, on the whole, there is no statistically significant distinction between the upper and lower arches. However, there are noteworthy statistical variances between the arches in PG, ML from Occlusal, BP (mid crown), and HP, MC (gingival) points. Additionally, the smallest deviation was observed in the ML (occlusal) point in the lower arch, while the largest was identified in the HP (gingival) point in the upper arch. Bland Altman plots were plotted for all 3 groups which shows more deviation was shown in 2K and least deviation was shown in 8K printers (Table 1 and Table 2).

Discussion

Three-dimensional (3D) printing has transformed digital dentistry, particularly in the production of clear aligners. Among the VAT photopolymerization technologies, Stereolithography (SLA), Digital Light Processing (DLP), and Liquid Crystal Display (LCD) are the most widely used. SLA employs a focused UV laser to cure resin incrementally, DLP projects light through micromirrors to polymerize an entire layer, and LCD printers use masked light from an LCD screen. Due to simpler light sources and lower component costs, LCD systems offer a cost-effective and efficient option for dental model fabrication, with reduced pixel distortion compared to DLP units (8).

Studies comparing LCD printing with other techniques remain limited. Tsolakis et al. (12) reported that both DLP and LCD printers can produce models of acceptable orthodontic accuracy, whereas Grassia et al. (13) cautioned that some entry-level LCD printers may reach the upper limit of clinically acceptable error. According to ISO 5725-1:1994, accuracy comprises trueness (closeness to the true value) and precision (reproducibility). The present study compared the dimensional accuracy of three LCD printers—2K (Creality Halot One), 4K (Phrozen Sonic Mighty 4K), and 8K (Phrozen Sonic Mighty 8K)—to determine whether higher horizontal resolution improves accuracy.

The 3D printer coordinate system defines the X and Y axes as horizontal and the Z axis as vertical. Resolution in the XY plane, determined by pixel size, influences the minimal feature size (14, 15). As noted by Nulty et al (16), smaller pixels correspond to greater horizontal accuracy. The printers used in this study offered 50 µm (2K), 52 µm (4K), and 28 µm (8K) horizontal resolutions. The results demonstrated that horizontal resolution significantly affects dimensional fidelity, leading to rejection of the null hypothesis.

Printing parameters were standardized to minimize variability. All models were printed with a 90° build orientation, auto-generated supports, and 100 µm layer thickness (17, 18). Favero et al. (9) found that finer layer heights (<100 µm) increase distortion, while Loflin et al. (19) confirmed that 100 µm layers provide clinically acceptable results for orthodontic models. Sim et al. (20) further noted that although DLP remains marginally more accurate than LCD, higher LCD resolutions substantially improve outcomes. This appears to be the first study evaluating entry-level LCD 3D printers based solely on horizontal (XY) resolution. A digital superimposition technique was employed using Geomagic Control X software, which applies a surface-based "best-fit alignment" algorithm using thousands of points for accurate registration. This approach minimizes the influence of local discrepancies and provides a comprehensive 3D deviation assessment. RMS (root-mean-square) deviation was used to quantify accuracy since it is less sensitive to compensation between positive and negative errors, providing a robust measure of both trueness and precision (21, 22). Analysis of point deviations revealed that the 8K printer achieved the highest trueness, followed by the 4K and 2K printers. Deviations were most pronounced in the occlusal and gingival regions, areas with complex surface geometry, while smoother mid-crown areas showed minimal deviation. The mean RMS values were 0.266 ± 0.015 mm for 2K, 0.223 ± 0.017 mm for 4K, and 0.121 ± 0.012 mm for 8K printers (p < 0.05). RMS values below 0.25 mm are considered clinically acceptable for orthodontic applications (5, 23); therefore, both 4K and 8K printers produced models suitable for aligner fabrication, while the 2K printer exceeded this threshold.

Precision, the reproducibility of model dimensions, was assessed by comparing repeated prints. The 8K printer consistently exhibited the highest precision (upper arch 0.1287 ± 0.0119 mm; lower arch 0.1135 ± 0.0061 mm), followed by 4K and 2K printers (p < 0.05). Although no significant difference was observed between upper and lower arches within each printer (p > 0.05), overall reproducibility improved as resolution increased. These results align with findings by Lo Giudice et al. (23) and Dong et al. (24), who reported enhanced trueness and precision with higher-resolution LCD and DLP systems. An internal comparison between the ELEGOO Saturn S-4K and Creality Halot 2K for dental model printing is described in a recent abstract by from a dental congress. It found that the 4K prints showed smaller deviations in important regions (molar and canine) compared with the 2K prints, suggesting that higher resolution printers produced better surface fidelity and dimensional accuracy (25). In comparison to lower-resolution printers, a study by Narongdej et al. (26) assessing high-resolution LCD printers including 8K models reported higher trueness values. This improvement was attributed to smaller pixels and better surface detail reproduction, especially in anatomically complex areas like gingival margins and cuspal tips. Comparative analyses of inexpensive resin printers published in by Oğuz et al. (27) also showed that, while all tested printers created models within clinically acceptable bounds for orthodontic applications, printers with higher native resolution yielded noticeably superior dimensional accuracy.

Three-dimensional color maps generated by Geomagic visualized the distribution of deviations, with green indicating minimal error (<0.01 mm), yellow-red showing positive deviation, and blue-turquoise representing negative deviation (Figure 2, Figure 3 and Figure 4). All printers exhibited localized discrepancies on cusp tips and gingival margins, while the 8K printer displayed the least deviation overall. These deviations likely result from resin polymerization shrinkage, post-curing contraction, and data conversion steps inherent to additive manufacturing. Previous studies have established that dimensional deviations of 0.20–0.50 mm fall within clinically acceptable limits (11, 28, 29, 30, 31). Taking this range into account, the 4K and 8K printers in this study produced models suitable for orthodontic diagnosis, appliance design, and aligner production. An important characteristic of our methodology is that the original digital models were obtained directly from the oral environment, and then compared to the scanned 3D printed models.

This allowed us to assess accuracy of 3D printed models while eliminating variables that could have been introduced during acquisition by means of alginate impressions. On the other hand, the 3D printed models had to be scanned to be digitally superimposed with the original models. However, the Aoralscan 3 (Shining 3D) intraoral scanner provides high accuracy, and the errors introduced by the digitalization process should be considerably less significant than those introduced by the printing techniques (32).

The present study has some limitations. The experimental design evaluated the accuracy of three entry-level LCD 3D printers; therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution and not generalized to all printers (33, 34). Possible inaccuracies may have arisen from the best-fit alignment method, which, although commonly used, can produce a mean translation error of 0.13 mm (35). The well-aligned master model limited assessment of crowded or interproximal surfaces. Additional influencing factors include scanner resolution, software performance (23), resin polymerization, support structure design, layer thickness, and surface finishing, all of which can introduce deviations during the printing and post-processing stages of model fabrication.

Conclusion

The 4K and 8K LCD-printed models showed RMS values of 0.2230 ± 0.0166 mm and 0.1211 ± 0.0119 mm, respectively, which are within the clinically acceptable limit of 0.25 mm for orthodontic applications, while the 2K printer (0.2660 ± 0.0153 mm) exceeded this threshold. The 8K printer demonstrated the highest trueness and precision, attributed to its smaller pixel size (28 µm) and higher XY resolution (7680 × 4320 ppi). Deviations were primarily observed in occlusal and gingival regions with intricate morphology. Overall, the 4K and 8K entry-level LCD printers exhibited dimensional accuracy comparable to SLA and DLP systems, offering a cost-effective, clinically viable option for in-house aligner fabrication.
