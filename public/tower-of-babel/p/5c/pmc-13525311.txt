Medial Meniscal Transplant Allograft Using A Hybrid Root Fixation Technique
Authors: Romed P. Vieider, Vince Morgan, Sahil Dadoo, Robert E. Bilodeau, Shohei Yamauchi, Seth L. Sherman, Volker Musahl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:High rates of osteoarthritis have been reported after partial and total meniscectomy, attributed to increased joint contact stresses in meniscus-deficient knees. Meniscal allograft transplantation (MAT) with hybrid root fixation has emerged as a treatment for meniscal deficiency, combining the advantages of bony and all-soft-tissue techniques.Indications:MAT is best suited for patients <55 years with symptomatic meniscal deficiency and may require correction of modifiable factors, such as obesity, malalignment, instability, or cartilage loss, before the procedure.Technique Description:A size-matched fresh-frozen meniscal allograft is prepared with meticulous preservation of 1 to 2 mm bony wafers at the anterior and posterior roots to maintain meniscal length and enhance fixation. Background and Indications

Ideal candidates for meniscal allograft transplantation (MAT) are patients with irreparable meniscal deficiency or a functional loss of the meniscus associated with pain in patients <55 years.2,7 Associated modifiable comorbidities, including body mass index >30 kg/m2, valgus or varus joint alignment, ligamentous instability, or cartilage loss, may require correction before MAT. These can often be achieved with adjunctive or staged procedures, and correction is associated with better procedural outcomes.1,4,5

Several fixation techniques for MAT have been described and can be divided into bony fixation or all-soft-tissue fixation.1,2,4 Bony fixation allows for bone-to-bone healing, and studies have demonstrated less extrusion.1,3 However, an open-accessory approach is necessary to shuttle the transplant into the joint, and limited intraoperative adjustability of the root fixation is possible. All soft-tissue fixation methods are notable for their minimally invasive approach, allowing adjustable intraoperative control of the final fixation point. Soft tissue fixation is limited by its higher rate of graft extrusion, and some studies have reported potentially higher rates of root failure.1,8 This video demonstrates a hybrid fixation technique that combines the stability of bone-to-bone healing with the minimally invasive, flexible fixation profile of an all-soft-tissue approach.

A 30-year-old male laborer presented to our clinic with left focal knee medial joint line tenderness that significantly affected his quality of life. He noted worsening pain with deep flexion and at the end of the day after activity. Twelve months prior, the patient was diagnosed with a chronic bucket-handle medial meniscal tear, which was elected to be treated nonoperatively. During his course of physical therapy, the patient reported subsequent injury and elected to undergo partial meniscectomy 6 months before presentation for symptom management. On examination, he demonstrated neutral alignment, a positive medial McMurray, focal medial joint line tenderness, and a stable ligamentous examination.

Plain radiographs demonstrated a medial joint space narrowing without evidence of osteophyte formation. Neutral coronal alignment on the long leg radiographs was observed. The patient underwent magnetic resonance imaging. Medial meniscal deficiency consistent with prior partial meniscectomy was observed in the sagittal plane. There were no evident focal or diffuse cartilage injuries, and the ligaments were intact. Similarly, a medial meniscal deficiency consistent with previous partial meniscectomy was observed in the coronal plane. There was no evidence for focal or diffuse cartilage injuries. The patient elected to undergo medial meniscal allograft transplantation. The following video demonstrates a technique for medial meniscal allograft transplantation, focusing on root preparation and a hybrid fixation technique combining a bony and an all-soft-tissue approach.

Technique Description

The patient is positioned supine with a lateral post. A standard examination under anesthesia is performed to evaluate the range of motion and ligamentous stability.

Graft Preparation

A fresh-frozen meniscal allograft hemiplateau, size-matched using the Pollard method, was prepared on the back table. 6 Landmarks corresponding to the posterior horn-midbody junction and midbody-anterior horn junction were marked to aid in orientation during implantation.

Using an osteotome, the posterior root was carefully separated from the tibial bone. The meniscocapsular junction was incised, maintaining a thin rim (approximately 1-2 mm) of capsule along the periphery of the graft. The root is meticulously trimmed, leaving a small 1 to 2 mm sliver of bone attached to preserve the full length of the meniscus. Two nonabsorbable sutures were passed through the bony wafer at the posterior root in a horizontal mattress configuration for later shuttling and fixation. The anterior root was then prepared similarly to the posterior root. A thin bony sliver was left attached to the root, and the root was secured with sutures as before. Planned suture fixation points were marked on the superior surface of the graft.

Arthroscopic Preparation

The diagnostic arthroscopy showed the native medial meniscus remnant and intact chondral surfaces of the medial tibial plateau and femoral condyle. To improve access to the medial compartment, the medial collateral ligament (MCL) was trephined just distal to the joint line using a 23-gauge needle. The residual meniscal tissue was trimmed to a stable peripheral rim of approximately 1 to 2 mm. The anterior and posterior root attachment sites were debrided with a radiofrequency probe.

Posterior Root Tunnel Creation and Graft Passage

An anterior cruciate ligament tibial guide was positioned at the anatomic footprint of the posterior root, and a tibial tunnel was drilled from the anteromedial cortex using a 2.4-mm cannulated drill. The suture was shuttled through this tunnel and retrieved via the anteromedial portal. The MAT graft was then inserted into the joint through the anteromedial portal using a grasper to prevent twisting and facilitate easier positioning. Simultaneously, the sutures of the posterior root of the graft were provisionally tensioned into its anatomic tunnel.

Meniscocapsular Fixation

All-inside meniscal repair devices were used to secure the graft to the posterior capsule, beginning at the superior surface. Additional vertical mattress sutures were placed progressively anteriorly, on both the upper and undersurfaces of the graft, until it was evenly balanced and stable. Typically, 6 to 8 all-inside devices are used.

Anterior Root Tunnel and Fixation

Attention was then turned to the anterior root site. The tibial tunnel was created from the anteromedial aspect of the tibia. This tunnel was drilled more anteriorly and medially than the posterior root tunnel. A suture shuttle was passed through this tunnel and retrieved from the anteromedial portal, allowing the anterior root sutures of the graft to be passed and tensioned in position. An additional all-inside suture was placed at the body-anterior horn junction to complete fixation.

Transtibial Fixation

The transtibial sutures were sequentially tied over cortical buttons with the knee maintained at 60° of flexion.

Pearls

An extended incision on the medial side is recommended to ensure adequate visualization and working space. Trephination of the MCL should be performed to gain access to the medial compartment. The remaining meniscal tissue should be carefully dissected and debrided to create sufficient space for graft placement. The allograft is then shuttled into the joint using a clamp. For tibial fixation, 2 separate bone tunnels are recommended, 1 for the anterior root and 1 for the posterior root, to ensure anatomic restoration of the meniscal attachment points and optimal load distribution.

Postoperative Protocol

Postoperatively, the patient was kept nonweightbearing in a hinged knee brace for 6 weeks, using crutches for ambulation. Range of motion was limited to 0° to 60° during the first 4 weeks. An unloader brace was initiated at 3 months, with restrictions against deep flexion for 6 months and high-impact activity for 1 year. Return to full athletic activity was typically permitted at approximately 12 months postoperatively.

Results and Discussion

Long-term outcomes of meniscal allograft transplantation have shown promising clinical results. A systematic review encompassing 688 MATs at a mean age of 33 years found 74% survivorship at 10 years and 60% at 15 years post-MAT. 5 This cohort was composed of 53.8% bony fixation and 46.2% suture-only fixation techniques. 5 Postoperative IKDC scores ranged from 46 to 77, and patients saw improvement in patient-reported outcomes compared to preoperative measures.

Another recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 25 studies examined graft extrusion after MAT in patients who had soft-tissue or bony fixation. 1 The authors found graft extrusion is common after both MMAT and lateral MAT and was independent of the root fixation technique. However, MAT extrusion with bony fixation was, depending on the outcome of measurement, lower than or equal to that with soft-tissue fixation. The study also highlighted limited and conflicting evidence surrounding the use of capsulodesis to reduce MAT extrusion. 1

Conclusion

MAT represents a suitable option for young, active patients with total or functional loss of the meniscus. The hybrid root fixation technique combines the flexibility of soft-tissue–based methods with the advantages of bone-to-bone healing. Although all soft-tissue fixation tends to result in greater extrusion, it is less invasive than bony fixation, and the clinical relevance of this extrusion remains uncertain. Long-term outcomes of hybrid fixation are still pending.
