Cardiovascular Adverse Events Following Bispecific T-Cell Redirecting Antibody Therapy
Authors: Eli Grunblatt, Sawsan A Alkhalili, Zhiying Meng, Abigail S Baldridge, Shira Dinner, Leo I Gordon, Jayesh Mehta, Anjali Rao, Seema Singhal, Reem Karmali, Nausheen Akhter
Published: 2026

Bispecific T-cell redirecting antibody (BsAb) therapies are a class of immunotherapies that use bivalent antibodies engineered to contain 2 antigen-binding sites that target cancer cell–specific antigens and native T-cell-specific antigens to colocalize T-cells to tumor sites, leading to the destruction of cancer cells by the immune system.1,2 As of 2025, BsAb therapies are available for treatment of relapsed, refractory leukemia, lymphoma, and multiple myeloma.1 The advent of BsAb therapies has shown significant promise for patients; however, data regarding the effects of these therapies on the cardiovascular system are limited. In this study, we sought to characterize cardiovascular adverse events (CVAEs) following treatment with BsAb therapies.

We retrospectively identified adult patients who received BsAb therapy at Northwestern Medicine between 2022 and 2024. Clinical data were collected via chart review. CVAEs were retrospectively adjudicated according to guidelines3 and defined as the first occurrence of any of the following through 6 months of follow-up: 1) cancer therapy–related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD), defined as new left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) reduction to <40% (severe), new LVEF reduction by ≥10% points to an LVEF of 40% to 49% (moderate), or symptomatic heart failure requiring intensification of diuretic and/or heart failure therapy; 2) new-onset atrial fibrillation among patients without histories of atrial fibrillation; and 3) myocardial infarction (MI), defined by a presentation consistent with acute coronary syndrome. Mild CTRCD (defined by LVEF >50%, decline in global longitudinal strain, or increases in cardiac biomarkers without symptoms) was not classified as a CVAE but was noted as a comparator. Baseline characteristics were compared between patients with and those without CVAEs using the chi-square test, Fisher exact test, 2-sample Student’s t-test, or Wilcoxon test as appropriate. The 6-month cumulative incidence of CVAEs was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier function; competing risks were not used, as no deaths occurred prior to 6 months. The Northwestern University Institutional Review Board approved this study.

Overall, 140 patients received BsAb therapies, including 79 patients with multiple myeloma treated with teclistamab, 38 patients with leukemia treated with blinatumomab, and 20 patients with lymphoma treated with glofitamab, epcoritamab, or mosunetuzumab. Baseline characteristics of the study cohort are shown in Table 1. The mean age of the cohort was 64 ± 17 years, and 51.4% of patients (72 of 140) were woman. Almost one-third (32.3%) of patients (50 of 133) identified as non-White. The median number of cycles of BsAb therapy received was 4 (Q1-Q3: 2-9). Prior to receiving BsAb therapy, 42.1% of patients (59 of 140) received anthracycline therapy, 41.4% (58 of 140) received carfilzomib, 26.4% (37 of 140) received chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy, and 40.0% (56 of 140) underwent stem cell transplantation.

Among patients who received BsAb therapies, the cumulative incidence for any CVAE was 12.3% (95% CI: 7.2%-18.5%). This included moderate to severe CTRCD, 6.6% (95% CI: 2.9%-11.6%); atrial fibrillation, 5.4% (95% CI: 2.2%-9.9%); and MI, 0.8% (95% CI: 0.0004%-3.1%). Mild CTRCD, not included in the CVAE definition, occurred in 5.8% (95% CI: 2.3%-10.6%) of patients. The median time to CVAE development was 47 days (Q1-Q3: 15-107 days) following cycle 1 of BsAb therapy. Seventy-five percent of CVAEs (12 of 16) occurred during cycle 1 of therapy. Overall, 34.3% of patients (48 of 140) developed cytokine release syndrome (CRS) following BsAb therapy (grade 1 in 37 patients, grade 2 in 8 patients, grade 3 in 2 patients, and grade 4 in 1 patient). Three of these patients developed CRS (all grade 1) following cycle 1 and experienced CVAEs. There was no significant association between post-BsAb CRS development and subsequent CVAE (P = 0.69).

Few studies have comprehensively assessed CVAEs in patients with multiple myeloma, lymphoma, and leukemia treated with BsAb therapy. Previous retrospective studies were limited to patients with leukemia treated with blinatumomab.4 In this single-center analysis, the cumulative incidence of CVAEs, including atrial fibrillation, moderate to severe CTRCD, and MI within 6 months of cycle 1 of treatment with BsAb was 12.3% (95% CI: 7.2%-18.5%). There was no association between post-BsAb CRS and subsequent CVAEs. This is in contrast to CAR T-cell therapy, for which many studies have shown a relationship between CRS and CVAE development.5

Limitations of this study include its retrospective design and lack of a control arm. Additionally, as BsAb therapy is currently used as non-first-line therapy for refractory and relapsed malignancies, most of the patients in this study had received extensive previous treatment with anthracyclines, CAR T-cell therapy, and/or stem cell transplantation, which are known to cause CVAEs. Although our study cohort is representative of the type of real-world high-risk patient who would receive BsAb therapy after multiple prior lines of cardiotoxic therapy, given this study’s retrospective design, it is unknown whether the observed cardiotoxicities are a result of delayed toxicities from prior treatments, a direct result of BsAb therapy, or a combination. Further prospective and mechanistic studies are needed to address this question and the differences in CRS-associated CVAEs between CAR T-cell and BsAb therapy. Overall, the relatively low rate of CVAEs following BsAb treatment in this cohort is reassuring for the cardiovascular management of these patients.
