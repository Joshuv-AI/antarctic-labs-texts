Etiologies, outcomes, and predictors of postoperative complications in the surgical management of extrahepatic biliary obstruction: a study at Tikur Anbesa specialized hospital, Ethiopia
Authors: Tebarek Jemal Hassen, Zeki Abubeker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObstructive jaundice has contributed a sizable burden of global mortality, morbidity, economic cost, and hospitalization worldwide cause by benign and malignant conditions.ObjectiveTo assess the etiological pattern, predictors for postoperative complications, and short-term outcomes among patients undergoing surgical intervention for EHBO at Tikur Anbesa Specialized Hospital, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, 2024.MethodsA hospital-based retrospective cohort study was conducted to evaluate the etiological patterns, outcomes, and predictors of complications following surgical intervention for extrahepatic biliary obstruction (EHBO). The study included 122 EHBO patients who were consecutively enrolled during the study period. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test; normally distributed data were expressed as means with standard deviations, whereas skewed variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges. Univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of 30-day postoperative complications. Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsAmong 122 patients undergoing surgical intervention for EHBO, the mean age was 51.8 years (SD 13.2), with a slight male predominance (53.3%). Most patients had good performance status (ECOG 0–1 in 91.8%). Malignant biliary obstruction (MBO) was present in 67 (54.9%), predominantly due to pancreatic cancer (23.0%) and periampullary tumors (18.9%). In MBO patients, mean serum bilirubin decreased from 18.8 mg/dL preoperatively to 1.7 mg/dL at 4 weeks, representing a 90.1% reduction. Curative resection was achieved in 22 (32.8%) patients (Whipple procedure in 19, bile duct excision in 3); the remainder underwent palliative surgery. The 30-day postoperative complication rate was 25.4% (31/122), with mortality of 1.6% (2/122). Surgical site infection was the most frequent complication (19.7%), followed by anastomotic leakage (6.6%). Multivariable analysis identified poorer ECOG status (AOR 6.1, 95% CI 2.0–18.4; p = 0.001) and jaundice duration > 8 weeks (AOR 2.1, 95% CI 1.7–6.3; p = 0.003) as independent predictors of postoperative complications.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that favorable surgical outcomes for EHBO are achievable even in resource-constrained environments. However, further mitigating postoperative morbidity requires targeted perioperative optimization of high-risk patients, specifically those presenting with prolonged jaundice (> 8 weeks), preoperative cholangitis, poor ECOG performance status, hypoalbuminemia, and malignant biliary obstruction (MBO). Consequently, implementing systematic risk stratification and addressing these key clinical predictors are imperative to minimize complications and optimize patient outcomes.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-04000-0.Keywords: Extrahepatic Biliary Obstruction (EHBO), Obstructive jaundice outcome, Predictive factors, Surgical management, Ethiopia

Introduction

Obstructive jaundice represents a prevalent surgical challenge which has been associated with significant morbidity, mortality, and economic burden. It has been characterized by a partial or complete blockage in the biliary tree, leads to an elevation in biliary pressure exceeding 10 cm of water [1], effectively preventing the normal ante-grade flow of bile from the liver and gallbladder into the duodenum. This disruption results in the accumulation of toxic bile salts and metabolic byproducts within the system [1]. Consequently, patients experience a cascade of adverse effects, including significant clinical derangement characterized by liver dysfunction, coagulopathy, organ failure, malnutrition, compromised immunity, and increased susceptibility to infections [2]. These interconnected factors contribute to the overall increase in morbidity and mortality observed in individuals with obstructive jaundice [1] .

Obstruction of the extrahepatic biliary system (EHBO) is generally more common cause of obstructive jaundice than intrahepatic biliary obstruction. The underlying causes of obstructive jaundice are broadly categorized into benign biliary obstruction (BBO) and malignant biliary obstruction (MBO). BBO etiologies include choledocholithiasis (common bile duct stones), benign strictures and biliary sludge. In contrast, MBO can result from various malignancies, such as cholangiocarcinoma, pancreatic cancer, periampullary carcinoma, and metastatic tumors that compress or invade the biliary tree [3, 4]. Malignant obstructive jaundice (MOJ) represents a significant proportion of jaundice cases encountered in hepatobiliary and pancreatic surgery [3]. It stems from either extrinsic compression of the bile duct or intrinsic growth within the duct, whether primary or secondary due to metastases. These processes lead to constriction, or even complete blockage, of the bile ducts, resulting in bile stasis and elevated bilirubin levels, ultimately leading to the clinical manifestations of jaundice. The management of MOJ often presents a complex challenge requiring a multidisciplinary approach to optimize patient outcomes [3]. The most common etiologies include pancreatic adenocarcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma, ampullary/duodenal carcinoma, gallbladder cancer, and metastatic diseases that infiltrate the head of the pancreas and the common bile duct [3]. An analysis of 360 patients with obstructive jaundice treated at the Hepatobiliary Unit of the Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital in Ghana revealed that malignant causes accounted for the majority of cases (314, 87.2%), while benign causes were less frequent (46, 12.8%) [5] In contrast to these findings, studies within Ethiopia suggest a slight predominance of BBO as the cause of obstructive jaundice. Specifically, three hospital-based studies conducted at St. Paul’s Hospital and Tikur Anbessa Hospital, both prominent hepatobiliary centers in Ethiopia, reported that BBO accounted for 68% [6], 50%, and 55% of obstructive jaundice cases, respectively. These diverse findings underscore the importance of understanding the local epidemiology of obstructive jaundice to guide diagnostic and management strategies.

The optimal surgical approach for obstructive jaundice depends on the underlying etiology, whether benign (BBO) or malignant (MBO). Regardless of the cause, successful management often requires advanced hepatobiliary-pancreatic surgical expertise to minimize morbidity and mortality and to improve the patient’s quality of life [7]. The management of MBO can be directed towards curative resection or palliative treatment, focusing on alleviating biliary obstruction, gastric outlet obstruction, and pain control. Curative resection of the underlying cause of MBO is the primary goal of diagnosis and management. However, due to the lack of effective screening mechanisms, the advanced stage at presentation, and the inherently aggressive and lethal nature of many malignancies, palliative decompression of advanced malignant obstruction by biliary catheterization or stent placement often remains a crucial component in the management of these patients. This palliative approach aims to improve biliary drainage, reduce jaundice, and alleviate associated symptoms, ultimately enhancing the patient’s comfort and overall well-being. To achieve biliary palliation, surgical bypass procedures involve creating biliary-enteric anastomosis proximal to the obstruction. This anastomosis diverts the flow of bile directly into the gastrointestinal tract. This can be accomplished using either a Roux-en-Y jejunal limb or a simpler loop configuration utilizing a Braun anastomosis [7]. These surgical bypass options for EHBO include cholecystojejunostomy, hepaticojejunostomy, and choledochojejunostomy [7].

The Lancet Global Surgery initiative has clearly emphasized that perioperative surgical outcomes are a crucial indicator of quality surgical care worldwide. Perioperative complications contribute significantly to global mortality, morbidity, and disability. Surgical interventions for obstructive jaundice, in particular, present a challenging and potentially morbid undertaking. The Lancet Commission on Global Health further suggests that the quality of patient care is fundamentally linked to the strength of a healthcare system’s foundation and the processes of care it employs. Therefore, the outcomes of surgical interventions for obstructive jaundice are likely influenced by a country’s Human Development Index (HDI), the availability of human resources, and the quality of infrastructure, in addition to patient-specific factors. This suggests that surgical outcomes for obstructive jaundice can vary significantly across different geographic regions. For example, studies conducted in two centers in Tanzania revealed overall morbidity rates ranging from 23% to 30% [8, 9] and overall mortality rates ranging from 16% to 20%, with prolonged jaundice, malignancy, and older age identified as significant risk factors for adverse outcomes. Evidence on the aetiology of extrahepatic biliary obstruction and perioperative outcomes in Ethiopia remains scarce. The primary objective of this study was to analyze the causes of extrahepatic obstructive jaundice and associated perioperative outcomes, with the aim of generating data to inform policymakers, researchers, and healthcare providers.

Method

Study area

This study was conducted within the Department of Surgery at Tikur Anbesa Specialized Hospital (TASH), a prominent healthcare institution established in 1974. TASH serves as the primary teaching hospital for the School of Medicine, School of Pharmacy, School of Public Health, and School of Allied Health Sciences. As the largest referral and teaching hospital in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, TASH provides comprehensive diagnostic and treatment services to approximately 400,000 patients annually. The hospital plays a crucial role in the community, offering a wide range of specialty and subspecialty medical services. With a dedicated workforce exceeding 1500 clinical and academic staff, TASH maintains a significant capacity for inpatient care, boasting over 800 beds. Notably, Tikur Anbesa Specialized Hospital stands as the leading high-volume center for hepatobiliary and pancreatic surgery in the country.

Data Collection

The structured questionnaire utilized in the study was crafted based on existing literature and encompassed sections on sociodemographic data, clinical profiles, surgical intervention, radiological and pathological report, laboratory and surgical outcomes. Prior to the primary data collection, the questionnaire underwent a pretest with 5% of the sample size to ensure the clarity and relevance of the questions. Secondary data was taken from electronic medical records in addition to primary data collected via the questionnaire. These records complemented and validated the self-reported data gathered during the study by offering comprehensive clinical and biochemical information about patients who had had surgery for EHBO. Data collection was done by trained two general practitioners and the principal investigator checked the data consistency and completeness in each day.

Data processing and analysis

Following data collection, each questionnaire was reviewed for completeness, referencing the codes assigned during the data collection process. The data were then entered into Epi-Info version 7 before being exported and analyzed using SPSS version 25. Data analysis was performed using SPSS software, version 25. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, and comparisons between groups were conducted using the chi-square test. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were described using means and standard deviations, while skewed data were characterized using medians and interquartile ranges. Prior to multivariable analysis, a multicollinearity test was performed to evaluate potential correlations between categorical, continuous, and binary variables. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values. Variables exhibiting a VIF below 5 and a tolerance above 0.1 were considered sufficiently independent and were subsequently included in the multivariable binary logistic regression analysis. Conversely, variables with a VIF score 5 and above and a tolerance value below 0.1, were excluded from the final model to avoid issues arising from multicollinearity. To identify potential predictors, a univariate binary logistic regression analysis was initially performed to examine factors associated with 30-day mortality and 30th day postoperative complications. Variables demonstrating a p-value of 0.25 or less in the bivariate analysis were then entered into a multivariable binary logistic regression model. This model was used to assess the independent associations between the various factors and the outcomes of 30-day mortality and postoperative complications. The results of the logistic regression analysis are reported as adjusted odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. A p-value less than 0.05 were used as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

The study cohort comprised 122 patients with a mean age of 51.8 ± 13.2 years and a slight male predominance (53.3%). A normal BMI was noted in 80.3% (98/120) of the evaluated patients. Pre-existing comorbidities were documented in 28.7% of cases, with diabetes mellitus and hypertension affecting 15.6% and 14.8% of the cohort, respectively; 14.8% of patients had two or more comorbidities. Symptomatically, abdominal pain (98.4%) and jaundice (95.9%) were nearly universal. Classical signs of biliary obstruction were highly prevalent, including dark urine and/or pale stools in 77.0% of patients, and pruritus in 73.0%. Diagnosis and causes of obstructive jaundice

Most of the 122 patients presented with good performance status: 40 (32.8%) were ECOG 0 and 72 (59.0%) were ECOG 1. Diagnostic modalities varied based on the initial clinical suspicion; a combination of ultrasound and CT was most common (57.3%, n = 70), followed by ultrasound with MRCP (15.6%, n = 19). Distal obstruction was the predominant anatomical site (81.1%, n = 99), and most cases had malignant etiologies (54.9%, n = 67). Overall, common bile duct (CBD) stones (25.4%) and pancreatic cancer (23.0%) were the most frequent causes of jaundice. Among benign biliary obstructions (BBO), choledocholithiasis (56.4%) and CBD strictures (18.2%) predominated. In contrast, pancreatic cancer (41.8%) and periampullary cancer (34.3%) were the primary drivers of malignant biliary obstruction (MBO) (Table 2; Figs. 1, 2 and 3).

Post treatment laboratory values

Following treatment for obstructive jaundice, laboratory values, particularly bilirubin levels, demonstrated a rapid decline. The mean total bilirubin of MBO concentration decreased from 18.8 mg/dL preoperatively to 5.7 mg/dL at the first week post-surgery, representing a 69.7% reduction. By the fourth week, the mean total bilirubin of MBO further decreased to 1.7 mg/dL, reflecting an overall reduction of 90.1% from the preoperative level. The 30-day postoperative complication rate after surgical interventions of obstructive jaundice was 31 out of 122 patients (25.4%), while the mortality rate was 2 out of 122 patients (1.6%). The majority of complications were classified as Clavien-Dindo Grade I, occurring in 12 patients (9.8%), followed by Grade II complications in 8 patients (6.6%). The most common types of complications included surgical site infections (SSI) in 24 patients (19.7%), anastomotic leakage in 8 patients (6.6%), and renal failure in 7 patients (5.4%). Discussion

This study investigated the etiology of obstructive jaundice, perioperative outcomes, and predictors of postoperative complications in patients with extrahepatic biliary obstruction (EHBO) managed at a high‑volume hepatobiliary–pancreatic center. Our cohort consisted of relatively young patients with low American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status and favorable Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance scores. The observed 30‑day mortality and complication rates—1.6% and 25.4%, respectively—are low and comparable with reports from both high‑resource centers and some limited‑resource settings.

Obstructive jaundice demonstrated a near-equal gender distribution, with a slight male predominance (male: female ratio of 1.14:1). While the overall trend showed this almost similar distribution, a divergence was observed in the underlying causes; benign biliary obstruction (BBO) was more prevalent in females, while malignant biliary obstruction (MBO) was more frequently diagnosed in males. This disparity may be attributed to differences in risk factor profiles between the sexes. Specifically, common bile duct (CBD) stones, the leading cause of BBO in our study, were significantly more common in females. Conversely, pancreatic and periampullary cancers, the leading causes of MBO, were primarily associated with smoking, a more prevalent habit among males in our patient population. These findings align with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia [6], as well as research from other countries, including Tanzania [9], Mali, Nigeria [10], Uganda [11] ,and India [12], suggesting a consistent global pattern in the etiology and gender distribution of obstructive jaundice. Surgical intervention for extrahepatic biliary obstruction (EHBO) encompasses both curative resections and palliative bypass procedures. Palliative interventions aim to address the obstruction by diverting bile flow from the gallbladder and liver, managing gastric outlet obstruction or prophylaxis, and relieving debilitating pruritus. Our study revealed that approximately 32.8% of patients with malignant biliary obstruction (MBO) underwent curative surgical resection, while the remaining patients received open surgical bypass procedures. Despite the potential for curative resection, several factors contribute to the limited number of patients eligible for this approach. The inherent invasiveness of many cancers causing MBO, coupled with the late appearance of symptoms often results in advanced disease stages at the time of diagnosis [13].

Perioperative complications and mortality serve as key global indicators of surgical quality and safety. In our study at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital, we observed a 30-day complication rate of 25.4% and a mortality rate of 1.6% within our high-volume hepatobiliary and pancreatic surgery setting. When benchmarked against international literature, these results are within expected ranges for major hepatobiliary surgery. Our findings demonstrate that safe surgical outcomes are attainable at our center despite resource constraints. The low mortality rate in our study could also be partly from high proportion of palliative surgery for MBO. Consequently, the focus of risk management should be on the identified predictors of morbidity to further refine the quality of perioperative care. Optimizing patient outcomes further relies on addressing our study key independent predictors of morbidity: poorer baseline functional status (ECOG 1 or 2), prolonged jaundice (> 8 weeks), hypoalbuminemia (< 3 g/dL), and malignant biliary obstruction (MBO). Preoperative cholangitis also played a critical role, likely acting as a physiological stressor that compromised systemic defenses. These results align with international literature, suggesting these predictors of morbidity have broad clinical applicability. Finally, our study identified an intriguing relationship between age and morbidity, where younger patients experienced higher complication rates. This unexpected finding was likely driven by the predominance of malignant biliary obstructions (MBO) within the younger demographic of our patient cohort.

A previous analysis of 116 operated patients at Saint Paul’s Millennium Medical College, also in Ethiopia, reported a significantly lower overall postoperative complication rate of 15.5% [6]. Conversely, another study conducted at Yekatit 12 Hospital Medical College, Ethiopia, reported a postoperative complication rate of approximately 36%, considerably higher than our findings [14]. These observable differences in complication rates across institutions may be associated with variations in surgical expertise and the quality of available healthcare infrastructure. Perioperative complications following surgical interventions for extrahepatic biliary obstruction (EHBO) exhibit significant variability, leading to differing surgical outcomes across geographic regions and patient populations. Various studies have reported diverse rates of perioperative complications after EHBO treatment. When benchmarked against international literature, a mortality rate of 1.6% is objectively low and compares favorably with high-volume centers globally, in major hepatobiliary surgery. For instance, large-scale studies in high-resource settings report mortality rates between 1% and 6% for similar EHBO interventions [15–18]. Similarly, a 25.4% morbidity rate is consistent with reported outcomes from both high-resource and some similar limited-resource settings [11, 19–21]. For example, studies conducted in two centers in Tanzania reported overall morbidity rates ranging from 23% to 30% [8, 9] and overall higher mortality rates ranging from 16% to 20%, with prolonged jaundice identified as a notable contributing factor.

This study provides significant insights into the clinical characteristics, postoperative outcome, and predictors of postoperative complication after surgical management of EHBO. Nevertheless, the retrospective nature of the data introduces inherent biases and exclusion of non-operative cases limits findings to the surgical subset, potentially restricting generalizability to the broader EHBO population. Future research should prioritize multicenter prospective studies that include non-operative cohorts and larger data sample to provide a more holistic view of EHBO. Furthermore, targeted investigation is required to elucidate the underlying causes of the unexpectedly high complication rates observed in younger patients undergoing surgical intervention.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that favorable surgical outcomes for EHBO are achievable even in resource-constrained environments. In our setting, open surgical intervention remains the primary approach for extrahepatic biliary obstruction (EHBO), as minimally invasive procedures are scarce. The inherent multifactorial risks associated with EHBO are further amplified by open surgical approaches, increasing the likelihood of adverse postoperative outcomes. Therefore, rigorous patient risk stratification, thorough perioperative optimization, and precise management of identified risk factors are imperative to reduce complications and enhance patient outcomes.
