A Case of Parapharyngeal Emphysema due to Temporal Bone Fracture as a Sequel of Traumatic Road Accident
Authors: Muhammad Umer Ishaq, Muhammad Ans, Muhammad Soban Khan, Abdul Sattar, Anisha Sah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report the case of a 45‐year‐old male who presented to the emergency department with otorrhagia following a road traffic accident. Initial examination showed no neurological deficits, and computed tomography (CT) imaging revealed a subtle right temporal bone fracture with associated parapharyngeal emphysema. The emphysema resolved within 2.5 weeks after symptomatic treatment, with no complications on follow‐up. This case highlights the importance of vigilance in detecting subtle temporal bone fractures and their rare complications, such as parapharyngeal emphysema, to prevent serious outcomes, including brain herniation.

Introduction

The parapharyngeal space extending from the skull base to the hyoid bone is a potential space that can accumulate fluid or, rarely, air due to trauma [1]. Parapharyngeal emphysema, presenting as air‐fluid levels on imaging, is a rare complication of temporal bone fractures, which more commonly result in facial nerve palsy, hearing loss, vertigo, and cerebrospinal fluid leaks [2, 3]. Due to its rarity and the possibility of serious adverse outcomes such as brain herniation, prompt recognition through imaging is critical [4]. We present the case of a 45‐year‐old male who presented to the emergency department following a road traffic accident, with computed tomography (CT) scan confirming subtle temporal bone fracture with associated parapharyngeal emphysema.

Case History/Examination

A 45‐year‐old Pakistani male presented to the emergency ward with otorrhagia following a road traffic accident after striking his head on the ground. Examination revealed active bleeding from the external acoustic meatus without evidence of conductive hearing loss. The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) was tender but exhibited normal mobility. There was no evidence of other head and neck injuries. There were no neurological deficits as evidenced by his symmetric face and ability to close his eyes with no diplopia or inability to abduct his eyes. The patient had no history of previous head trauma, skull fractures, chronic otitis media, or prior ENT interventions.

Initial CT scan revealed no visible fracture of the temporal bone with the possibility of a subtle fracture. Further CT scan revealed a fracture line in the right temporal bone with emphysema in the parapharyngeal space and a fracture of the external auditory canal (Figure 1). Patient was observed and treated symptomatically. His emphysema diminished within two and a half weeks. He had no further complications when observed in follow‐up visits.

Methods/Differential Diagnosis

The preliminary differential diagnosis included transverse temporal fracture, basilar skull fracture, pneumomediastinum with air dissection, and tracheobronchial traumatic injury. CSF leak was considered but ruled out with no evidence of otorrhea or rhinorrhea. Chest X‐ray showed no mediastinal air, ruling out pneumomediastinum. Non‐contrast CT scan revealed mild compression in the right temporal region with subtle midline shift, raising concerns for brain herniation due to mass effect. Brain CT also revealed a subtle temporal bone fracture with associated parapharyngeal emphysema. The middle ear structures were intact, indicating no conductive hearing loss. These findings prompted multidisciplinary consultation with ENT and neurosurgery to guide further management.

Conclusion/Results

Non‐contrast brain CT, viewed in brain window, revealed hyperdensity along the right cerebral convexity, consistent with extradural hematoma (Figure 2).

Axial non‐contrast CT scan brain window showing lens shaped hyper‐density along the right cerebral convexity implying extradural hematoma (red arrow).

On further inspection of the imaging, the right mastoid air cells showed opacification, indicating involvement of the right petrous part of the temporal bone (Figure 3). There was air within soft tissue on the right side, indicating communication with the external environment, likely a subtle non‐displaced fracture not initially visible on CT (Figure 4). The middle ear structures were intact, as shown by the axial CT scan of the skull base (Figure 5). The patient was observed for any worsening neurological focal deficits due to potential hematoma expansion, with management guided by ENT and Neurosurgical consultation. Facial nerve examination was unremarkable.

The parapharyngeal emphysema resolved within 2.5 weeks with symptomatic treatment, and no adverse outcomes were noted at a 1‐week follow‐up.

Discussion

Temporal bone fracture is common after motor vehicle accidents, sports‐related trauma, and interpersonal violence, and it can affect critical structures such as the facial nerve and auditory system [5] Parapharyngeal emphysema, a rare complication of temporal bone fracture, poses diagnostic challenges due to its subtle image findings.

The facial nerve is at risk of injury in temporal bone fractures with an estimated 6%–7% incidence of facial nerve palsy. Immediate facial nerve decompression is indicated in such cases, with imaging and otologic examination assisting in the decision for surgery [6]. In our case, the patient had no facial palsy, as evident by no facial drooping or any facial nerve deficits.

Unlike previous cases involving facial nerve palsy [7], middle ear effusion with hearing loss [8], or mild conductive hearing loss [9], our patient presented with isolated parapharyngeal emphysema without neurological deficits, facial nerve palsy, and hearing impairment. This unique presentation highlights the variability in the presentation of temporal bone fractures (Table 1).

Rao et al., presented a case of temporal bone fracture with middle ear effusion, where initial CT scan revealed posterior scalp hematoma but missed longitudinal fracture through right mastoid bone, similar to our case, further supporting the challenge of detecting subtle temporal bone fractures on imaging [8]. There are multiple factors contributing to missed findings on CT imaging, as underlined by Spies et al., like lack of experience, fatigue, heavy workloads or inadequate training of radiologists but overlooked the challenge of detecting non‐displaced fractures [7]. Like our case, their case was managed conservatively with observation for neurological deficit.

Sbai et al. presented a case following a temporal bone fracture from a motor vehicle accident with concomitant abducens and facial nerve palsy, presenting with facial droop and an inability to abduct the eyes, unlike our patient who had no neurological deficits. They stated that the management of facial nerve palsy depends on symptom onset, with late presenting symptoms often treated with corticosteroids, whereas our case only required symptomatic treatment with a thorough evaluation of neurological status [9].

This case emphasizes the need for thorough evaluation of imaging of such patients and clinical correlation to identify such findings. The resolution of parapharyngeal emphysema within 2.5 weeks with conservative symptomatic treatment highlights the efficacy of conservative management. Unlike cases requiring a surgical approach for facial nerve palsy or hearing loss [7, 10], our patient's unremarkable recovery underscores the role of careful monitoring and multidisciplinary evaluation in managing this rare complication of temporal bone fracture.

Conclusion

Parapharyngeal emphysema is a rare sequel of head trauma. Our case highlights the role of careful imaging review and a multidisciplinary approach involving otorhinolaryngologists and radiologists for accurate identification of subtle temporal bone fractures and timely management of any potential adverse outcomes such as brain herniation.
