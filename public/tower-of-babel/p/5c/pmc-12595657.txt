Integrated circulating tumor DNA-based prognostic algorithm for limited stage small-cell lung cancer under definitive chemoradiotherapy and utility in consolidation immunotherapy benefit prediction
Authors: Yao Fu, Jianming Zhou, Xiaoxi Chen, Naiqing Ding, Xiaotian Zhao, Hua Bao, Yang Yang, Ning Xia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReliable biomarkers to identify inoperable limited stage small-cell lung cancer (LS-SCLC) benefiting from post-definitive chemoradiotherapy (dCRT) immunotherapy is valuable. This study aims to develop a circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA)-based algorithm to stratify progression risk and predict survival benefit from consolidation immunotherapy.MethodsBaseline tumor tissues from 203 consecutive LS-SCLC receiving dCRT and a cBioPortal LS-SCLC cohort (n = 218) were analyzed to identify tissue-based prognostic genetic alterations. Plasma ctDNA after induction chemotherapy initiation (post-ICT) and/or during subsequent thoracic radiotherapy (TRT) were collected from two independent dCRT-only cohorts (training, n = 49; test, n = 32) and from 86 patients receiving post-dCRT consolidation immunotherapy, for prognostic algorithm development and utility investigation.ResultsTissue-based prognostic biomarkers were generally rare except for PTEN, whose mutations were associated with antigen processing and presentation pathway enrichment (p.adjust = 0.008), and better progression-free survival (PFS, p = 0.047) and overall survival (p = 0.040). A Bayesian inference prognostic algorithm, combining post-ICT and TRT ctDNA detection, receipt of prophylactic cranial irradiation, and post-ICT shrinkage, accurately predicted 3-year progression (time-dependent AUC = 0.796) and stratified the training cohort into two subgroups exhibiting significantly different PFS (p = 0.008), with consistent performance in the test cohort (time-dependent AUC = 0.745; PFS, p = 0.098). The posterior Bayesian algorithm involving the test cohort revealed ctDNA-based risk classification as an independent predictor of PFS (p < 0.001). Notably, significantly improved PFS under consolidation immunotherapy was exclusively observed in patients predicted as high-risk (p = 0.004), with increasing benefit observed at higher high-risk thresholds.ConclusionsSerial ctDNA monitoring during dCRT is a critical approach to predicting inoperable LS-SCLC progression and consolidation immunotherapy benefit identification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02462-y.Keywords: LS-SCLS, CtDNA, NGS, Bayesian inference

Introduction

Small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) accounts for approximately 15% of newly diagnosed lung cancer cases, predominantly in current or former smokers [1, 2]. Patients with limited stage SCLC (LS-SCLC) who are not eligible for surgical treatment used to receive definitive chemoradiotherapy (dCRT) plus prophylactic cranial irradiation (PCI), despite initial therapeutic response followed by rapid disease progression and poor prognosis [3, 4]. The median overall survival (OS) of patients with stage III SCLC ranges from approximately 12 to 18 months (mons), significantly worse than those with stage I/II diseases [5]. The promising results of the ADRIATIC study has demonstrated the significant survival benefits of post-dCRT consolidation immunotherapy (IO). Compared to placebo, patients assigned to the durvalumab group exhibited superior OS (median, 55.9 vs 33.4 mons; hazard ratio [HR], 0.73) and progression-free survival (PFS; median, 16.6 vs 9.2 mons; HR, 0.76) [6]. Although consolidation durvalumab could generally improve survival outcomes among LS-SCLC receiving dCRT, the investigation of predictive biomarkers to identify patients who will benefit from post-dCRT consolidation IO is valuable [6, 7].

Multiple recurrent genetic alterations have been identified in SCLC, including both inactivating mutations of tumor suppressors (TP53, RB1, KMT2D, etc.) and activating mutations of oncogenes (PIK3CA, EGFR, KRAS, etc.) [8]. In addition to mutated genes underlying SCLC, affected molecular pathways have also been revealed, such as the cell cycle progression pathway related to cell cycle arrest and apoptosis and the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway involved in SCLC proliferation [9]. Of note, the small amounts of available baseline tumor tissue is a prevalent issue that limits the exploratory biomarker research for LS-SCLC prognosis prediction [10].

Plasma circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), which is a non-invasive liquid biopsy approach showing high accuracy and concordance compared to tissue biopsies, has demonstrated advantages in assessing treatment response, tracking disease progression, and personalizing therapeutic strategies in lung cancer [11–13]. Previous studies have revealed the prognostic value of ctDNA detection in LS-SCLC patients undergoing dCRT. For example, a plasma-based ctDNA assay covering 14 frequently mutated genes was evaluated as a biomarker for disease progression in patients receiving dCRT [14]; a 105-gene next-generation sequencing (NGS) assay was applied to the investigation of ctDNA dynamic changes in SCLC [15]. However, the role and clinical capability of ctDNA at various phases of LS-SCLC treatment has not been well studied in large cohorts in terms of the promising value of prognosis prediction and identification of patients benefiting from post-dCRT consolidation IO.

Herein, we first performed an exploratory analysis of baseline tumor tissue to identify prognostic genetic alterations. Subsequently, leveraging the advantages of liquid biopsy for dynamic monitoring, we developed and validated an integrated prognostic algorithm based on serial plasma ctDNA and clinical factors under conventional dCRT. We further evaluated the utility of the algorithm in identifying patients who could benefit significantly from post-dCRT consolidation IO.

Methods

Study cohorts and clinical data

This study was approved by Clinical Medicine Department of Nanjing Geneseeq Technology Inc., and all patients provided informed consent. This study was conducted in accordance with the Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Consecutive patients with LS-SCLC who had baseline tumor tissue biopsy and were subsequently treated with dCRT were retrospectively enrolled between February 2012 and Dec 2018 at The First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University, Zhongda Hospital and Nanjing Brain Hospital (Fig. 1). Patients fulfilling the following inclusion criteria were incorporated: (1) aged ≥ 18 years; (2) histologically proven LS-SCLC according to Veterans Administration Lung Study Group (VALG); (3) available treatment-naïve baseline tumor tissue biopsy; (4) with at least one detectable somatic genetic alteration; (5) inoperable patients receiving dCRT alone before disease progression. In addition, patients with LS-SCLC with plasma ctDNA samples during dCRT were retrospectively enrolled between Dec 2020 and Nov 2023 at The First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University and Drum Tower Hospital. Patients fulfilling the following inclusion criteria were incorporated: (1) aged ≥ 18 years; (2) histologically proven LS-SCLC according to VALG; (3) available plasma ctDNA samples collected after induction chemotherapy (post-ICT) and/or during subsequent thoracic radiotherapy (TRT); (4) inoperable patients receiving dCRT alone (ctDNA dCRT cohort) or with additional post-dCRT consolidation IO (ctDNA IO cohort). An external LS-SCLC cohort was grouped based on publicly available data from cBioPortal database.

Study design and study cohorts. This study included both baseline tumor tissue biopsies and subsequent plasma ctDNA liquid biopsies to investigate molecular prognostic biomarkers under conventional dCRT which were then integrated in the development of an algorithm for survival outcome prediction and identifying patients who could benefit significantly from post-dCRT consolidation IO

Most patients received at least 4 cycles of standard-of-care induction chemotherapy (ICT) with Etoposide + Cisplatin/Carboplatin. Thoracic Radiotherapy (TRT) was delivered concurrently with the 3rd or 4th cycle of chemotherapy. The standard total dose was either 60–66 Gy in 30 fractions over 6 weeks for the standard regimen or 45 Gy in 15 fractions over 3 weeks for hyper-fractionated bid schedules. Radiotherapy was administered using intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) or volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), and treatment planning was performed based on CT imaging to define the gross tumor volume and clinical target volume, encompassing the primary tumor and involved lymph nodes. Following dCRT, Prophylactic Cranial Irradiation (PCI) was offered to patients who achieved a complete or partial response, with a standard dose of 25 Gy in 10 fractions within 2 weeks. Patients in the ctDNA IO cohort received consolidation immunotherapy (IO) with an anti-PD-L1 agent, predominantly durvalumab, Serplulimab, or Sugemalimab, which was administered every two to three weeks for a maximum of one year or until disease progression or intolerable toxicity.

Sample collection, cell-free DNA extract, library preparation, and targeted panel next-generation sequencing

Genomic DNA of tumor tissue was extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen). Approximately 10 mL of peripheral blood was collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tubes and subsequently centrifuged at 1800 × g for 10 min at room temperature within 2 h to separate plasma and leukocytes which served as the normal controls to filter germline mutations. Plasma cell-free DNA (cfDNA) was extracted from approximately 2 mL plasma using the QIAamp DNA extraction kit (Qiagen). The concentrations and purities of cfDNA were verified using the Nanodrop2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by sample quantification using the dsDNA HS Assay Kit on the Qubit 3.0 (Life Technologies). The library preparation of cfDNA was performed using KAPA Hyper Prep kit (KAPA Biosystems), involving end repair and adapter ligation, which were carried out using Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter). Sequencing libraries were then amplified by polymerase chain reaction, followed by purification. The size and distribution of the library were checked using the 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). Customized probes Pulmocan™ (Nanjing Geneseeq Technology Inc.) was used for hybridization enrichment. After enrichment, libraries were sequenced on the Hiseq4000 NGS platform (Illumina). The targeted depth of leukocytes, tumor tissue, and plasma sample were 200 ×, 1000 ×, and 30,000 ×, respectively.

Sequence data processing, mutation calling, and ctDNA detection identification

Trimmomatic was employed for FASTQ file quality control [16], and leading and trailing low-quality or N bases (quality reading < 20) were removed. High-quality reads were aligned with the reference genome (GRCh37-hg19) using modified Burrows-Wheeler Aligner with BWA-MEM algorithm (BWA-mem, v0.7.12) [17], followed by deduplication. Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions/deletions (indels) were identified with SCALPEL (http://scalpel.sourceforge.net) and the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK), respectively. Germline mutations from leukocyte samples were identified using GATK. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms were excluded if prevalence was above 1% in the 1000 Genomes Project database, the Exome Aggregation Consortium, or the gnomAD database. For tissue samples, SNVs and indels with variant allele frequency ≥ 2% and ≥ 4 supporting reads were retained; for liquid biopsies, SNVs and indels with variant allele frequency ≥ 0.02% and ≥ 2 supporting reads were retained. Tumor purity was analyzed via ABSOLUTE [18]. The CNVKit ADTEx (http://adtex.sourceforge.net) [19] was used to calculate purity adjusted gene-level copy number variations (CNVs), with fold changes of > 1.6 and < 0.6 for detecting CNV gain and CNV loss respectively. ctDNA detection was defined by a tumor-naïve method as ctDNA-positive if ≥ 1 somatic alteration was retained after filtering clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential.

RNA-sequencing data analysis

In the external LS-SCLC cohort, differentially expressed genes were identified by comparing geometric means for individual genes between subgroups. The pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes for Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Gene Ontology (GO), and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using the ClusterProfiler package in R. To correct for multiple comparisons across all tested genes, p-values were adjusted using the FDR method with a cutoff of 0.01.

Survival prediction model development and Bayesian approach

Patients in the ctDNA dCRT cohort with ctDNA samples at both post-ICT and TRT time points were split into a training cohort and a test cohort by the enrollment date. Those enrolled between July 2018 and July 2021 were included in the training cohort, and the remaining patients enrolled after August 2021 were included in the test cohort. ctDNA detection and clinical variable (such as patient age, sex, smoking history, disease stage, PCI treatment, tumor shrinkage percentages after ICT, etc.) selection was performed using Elastic-Net penalised Cox regression (α = 0.5, type.measure = "deviance", nfolds = 5) by the glmnet package in R, with PFS survival data as the outcome. To mitigate the random effect during variable selection, we repeated the process for 100 times and included variables selected for ≥ 40 times. The performance of Elastic-Net penalised Cox based prediction model was sequentially evaluated in the training cohort itself, leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) analysis, and in the test cohort. A threshold for risk scores to stratify patients with high or low progression risk was determined using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves with area under the curve (AUC).

Similarly, retained ctDNA and clinical predictors were used in training a Bayesian prediction model with non-informative priors based on the training cohort (family = lognormal, iter = 10,000), using the brms package in R [20]. After performance assessment in the training cohort, LOOCV analysis, and in the test cohort, the test cohort was reused as new evidence for the trained model where coefficient distributions based on the training cohort (posteriors of the previous step) were considered as priors, to obtain updated posterior beliefs, which was followed by performance evaluation using the entire patients with ctDNA samples. The predicted Bayesian risk scores were calculated using the posterior_predict function of the brms package. The threshold for Bayesian risk scores for progression risk stratification was determined by time-dependent ROC with AUC, where 1000 times bootstrap with replacement was employed and the median value of thresholds in 1000 bootstrap loops was used to classify progression risk.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were performed on clinical characteristics. Fisher’s exact/Chi-squared tests were used to compare the frequencies of categorical variables. (Paired) student's t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to test the difference in continuous variables. The increased or decreased trends of continuous variables were tested by Kraskal-Wallis tests. Survival analyses were carried out using Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests, and cox proportional hazards models were fitted to estimate HR with 95% confidence interval (CI), and the proportionality of hazards was assessed using log(-log) survival plots. Multivariable survival analyses were carried out using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models adjusting for confounding effects. The discrimination of prediction models was assessed using ROC curves combined with AUC and time-dependent discrimination accuracy evaluation approach [21], using the pROC and survivalROC packages in R; the calibration was visualized at various time points separately (12, 24, and 36 mons after treatment initiation) using the rms package in R [22]. Individuals with missing data were excluded from analysis. All quoted p-values were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 were statistically significant unless otherwise stated. Data were analyzed using R software (version 4.2.2), additionally using the survival, survminer, and epiR packages.

Results

Patients and samples

A total of 203 LS-SCLC patients who had baseline tumor tissue biopsy and were subsequently received dCRT alone were retrospectively enrolled (Fig. 1). The average age at diagnosis was 59 yr, including 47.3% patients ≥ 60 yr (Table S1). 70.4% of patients were males, while current or former smokers accounted for 44.3% of this cohort. Within the cBioPortal database, 218 patients with LS-SCLC from four studies [23–26] were grouped into the external cohort for results validation and transcriptional analysis (Table S2). Although 21.1% of the external cohort had missing values about clinical stage at diagnosis, 29.8% of them had stage I diseases, which was eligible for surgical treatment. The LS-SCLC ctDNA dCRT cohort included 81 LS-SCLC inoperable patients who received dCRT alone and had plasma ctDNA samples at both post-ICT and TRT time points. This cohort was further divided into training cohort (n = 45) and test cohort (n = 29) based on sites of recruiting (seven patients without tumor shrinkage data either after ICT or during dCRT were not included in the prediction model training and test) (Table S3). Balanced baseline clinical characteristics were observed between the training and test cohort. The ctDNA IO cohort consisted of 86 LS-SCLC patients who were treated with post-dCRT consolidation IO, and 89.% of them had stage III diseases (Table S4).

Tissue-based baseline PTEN alteration is a positive prognostic biomarker for LS-SCLC receiving dCRT

The genetic profiles of 203 LS-SCLC patients with baseline tumor tissue samples are summarized in Fig. 2A. TP53 and RB1 were the most commonly altered genes, detected in approximately 93% and 82% of this cohort. Other somatic mutations exhibited relatively low prevalence (< 20%), such as FAT1, CREBBP, LRP1B, ATR, NOTCH1, NF1, PTEN, etc. Among genes mutated in ≥ 5% of patients, only PTEN was strongly associated with both good PFS (p = 0.047) and prolonged OS (p = 0.04), though GATA4, GRIN2A, NOTCH1, and RECQL4 alterations were significantly related to superior or inferior prognosis alone (Fig. 2B, C). In the external LS-SCLC cohort, a trend of better OS was observed in patients carrying PTEN alterations (p = 0.064) while PFS data were not available for validation (Fig. 2D).

The GSEA analysis using the transcriptional data of the external cohort demonstrated multiple immune-related signaling pathways enriched in LS-SCLC harboring mutated PTEN genes (Fig. 2E, F), including antigen processing and presentation (KEGG, NES = 1.840, p.adjust = 0.008), proteasome (KEGG, NES = 1.982, p.adjust = 0.008), T cell mediated immunity (GO, NES = 1.185, p.adjust = 0.002), response to interferon-gamma (GO, NES = 2.014, p.adjust < 0.001), positive regulation of T cell activation (GO, NES = 1.590, p.adjust = 0.004), and positive regulation of immune response (GO, NES = 1.446, p.adjust = 0.005). The full results of the KEGG and GO analysis are presented in Figure S1.

Moreover, in our study cohort, detectable pathogenic mutations of the RTK-RAS pathway were associated with significantly worse PFS and OS, while LS-SCLC patients with non-pathogenic mutations of this pathway exhibited better prognosis than patients with wild-type counterparts (Fig. 2G). We also observed a trend of increased number of somatic mutations across LS-SCLC carrying various mutational status of RTK-RAS pathway (p < 0.001, Fig. 2H), which is consistent with the findings of tumor mutation burden in the external cohort (p < 0.001, Fig. 2I).

ctDNA during dCRT combined with baseline clinical characteristics predicts LS-SCLC prognosis

Given the availability baseline tumor tissue of LS-SCLC as well as the limited baseline biomarker, we aimed to develop a comprehensive PFS prediction algorithm for LS-SCLC receiving dCRT by ctDNA detection via liquid biopsy. After excluding the seven patients whose tumor shrinkage assessed after ICT was unavailable, we developed the algorithm using the training cohort and then evaluated its performance in the test cohort (Fig. 3A).

ctDNA detection at post-ICT (p < 0.01) and during TRT (p = 0.012) was related to significantly worse PFS (Fig. 3B) and a trend of poorer OS (Figure S2A, B). Additionally, at least one detectable ctDNA during dCRT predicted significantly unfavorable prognosis (Figure S2C, D), suggesting the predictive value of ctDNA during dCRT. Four variables were selected by Elastic-Net penalised Cox regression with lambda.min, including tumor shrinkage after ICT, PCI treatment, post-ICT ctDNA and TRT ctDNA detection (Fig. 3C). The forest plot of a multivariable Cox regression including these four variables displays their effect on PFS, with bigger tumor shrinkage as well as receiving PCI related to improved PFS and ctDNA detection associated with unfavourable PFS (Fig. 3D). Figure 3E summarizes five different combinations of predictors determined in 100 repeats, suggesting that the four variables were frequently selected with over 40% chance.

Excellent calibration was observed in the training cohort (Fig. 3F, G) with small difference between observed and fitted survival curves (Figure S2E), particularly in the long-term PFS of 36 mons (Fig. 3F, Figure S2F, G). Although the prediction algorithm used PFS survival data as the outcome, the algorithm exhibited good discrimination for progressive disease (PD) by the end of follow-up, achieving AUC of 0.7937 and 0.7262 in the training cohort and LOOCV, respectively (Fig. 3H). Owning to the best calibration at 36 mons, the time-dependent ROC was plotted for this time point, with AUC of 0.7907 and 0.6508 in the training and LOOCV, respectively, and the threshold to identify patients with high PD risk was determined as 1.172 according to the 36-mon time-dependent ROC in the whole training cohort. As expected, patients with PD had significantly higher progression risk than those not developing progression in both training cohort (p < 0.001) and LOOCV (p = 0.009) (Fig. 3I). Patients with low-risk score (< 1.172) exhibited superior PFS than those classified as at high risk (p < 0.001), with approximately 80% progression-free survival rate after at least 3-year follow-up (Fig. 3J), and a similar trend of OS was also observed (Figure S2H). Multivariable Cox regression analysis further revealed that the risk group identified by predicted risk scores was an independent predictor of PFS, when controlling for patient age, sex, smoking, disease stage, and PCI treatment (Figure S2J).

Performance assessment in the test cohort and the improvement of cutoff identification

A trend of elevated risk scores in PD patients compared to non-PD patients was observed in the test cohort (p = 0.055, Fig. 4A). The algorithm showed underestimated PD risk when comparing predicted with observed PD proportion, with a slightly poorer calibration than that in the training cohort (Fig. 4B). For instance, one patient having two negative ctDNA test results and a risk score of 0.759 developed PD shortly after approximately 6 mons follow-up and died by the end of follow-up, and a similar case with risk score of 0.920 who experienced PD at approximately 9 mons was also observed (Fig. 4C). The discrimination performance is summarized in Fig. 4D, achieving time-dependent AUC of 0.671 at Month 36 and a trend of decreased time-dependent AUC with the increase of follow-up period. Intriguingly, in the test cohort, TRT ctDNA detection could not predict PFS wile post-ICT ctDNA remained related to worse PFS (Figure S4A, B). There was a trend of worse PFS (p = 0.38, Fig. 4E) and OS (p = 0.063, Figure S4C) in patients at predicted high risk compared to those at low risk, despite being not statistically significant.

As patients in the test cohort were recruited later than the training cohort with a shorter follow-up period, we then investigated the association between the cutoff for risk stratification and the predict time used for cutoff determination in the training cohort (Fig. 4F). A dropping trending of the cutoff values was observed, show a 12-mon cutoff of 2.60 and a 24/36-mon cutoff of 1.172, which was we defined before. This means that a higher cutoff is better to identify patients with more aggressive disease and higher probabilities of short-term progression. As expected, a greater difference in 12-mon survival rates was observed between high and low risk patients defined using the determination time of 12 mons than using a determination time of 24/36 mons (Fig. 4G). Figure 4H presents two patients with detectable post-ICT ctDNA and non-detectable TRT ctDNA who had been previously defined at high risk using the cutoff of 1.172 were reclassified as low-risk using the 12-mon cutoff of 2.60. In addition, we observed a decreased trend of HR between high and low risk subgroups of the training cohort across time for cutoff determination, with a largest HR of 3.93 based on 11/12-mon determination time (Fig. 4I). Thus, patients in the test cohort were reclassified using a 12-mon cutoff, and high-risk patients exhibited significantly worse PFS (p < 0.01, Fig. 4J) and OS (p = 0.01, Figure S3D) than low-risk patients. Multivariable Cox regression analysis further revealed that the updated risk group was an independent predictor of PFS and OS (Figure S3E, J).

Development and evaluation of a prediction algorithm using the Bayesian approach

Owing to the prevalent issue that small amounts of available LS-SCLC cohorts and limited sample size of each study population, we then developed a Bayesian inference-based survival prediction algorithm for potentially continuous knowledge updating (Fig. 5A). The Bayesian algorithm achieved AUC of 0.7956 and 0.7877 to predict PD by the end of follow-up in the training cohort and LOOCV, respectively (Fig. 5B), and time-dependent AUC for 36-mon follow-up showed similar discrimination ability (training, 0.7629; LOOCV, 0.7960; Fig. 5C), with significantly higher Bayesian prediction score observed in patients who had developed PD by the end of follow-up (p < 0.001, Fig. 5D). The cutoff based on 36-mon time-dependent ROC was determined as 0.235 after 1000 times bootstrap, and patients grouped into the low-risk subcohort exhibited significantly superior PFS than those with risk scores ≥ 0.235 (p < 0.001, Fig. 5E). We then defined a short-term cutoff based on 12-mon time-dependent ROC as 0.463, which was higher than the cutoff for 36 mons, and better PFS was observed in low-risk patients (p = 0.008, Fig. 5E). Similar results were obtained in OS (Figure S4A, B). The performance of the Bayesian algorithm in the test cohort is summarized in Fig. 5F, revealing slightly reduced ability to predict PFS compared to the training cohort. A trend of worse PFS (Fig. 5G) and OS (Figure S4C, D) in patients identified at high risks was observed using both the 36-mon and 12-mon cutoffs while PD patients showed significantly higher predicted scores (p = 0.039, Figure S4E).

Next, the Bayesian algorithm was updated to get posteriors using the algorithm based on the training cohort as priors and the test cohort data as new evidence. A weighted average of the cutoff defined in the training and test cohorts based on the sample size was calculated (Figure S4F), with 36 and 12-mon cutoffs of 0.319 and 0.457 respectively. The updated Bayesian algorithm was applied to all samples with included variables to generate the updated Bayesian risk scores, which mean five previously excluded samples due to missing tumor shrinkage data during dCRT could be used to roughly assess the updated algorithm despite the limited sample size. Significantly elevated risk scores were observed in PD patients compared to non-PD patients, and five testing samples were predicted correctly using the updated 12-mon cutoff (p < 0.001, Fig. 5H). In the test cohort alone, we also detected higher predicted score in PD patients than non-PD patients (p = 0.014, Figure S5A), and patients with higher updated Bayesian risk scores exhibited poorer PFS and OS than those with lower scores, particularly when using the 12-mon cutoff (Fig. 5I, Figure S5B, C). Similar finding was observed in the entire cohort, suggesting that risk classification using updated Bayesian algorithm with either the 12-mon (Fig. 5J, K) or 36-mon cutoff served as an independent predictor for LS-SCLC patients receiving dCRT (Figure S5 D, E).

Survival benefits from consolidation IO are exclusively observed in patients at high PD risks under dCRT

We further investigated if the risk classification was able to be used as a predictive indicator for post-dCRT consolidation IO. The updated Bayesian algorithm was applied to the ctDNA IO cohort, by which patients’ counterfactual PD risks were predicted, and non-significant difference was detected between the dCRT and IO cohort (p = 0.12, Fig. 6A). Based on the 12-mon and 36-mon cutoffs, the ctDNA IO cohort was divided into three subgroups, including 33 patients > 0.457, 17 patients between 0.319 and 0.457, and 36 patients < 0.319 (Fig. 6B). Notably, significantly improved PFS was associated with post-dCRT consolidation IO among patients with updated Bayesian prediction scores > 0.457 (IO cohort vs dCRT cohort, p < 0.001, Fig. 6C). When controlling for potential confounders including sex, patient age, smoking, stage, and PCI treatment, the association remained statistically significant (Fig. 6D). However, obvious improvement in PFS was neither observed among patients with scores 0.319–0.457 (p = 0.629, Fig. 6E) nor with scores < 0.319 (p = 0.16, Fig. 6F). Furthermore, the adjusted HRs demonstrated a decreased trend with the increase in cutoff for high-risk patient classification, suggesting that patients who were more likely to develop PD might benefit more from post-dCRT consolidation IO (Fig. 6G).

Discussion

In this study, we began with an exploratory analysis of baseline tumor tissue, which identified PTEN mutations as a favorable prognostic biomarker associated with an immune-enriched tumor microenvironment. While this finding offers valuable biological insight, the low prevalence of PTEN alterations limits its utility as a broad prognostic tool. This prompted our subsequent development of a more universally applicable algorithm based on serial ctDNA monitoring. By integrating longitudinal ctDNA detection and clinical characteristics, we constructed and validated a survival prediction algorithm that provides individualized risk assessment. Moreover, we refined the model using a Bayesian approach to enable continuous updating and improve performance in small, heterogeneous cohorts. These findings contribute to addressing the current lack of prognostic and predictive tools in LS-SCLC and offer a foundation for future biomarker-driven clinical decision-making.

Tissue-based analyses revealed that PTEN mutation, although relatively infrequent, was significantly associated with improved PFS and OS in LS-SCLC patients treated with dCRT. This association remained consistent in an independent validation cohort, suggesting robustness across different datasets. Importantly, PTEN-mutant tumors exhibited enrichment in immune-related pathways. As PTEN is a key negative regulator of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, and its loss has been linked to enhanced immune infiltration and improved responses to immunotherapy [27]. However, Peng et al. revealed that loss of PTEN could promotes immune resistance related to T cell–mediated IO in preclinical models of melanoma [28]. In addition, PTEN loss was reported as a predictor tumor heterogeneity and unfavorable prognosis in EGFR-mutant advanced non-small-cell lung cancer treated with EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors [29], and decreased expression of PTEN was associated with poor prognosis in non-small-cell lung cancer [30]. Our results suggest that PTEN mutation may denote an immunologically active tumor phenotype with better treatment responsiveness in LS-SCLC, although the functional mechanisms warrant further investigation.

Owing to the difficulties in getting baseline tumor tissue samples in LS-SCLC, we investigated if dynamic liquid biopsy markers were able to be used for prognostic algorithm development. Serial ctDNA detection after ICT and during TRT was associated with worse survival outcomes, underscoring its value in real-time treatment monitoring. Our elastic-net penalized Cox regression model selected four predictors, including tumor shrinkage after ICT, PCI, post-ICT ctDNA, and TRT ctDNA, to stratify patients by recurrence risk. The model demonstrated strong calibration and discrimination in the training cohort and maintained moderate predictive power under LOOCV, indicating robustness. The model's predictive accuracy decreased in the test cohort, highlighting challenges in generalizability. Notably, TRT ctDNA lost statistical significance in the test cohort, suggesting possible biological or technical variability in ctDNA dynamics during radiotherapy. Thus, additional external cohorts are required to confirm if ctDNA detection during TRT is a key predictor for PFS of LS-SCLC treated with dCRT alone. The model still effectively stratified patients when the risk score cutoff was recalibrated to predict shorter-term outcomes. Specifically, a 12-mon cutoff improved PFS and OS discrimination when compared to a 36-mon cutoff, particularly for identifying patients with aggressive disease prone to early relapse. This observation emphasizes the importance of time-specific risk stratification in LS-SCLC, where most relapses occur within the first two years of treatment [31, 32].

To further enhance prediction in the context of small and evolving datasets, we developed a Bayesian survival model that supports sequential learning and dynamic refinement [33]. The Bayesian algorithm demonstrated strong performance in the training cohort and LOOCV, with high AUCs and clear risk score separation between progressors and non-progressors. Importantly, this approach facilitates model updating as more patient data become available, potentially overcoming the limitations of static models trained on small cohorts. Such adaptability is particularly relevant in LS-SCLC, where multicenter data harmonization remains a challenge. Notably, in terms of patients classified into the low-risk subgroup, their 2-year OS rate was approximately 75%, which is similar to stage I SCLC patients who might be eligible for surgical treatment [5]. By contrast, patients at high risks under dCRT exhibited significant PFS improvement under consolidation IO, suggesting that dCRT plus consolidation ICI should be prioritized. Consistently, the ADRIATIC study has revealed that relatively obvious efficacy of Durvalumab was detected in patients with partial response or stable disease to concurrent CRT [6]. Since ctDNA detection during dCRT could reflect the possible residual tumor [34], it is biologically plausible to observe survival benefits among high-risk patients who were likely to have detectable ctDNA after ICT or during TRT. Moreover, similar relationship between ctDNA-positive patients and better efficacy of IO has been revealed in non-small cell lung cancer as well [11].

This study has limitations.. A major limitation is the relatively small sample size of our ctDNA cohorts, which is common in studies of this rare and aggressive disease subtype. To mitigate the risk of overfitting, we employed rigorous methods such as leave-one-out cross-validation and developed a Bayesian framework, which is adept at handling uncertainty in smaller datasets. Nevertheless, our findings must be considered exploratory and require validation in larger, independent, and prospectively collected cohorts before clinical implementation. In addition, the ctDNA dCRT cohort was split according to the enrollment date into a training and a test cohort to evaluate the performance of our prediction algorithm, but external cohorts recruited at other study centers with large sample sizes are needed to mitigate the overfitting issue. Another key limitation stemming from this design is the absence of pre-treatment baseline ctDNA samples for the dCRT and IO cohorts. While baseline ctDNA could offer insights into initial tumor burden, our analysis focused on on-treatment ctDNA dynamics, which capture the early response to therapy. Furthermore, detailed clinical data such as baseline tumor volume were not uniformly available in the retrospective records, precluding their inclusion in our prognostic algorithm. Moreover, prospective cohort studies or clinical trials are required to confirm our findings that high-risk patients identified by a ctDNA-based prediction algorithm could benefit obviously form post-dCRT consolidation IO. Finally, the time points of ctDNA test during TRT were not uniform due to the nature of this retrospective study, which means we were unable to investigate if the dynamics of ctDNA detection under TRT could affect prediction model performance.

Conclusions

Our study identifies baseline PTEN alterations detected in tumor tissue as a favorable prognostic biomarker and demonstrates that ctDNA kinetics during dCRT, when integrated with clinical variables, can effectively predict disease progression in LS-SCLC. The development of a Bayesian prediction framework further enhances the applicability of these models in real-world clinical settings. These findings support the feasibility and clinical relevance of biomarker-informed risk stratification in LS-SCLC and provide a foundation for future research into personalized treatment approaches.
