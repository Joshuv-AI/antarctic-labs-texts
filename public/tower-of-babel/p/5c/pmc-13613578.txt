Lumbar epidural haematoma after acupuncture: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Wenbing Cao, Xing Zhang, Dongdong Li, Zhiyu Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcupuncture is widely used for lumbar pain, yet needle-related spinal epidural haematoma (SEH) remains a rarely reported but potentially catastrophic complication. The lumbar segment is often assumed to be a "safe" needling zone, and the true incidence, mechanistic basis and preventive strategy for iatrogenic SEH are poorly defined.Case presentationAn 80-year-old Chinese man developed progressive right lumbosciatalgia 10 days after three sessions of electro-acupuncture and a right L5-S1 paravertebral nerve block. Formal WHO-UMC causality assessment rated the event 'Probable' iatrogenic, though competing causality with the nerve block cannot be fully excluded. Key preventive measures include pre-procedure MRI in high-risk subjects, careful individualization of needle depth and electro-acupuncture parameters based on patient anatomy and clinical context. Advanced age and spinal degeneration are not surgical contraindications; timely decompression restores function even after prolonged compression.

Background

Spinal epidural haematoma (SEH) is an uncommon but potentially devastating condition that accounts for < 1% of all space-occupying lesions of the spinal canal [1]. Although most frequently reported in the cervicothoracic region, SEH may also arise in the lumbar segment where its incidence is lowest but its clinical impact remains significant [2, 3]. The aetiology is multifactorial: trauma, vascular malformations, coagulopathies, hypertension and antithrombotic therapy are well-recognised precipitants [4, 5], while "spontaneous" cases without obvious trigger are described. Irrespective of cause, the common end-point is rupture of the rich, valve-less epidural venous plexus, resulting in a rapidly expanding clot that compromises the thecal sac and nerve roots [6].

Acupuncture is widely used for chronic low-back pain and sciatica [7, 8], but traumatic complications are increasingly recognised. Case series and isolated reports have linked needling or electro-acupuncture to cervical and thoracic SHE [9, 10], yet lumbar involvement after acupuncture remains exceptionally rare and has not been systematically analysed. The lumbar spinal canal possesses a relatively capacious cross-section and abundant epidural fat, factors that may mask early compression and delay diagnosis. Because initial symptoms (acute lumbago, radicular pain, sensory loss or motor weakness) mimic common degenerative conditions such as disc herniation, clinicians may fail to appreciate that an iatrogenic bleed is evolving. Magnetic resonance imaging is the diagnostic tool of choice, classically revealing a fusiform, heterogeneous epidural mass that displaces the dural sac and narrows the canal [11, 12]. Definitive proof; however, requires operative evacuation and histopathological confirmation of organised clot.

Management protocols are still debated. Small, neurologically intact haematomas may resolve conservatively [13], but any deterioration or significant baseline deficit is an indication for urgent decompressive laminectomy; the majority of patients operated within 24–48 h achieve favourable neurological recovery [14–16]. Even when presentation is delayed, prompt surgery can still reverse root compression and restore function, as illustrated by the present case.

We therefore report an 80-year-old Chinese man who developed a large, organised epidural haematoma at L5-S1 shortly after repeated lumbar acupuncture and paravertebral nerve-block injections. By integrating the clinical course, imaging evolution, surgical findings and outcome with a systematic review of the seven previously published English-language cases of acupuncture-related lumbar SEH since 2010, we aim to: 1.raise awareness that needling-induced bleeding can occur in the lower spinal segments; 2.highlight the importance of comparing pre- and post-procedure imaging to establish causality; 3.offer practical recommendations (coagulation screening, needle-depth limits, early MRI for disproportionate pain) that may reduce risk and facilitate timely intervention.

Case presentation

Patient information

An 80-year-old Chinese man with a 10-year history of hypertension and healed renal tuberculosis was referred to our spine centre on Day 7 with progressive right-sided lumbosciatalgia and foot drop. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Jiangdu People's Hospital (approval No.202402101), and written informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient.

Timeline and acupuncture history

On Day -68, an outpatient lumbar CT showed only an L5-S1 disc bulge with no epidural collection (Fig. 1a). Between Day -63 and Day -10, the patient attended a community pain clinic where three sessions of electro-acupuncture (EA; 2 Hz/100 Hz alternating, 0.30 × 50 mm Hwato needles, bilaterally at GV3, EX-B8, BL23, BL25, BL54 and GB30, 30 min each) were performed for presumed discogenic pain. On Day -17, a right L5-S1 paravertebral nerve block was added under C-arm fluoroscopy: using a right posterolateral oblique approach, a 22-G spinal needle was advanced to the posterior aspect of the right L5-S1 intervertebral foramen at a depth of approximately 6 cm from the skin, and 4 mL of 1% lidocaine plus 4 mg dexamethasone was injected. The needle tip remained lateral to the spinal canal wall. Ten days after the final intervention, on Day 0, the patient developed new, severe right-sided radicular pain. Clinical findings

Physical examination revealed a non-febrile patient with normal spinal curvature. There was marked limitation of lumbar flexion/extension, positive right straight-leg-raise at 40°, reinforcement-positive, and hypo-aesthesia in the right L5–S1 dermatomes. Power of right ankle dorsiflexion and great-toe extension was Medical Research Council (MRC) grade 4/5; ankle reflex absent. Left lower limb and perineal sensation, bladder/bowel function were intact. Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score = 9; Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) = 8; Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) = 59%.

Diagnostic assessment

Laboratory tests on admission: haemoglobin 131 g/L, platelets 187 × 10⁹/L, prothrombin time 11.2 s, INR 1.03, aPTT 28 s; biochemistry, tumour markers and vasculitis screen normal.

Radiology

Plain radiographs obtained at presentation showed degenerative lumbar scoliosis with narrowing of the L5–S1 disc space. Contrast-enhanced CT performed on Day 7 revealed a 2.8 × 1.6 cm right posterolateral epidural mass at the L5-S1 level, compressing the thecal sac and exhibiting attenuation values of 55–70 HU. Subsequent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) on the same day (Fig. 1) demonstrated an iso-intense lesion on T1-weighted sequences and heterogeneous high signal on T2-weighted images with a peripheral low-signal rim, consistent with an organized haematoma. Severe spinal stenosis and displacement of the right S1 nerve root were evident. No vascular malformation or abnormal contrast enhancement was identified.

WHO-UMC causality screen

We formally assessed the causal relationship between acupuncture and lumbar SEH using the WHO-UMC standardized tool (Table 1). The temporal sequence was consistent (onset 10 days after the last session), the reaction is biologically plausible given seven previously reported cases, and alternative causes—including coagulopathy, vascular malformation, or trauma—were excluded by laboratory, imaging and pathological findings. Therapeutic intervention

Considering the severity of the patient's lumbar and leg pain, as well as significant spinal canal compression, posterior lumbar laminectomy, spinal canal exploration, bone graft fusion, and cement-augmented pedicle-screw fixation surgery were performed on the fourth day after admission. Intra-operative findings (Fig. 1f, g): gross osteoporosis, 3 × 1.5 × 1 cm dark-red, partly organised clot displacing dura and right S1 root; no abnormal vessels. Complete evacuation was achieved, followed by L5–S1 bilateral pedicle-screw fixation with bone-cement augmentation and inter-laminar autograft. Operative duration 105 min, estimated blood loss 220 mL.

Post-operative course

Immediate pain relief; VAS fell to 1 on day 1. JOA improved to 23 and ODI to 32% at 1 week. At 2-week suture removal the patient walked independently with a lumbar brace; at 3-month visit he remained pain-free with full power (MRC 5/5) and normal sphincter function.

Follow-up imaging

Post-operative radiographs and MRI (day 3, Fig. 2a–f) demonstrated complete decompression, correctly positioned bone-cement screws, and restored spinal canal area. Postoperative pathology (Fig. 2g) revealed an organized blood clot with partial necrosis and degeneration. Literature review results

A systematic search of English-language reports yielded eight patients (including the present case) who developed spinal epidural haematoma (SEH) after acupuncture or its combination with perispinal injection (Table 2). Mean age was 66.7 ± 10.3 years (48–80 yr) with a male-to-female ratio of 6:2. Across the entire cohort, males predominated in both cervical and lumbar subgroups, reflecting larger epidural venous calibre and higher wall tension under traction. Needling characteristics differed by segment: the cervical group (n = 9) was dominated by manual acupuncture (7/9), with only two cases employing low-frequency (2 Hz) electro-acupuncture and none combined with nerve-block injection, the single lumbar case utilized 2 Hz electro-acupuncture plus an L5-S1 para-vertebral dexamethasone/lidocaine block. All cervical haematomas were located posterior to C2-T1, with a mean axial diameter of 2.5 mm (range 0.25–3 mm), significantly smaller than the 30 mm lumbar clot. Latency from last needling to symptom onset was bimodal: cervical cases presented at a median of 3 h (range immediate-48 h), whereas the lumbar case presented at 10 days, the longest latency recorded. Neurological deficit at presentation was severe: 78% of cervical cases showed ASIA C impairment, while the lumbar case had a JOA score of 9. Management was segment-dependent: 44% of cervical cases were managed conservatively with neurological recovery to ASIA E within 2–12 months; 56% underwent laminectomy with mean recovery to ASIA E at 3 months. The lumbar case received cement-augmented laminectomy and screw fixation, achieving JOA 23 at 3 months. To further assess competing causality, we also searched for cases of spinal epidural haematoma following neuraxial blocks (including epidural anesthesia and paravertebral nerve blocks). A systematic review of 166 cases reported that most presented within 24–72 h, although delayed onset up to several weeks has been documented [17]. However, these cases predominantly involved epidural anesthesia/analgesia using larger-bore needles or catheters, which differ from a single-injection paravertebral nerve block. Importantly, no case of SEH occurring 10 days after a standalone paravertebral nerve block (the procedure received by our patient) has been identified in the literature. The strong temporal and anatomical association with the needling points (GV3, EX-B8) in our case, together with the 10-day latency less typical for a single nerve block, supports acupuncture as the more likely primary cause, while not excluding a synergistic contribution from the nerve block.

Discussion

Competing causality with the paravertebral nerve block

The right L5-S1 paravertebral nerve block performed 10 days before symptom onset could theoretically have contributed to the haematoma. However, several observations favour acupuncture as the dominant cause. First, the nerve block was performed under fluoroscopic guidance with a 22-G needle, and the injection was confined to the extraforaminal space; post-procedure imaging showed no immediate epidural collection. Second, the latency of 10 days is considerably longer than the typical 24–48 h window for nerve-block-induced haematoma reported in the literature. Third, the organized nature of the clot (3 × 1.5 × 1 cm) suggests repeated or cumulative vascular insult, which is more consistent with three sessions of deep needling at local acupoints (GV3, EX-B8) directly overlying the epidural venous plexus. Fourth, systematic review identified no case of SEH occurring 10 days after a single nerve block alone. While the nerve block may have acted as a contributory factor by increasing local vascular fragility (via dexamethasone) or causing micro-injury, the primary causal role is attributed to acupuncture. This interpretation aligns with the WHO-UMC "Probable" rating, which does not require exclusion of all competing causes, only that the index intervention is the most plausible.

Ten days after repeated lumbosacral acupuncture and a right L5–S1 paravertebral nerve block, the patient experienced an acute exacerbation of radicular pain. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a new-onset epidural mass at the same level. During surgery, an organised haematoma measuring 3 × 1.5 × 1 cm was completely evacuated; histopathology showed no vascular malformation. Although the patient was elderly and had advanced spinal degeneration, but: (1) CT performed 4 weeks earlier at an outside hospital had demonstrated no epidural collection; (2) Laboratory coagulation tests were normal, and there was no history of anticoagulant or antiplatelet use. According to the WHO-UMC causality criteria, the event was rated "Probable" iatrogenic bleeding. We acknowledge that spontaneous spinal epidural haematoma (SSEH) cannot be entirely excluded; however, its annual incidence is only 0.1 per 100,000, and it most commonly occurs in the thoracic or cervical regions [25]. In the lumbar spine, in the absence of coagulopathy, and with a close temporal relationship to an invasive procedure, the probability of SSEH is far lower than that of an iatrogenic event [26]. In the present case, acupuncture was performed at the local acupoints GV3 (Yaoyangguan) and EX-B8 (Extraordinary point), which directly overlie the L5-S1 segment. This allows for a more specific mechanistic explanation for the development of the epidural haematoma, involving both direct local factors and synergistic contributions from the other interventions.

First, the direct local factor (primary mechanism): acupuncture at GV3 and EX-B8 provides the most direct anatomical explanation for the L5-S1 epidural haematoma. GV3 is located between the spinous processes of L4 and L5, while EX-B8 lies on the posterior midline of the lumbosacral region, below the spinous process of the fifth lumbar vertebra. Deep needling at these points can reach the vicinity of the vertebral lamina or intervertebral foramen. These acupoints exhibit significant anatomical overlap with the vertebral canal venous plexus at the L5-S1 level. Vascular injury caused by needling at these sites could directly lead to haematoma formation at this specific segment. This finding validates that acupoints closely related to the haematoma location were indeed utilized in the treatment. Second, synergistic factors (auxiliary mechanism): The distal acupoints (BL54, GB30) were needled deeply (0.30 × 50 mm needles), with needle tips potentially reaching the sacrococcygeal or paravertebral regions. Combined with the recent L5-S1 paravertebral nerve block (22G needle), the following synergistic effects may be postulated: (1) The nerve block may have caused microvascular injury, creating a potential substrate for haematoma formation; (2) Subsequent deep needling at distal points could have exacerbated bleeding or enlarged an existing subclinical haematoma; (3) The extensive anastomoses of the paravertebral venous plexus allow haemorrhage to spread and accumulate along anatomical fascial planes. This multifactorial mechanism may explain why clinical manifestations at the L5-S1 level ultimately developed following multiple interventions. These case-specific observations align with the broader pathophysiological framework described in the literature. Despite its clinical rarity, spinal epidural haematoma (SEH) following lumbar acupuncture is not a random event; it reflects a predictable interplay between mechanical, degenerative and pharmacological factors that collectively destabilise the valve-less epidural venous plexus [27, 28]. Understanding the precise pathophysiology is essential if we are to move from reactive treatment to evidence-based prevention. Below, we dissect the cascade of injury and identify modifiable risk determinants emerging from the current case series and contemporary vascular-biomechanical data. (1) Mechanical injury: the lumbar epidural venous plexus is valveless and abundant; deep needle insertion or electro-acupuncture may tear these veins. Multiple punctures can create a "stair-step" channel through which blood dissects when intra-abdominal pressure rises suddenly [29]. (2) Degenerative factors: narrowed disc spaces and reduced epidural fat in elderly patients decrease the buffering capacity of the spinal canal; osteophytes or calcified ligaments may abrade vessels [30, 31]. (3) Pharmacological factors: corticosteroids added to nerve-block solutions increase local vascular fragility and impair haemostasis [32, 33].

Although the incidence of acupuncture-related lumbar spinal epidural haematoma is low, every reported case represents a preventable adverse event. This reality obliges clinicians to shift from retrospective regret to proactive mitigation. A systematic, step-wise approach can be integrated into routine practice without disrupting workflow. First, a targeted pre-procedure imaging strategy is essential: patients ≥ 65 years, those with severe degenerative change, or anyone receiving antiplatelet therapy should undergo MRI to exclude occult vascular malformations that might amplify bleeding risk [12, 27]. Second, a baseline coagulation screen (PT, aPTT, platelet count) must be performed, and anticoagulant or antiplatelet drugs should be discontinued or bridged according to current peri-operative guidelines [26]. Third, careful needle selection and depth control are critical: use fine needles (0.25–0.30 mm diameter), needle length should be individualized based on anatomy and patient factors, given that 40–50 mm needles were used in this and several prior cases, caution is warranted during deep lumbosacral needling [34, 35]. Limit electro-acupuncture frequency to ≤ 2 Hz and intensity to ≤ 2 mA, and during para-vertebral block ensure the needle tip remains lateral to the spinal canal wall. Fourth, strict technical discipline should be maintained: avoid repeated thrusting at the same point, and if the patient reports an electric-shock sensation, withdraw the needle immediately to prevent intradural or vascular breach. Finally, vigilant monitoring and follow-up are mandatory: observe the patient for 30 min after treatment, and any new radicular pain or motor deficit within 7 days should prompt urgent re-evaluation and MRI to permit early diagnosis and intervention before irreversible neurological compromise occurs [27].

Despite a 3-month delay between symptom onset and surgery, our patient regained normal neurological function, indicating that chronic, organised haematomas can still be reversible if neural compression is relieved [36].Conservative management with close imaging surveillance may be appropriate for patients with intact cauda-equina function, haematoma < 1 cm and mild symptoms [1, 13]. Once motor deficit or bladder dysfunction appears, decompressive laminectomy and clot evacuation should be performed within 24 h; instrumented fusion with cement-augmented screws can be added when significant osteoporosis is present.

Every needle insertion carries an invisible foot-print of risk. Although acupuncture-related complications are rare, they can be catastrophic; the lumbar region is emphatically not a "safe blind zone." What happens after the needle leaves the skin is just as important as what occurs during the session—hence detailed documentation of invasive procedures and side-by-side comparison of pre- and post-intervention imaging are essential to establish causality and to defend both patient and practitioner. Advanced age and pre-existing degenerative change are not contraindications to surgery; they are markers of urgency. Remember: image early, document meticulously, and intervene decisively—because the only thing worse than a rare complication is a preventable one that was missed.

Conclusion

This case supports a clinical pathway of "repeated lumbar needling/nerve block → epidural venous injury → organised haematoma → nerve-root compression." The 10-day latency, organised clot, and direct needling at L5-S1-adjacent acupoints (GV3, EX-B8) suggest that acupuncture played the dominant causal role, with the paravertebral nerve block potentially contributing as a synergistic factor.

Take-home message for clinicians

(1) Even when local acupoints are needled, delayed onset of radicular pain (up to 10 days) can indicate an evolving epidural haematoma; (2) Pre-procedure MRI should be strongly considered in elderly patients with severe degeneration; (3) Needle depth should be individualized, and any post-acupuncture disproportionate radicular pain warrants urgent MRI; (4) Delayed surgery (even after 3 months) can achieve good neurological recovery if decompression is adequate.
