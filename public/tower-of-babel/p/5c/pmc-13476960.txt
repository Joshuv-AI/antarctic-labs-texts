Application of a retrograde resection technique in robot-assisted laparoscopic nephron-sparing surgery for large complex renal tumors
Authors: Jinhuan Wei, Junjie Cen, Xu Chen, Jun Lu, Wei Chen, Junhang Luo
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

With the development of robot-assisted minimally invasive surgical technology, the indications for partial nephrectomy have gradually expanded to include large complex renal tumors such as those of cT2a or cT2b [[1], [2], [3]]. Robot-assisted laparoscopic partial nephrectomy (RAPN) for cT2 renal tumors is usually performed via the transperitoneal approach [4]. cT2 tumors located in the upper pole of the kidney always compress the liver, pancreas, and spleen, and it is very difficult to expose the surgical field and mobilize the kidney and the large tumor from the surrounding tissue and organs before excision of the tumor during the standard procedure of RAPN. Furthermore, severe compression of the tumor by surgical instruments increases the risk of tumor rupture (especially in cystic renal cancer), cancer recurrence and metastasis, and surrounding organ injury [5]. We aimed to describe a retrograde resection technique during RAPN for large complex renal tumors. For this retrograde resection technique, we first resected the tumor from the kidney without compressing the large renal mass, and then carefully dissected the distal margin of the tumor from the surrounding normal tissue to achieve complete excision. The use of the retrograde resection technique solved the problem of difficulty in exposing large renal tumors, and avoided severe compression of the tumor by endoscopic instruments, which might result in tumor rupture and postoperative metastasis. Both patients provided written informed consent for the surgical procedure and for the publication of this research, including relevant clinical information and accompanying images. All identifiable personal information has been removed to ensure patient confidentiality.

Patient 1 was a 35-year-old man who was admitted to the hospital after a physical examination revealed a tumor in the left kidney. CT showed cystic renal cell carcinoma with a maximum diameter of 7.2 cm in the upper pole of the left kidney, and the clinical stage was T2aN0M0. The maximum diameter of the tumor was 7.2 cm (Fig. 1A); the tumor exophytic percentage was less than 50%; the distance to the collecting system was less than 4 mm. Patient 2, a 41-year-old man, was admitted to the hospital with progressive enlargement of the right renal tumor over a 6-year period. CT showed cystic and solid renal cell carcinoma with a maximum diameter of 10.8 cm in the upper pole of the right kidney (Fig. 1B). CT revealed no evidence of metastatic disease, and the clinical stage was T2bN0M0. The tumor exophytic percentage was less than 50%; the distance to the collecting system was less than 4 mm. Both tumors were prospectively scored using the R.E.N.A.L. nephrometry system. Both patients had a preoperative R.E.N.A.L. score of 10. We used a preoperative predictive nomogram to predict the risk of chronic kidney disease upstaging at 3 years postoperatively [6]. Both patients demonstrated a 5% risk of chronic kidney disease upstaging at 3 years postoperatively.Figure 1Preoperative coronal CT images of the patients. (A) Patient 1; (B) Patient 2.Figure 1

The patient was positioned in a modified flank position at a 60-degree angle. A 2-cm vertical incision was created approximately 4 cm paramedian to the umbilicus, accommodating a 12-mm robotic camera trocar. Under direct visualization, two 8-mm robotic instrument ports were placed: one at the midclavicular line and another near the anterior axillary line, both positioned 8 cm cephalad to the camera port. Two 12-mm assistant ports were inserted 5 cm along the perpendicular bisector between the instrument ports and the camera port. For right-sided tumors, an optional 5-mm port may be placed at the xiphoid level for liver retraction. Colonic mobilization was performed first, followed by careful exposure of the renal hilum. The main renal vein was identified and dissected, after which the renal artery was isolated. Following complete vascular control, perirenal fat dissection was conducted to delineate the tumor. We first resected the junction between the tumor and the normal renal parenchyma by sharp and blunt dissection. Once the renal tumor was completely excised, renorrhaphy was performed. The medullary layer was closed with a 3–0 absorbable barbed suture in a continuous fashion. After that, a 2–0 absorbable barbed suture was used for the outer running stitches. The vascular bulldog clamp was removed. Subsequently, the tumor parts that were distant from the junction between the tumor and the normal renal parenchyma were resected (Fig. 2). Hemostasis was confirmed at the surgical site, and clear effluent was verified via the Foley catheter. The specimen was placed in an endoscopic retrieval bag. A closed-suction drain was positioned in the retroperitoneum. The camera port incision was extended for specimen extraction, followed by layered fascial closure of the incision.Figure 2Representative intraoperative photographs demonstrating the retrograde resection technique. Patient 1 underwent RAPN with an estimated blood loss of 50 mL, a total operative time of 188 min, and a warm ischemia time of 23 min. No Clavien–Dindo grade ≥II complications occurred. Histopathology confirmed clear cell renal cell carcinoma with negative margins. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 3. Preoperative and postoperative estimated glomerular filtration rate values were 116 mL/min and 101 mL/min, respectively. No intraoperative tumor rupture or capsule violation occurred. During the 3-month postoperative follow-up, no tumor recurrence or metastasis was detected.

The estimated blood loss of Patient 2 was 200 mL; the total operative time was 335 min; the warm ischemia time was 25 min. No Clavien–Dindo grade ≥II complications occurred. Histopathology confirmed clear cell renal cell carcinoma with negative margins. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 7. Preoperative and postoperative estimated glomerular filtration rate values were 111 mL/min and 108 mL/min, respectively. No instances of intraoperative tumor rupture or capsule violation occurred. During 30 months of regular postoperative follow-up, no tumor recurrence or metastasis was observed in Patient 2.

Retrograde resection techniques have been reported for complex cholecystectomies and appendectomies. In conditions such as gallbladder edema or inflammatory tissue adhesions, these procedures carry an increased risk of bile duct and vascular injuries. Laparoscopic retrograde cholecystectomy is employed for complex gallbladder stones [7,8]. Similarly, retrograde resection is utilized in laparoscopic appendectomy for complex appendicitis, reducing the incidence of postoperative bleeding, enteric fistula, and abdominal infection, thereby enhancing the safety of this approach [9,10]. The application of the retrograde resection technique in RAPN has not yet been reported. For larger complex renal tumors, it is usually very difficult to expose the tumors before their resection. The application of the retrograde resection technique enables successful completion of the operation. In our retrograde resection technique during RAPN, we intentionally deferred mobilization of the kidney and upper pole tumor from surrounding structures (diaphragm, liver, or spleen ligaments) until after tumor excision. Only the perirenal fat at the tumor-normal parenchyma junction was dissected initially. Following tumor resection, renorrhaphy, and clamp removal, we mobilized the large tumor specimen from adjacent organs (liver, pancreas, and spleen). This sequence enhanced exposure of the surgical planes and prevented instrument compression of the tumor during critical dissection phases. Compared with the conventional approach, the retrograde resection technique avoids the need for significant tumor manipulation to achieve complete separation of large tumors. This significantly reduces the risk of tumor implantation and metastasis, aligning more closely with the principle of minimizing tumor manipulation (no-touch technique) in oncological surgery.

Our retrograde resection strategy warrants comparative evaluation against established techniques for RAPN in large complex renal tumors, as each approach exhibits distinct risk-benefit profiles. Off-clamp RAPN is acknowledged for its nephron-sparing advantage, but its use in large, highly vascularized tumors is limited due to the challenge of achieving hemostasis, as noted by Huang et al. [11]. Selective clamping is referenced as beneficial for segmentally vascularized polar tumors but less feasible in distorted vascular anatomy or large lesions [12]. Given that each technique carries unique theoretical benefits and limitations, no single strategy is universally optimal for RAPN in complex renal tumors.

While our retrograde dissection technique overcomes exposure limitations inherent in conventional robotic systems, emerging platforms provide enhanced articulation and reduced arm collisions in confined spaces [13]. Such technological advances complement rather than negate the value of technique-specific solutions like retrograde dissection. Our surgical experience with extremely large tumors or giant cystic renal cancer is summarized as follows. Robot-assisted surgical systems have greater advantages over conventional laparoscopic surgery for such complex tumors [14]. The use of gradual segmental artery unclamping can reduce the warm ischemia time during resection of large complex renal cancer with RAPN [15]. Intracorporeal ultrasound is recommended to assist in large complex tumor margin assessment [16]. Preoperative planning with three-dimensional virtual models enhances the precision of RAPN [17]. For tumors located close to the renal hilum, it is necessary to isolate the branches of the renal arteries and veins to avoid vascular injuries during the resection process. For large complex tumors located in the upper or lower poles of the kidney, violent compression of the tumor should not be used to prevent tumor rupture and dissemination; we suggest using the retrograde resection technique during RAPN. The resection of giant cystic renal cell carcinoma should be performed cautiously; the surgical margin distance should be appropriately extended; and a combination of blunt and sharp dissection methods should be used to resect the tumor.

The retrograde resection technique is technically feasible in RAPN for large renal tumors. Compared with the conventional approach, the retrograde resection technique provides better exposure and a clearer surgical field and does not require excessive compression of the tumor to completely dissect the large tumor from the surrounding tissue and organs, decreasing the risk of tumor rupture (especially in cystic renal cancer), cancer recurrence and metastasis, and surrounding organ injury.
