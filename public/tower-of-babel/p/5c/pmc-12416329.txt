Intracranial tumors like meningiomas share overlapping features with a rare and emerging category of mesenchymal intracranial neoplasms with FET::CREB fusions. These fusions are present in myxoid spindle cell neoplasms and radiographically and histologically may closely resemble meningiomas, presenting with an extra-axial dural tail and whorl-like features.1

Prior literature has classified these tumors as intracranial mixed mesenchymal tumors or intracranial angiomatoid fibrous histiocytomas (AFHs). In particular, myxoid spindle cell neoplasms are heterogeneous sarcomas prone to recurrence and exhibit a high mortality rate. They recur, metastasize, and lead to increased morbidity and mortality, especially after partial resection. Epidemiologically, these tumors show a female predominance across a broad age range, from young children to individuals in their 70s, with a higher prevalence in the 2nd and 3rd decades of life.1 They are predominantly extra-axial or intraventricular tumors, occurring throughout the neuroaxis.

Histologically, they feature spindle cells in a myxoid matrix and express immunohistochemical markers such as desmin, EMA, and CD99. Conversely, these tumors test negative for PR, SSTR2, SMA, STAT6, and MUC4.2,3 Desmin, a type III intermediate filament protein, plays a crucial role in maintaining structural integrity and resisting mechanical stress in muscle cells.1,4 EMA, a member of the glycoprotein family, serves as an effective marker for establishing the epithelial nature of neoplastic cells when detected immunohistochemically. CD99, expressed in Ewing sarcoma, synovial sarcoma, and low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma, may indicate tumor aggressiveness.1,3

Meningiomas, on the other hand, are prevalent among middle-aged to older women and are primarily benign, slow-growing tumors originating from meningothelial cells.5 About 80.5% are classified as WHO grade 1, while WHO grade 2 and 3 tumors constitute 17.7% and 1.7%, respectively. A Ki-67 proliferation index of > 4% poses an increased risk of recurrence and mortality.5,6

Given the strikingly similar presentation, rarity, and diagnostic ambiguity of these tumors, they bring up a new challenge for neurosurgeons. While gross-total resection remains the cornerstone of treatment, diagnosis is often refined postoperatively via molecular testing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS), a technology enabling rapid DNA and RNA sequencing of hundreds or thousands of genes, can determine the presence or absence of FET::CREB fusion, providing a definitive identification of the neoplasm type.7–9 This case emphasizes the importance of considering such a rare neoplasm in the differential diagnosis when evaluating presumed cases of meningioma.

Illustrative Case

The patient, a woman in her late 60s, presented following a new-onset witnessed seizure characterized by right arm tonic-clonic movements, left arm straightening, eyes rolled back, and transient unresponsiveness lasting about 3 minutes. A subsequent episode soon followed involving left head turn, left upward gaze, and tongue biting. Her neurological examination was otherwise unremarkable.

CT scans of the head without contrast revealed a small area of hypoattenuation in the anterior right frontal lobe, suggesting a prior insult or contusion. MRI indicated a 1.7 × 1.7 × 1.8–cm avidly enhancing extra-axial lesion with a dural tail, consistent with an initial working diagnosis of meningioma (Fig. 1). This lesion caused mass effect and localized indentation of the right frontal lobe, along with mild adjacent subcortical vasogenic edema.

A: Axial T1-weighted MR image of the head illustrating right frontal lobe extra-axial mass with dural tail. The dimension of the mass was 1.7 × 1.7 × 1.8 cm. B: Axial contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MR image of the head illustrating the absence of right frontal lobe mass postresection.

Given the radiographic findings and symptomatic presentation, the patient underwent right frontal craniotomy and resection of the brain tumor using neuronavigation, microscope, and neuromonitoring (Fig. 2). Gross-total resection was achieved without intraoperative complications. Postoperatively, the patient remained seizure free. She reported improvement in her preoperative symptoms, including resolution of transient confusion and weakness. She was continued on levetiracetam.

Pathology Report

The histopathological evaluation report delineated the presence of a myxoid spindle cell neoplasm, and immunochemistry showed tumor cell expression of desmin, EMA, and CD99 in varying quantities, while testing negative for PR, SSTR2, SMA, STAT6, and MUC4. Furthermore, the neoplasm exhibited a relatively low-grade nature, with only scattered mitotic figures present, and the Ki-67 index did not exceed 5% (Fig. 2). Molecular testing confirmed FET::CREB fusion, consistent with an intracranial mesenchymal tumor, FET::CREB fusion–positive, per the 2021 WHO classification.

Discussion

Observations

In this report, we present the case of a female in her late 60s who presented with witnessed seizure that lasted for about 3 minutes, with a brief respite followed by a second episode characterized by left head turn, left upward gaze, and tongue biting. The physical examination findings, EEG, and blood laboratory report did not reveal any significant acute findings.

The radiographic similarity of FET::CREB fusion–positive intracranial mesenchymal tumors to meningiomas poses a diagnostic challenge. Neurosurgeons may initially plan intervention under the assumption of a benign meningioma, potentially impacting long-term management. This case highlights the importance of molecular testing in cases with ambiguous histology. Myxoid spindle cell neoplasms are characterized histologically and immunophenotypically by the presence of FET::CREB fusion.1,3,4,10,11 Typically, these tumors feature spindle cell elements arranged in reticulated patterns within a distinctly myxoid matrix, occasionally displaying solid or epithelioid characteristics.1,4,12,13 These tumors often exhibit syncytial growth and meningioma-like whorls. Immunohistochemical markers such as EMA positivity, desmin positivity, and SSTRA2A negativity contribute to the complexity of differential diagnosis. However, a lack of nuclear STAT6 immunopositivity and the unique NAB2::STAT6 fusion help distinguish these tumors from other entities.1,4,14,15

In the realm of CNS neoplasms, those with FET::CREB fusions form a rare group variably termed intracranial “angiomatoid fibrous histiocytoma” or “intracranial myxoid mesenchymal tumor” (IMMT).14,16–21 Both AFH and IMMT are defined by gene fusions involving FET RNA-binding proteins and CREB family transcription factors.

Our patient’s pathology report indicated CD99 positivity, a marker commonly expressed in Ewing sarcoma, synovial sarcoma, and low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma, suggesting a potential marker for tumor aggressiveness. Among the FET::CREB fusion genes, FUS-CREB3L2 is prevalent in well-defined low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma.3,14,19,22,23

In terms of clinical outcomes, these tumors tend to demonstrate a proclivity for local recurrence, with variable growth rates and recurrence intervals, sometimes extending up to 10 years. Dissemination or metastasis can occur, leading to mortality. Gross-total resection correlates with improved outcomes, but the definitive treatment approach remains debatable, especially for partially resected tumors. A definitive diagnosis often requires genome sequencing such as NGS to confirm the FET::CREB fusion, emphasizing the need for larger cohorts to establish prognostic criteria for these neoplasms14–20

Lessons

This case highlights the importance of maintaining a broad differential for extra-axial masses. The deceptive radiographic and histological resemblance of myxoid spindle cell neoplasm to meningiomas poses a challenge to neurosurgeons. Neurosurgeons should be aware of this entity, particularly in cases in which immunohistochemical markers suggest ambiguity. Molecular testing such as NGS plays a crucial role in definitive diagnosis, influencing prognosis and surveillance planning. Furthermore, our case underscores the necessity of larger cohorts to establish robust prognostic criteria for this uncommon pathology.
