Diagnostic performance of SD‐ΔRR during physical stressors for identifying diabetic sensorimotor polyneuropathy
Authors: Katsuyoshi Katayama, Haruhito A Uchida, Toshihiko Nagao, Jun Eguchi, Kenji Tsuji, Yoshinori Matsumoto, Ichiro Nojima, Keiko Tanaka, Hidemi Takeuchi, Yasuhiro Onishi, Rika Takemoto, Satoshi Miyamoto, Kenichi Shikata, Jun Wada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsThis study aimed to evaluate the clinical efficacy of SD‐ΔRR as a novel methodology for short‐duration heart rate variability (HRV) assessment for the diagnosis of diabetic sensorimotor polyneuropathy (DSPN) in patients with diabetes.Materials and MethodsParticipants were assigned into normal fasting glucose (NFG) and diabetes groups. Patients with diabetes were further classified as patients with and without DSPN. Electrocardiograms were recorded at rest, during standing, and during deep breathing to evaluate HRV parameters. HRV was statistically compared between the groups.ResultsA total of 103 patients with diabetes (18 with DSPN, 85 without) and 52 with NFG were included in the analysis. SD‐ΔRR values were comparable between the diabetes and NFG groups. However, among patients with diabetes, patients with DSPN had significantly lower SD‐ΔRRs than patients without DSPN (P < 0.05). Despite being age‐ and sex‐matched, patients with DSPN had a significantly higher prevalence of hypertension and advanced chronic kidney disease than those without DSPN. Logistic regression analysis demonstrated that SD‐ΔRRs during standing and deep breathing were significantly associated with the presence of DSPN in patients with diabetes (P < 0.05 each). After adjustment for confounding factors, only SD‐ΔRR during standing remained statistically significant. The cutoff value for SD‐ΔRR during standing showed high sensitivity (83%), whereas that during deep breathing demonstrated high specificity (84%) for diagnosis of DSPN.ConclusionsSD‐ΔRR may serve as a clinically informative parameter for identifying DSPN in patients with diabetes. In combination with physical stressors, this metric offers distinct diagnostic advantages with high sensitivity and specificity.Keywords: Cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy, Diabetic neuropathy, Heart rate variability analysis

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes is a critical global health concern affecting over 550 million patients worldwide. 1 The metabolic disorder is associated with several microvascular (nephropathy, retinopathy, and neuropathy) and macrovascular complications (stroke, ischemic heart disease, and peripheral arterial disease). 2 , 3 Among them, diabetic neuropathy affects more than 50% of patients and is one of the most prevalent complications of diabetes. 4 Nerve function is impaired by chronic hyperglycemia, leading to a variety of neurological symptoms, 5 , 6 such as sensory, motor, and autonomic nerve dysfunction, depending on the affected nerve type.

Cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy (CAN), a dysfunction of the cardiovascular autonomic nervous system, is a subtype of diabetic neuropathy. CAN is associated with circulatory abnormalities, such as orthostatic hypotension, postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, 7 , 8 acceleration of atherosclerosis, 9 , 10 and an increased risk of cardiovascular mortality. 11 , 12 , 13 Therefore, early screening for CAN has been proposed as an important approach for cardiovascular risk stratification in patients with diabetes. 14 Heart rate variability (HRV) analysis is a noninvasive method used to assess CAN. 15 , 16 HRV analysis consists of time‐domain analysis, such as analysis of the standard deviation of R–R intervals (SD‐RR) and frequency‐domain analysis, including high‐frequency (HF) and low‐frequency (LF) power. HRV is also associated with diabetic sensorimotor polyneuropathy (DSPN), as demonstrated by the link between HRV and peripheral nerve conduction velocity in patients with diabetes. 17 Furthermore, the HF and LF components are reduced in patients with DSPN, with low HF values associated with the presence of DSPN. 18

Although many HRV studies have employed 24‐h electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring, there remains a need for practical short‐term HRV assessments that can be completed within minutes. 19 , 20 Short‐term assessments significantly reduce the physical and temporal burden on patients and are readily applicable in routine clinical practice. Moreover, because both data acquisition and analysis can be completed rapidly, autonomic function can be evaluated during the same outpatient visit.

To assess beat‐to‐beat variability more effectively than the SD‐RR, the standard deviation of the differences between adjacent R–R intervals (SD‐ΔRR) has been analyzed in arrhythmia research. 21 In a study of patients with long QT syndrome, 21 SD‐ΔRR did not show significant differences between patients with long QT syndrome and healthy controls, either at baseline or in response to sympathetic stimulation via epinephrine. Nevertheless, considering its capacity to capture beat‐to‐beat dynamics, we hypothesized that SD‐ΔRR could serve as a robust time‐domain parameter for short‐term CAN screening. However, the clinical efficacy of SD‐ΔRR as a tool in the treatment of diabetic neuropathy remains unclear.

In this study, we aimed to assess the utility of SD‐ΔRR as a novel methodology for short‐duration HRV assessment for distinguishing DSPN in patients with diabetes. We measured HRV across three physiological conditions: rest, standing, and deep breathing, within a total of 10 min in patients with diabetes and those with normal fasting glucose (NFG).

METHODS

Study design

This cross‐sectional study, titled the FRACTAL study (Evaluation of autonomic nerve function by very short‐term heart rate variability: clinical study of diabetic autonomic nerve complications), was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Okayama University Hospital (K2004‐009) and Ako Central Hospital (20201116).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients who visited Okayama University Hospital or Ako Central Hospital as inpatients or outpatients between April 1, 2020 and March 31, 2025 were recruited after obtaining informed consent. Specifically, participants were recruited from the specialized departments of both institutions, including nephrology, diabetology and endocrinology, rheumatology and cardiology.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) participants assigned to the diabetes group had a pre‐existing diagnosis of diabetes or were those meeting the diagnostic criteria 22 at the time of enrollment. (2) The NFG group consisted of age‐ and sex‐matched individuals without diabetes. (3) The age range of participants was set at 20 years or older and less than 80 years old.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) uninterpretable ECG abnormalities, (2) use of alpha‐ or beta blockers as vasoactive medications, (3) patients receiving glucocorticoids, and (4) patients with cancer undergoing active treatment or under operative assessment.

Clinical and laboratory assessments

Participants completed a questionnaire about currently used medications, such as the use of antihypertensive and antidiabetic drugs; lifestyle habits, such as smoking and drinking; and physical examinations, including physical measurements for body mass index (BMI) and blood pressure.

Laboratory test results were obtained within 1 month of the ECG. The test items included measurements for hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), creatinine, urinary albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (uACR), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), uric acid, high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, and triglycerides.

Neurological assessment and DSPN definition

DSPN was diagnosed based on the criteria established by the Toronto Consensus. 23 Patients were classified as having possible DSPN if they presented with one of the following and probable DSPN if two or more criteria were present:

Clinical symptoms: Presence of symptoms and/or signs of peripheral neural dysfunction, typically presenting in a symmetric distal pattern, which includes positive neuropathic sensory symptoms (e.g., searing pain, paresthesia, and allodynia) and negative neuropathic sensory symptoms (e.g., numbness, sensory loss to touch, vibration, and/or temperature). 24 , 25 , 26

Achilles Tendon Reflex: Reflexes were assessed with the patient in a seated or kneeling position. Diminished or absent reflexes were recorded as abnormal, as described previously. 27

Vibration Perception Threshold: Vibration Perception Threshold was measured at the medial malleolus using a tuning fork. Sensory impairment was defined as bilateral perception durations of < 10 s. 28

Other causes of peripheral neuropathy 29 were excluded based on a thorough review of medical histories and clinical charts, as well as routine laboratory and clinical examinations performed at enrollment.

ECG protocol and HRV measurement

Participants abstained from food, caffeine, and smoking for at least 3 h before the measurements. After a 5‐min supine rest, a 10‐min ECG recording was performed using LRR‐03 (GMS Co., Ltd. Tokyo, Japan) at a sampling frequency of 1,000 Hz. The protocol consisted of three phases (Figure 1):

ECG methodology and an example of heart rate dynamics. (a) Representative heart rate changes across the three physiological conditions. (b) Schematic representation of the measurement protocol, including resting, standing, and deep breathing stressors.

(1) Rest: 5 min (data analyzed from 60 to 300 s).

(2) Standing: 1.5 min (data analyzed from 315 to 390 s).

(3) Deep breathing: 1 min (5‐s inhalation/exhalation cycle, analyzed from 450 to 570 s).

R–R intervals were automatically detected and processed using Kiritsu Meijin software (Crosswell Co., Yokohama, Japan). 30 HF and LF components were automatically calculated using the standard algorithms implemented in this software.

Data processing and novel HRV metrics (SD‐ΔRR)

To define the novel metric SD‐ΔRR, the raw data for the R–R interval, Ax, were processed to calculate the beat‐to‐beat difference Bx = Ax – Ax‐1.

The standard deviation of 33 consecutive R–R intervals is denoted by Cx.

Two different 33‐beat windows were used during data processing. A preceding window (Ax−33 to Ax−1) was used to identify abrupt R–R interval changes relative to the immediately preceding rhythm and to detect the onset of arrhythmia‐like fluctuations. In contrast, a centered window (Ax−16 to Ax+16) was used to evaluate local R–R interval variability while reducing the influence of large fluctuations occurring within the preceding window.

The elapsed time from the start was calculated as Kx:

The R–R intervals and their differences were excluded from the analysis based on the following criteria to ensure data quality:

1. Spontaneous variation: If |B(x)| > 3 × SD (Ax‐33 to Ax‐1), then Ax‐1 to Ax+1 was excluded.

2. Consecutive extra systole: If |Bx|/MIN (Ax‐1 to Ax) ≥ 0.5, then Ax was excluded.

3. Sustained variation: If |Bx| > 3 × Cx, then Ax was excluded.

4. Sawtooth wave pattern: If Cx/ Σ(Bx‐15 to Bx+16) < 0.03, then Ax was excluded.

Note: Σ(Bx − 15 to Bx + 16) denotes the signed sum of consecutive beat‐to‐beat R–R interval differences within the defined window.

The Bx values were removed from the analysis if the Ax values were excluded. An example of a filtered abnormal ECG wave is shown in Figure S1.

The remaining Ax and Bx values within the defined elapsed time, Kx, for each physical stressor were used in the calculation.

Rest: 60 ≤ Kx < 300 s.

Standing: 315 ≤ Kx < 390 s.

Deep breath: 450 ≤ Kx < 570 s.

The heart rate during each stressor was calculated from the remaining Ax values. The SD‐ΔRR for each stressor was defined as the standard deviation of the remaining Bx values.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was set at a minimum of 50 patients per group (NFG and diabetes) based on feasibility and consistent with previous exploratory studies investigating HRV. 10 The uACR was log‐transformed prior to analysis to account for its skewed distribution. Categorical variables were compared using the Fisher's exact test. For continuous variables, the Shapiro–Wilk test was first applied to evaluate normality. For two‐group comparisons, normally distributed data were analyzed using Student's t‐test, whereas nonnormally distributed data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. For multiple‐group comparisons, one‐way anova followed by Tukey's HSD post‐hoc test or the Kruskal―Wallis rank sum test was employed, as appropriate.

Logistic regression analysis was performed to estimate odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals for HRV parameters. Multiple logistic regression analysis included age and sex as covariates, as these factors have been consistently identified as significant confounders of HRV in previous literature. 31 All statistical analyses were performed using the JMP Student Edition 18 (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, Cary, NC, USA) and Stata software (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

RESULTS

Participants' characteristics

A total of 214 patients were screened, of whom 59 were excluded due to the following reasons: use of alpha‐ and/or beta‐blockers (n = 10), use of glucocorticoids (n = 27), active cancer or preoperative status (n = 13) or ECG abnormalities that could not be analyzed (n = 18). A few patients met multiple exclusion criteria. The final analysis included 155 participants (52 with NFG and 103 with diabetes). Among patients with diabetes, 39% (n = 40) were classified as having possible DSPN, while 17% (n = 18) were classified as having probable DSPN (Figure 2). For the purpose of this study, the DSPN‐positive group was defined as those diagnosed with probable DSPN.

However, the diabetes group exhibited significantly higher BMI (NFG vs. diabetes; 24.0 ± 4.9 vs. 26.2 ± 5.6 kg/m2, P = 0.0138) and significantly lower prevalence of hypertension (64.7% vs. 57.8%, P = 0.0114) than the NFG group. Laboratory data showed significantly higher eGFR (62.3 ± 23.0 vs. 74.7 ± 28.4 mL/min/1.73 m2, P = 0.0074) and significantly lower uACR (87 (8–356) vs. 11 (7–38) mg/g, P < 0.0001) in the diabetes group than the NFG group. Regarding renal failure risk using the KDIGO chronic kidney disease (CKD) staging system, 32 the NFG group had significantly higher prevalence of advanced CKD. No significant differences were found in HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, or uric acid levels between the two groups (Table 1).

Identification of DSPN within the diabetes group

To evaluate the specific utility of HRV in DSPN screening, we focused on patients with diabetes. In contrast to the NFG vs. diabetes group comparison, SD‐ΔRR, RMSSD, HF, and LF values were significantly lower in patients with DSPN compared to those without DSPN across all three stressors (P < 0.05, Figure 4a–i, Table 3). Notably, the heart rate and LF/HF ratio did not differ significantly between these subgroups, highlighting that absolute variability (SD‐ΔRR) and vagal components (HF) are more sensitive markers for DSPN than sympathetic‐vagal balance (Figure S2, Table S3).

Diagnostic performance of SD‐ΔRR

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified seven parameters significantly associated with the presence of DSPN: SD‐ΔRR during standing, SD‐ΔRR during deep breathing, RMSSD at rest, RMSSD during deep breath, HF during rest, HF during standing, and SD‐RR during deep breath. After adjusting for age and sex (model 1) in multivariate logistic regression models, five parameters remained significantly associated: SD‐ΔRR during standing, SD‐ΔRR during deep breathing, RMSSD during deep breath, HF during rest, and HF during standing (Table 4, Tables S1 and S2). Further adjustment for eGFR (Model 1 + eGFR) showed that only SD‐ΔRR during standing and HF during standing remained independently associated with DSPN (Table 4, Table S3). The ROC curve analysis for identifying DSPN based on model 1 revealed that the AUC values for SD‐ΔRR and HF were 0.7268, 0.6967, 0.7497, and 0.7340, respectively (Figure S3). Comparison of these AUCs using DeLong's method showed no significant differences (P = 0.7541), suggesting that the SD‐ΔRR shows a diagnostic performance comparable to traditional frequency‐domain parameters (Table 4). Using the optimal cutoff values determined using Youden's index, we further evaluated the diagnostic performance of these metrics (Table 5). SD‐ΔRR during standing, with a cutoff of 10, demonstrated the highest sensitivity (83.3%). Conversely, SD‐ΔRR during deep breathing, using a cutoff of 14, showed the highest specificity (84.4%). HF during rest (cutoff: 26) showed the highest Youden's index of 0.51.

DISCUSSION

The objective of this study was to evaluate the clinical efficacy of SD‐ΔRR as a novel methodology for HRV assessment in screening for DSPN using short‐term physical stressors. Our results demonstrated that SD‐ΔRR values were significantly lower in patients with DSPN than those without DSPN in patients with diabetes, although no significant differences were observed between the NFG and diabetes groups. Notably, SD‐ΔRR during standing and deep breathing emerged as robust, independent predictors of DSPN. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate the clinical significance of SD‐ΔRR in the evaluation of DSPN.

The established cutoff values for SD‐ΔRR offer a strategic framework for DSPN screening. SD‐ΔRR during standing (threshold of 10) provided high sensitivity (83%), making it an excellent primary screening tool to minimize false negatives. Conversely, SD‐ΔRR during deep breathing (threshold of 14) yielded high specificity (84%), serving as a reliable confirmatory measure to accurately rule out DSPN. The use of these practical integer thresholds, which closely approximate the optimal cutoff value derived using Youden's index, enhances the ease of implementation in routine clinical practice.

A noteworthy finding was the lack of a significant difference in the HRV parameters between the NFG and diabetes groups, which contrasts with previous studies reporting lower HRV in patients with diabetes. 33 This discrepancy likely stemmed from the high prevalence of renal dysfunction in the NFG cohort. Patients with NFG exhibited lower eGFR, higher UACR, and an increased risk of CKD progression based on KDIGO staging. 32 This suggests that HRV reduction in the NFG group may be attributed to uremic neuropathy, a distinct pathological condition resulting due to impaired kidney function. 34 , 35 Therefore, clinical interpretations of HRV should account for renal prognosis as a significant confounder regardless of diabetic status.

An important methodological consideration is the relationship between SD‐ΔRR and established HRV indices. SD‐ΔRR is mathematically related to established short‐term HRV indices derived from successive R–R interval differences, such as standard deviation of successive differences (SDSD) and root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD). However, the present methodology incorporates predefined preprocessing specifically designed for short‐duration autonomic assessment. These procedures used in this study, including exclusion of ectopic beats and artifact‐related patterns, require only simple computational operations and can be performed automatically with minimal processing burden. Therefore, SD‐ΔRR can be calculated rapidly after data acquisition, making it suitable for efficient autonomic function assessment during routine outpatient evaluation.

The use of a 33‐beat interval for data processing was based on three physiological rationales: (1) capturing the peak heart rate change after standing (approximately 10s) and subsequent stabilization (approximately 20s) 36 ; (2) ensuring adequate data collection for patients with bradycardia (down to 50 bpm), 37 and (3) aligning with ultra‐short‐term HRV standards (approximately 60s). 38 , 39 , 40 By analyzing 33 beats, we ensured a sufficient window (up to 40 s at 50 bpm) to accurately capture orthostatic tachycardia and its associated fluctuations. Because this assessment can be completed within 10 min, it may provide an efficient approach for autonomic evaluation in routine outpatient settings.

In comparing patients with diabetes by DSPN presence, SD‐ΔRR, as well as RMSSD, HF and LF, was significantly diminished across all stressors in the presence of DSPN. These findings suggest that the cardiac autonomic function is progressively impaired with peripheral nerve damage in diabetes. Logistic regression analysis after adjusted by age and sex identified SD‐ΔRR (standing and deep breathing), RMSSD (during deep breath) and HF (rest and standing) as independent predictors of DSPN. Interestingly, LF did not effectively distinguish DSPN, possibly because the short measurement duration was insufficient to accurately capture low‐frequency oscillations.

Furthermore, HRV parameters obtained during deep breathing were not retained as independent predictors after adjustment for renal function. One possible explanation is that slow and deep breathing at cycles of 5–6 s alters the frequency distribution of heart rate oscillations, shifting respiratory sinus arrhythmia from the HF range toward the LF range. 41 , 42 Consequently, HRV responses during programmed deep breathing may reflect physiological mechanisms different from those assessed during spontaneous breathing and may be less robust than orthostatic HRV responses in identifying DSPN.

This study has several limitations. First, to ensure the integrity of HRV as a surrogate marker for diabetic neuropathy, we strictly excluded patients taking glucocorticoids or alpha/beta‐blockers, or those with active malignancies, as these factors may modulate autonomic tone or glucose metabolism. 43 , 44 Second, patients with arrhythmias or cognitive or physical limitations were excluded from the analysis. Although these exclusions strengthen our internal validity, the generalizability of our findings may be limited. Third, this study was limited by its small sample size and was conducted using a single‐country cohort. Finally, the NFG group was recruited from specialty departments at a university hospital and a general hospital, which resulted in a relatively high prevalence of comorbid conditions, potentially limiting comparability with a healthy control population. Further studies are needed to determine its reproducibility and clinical utility in broader patient populations and across different clinical settings.

In conclusion, the SD‐ΔRR could be clinically useful in distinguishing the presence of DSPN from the absence. Its high sensitivity during standing and high specificity during deep breathing suggest that it can serve as a robust, non‐invasive screening tool for the management of diabetic complications.
