Arabic Mental Health Apps: A Narrative Review of Therapeutic and Engagement Features
Authors: Alya Al Harrasi, Tamadhir Al-Mahrouqi, Mohammed Al-Alawi, Abdullah Al Lawati, Amal Al Fahdi, Adhari Al Zaabi, Hamed Al Sinawi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mental health disorders constitute a significant global health burden, with stigma creating additional barriers to care in the Middle East. Mobile health technologies present potential solutions, yet their implementation in Arab countries remains largely unexplored. This narrative review examines the current landscape of Arabic mental health mobile applications as an emerging solution to these barriers. Through a comprehensive analysis of literature, we evaluate the therapeutic and engagement features of existing Arabic mental health apps, comparing them with well-documented Western platforms. While these apps show potential for improving healthcare accessibility and reducing stigma, they lack essential features for effective mental health support. Our findings revealed minimal implementation of engagement features, particularly in visualization, gamification, and social networking capabilities. Similarly, therapeutic features showed limited customization options and insufficient integration of evidence-based interventions. This review concludes that future development of Arabic mental health apps requires collaborative efforts between healthcare professionals, policymakers, and developers to create culturally sensitive, evidence-based digital solutions that effectively serve Arab populations.

INTRODUCTION

Mental health issues have been rising worldwide, including in Arab countries, where conflict, war, and socioeconomic challenges contribute to a high burden of mental disorders.[12] According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the prevalence of mental disorders in the Eastern Mediterranean Region, which covers the Arab countries, can range from 11% to >40%.[2] Despite an increasing acceptance of mental health disorders in the Arab Gulf region due to awareness campaigns and public health education, deeply rooted sociocultural challenges such as shame and stigma continue to influence how mental health is perceived.[3] Findings in existing Arab mental health research show that individuals tend to underutilize mental health services, showing negative attitudes toward mental health and psychological services.[4] Many Arabs are inclined to seek informal resources (e.g., a religious consultant, close friend, or family member) rather than formal resources for their mental illnesses to avoid confronting the stigma of seeking professional psychiatric help.[5] Additionally, psychological and psychiatric symptoms are frequently interpreted as manifestations of magic, the evil eye, or possession by “Jinn”.[6] This cultural interpretation makes seeking assistance from faith healers more socially acceptable in society compared to engaging with psychiatric or psychological professionals.[789] Consequently, these factors significantly influence seeking mental health care.[67]

One promising avenue to improve access to mental health care is the use of mobile health (mHealth) technology. The rise of mobile technology in healthcare established the field of mHealth, which is defined by the WHO Global Observatory for eHealth as “medical and public health practice supported by mobile devices, such as mobile phones, patient monitoring devices, personal digital assistants, and other wireless devices”.[10] During the first decade of the 21st century, a significant surge in the popularity of mHealth apps occurred, leading to users spending more time improving their health through these apps.[11] Furthermore, growing literature reviews demonstrated that app-delivered interventions have garnered acceptance in addressing mental health concerns and have shown efficacy in managing conditions such as anxiety and depression.[121314] These apps help address traditional clinical constraints such as long waitlists, limited clinic hours, and geographic barriers.[15] It is especially valuable in situations when individuals require in-the-moment emotional relief and traditional support might not be as effective by the time it becomes available.[16] In addition, mobile apps offer support mechanisms that reduce stigma and provide self-help tools for individuals who prefer to manage their issues independently.[7171819]

Cultural and religious aspects play an important role in the acceptance and adoption of digital health interventions in Arab populations.[720] Recently, many Arab individuals have preferred digital mental health interventions over traditional in-person consultations.[2122] Al-Mahrouqi et al. found that compared to in-person consultations, telephone appointments provided psychiatrists with the convenience and flexibility to manage non-clinical responsibilities and deliver tailored consultations while simultaneously benefiting patients by enhancing appointment adherence, reducing stigma, and offering financial savings.[23] In addition, Al-Alawi et al. reported that a 6-week, therapist-guided online therapy in Oman was more effective at improving COVID-19 related anxiety and depression than self-help, internet-based interventions.[24] Similarly, findings from a qualitative study indicated that telehealth participants believed remote consultations helped remove stigma and offered greater accessibility.[25] A randomized controlled trial of Sanadak, an Arabic self-help app, revealed that the app helped reduce self-stigma significantly.[26] In 2022, Labayh, a Saudi mental health app, successfully reached over 1 million users and >300 licensed specialists.[7] The app offers a range of services, including anonymous teleconsultations, mood-tracking features, and support groups tailored to the needs of the Saudi population.[7] Likewise, Qareboon, developed by the National Committee for Mental Health Promotion, offers Arabic mental health educational materials in various formats, including text, infographics, and video content, alongside free psychological text consultations.[727] Overall, these studies highlight the critical role of digital interventions in overcoming traditional barriers and advancing mental health support for Arab individuals.

Despite the major advantages mHealth apps offer, it remains essential to address the barriers that may limit their widespread adoption. An umbrella review by Koh et al. examined 36 studies on mental health apps and highlighted that privacy and confidentiality breaches were consistently cited as major concerns, particularly relating to third-party access to confidential information and the lack of a transparent privacy policy.[28] Similarly, an evaluation of 61 mental health apps found that nearly half of the apps (41%) did not have a privacy policy to inform users about how and when personal information would be collected or shared with third parties, despite this being a standard recommendation of privacy regulations.[29] Likewise, a Saudi study assessing the Sehhaty (“My Health”) app reported that privacy and security concerns, especially regarding transparency and data protection, were major deterrents to app usage.[30] Therefore, addressing privacy concerns is particularly important in the Arab region, where actual or perceived data breaches may result in serious reputational harm and deter individuals from seeking mental health support.

Globally, there are 300,000 mHealth apps available, including 10,000 apps that specifically focus on mental health.[5] Of these, >100 support the Arabic language, compared with only 23 apps that existed in 2018.[7] Additionally, some Arabic mental health apps have exceeded 100,000 downloads, indicating a growing market within the region. The surge in mental health apps underscores the need for rigorous app evaluation at several levels, such as user-based app marketplace evaluation (e.g., star ratings and user reviews), expert review platforms (e.g., PsyberGuide and RankedHealth), and point-based app evaluation.[31] Several systematic reviews have used point-based scales to assess mental health apps, such as the Mobile App Rating Scale (MARS), the Mhealth Index and Navigation Database, and the Mobile App Development and Assessment Guide.[323334] Notably, MARS-At was specifically developed and validated to reliably evaluate the quality of Arabic health applications.[35] This narrative review aims to assess the current status of Arabic mental health apps by analyzing therapeutic and engagement features. The review also evaluates existing clinical evidence and offers recommendations for future app development.

According to Grant and Booth, a narrative review involves a broad examination of recent or current literature to summarize information and highlight areas of improvement.[36] Given that narrative reviews do not require a formal or systematic approach to identifying and selecting literature, the current review did not adopt or report an exhaustive search process. An electronic database search of PubMed, PsycINFO, and Google Scholar was performed of articles published between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2024. The search keywords were “mHealth OR mobile health OR digital health OR AI” AND “mental health OR well-being” AND “Arabic OR Arab OR Middle East OR Gulf.” The inclusion criteria for the studies were as follows: fully peer-reviewed, written in English or translated into English, and focused on digital health interventions for treating or assessing any mental health condition in Arab populations. The research team screened and selected sources based on their relevance to the review’s focus on Arabic mental health apps and the depth of information they provided. For final inclusion, we screened the abstracts of potentially eligible publications, removing duplicates and unrelated articles.

THERAPEUTIC FEATURES

Therapeutic features refer to evidence-based functionalities designed to assist users in managing their mental health symptoms and overall well-being. Common examples include modules for identifying and restructuring maladaptive thinking patterns (i.e., “automatic negative thoughts”), scheduling daily activities, and setting personalized goals. In addition, many applications incorporate tools for writing and self-reflection, such as gratitude journaling and affirmations, standardized tests, scales, and relaxation exercises (e.g., guided breathing or meditation).

A systematic review by Alnaghaimshi et al. of 22 Arabic mHealth apps revealed that many lacked robust evidence-based interventions.[33] Both Saudi and Western English mental health applications rarely integrate therapeutic interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT).[7] In contrast, psychoeducation is the most commonly implemented feature in app stores across these regions. Possibly the limited integration of CBT in Arabic-language mental health apps may stem from several factors. These include resource constraints, such as limited access to trained CBT professionals and funding for evidence-based content development. Cultural and linguistic adaptation challenges also play a role, as CBT concepts may not always align easily with local norms or expressions. Additionally, there may be limited awareness or demand for CBT-based interventions among both users and developers in Arabic-speaking regions.[7] Among 110 Arabic mental health apps analyzed by Aldaweesh et al., frequently reported therapeutic features included sharing capabilities (42.7% of apps), self-monitoring tools (29.1%), visual aids (18.2%), healthcare professional support (16.4%), and customization options (9.1%).[32] Self-monitoring features, which allow users to input personal data such as mood, thoughts, behaviors, goals, diaries, and symptoms, are crucial for fostering self-awareness.[32] Despite their proven effectiveness, these self-tracking and monitoring strategies are extensively implemented in English mental health apps, whereas their incorporation in Arabic mental health apps is considerably less prevalent.[7]

ENGAGEMENT FEATURES

Engagement features are intended to promote consistent app usage, enhance user motivation, and sustain therapeutic involvement.[36] Such features include regular prompts or reminders, interactive challenges, progress visualization (charts or graphs), and options for sharing achievements with peers or professionals. By increasing user adherence and fostering active participation, engagement features can augment the overall effectiveness of the therapeutic components.

Poor user engagement is a common issue related to mHealth interventions.[32] Studies have shown that psychological interventions are less effective when delivered in a way that does not sustain an individual’s engagement with the intervention.[37383940] Despite their importance, several reviews identified an overall lack of features that foster an engaging user experience in many Arabic mHealth apps.[323334] A review of 61 Arabic mHealth apps using the MARS scale found that engagement and aesthetics consistently received the lowest ratings across most applications.[41] In regard to Arabic mental health apps, Aldaweesh et al. (2023) revealed that of 110 apps, the most common engagement features in the review were offline mode (53), reminders (46), and gamification (11).[32] Sokoon, a gamified CBT app for Arabic individuals, was designed for adults with anxiety and depression by utilizing Hexad theory, gamification, and a dynamic difficulty adjustment algorithm to personalize user experiences based on individual personality traits.[42] Over a 2-week trial, a sample of 30 participants found that Sokoon significantly reduced symptoms of depression and anxiety. The study shows that gamifying CBT increases engagement and motivation and reduces treatment time and cost.[42]

Moreover, Arabic mental well-being apps displayed poorly implemented engagement features and essential functions such as sharing and reminders.[32] There is a notable absence of peer and social support features in Arabic mental health apps despite the importance of social and family-based support within the Gulf society.[7] Of the 22 Arabic mental health applications reviewed, only one provided social networking support by establishing a virtual community identity.[34] A recent review found that social networking features were integrated into 15% (5/34) of Western CBT mHealth apps, enabling user-to-user connections. These social elements aimed to address loneliness and social isolation common in mental illness while enhancing app adherence and attractiveness.[43]

A recent systematic review of Western mental health apps argued that the app interface and the screen’s appearance directly impacted the user’s impression and engagement. The two identified themes to improve interface aesthetics were attention-grabbing and a simple and consistent style.[44] To address these shortcomings, Torous et al. recommended incorporating “challenges” into mental health apps to improve engagement over short periods.[45] These challenges aim to turn the use of the app into a habit, thereby creating tangible behavioral change.

Furthermore, the integration of mobile sensors for mental health promotion is often overlooked in Arabic mHealth apps. Incorporating mobile sensing can increase users’ self-awareness, personalize app content based on real-time mental states, and predict future mental health needs.[32] Studies of Ecological Momentary Interventions (EMI) show that sensor-based systems can collect “passive” data (automatically gathered through device sensors) and “active” data (user-provided information) to enable precisely timed support.[46] These “Smart-EMI” systems can trigger personalized interventions based on sensor data, potentially enhancing digital dopamine production, a reward mechanism that supports therapeutic engagement and behavioral change in users’ natural environments.[46]

DEMOGRAPHICS AND mHEALTH USE

Age, gender, and education appear to shape mHealth app adoption in Arab populations. A study including 376 Saudi mental health patients found a significant association between mHealth application usage and age groups, with more users aged between 18 and 30 years (n = 160).[47] Moreover, findings from a Jordanian sample revealed that male participants and individuals aged >40 years were less likely to download mHealth applications.[48] Conversely, those who had pursued post-secondary education were more likely to adopt these digital health tools than participants of secondary school or below educational level.[48] These patterns are consistent with findings from a U.S. population-based survey, where men, older adults (45–64 years), and individuals with lower educational backgrounds were less likely to engage with mHealth technologies.[49]

REAL-WORLD STUDIES AND CLINICAL IMPACT

There are limited real-world studies that evaluate the usage of mental health and well-being apps, especially Arabic ones. A recent systematic review by Lau et al. of over 1,000 publicly available mental health apps found that only 2% had undergone scientific evaluation.[50] Additionally, an assessment of 21 apps listed on mental health app ranking lists revealed that only 29% (6/21) had associated scientific publications or clinical trial results.[43] This makes it challenging for users and health professionals to recognize high-quality, evidence-based apps.[43]

Among the well-studied apps are IntelliCare, which integrates CBT, acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), and positive psychology into a skills-based platform,[1951] and PTSD Coach, designed to support individuals with posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD).[4650] The PTSD Coach app has been shown to reduce PTSD symptoms and enhance coping strategies.[5253] Likewise, in a naturalistic 30-day trial of the HeadGear app, researchers found that greater user engagement correlated with significant decreases in depressive symptoms, alongside improvements in well-being and work performance.[54] Similarly, the Sanadak app, an Arabic-language smartphone-based self-help intervention, was designed to reduce posttraumatic stress in Syrian refugees living in Germany.[26] Findings revealed that although there was a general reduction in symptom severity among users, Sanadak did not significantly outperform the control group, which received psychoeducational materials on trauma and PTSD at both 4-week and 4-month follow-ups.[26] However, Sanadak was notably effective in reducing self-stigma, specifically stereotype agreement and stereotype application, compared to the control group.[26] These studies help assess how mental health apps are being used in the open market to ensure their efficacy and guide future developments. Therefore, it is essential that future apps undergo scientific validation to ensure they deliver real therapeutic value.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Recent reviews emphasized the value of involving healthcare professionals and policymakers in designing Arabic digital psychological apps.[734] Their involvement can enhance the apps’ quality, reliability, efficacy, and privacy.[34] Furthermore, developers should adhere to internationally recognized standards and adapt their products to local languages, cultures, and varying health literacy levels, especially for individuals with low literacy.[34] A key consideration in Arabic mental health app development is the coexistence of Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) and Dialectal Arabic (DA). MSA serves as the formal written language, while DA is the informal spoken language that varies significantly across regions and countries.[55] Although the extensive use of Arabic dialects in social media made data abundant,[55] their variation across regions makes it challenging to design apps that accommodate all users. Therefore, using MSA in the initial development of mental health apps may ensure broader accessibility and understanding among Arabic speakers. Nevertheless, Arabic mental health apps should incorporate culturally relevant content such as family structures, community values, and religious beliefs to foster user resonance and trust. App developers might incorporate visible assurances (e.g., clear privacy policies and professional credentials of content creators) to signal trustworthiness. In support of Vision 2023 for digital health transformation, which aims to develop a secure and connected healthcare system,[56] the Saudi Ministry of Health has accredited several mental health apps, including “Labayh,” “Famcare,” and “Estenarh”.[7] Thus, to support a seamless patient management plan and reduce inconvenience for both psychiatrists and patients, it is important to unify communication channels across primary, secondary, and tertiary healthcare levels and to implement electronic prescriptions.[23]

Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) has been projected to have the greatest benefits in diagnostic accuracy for certain illnesses. The use of GenAI in psychiatric settings can facilitate administrative tasks, enhance diagnostic support, and improve patient engagement.[57] Replika and Wysa are two widely used Artificial Intelligence (AI) conversational agents (chatbots), collectively reaching over 840,000 users.[19] These chatbots enable users to engage in freeform dialogues, providing both a sense of social connection and the opportunity to learn therapeutic techniques. Despite their shared functionalities, Wysa focuses on structured therapeutic activities, whereas Replika prioritizes unstructured, conversational interactions. Either app may serve as a standalone intervention for some users, and Wysa may be helpful as a supplement to therapy, allowing users to practice the skills learned between therapy sessions.[19] In addition, Replika includes features that allow users to customize the chatbot’s appearance, which may enhance user engagement. It is important to note that some users might find certain interactions within these apps unsettling or uncomfortable, particularly when the AI chatbot is designed to mimic humans.[19]

While AI applications have been increasingly integrated into mental health care, recent reviews demonstrate the absence of employing AI techniques in existing Arabic mental health apps.[3258] Additionally, the development of automated support systems that are guided by machine learning is necessary so that users receive real-time, personalized guidance and prompts based on their needs.[59] However, the use of GenAI in mental health applications raises several concerns, including the accuracy of outputs, bias, privacy, and security.[57] Studies testing GenAI’s effectiveness in different mental health scenarios have occasionally found “inappropriate” and “dangerous” recommendations.[60] Furthermore, feedback from individuals using ChatGPT suggests a lack of awareness of culturally specific Arabic vocabulary and culturally rooted fears that the doctor would understand.[61] For these reasons, it is necessary to conduct a comprehensive testing of AI technologies to evaluate their efficacy.

CONCLUSION

Arabic mental health apps show significant potential in reducing mental health stigma and enhancing accessibility. Future progress in this field requires a collaborative effort among healthcare professionals, policymakers, and developers to create culturally sensitive designs tailored to Arab populations. A well-developed Arabic mental health application combines therapeutic and engagement features while effectively integrating AI.
