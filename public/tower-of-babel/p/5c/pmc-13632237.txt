Integrin-mediated mechanotransduction in the tumor microenvironment: macrophage-centered signaling mechanisms and immune remodeling
Authors: Guanghui Liu, Qi Sun, Yuting Zhong, Lijuan Liu, Changgang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) play a critical role in the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). Although the biochemical signaling pathways regulating TAMs have been extensively elucidated, how these cells persistently sense physical properties of the tumor stroma—such as matrix stiffness, tension, and compression—and translate them into sustained immunosuppressive programs remains to be systematically addressed.Main bodyUnlike other infiltrating immune cell populations that primarily engage in transient adhesive interactions, TAMs utilize integrins for stable spatial anchoring and continuous mechanotransduction. In this review, we synthesize current evidence on the integrin-TAM mechanosensing axis. We first distinguish the persistent mechanobiological features of TAMs from those of structural stromal cells and other transiently infiltrating lymphocytes. Subsequently, we delineate how force-dependent signaling shapes TAM phenotypic plasticity, metabolic reprogramming, and spatial organization. Crucially, we define a self-amplifying biomechanical-immune feedback framework, demonstrating how mechanically remodeled TAMs actively exacerbate extracellular matrix stiffening to consolidate the immunosuppressive niche. Furthermore, we critically evaluate the translational relevance of this axis, analyzing the limitations of past single-target integrin inhibitors and highlighting the emerging potential of multidimensional combination therapeutic strategies.ConclusionsTargeting integrin-dependent mechanotransduction provides a rational, system-level strategy to dismantle mechanically reinforced immune barriers. Breaking this interlocking feedback framework offers new insights for remodeling the TME and enhancing the efficacy of current immunotherapies.

Introduction

Immunotherapy, as the third revolution in the treatment of malignant tumors, has profoundly changed the landscape of cancer therapy [1]. Although immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have achieved remarkable efficacy in many types of malignancies [2], a considerable number of patients still fail to obtain long-term benefit [3]. This is mainly due to the suppression of immune cells by the tumor microenvironment (TME) [4]. Such functional or structural suppression is a major reason why immunotherapy does not translate into durable clinical benefit. Reversing this suppression requires not only understanding the intrinsic mechanisms of immune cells but also fully considering the external influences of the TME on immune cells [5].

Among environmental factors, the biomechanical properties of the TME represent an important yet easily overlooked dimension [6]. Mechanical factors such as matrix stiffness, interstitial pressure, and local tension affect the migration, retention, and function of immune cells [7]. Cells perceive these mechanical signals through specialized mechanosensing apparatus and convert them into intracellular signaling programs that control cytoskeletal states, gene expression, and metabolic activity [8]. Although such mechanotransduction pathways have been recognized in tumor and stromal cells, their role in shaping the immune landscape has not been sufficiently emphasized Existing evidence indicates that biomechanical signals can shift macrophage polarization, regulate immune checkpoint expression, and induce the formation of spatially defined immunosuppressive niches that exclude T-cell infiltration [9]. However, despite increasing recognition of biomechanical–immune interactions, it remains unclear which immune population serves as the primary mechanosensitive effector within this system.

Within the potential biomechanical-immune interaction network, integrins and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are of significant research value. As key mechanosensing apparatus, integrins can effectively convert extracellular mechanical signals into intracellular biochemical signals [10]. Among immune cell populations in the TME, TAMs stand out as a prominent population that possess a highly integrated pathway that includes sensitive mechanical sensing, efficient signal transduction, and stable immune reprogramming. First, TAMs have a broad and high-density repertoire of β1, β2, and αv-family integrins, which enables them to maintain direct and sustained contact with extracellular matrix (ECM) components [11, 12]. In this respect, T cells, Natural killer (NK) cells, and Dendritic cells (DCs) typically exhibit distinct interaction dynamics with ECM components, often characterized by lower intensity or shorter duration compared to the sustained engagement observed in TAMs [13, 14]. Second, TAMs possess a signaling repertoire analogous to the mechanotransduction pathways characterized in stromal cells, incorporating key elements such as Talin/Kindlin recruitment, focal adhesion kinase (FAK)/Src activation, cytoskeletal tension generation, and transcriptional reprogramming dependent on YAP/TAZ or STAT3/6 [10]. These structural and signaling attributes provide TAMs with an enhanced mechanotransduction capacity relative to lymphocytes, facilitating persistent transcriptional and metabolic reprogramming. In contrast, integrin signaling in lymphocytes is primarily tailored for transient adhesion dynamics and exhibits a restricted capacity for generating sustained downstream signaling programs [15, 16]. Third, under these influences, TAMs actively modulate the remodeling of immune output. Compared with lymphocytes and other myeloid subsets, TAMs function as key orchestrators that actively promote a stable immunosuppressive microenvironment through programmed death-ligand 1(PD-L1) induction, IL-10/ transforming growth factor (TGF-β) production, and matrix remodeling. Importantly, they do not merely respond to pre-existing suppressive conditions but actively reinforce them [17–19]. These distinctive dual capacities in biomechanical sensing and immune regulation distinguish TAMs from T cells, NK cells, and other myeloid subsets, positioning them as a critical entry point for analyzing how biomechanical cues shape immune homeostasis.

This review integrates recent research findings and, for the first time, conceptualizes the integrin-TAM axis as a key regulatory node of a biomechanical-immune feedback system. We first summarize the structural features of integrins and their mechanotransduction mechanisms in immune cells. We then examine how integrin signaling shapes TAM phenotype, spatial distribution, and immunoregulatory output. Subsequently, we synthesize spatial and functional evidence to reveal how these processes collectively contribute to the formation of immunosuppressive niches. Finally, we discuss intervention strategies targeting structural, signaling, and delivery nodes with the aim of breaking this systemic feedback loop. The purpose of this review is not merely to list literature, but to integrate multi-dimensional findings into a coherent feedback loop model and identify key actionable points for achieving precision immunotherapy.

Integrins as mechanotransductive hubs in the tumor microenvironment

Integrins, as transmembrane receptors, function as central hubs that connect the intracellular and extracellular environments and mediate mechanical signal transduction. However, this mechanosensing capacity exhibits pronounced cell type specificity within the TME. Among immune cell subsets, TAMs, owing to their long-term tissue residency and distinctive integrin expression patterns, are uniquely positioned at the interface of sustained ECM engagement and immune regulation. Accordingly, this section first addresses the structural basis of integrin-mediated mechanotransduction, then examines the comparative biomechanical advantages and distribution characteristics of TAMs, and further elucidates the modular signaling transduction machinery through which mechanical physical loads are converted into a transcriptional priming state.

Structural basis of integrin-mediated mechanotransduction

Integrins are heterodimeric transmembrane receptors composed of 18 α-subunits and 8 β-subunits, forming 24 distinct heterodimers that serve as central conduits for biomechanical sensing and signal transduction in the TME [20]. Each integrin consists of three primary domains: the extracellular domain mediates ligand recognition and binding, the transmembrane domain plays a critical role in vertical transmission of mechanical forces, and the relatively short cytoplasmic domain connects to intracellular signaling complexes, further regulating cellular functions [21].

The α-subunit extracellular domain typically adopts a β-propeller structure, with some subtypes containing an inserted I-domain for direct ligand engagement, while the β-subunit harbors an I-like domain with a metal ion-dependent adhesion site (MIDAS) that coordinates divalent cations such as Mg²⁺ and Mn²⁺ to stabilize ligand carboxyl groups, thereby enhancing integrin-ECM binding affinity [22–26]. Different heterodimer combinations confer distinct ligand preferences, including arginine-glycine-aspartate (RGD)-binding integrins recognizing Arg-Gly-Asp motifs in fibronectin, vitronectin, or osteopontin; collagen-binding integrins favoring Gly-Phe-Hyp-Gly-Glu-Arg sequences; laminin-binding integrins modulating epithelial polarity; and immune-cell-specific integrins, such as the β2 family, interacting with intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) or iC3b to regulate adhesion, migration, and phagocytosis [27–32].

The transmembrane domain not only serves as a structural linker but also transmits mechanical tension, whereas the cytoplasmic domain-particularly the β-subunit tail containing NPXY/NXXY motifs-recruits Talin, Kindlin, Paxillin, and integrin-linked kinase (ILK), connecting integrins to the actin cytoskeleton to regulate cellular tension, migration, and mechanosensitivity [10, 33–35]. Integrin conformational dynamics, ranging from bent-closed to extended-closed and extended-open states, allow ligand affinity and downstream signaling to respond to ECM density, spatial distribution, and mechanical cues [36–39]. Moreover, integrins exhibit force-dependent catch-bond behavior, where bond lifetimes increase under tensile force, enabling TAMs to maintain stable adhesion in highly cross-linked ECM regions while remaining sensitive to low-tension environments, providing a molecular basis for translating subtle biomechanical gradients into stable immunoregulatory outputs.

Comparative mechanotransduction and specific integrin distribution of TAMs

Within the highly orchestrated, multicellular ecosystem of the TME, mechanotransduction is not an isolated phenomenon restricted to a single cell type. Structural stromal cells, particularly cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and tumor endothelial cells (TECs), are widely recognized as the primary architects of the biomechanical microenvironment. Through integrin and mechanosensitive ion channel signaling, CAFs drive continuous ECM remodeling, collagen cross-linking, and matrix stiffening, while TECs adapt to local physical cues to orchestrate disorganized angiogenesis. Concurrently, various infiltrating immune cells exhibit highly specialized, context-dependent mechanosensitive profiles. Rather than operating as the sole mechanosensors, TAMs function as a vital regulatory interface within this diverse network, translating stromal-derived physical constraints into profound immunosuppressive programming. To better contextualize the unique bridging role of TAMs, it is essential to first compare their mechanosensing paradigms with those of other key cellular compartments.

Mechanosensing profiles of structural and malignant cells

In the TME, integrin expression exhibits marked cell-type specificity that constructs the fundamental physical barriers. In malignant epithelial cells, α6β4 and αv family integrins are primarily involved in maintaining polarity or, in response to changes in ECM stiffness and ligand density, driving the development of invasive and highly mechanosensitive phenotype [36–38, 40].

Simultaneously, structural stromal cells establish the biomechanical foundation. CAFs predominantly utilize β1 and α11-containing integrins, coupled with mechanosensitive ion channels (e.g., Piezo1), to regulate collagen cross-linking, fiber organization, and continuous matrix stiffening [41, 42]. This continuous ECM remodeling erected by CAFs not only generates elevated local tension but also constructs dense mechanical barriers that profoundly restrict immune cell infiltration. Concurrently, tumor endothelial cells (TECs) constantly integrate hemodynamic shear stress and local ECM stiffness to orchestrate disorganized neo-angiogenesis [43, 44]. Collectively, the mechanotransduction within malignant and stromal cells is fundamentally geared toward generating physical forces and erecting structural barriers, setting the mechanical stage to which all subsequent infiltrating immune cells must respond and adapt.

Mechanosensing profiles of other tumor-infiltrating immune cells

In T cells, integrins constitute essential mechanotransduction units tailored to support antigen recognition and migration with high spatiotemporal precision, distinguishing them from the stroma-anchored dynamics of TAMs. Classic leukocyte integrins like lymphocyte function-associated antigen 1 (LFA-1) (αLβ2) and very late antigen-4 (VLA-4) (α4β1) transduce mechanical cues into cytoskeletal remodeling via interactions with ICAM-1, vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1), and matrix proteins [45, 46]. Tissue-specific repertoires, such as αEβ7 (CD103), mediate sustained adhesion to E-cadherin⁺ niches [47]. T cell mechanotransduction spans distinct temporal scales: T-cell receptor (TCR)-triggered inside-out signaling rapidly increases LFA-1 affinity within seconds, while subsequent Rap1-talin-dependent actin reorganization establishes a stable immunological synapse lasting for tens of minutes, prolonging Ca²⁺ activation [45, 46, 48]. Crucially, LFA-1-mediated anchorage-dependent signals facilitate the G1-S transition and interact with Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT) complexes to impact long-term effector differentiation [49]. Epigenetically, KMT2D maintains the transcriptional accessibility of leukocyte-specific integrins via H3K4me3 modification, its loss driving stable reprogramming of T cell subpopulations [50]. Mechanical coupling among LFA-1, Crk adaptors, phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K), and actin enables persistent adjustment of migration modes [48]. Furthermore, mechanical forces within the TME modulate T cell activation and exhaustion trajectories via actomyosin-dependent YAP signaling, highlighting another layer of parallel mechanotransduction.

Parallel to T cells, NK cells exhibit highly dynamic and temporally tuned mechanosensing profiles. Circulating effector NK cells depend on β2 integrins (e.g., LFA-1) for transient serial killing, where adhesive contacts can be acutely suppressed by neuroendocrine cues (e.g., β2-adrenergic stimulation) to regulate rapid cycles of degranulation and detachment [51]. Conversely, chronic TME exposure imprints durable dysfunction. Recruited conventional NK cells undergo integrin-associated transcriptional reprogramming, progressively upregulating α1β1 (CD49a) and αEβ7 (CD103) to acquire a stable tissue-retained profile coupled with effector loss [52–55]. Tumor-imposed αv integrin signaling further drives the cis-activation of TGF-β directly on NK cells, locking them into a tumor-adapted dysfunctional state [56].

Similarly, DCs utilize stage-dependent mechanotransduction to couple physical adhesion with functional maturation. Immature DCs rely on β1/β2 integrins (e.g., CR3, CR4) for environmental sensing, with specific pairs like α7-laminin maintaining lineage identity [57–59]. Sustained β2-integrin adhesion restrains DC maturation; however, upon encountering inflammatory cues, the rapid recycling of these integrins dismantles podosomes, initiating dynamic 3D migration [58, 59]. Crucially, this disruption of mechanical anchoring (e.g., kindlin-3 dissociation) activates the Ras homolog family member A (RhoA)-MRTF-A/SRF transcriptional module, imprinting a long-lived epigenetic landscape (H3K4me3/H3K27me3) that durably locks DCs into a mature, lymph node-homing phenotype [60–62]. Blocking sensors like α7 integrin stably redirects them toward a macrophage-like state. Thus, DCs’ mechanical machinery is fundamentally specialized for licensing maturation and migratory escape.

TAMs: Sustained biomechanical advantages and stable niche remodeling

Among the diverse immune populations in the TME, TAMs play a distinct, indispensable role at the interface between mechanical signaling and immune regulation [63]. Unlike the transient synapse stabilization of T cells, the acute serial killing of NK cells, or the migratory escape of DCs, TAMs establish longer-lasting and more stable contacts with ECM components, enabling sustained integrin-mediated signal transduction [64–66]. This property is intrinsically associated with their myeloid origin and long-term residency within the tumor stroma, allowing them to sense biomechanical cues early and respond accordingly [65, 67].

Compared with other immune subsets, TAMs exhibit a broader and higher-density integrin expression repertoire (commonly expressing β1, β2, and αv families), enabling simultaneous interactions with multiple ECM ligands [68, 69]. Beyond surface characteristics, TAMs possess an extensive intracellular mechanotransduction program that efficiently converts mechanical cues into stable signaling inputs [70, 71]. This subsequently regulates mechanosensitive transcriptional factors, allowing mechanical inputs to drive stable transcriptional and metabolic reprogramming [72]. Importantly, TAMs do not function merely as passive recipients of physical forces; they act as proactive executors that remodel the microenvironment. Integrin-dependent mechanosensing actively reinforces immunosuppression, including the induction of PD-L1, secretion of IL-10 and TGF-β, and the enhancement of extracellular matrix remodeling [73].

Furthermore, this highly diversified integrin expression in TAMs is neither random nor static but follows ordered, context-dependent patterns [12]. First, differences in immune polarization states are associated with characteristic integrin combinations, driving corresponding adhesion modes and functional outputs [74]. Second, spatial heterogeneity profoundly dictates integrin profiles. TAMs dynamically adjust their repertoire in response to local differences in ECM composition, stiffness, and tension, accommodating region-specific structural demands for either migration or stable anchoring [70]. Additionally, tumor-type-specific cell-cell interaction signals further modulate these expression frameworks [75](Table 1).

Overall, this sustained mechanosensing capacity, highly specific integrin distribution, and stable immunoregulatory output provide the clear mechanistic rationale for positioning TAMs as the pivotal mechanosensitive nodes within immunosuppressive niches.

Mechanotransduction: From physical loading to transcriptional priming

Mechanical anchoring: From conformational activation to molecular clutch coupling

Integrin-mediated mechanosensing arises from the coordinated operation of Inside-out and Outside-in signaling modes [83]. Under resting conditions, most integrins adopt a bent-closed conformation, characterized by low ligand affinity and restricted association of cytoplasmic tails with signaling adaptor proteins [36, 84]. Inside-out signaling is initiated by intracellular stimuli and promotes the binding of Talin, Kindlin, and related proteins to the β-subunit cytoplasmic tail, thereby disrupting the stabilizing interactions between the α/β transmembrane helices and driving conformational transitions toward extended-closed and extended-open states [85]. In parallel, ligand engagement and extracellular mechanical forces can trigger Outside-in signaling, enabling mechanical signals to be transmitted across the membrane and initiating the assembly of focal adhesion complex (FAC) [86].

Within the TME, TAMs are chronically exposed to elevated matrix stiffness, which fundamentally reshapes integrin activation kinetics. Under these conditions, integrin activation no longer manifests as a simple binary on/off switch but instead resides in a primed state with a reduced activation threshold [83, 87]. This feature is attributed to the relative enrichment of Talin and Kindlin in TAMs, which increases the probability of β-subunit cytoplasmic tail engagement and stabilizes partially extended conformations even in the absence of overt external force, rendering TAMs highly sensitive to subtle mechanical perturbations within the TME [88, 89].

This primed state is thought to facilitate efficient mechanical anchoring. It is well established that TAMs highly express and functionally rely on diverse integrins to mediate adhesion and interactions with the tumor stroma and malignant cells [90, 91]. Upon ligand binding, as extensively characterized in classic cellular models, activated integrins under tension engage the Outside-in signaling cascade under tension and form high-density clusters [92, 93]. These clusters do not function merely as adhesive structures but instead serve as mechanical coupling nodes that determine whether extracellular forces can be effectively transmitted to the actin cytoskeleton. This process is commonly described by the molecular clutch model, enabling TAMs to achieve mechanical anchoring within the tumor matrix [94]. Sustained tension at adhesion sites further permits force-induced conformational changes, such as Talin stretching and unfolding, which expose cryptic binding sites required for downstream signal molecule recruitment. Through Inside-out-mediated priming, force-stabilized clustering, and molecular clutch engagement, TAMs are proposed to establish the mechanical entry point required for integrin-dependent signal initiation.

Mechanosensitive signal transduction

Following molecular clutch engagement, integrin clusters transition from simple biomechanical couplers into signal initiation platforms, converting sustained mechanical load into effective signaling output. In TAMs, this transduction is achieved through a modular signaling architecture in which distinct mechanotransduction pathways assume clearly defined functional roles.

The FAK/Src module serves as the initiation point for mechanical signal conversion. Mechanically induced integrin clustering promotes FAK autophosphorylation at the Y397 site, generating a high-affinity docking platform for Src family kinases [95]. This constitutes the first round of signaling input following mechanical engagement, allowing the recruitment of intermediary signaling molecules such as PI3K and Shc and amplifying mechanical signals within localized adhesion sites [96, 97].

Concurrently, mechanical cues activate the YAP/TAZ module, which functions as a mechanosensitive transcriptional gate. Sustained cytoskeletal tension promotes YAP/TAZ dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation [98]. Importantly, at this stage, nuclear YAP/TAZ should not be interpreted as direct executors of specific immune programs but rather as indicators of a transcriptional priming state. This state establishes a nuclear environment permissive to mechanical inputs, thereby lowering the threshold for TAMs to respond to subsequent cytokine or metabolic signals within the TME [99, 100].

Signal output is further stabilized by the RhoA/ Rho-associated coiled-coil-containing protein kinase (ROCK) feedback module. Through FAK-dependent recruitment of guanine nucleotide exchange factors such as guanine nucleotide exchange factor H1 (GEF-H1), integrin signaling activates the RhoA pathway, enhancing actomyosin contractility and promoting stress fiber formation [101, 102]. This process elevates intracellular tension and strengthens integrin-cytoskeleton coupling, thereby stabilizing molecular clutch engagement and preventing premature mechanical disengagement.

Collectively, these signaling modules convert transient physical contacts into a persistent mechanotransduction program. Although integrin-mediated mechanotransduction does not directly determine the immunoeffector functions of TAMs, it establishes a transcriptional priming state by coordinating kinase activation, nuclear accessibility of mechanosensitive transcriptional regulators, and maintenance of cytoskeletal tension. This state provides the essential biomechanical and signaling foundation for the TAMs phenotypic remodeling and immunoregulatory outputs discussed in subsequent Sect [103]. (Fig. 1).

Integrin-mediated functional changes of TAMs

TAMs are the critical responders and executors of biomechanical cues within the TME. Positioned downstream of integrin-mediated mechanotransduction, TAMs convert extracellular physical stimuli into intracellular signaling cascades, thereby reshaping their phenotype, metabolism, and immune functions. This chapter systematically reviews three dimensions: the sources of extracellular biomechanical inputs, TAM remodeling, and the formation of feedback systems. By tracing this mechanistic chain, we reveal how TAMs function as a key integrative node linking biomechanical transduction with immune regulation.

Sources and characteristics of biomechanical signals in the TME

ECM: Increased stiffness and matrix densification

The most prominent mechanical alteration in tumor tissues is a marked increase in ECM stiffness, driven by extensive collagen cross-linking, abnormal deposition of matrix proteins, and enhanced alignment and anisotropy of ECM fibers [104]. This stiffened ECM not only alters tumor cell adhesion and migration but also profoundly influences the spatial localization and mechanosensing capacity of TAMs via integrins [105]. Increased collagen cross-linking and the resulting stiffness promote clustering and adhesive stabilization of αvβ3 and α5β1 integrins, thereby amplifying focal adhesion assembly and FAK/Src signaling [106]. Direct experimental evidence demonstrating this mechanism in TAMs remains limited; nevertheless, TAMs broadly express these integrins and are enriched in collagen-rich regions, suggesting they may form adhesion-enhanced retention niches that contribute to the spatial establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment [107].

Solid stress and tissue tension: Sustained compression driven by tumor expansion

As tumors undergo rapid growth, increased cell density, and insufficient angiogenesis, substantial solid stress accumulates within the tumor microenvironment. This stress arises as tumor cells expand within a confined space, exerting compressive forces on the surrounding tissues [6]. Such mechanical pressure not only collapses blood vessels and compromises perfusion and oxygen delivery, but also imposes persistent mechanical stimulation on TAMs, leading to alterations in adhesion patterns, cellular deformability, and integrin activation states [108, 109]. Integrin-mediated mechanosensitive pathways may function as early effectors, coordinating TAM mechanosensing and subsequent functional remodeling [100, 110].In addition, elevated interstitial fluid pressure, frequently induced by intratumoral hypoxia and aberrant vascularization, impedes interstitial fluid drainage, which places TAMs in regions with high pressure, low flow, and hypoxia [111]. These conditions collectively drive TAM polarization toward an M2-like phenotype [112].

Shear and spatial tension gradients: Guiding TAMs migration and colonization

Within tumors, rapid and heterogeneous proliferation generates pronounced spatial gradients of solid stress and interstitial shear stress, elevated pressure in the core and at the periphery. These shear stresses arise from heterogeneity in cellular expansion rates and matrix slippage [113, 114]. High-shear, fibrotic regions are commonly associated with ECM densification and are enriched in αv-expressing secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1)⁺ macrophages that exhibit robust TGF-β pathway activation and potent suppression of CD8⁺ T-cell activity [115]. Spatially resolved single-cell and imaging technologies have consistently observed TAMs redistribution along fibrotic, high-tension stromal tracts and at tumor-stroma interfaces, indicating that regional tissue tension and stromal density jointly shape TAMs positioning and provide key evidence for mechanosensing-mediated remodeling of immune microdomains [116, 117].Notably, shear forces can also unmask cryptic RGD motifs by stretching ECM fibers [118], thereby enhancing TAMs localization and adhesive strength and realizing a tripartite coupling among mechanical force, spatial positioning, and functional output.

Biomechanical response characteristics of TAMs

Integrin clustering and adhesion stability: Biomechanical basis of TAMs anchoring

As stated in Sect. 3.1, increased ECM stiffness, elevated collagen cross-link density, and the emergence of tension gradients in the TME constitute prominent biomechanical cues. These physical perturbations do not influence all immune cell types uniformly; TAMs are particularly sensitive to them. Integrin-mediated structural contacts enable TAMs to sense and anchor within matrix regions with mechanical gradients, promoting adhesive engagement and subsequent functional activation [117, 119].

Multiple integrin isoforms on the surface of TAMs form initial integrin-ligand contacts with ECM components. Under sustained mechanical traction, integrins cluster and undergo conformational activation, driving reorganization of signaling microdomains and recruiting FAC proteins such as Talin and Kindlin [120, 121]. Extrapolating from foundational integrin activation models [92], these events are proposed to drive the assembly of a stable TAM-ECM integrin adhesion complex (IAC). Functionally, this transition represents a biomechanical shift in which TAMs convert from migratory cells into anchored, functionally active cells.Adhesion following integrin activation not only stabilizes TAM spatial localization within the ECM but also serves as a biomechanical locus for intracellular signal initiation, which activates key downstream pathways including FAK, RhoA/ROCK, and YAP/TAZ [99]. This adhesion-signaling coupling further cooperates with tumor-derived factors such as TGF-β and IL-10 to promote TAM polarization toward an immunosuppressive phenotype [122]. Moreover, integrin-FAK signaling can modulate TAM metabolic programs and mitochondrial positioning, establishing a biomechanical preconditioning that facilitates phenotypic transitions [97, 123].

Importantly, after establishing stable anchorage, TAMs reciprocally remodel the local ECM by releasing ECM-remodeling enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) [124] and immunomodulatory mediators including TGF-β and IL-10, creating an immunosuppressive, biomechanically dependent positive-feedback loop [99, 125]. This mechanism indicates that integrins are not merely passive biomechanical sensors but function as dynamic modulators that regulate tumor microstructure and immune landscape. On this basis, TAMs progress to a subsequent behavioral stage characterized by selective retention, spatial enrichment, and the establishment of localized immunoregulatory niches.

Spatial localization and migration: Stiffness gradient-guided enrichment of TAMs

Within the TME, the ECM exhibits pronounced stiffness heterogeneity, and such biomechanical gradients serve as critical drivers regulating TAM migration and spatial enrichment [126]. TAMs sense these stiffness differences via integrin clustering and conformational activation, exhibiting migration toward denser, higher-tension regions of the ECM [127]. This process is primarily mediated by integrins αvβ3 and α5β1 and orchestrated through the FAK-RhoA/ROCK-YAP/TAZ axis, enabling cells to execute a biomechanically sensitive migratory loop encompassing detection, anchoring, and adhesion reinforcement [128]. More importantly, integrins are not merely activated; they interact with signaling molecules within the TME, such as TGF‑β and IL‑10 [129, 130]. These signals feedback to regulate integrin expression and its mediation of spatial anchoring, establishing a self-sustaining, cyclical program for TAMs migration, enrichment, and immune remodeling [69, 122].

Pre-polarization remodeling: Integrin-mediated signal integration and functional shaping of TAMs

Prior to polarization, TAMs undergo a series of integrin-mediated signal-integration events that not only influence their polarization direction but also drive subsequent functional remodeling. Integrin β3 promotes TAMs polarization toward an M2-like phenotype and augments their immunosuppressive functions by upregulating peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) [77, 79]. Drawing parallels from tissue repair models, where the loss of integrin β3 has been associated with impaired muscle regeneration and increased fibrosis [77, 131], it is plausible that β3 integrin similarly regulates stromal remodeling and fibrotic responses within the TME. Integrin β8 enhances C-C motif chemokine ligand 5 expression via the PI3K-AKT-IRF9 (interferon regulatory factor 9) signaling axis, promoting TAM polarization to an M2-like state and facilitating their chemotactic recruitment and accumulation. This establishes a positive feedback loop between tumor cells and TAMs that drives tumor proliferation and invasion [132]. Integrin αMβ2 promotes TAMs toward anti-inflammatory programs via the Src-AKT signaling axis. Upon activation, αMβ2 enhances IL-10 secretion through interconnecting Src/Syk and PI3K-AKT networks, promoting polarization toward an immunosuppressive, M2-like state. In parallel, αMβ2 functions as a mechanosensor that couples structural inputs to functional signaling, further reinforcing its effector programs [133, 134].Integrin β1 is pivotal for TAM-ECM interactions. Loss of β1-mediated adhesion favors an M1-like state, whereas in the presence of IL-4, β1 activation drives M2-like polarization [135]. Notably, even with IL-4, M2 polarization is difficult to achieve when adhesion is disabled, underscoring the requirement for integrin-dependent anchorage [136](Fig. 2).

Integrin-driven triple mechanisms of programmed remodeling of TAMs

Phenotypic remodeling: Integrin-driven polarization and the emergence of TAM heterogeneity

TAMs achieve polarization to the M2 phenotype in the TME via integrin-mediated signaling pathways, which promotes immune escape and tumor progression. Integrins activate downstream inflammatory signaling axes to drive transcriptional programs that determine macrophage-specific immune phenotypes, i.e., transcriptional reprogramming dictates their polarization fate toward either the M2 or M1 state [137].

Direct experimental evidence from breast and lung cancer models demonstrates that integrin αvβ3 is highly expressed in M2-type TAMs and drives the formation of an immunosuppressive phenotype through activation of the FAK-SRC-STAT6 pathway. Blockade of αvβ3 significantly inhibits STAT6 phosphorylation and reduces the expression of M2 markers such as Arg1 and CD206 [69, 138]. Similarly, tumor-specific studies indicate that integrin β3 promotes M2 polarization of TAMs by upregulating PPARγ in breast cancer microenvironments [77]. Corroborating these findings, evidence extrapolated from non-tumor models, such as microglia in traumatic brain injury, suggests that pharmacologic inhibition of integrin β3 using selective antagonists or blocking antibodies can shift these cells toward an M1-like phenotype [139], highlighting a potentially conserved integrin-dependent polarization switch. In addition, elevated interstitial fluid flow in tumor tissues can act as a mechanical signal that induces M2 polarization. In vitro mechanobiological studies and corresponding in vivo tumor models demonstrate that this flow-induced polarization operates via the integrin-Src-STAT3/6 pathway, enhancing their pro-migratory properties and promoting tumor cell invasion [112, 140]. Furthermore, recent studies confirm that integrin β8 induces M2 polarization of TAMs and promotes tumor progression by upregulating chemokine CCL5 and activating the PI3K/Akt-IRF9 signaling axis [132, 141].

Building upon these classical polarization pathways, recent single-cell transcriptomic advances reveal that integrin-mediated phenotypic remodeling drives the emergence of highly specific immunosuppressive TAM subsets beyond the traditional M1/M2 binary. However, the current experimental evidence linking these distinct subsets to integrin-mediated mechanotransduction varies significantly, ranging from direct mechanistic validation to emerging hypothesis-generating associations and profound knowledge gaps.

First, supported by direct mechanistic data, SPP1⁺ TAMs constitute a highly immunosuppressive and mechanosensitive hub. In glioblastoma and glioma models, the emergence of SPP1⁺ myeloid cells are directly driven by tumor-derived ligands engaging with αvβ3/αvβ5 integrins; disrupting this interaction with the 7aaRGD peptide reshapes the myeloid compartment toward a pro-inflammatory state and enhances anti-PD-1 efficacy [142]. This axis is conserved across aggressive malignancies, where SPP1⁺ TAMs form a neutrophil-recruiting niche via VSIG4 co-expression [143], and utilize CD44/PI3K-AKT signaling—acting cooperatively with integrin networks—to drive an M2-like, pro-angiogenic state [144].

Second, in contrast to the definitive integrin-SPP1 axis, the relationship between mechanotransduction and triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2+ (TREM2+) TAMs remain primarily hypothesis-generating. TREM2⁺ TAMs represent another critical subset correlating with exhausted CD8⁺ T-cells across human cancers [145]. While relying on immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif (ITAM) adaptors, TREM2 signaling fundamentally converges on integrin-linked cytoskeletal and adhesion pathways to orchestrate suppression. However, direct biophysical evidence demonstrating that mechanical force definitively activates this pathway in TAMs is still pending.

Finally, the identification of these mechanosensitive subsets also highlights critical knowledge gaps; for instance, while complement C1q subcomponent subunit C (C1QC) ⁺ macrophages are frequently inferred as tissue-resident-like TAMs, direct evidence linking their specific differentiation to integrin or ECM mechanical control remains largely uncharacterized.

Taken together, evidence across diverse tumor models and analogous tissue injury systems indicates that integrins drive the inflammatory phenotypic programming of TAMs-ranging from activating classical signaling pathways for M2 polarization, to dictating the emergence of specialized immunosuppressive subsets like SPP1⁺ and TREM2⁺. This comprehensive phenotypic remodeling supports immune escape and tumor progression. An in-depth understanding of these mechanisms can facilitate the development of TAM-targeted immunotherapies and improve therapeutic outcomes in cancer patients.

Metabolic remodeling: Bidirectional coupling of biomechanical-metabolic networks

TAMs are not static immune cells; under combined biochemical cues and biomechanical stimuli in the TME, they undergo profound metabolic reprogramming. Integrin-dominated signaling axes serve as key integrative nodes regulating TAM metabolic programs. In glucose metabolism, hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) are well-established drivers of TAM glycolysis, promoting the transcription of glycolytic genes including glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), hexokinase 2 (HK2), and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) [146–148]. While the precise upstream mechanical triggers in TAMs remain under investigation, evidence from malignant epithelial models demonstrates that activation of integrin αvβ5 recruits FAK and AKT to directly upregulate this HIF-1α/STAT3 axis [149–151]. Consequently, it is highly plausible that a parallel integrin-mediated mechanism operates in TAMs to enhance lactate production and tolerance, supporting their survival and immunomodulatory functions in hypoxic and acidic niches. Moreover, STAT3 links metabolism to immune suppression by inducing arginase 1 (ARG1) and TGF-β, reinforcing TAM-mediated immunosuppression in the TME [152].

Beyond glycolysis, lipid metabolic reprogramming is similarly regulated by integrins. Direct studies in TAMs demonstrate that in an milk fat globule-EGF factor 8 (MFG-E8)-dependent pathway, integrin-mediated activation of the FAK-AKT axis upregulates PPARγ [153]. Furthermore, integrating foundational mechanosensory research which identifies sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (SREBP-1) as a universal mechanical target [154], it is proposed that these signals synergistically operate in TAMs, promoting lipogenesis and lipid-droplet biogenesis. This shift not only provides membrane precursors but also fuels fatty acid oxidation (FAO), releasing ATP and NADPH to sustain the oxidative metabolic dominance of M2-like TAMs [155, 156]. Furthermore, macrophage energy metabolism frequently shifts toward mitochondrial oxidation. Drawing insights from generalized macrophage polarization models, YAP activation through the mammalian STE20-like protein kinase 1/2 (MST1/2)-YAP-STAT6 axis is known to increase carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (CPT1A) expression while suppressing glycolysis [157, 158]. Crucially, direct evidence within the TME confirms that this enhanced CPT1A-driven fatty acid oxidation is fundamentally required for TAM differentiation and activation [159]. This targeted metabolic rewiring shifts TAM energy metabolism toward mitochondrial oxidation, a change directly correlated with enhanced IL-10 secretion and reduced cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) activity. Importantly, direct evidence in tumor models confirms that integrin-triggered metabolic reprogramming is not linear but forms a feedback-stabilized loop [160]. Lactate produced by TAMs stabilizes HIF-1α and enhances STAT3 phosphorylation, amplifying integrin-driven metabolic-immune effects [161, 162]. Accumulated lactate also inhibits dendritic-cell antigen presentation and NK-cell activity, forming a metabolic-immune-structural loop that consolidates immunosuppression [163].

Taken together, integrins function as pivotal hubs in TAM metabolic remodeling: from enhanced glycolysis and adaptive lipid metabolism to lactate-driven feedback loops, which shapes TAM localization, polarization, and function within the TME.

Functional remodeling: Synergistic activation of immunosuppressive factor networks

In the TME, integrins not only mediate TAMs adhesion and migration but also promote the expression of immunosuppressive factors through specific signaling pathways, sustaining an immunosuppressive state.

In the TME, integrin αvβ8 serves as a highly specialized mechanotransducer for latent TGF-β activation. While this mothers against decapentaplegic homolog 2/3 (SMAD2/3)-activating mechanism is classically established in regulatory T cells (Tregs) for suppressing effector T cell activity [164–166], recent breakthrough evidence demonstrates that TAMs similarly exploit this precise pathway. Tumor-infiltrating macrophages highly express αvβ8 to engage local latent TGF-β, orchestrating a localized immunosuppressive niche. Crucially, targeted blockade of this specific αvβ8-TGF-β axis effectively reprograms TAM polarization and restores cytotoxic immune infiltration [122], highlighting a critical myeloid-driven mechanism for immune evasion. Direct evidence in TAMs demonstrates that integrin β1 activates the PI3K/AKT pathway [107]. Supported by analogous findings in general macrophages (e.g., via αvβ3) [167], this cascade increases STAT3 phosphorylation, promoting its nuclear translocation, and enhancing its transcriptional activity. Activated STAT3 upregulates IL-10 and PD-L1, further suppressing T cell function and reinforcing immunosuppressive effects. In addition, IL-6 activates the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, establishing a positive feedback loop that continuously promotes the expression of immunosuppressive factors and sustains the immunosuppressive state [168]. β2 integrins synergize with adhesion signaling elements in myeloid cells to activate the NF-κB pathway [169, 170], enhancing nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity of the p65 subunit, thereby inducing expression of immunoregulatory factors, including IL-10, TGF-β, and the chemokine CCL22 [171]. Furthermore, the synergistic engagement of the STAT3 and nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) pathways is fundamentally required for maintaining a pro-immunosuppressive TAMs phenotype [172]. Crucially, however, integrin-mediated immunoregulatory outputs are highly context-dependent. In stark contrast to the general suppressive paradigm, direct evidence demonstrates that targeted pharmacological activation of integrin CD11b overrides these suppressive networks to drive anti-tumor innate immunity [130].

Taken together, integrins serve as key regulators of signal transduction in TAMs, synergizing multiple pathways to drive immunosuppressive factor expression and maintain the stability of the immunosuppressive state via positive feedback loops, promoting tumor immune evasion and progression (Fig. 3).

Intervention framework for the integrin–TAM axis based on a biomechanical-immune feedback loop model

The integrin-TAM axis in the TME forms a dynamic feedback system through sensing, transducing, regulating, and remodeling, which results in continuously increased matrix stiffness and progressively enhanced immune suppression. This system jointly reinforces immune evasion and represents a critical driver for the limited efficacy of current immunotherapies. Therefore, intervening in this feedback and reshaping the local immune microenvironment has become essential for effective therapeutic strategies. This chapter proposes multidimensional therapeutic approaches for the integrin–TAMs–immune axis across three levels: signaling, structure, and system.

Formation and maintenance of the biomechanical–immune feedback loop

The integrin–TAM axis in the TME establishes a dynamic feedback circuit through coordinated processes of mechanosensing, signal transduction, immune regulation, and matrix remodeling. This circuit progressively amplifies matrix stiffness while reinforcing local immunosuppression, forming an interlocking biomechanical–immune loop that contributes to the limited efficacy of current immunotherapies. Consequently, disrupting this feedback and reshaping the local immune microenvironment have become key objectives for effective therapeutic strategies (Fig. 4).

Biomechanical sensing and structural initiation

Tissue stiffness increases markedly during tumor progression. In regions of elevated ECM rigidity, TAMs detect mechanical cues through integrin clustering and matrix engagement. αvβ3 primarily mediates tension-dependent interactions with fibronectin, whereas β1 integrins dominate stiffness sensing within collagen-rich matrices [173, 174]. These mechanosensing events activate classical force-responsive pathways, including FAK and YAP, which drive TAM expression of immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β [11].

On this basis, integrin–Piezo1 cooperation further sustains stiffness feedback. Piezo1, a mechanosensitive Ca²⁺ channel enriched on TAM surfaces, is activated by integrin-mediated adhesion forces. The resulting Ca²⁺ influx promotes immunosuppressive polarization [175–177]. Importantly, Piezo1 links to a Ca²⁺–AKT–FAK–YAP signaling cascade [178, 179]. that stabilizes the accumulation of immunoregulatory molecules, including PD-L1 and TGF-β [180, 181]. In the absence of Piezo1, TAMs exhibit reduced motility and attenuated M2 polarization in stiff matrices, underscoring its functional role in sustaining the structural feedback loop [136, 173, 182].

Structural initiation of this process also depends on early cellular interactions. Adhesive forces between TAMs and fibroblasts can activate the αvβ3–Piezo1 axis, inducing YAP nuclear translocation in fibroblasts and rapidly generating traction stress that promotes ECM deposition [183]. Meanwhile, M2 polarization of TAMs increases TGF-β production, which upregulates collagen cross-linking enzymes such as lysyl oxidase (LOX), driving irreversible collagen alignment and shifting the biomechanical state toward stabilized matrix architecture [69, 184].

Spatial anchoring and immune-functional feedback

Immunosuppressive organization within the TME is strongly shaped by the integrin–TAM axis. Integrin signaling guides TAMs toward specific spatial niches through interactions with ECM ligands. Engagement of αvβ3 and β1 integrins activates FAK signaling, which cooperates with transcriptional regulators such as YAP and STAT3 to induce immune checkpoint molecules including PD-L1 and T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3) [185, 186]. In parallel, integrin αvβ8 activates latent TGF-β, triggering the SMAD2/3 program. These pathways function synergistically with tumor-cell–derived checkpoints; for instance, elevated Galectin-9 strengthens inhibitory signaling through the TIM-3 pathway [187–189].

This regulatory network is further reinforced by metabolic and inflammatory crosstalk. Lactic acid exposure stimulates TAMs to release IL-1β, which activates NF-κB–dependent PD-L1 upregulation in tumor cells and enhances CCL2 secretion [190, 191]. The resulting chemokine gradient recruits additional TAMs, reinforcing spatially organized macrophage accumulation [192]. At the same time, tumor-derived exosomes are internalized by TAMs, where activation of the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)–myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88)–STAT3 pathway promotes immunosuppressive polarization and coordinated upregulation of PD-L1 and IL-10 [17].

Through these processes, TAMs become spatially anchored within distinct microanatomical regions. In hepatocellular carcinoma, M2-like TAMs accumulate in fibrotic zones, forming immune-exclusion barriers [193]. Similar structural exclusion patterns emerge along the CAF–TAM chondroitin-6-sulfate–STAT3 axis [194], further reinforced by IL-34–mediated AKT signaling [195]. Additionally, TGF-β coexpressed by CAFs and TAMs suppresses major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I expression in tumor cells while increasing PD-L1 levels, further consolidating local immunosuppression [196].

Bidirectional interaction and maintenance of the double feedback loop

Within the TME, the integrin–TAM axis establishes bidirectional coupling between biomechanical remodeling and immune regulation.

First, the mechanical loop actively promotes immune suppression. Increased matrix stiffness enhances PD-L1 expression through the integrin–YAP/STAT3 pathway, thereby limiting CD8⁺ T cell activity [197]. The tumor ECM displays pronounced spatial heterogeneity, where high-stiffness regions favor TAM aggregation and stabilize immunosuppressive phenotypes through the integrin β1–FAK–YAP axis, generating PD-L1–enriched cores within the microenvironment [198, 199].

Second, immune signaling reciprocally reinforces mechanical barriers. CAF-derived factors such as TGF-β and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) activate TAMs, which in turn promote ECM stiffening [200–202]. Cooperation between M2-polarized TAMs and CAFs enhances collagen cross-linking, induces LOX expression, and drives anisotropic collagen remodeling, progressively increasing tissue stiffness [203, 204].

As these processes converge, dense collagen organization and localized tension gradients restrict CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, while TAM-derived immunoregulatory mediators accumulate within the same regions [205, 206]. The resulting microdomains integrate structural exclusion with functional immune suppression. In this model, CAF-mediated matrix remodeling and TAM-driven signaling act together to generate a coupled biomechanical–immune barrier that stabilizes a therapy-resistant TME [207–210]. These findings suggest that targeting spatial mechanical organization may represent an important strategy for disrupting the immune closed loop (Fig. 5).

Therapeutic strategies targeting the integrin-TAM axis

Targeting signaling pathway nodes

Within the framework of the immunosuppressive feedback loop established by the integrin–TAM axis, the programmed cascade amplification of signaling pathways is a critical determinant for maintaining TAM phenotypic stability. Current therapeutic strategies are evolving from solely inhibiting tumor cell metastasis toward actively intervening in the microenvironmental ecology. Specifically, these strategies unfold across three primary dimensions: direct modulation of macrophage integrin signaling to reprogram TAMs, blockade of integrin-mediated M2-TAM recruitment, and indirect remodeling of TAM polarization and immune function via the integrin-FAK mechanotransduction axis.

First, directly targeting myeloid integrins and their downstream metabolic/signaling nodes represents a core strategy for achieving TAM reprogramming. Small-molecule agonists targeting the myeloid integrin CD11b (αMβ2) constitute a relatively mature and highly promising class of targeted therapeutics. CD11b agonists are capable of rewiring TAM inflammatory pathways: on the one hand, they inhibit NF-κB activation by promoting p65 degradation; on the other hand, they trigger the stimulator of interferon genes (STING)–STAT1–type I interferon pathway through FAK-dependent mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby inducing a broad local interferon response and anti-tumor immunity within the tumor niche [211]. In tumor specimens from phase I clinical trials, treatment with GB1275 significantly elevated STING and STAT1 phosphorylation levels in TAMs, suggesting that functional reprogramming via the CD11b–FAK–STING–IFN pathway can be successfully translated to human tumors [211]. Rather than merely inhibiting cellular adhesion, these integrin agonists precisely push TAMs from an immunosuppressive state toward an immune-activating phenotype. Beyond direct integrin receptor modulation, interventions targeting established metabolic and signaling nodes remain crucial. For instance, integrin β3 activation drives M2 polarization by upregulating PPARγ, whereas PPARγ blockade can reverse this metabolic-functional circuit [77, 79, 212]. At a higher hierarchical level, PI3Kγ promotes α4 integrin expression and reinforces immunosuppressive polarization; targeting PI3Kγ can effectively break the tandem loop of recruitment-polarization-immunosuppression [213].

Second, recent translational research has uncovered a tumor–TAM recruitment axis centered on macrophage integrins, providing new targets for disruption at the source. In metastatic prostate cancer models, tumor cells upregulate IL-32 secretion via the cathepsin Z (CTSZ)–transformer 2 alpha homolog (TRA2A)–IL-32 cascade; IL-32 subsequently binds to macrophage ITGA5 (integrin α5) via its RGD motif, driving the massive infiltration of M2-TAMs into metastatic niches and promoting bone and lung metastasis [214]. Pharmacological blockade of ITGA5 (e.g., using the antagonist GLPG0187) significantly reduces M2-TAM infiltration and lowers the metastatic burden [214]. These findings demonstrate that intervening in specific ligand–integrin pairs not only inhibits tumor metastasis but also interrupts the immunosuppressive loop at both physical and signaling levels by restricting the spatial recruitment of macrophages.

Third, inhibiting integrin–FAK signaling acts as an immune microenvironment remodeler by modulating the bidirectional interactions between tumor cells and TAMs. The integrin-FAK-STAT pathway is the signaling core sustaining the suppressive function of the TME. Previous studies have confirmed that FAK inhibitors (e.g., defactinib) markedly reduce TAM infiltration, decrease the expression of immunosuppressive factors such as IL-6 and IL-8, and demonstrate synergistic immune activation potential in combination with PD-1 therapy [69, 97, 215, 216]. At a more granular interaction level, drug-tolerant lung cancer models show that simvastatin, by disrupting cholesterol-enriched lipid rafts, inhibits integrin β3–FAK–adhesion signaling. This not only re-sensitizes chemoresistant cells to paclitaxel but also shifts M2-like TAMs toward an M1-like phenotype via regulation of the cholesterol–liver X receptor (LXR)/ ATP binding cassette subfamily A member 1 (ABCA1) axis (accompanied by upregulated tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and downregulated TGF-β and IL-10) [153]. Utilizing multifunctional liposomes activated by the tumor-associated protease legumain to co-deliver simvastatin and paclitaxel achieves dual targeting of high integrin β3-expressing tumor cells and TAMs [153]. Notably, TAMs themselves can reversely shape the tumor’s dependency on FAK inhibition. Research in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma reveals that TAM-secreted CCL22 assembles the diacylglycerol kinase α (DGKα)/FAK complex via the CCR4–DGKα axis, leading to sustained high FAK activation [217]. In such tumors characterized by high CCL22 expression and high pFAK activity, the TAM-induced FAK addiction can be effectively exploited by FAK inhibitor therapy [217].

However, the clinical translational limitations of single-target interventions on mechanosensing pathways must be viewed with strict objectivity. Despite these strategies demonstrating formidable potential to break TME homeostasis in preclinical models and early-phase trials, the interactions between integrins and the extracellular matrix possess extreme redundancy and compensatory nature within the complex human tumor network. The most representative counter-example is cilengitide, a specific antagonist targeting αvβ3 and αvβ5 integrins. Although it potently suppressed TAM-mediated angiogenesis and microenvironmental remodeling in murine models [218], cilengitide ultimately failed to improve overall survival in the phase III CENTRIC clinical trial for glioblastoma [219]. This profound clinical failure starkly indicates that solely blocking the physical adhesion of specific integrins is easily overcome by compensatory receptors or bypass signaling within the TME, and inappropriate low dosing may even paradoxically stimulate angiogenesis.

Taken together, these preclinical, early clinical, and unsuccessful translational evidences point to a clear conclusion: integrins and FAK must not be viewed simply as unidirectional migration/survival factors within cells, but rather integrated into a broader integrin–TAM axis. Acknowledging the clinical translational limitations of single-target inhibition, future therapies must finely tune TAM recruitment, polarization, and bidirectional communication via integrins and downstream FAK signaling. This approach will establish a robust sensitizing platform for subsequent multi-target combination therapies, particularly combinations with immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Structural and spatial remodeling and targeted delivery

The primary cause of immunotherapy failure often lies not in intrinsic defects of immune cells, but in their spatially inaccessible state. Within the TME, spatially organized immune-excluded structures, collaboratively established by TAMs, CAFs, and the ECM, represent a critical manifestation of the programmed closure along the integrin-TAM axis. The spatial distribution of integrins dictates the localization of immunosuppressive signals, with TAMs and CAFs co-expressing PD-L1 and TGF-β, and generating CD8⁺ T-cell exclusion zones via the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis, thereby restricting T-cell positioning and impairing T-cell activation [220, 221].

Dismantling local microregional structures is a key strategy to overcome structural immune failure [209]. Unlike conventional approaches that solely relieve functional inhibition, spatial deconstruction emphasizes the organization of microregional structures. CXCR4 inhibitors such as NOX-A12 and AMD3100 effectively overcome the chemotactic barrier within the CAF-TAMs niche, restoring T-cell trafficking [222, 223]. To achieve this spatial disruption with higher precision, TAM-targeted biomaterial delivery systems have been extensively developed. For instance, iRGD-modified nanomedicines disrupt immune-suppressive microregions through locally targeted release and synergizing potently with PD-1 therapy [224]. Studies using a PD-L1 nanotrap system in liver cancer models further demonstrated that targeted delivery outperforms conventional antibodies in relieving structural inhibitory barriers [193, 225, 226]. Fundamentally, these strategies aim to convert the integrin-TAM axis from an organizer into a deconstructor.

Despite the conceptual elegance of these TAM-targeted delivery strategies, translating them into clinical reality faces formidable feasibility challenges. Nanomedicines heavily rely on the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect for tumor accumulation; however, the EPR effect is notoriously heterogeneous in human solid tumors [227]. Paradoxically, the dense, integrin-crosslinked fibrotic stroma that these nanomedicines aim to dismantle acts as a severe physical barrier, restricting nanoparticle penetration [228]. Furthermore, systemically administered nanocarriers are highly prone to off-target sequestration by the reticuloendothelial system (RES) in the liver and spleen, drastically reducing the effective dose reaching the TME while posing systemic toxicity risks [229].

To bridge this translational gap, recent advances in mechanical immunoengineering provide concrete, feasible solutions to bypass these systemic bottlenecks. First, actively upgrading systemic targeting strategies beyond passive EPR accumulation is crucial. Current designs exploit receptors highly expressed on pro-tumoral TAMs, such as CD163, CD206, and Siglecs, to achieve selective endocytosis [230, 231]. By utilizing these receptors, advanced nanovesicles can precisely deliver informational biomolecules to rewire mechanosensing networks. For example, CRISPR/Cas9-based delivery platforms have been engineered to target PI3Kγ—a critical relay node for integrin α4 and TAM migration. Co-delivery of Cas9 complexes targeting Pik3cg via specific nanovesicles simultaneously blocks the PI3Kγ mechanical signaling hub, significantly reducing the proportion of immunosuppressive TAMs and rendering cold tumors highly responsive to PD-1 blockade [232–234].

Second, utilizing mechanically programmable hydrogels and scaffolds offers a transformative approach to bypass systemic RES clearance entirely. Instead of intravenous injection, these implantable or injectable systems act as localized drug reservoirs directly within the tumor cavity or post-operative resection bed. By spatiotemporally controlling the release of mechanotransduction inhibitors (e.g., FAK inhibitors or specific integrin antagonists), these biomaterials overcome spatial barriers without the risk of off-target systemic mechanosensing blockade [235–237]. Moreover, the 3D architecture and matrix stiffness of these scaffolds can be rationally designed to physically mimic or actively disrupt the local fibrotic niche, achieving a localized active softening of the TME that dismantles the integrin-TAM structural closure at its source.

Multi target combination therapy

Within the feedback loop architecture of the integrin-TAM-microenvironment axis, multiple programmed relay nodes function as amplifiers that sustain and escalate both immune and biomechanical loops. Inhibition of FAK concurrently suppresses phosphorylation of STAT1, STAT3, and STAT6 during TAM polarization, effectively blocking amplification and maintenance of the immunosuppressive program [69, 77, 211]. A systemic strategy should therefore prioritize these relay amplifiers to achieve efficient shutdown of the self-reinforcing loops. Integrating TAM-targeted interventions with systemic modalities—including ICIs, anti-angiogenic therapies, and radiochemotherapy—holds immense promise for simultaneously overcoming immune tolerance and reshaping the mechanical microenvironment.

Single-target interventions rarely break the programmed steady state of the integrin-TAM loop. Combination approaches show stronger translational promise. Co-targeting β3 and PPARγ synchronously remodels TAM behavior at phenotypic and metabolic levels, since β3 drives M2 programming by inducing PPARγ [77, 91]. Dual inhibition of FAK and c-Myc markedly restrains ovarian tumor growth and, via the PI3K-AKT pathway, interrupts multi-axis signal coupling [238]. Simvastatin simultaneously downregulates the β3-FAK pathway and activates the LXR/ABCA1 axis, inducing TAMs repolarization and reversing epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-associated resistance [153, 239]. These coordinated strikes deliver bidirectional control across program and function, rebuilding an integrated network of structure and activity.

Among these paradigms, the integration of integrin-TAM axis-targeting strategies with ICIs is supported by the most substantial translational evidence. TAM-derived vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), MMPs, and chemokines sustain dense ECM deposition and restricted immune cell infiltration, collectively reinforcing a mechanically hostile niche that confers resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. On this basis, combining integrin/FAK-targeted interventions with ICIs generates profound synergistic effects. Mechanistically, targeting relay nodes like PI3Kγ—which operates downstream to promote integrin α4 expression—reprograms TAMs toward an M1-like phenotype. In the phase II MARIO-3 trial, the PI3Kγ inhibitor eganelisib combined with an anti–PD-L1 antibody and nab-paclitaxel not only induced TAM reprogramming but was also accompanied by the downregulation of ECM-related transcriptional signatures and the reorganization of vascular–immune gene programs [240].

Crucially, however, analyzing the potential risks of such combinatorial approaches is essential to guide safe clinical translation. Because mechanotransduction pathways are fundamental for vascular integrity, broad-spectrum mechanical inhibition combined with immune hyperactivation may lead to severe tissue damage, impaired wound healing, or hemorrhage [7, 241]. Furthermore, profound systemic disruption of the myeloid compartment concurrently with checkpoint blockade could paradoxically trigger severe immune-related adverse events (irAEs) or induce hyperprogressive disease (HPD) [242, 243]. Establishing precision dosing windows is mandatory to safely harness this synergy.

Beyond immune checkpoints, the combination of integrin-TAM axis-targeted strategies with anti-angiogenic therapies focuses on modulating the mechanical state of the TME at the interface of vasculature, ECM, and immune cells. Prolonged angiogenesis inhibition readily induces secondary hypoxia, which actively drives the accumulation of M2-like TAMs and triggers integrin-mediated pro-fibrotic programs, accelerating ECM deposition and ultimately reinforcing tissue stiffness [244]. Combining anti-VEGF agents with integrin β3 or FAK inhibitors can attenuate pro-fibrotic interactions between M2-like TAMs and CAFs, improving vascular morphology while simultaneously decreasing collagen crosslinking and relieving elevated interstitial pressure at its source [233, 245, 246].

Parallel to vascular interventions, combining TAM mechanomodulation with radiochemotherapy reflects a necessary evolution in overcoming physically resistant tumors. Radiotherapy severely alters tumor-region matrix architecture through endothelial injury and parenchymal inflammation [247]. During this process, TAMs participate in post-irradiation tissue repair, often shifting toward a pro-fibrotic M2-like phenotype that exacerbates ECM remodeling [248, 249]. Integrating integrin/FAK inhibitors into radiotherapy regimens restrains excessive ECM crosslinking and integrin-driven fibrosis during early radiation-induced tissue remodeling. This combination prevents the sustained loss of stromal compliance and restricted drug diffusion, thereby maintaining the structural foundation necessary for robust immunogenic cell death (ICD)-driven T-cell responses (Table 2).

Ultimately, next-generation delivery systems extend the spatiotemporal reach of these multi-target strategies. iRGD-modified nanoparticles co-deliver the CXCR4 antagonist AMD3100 and the PD-L1 inhibitor BMS-1, simultaneously disrupting the CAF-TAM adhesion axis and immunosuppressive signaling, and enhancing CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and effector function [224, 250, 251]. This spatially coordinated, dual-node design operationalizes a three-pronged loop-breaking strategy that severs the signaling hub, dismantles structural anchoring, and curtails immunosuppressive output (Fig. 6).

A critical synthesis of the aforementioned translational efforts reveals that the limited efficacy of prior interventions stems from several fundamental and interconnected bottlenecks. The extreme redundancy of integrin-ECM interactions frequently allows isolated receptor blockade to be rapidly bypassed by compensatory mechanosensors or alternative signaling pathways, thereby diluting the therapeutic impact. Simultaneously, the dense, integrin-crosslinked fibrotic stroma functions as a formidable physical barrier that imposes severe delivery constraints, restricting the penetration and distribution of therapeutic agents within the tumor core. Furthermore, the broad-spectrum inhibition of mechanotransduction carries significant toxicity-related risks, including impaired vascular integrity and the potential to exacerbate immune-related adverse events. Finally, the lack of precise patient stratification based on biomechanical biomarkers has historically hindered the identification of specific patient subgroups most likely to benefit from these targeted approaches. Overcoming these clinical barriers necessitates a paradigm shift toward multi-target strategies and precision delivery systems that are strictly guided by the spatial and mechanical heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment.

Outlook and discussion: Multidimensional intervention implications for biomechanics-immune regulation

Conceptual implications and current limitations of the biomechanical–immune loop

Based on the integration of multidimensional findings, this review proposes the integrin–TAM axis as a central regulatory node within a biomechanical–immune feedback system. It is important to reiterate, however, that this framework is best viewed as a working and field-synthesizing model rather than a fully established, unified mechanism. While the proposed model provides a conceptual roadmap for understanding the non-linear, programmed interactions within the TME, its universal applicability across diverse tumor types remains to be strictly validated through direct, TAM-specific biophysical characterization and single-cell mechanobiological evidence. While integrins are traditionally studied for cell adhesion and mechanotransduction, and TAMs for immune regulation, their shared biomechanical–immune duality remains underexplored. Many studies rely on linear stimulus–signal–response paradigms to explain immune imbalance and biomechanical abnormalities; however, tissue structure, mechanical heterogeneity, and immune responses are deeply nested and interactive. The integrin–TAM regulatory axis provides a conceptual framework for understanding this nonlinear, programmed interaction network.

We acknowledge that this theoretical framework remains in an early exploratory stage. Systematic and direct characterization of the interactions among integrin subtypes, TAMs, and the biomechanical–immune regulatory cycle is still lacking. It also remains necessary to determine whether the significance of this axis is conserved across different tumor types—for example, between solid tumors and hematologic malignancies, or between highly immunogenic and highly immunosuppressive tumors. Investigating tumor-type-specific differences may reveal how biomechanical and immune features drive distinct patterns of tumor progression.

Emerging analytical technologies to probe integrin–TAM mechanotransduction

Elucidating integrin–TAM mechanotransduction and experimentally validating the proposed biomechanical–immune feedback loop model requires analytical tools capable of measuring forces across molecular, cellular, and tissue scales. Recent advances in mechanobiological and spatially resolved technologies now allow quantitative interrogation of integrin-dependent mechanics and their biochemical consequences.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) has emerged as a central platform in mechanobiology, enabling quantitative mapping of morphology and mechanical parameters—including stiffness, adhesion, and viscoelasticity—from subcellular structures to tissue regions [252, 253]. AFM-based nanoindentation can resolve biomechanical heterogeneity within the ECM and tumor niches [253, 254]. In the context of integrin–TAM interactions, AFM provides a direct approach to evaluate how integrin inhibition or TAM reprogramming reshapes tumor stiffness. Coupling AFM with fluorescence or signaling readouts further enables correlation of local mechanical states with integrin–FAK–YAP activation and macrophage polarization markers [255, 256].

Traction force microscopy (TFM) and related approaches quantify the forces that cells exert on deformable substrates through integrins and focal adhesions [257, 258]. Applying 2D or 3D TFM to tumor–TAM co-culture systems or organoid models enables spatiotemporal mapping of integrin-dependent traction forces. Such measurements can directly test whether reciprocal force transmission sustains the predicted mechanical feedback loop, identifying cell populations whose invasive behavior depends on elevated traction forces.

At the molecular scale, emerging integrin tension probes enable direct measurement of piconewton-scale forces at individual receptor–ligand bonds. Molecular tension fluorescence microscopy (MTFM) and DNA-based tension sensors convert integrin-mediated forces into optical signals with high spatial resolution [258]. Recent developments, including CRISPR-Cas12a–amplified tension gauge tethers(nanoscale biophysical tools that convert mechanical forces into readable DNA signals), further enable high-throughput quantification of integrin force dynamics across large cell populations [259–261], facilitating high-content screening for agents that selectively disrupt pathogenic force regimes.

Beyond mechanical readouts, multimodal approaches integrating mechanical and biochemical measurements provide new insight into downstream consequences of force signaling. AFM combined with Raman spectroscopy enables simultaneous acquisition of mechanical and chemical information at the single-cell level [255]. Extending these analyses with spatially resolved metabolomics can further reveal force-dependent metabolic niches within tumors [262]. Such approaches may identify localized metabolic adaptations driven by integrin signaling in TAM-enriched regions, highlighting mechanobiology-defined vulnerabilities that could be exploited therapeutically.

Future translational directions and concluding remarks

Despite encouraging preclinical findings, targeting this axis remains constrained by spatial heterogeneity, structural immune suppression, and persistent feedback-driven inhibition. The immune and mechanical microenvironments of tumors are highly complex, and traditional single-disciplinary approaches cannot provide the depth required.

We propose three research priorities with translational significance: first, to use spatial transcriptomics and single-cell mechanobiology to construct a multimodal expression map of integrin–TAM; second, to combine AI-assisted modeling with dynamic pathological imaging to track the formation of integrin-TAM structural loops and identify clinically actionable biomarkers; and third, to use advances in nanodelivery and carrier engineering to develop loop-based, multi-breakpoint intervention systems that target structure, signaling, and function simultaneously.

In summary, the complexity of the biomechanical–immune system in the TME requires a shift from linear thinking to cross-network approaches. With rapid development in materials science, bioengineering, and translational medical engineering, this interdisciplinary perspective may become a key pathway for the next generation of breakthroughs in tumor immunotherapy.

Conclusion

This study, drawing on extensive existing evidence, systematically introduces the concept of the integrin-TAM immune-regulatory axis and, based on this framework, constructs a biomechanical-immune double feedback loop model integrating structure, signaling and function. Importantly, we reiterate that at the current stage of experimental validation, this framework is best viewed as a working and field-synthesizing model rather than a fully established unified mechanism. Within this model, integrins act as precise mechanosensors and TAMs serve as functional regulatory hubs, together forming a coordinated programmatic cascade that culminates in immune suppression. Dismantling this immune-suppressive feedback loop and implementing multidimensional interventions along the structure-signal-function axis will open new avenues for tumor immunotherapy.
