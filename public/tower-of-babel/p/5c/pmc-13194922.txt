Adolescent envy-like social comparison stress induces HPA axis hypoactivity and anxiety in female mice: implications for somatic symptom disorder
Authors: Hiroshi Ueno, Yoshihiro Tanaka, Eriko Kitano, Yu Takahashi, Sachiko Mori, Shinji Murakami, Kenta Wani, Yosuke Matsumoto, Motoi Okamoto, Takeshi Ishihara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdolescence is a critical developmental window marked by heightened neuroplasticity and maturation of the stress response system, conferring vulnerability to psychosocial stress. Chronic stress during this period increases the risk of anxiety and depressive disorders and may contribute to somatic symptom disorder (SSD), which is characterized by medically unexplained physical complaints and shows a striking female predominance. The impact of envy-like stress arising from social comparison, a pervasive psychosocial factor in modern society, remains poorly understood. Male and female C57BL/6N mice were exposed to chronic envy-like stress from postnatal day (P)21 to P52 by housing them adjacent to enriched cages, while control mice were housed without such exposure. From P52 onward, mice underwent behavioral testing of anxiety-like behavior, exploratory activity, social interaction, spatial working memory, motor coordination, nociception, and depression-like behavior. Serum corticosterone and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) concentrations were measured to assess hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis function. Envy-like stress induced sex-specific phenotypes. Male mice exhibited hyperactivity, reduced social interaction, and impaired spatial working memory. In contrast, female mice displayed robust increases in anxiety-like behavior, impaired motor coordination, and significant reductions in basal corticosterone and ACTH levels. Chronic envy-like stress during adolescence elicits distinct, sex-dependent behavioral and endocrine alterations. The phenotype observed in female mice—characterized by heightened anxiety and lower basal HPA axis hormone levels—shares some biological features with clinical observations in SSD. Introduction

Adolescence is a unique developmental period characterized by the reorganization of neural circuits and maturation of the endocrine system1. As a critical phase in an individual’s development, adolescence is one of the most vulnerable periods for psychosocial stress. During this stage, high neuroplasticity and the ongoing maturation of stress-response systems, including the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, render individuals susceptible to external environmental factors and social stress2,3. Stress exposure during adolescence is a major risk factor for the onset of anxiety disorders and depression in adulthood3. Furthermore, Somatic Symptom Disorder (SSD), characterized by medically unexplained physical complaints, has been recognized as a disorder closely associated with stress4. SSD is reported to be particularly prevalent in women, and understanding its pathophysiology and establishing appropriate animal models remain clinically important challenges5.

One of the primary sources of psychological stress in human society is "social comparison," the process of comparing oneself to others. In particular, "upward social comparison," which involves continuously comparing oneself to others perceived as better off, can evoke envy and inferiority and is associated with negative emotions such as depression, anger, and anxiety6. With the recent expansion of social media use, the impact of social comparison and envy on mental health has been emphasized, and these factors are gaining attention as new risk factors for stress-related disorders6,7. Recently, clinical researchers have begun to highlight the need to understand the extent and nature of the relationship between envy and mental health, as well as the urgent necessity to develop interventions specifically designed to mitigate envy and its effects8. However, studies that replicate envy and social comparison in animal models to analyze their effects are extremely limited.

To replicate persistent psychological stress without physical contact, such as envy, in animals, we previously established a novel envy-like stress model, defined here as a paradigm of social comparison-based stress in which subjects unilaterally observe conspecifics housed in an enriched environment9. We emphasize that this term describes the experimental condition of environmental disparity rather than the subjective affective state of the animals. However, our previous research has primarily targeted adult mice, and the effects during the developmental period of adolescence have not been clarified. Additionally, analyses considering sex differences have been limited, and the effects on female mice have not been sufficiently examined. Although existing stress models (e.g., social defeat stress, restraint stress, chronic variable stress) are useful, they do not directly reflect higher-order social stressors like envy and social comparison. While conventional models can capture aspects of anxiety-like and depressive-like behaviors, mimicking the psychological distress arising from comparison with others, as seen clinically, has been difficult. Considering the pronounced sex difference in the prevalence of SSD, clarifying sex differences in this stress response is essential for a comprehensive understanding of its pathophysiology.

In the present study, we employ a paradigm of chronic social comparison-based stress (referred to here as envy-like stress) to model the psychological impact of environmental disparity. This operational definition specifically focuses on the distress arising from the persistent observation of an enriched environment that remains inaccessible to the subject. While we acknowledge that subjective affective states such as envy cannot be directly quantified in rodents, this terminology reflects the paradigm’s conceptual relevance to human psychosocial stressors. By distinguishing this condition from simple sensory or social stimulation, we aim to explore how the perception of relative advantage in others functions as a potent psychogenic stressor, particularly during the sensitive period of adolescence.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to comprehensively evaluate the effects of chronic social comparison-based stress (referred to here as envy-like stress) during adolescence on subsequent behavioral characteristics and endocrine responses in both male and female C57BL/6N mice. This operational definition focuses on the psychological impact of environmental disparity rather than the assessment of subjective emotional states. Specifically, we aimed to elucidate sex-specific behavioral and physiological changes by analyzing anxiety-like behavior, exploratory behavior, social behavior, working memory, and depressive-like behavior, in addition to measuring serum corticosterone and ACTH concentrations. We sought to investigate the potential of this adolescent envy-like stress paradigm to contribute to the development of a novel animal model for stress-related disorders and Somatic Symptom Disorder.

Experimental procedures

Ethics statements

All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of Kawasaki Medical School. The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kawasaki Medical School (Approval No. 24–026). All efforts were made to minimize the number of animals used and to alleviate any pain or discomfort. The present study also complied with relevant guidelines and regulations and adhered to ARRIVE guidelines (https://www.nc3rs.org.uk/arrive-guidelines) and the U.S. National Institutes of Health’s (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 80–23, revised in 1996).

Animals

Three-week-old male and female C57BL/6N mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory Japan (Tokyo, Japan). Mice were housed in groups of five per cage in transparent plastic mesh cages (220 × 340 × 150 mm) with a nonwoven fabric filter cap. Nesting material, food (MF-R, Oriental Yeast Co., Tokyo, Japan), and water were provided ad libitum. Animals were maintained at 23–26 °C under a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 08:00, lights off at 20:00). Because this study aimed to compare behavioral differences between sexes, both male and female mice were used, and they were housed in separate cages located in separate enclosures. Cages were cleaned once per week. All behavioral experiments were conducted in a room separate from the animal facility to avoid environmental influences such as odor, sound, and location. Within the vivarium, cages for all experimental groups were placed on racks at the same height to minimize positional variation, and all groups were tested simultaneously within each experiment rather than in separate cohorts.

Rearing in an envy-like environment during postnatal development

C57BL/6N mice were reared in a chronic envy-like stress environment for four weeks during postnatal development. After this period, mice underwent behavioral testing to examine the effects of envy-like stress on behavior (Fig. 1A). At postnatal day (P)21, mice were randomly assigned to one of three groups (control, envy-like stress, or enrichment) and housed in adjacent cages (randomization performed using www.randomizer.org). Each experimental group consisted of 10 mice. Mice were group-housed, with five animals per cage, under standard laboratory conditions. Sample size is consistent with previous similar studies. To habituate all animals to the enrichment apparatus, objects such as tubes, igloos, and toys were placed in the cages of all groups for three days. Thereafter, enrichment apparatuses were kept only in the cages of the enrichment group (Fig. 1B,C). For the enriched cages, environmental enrichment objects such as tubes, igloos, and small toys (smaller than the body size of adult mice) were provided. To minimize habituation, enrichment items were randomly changed every three days throughout the experimental period. Mice in the envy-like stress group were housed in cages adjacent to enriched cages containing sex- and age-matched conspecific mice, allowing them to observe conspecifics interacting with the enrichment apparatus. All adjacent cage pairs consisted of mice of the same sex and similar age, ensuring that visual and social exposure occurred between developmentally comparable animals. Control group mice were housed in transparent cages adjacent to demonstrator cages containing conspecifics maintained under standard laboratory conditions (without environmental enrichment objects). This configuration ensured that control mice were exposed to the same baseline of visual and social cues as the envy-like stress group, thereby isolating environmental disparity as the primary experimental variable. These housing conditions were maintained for four weeks, after which behavioral testing was conducted (Fig. 1A).

Behavioural tests

All behavioral tests were conducted during the light phase of the light–dark cycle (between 10:00 and 17:00). For each behavioral paradigm, testing was performed within a relatively narrow and consistent time window on a given day, rather than being spread across the entire time range, to minimize potential circadian variability. Differences in testing times across days reflected practical experimental scheduling rather than within-test variability. The order in which individual mice were tested was randomized across experimental groups to reduce potential order effects. To minimize potential confounds from olfactory cues, all apparatuses and objects were thoroughly cleaned with 70% ethanol and hypochlorous acid solution after each use. Behavioral assessments were performed using experimentally naïve mice (i.e., mice not previously exposed to any behavioral tests). To reduce carryover effects, tasks were conducted in a predetermined sequence with an inter-test interval of at least 24 h. While conventional batteries prioritize low-stress exploratory tests first, we positioned the hot plate and grip strength tests early to ensure that the assessment of basic physical and nociceptive functions remained unaffected by the cumulative stress of subsequent psychological paradigms. We acknowledge that early exposure to thermal stimuli may influence later behavioral readouts, and thus all groups were tested in the same rigid sequence to maintain experimental consistency. Testing was initiated from postnatal day (P)52 onward and completed over consecutive days as follows: grip strength test, hot plate test, cotton swab biting test, rotarod test, light/dark transition test, open field test, social interaction test, Y-maze test, tail suspension test, and finally the Porsolt forced swim test. Each mouse underwent one behavioral test per day. All behavioral tests were conducted during the light phase between 10:00 and 17:00, and testing times were counterbalanced across experimental groups to minimize potential time-of-day effects. All behavioral assessments were carried out by trained observers who were blinded to the experimental conditions.

Grip-strength test

Neuromuscular strength was assessed using a grip-strength meter (O’Hara & Co., Tokyo, Japan). Each mouse was gently lifted by the tail and allowed to grasp the wire mesh of the dynamometer with its forepaws. The mouse was then steadily pulled backward in a horizontal plane until it released its grip. The peak force (centinewtons, cN) was automatically recorded by the apparatus. Each mouse was tested in five consecutive trials, and the mean value was used for analysis.

Hotplate test

The hot plate test was used to assess nociceptive responses. Mice were placed on a heated plate maintained at 55.0 ± 0.3 °C, and the latency to the first paw response (e.g., hind paw licking, shaking, or trembling) was recorded. To prevent tissue damage, a cut-off time of 30 s was applied. Each mouse was tested once, and the latency was used for analysis.

Cotton bud biting test

Defensive and irritability-related behavior was assessed using the cotton swab biting test. This test evaluates biting responses toward a mildly aversive, non-social stimulus and is commonly used as an index of stress-induced irritability or impulsive defensive reactivity, rather than aggression toward conspecifics. Mice were gently grasped by the experimenter and presented with a sterile cotton swab near the face. Each mouse was tested 10 times, and the total number of biting responses was recorded.

Rotarod test

Motor coordination and balance were assessed using an accelerating rotarod apparatus (RTR-M5, Melquest, Toyama, Japan). Mice were placed on a rotating drum (3.9 cm in diameter), and the latency to fall was automatically recorded by the device. The rotation speed accelerated from 4 to 40 rpm over a 5-min period. Each mouse underwent three trials, with an intertrial interval of 20 min. All mice were tested without prior training.

Light/dark transition test

Anxiety-like behavior was assessed using the light/dark transition test. The apparatus consisted of an acrylic cage (22 × 44 × 40 cm) divided into two compartments connected by a small opening (5 × 5 cm). One chamber had white acrylic walls and was brightly illuminated (200 lx) by an overhead light, whereas the other chamber had black acrylic walls and was dimly lit (50 lx). Both chambers had white acrylic floors. At the start of the test, each mouse was placed in the dark chamber and allowed to move freely between the two chambers for 6 min. The parameters measured were distance traveled (m), number of entries into the light chamber, time spent in the light chamber (s), and latency to the first entry into the light chamber (s). Behavioral data were analyzed using ANY-maze software (Stoelting Co., IL, USA).

Open field test

Exploratory behavior, anxiety-like behavior, and general locomotor activity were assessed using the open field test. The apparatus consisted of a square arena (45 × 45 × 40 cm) enclosed by white acrylic walls. Each mouse was placed in the center of the arena at the start of the test. The central area was defined as a 20 × 20 cm zone in the middle of the arena. The chamber was illuminated at 100 lx, and behavior was recorded for 20 min. The parameters measured were total distance traveled (m), number of entries into the central area, and time spent in the central area (s). Data were analyzed using ANY-maze software.

Social interaction test in a novel environment

Social behavior was assessed using a social interaction test conducted in a novel environment. The apparatus consisted of a square white acrylic box (45 × 45 × 40 cm) illuminated at 100 lx. Two unfamiliar mice of the same sex originating from different home cages but maintained under the same housing condition (control–control or envy-like stress–envy-like stress) were simultaneously placed in opposite corners of the arena. This design was used to minimize potential confounding effects arising from interactions between animals with different housing histories. Each pair was allowed to interact freely for 10 min. The parameters measured were total distance traveled (m), number of contacts, total contact time (s), and mean duration per contact (s). Behavioral data were analyzed using SMART software, version 3.0 (PanLab, Harvard Apparatus, Spain).

Y-maze test

Spatial working memory was assessed using the Y-maze test. The apparatus consisted of three identical white acrylic arms (arm length: 40 cm; lower arm width: 3 cm; upper arm width: 10 cm; wall height: 12 cm) arranged at 120° angles to each other. Each mouse was placed in the center of the maze and allowed to explore freely for 6 min. Visual cues were positioned around the periphery of the testing room and remained visible throughout the session. Mice were tested without prior exposure or habituation to the maze. The parameters recorded and analyzed using ANY-maze software were total distance traveled (m), number of arm entries, number of alternations, and percentage of spontaneous alternations. A spontaneous alternation was defined as consecutive entries into all three arms without repetition.

Tail-suspension test

Depressive-like behavior was assessed using the tail suspension test. Mice were suspended by the tail in a white plastic chamber 60 cm above the floor, secured with adhesive tape placed < 1 cm from the tip of the tail. Behavior was recorded for 6 min using a video camera, and immobility time (s) was quantified. Immobility was defined as the absence of active movement for ≥ 1 s, with only passive swinging permitted. Each mouse was tested once. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using ANY-maze software.

Porsolt forced-swim test

Depression-like behavior was evaluated using the forced swim test. Immobility was defined as the absence of active escape-directed movements for a duration of at least 1 s, during which the mouse remained floating with only minimal movements necessary to keep its head above water. The percentage of total immobility time during the test session was calculated and used for statistical analysis. The apparatus consisted of a Plexiglas cylinder (20 cm in height × 10 cm in diameter) filled with water at 23 °C to a depth of 7.5 cm. Mice were placed in the cylinder for 6 min, and their behavior was recorded. Each mouse was tested once. As with the tail suspension test, immobility time was assessed using ANY-maze software.

Corticosterone and adrenocorticotropic hormone measurements

Blood samples for corticosterone and ACTH measurements were collected on the day following completion of the entire battery of behavioral tests, immediately prior to euthanasia, and not after any specific behavioral task. Sampling was performed during the light phase within a narrow and consistent time window to minimize circadian variation in hormone levels. Thus, the obtained values are considered to reflect basal endocrine status rather than acute stress-induced hormonal responses. Following deep anesthesia with pentobarbital sodium (120 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection), mice were euthanized by cardiac puncture. The blood samples were centrifuged at 0.8 × g for 10 min, and the resulting serum was collected and stored at –80 °C until analysis. Serum corticosterone concentrations were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (catalog no. K014-H5, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Serum ACTH concentrations were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (catalog no. ab263880, Abcam, Cambridge, UK).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Prior to group comparisons, the normality of all data sets was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA or Student’s t-test, as appropriate. Results are presented as box plots. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Impact of adolescent envy-like stress on physical and basic sensorimotor functions

To evaluate the long-term effects of adolescent envy-like stress on general development and motor function, we assessed physical characteristics and sensorimotor performance in adulthood. While body weight at postnatal day (P) 52 and the percentage of weight gain from P21 to P52 remained comparable between groups for both sexes (p > 0.05; Fig. 2A, A‘, B, B’; Table 1), several sex-specific alterations emerged in sensorimotor tasks. Specifically, female mice in the envy-like stress group exhibited significantly higher forelimb grip strength (t(18) = 2.288, p = 0.0382; Fig. 2C‘) and a marked impairment in motor coordination on the rotarod, as evidenced by a significantly shorter latency to fall (F(1,18) = 5.761, p = 0.0274; Fig. 2F‘). In contrast, male mice showed no significant changes in these motor parameters (p > 0.05; Fig. 2C, F) but displayed a significantly shorter latency to respond in the hot plate test, suggesting stress-induced thermal hyperalgesia (t(18) = 2.744, p = 0.0151; Fig. 2D). No significant differences were observed in irritability as measured by the cotton bud biting test for either sex (p > 0.05; Fig. 2E,E‘).

Anxiety-like and exploratory behavior in the light/dark transition and open field tests

The impact of adolescent stress on adult anxiety was first assessed using the light/dark transition test. Male mice showed no significant group differences across any parameters (p > 0.05; Fig. 3A-D). Conversely, stressed females displayed robust anxiety-like behavior, characterized by significantly shorter distances traveled (t(18) = 2.211, p = 0.0419; Fig. 3A‘), reduced time spent in the light chamber (t(18) = 2.615, p = 0.0175; Fig. 3C‘), and a prolonged latency to first entry into the light area (t(18) = 2.585, p = 0.0207; Fig. 3D‘).

Further examination in the open field test revealed a distinct male phenotype. Stressed males exhibited hyperactivity and altered risk assessment, traveling longer total distances (t(18) = 2.211, p = 0.0410; Fig. 3E) and spending more time in the central area compared to controls (t(18) = 2.781, p = 0.0134; Fig. 3G). In female mice, no significant group differences were detected in locomotor activity or central area exploration in this paradigm (p > 0.05; Fig. 3E‘-G’).

In the social interaction test, male mice exposed to envy-like stress exhibited a significant reduction in total contact time with unfamiliar conspecifics (t(18) = 2.954, p = 0.0213; Fig. 4C), suggesting diminished social motivation or avoidance. Female social behavior, however, remained unaffected by the stress paradigm (p > 0.05; Fig. 4A‘-D’).

Assessments of depression-like behavior

Depression-like behavior was evaluated using the tail suspension and Porsolt forced swim tests. In contrast to the anxiety-related findings, adolescent envy-like stress did not significantly alter immobility time in either the tail suspension test or the forced swim test for male or female mice (all p > 0.38; Fig. 6A, B, A‘, B’; Table 1).

Basal serum corticosterone and ACTH levels

To investigate the endocrine correlates of these behavioral changes, we measured basal HPA axis hormone levels. In male mice, no significant group differences were found in serum corticosterone or ACTH levels (p > 0.22; Fig. 7A, B). However, female mice in the envy-like stress group exhibited a significant suppression of the basal HPA axis, showing lower concentrations of both serum corticosterone (t(18) = 2.532, p = 0.0222; Fig. 7A‘) and ACTH (t(18) = 2.757, p = 0.0140; Fig. 7B‘). These findings demonstrate that chronic envy-like stress elicits a female-specific reduction in basal endocrine output.

Discussion

In the present study, chronic envy-like stress during adolescence induced clear sex-dependent behavioral and neuroendocrine alterations. Male mice exhibited changes predominantly in locomotor activity, social interaction, and spatial working memory, whereas female mice showed enhanced anxiety-like behavior accompanied by reduced motor coordination and marked decreases in serum corticosterone and ACTH levels. Together, these findings suggest that adolescent envy-like stress engages partially distinct behavioral and physiological response profiles in males and females.

While we employ the term envy-like stress to describe this paradigm, it is important to distinguish this experimental model from the subjective human experience of envy. As suggested, this condition could also be characterized as “enrichment witnessing” or "social comparison-based stress". Our results indicate that the observed phenotype is a specific response to witnessing an environmental advantage in others, highlighting its utility as a model for the psychosocial stressors that contribute to Somatic Symptom Disorder (SSD).

The most noteworthy finding of this study is the significant reduction in basal serum corticosterone and ACTH concentrations in female mice. These results suggest a hypoactivity of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis10. The HPA axis is a critical part of the biological response to stress, promoting adaptation to threatening or traumatic exposure11. Exposure to stress triggers a cascade of events in HPA activity, culminating in the release of the glucocorticoid hormone cortisol. The observed decreases in serum corticosterone and ACTH may reflect a reduced reactivity of the HPA axis, which is consistent with the “hyporesponsiveness” reported in individuals exposed to chronic stress. This HPA axis hypoactivity aligns with biological features repeatedly reported in patients with specific stress-related disorders such as Somatic Symptom Disorder (SSD), Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD), and chronic fatigue syndrome12–14. The reduction was particularly pronounced in female mice, a finding that supports a pathophysiological link with SSD and other stress-related disorders that are more prevalent in women. Because hormone measurements were obtained during the light phase under resting conditions following completion of behavioral testing, the reduced corticosterone and ACTH levels observed in female mice are most consistent with chronic suppression or hypoactivity of HPA axis regulation, rather than circadian phase shifts or blunted responses to an acute stressor. While this suppression of basal hormone levels aligns with endocrine patterns observed in some stress-related disorders, it remains to be determined whether this reflects true HPA axis hypoactivity, enhanced negative feedback, or a context-dependent adaptation. Since our measurements were limited to a single basal time point, future studies assessing HPA axis reactivity to acute stress or circadian fluctuations are necessary to clarify the functional state of the stress response system.

In females, increased forelimb grip and shortened fall latency in the rotarod test indicate impairments in strength and motor coordination. Limb strength in laboratory mice can be assessed with commercial grip meters15, and motor coordination is conventionally evaluated by the accelerating rotarod test16,17. In this assay, animals must maintain an upright posture and move forward to avoid falling,it is sensitive, objective, and quantifiable. Rodents with motor impairment fall earlier than neurologically intact rodents18. The decline in rotarod performance in females exposed to envy-like stress suggests that chronic psychosocial stress can extend to bodily functions. This overlaps with the non-specific bodily complaints typical of SSD (e.g., fatigue and weakness)19,20, supporting the coupling of bodily symptoms with psychological stress. Conversely, the unexpected finding of higher grip strength than controls may reflect increased muscle tension under sustained psychological stress, pointing to a complex relationship between psychological state and bodily responses. Although social or chronic stress is often considered to exert limited effects on motor function, our results imply that envy-like stress can generalize to physical performance—an observation of interest in the context of the non-specific somatic complaints (e.g., perceived weakness, fatigue) often reported in SSD.

Analgesia was assessed with the classic hot plate assay, which captures a distinct modality of thermal nociception21. The shortened latency observed in male mice exposed to envy-like stress suggests stress-induced hyperalgesia. Whereas stress-induced analgesia has often been reported in traditional stress models22,23, our paradigm may reflect a different mechanism. Hyperalgesia under chronic envy-like stress may relate to human bodily symptoms and the pathophysiology of fibromyalgia, suggesting that adolescent envy-like stress can influence sensory processing and pain-related behavior.

The light/dark transition test is a standard assay for anxiety24,25. In females exposed to envy-like stress during adolescence, decreased distance traveled, reduced time in the light compartment, and prolonged latency to first light entry indicate heightened anxiety-like behavior. In the open field, no significant between-group differences were detected in females. Prior reports indicate that the open field, elevated plus maze, and light/dark box may not capture identical facets of anxiety-like behavior26. In the open-field test, male mice exposed to envy-like stress showed increased locomotor activity and increased time spent in the center area. Although increased center exploration is often interpreted as reduced anxiety, the concomitant hyperactivity and reduced social contact time observed in the social interaction test suggest that this pattern may alternatively reflect stress-induced behavioral disinhibition, altered risk assessment, or an active coping style rather than a simple anxiolytic effect. Thus, the male phenotype is better conceptualized as a shift in behavioral strategy characterized by heightened activity and altered social engagement, while female mice displayed a distinct profile marked by increased anxiety-like behavior and reduced HPA-axis output.

In the Y-maze, males exposed to envy-like stress exhibited reduced spontaneous alternation rates, suggesting impaired working memory, whereas females showed a decrease in total arm entries. Social defeat and isolation stress are known to impair working memory27,28, and our results extend this literature by demonstrating that an indirect psychosocial experience—envy-like stress—can also affect cognitive function. Memory, anxiety, and skilled motor performance are interrelated, and these links may exist under physiological conditions as well. For example, trait anxiety under performance pressure can alter motor outcomes (Wankel, 2014)29, musicians with state anxiety may produce disorganized finger movements30, and higher anxiety can disrupt movement control and smoothness31. Anxiety-like behavior has been shown to influence spatial memory performance in mice32. Thus, increased anxiety induced by the envy-like environment may have contributed to the decline in Y-maze performance.

In the social interaction test conducted in a novel environment—where pairs of unfamiliar mice freely interact in a chamber33—male mice exposed to envy-like stress exhibited shortened contact time, whereas females showed no prominent change. Reduced social contact time suggests diminished motivation for social interaction or social avoidance. Social anxiety, like PTSD, anxiety disorders, and depression, has environmental underpinnings and biological correlates,socially anxious individuals can show lower basal glucocorticoid levels and blunted reactivity later in life34. The increased total distance and center time in the male open field, often interpreted as anxiolytic, may—considering concomitant hyperactivity—reflect heightened impulsivity or disinhibition rather than reduced anxiety, potentially representing a behavioral coping strategy akin to a “fight-or-flight” response. Together, these findings suggest that envy-like stress can suppress social engagement in males, whereas in females its impact may be expressed primarily as anxiety and bodily complaints. Such sex differences likely reflect divergent behavioral phenotypes and biological pathways of stress reactivity35,36. Specifically, the female HPA axis undergoes distinct maturation processes during adolescence compared to males, characterized by a more rapid and prolonged response to certain stressors. These sex-specific developmental trajectories may render females more sensitive to the psychosocial frustration induced by our envy-like stress paradigm.

No prominent changes were observed in the tail suspension or forced swim tests in either male or female mice exposed to envy-like stress during adolescence. These assays are widely used to index depression-like behavior. Repeated social defeat stress induces robust depression-like phenotypes characterized by anhedonia, anxiety, and social avoidance37,38, and chronic restraint stress can also increase depression-like behavior39. In contrast, our adolescent envy-like environment did not alter depression-like behavior, which is a novel observation; the exact reason remains unclear. Unlike many established paradigms that rely on physical stressors, the envy-like environment constitutes a primarily psychological stressor. Our results indicate that the envy-like rearing condition is not a stressor that increases depression-like behavior in mice.

An important consideration is whether the observed alterations result from envy or from general sensory and social stimulation provided by the enriched cages. If the stimuli from the adjacent cages served merely as surrogate enrichment, one would expect a reduction in anxiety-like behaviors. However, the female-specific increase in anxiety and the suppression of the basal HPA axis suggest an aversive response to the environmental disparity. This supports the interpretation that the paradigm induces a state of frustration or social comparison-based stress, rather than being a byproduct of increased novelty or social cues.

This study is significant in demonstrating that psychosocial stressors often considered specific to humans—envy and social comparison—can elicit clear behavioral and endocrine changes in a mouse model. Envy is an aversive emotion characterized not only by feelings of inferiority but also by latent hostility and resentment toward others40. It typically arises from comparisons with others who possess desired attributes or goods, and its intensity depends on multiple factors40. Envy can be classified as a form of anxiety41 and has been proposed to be an affective phenomenon that does not rely on logical reasoning42. It correlates positively with depression, anxiety, somatization, obsessive–compulsive symptoms, and paranoid ideation43. Studies of adults suggest that envy is most often expressed toward same-sex, similar-age individuals44. Human envy also shows sex differences: men tend to report higher degrees of envy and express it more actively/behaviorally, whereas women tend to express it more passively and emotionally45. Capturing female vulnerability to envy-like stress in our study provides insight relevant to stress-related disorders and SSD, which are more prevalent in women.

Patients with medically unexplained symptoms are highly prevalent: one-third of somatic complaints in primary care are medically unexplained, with even higher rates in secondary care46–48. In DSM-5, such presentations are classified as “somatic symptom and related disorders (SSD)”49,50. These symptoms may or may not be explained by medical disease. Although the precise mechanisms remain unclear, stressful life events, substance use, and psychosocial stressors are all implicated as risk factors for SSD4. Envy in women may be more concealed due to social pressures and has been less thoroughly investigated51. Consequently, the neuropsychological impact of envy-like stress in humans remains poorly delineated. Our model, which reproduces the effects of social comparison stress during adolescence, may be useful for constructing animal models of stress-related disorders and for advancing understanding of SSD, particularly in younger females.

The envy-like stress model employed here is distinct from conventional animal stress paradigms. Unlike social defeat or restraint stress, which involve physical or bodily threats, our model constitutes a purely psychological stressor rooted in social comparison with others. By reproducing in animals the modern situation—amplified by social media—in which individuals are unilaterally exposed to others’ advantageous circumstances, this model likely possesses high clinical relevance for the study of contemporary stressors.

One could argue that sensory stimuli (auditory or olfactory) from adjacent enriched cages might serve as a form of ‘surrogate enrichment’ for the control-housed mice, potentially improving their welfare through vicarious experience. However, our findings in female mice—specifically the increase in anxiety-like behaviors and the suppression of basal corticosterone/ACTH levels—strongly suggest that this environmental disparity functions as a psychogenic stressor. Rather than benefiting from the neighbors’ enrichment, the subjects appeared to experience a form of social comparison-based stress or frustration. This is consistent with the concept that adolescent social environments are highly sensitive to perceived disparities, where the ‘relative’ deprivation may outweigh any potential vicarious benefits.

It is important to consider whether the observed phenotypes arise from simple sensory stimulation or social arousal rather than the psychosocial stress of environmental disparity. If the visual and olfactory cues from the adjacent enriched cages served merely as a form of surrogate stimulation, a reduction in anxiety-like behaviors might be expected. However, the female-specific increase in anxiety and the suppression of basal HPA axis activity suggest a predominantly aversive response. This pattern is consistent with frustration-induced stress, where the persistent observation of an inaccessible reward leads to psychological distress. Therefore, while elements of general arousal may be present, the cumulative behavioral and endocrine evidence supports the interpretation of this paradigm as a model for the negative impact of social comparison.

It is important to note that the present study does not directly demonstrate that mice experience subjective stress or envy while observing conspecifics interacting with environmental enrichment. Rather, the paradigm models a condition of sustained social comparison arising from exposure to conspecifics living under more favorable environmental conditions. Although the revised control condition minimizes the contribution of visual deprivation or social isolation by using transparent cages adjacent to non-enriched conspecifics, alternative interpretations—such as responses to visual social stimuli per se—cannot be fully excluded. Future studies incorporating additional control conditions, such as transparent cages with identical enrichment levels, as well as direct measures of acute stress reactivity, will be necessary to further dissociate social comparison–related stress from general social or sensory stimulation.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, our investigation of the HPA axis was limited to the measurement of basal serum corticosterone and ACTH levels at a single time point. We did not conduct circadian profiling or assess HPA axis reactivity to an acute stress challenge, nor did we examine central regulatory markers such as CRH expression in the paraventricular nucleus or glucocorticoid receptor sensitivity. Consequently, while the observed reductions in basal hormone levels in females are robust, we cannot definitively conclude whether this represents chronic stress-induced hypoactivity, enhanced negative feedback, or a state of social buffering. Second, the descriptive nature of this study means the underlying neural circuits and molecular pathways mediating these sex-specific changes remain to be elucidated. A critical unresolved issue in this regard is the absence of direct neuroanatomical evidence confirming that the envy-like housing condition engaged stress-related neural circuits. Specifically, c-Fos immunohistochemistry in key stress-processing brain regions—such as the basolateral amygdala, the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus (PVN), and the medial prefrontal cortex—would provide cellular-level confirmation of differential neural activation between the envy-like and control groups. Elevated c-Fos immunoreactivity in these regions would constitute direct evidence that the envy-like condition functioned as a genuine psychogenic stressor rather than a source of passive sensory or social stimulation. Unfortunately, brain tissue for c-Fos analysis was not collected in the current cohort, as experimental resources were directed toward behavioral phenotyping and blood-based endocrine measurements. We acknowledge this as a significant limitation and recognize that direct neural activation data would substantially strengthen causal inferences about the stress-inducing nature of the paradigm. Future studies will incorporate c-Fos mapping, along with CRH immunohistochemistry in the PVN and assessment of glucocorticoid receptor sensitivity, to establish the mechanistic basis of the sex-specific behavioral and neuroendocrine phenotypes observed here. Fourth, although the female phenotype shares biological features with clinical Somatic Symptom Disorder (SSD), such as heightened anxiety and blunted basal HPA activity, this translational link remains indirect and requires further validation through pharmacological or circuit-based interventions. Fifth, we did not monitor the estrous cycle in female mice; given that ovarian hormones significantly influence both stress reactivity and anxiety-like behavior, this remains a potential confounding factor. Finally, the specific sequence of our behavioral battery—such as conducting the hot plate test prior to the open field test—may have introduced carryover effects. While we employed a 24-h interval between tests to mitigate acute stress, the possibility of long-term behavioral modulation by early-stage testing is a common challenge in comprehensive phenotyping studies that cannot be entirely excluded. Furthermore, we did not monitor the estrous cycle or measure circulating sex hormone levels in female mice. Given that ovarian hormones, such as estrogen, are potent modulators of HPA axis function and anxiety-related behaviors, the lack of data on the hormonal status of our subjects remains a significant limitation. Future studies incorporating estrous cycle synchronization or gonadectomy would be valuable to isolate the contribution of sex hormones to the envy-induced endocrine and behavioral profiles observed here.

In conclusion, chronic envy-like stress during adolescence exerts sex-dependent behavioral and physiological effects in mice. In particular, females showed increased anxiety-like behavior and impaired motor coordination, together with reduced serum corticosterone and ACTH. The unique phenotype in females—heightened anxiety, impaired motor function, and suppression of the HPA axis—suggests that this paradigm may serve as a novel animal model contributing to the elucidation of SSD pathophysiology and the development of new therapeutic strategies, an area in which suitable models have been scarce.
