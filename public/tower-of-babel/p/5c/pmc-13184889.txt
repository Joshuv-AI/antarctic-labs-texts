Bilateral Coronary Artery–to–Pulmonary Artery Fistula Treated Using Minimally Invasive Coils and Vascular Embolization Devices
Authors: Ryoto Takagi, Jun Irita, Yusuke Akazawa, Kosuke Kashiwagi, Toshiyuki Chisaka, Michitsugu Nakamura, Yukio Kazatani, Takafumi Okura, Takashi Higaki, Osamu Yamaguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronary artery–to–pulmonary artery fistula (CAPF) is a rare condition that is often asymptomatic; however, intervention is indicated in symptomatic patients or in those with myocardial ischemia.Case SummaryA 44-year-old woman with a cardiac murmur since early adulthood presented with progressive exertional dyspnea. Cardiac computed tomography revealed bilateral plexus-like CAPF with aneurysmal formation. Although the Qp/Qs ratio was 1.08, stress cardiac magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated myocardial ischemia. Pulmonary artery–side outflow embolization was performed using a vascular occlusion device, followed by coil embolization of the coronary artery–side inflow sites, resulting in resolution of symptoms and improvement of ischemia.DiscussionAlthough pulmonary artery–side embolization can be effective when fistulous drainage converges into a single site, intercommunication between bilateral fistulas may elicit to-and-fro flow and compromise coronary perfusion.Take-Home MessageIn plexus-like CAPFs with intercommunication, combined pulmonary artery outflow and coronary artery inflow embolization should be considered according to vascular morphology.

History of Presentation

A 44-year-old woman with a cardiac murmur noted since she was approximately 20 years old presented to our institution with a 3-year history of progressive exertional dyspnea. At presentation, her vital signs were stable, with a regular heart rate of 64 beats/min, blood pressure of 135/81 mm Hg, respiratory rate of 14 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation of 100% on room air. Cardiac auscultation revealed a continuous grade III/VI murmur best heard at the third left intercostal space along the left sternal border. Lung auscultation was unremarkable, and there were no signs of jugular venous distention, peripheral edema, or other findings suggestive of congestive heart failure.Take-Home Message•In plexus-like coronary artery–to–pulmonary artery fistula with a single outflow converging into the pulmonary artery, a combined pulmonary artery–side and coronary artery–side approach may be considered to facilitate effective fistula closure.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of Hashimoto's thyroiditis but was not receiving treatment for any active medical conditions at the time of presentation. She had no history of smoking or alcohol consumption. There was no family history of cardiovascular disease.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnoses included adult congenital heart disease, valvular heart disease, ischemic heart disease, cardiomyopathy, heart failure, chronic thromboembolic pulmonary disease, anemia, renal dysfunction, and thyroid disorders.

Investigations

Electrocardiography and laboratory testing, including complete blood count, metabolic panel, lipid profile, and thyroid function, were within normal limits. Cardiac biomarkers and N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide were not elevated.

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed abnormal to-and-fro flow anterior to the pulmonary artery (PA), partially communicating with the PA (Figure 1). Left ventricular ejection fraction was 66%, left ventricular end-diastolic and end-systolic diameters were 54.5 and 39.2 mm, respectively, and the tricuspid regurgitation pressure gradient was 16.7 mm Hg. There was no evidence of left ventricular systolic dysfunction or significant valvular disease. Based on these findings, a coronary artery fistula was suspected.Figure 1Transthoracic EchocardiographyAbnormal to-and-fro flow is observed anterior to the pulmonary artery. Cardiac computed tomography demonstrated bilateral plexus-like vascular proliferation arising from both coronary arteries, with aneurysmal formation on the anterior aspect of the PA draining into the main PA (Figure 2), consistent with coronary artery–to–pulmonary artery fistula (CAPF). On computed tomography, the maximum diameter of the aneurysm was 14 mm, and the diameter of the PA communication was 6 to 7 mm. Further diagnostic evaluation was planned under the diagnosis of CAPF. Coronary angiography demonstrated fistulous vessels arising from the proximal right coronary artery and the proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD), with drainage into the PA. LAD narrowing (approximately 50%) was observed immediately distal to the fistula origin (Figure 4, Videos 1 and 2). The abnormal to-and-fro flow observed on transthoracic echocardiography was not clearly identified on coronary angiography. Right heart catheterization demonstrated a pulmonary-to-systemic blood flow ratio (Qp/Qs) of 1.08, a mean PA pressure of 20 mm Hg, a pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 13 mm Hg, and a pulmonary vascular resistance index of 1.81 WU·m2.Figure 4Coronary Angiography(A) Right coronary angiography demonstrates a fistula originating from the proximal segment, with an associated aneurysm. Management

Although the shunt fraction was not hemodynamically significant, intervention was warranted because CMR demonstrated myocardial ischemia with persistent exertional symptoms. In the absence of plaque on coronary computed tomography and with moderate LAD narrowing (approximately 50%) adjacent to the fistula origin, the lesion was considered functional, favoring fistula occlusion over primary LAD percutaneous coronary intervention. Given the diffuse plexus-like morphology, coronary artery–side coil embolization alone was considered likely to require multiple embolizations and result in incomplete occlusion; therefore, PA-side outflow occlusion was selected as the initial strategy.

Coronary angiography was performed via a right femoral arterial approach. For PA-side occlusion, vascular access was obtained via the right femoral vein, and a catheter was advanced into the PA. A Judkins right 4.0 catheter was advanced into the aneurysm, which had an orifice diameter of 6.5 to 7.0 mm and a maximum diameter of 12 mm. After catheter removal, a guidewire was advanced into the aneurysm using a microcatheter and a 0.014-inch wire, and an Amplatzer Duct Occluder II 6/6 (Abbott) was deployed at the pulmonary artery end of the fistula (Figure 5).Figure 5Embolization of the Coronary Artery Fistula–Pulmonary Artery Communication Using an Occlusion Device (Amplatzer Duct Occluder II)(A) Coronary angiography confirms the aneurysm, and a Judkins right catheter is advanced into the aneurysm using a long sheath. After Amplatzer Duct Occluder deployment, coronary-to-PA flow was eliminated; however, residual communication between the right and left fistulas elicited to-and-fro flow, with angiographic evidence of coronary flow stagnation (Figure 5, Video 3). Although no ST-T changes were observed, further improvement of coronary perfusion was deemed necessary. Subsequently, coronary artery–side coil embolization was performed.

The left coronary fistula was embolized with a Galaxy G3 4 × 80 mm (Cerenovus), i-ED coil 3-5 × 150 mm (Kaneka Medix), i-ED coil 3-5 × 200 mm, and Azure Soft 3D 2 × 80 mm (Terumo), resulting in marked reduction of shunt flow (Figure 6, Video 4). The right coronary fistula was then embolized using a Galaxy G3 4 × 100 mm, i-ED coil 3-5 × 150 mm, Azure Soft 3D 3 × 100 mm, and i-ED coil 4-8 × 150 mm (Figure 6, Video 5). After coil embolization, coronary flow improved with complete occlusion from the coronary artery side, and the Amplatzer Duct Occluder was subsequently detached.Figure 6Coronary Artery Approach: Coil Embolization of Bilateral Coronary Artery FistulasThe fistulas arising from both the left (A) and right (B) coronary arteries were treated with coil embolization, resulting in improved antegrade blood flow in the main coronary arteries.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Warfarin was initiated postoperatively (target international normalized ratio: 2.0-3.0) to prevent thrombus formation within the blind-ended segments created by coil embolization as well as thrombus formation associated with blood flow stagnation in the dilated coronary artery proximal to the fistula origin. No antiplatelet therapy was administered during the treatment period. On follow-up coronary angiography performed at 6 months, complete occlusion of the CAPFs was confirmed (Figure 7, Videos 6 and 7), and the narrowing of the LAD had also improved. After confirming that blood flow in the main coronary arteries was satisfactory, warfarin was discontinued. Follow-up CMR demonstrated resolution of myocardial ischemia, and the patient has remained free of exertional dyspnea.Figure 7Follow-Up Coronary Angiography at 6 Months(A) Follow-up right coronary angiography demonstrates regression of the fistula, with only minimal residual contrast opacification. Discussion

CAPF is often discovered incidentally and is identified in approximately 0.1% of patients undergoing coronary angiography.1 Fistulas originating from both coronary arteries to the PA, as observed in the present case, account for approximately 25% of all CAPFs.1 In this case, exertional dyspnea and a continuous murmur prompted diagnostic evaluation. A continuous murmur resembling that of patent ductus arteriosus was noted at presentation. The grade III continuous murmur was considered to be attributable to the CAPF, in which the PA inflow site was located close to the anterior chest wall.

Conservative medical management with beta-blockers or calcium-channel blockers may be considered for symptomatic relief2; however, current guidelines indicate that surgical or catheter-based interventions are indicated to mitigate or prevent complications in patients with CAPF, without specifying a preferred modality—catheter-based or surgical intervention for the treatment of CAPF.3

Although symptomatic cases have been reported with even a small shunt fraction,4 no significant LAD stenosis was present in our patient, and no plaque was identified on computed tomography, suggesting functional narrowing related to a coronary steal phenomenon, and given previous reports describing normalization of coronary artery diameter through remodeling after fistula closure,5 the fistula was selected as the therapeutic target.

In plexus-like coronary artery fistulas, embolization from the coronary artery side alone may leave multiple residual inflow channels, resulting in incomplete closure.6, 7, 8 In such complex anatomical settings, PA-side embolization offers a potential advantage by effectively interrupting fistulous flow when the fistula network converges into a single outflow tract to the pulmonary artery.4 A potential disadvantage of PA-side embolization is the risk of retrograde thrombus formation originating from the residual vascular segment, which may adversely affect the coronary circulation. In the present case, PA-side embolization was selected as the initial treatment with the expectation of achieving reliable fistula closure, and strict postprocedural anticoagulation was planned. Although covered stents can be effective in the treatment of complex coronary artery fistulas,8 they were not used in the present case because of the patient's young age and the requirement for long-term antiplatelet therapy. However, interfistulous communications persisted after PA-side occlusion, leading to the development of to-and-fro flow within the fistula network and subsequent stagnation of coronary arterial flow. This observation highlights an important limitation of PA-side occlusion alone, namely, its inability to fully normalize abnormal coronary hemodynamics in the presence of complex interconnections. Consequently, residual coronary flow disturbance and an increased risk of thrombus formation were considered. Therefore, additional coronary artery–side coil embolization was performed, and combined embolization from both the PA and coronary artery sides achieved complete and durable closure of the fistula network. These findings suggest that in complex plexus-like CAPF, a single-sided approach may be insufficient, and a combined PA-side and coronary artery–side embolization strategy may be necessary to achieve effective and sustained fistula closure.

Because antithrombotic management after coronary artery fistula closure is not standardized and robust evidence for long-term therapy is limited, careful postprocedural management is required. Some reports have suggested that anticoagulation therapy should be administered for 6 months with a target prothrombin time test with international normalized ratio of 2.0 to 3.0, and that follow-up coronary angiography is necessary to confirm normalization of coronary artery diameter9; this approach was also adopted in the present case.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates that in plexus-like CAPF, occlusion using a combined approach targeting both the coronary artery inflow and the pulmonary artery outflow can lead to complete fistula closure.
