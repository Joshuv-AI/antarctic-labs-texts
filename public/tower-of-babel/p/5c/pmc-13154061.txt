Ventricular Tachycardia Due to Intracardiac Metastases
Authors: Tsz Ho Chan, Hau Lam Leung, Chi Na Cheng, Chi Wing Yam, Kin Ho Wong, Cheuk Sum Lam, Chi Wing Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntracardiac metastases are rare and often clinically silent but may present with malignant ventricular arrhythmias. Early recognition relies on multimodality imaging to establish diagnosis, guide management, and inform prognosis.Case SummaryA 57-year-old man with advanced metastatic renal cell carcinoma presented with syncope and subsequently developed hemodynamically unstable monomorphic ventricular tachycardia. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated apparent apical left ventricular hypertrophy with preserved systolic function. History of Presentation

A 57-year-old man presented with a syncopal episode lasting approximately 5 minutes, preceded by dizziness and diaphoresis, without chest pain or palpitations. He regained full consciousness on hospital arrival. On examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable with no focal neurological deficits. Cardiovascular examination revealed normal dual heart sounds without murmurs. Shortly after admission, the patient developed hemodynamically unstable monomorphic ventricular tachycardia (VT), with electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrating a regular wide-complex tachycardia at a rate of up to 239 beats/min (Figure 1). He underwent immediate synchronized cardioversion, with restoration of sinus rhythm (Figure 2).

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history was significant for metastatic right renal clear cell carcinoma with bilateral pleural and bony metastases on palliative oncological therapy. Additional comorbidities included hypertension, gout, ischemic heart disease, benign prostatic hypertrophy, and a nonfunctional left adrenal incidentaloma.

Investigations

The admission ECG showed sinus rhythm at 98 beats/min with an old right bundle branch block and lateral T-wave inversions. Initial laboratory investigations demonstrated mild leukocytosis, normal electrolyte levels, and a mildly elevated cardiac troponin I level without significant rise on serial testing. Computed tomography (CT) of the brain excluded intracranial hemorrhage or metastatic disease.

Further cardiac evaluation included transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR). TTE demonstrated ill-defined focal apical hypertrophy involving the lateral wall and a moderate circumferential pericardial effusion without tamponade physiology. Left ventricular systolic function and valvular structures were preserved. Contrast-enhanced (SonoVue, Bracco) echocardiography showed heterogeneous perfusion (Figure 3). Focal early hyperenhancement was demonstrated 1 beat after “flash” high–mechanical index ultrasound beams (Figure 4, top right), corresponding to apparent apical pseudohypertrophy from an intramyocardial tumor. CMR confirmed multiple poorly circumscribed, contrast-enhancing myocardial masses within the left ventricle (Figure 5).

Management

The patient was treated with intravenous amiodarone followed by oral amiodarone and propranolol. After multidisciplinary discussion with the oncology team, no further disease-directed oncological therapy was offered owing to advanced metastatic disease and an estimated life expectancy of less than 1 year. Placement of an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator was not pursued given the poor prognosis and absence of recurrent arrhythmia on medical therapy.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was discharged on long-term antiarrhythmic therapy but was readmitted 2 months later with progressive dyspnea and generalized edema due to disease progression, and he subsequently died.

Discussion

Imaging analysis and differentiation of intracardiac metastasis

While echocardiography is typically the first-line imaging modality for assessing cardiac masses, its weaknesses are often considered in tissue characterization and defining the extent of myocardial infiltration. CMR is the noninvasive gold standard, providing superior spatial resolution and detailed tissue characterization.1 Late gadolinium enhancement is the key diagnostic sequence for differentiating vascularized tumors from avascular masses such as thrombi.2 Metastatic lesions typically demonstrate heterogeneous late gadolinium enhancement reflecting neovascularization and necrosis, whereas thrombi remain nonenhancing and appear as uniformly dark masses on postcontrast imaging.3

Cardiac CT offers complementary information with excellent spatial resolution, enabling accurate delineation of anatomical relationships and detection of calcification, which is better visualized on CT than on CMR.1,2 Positron emission tomography further aids characterization by assessing metabolic activity, with contrast-enhancing lesions on CMR typically corresponding to increased fluorodeoxyglucose uptake, supporting a diagnosis of metabolically active tumor rather than thrombus or cystic lesion.3

Contrast perfusion echocardiography is a rapid bedside method for assessing myocardial perfusion. This technique aids in the differentiation of cardiac masses, as malignant and vascular tumors are usually hyperenhanced compared with the adjacent myocardium, except those fast-growing malignant tumors with central tumor necrosis. The difference in maximal pixel intensity between the tumor and the adjacent myocardium can differentiate benign and malignant cardiac tumors with high specificity and sensitivity. As illustrated in the present case, multiple poorly circumscribed myocardial masses were clearly identified within the left ventricle, which demonstrated significant early hyperenhancement with contrast perfusion echocardiography. In the context of known advanced renal cell carcinoma, this imaging pattern was highly suggestive of cardiac metastases resulting from hematogenous spread.1,2 Conversely, benign tumors such as lipomas are usually well circumscribed, with minimal or no enhancement. The appearance was also inconsistent with cardiac myxoma, which most commonly arises from the left atrial septum, or papillary fibroelastoma, which typically involves cardiac valves.1,2 Primary myocardial tumors such as rhabdomyomas predominantly occur in children and were not compatible with our patient's age or the clinical context.2

It is important to note that nonenhancing intracardiac masses may also be encountered. Thrombi and cysts are typically nonenhancing on both echocardiography and CMR, while myocardial scarring may demonstrate enhancement depending on infarct thickness and chronicity.2 Taken together, the presence of multiple contrast-enhancing left ventricular masses with poorly defined borders in a patient with known metastatic malignancy strongly supports the diagnosis of intracardiac metastases.

ECG, imaging, and their relationship to ventricular tachycardia

Malignancies and anticancer therapies are associated with an increased risk of ventricular arrhythmias owing to direct myocardial infiltration, fibrosis, or treatment-related cardiotoxicity.4, 5, 6 VT morphology on ECG can provide insight into the arrhythmic substrate when interpreted in conjunction with imaging findings.

Tumor-related VT is most commonly mediated by reentry around areas of myocardial infiltration and fibrosis. The ECG morphology is largely determined by the exit site of the reentrant circuit rather than the full extent of scar burden.6 A left bundle branch block morphology with late precordial transition suggests a left ventricular origin, with apical sites associated with delayed transition across the precordial leads.6,7

In this case, VT demonstrated a left bundle branch block morphology with late precordial transition at lead V6, consistent with an apical left ventricular origin. This correlated with imaging findings on TTE and CMR demonstrating metastatic involvement of the left ventricular apex. These findings support a scar-related reentrant mechanism arising from tumor-infiltrated myocardium, providing a plausible explanation for the patient's malignant ventricular arrhythmia.

Management of VT in cardiac metastases

Management of arrhythmias associated with cardiac metastases is often palliative, reflecting advanced disease stage and limited life expectancy.8 Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement may not provide meaningful benefit and may adversely affect quality of life. Catheter ablation has been reported in selected cases of drug-refractory VT related to metastatic myocardial infiltration.9 Disease-directed therapies, including radiotherapy or systemic oncological treatment, may reduce arrhythmic burden in carefully selected patients, while surgical resection is rarely feasible and reserved for isolated cardiac metastases.10 The primary goals are symptom control, prevention of hemodynamic compromise, and optimization of quality of life.

Conclusions

VT associated with intracardiac metastases is rare. Contrast perfusion echocardiography provides a fast and easily accessible way to differentiate malignant intracardiac tumor from benign pathology. Management of tumor-related VT is highly individualized and requires multidisciplinary approach involving the cardiology, oncology, and palliative care teams.
