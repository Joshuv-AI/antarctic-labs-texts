Pulmonary tuberculosis complicated with bronchial lipoma: a case report and literature review
Authors: Junhui Liu, Qiusheng Jing, Zhan Lian, Chunxiang Song, Qi Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis case report presents a rare instance of intrabronchial lipoma in a patient with active pulmonary tuberculosis. A tumor located in the right lower bronchus was unexpectedly discovered during a bronchoscopy. Following a biopsy, the histopathological analysis confirmed it to be a lipoma. After six months of regular anti-tuberculosis treatment, most of the patient’s tuberculosis lesions were absorbed. The lipoma in the right lower bronchus was completely removed using bronchoscopic High-frequency electric snare. After 339 days, a follow-up bronchoscopy revealed that only a few white scars remained in the wall of the right lower bronchus. The size of the lumen was unaffected, and there was no deterioration in the patient’s pulmonary tuberculosis. However, it remains unclear whether the removal of the right lower bronchial lipoma contributed to the recovery from pulmonary tuberculosis. Narrow Band Imaging (NBI) technology proved useful in safely guiding the High-frequency electric snare procedure.Keywords: Lipoma, Bronchus, Tuberculosis, Case report

Background

Endobronchial lipoma is a rare condition that is often misdiagnosed. Typically, cases are not identified until a patient is being evaluated for another medical issue [1]. Over the past five years, the endoscopy center at our hospital has handled more than 50,000 bronchoscopy cases. During one procedure, a patient with active pulmonary tuberculosis was discovered to have a neoplasm in his right lower bronchus (see Fig. 1A). The pathological diagnosis confirmed it as a lipoma (see Fig. 1H). The lipoma was successfully removed through bronchoscopic intervention utilizing High-frequency electric snare (see Fig. 1C). Remarkably, after 339 days, there was no evidence of regrowth of the lipoma.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old male was admitted to the hospital due to experiencing night sweats for over two months. Physical Examination: T: 36.6 °C, P: 86 bpm, R: 21 bpm, BP: 114/82 mmHg. Upon admission, a CT scan revealed multiple pulmonary infections, with a possible diagnosis of tuberculosis and several indurated calcifications. There was also evidence of emphysema (see Fig. 2, A, B). In a previous bronchoscopy conducted at another hospital, a neoplasm was reported in the right lower bronchus, though no biopsy was performed. The patient has a significant smoking history of 50 years, averaging 20 cigarettes per day. It is also noted that he suffered a right hip fracture 15 years ago, requiring surgical intervention. The blood and urine tests returned normal results, and both liver and kidney functions were also within the normal range. The carcinoembryonic antigen was negative. However, the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was elevated at 85 mm/h. A pulmonary function test revealed mild mixed ventilation dysfunction, a moderate decrease in the maximum volume of air, a mild reduction in lung diffusion capacity, and a decrease in specific diffusion volume. Laboratory examination revealed the presence of mycobacterium tuberculosis in the sputum. During a bronchoscopy, a neoplasm was unexpectedly found in the right lower bronchus and was subsequently removed using High-frequency electric snare. The neoplasm measured approximately 6 mm × 6 mm, leaving electrical burn marks on the bronchial wall of the right lower bronchus (see Fig. 1, A, B, C, D, E, F). The bronchoscopic diagnosis indicated a tumor in the right lower bronchus. Histopathological reports confirmed the presence of a right lower bronchial lipoma (see Fig. 1, H). During the bronchoscopy, a residual white scar was observed on the bronchial wall of the right lower bronchus, 339 days post-procedure (see Fig. 1, G). The clinical diagnoses were as follows: (1) Secondary pulmonary tuberculosis affecting both lungs, with molecular biology tests returning positive results. Initial treatment was initiated. (2) An acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) along with a lower respiratory tract infection. The patient received regular antituberculosis therapy, specifically isoniazid (H) at a dosage of 0.3 g daily, rifampicin (R) at 0.45 g daily, and ethambutol (E) at 0.75 g daily. Upon completion of the treatment, a follow-up CT scan revealed partial absorption of the lung lesions (see Fig. 2, C, D).

Discussion and conclusions

This case involves a patient with active pulmonary tuberculosis in whom a tumor of the right lower bronchus was unexpectedly discovered during bronchoscopy. Biopsy results indicated that the tumor was a lipoma. After six months of regular anti-tuberculosis therapy, the majority of the tuberculosis infection had resolved (see Fig. 2), and sputum tests for acid-fast bacilli returned negative. The lipoma in the right lower bronchus was completely removed using a bronchoscopic high-frequency electric snare (see Fig. 1, D, E). A follow-up bronchoscopy conducted 339 days later revealed only a few small white scars on the wall of the right lower bronchus (see Fig. 1, G). Importantly, the size of the bronchial lumen remained unchanged, and there was no deterioration in the patient’s tuberculosis condition. However, it remains unclear whether the removal of the lipoma from the right lower bronchus contributes to the recovery from tuberculosis.

Endobronchial lipoma is an extremely rare benign lung tumor, with an incidence rate of 0.1% to 0.4%. These tumors are typically asymptomatic and may present with non-specific symptoms, which can result in excessive testing or significant delays in diagnosis [2]. The occurrence of pulmonary tuberculosis in combination with endobronchial lipoma is even rarer, and there are usually no distinct clinical symptoms to alert healthcare providers to the presence of a lipoma. Misdiagnosis is common [1]. Over time, enlarging lipomas can obstruct the bronchial lumen, potentially leading to atelectasis [3], which, in some cases, can be life-threatening [4]. This case involves an active instance of pulmonary tuberculosis. A neoplasm was incidentally discovered in the right lower bronchus during a bronchoscopy. Histopathology identified it as a lipoma, which did not obstruct the bronchus. The endobronchial lipoma was successfully removed through bronchoscopic intervention using high-frequency electrosnare treatment, and follow-up bronchoscopic examinations revealed no regrowth of the lipoma. Various studies have reported that endobronchial lipomas can be treated using techniques such as high-frequency electrotome, electrosnare, argon plasma coagulation (APC), cryotherapy, and laser therapy [5–8]. Most cases demonstrate satisfactory therapeutic outcomes and prognosis; however, some cannot be adequately addressed with laser therapy due to excessive bleeding [9], necessitating single-hole thoracoscopic surgery instead [10]. The location and quantity of lipomas in the tracheobronchus are nonspecific, usually appearing sporadically and sometimes linked to genetic syndromes. Intrathoracic lesions are uncommon and primarily occur in the mediastinum, bronchus, or lungs, often diagnosed incidentally [11]. Literature indicates that most lesions are found in the bronchus at a depth below grade 3 [6, 7]. The Narrow Band Imaging (NBI) technique is useful for the differential diagnosis of endobronchial tumors [12]. Under NBI mode, bronchial lipoma appears as a mass of varying sizes, with sac-like changes, a smooth surface, no exposed blood vessels, and bloody secretions (see Fig. 1, B). NBI technology helps assess whether high-frequency electrosnare technology is safely applicable, although the ability to distinguish between benign and malignant endobronchial tumors remains a topic for further discussion.
