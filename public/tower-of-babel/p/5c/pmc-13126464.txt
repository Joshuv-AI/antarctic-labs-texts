Lung transplant outcomes in recipients waitlisted for both single and double lung transplantation
Authors: Victoria Van Kirk, Shreya Guha, Ajay Prasad, William Zeng, Graeme M Rosenberg, Sean C Wightman, Brooks V Udelsman, Sivageni Ganesh, Scott M Atay, Anthony W Kim, Takashi Harano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDouble lung transplantation (DLTx) generally provides better survival than single lung transplantation (SLTx). However, SLTx recipients may often be more deconditioned, making direct comparisons challenging. The objective of this study is to investigate the transplant outcomes in patients waitlisted for both SLTx and DLTx to minimize differences in recipient background.MethodsThe United Network for Organ Sharing Database was retrospectively analyzed. The patients who were waitlisted from December 2017 to February 2023, then removed from the waiting list in the same time periods, were included in the analysis. Pediatric lung transplantation, multiorgan transplantation, and lung retransplantation were excluded.ResultsOf 4056 patients who were waitlisted for both SLTx and DLTx, 2127 patients (52.4%) received DLTx, 1351 patients (33.3%) received SLTx, and 578 patients (14.3%) were removed from waiting list without receiving lung transplantation. Median days on the waitlist were 36 days (IQR 12-93) for DLTx and 43 days (IQR 15-120) for SLTx. The lung allocation score at the time of transplantation was higher in DLTx group (50.58 ± 19.02 vs 44.67 ± 14.80, p < 0.001). DLTx recipients had significantly higher rates of prolonged intubation at 72 hours after transplantation (33.9% vs 19.9%, p < 0.001) and reintubation (19.3% vs 13.0%, p < 0.001). Median post-transplant length of hospital stay was longer in DLTx group (19 [IQR 14-33] vs 15 days [IQR 11-24], p < 0.001). However, DLTx group had significantly lower mortality than SLTx group (n = 3478, log-rank test p < 0.001).ConclusionsAmong patients waitlisted for both DLTx and SLTx, DLTx recipients had lower post-transplant mortality, despite a more complicated immediate postoperative course compared to SLTx.Keywords: single lung transplantation, double lung transplantation, overall survival, UNOS, OPTN

Single lung transplantation (SLTx) and double lung transplantation (DLTx) are established as common transplantation approaches for patients with end-stage lung disease. SLTx can increase organ utilization by allowing transplantation of one lung when the other is unsuitable, thereby reducing the number of discarded organs.1 Moreover, if both lungs from a donor are viable, performing SLTx enables 2 recipients to be transplanted, further optimizing organ use.

Despite these advantages of SLTx, DLTx remains the more commonly performed procedure, with a DLTx-to-SLTx ratio of approximately 4:1.2, 3 This preference is largely supported by studies demonstrating superior long-term survival, better functional outcomes such as improved forced expiratory volume in 1 sec (FEV1) and 6-minute walk distance, and enhanced quality of life following DLTx.4, 5 In contrast, SLTx is often selected for patients who are more medically deconditioned or older, and who may not tolerate the physiologic burden of DLTx.5, 6 While DLTx is associated with better survival in the overall transplant population, this survival advantage may be confounded by differences in baseline characteristics, particularly the more physically compromised status of patients selected for SLTx.3 In certain subgroups, such as elderly,6, 7 SLTx may offer outcomes comparable to DLTx.

To better isolate the effect of the transplant procedure itself on survival, we examine outcomes of each transplant type among patients who are waitlisted for both SLTx and DLTx (S+DLTx). These patients are considered eligible for either procedure and procedure type is primarily determined by the type of donor lung available at the time of transplantation. We hypothesize that among patients waitlisted for both transplant types, those who undergo DLTx may still experience improved survival compared to those who receive SLTx. This study aims to clarify the impact of transplant type on survival in this unique population, where eligibility for both procedures helps reduce differences in patient background and allows for a more balanced comparison. The findings may provide insights that guide organ allocation strategies and inform clinical decision-making.

Methods

The United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS)/ Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN) Database from December 2017 to February 2023 was queried for all patients who were waitlisted for lung transplantation. The University of Southern California Institutional Review Board (IRB) granted exemption from IRB approval after reviewing the study protocol (HS-25–00132) on March 14, 2025. Individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived because the UNOS database is de-identified during distribution.

Adult lung transplant candidates who were placed on the waitlist were included. Pediatric transplant candidates (younger than 18 years old), multiorgan transplantation candidates, lung retransplantation candidates, and the candidates who remained active on both waitlists as of February 28, 2023, were excluded from the analysis. The study period was determined based on allocation policy. In November 2017, OPTN replaced the allocation policy based on Donation Service Areas with a 250-nautical-mile radius around the donor hospital as the primary geographic unit and this allocation policy was used until the composite allocation score policy was implemented in March 2023. For patients waitlisted for both S+DLTx, the determination of the final transplant procedure was based on donor organ availability and clinical judgment at the time of the offer. In the United States, there are no standardized national criteria for selecting between SLTx and DLTx for dual-listed candidates.

The primary outcome of this study was overall survival (OS) after lung transplantation. Secondary outcomes included wait time from waitlisting to transplantation, length of hospital stay following transplantation, and the use of mechanical ventilation or ECMO at 72 hours after lung transplantation. Preoperative recipient characteristics and postoperative outcomes/complications were compared between 2 groups using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, and the independent samples t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, depending on data distribution. For comparisons among 3 groups, 1-way ANOVA was used for normally distributed continuous variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables. OS was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and was compared using log-rank tests. A 2-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

We conducted a propensity score–matched analysis to reduce confounding when comparing outcomes between SLTx and DLTx groups. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression based on recipient covariates of interest: age, blood type, diagnosis, Lung Allocation Score (LAS) at transplantation, ECMO bridge to lung transplantation, mechanical ventilator bridge to lung transplantation, medical condition at lung transplantation, and waitlist time. One-to-one (1:1) nearest-neighbor matching and a caliper width of 0.10 were used. After matching, balance between groups was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with values <0.1 considered acceptable. Residual imbalances (SMD >0.2) were further adjusted for in subsequent Cox regression models. To ensure the validity of the Cox regression model, multicollinearity among covariates was also assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). All analyses were done in SPSS version 29.0.2.0 and R version 4.2.3.

Results

During the study period, 16,114 patients were waitlisted for lung transplantation, and 14,047 patients were included in the analysis (Figure 1). Among these, 1512 patients (10.8%) were waitlisted only for SLTx, 8479 patients (60.4%) were waitlisted only for double lung transplantation DLTx, and 4056 patients (28.9%) were waitlisted for S+DLTx. Among 4056 patients in the S+DLTx group, 2127 patients (52.4%) underwent DLTx, 1351 patients (33.3%) underwent SLTx, and 578 patients (14.3%) were de-listed without lung transplantation. The recipient characteristics by transplant type are summarized in Table 1. The characteristics of de-listed patients without transplantation are provided in Supplemental Table 2. SLTx recipients were older compared to DLTx recipients (64.77 ± 7.34 vs 61.39 ± 9.02 years, p < 0.001). The proportion of obstructive lung disease (21.5% vs 20.3%) and restrictive lung disease (76.0% vs 75.5%) was similar between the SLTx group and DLTx group. LAS at transplant was significantly lower in the SLTx group (44.67 ± 14.80) compared to the DLTx group (50.58 ± 19.02, p < 0.001). DLTx recipients were more likely to be on ECMO (7.4% vs 1.3%, p < 0.001) or on mechanical ventilation (10.5% vs 5.6%, p < 0.001) at the time of transplant. Additionally, DLTx recipients were more frequently in the ICU at transplantation (16.0% vs 4.4%), and fewer were not hospitalized (72.9% vs 86.7%). Median waitlist time was 36 days (IQR 12-93) for DLTx recipients and SLTx recipients 43 days (IQR 15-120). The donor characteristics by transplant type are summarized in supplemental Table 3.

The transplant outcomes of DLTx and SLTx recipients were shown in Table 2. The median length of hospital stay was significantly longer in DLTx recipients compared to SLTx recipients (19 days [IQR 14-33] vs 15 days [IQR 11-24], p < 0.001). The need for ECMO at 72 hours after transplant did not differ significantly between the groups (9.1% DLTx vs 7.3% SLTx, p = 0.206). However, DLTx recipients had significantly higher rates of prolonged intubation at 72 hours after transplant (33.9% vs 19.9%, p < 0.001) and reintubation (19.3% vs 13.0%, p < 0.001). The incidence of renal failure necessitating dialysis was more frequent in the DLTx group (8.6% vs 3.3%, p < 0.001). There was no statistically significant difference in short-term outcomes: 90-day mortality was 3.8% in DLTx vs 3.4% in SLTx (p = 0.537), and 1-year mortality was 15.2% in DLTx vs 14.7% in SLTx (p = 0.642). However, the risk of death over time was significantly lower in DLTx group (n = 3478, log-rank test p < 0.001, Figure 2).

The patient characteristics and transplant outcomes were stratified by underlying lung disease among recipients waitlisted for both SLTx and DLTx (Supplemental Tables 4 and 5). After stratification, the difference in waiting time between SLTx (53.5 days, IQR 20-156.5) and DLTx (65 days, IQR 20-205) among patients with obstructive lung disease was no longer statistically significant (p = 0.141). In this subgroup, waiting time tended to be longer for DLTx. This was in contrast to the analysis of the overall cohort (Table 1), in which SLTx had a significantly longer waiting time. The risk of death over time was significantly lower in DLTx group for both obstructive lung disease and restrictive lung disease. (obstructive, n = 721, log-rank test p = 0.001, Supplemental Figure 1; restrictive, n = 2636, log-rank test p < 0.001, Supplemental Figure 2).

To reduce potential confounding in the comparison between SLTx and DLTx recipients, we conducted 1:1 propensity score matching. After matching, a total of 1351 matched pairs were identified. Covariate balance between groups was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs) (Table 3). Age at transplant (SMD 0.324), LAS at transplant (SMD 0.225), and medical condition at transplant (SMD 0.253) were further adjusted for in subsequent multivariable analyses. In the Cox proportional hazards model, SLTx was significantly associated with higher long-term mortality compared with DLTx (hazard ratio [HR] 1.321, 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.159-1.504, p < 0.001) as well as age (HR 1.015 per year increase, 95% CI 1.006-1.024, p = 0.001), while LAS and medical condition were not statistically significant. No significant multicollinearity was detected among covariates (all VIFs < 2.5). The Kaplan-Meier survival curves for the matched cohort are shown in Figure 3, which showed significant lower mortality in DLTx group (n = 2702, log-rank test p < 0.001).

Discussion

Among all the lung transplant recipients, it is well known that DLTx results in better long-term outcomes than SLTx.3, 4, 5, 8 However, SLTx recipients often have greater baseline medical vulnerability, making side-by-side comparisons difficult.3 To account for this discrepancy, our study focused on patients waitlisted for both DLTx and SLTx. In this cohort, SLTx recipients were older but had shorter hospital stays and fewer postoperative complications, with comparable short-term mortality to those who received DLTx. However, these modest perioperative benefits did not translate into improved long-term outcomes. Despite their longer postoperative course, DLTx recipients demonstrated significantly lower post-transplant mortality. This finding persisted even after further adjustment using propensity score matching based on pre-transplant variables.

There is a clear benefit to offering SLTx among transplant candidates who may not be able to tolerate DLTx.7 When investigating on the transplant candidates’ characteristics for each listing type (DLTx, SLTx, S+DLTx), SLTx candidates tended to be significantly older, with lower LAS, and less frequent use of pre-transplant ECMO or mechanical ventilation as a bridge to lung transplantation. This trend persisted in the comparison of DLTx and SLTx recipients’ characteristics among patients who were waitlisted for both SLTx and DLTx, which may imply that a physician’s decision to accept the offer of double or single lung transplant may still be influenced by recipient characteristics. These differences in recipient characteristics prompted further analysis using propensity score matching and multivariable Cox regression analysis to investigate whether the survival benefit experienced by DLTx recipients was due to the procedure itself.

Compared to DLTx, SLTx is a shorter, technically less complex procedure which places less physiologic stress on recipients.5 Our results are consistent with findings from prior literature that demonstrated shorter recovery times, fewer complications in the immediate postoperative period, and comparable short-term survival rates to DLTx recipients.3, 5, 6 However, the comparatively benign post-surgical course experienced by SLTx recipients cannot be attributed to the procedure type alone; on average, DLTx recipients had a higher LAS and degree of medical urgency at the time of transplant. The association between increased LAS and morbidity post-procedure is well established in the literature and most likely contributed to DLTx recipients’ more complicated recovery course.9, 10, 11

It is unknown whether waitlisting patients for both DLTx and SLTx can effectively reduce waitlist time. Among S+DLTx candidates, SLTx recipients had longer median waitlist time (43 days) than DLTx recipients (36 days). The lowest LAS among SLTx recipients in the S+DLTx group suggests that some may not have been transplanted if they had been listed only for DLTx, implying improved access with SLTx. Similarly, the S+DLTx listing approach may allow transplantation to occur before patients begin to rapidly deteriorate. However, the observed differences in waiting time should be interpreted descriptively rather than causally. Waiting time is driven primarily by allocation priority, particularly LAS, as well as donor availability and laterality. Therefore, these findings do not support a causal effect of the listing strategy itself on waiting time.

While S+DLTx listing may increase the odds of receiving a transplant, the question remains whether we can justify the worse long-term outcomes experienced by single lung transplant recipients. Patients may prefer to wait longer and accept more postoperative complications in pursuit of longer overall survival. SLTx recipients have more complicated long-term post-transplant courses, which may be in part due to the fact that disease processes which necessitate SLTx frequently affect the contralateral lung as well. SLTx recipients are more prone to complications such as pneumothorax, native lung hyperinflation, Aspergillus infection, and malignancy in native lung,12, 13, 14, 15 each of which can cause acute or chronic decompensation in the patient and precipitate earlier mortality compared to patients who undergo DLTx. Before placing patients on both single and double lung transplant waitlists, physicians should thoroughly counsel patients on the possible consequences of unrestricted waitlisting. By waiting until SLTx is offered to provide this information, physicians may inadvertently place undue stress on patients, who must then quickly weigh the pros and cons of this transplant option within the limited time available before proceeding to SLTx.

Our findings are consistent with multiple contemporary studies using propensity-matched or adjusted registry analyses. Weingarten et al. demonstrated that among recipients aged ≥65 years, SLTx was associated with reduced perioperative morbidity and comparable short-term survival, but significantly lower 5-year survival compared with DLTx after propensity matching.6 Similarly, Schaffer et al. and Villavicencio et al. reported improved long-term survival with DLTx in patients with restrictive lung disease, even after adjustment for recipient and donor characteristics.16, 17 Additional analysis by Chauhan et al. suggests that greater utilization of SLTx may increase overall organ availability without adversely affecting post-transplant outcomes,18 while Munson et al. conclude that although SLTx maximizes access, DLTx confers superior durability of survival over time.19 Together, these studies reinforce our observation that among candidates eligible for either procedure, DLTx is associated with a long-term mortality advantage despite greater perioperative complexity.

This study had several limitations. Although the use of the UNOS database enabled analysis of a large number of transplant recipients, this dataset included only a limited set of variables. While we attempted to reduce selection bias by defining the study cohort, applying propensity score matching, and performing multivariable analyses, residual confounding may persist due to unmeasured or unavailable variables. The large national database is also subject to coding errors. Although our findings suggest a clear long-term advantage in post-transplant mortality risk for DLTx, a prospective randomized trial would be necessary to definitively determine if the mortality benefit of DLTx is inherent to the procedure itself and whether SLTx should be utilized less frequently in this specific patient population. Additionally, the retrospective nature of the study limits the ability to draw causal inferences, especially in comparison to prospective study designs. Furthermore, a limitation is that our analysis primarily reflects practices under the Lung Allocation Score (LAS) and the geographic allocation policy. The current allocation system is the Composite Allocation Score (CAS), which utilizes a continuous framework to prioritize candidates. Therefore, our findings may require reevaluation as more data becomes available in the CAS era.

Conclusion

While SLTx is associated with improved short-term postoperative outcomes compared to DLTx among recipients waitlisted for both procedures, these benefits do not translate into a reduction in long-term post-transplant mortality. DLTx recipients experienced a reduced risk of post-transplant death compared to those who underwent SLTx. Nonetheless, SLTx remains a viable treatment option for selected patients with end-stage pulmonary disease. When listing candidates for both DLTx and SLTx, it is important to clearly communicate the risks and benefits of each approach to support informed decision-making.
