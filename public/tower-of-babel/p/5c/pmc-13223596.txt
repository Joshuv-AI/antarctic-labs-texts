Schoolboys’ experiences of information and communication regarding the human papillomavirus vaccination
Authors: Eva Runngren, Mats Eriksson, Karin Blomberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo maintain sufficient vaccination rates for human papillomavirus (HPV), it is important to understand the experiences of the target group. Boys were included in the Swedish National Immunization program for HPV more recently, in 2020, than girls, so boys’ experiences of the program are relatively underexplored. This study therefore aimed to explore schoolboys’ experiences of information and communication regarding HPV vaccination.MethodsThe study had a qualitative explorative design. Individual semi-structured interviews were conducted with 11-year-old schoolboys (n = 13) and analysed using interpretive description.ResultsThree themes were identified: information and communication about HPV vaccination in school, schoolboys’ knowledge about HPV and the vaccination, and lack of family involvement in the HPV vaccination.ConclusionBoys lacked knowledge about HPV vaccination, and parents only partly involved the boys in the vaccination process. There is a need for school nurses to be given clear guidelines for informing boys and parents about HPV, and for the teachers to be provided with health education regarding HPV vaccination.

Introduction

The World Health Organization emphasizes the importance of vaccinating against human papillomavirus (HPV) since this can prevent up to 90% of various types of cancers, such as cervical, anal, oropharyngeal, penile, vulva, and vaginal cancer.1 Vaccination is considered one of the most effective practices against HPV2 and data confirm its safety.3 As of November 2024, 146 of 194 countries worldwide had included girls in their vaccination programs, and 76 had included boys as well.4

In Sweden, HPV vaccination for girls has been part of the national immunization program (NIP) since 2012, but this was not extended to boys until 2020.5 The program includes two doses of Gardasil-9 and all children are offered the HPV vaccination free of charge by the School Health Service at age 11. School nurses are responsible for all logistics related to the HPV vaccination. The vaccination coverage for two doses for girls was 87.3% and for boys 82.2% in 2023.6

Almost all men and women will be infected with HPV at some point in their lives, but most such infections are asymptomatic and resolve spontaneously.1 It is beneficial for boys to be included in the NIP for HPV, not only to avoid becoming carriers of this sexually transmitted infection, but also because men are at risk of HPV-related diseases.7 The prevalence of HPV in boys aged 15–19 (28% [95% CI 24–32]) indicates that young men may become infected during their first sexual contact.8

Most research on information and communication regarding the HPV vaccine has focused on parents, since the primary vaccination target group is children aged 11–149,10 and so parents must give their consent to the vaccination.11 A study by Heyde et al. revealed significant geographical variations in parental acceptance of HPV vaccinations, with the highest acceptance rate in Africa (79.6%) and the lowest in North America (56.7%). Common barriers to vaccination include fears of adverse effects and concerns about vaccine safety. Knowledge about HPV and its vaccination varies widely, with moderate to high awareness that HPV is sexually transmitted and that the vaccine protects against cervical cancer. Mothers generally show greater willingness to vaccinate their daughters than their sons, a trend consistent across regions. Parental acceptance rates range from 12.0% to 97.5%.12 Parents need useful and trustworthy information.13 Health professionals and public health authorities14 are seen as a trusted source of information to decide whether to vaccinate their child,13 but parents’ knowledge regarding HPV for boys is limited.15–17 Moreover, boys have less knowledge than girls regarding HPV infections18,19 and make use of fewer information sources related to HPV.20

As HPV vaccination for boys was included in the NIP relatively recently, it is of interest to explore their experiences of the vaccination. Information and communication regarding HPV may increase boys’ knowledge, which can impact vaccination rates and reduce the spread of HPV if given before exposure. This study aimed to explore schoolboys’ experience of information and communication regarding HPV vaccination.

Method

Study design

An interpretive descriptive methodological approach21,22 was used. The study was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (registration numbers: 2022-02175-01, 2023-05637-02).

Participants

The study included 13 11-year-old schoolboys from public (n = 8) and private schools (n = 5) in three counties in mid-Sweden, all of whom had been offered the first dose of HPV vaccine by the school nurse. Study information and invitation letter were distributed via post or digital platforms by school nurses or principals. In total, 54 boys from 6 schools, grade 5, were invited to participate. Parents sent an e-mail to the first author to announce their son’s interest in taking part in the study. Some parents actively declined their son’s participation due to opposition to the HPV vaccine (n = 2), while others proactively contacted the researcher to join (n = 6). Most participants had siblings (n = 1–8), though two did not.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews

Face-to-face semi-structured interviews were conducted by the first author from January 2022 to November 2024. An interview guide was developed, based on previous research13 and the authors’ experiences of developing other interview guides, focusing on schoolboys’ experiences of HPV vaccination. The opening question was: “What do you think about vaccinations?” The boys were then asked questions such as whether they knew what the HPV vaccination protected against, where or from whom they obtained information about HPV vaccination, and what information they received from the school nurse and their parents regarding HPV. The interview guide was not pilot tested. The interviewer had prior experiences of interacting with children from clinical practice but did not have any personal or professional relationships with the participants. Audio-recorded interviews were carried out at the boy’s school, at the boy’s home, or in a room at the university, and lasted for 15–20 min. The interview duration was not predetermined and was guided by the boy’s willingness and ability to participate in the discussion. After each interview, the first author transcribed the interview verbatim.

Data analysis

The analysis followed the principle of interpretive description.21 This type of analysis goes beyond a simple description of data to a level of interpretation that requires inductive reasoning and pattern recognition. The analysis first involved spending time immersed in the data to develop a sense of the “whole” beyond the immediate impressions of the transcripts. The next phase involved repeatedly asking what the data were telling us. The researchers identified patterns and inductively generated initial codes according to the study’s aim. This involved engaging with the data and sorting it. The codes were grouped based on similarities and differences in the study’s aim. This was followed by searching for themes, reviewing them, and defining and naming them.22 The authors then discussed the relationships among the themes to ensure they were correctly interpreted in light of the data. To ensure credibility and consistency, all authors were involved in coding and interpretation. Codes and emerging patterns were discussed collaboratively, and discrepancies were resolved through reflexive dialog. This approach supported analytical depth and helped maintain a coherent interpretation across the dataset. An example of the analytical process is given in Table 1. Quotations were identified to give perspective to the results and increase the credibility and are labeled in this article with a code for each informant (SB = schoolboy).

Findings

The findings are presented in three main themes from the analysis: Information and communication about HPV vaccination in the school; Schoolboys’ knowledge about HPV and vaccination; Lack of family involvement in HPV vaccination.

Information and communication about HPV vaccination in the school

Several boys said that they received oral information from the school nurse about the HPV vaccination, and some were also shown a picture. This information varied: some school nurses explained why it was good to vaccinate against HPV, others described what would happen at the vaccination occasion, and others informed the boys that the vaccine would not harm them. The information was given to the whole class together, including the girls. Some of the boys said that they could not remember receiving any information about HPV vaccination from the school nurse. Of these, some said they received information from the school’s information platform in good time before the vaccination, allowing them to be prepared for the occasion.

I think the school nurse said something about venereal diseases, so it was better to get the vaccination now than when it was too late. (SB7)

The school nurse said it’s not dangerous to get vaccinate; she didn’t explain much. (SB1)

The teacher was another source of information, providing much the same information as the school nurse, but according to the boys this information was not proactively. Some of the boys described social media and the Internet (TikTok or Google) to find out more information about vaccinations. Those who had questions about vaccines said that they could have asked their parents, but that they had not done so. Interest in knowing more about vaccinations was low, only few of the boys said that it would be good and interesting to know more in general.

I wouldn’t want to; I don’t need to know more (about HPV). (SB12)

There’s nothing dangerous at all in knowing more. (SB2)

Sometimes the boys said they could talk to each other about the vaccination; this mostly occurred after the vaccination occasion, e.g., discussing whether the injection had hurt and how they felt afterwards. One boy said that although he used to talk to his schoolmates about vaccinations, he no longer did so because he now felt calm and was not worried about them anymore.

Schoolboys’ knowledge about HPV and the vaccination

Overall, the boys said that vaccination was good, though they could not describe in detail why. They knew that vaccinations protected against viruses and that vaccines could help people avoid becoming ill. They did not describe any specific vaccinations but expressed a more general perception of vaccinations they had received in school. Some boys mentioned receiving COVID-19 and tick-borne encephalitis vaccines, though this did not occur at school.

But I don’t care, I still want to get it (vaccination), because I want to feel healthy, so it’s not a YOLO thing, you just have to get it, I think. (SB1)

It was obvious that the boys lacked knowledge about HPV and the HPV vaccination, since most of them did not know what HPV was; only that they had received an injection. Those who did have knowledge said that the HPV vaccination protected against cancer, but not what type of cancer. Some said that the vaccination protected against venereal disease but could not describe this in more detail. Overall, they understood to some extent that HPV vaccination was protective against cancer, but very few of them were able to link to sexually transmitted diseases. Some of them thought that the vaccine gave protection against cancer types such as prostate cancer and lung cancer and one boy referred to his grandfather needing a “bag on his stomach” because of cancer.

Yes, you could get something, that could lead to something, like you could get cancer in a few places. (SB4)

HPV, it stands for something… I don’t know. (SB3)

Lack of family involvement in the HPV vaccination

Several boys received some information from their parents about HPV, but this information was described as being more general; their parents mostly told them when it was time for the vaccination, and sometimes the reason why they were getting the vaccine. Some of the boys did not receive any information about the HPV vaccination from their parents; instead, it was the boys who informed their parents when it was time for the vaccination. As one schoolboy put it, the HPV vaccination was given during school hours, so he saw no need to talk about it with his parents.

We don’t talk much about the HPV vaccination at home, because then you can get stressed. (SB1)

My parents tell me what the vaccination does, what it’s good for. (SB6)

Children were involved to varying degrees in the decision about whether they should be vaccinated. For most of the boys, it was the parents, and sometimes only the mother’s decision, while in other cases, the boys said that their parents and the school made the decision jointly. One boy said that he and his classmates were too young to decide for themselves whether they should be vaccinated, so it was better if an adult decided for them. A couple of the boys said that the decision had been left up to them. One boy wanted to get as many vaccinations as he could. Another said he did not want the vaccination, but his parents wanted him to get it, so he had to. He felt it was unnecessary.

It’s my parents who decide; they always think I should be vaccinated. (SB5)

It’s a bit mixed; it’s both my parents and me, except when it’s a vaccination that’s very important, my parents decide. (SB3)

Discussion

Main findings of this study

This study showed that the schoolboys lacked knowledge about HPV vaccination, and parents only partly involved them in the vaccination process. Despite this, the boys were positive to vaccination in general. School nurses and teachers are important to promote HPV vaccination.

What is already known on this topic

The boys described vaccination as something positive that protects against diseases, but their knowledge about HPV vaccination was limited. This lack of knowledge among children and parents has been described in several studies,9,13,18,23,24 and girls have generally shown greater awareness than boys18,20,25,26—likely due to having been offered the vaccine nearly a decade longer. Knowledge is important, as it can increase children’s positivity toward vaccination and their involvement in decision-making.27

One study has found that when parents received information about both gender and HPV, some of them were more willing to vaccinate their daughters.28 While Swedish parents tend to trust the NIP,16 recent findings from the Netherlands indicate a decline in parent’s unquestioning acceptance of NIP recommendations,29 highlighting the need for continued efforts to maintain public confidence.

To effectively provide information, school nurses need sufficient knowledge and access to high-quality materials, as healthcare providers’ understanding is linked to their HPV vaccine recommendations.30,31 Teachers were also mentioned as information sources by the boys, but previous research shows that teachers often lack knowledge about HPV32,33 and may hold negative attitudes due to concerns about side effects or the perceived relevance of HPV education.32 This highlights a need for improved HPV education among school staff.32–34

Parent’s communication with their sons focused more on the timing of vaccination than its purpose, suggesting missed opportunities for health education. Limited parental knowledge about HPV may contribute to this.35 For example, research from the Netherlands found that some parents viewed preventing infection transmission as the main reason for vaccinating their sons.36

The boys in the present study were rarely involved in the decision-making process. Previous research shows that parents often avoid discussing HPV vaccination with younger children, believing they are too young to understand.16 This aligns with findings that boys aged 12 and up are more involved than younger boys.37 According to the Convention on the Rights of the Child, children have the right to express their views in matters affecting them, with consideration for age and maturity.38 Few boys in this study showed interest in learning about HPV, contrasting with an Italian study where children were more curious about the HPV virus and the vaccine.39 Boys lack of interest in HPV may be explained by their young age, as the relevance of the vaccine and its implications might not be fully understood or personally meaningful to them.

What this study adds

Findings of this study showed that there was a lack of knowledge among boys regarding HPV and HPV vaccination. Addressing this is crucial for several reasons. Firstly, increasing awareness among boys about HPV and it is potential health risks can lead to a good vaccination coverage, thereby reducing the incidence of HPV-related cancers in men. Secondly, educating boys about HPV can help dispel myths and reduce vaccine hesitancy, ensuring broader public health benefits.

Parents and schools play a pivotal role in bridging this knowledge gap. Parents can increase awareness and knowledge about HPV in the boys if the parents communicate about vaccination at home. However, it can be challenging due to the sensitive nature of the topic, lack of parental knowledge, fear of worrying the children, cultural and social norms, and uncertainty about the right timing for the conversation. School nurses and teachers can actively promote HPV vaccination, emphasizing its importance for both boys and girls. Through education, schools can contribute to increasing HPV vaccination coverage among boys, ultimately protecting them from the serious health consequences associated with HPV.

Limitations of this study

Despite our best efforts, only 13 boys participated in the study. Recruitment was challenging as school nurses individually decided whether to share study information with boys and their parents, potentially introducing selection bias. Furthermore, only vaccinated boys were included, resulting in a selective sample that limits the generalizability. Including unvaccinated boys could provide valuable insights into reasons for vaccine hesitancy and broaden the understanding of their perspectives to HPV vaccination. The small sample size also means that not all perspectives among schoolboys in this context could be captured. Nevertheless, this small qualitative study may still be of interest, since HPV vaccination for boys in Sweden is a relatively new phenomenon, and it is of importance to include children in research to ensure that their perspectives are heard. Furthermore, the interview guide was not pilot tested, which may have affected the depth of responses, as the questions might not have been sufficiently defined to elicit detailed or nuanced answers. The immediate transcription after each interview allowed reflection on data quality and did not indicate a need to change the interview guide.

Conclusion

This study highlights important aspects of schoolboys’ experiences of information and communication regarding HPV vaccination. The information they received from the schools and parents often focused on the vaccination occasion rather than HPV itself, which may explain their limited knowledge. Parental involvement was minimal, and the boys showed little interest in learning more. There is a need for targeted communication for this age group, as well as clearer guidelines and education efforts to strengthen HPV knowledge among teachers and school nurses. Future research could explore interventions that promote children’s involvement in vaccination decisions.
