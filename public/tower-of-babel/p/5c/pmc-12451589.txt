Ramadan is the holiest month in the Islamic calendar and majority of adult Muslims fast from dawn to sunset during this month as part of their religious beliefs. There are almost two billion Muslims around the world, and the impact of fasting on health conditions cannot be underestimated, especially during extreme weather conditions. The average duration of fast is 12–16 h depending on the location, and it can result in significant disruption to daily routine. However, at extreme latitudes the duration of fast may go up to 20–22 h.

As Ramadan fasting (RF) is regarded as one of the five pillars of Islam and is obligatory for Muslim adults from the age of puberty (subject to health status and co-morbidities), Muslims with chronic health conditions are generally very keen to follow this religious practice and there is a reluctance to omit fasting during Ramadan. However, it can pose a dilemma to patients with chronic diseases such as diabetes mellitus, ischemic heart disease or chronic respiratory diseases such as asthma, COPD, bronchiectasis and interstitial lung disease (ILD).

There are several guidelines to help Muslim patients to manage diabetes during Ramadan and these have consistently been updated over the years [1–3]. However, there is dearth of published data on the impact of fasting and consensus-based recommendations for Muslims with chronic respiratory diseases who consider fasting during the holy month of Ramadan, making it challenging to objectively decide if they should fast. Moreover, guidance is lacking on how to adjust treatment regimens if patients decide to observe fasting.

There is lack of large, prospective, high-quality trials evaluating this important aspect of disease management in patients with respiratory diagnoses, resulting in extremely limited evidence-based guidance for Muslims who consider fasting every year. There are handful of studies on the effects of fasting, predominantly conducted in patients with airway disease in recent years. Ghaffary et al. [4] evaluated 120 patients with moderate to severe asthma during Ramadan by dichotomising them into two groups of fasting and non-fasting (each group n=60) and found that fasting had no significant impact on spirometric parameters or asthma and suggested that these patients can safely fast during Ramadan. Rejeb et al. [5] selected a small group of 15 stable COPD patients and evaluated the effects of RF on haematological and inflammatory indices. It was noted that fasting resulted in a significant reduction in haemoglobin, haematocrit, and red blood cell and white blood cell counts. However, there was no significant difference in inflammation as assessed by erythrocyte sedimentation rate or C-reactive protein. Mekki et al. [6] conducted a prospective study on 20 male patients with COPD. They evaluated physiological, psychological, anthropometric and postural characteristics in their cohort. The measurements were performed a week before Ramadan, and during the second and fourth week of the holy month. There was functional deterioration during early phase of Ramadan with some improvement by week four. However, these studies in COPD did not have a control group and all participants were male, making it hard to generalise the findings of these investigations to population at large.

In this issue of ERJ Open Research, Khan et al. [7] attempt to address the current gap in knowledge by conducting a systematic review and develop consensus-based recommendations following a comprehensive search strategy that followed a pre-specified protocol (PROSPERO CRD42024532759) and PRISMA guidelines [8] to identify relevant studies evaluating fasting outcomes in people with chronic respiratory conditions. The authors focused on common respiratory diagnoses including asthma, COPD, ILD and bronchiectasis. There were only 11 studies (nine prospective cohort and two cross-sectional) that met the inclusion criteria, and all of these were in patients with either asthma or COPD as no published studies on the effects of RF in bronchiectasis or ILD could be identified for the purpose of this systematic review.

The methodology of this study included formulation of an international group of medical and religious experts who refined the guidelines to achieve consensus approval for the recommendations, following draft of consensus statements. The strengths of this manuscript include a detailed search strategy (MEDLINE, EMBASE and Google Scholar) that was reviewed by two researchers followed by structured proforma with a view to assess the risk of bias by appropriate methodology. It is important to note that despite the literature search going as far back as 1946, there were no randomised controlled trials (RCTs) or multi-centre studies evaluating the effects of RF on clinical end points in chronic respiratory diseases.

The consensus recommendations were primarily derived from expert consensus (rather than as a direct result of systematic review of published studies) by a panel of 12 experts from around the world with a wide-ranging expertise and mixture of Muslim and non-Muslim healthcare professionals as well as religious scholars. The authors have proposed a risk-stratification and decision-making flow chart to help clinicians assess patients’ suitability for fasting. Furthermore, they propose a pre-Ramadan consultation with a view to approach patients with chronic respiratory conditions in a structured manner and categorise their risk into “low to moderate”, “high risk”, and “very high risk”. It is recommended that patients falling in low to moderate risk should be supported to fast and other groups should be advised not to fast. The efforts of this expert group and authors are commendable, and I congratulate the authors for their quest to advance knowledge in this important and religiously sensitive aspect for the Muslim population (with chronic respiratory conditions) who are in constant need of expert guidance during Ramadan.

The systematic review and consensus-based recommendations proposed by the authors help advance our understanding about RF in airway disease, ILD and bronchiectasis, with an aim to guide clinicians managing patients with these respiratory diseases with ultimate benefit to patients who have long been disadvantaged by the lack of professional/expert guidance. The major limitation of this work is the lack of robust evidence, and this highlights an urgent unmet need for researchers around the globe to conduct multi-centre studies to evaluate the clinical and scientific aspects of RF on conditions such as airway diseases, ILD, bronchiectasis and other respiratory conditions such as pulmonary hypertension and cystic fibrosis. Only then can we be in a better position to guide our patients about the actual risks of RF and disease control, so they can make an informed decision about observing this religious practice without detrimental health effects. It is particularly important for patients with ILD who are being commenced on anti-fibrotic therapies for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and progressive pulmonary fibrosis, as it is generally recommended to take these drugs on equal intervals (8 hourly for pirfenidone and 12 hourly for nintedanib), and they can be associated with weight loss and other gastroenterological adverse effects, making it crucial to evaluate the interplay of anti-fibrotic drugs with periods of fasting and disease stability.

It should be of paramount importance to consider establishing an International Task Force of respiratory clinicians and allied healthcare professionals including physiotherapists, pharmacists and specialist nurses to identify key research questions to be evaluated in future studies. We will benefit immensely from prospective RCTs to evaluate the effects of RF on common respiratory disorders. However, it will need global collaboration, careful planning and sensitive ethical considerations along with strong patient representation. This approach will advance us a step closer to developing evidence-based guidelines for Muslim adults with chronic respiratory conditions who have long been looking for professional recommendations to balance their long-term disease control and religious beliefs.
