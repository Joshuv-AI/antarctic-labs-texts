MindTS-Net: A multimodal mindfulness-based intervention framework for children with Tourette syndrome
Authors: Li Liping, Fang Xiaoxia, Zhang Huimin, Wang Jianping, Wu Haoyu, Li Qian, Song Huimin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTourette syndrome affects children’s daily functioning and creates a need for supervised support that responds to tic behaviors, emotional states, and interaction contexts. We developed MindTS-MMD, a Chinese multimodal dataset capturing emotion, tic-related behaviors, interaction context, and Valence-Arousal ratings, and MindTS-Net, a supervised digital adjunct linking multimodal emotion perception, mindfulness strategy planning, child-appropriate guidance generation, and dialogue-state tracking. Evaluation included dataset benchmarking, a 14-day primary clinical study with 60 children, and an independent external validation cohort of 57 children from a second clinical center. MindTS-Net improved emotion perception, strategy selection, and guidance quality compared with baseline approaches. In the primary study, children receiving MindTS-Net showed favorable Valence-Arousal changes and reduced tic burden. External validation showed consistent directions across perception, strategy, guidance, and intervention outcomes. These findings suggest that supervised multimodal digital support can complement pediatric tic management and provide a foundation for future multi-center research.Keywords: tourette syndrome, multimodal dataset, mindfulness-based intervention, digital behavioral support, emotion perception

Introduction

Tourette syndrome is a neuropsychiatric condition that typically emerges in childhood and is characterized by involuntary and repetitive motor or vocal tics, frequently accompanied by comorbidities such as attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and anxiety disorders.1,2 Heightened emotional arousal and affective instability have been reported as proximal triggers of tic exacerbation, although long-term outcomes are shaped by multiple factors including the waxing-and-waning course of tics, baseline severity, comorbidities, and access to evidence-based care.3 Consequently, developing child-centered interventions that support emotion regulation is of substantial clinical and societal significance. Mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs) cultivate present-moment awareness and non-judgmental acceptance and have been used as adjunctive therapies for childhood anxiety, impulse-control difficulties, and attentional deficits.4 Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have further opened opportunities for intelligent, interactive, and dynamically adaptive psychological support.5 However, existing affective dialogue systems mainly provide empathy and comfort while lacking goal-oriented intervention planning, semantic controllability, and adaptation to children’s developmental and cognitive profiles.6

Children with TS often present impairments in emotion recognition, expression, and regulation, frequently accompanied by impulsivity.7 Anxiety is more prevalent in this population and correlates positively with tic frequency,8 while comorbid ADHD can further exacerbate deficits in attentional and emotional control.9 Together, these factors may create a maladaptive cycle in which dysregulation both intensifies and is aggravated by tic severity.10 In current clinical practice, medication can alleviate tics but often provides limited emotional benefits and may be associated with side effects.11,12 Clinical guidelines therefore recommend cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)13 and habit reversal training (HRT)14 as first-line interventions. Comprehensive behavioral intervention for tics (CBITs),15 which integrates core HRT elements with psychoeducation, functional intervention, and relapse prevention, has demonstrated efficacy in randomized trials,16 and developmentally adapted formats such as CBIT-JR support the feasibility of behavioral treatment in younger children.17 However, these approaches remain therapist-intensive, usually require repeated in-person sessions, and are difficult to sustain in daily life. In this context, mindfulness-based strategies are better viewed as complementary to evidence-based behavioral treatments such as CBIT/HRT, because they primarily target emotional arousal and attentional control—proximal factors that often precede tic exacerbation in daily life.18 Behavioral studies further suggest that structured interventions can reduce tic frequency and improve emotional regulation.19,20,21 Neurobiological evidence has also implicated prefrontal-striatal abnormalities and Treg dysregulation in impaired inhibition, stress reactivity, and symptom fluctuation.22,23,24 The present study does not target immune mechanisms directly but instead focuses on modulating emotional arousal and stress reactivity as modifiable proximal triggers of tic exacerbation.25 Family and school environments also matter: positive parenting and emotional support promote resilience, whereas adversity, family conflict, and social exclusion may worsen symptoms and adjustment.26,27,28,29,30

Mindfulness-based methods have been explored to address these challenges. Evidence suggests that MBCT, CBT, and ACT can enhance emotion regulation and reduce symptom severity in related conditions.31 Digital MBIs such as headspace and calm,32 mindfulness-based music interventions,33 and related studies34,35 have broadened access, but real-time adaptability and deep therapeutic engagement remain limited. Researchers have therefore begun integrating multimodal perception and intelligent dialogue into intervention systems. Adaptive graph attention improves EEG-based emotion recognition,36 multi-source adaptive fusion enhances cross-domain robustness,37 interactive attention strengthens multimodal speech-text recognition,38 and multimodal physiological signals further improve emotional state identification.39 Related advances in multimodal datasets, fusion architectures, affective sensing, and dialogue modeling40,41,42,43,44 further support the feasibility of such systems. Despite these advances, most existing systems target adults or general contexts rather than children,45 multimodal corpora specifically designed for pediatric interventions remain scarce,46 and perception-strategy coupling, continuous state tracking, and mindfulness-oriented controllable generation are still insufficient.47,48,49 Emerging reviews further suggest that mindfulness-based approaches may offer potential benefits for Tourette syndrome and tic disorders, particularly in symptom management, emotional regulation, and treatment accessibility, although dedicated pediatric evidence remains limited.50

Taken together, these gaps point to the need for a supervised, child-centered digital support system that can recognize emotional states, provide developmentally appropriate regulation guidance, and assist daily monitoring beyond clinic-based sessions.51 The aim of this work is not to replace first-line behavioral treatment, provide autonomous diagnosis, recommend medication, or digitize CBIT as a fully automated intervention. Rather, we focus on a complementary and scalable between-visit support layer that regulates emotional arousal and supports context-sensitive monitoring under caregiver and researcher supervision. To address this need, we propose MindTS-Net, a supervised multimodal digital adjunct supported by MindTS-MMD, a multimodal dataset specifically designed for pediatric TS intervention research. MindTS-Net links multimodal emotion perception, mindfulness strategy planning, age-appropriate guidance generation, and dialogue-state tracking within a supervised intervention workflow. Results

The following analyses are based on the MindTS-MMD dataset and the MindTS-Net framework. MindTS-MMD provided synchronized multimodal interaction data with emotion, Valence-Arousal, and tic-related annotations, while MindTS-Net connected emotion perception, strategy planning, guidance generation, and dialogue-state tracking for supervised pediatric TS intervention. Details on dataset construction, cohort recruitment, model architecture, training, and evaluation are provided in STAR Methods.

Multimodal emotion perception

We first evaluated MindTS-Net on MindTS-MMD to examine whether multimodal signals improved the recognition of clinically relevant emotional states in children with TS. The full text-audio-visual configuration (T + A + V) was compared with the text-audio baseline (T + A) for both discrete emotion classification and continuous Valence-Arousal modeling.

As shown in Table 1, the integration of visual input improved performance across all seven discrete emotion categories. The largest gains were observed in categories characterized by sparse or ambiguous verbal expression, particularly Tense and Shy. For the Tense category, F1-score increased from 53.1% to 62.7%, while Shy improved from 48.4% to 60.0%. These findings suggest that visual cues provide clinically useful complementary information when children’s verbal or acoustic emotional cues are limited or ambiguous.

Samples were divided into low- and high-tic subsets using a median split of annotated tic frequency within each observation window. MindTS-Net generates clinically acceptable mindfulness strategies and guidance

We next evaluated whether MindTS-Net could translate recognized emotional states into appropriate mindfulness strategies and child-centered guidance. In closed-set strategy classification, MindTS-Net achieved higher strategy-selection accuracy than rule-based and text-only baselines. In open-set semantic matching, generated strategy representations showed stronger alignment with expert-authored references. These findings suggest that multimodal emotional information supported the selection of clinically meaningful mindfulness strategies.

MindTS-Net also produced guidance responses with higher semantic consistency, mindfulness-oriented style, and linguistic diversity than comparison systems. Automated metrics, including BLEU, ROUGE-L, BERT Score, cosine similarity, and Distinct scores, favored MindTS-Net. Subjective ratings by five professional counselors further supported the naturalness, mindfulness style, contextual consistency, and child-oriented appropriateness of the generated responses. Detailed benchmark comparisons for strategy classification, semantic matching, and language-generation quality are provided in Figure S2 and Tables S4–S6.

Representative examples further illustrate the clinical interpretability of the generated guidance. Primary clinical evaluation

The primary clinical evaluation examined whether MindTS-Net use was associated with changes in emotion regulation and observed tic frequency during the 14-day supervised protocol. Given the pilot design, clinical outcome analyses were interpreted as preliminary effect signals rather than definitive estimates of efficacy. Repeated daily observations were aggregated at the participant level before inferential testing.

During the intervention phase, the MindTS-Net group showed a clearer shift toward more positive and stable emotional states than the monitoring-only control group. As shown in Figure 3, the MindTS-Net group displayed a steady increase in Valence and a synchronous decrease in Arousal starting from approximately Day 5, whereas the control group showed only minor fluctuations without a clear directional trend.

Emotion score trends across groups

Tic analysis based on standardized video observation windows showed a reduction in observed daily tic counts in both groups. However, the reduction was numerically larger in the MindTS-Net group, with mean observed daily tic counts decreasing from 18.500 to 4.012, whereas the control group decreased from 18.400 to 6.874. As shown in Figure 4, the MindTS-Net group exhibited a rapid initial decline followed by relatively stable maintenance, whereas the control group showed a more gradual and fluctuating trajectory. Participant-level change-score analysis further showed a between-group difference in mean change of 3.024 events per day in favor of MindTS-Net.

To provide an exploratory view of how system-assigned strategies related to short-term emotional changes, outcomes were summarized by dominant strategy category. As shown in Table 5, breathing regulation (S1) and awareness guidance (S6) were associated with the largest average increases in ΔValence (+2.124 and +2.034, respectively) and the largest decreases in ΔArousal (−1.542 and −1.482, respectively). In contrast, emotion labeling (S3) showed a comparatively smaller mean increase in ΔValence (+1.473) and decrease in ΔArousal (−1.026). Because strategies were adaptively assigned and individual participants could contribute to multiple strategy categories, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and non-causal rather than as evidence of differential strategy efficacy.

We next examined whether improvements in emotional regulation were associated with reductions in tic frequency. Participant-level summaries from the post-intervention phase were used to assess the correlation between emotional alignment improvement, defined as a shift toward higher Valence and lower Arousal, and tic frequency reduction rate (TFRR). Pearson’s correlation analysis showed a significant positive association in the MindTS-Net group (r = 0.595, p < 0.001; R2 = 0.353), indicating that participants with greater emotional alignment improvement tended to show larger reductions in tic frequency. This relationship is shown in Figure 5. Because this analysis was correlational, it should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct causal effect.

Correlation between emotional alignment improvement and tic frequency reduction

To evaluate whether MindTS-Net provided flexible and personalized intervention support, we analyzed strategy allocation across tic-frequency states and individual users. Participants were divided into high- and low-frequency tic groups using a median split. As shown in Table 6, strategy allocation differed between the two groups. The high-frequency tic group received higher proportions of breathing regulation (S1: 31.242%) and emotion labeling (S3: 27.186%) than the low-frequency group, with mean differences of 15.558 and 10.759 percentage points, respectively. This pattern suggests that MindTS-Net tended to prioritize arousal reduction and emotional grounding when tic burden was higher. In contrast, the low-frequency group received more relaxation training (S5: 23.864%) and awareness guidance (S6: 22.131%), indicating a shift toward maintenance-oriented and awareness-based strategies under lower symptom frequency. Body scan (S2) and mindfulness attention (S4) showed no significant between-group differences, suggesting that these strategies functioned as stable supportive components across symptom states.

External validation supports cross-center consistency

To evaluate cross-center consistency, we compared the primary MindTS-Net group with an independent external validation cohort from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xinxiang Medical University. Because the external cohort received MindTS-Net-guided intervention in addition to conventional medication and did not include a separate control group, this analysis was interpreted as cross-center consistency rather than controlled efficacy.

Emotion perception showed only minor fluctuations in the external cohort compared with the primary MindTS-Net group. Accuracy decreased slightly, whereas Macro-F1 showed a small numerical increase. Continuous Valence-Arousal estimation also remained stable, with small bidirectional changes in PCC and MSE values. None of these differences reached statistical significance, and effect sizes remained small. These findings suggest that the emotion perception component retained stable performance under a second clinical setting.

Strategy selection and guidance generation also showed no statistically significant cross-center degradation. Strategy accuracy, strategy consistency, semantic matching, language-generation metrics, and expert ratings were broadly comparable between cohorts. These results indicate that the perception-strategy-guidance pathway retained functional stability and clinically acceptable output quality in the external cohort. Detailed cross-center comparisons of emotion perception, strategy selection, and guidance generation are provided in Figure S5, Tables S9 and S10.

The most clinically relevant comparison concerned intervention-related outcomes. As shown in Table 7, final Valence and Arousal scores were similar between the primary MindTS-Net group and the external validation cohort. Daily tic count was slightly higher in the external cohort, but the difference was not statistically significant. TFRR remained high in both cohorts, reaching 78.412% internally and 75.144% externally. Completion rates were also high and comparable across cohorts, supporting the feasibility and adherence of the supervised intervention workflow across clinical settings.

Taken together, the external validation results showed consistent outcome directions across emotion perception, strategy selection, guidance generation, and intervention-related indicators. These findings support preliminary cross-center consistency of the supervised MindTS-Net pipeline, while larger multi-center studies with active control conditions and longer follow-up are still required to confirm efficacy and long-term generalizability.

Discussion

MindTS-Net provides a supervised multimodal digital adjunct for children with Tourette syndrome by linking emotion perception, mindfulness strategy planning, child-appropriate guidance generation, and longitudinal state tracking within one intervention workflow. The central finding of this study is clinical rather than purely technical: during the 14-day supervised protocol, children in the MindTS-Net group showed a clearer shift toward higher Valence and lower Arousal than the monitoring-only control group, together with a larger numerical reduction in observed daily tic counts. These findings suggest that a child-centered digital support system targeting emotional arousal and daily regulation may provide useful between-visit support for children with TS. Given the pilot design, these results should be interpreted as preliminary clinical signals rather than definitive evidence of efficacy.

The emotional changes observed in this study are clinically meaningful because tic symptoms are often sensitive to affective arousal, stress, and contextual triggers. MindTS-Net does not directly retrain tic behaviors in the same manner as CBIT or HRT; instead, it focuses on emotion regulation, arousal stabilization, and context-sensitive mindfulness guidance. The positive association between emotional alignment improvement and tic-frequency reduction further supports this interpretation. Children who showed greater movement toward higher Valence and lower Arousal tended to show larger reductions in tic frequency. However, this relationship was correlational and should not be interpreted as evidence that emotional improvement directly caused tic reduction. Rather, it suggests that emotional regulation and tic burden may change together during supervised digital intervention.

The strategy-level findings provide additional insight into how MindTS-Net may support short-term regulation. Breathing regulation and awareness guidance were associated with larger average improvements in Valence and Arousal than some other strategies, while strategy allocation differed between high- and low-frequency tic states. In higher tic-frequency states, the system tended to assign more breathing regulation and emotion labeling, whereas lower tic-frequency states were more often associated with relaxation training and awareness guidance. This pattern suggests that MindTS-Net did not rely on a single fixed template but adjusted support according to symptom state and individual response patterns. Because strategies were adaptively assigned rather than randomized, these results should be viewed as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of differential strategy efficacy.

Multimodal emotion perception contributed to the clinical usability of the system. Children with TS may not always express emotional distress through clear verbal reports, and subtle facial, vocal, or behavioral cues may provide important complementary information. In this study, adding visual input improved recognition of low-salience emotional states such as shyness and tension, which are clinically relevant but often difficult to infer from text and audio alone. This suggests that multimodal sensing can help detect affective states that children may not clearly verbalize, thereby supporting more context-sensitive intervention planning. In this respect, the value of multimodal perception lies not only in benchmark performance but also in its potential to strengthen the system’s responsiveness to clinically relevant emotional states during supervised interactions.

The external validation cohort further supports the stability of the proposed intervention pipeline across clinical settings. Compared with the primary MindTS-Net group, the external cohort showed broadly comparable patterns in emotion perception, strategy selection, guidance generation, and intervention-related outcomes. Final Valence, Arousal, daily tic count, TFRR, and completion rate showed no statistically significant cross-center differences, and the overall direction of change was consistent. Because the external cohort did not include a separate control group, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary cross-center consistency rather than controlled external efficacy. Nevertheless, the results suggest that the supervised perception-strategy-intervention workflow was not limited to the original clinical setting.

From a clinical perspective, MindTS-Net should be understood as a complementary digital support tool rather than a replacement for first-line behavioral treatments such as CBIT or HRT. CBIT and HRT directly target tic behaviors through structured competing response training, functional analysis, and therapist-guided behavioral practice. By contrast, MindTS-Net focuses on emotion regulation, arousal reduction, and context-sensitive support between clinic visits. This distinction is important because the system is not intended to provide autonomous diagnosis, medication advice, or fully automated behavioral therapy. Its potential role is to extend supervised support into daily contexts where therapist-intensive intervention is not continuously available.

Together, these findings support MindTS-Net as a scalable, supervised, and clinically bounded digital adjunct for pediatric tic management. By combining child-centered multimodal emotion perception with mindfulness strategy planning and adaptive guidance, the framework provides preliminary evidence for supporting short-term emotional regulation and tic-related monitoring in daily contexts. The results also establish MindTS-MMD as a useful multimodal resource for pediatric TS intervention research. Further validation will be required before broad clinical deployment, but the present findings provide a foundation for larger controlled studies of AI-assisted, emotion-focused support for children with TS.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be considered. First, although this study included an independent external validation cohort, the validation was conducted in only one additional hospital, and the external cohort did not include a separate control group. Therefore, the external results should be interpreted as evidence of cross-center consistency rather than controlled external efficacy. Second, the intervention period was limited to 14 days, which is short relative to the waxing-and-waning course of tic symptoms; longer follow-up is needed to determine whether the observed improvements in Valence, Arousal, and daily tic counts can be sustained over time. Third, the primary control group received monitoring only rather than an engagement-matched active digital control, so expectancy effects, caregiver prompting, and adherence-related influences cannot be fully excluded. Finally, strategy-level analyses were exploratory because strategies were adaptively assigned rather than randomized, and MindTS-Net is currently optimized for Mandarin Chinese and the cultural-pragmatic context represented in MindTS-MMD. Future studies should include larger multi-center cohorts, longer follow-up, active control conditions, and culturally adapted validation to further establish the long-term clinical utility and real-world deploy ability of this supervised digital adjunct.

By demonstrating preliminary improvements in emotion regulation and tic-related outcomes under supervised use, this study provides a foundation for future controlled trials of multimodal, emotion-focused digital support for children with Tourette syndrome.

Experimental model and study participant details

This prospective supervised-intervention study enrolled Chinese children aged 6–12 years with clinician-confirmed Tourette syndrome, recruited from Xinxiang Central Hospital and the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xinxiang Medical University. Diagnosis was established according to DSM-5 criteria through structured clinical interviews and medical record review. No restrictions were applied based on biological sex, social gender, ancestry, race, or ethnicity.

Primary clinical evaluation cohort

At Xinxiang Central Hospital, 74 children were assessed for eligibility. After screening, 65 participants were randomized using a computer-generated allocation sequence in a 1:1 ratio. Sixty participants completed the study and were included in the final participant-level analysis, with 30 assigned to the MindTS-Net intervention group and 30 assigned to the monitoring-only control group. The study procedure consisted of a 14-day protocol including a 4-day baseline observation phase followed by a 10-day intervention phase.

External validation cohort

To evaluate cross-center consistency, an independent cohort was established at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xinxiang Medical University. A total of 63 children were recruited and followed the same 14-day protocol as the primary cohort. All participants in this cohort received MindTS-Net-guided intervention in addition to conventional medication. Six participants were excluded prior to statistical analysis due to predefined data-quality criteria, leaving 57 participants for the final analysis. As in the primary cohort, repeated daily observations were aggregated at the participant level before outcome comparisons. Data from the external cohort were used exclusively for independent validation and were not used for model training, hyperparameter tuning, or selection.

Method details

MindTS-MMD dataset construction

MindTS-MMD was constructed as a Chinese, child-centered multimodal dataset for pediatric Tourette syndrome intervention research. The dataset was designed to support supervised modeling of multimodal emotion perception, tic-related behavioral annotation, mindfulness strategy planning, and child-appropriate guidance generation. Existing multimodal corpora and facial-expression datasets do not fully meet the requirements of synchronized text–audio–video interaction data, tic-related annotations, and mindfulness-oriented pediatric intervention modeling; therefore, MindTS-MMD was developed specifically for this study.

Data were collected from children aged 6–12 years using a semi-structured emotion induction paradigm, including picture stimuli, story retelling, situational narration, and role-playing tasks. Each session contained 6–10 interaction rounds organized around six predefined mindfulness-based intervention strategies, while emotion labels were annotated independently from strategy labels. Task order was pseudo-randomized, and neutral or low-arousal segments were inserted between high-arousal conditions to reduce emotional carryover.

Each interaction round was designed to capture synchronized text, audio, and video data. Text data were obtained from Mandarin ASR transcripts, audio was recorded as 16 kHz WAV files, and video was recorded in H.264 MP4 format at 25–30 frames per second. Whisper was used for Mandarin ASR and timestamp alignment. COVAREP and OpenFace were used for acoustic and visual preprocessing, auxiliary feature extraction, and quality control. Word-level timestamps were used to align audio and video streams within a tolerance of ±120 ms, and segments exceeding this tolerance were excluded from synchronized multimodal analysis.

After preprocessing and quality control, synchronized multimodal records were annotated for emotion category, Valence–Arousal scores, tic frequency, and tic location. Seven elicited emotion categories were used: calm, relaxed, focused, shy, anxious, tense, and irritable. Valence–Arousal scores were obtained using a dual-source rating scheme combining child self-reports and experimenter observations, with both dimensions rated on a 9-point scale from −4 to +4. Inter-rater reliability for categorical emotion annotation reached Cohen’s κ = 0.83. Tic-related annotations were obtained through synchronized audio–video review by trained raters and were linked to the corresponding interaction windows for downstream modeling and clinical analysis.

The final fully labeled multimodal analysis set contained 5,498 rounds with valid video recordings and synchronized annotations for emotion category, Valence–Arousal scores, tic frequency, and tic location. Additional audio-only or text-available rounds retained during preprocessing were not included in the fully supervised multimodal benchmark. All multimodal recordings were de-identified before annotation and analysis, and personally identifiable information was removed from the research dataset. Detailed procedures for session-level distress ratings, tic-frequency assessment, acquisition settings, and annotation rules are provided in Supplementary Methods Sections 6.1–6.2.

MindTS-Net was designed as a closed-loop multimodal intervention framework linking sensing, emotion perception, strategy planning, guidance generation, and dialogue-state tracking. Quantification and statistical analysis

Dataset-level model performance was evaluated using task-specific metrics. Discrete emotion recognition was assessed using Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and Macro-F1, while continuous Valence–Arousal estimation was assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) and mean squared error (MSE). Mindfulness strategy selection was evaluated using Strategy Accuracy and consistency metrics, and guidance generation quality was evaluated using BLEU-1, BLEU-4, ROUGE-L, BERTScore, cosine similarity, Distinct-1, Distinct-2, and expert rating scores. Unless otherwise specified, dataset-level metrics were computed on the held-out test set.

Clinical analyses were conducted at the participant level. Repeated daily observations were aggregated within participants before inferential testing. Emotional outcomes included changes in Valence and Arousal between baseline and intervention phases, while tic-related outcomes included observed daily tic counts and tic frequency reduction rate (TFRR). Between-group comparisons were performed using participant-level change scores. Strategy-level analyses summarized emotional and tic-related changes according to the dominant mindfulness strategy assigned during intervention sessions. Because strategies were adaptively assigned and participants could contribute to multiple strategy categories, these analyses were interpreted as exploratory and non-causal rather than as evidence of differential therapeutic efficacy.

External validation analyses compared the primary MindTS-Net cohort with the independent external validation cohort. The external cohort was not used for model training, validation, hyperparameter tuning, or model selection. Cross-center analyses included emotion perception performance, strategy-selection consistency, guidance-generation quality, intervention completion rate, Valence–Arousal changes, observed daily tic counts, and TFRR. Because the external cohort did not include a separate control group, external validation results were interpreted as evidence of cross-center consistency rather than controlled external efficacy. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise specified. Effect sizes were reported using Cohen’s d with 95% confidence intervals where applicable. Model training used participant-level data splitting to reduce identity leakage, random seeds were fixed across repeated experiments whenever applicable, and early stopping was applied according to validation loss. Statistical analyses were performed using Python and R. Unless otherwise specified, all tests were two-sided and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.
