Dental imaging in Singapore: a survey of 2D radiographic techniques and CBCT practices
Authors: Almond Y Q Ng, Clement W M Lai, Clarissa X H Ho, Li Zhen Lim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis survey was conducted to identify dental radiography practices and knowledge gaps among dentists in Singapore with respect to both 2D and 3D imaging.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional survey conducted via an electronic platform from May to June 2023. All Singapore Dental Council-registered dentists were eligible to participate. We gathered data on demographics, intraoral (IO) radiography usage, cone-beam CT (CBCT) training, current CBCT usage, and interpretation.ResultsThree-hundred and five out of 2605 registered dentists completed the online survey (11.7% response rate). Radiograph positioning holders, digital imaging, and round collimation were used by most respondents for IO radiography (85.6%, 88.9%, and 89.2%, respectively). One-hundred and forty-two participants (46.6%) underwent CBCT training, and most lacked training in image acquisition. Dentists expressed interest in CBCT interpretation and the use of viewing software; 219 dentists (71.8%) are CBCT users. Most (74.9%) took 0-5 CBCT scans monthly. Implant planning was the most common indication (24.8%). Among CBCT users, 85.9% report some or all their own scans while 70.7% would engage a reporting service if available.ConclusionsThis survey provides insights in dental imaging that require increased educational efforts in Singapore. For IO radiography, there should be greater emphasis on rectangular collimation. With CBCT, there is potential for training in image acquisition, interpretation, and the use of viewing software.Advances in knowledgeIdentifying knowledge and training gaps in dental imaging is critical to ensure the safe use of ionizing radiation on patients.Keywords: questionnaire survey, dental imaging, dental radiography, cone-beam CT, X-ray machines, radiation protection

Introduction

Radiographic imaging is a fundamental component of diagnosis and treatment planning in dentistry, and a core skill that all dental practitioners must be competent in. In the past few decades, dental imaging technology has advanced rapidly with progression from 2D radiography to 3D imaging, namely cone-beam CT (CBCT).

The superior diagnostic ability of CBCT compared to 2D imaging is why it is now considered the imaging modality of choice for clinical indications such as dental implant planning,1 complex endodontic cases,2 and evaluation of osseous changes within the jaws and the temporomandibular joint,3 among many others. However, practitioners should be cognizant of the associated stochastic effects and practice judicious use of ionizing radiation. Since CBCT examinations are generally associated with higher doses compared to intraoral (IO) and panoramic radiographs,4,5 caution must be exercised with its use.

In Singapore, the number of radiography licences issued to dentists by the National Environment Agency (NEA) allowing them to operate X-ray apparatus more than doubled from 735 in 2010 to 1978 in 2023 [Data provided by NEA, personal communications]. Correspondingly, the number of dental radiography machines registered with NEA increased from 1169 to 2417 over the same period. Although the prevalence of dental imaging is clearly on the rise, the usage patterns among dentists in Singapore, especially in terms of CBCT, are unknown.

Within the dental school curriculum in Singapore, the theoretical and practical aspects of IO and panoramic imaging are covered in detail. In contrast, CBCT is only taught at an introductory level, and dental students are not expected to be proficient at acquiring or interpreting cross-sectional images. Additionally, oral and maxillofacial radiology (OMR) is not a recognized specialty in Singapore, and there is a shortage of dental imaging reporting services and providers. Hence, CBCT users are responsible for interpreting any images they acquire and must upskill themselves via continuing education courses post-graduation.

This survey was conducted to identify dental radiography practices and knowledge gaps among dentists in Singapore to formulate training recommendations. We aimed to gather data on the use of IO radiography (specifically use of digital imaging, positioning holders, and rectangular collimation [RC]). With CBCT, we investigated dentists’ current levels of training and practices including clinical usage, adherence to guidelines, and image interpretation. We also examined associations and trends between practices and demographics, confidence levels, and training experience.

Methods

This was a cross-sectional survey conducted through an electronic survey platform. Ethical approval was granted (NUS-IRB-2022-137).

Participants

All Singapore Dental Council-registered dentists in Singapore were eligible to participate. Dentists in Singapore who were not currently registered were excluded from this study.

Recruitment

This online survey was conducted on Qualtrics (Provo, UT, United States). The survey link was sent to various dental societies in Singapore as listed in our Acknowledgements section, and through the study teams’ contacts. Participants were also encouraged to further disseminate the survey link to those who met the eligibility criteria. The initial invitation was first sent out in May 2023, and another reminder was sent out to the dental societies in June 2023. The survey closed on June 30, 2023.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the distribution of demographic characteristics and survey response. A 1-sample t-test was conducted to assess whether the demographic distribution matched that reported in the SDC report. Fisher’s exact tests were performed to examine the association between demographic variables and survey responses. All statistical analyses were conducted in R.6

Results

Demographics of survey respondents

Three-hundred and five dentists completed the online survey. Based on 2605 registered dentists in Singapore in 2022, the response rate was 11.7%.7

In this survey, we collected data on years of experience as a proxy for age. Dental practitioners who practiced for 10 years or less (estimated to be below 35 years old) formed the highest proportion of respondents (41.3%), followed by those who practiced for 11-20 years (23.9%) and 31-40 years (18%). The proportion of respondents who were estimated to be below and above 35 years old did not differ significantly from the demographics reported by SDC. Almost half (47.5%) of the respondents were males, corresponding to the proportion of male dentists in Singapore (47.5%).

We defined “GPs with additional training” as postgraduate trainees undergoing residency training or dentists who are undergoing or have completed an SDC-approved diploma, for example in implant dentistry. We defined “specialists” as dentists who completed their residency training. General dental practitioners (GPs) and GPs with additional training made up the biggest proportion of the respondents (49.5% and 14.4%, respectively). Specialists were over-represented in our survey population (36.1%) as compared to the proportion of specialists based on the SDC report (16%), which defines specialists as those who complete additional examination requirements after residency (P < .001). Endodontists formed the highest proportion of specialist respondents at 26.4%, followed by orthodontists (13.6%) and periodontists (17.3%).

Almost half of the respondents worked in private group practices (47.2%), followed by institutions such as hospitals or tertiary dental centres (27.9%), and private solo practices (17.7%).

IO imaging

Most dentists acquired IO periapical and bitewing radiographs themselves (83.3%). Among the remaining 16.7%, IO radiographs were predominantly performed by a radiographer or radiation technologist (79.6%).

Radiograph positioning holders and digital imaging were used by most respondents (85.6% and 88.9%, respectively). A small proportion of dentists (6.6%) used both digital and film receptors. Most dentists were still using round collimation (89.2%); only a minority used RC (5.6%) and 5.2% reported that they did not know what type of collimation was being used.

CBCT training

One-hundred and forty-two participants (46.6%) stated that they had undergone CBCT training. The most common avenues were through continuing education courses (26.4%), postgraduate training (26.1%), or through vendors/sales representatives (23.4%). A smaller proportion underwent on-the-job training (12.6%) or learnt about CBCT in their undergraduate dental studies (11.1%).

Dentists with 6-30 years of practice were more likely to undergo CBCT training compared to other groups (0-5 years and more than 30 years of practice) (P = .009). Conversely, GPs were less likely to undergo CBCT training (31.8%) compared to specialists (60.9%, P < .001) and GPs with additional training (61.4%, P < .001).

We asked participants about the duration of training in the following aspects of CBCT: (1) acquisition including patient preparation, positioning, and scanning; (2) clinical indications; (3) use of viewing software; (4) interpretation of anatomy and pathology; (5) technical aspects including field of view (FOV), voxel sizes, and exposure parameters; and (6) importance of radiation safety and strategies to keep doses low.

The area in which the greatest number of the 142 respondents had no training was “Image acquisition” (n = 35, 24.6%). Most of the responses otherwise fell in the “up to 2 h” category across all topics. Conversely, topics in which dentists received more than 2 h of training were in “Clinical indications” and “CBCT interpretation” (both n = 62, 43.7%), “Use of viewing software” (n = 60, 42.3%), “Radiation safety” (n = 46, 32.4%), “Technical aspects” (n = 44, 31%), and “Image acquisition” (n = 36, 25.4%) (Figure 1).

Dentists’ training duration in each aspect of cone-beam CT (CBCT) imaging.

When asked about the aspects of CBCT imaging that dentists would be interested in receiving further training in, 9.1% of the 305 respondents reported that they were not interested in further training. Of the remaining respondents, the topics of interest in decreasing order were “Interpretation” (26.8%), “Use of viewing software” (20.7%), “Technical aspects” (16.2%), “Clinical indications” (13.4%), “Image acquisition” (11.4%), and “Radiation safety” (11.1%).

Confidence in using CBCT

All 305 participants were asked about their confidence in using CBCT, regardless of whether they were CBCT users. Dentists indicated that they were “Very confident” and “Quite confident” in “Clinical indications” (65.2%) and “Radiation safety” (48.5%), followed by “Use of viewing software” (46.9%), “Image acquisition” (37.7%), “CBCT interpretation” (31.5%), and “Technical aspects” (26.6%) (Figure 2).

Dentists’ confidence levels in each aspect of cone-beam CT (CBCT) imaging.

Males have higher confidence levels in all aspects of CBCT imaging. The number of males who reported being “Very confident” was double that of females, and the number of females reporting “Little confidence” was double that of males (P < .05). Dentists who had undergone CBCT training were likely to report being “Very confident” rather than “Little confidence” (P < .001). Among the different dental practitioners, GPs were more likely to report low confidence levels compared to GPs with additional training and specialists.

Current CBCT usage

Our study found that 219 (71.8%) dentists were CBCT users, and 44.8% acquired CBCTs themselves. Among the rest, CBCT acquisition was performed by a radiographer/radiation technologist (32.0%), referred to another practice (10%), done by another dentist in the practice (6.2%), oral health therapist (OHT) (1.7%), dental assistant (4.6%), or the CBCT vendor (0.4%).

Most dentists took 0-5 CBCT scans monthly (74.9%). Implant planning was the most common indication (24.8%), followed by pathology (19.5%) and impacted third molars (19.5%). Other less common indications included impacted teeth other than third molars (17.9%), endodontic treatment (11.3%), orthognathic surgery planning (3.8%), temporomandibular joint disorders (1.6%). Uncommon indications included dental caries, orofacial pain, root tip in sinus, cleft patients, and perio-endo lesions, contributing to 1.5% of CBCT scans.

We also looked at trends associated with CBCT usage. Males were more likely than females to use CBCT (82.8% vs 61.9%, P < .001). Dentists with 0-5 years and 31-40 years of training were least likely to use CBCT compared to all other age groups.

Specialists and GPs with additional training were more likely to use CBCT compared to GPs (P < .001). Dentists who had undergone CBCT training, those working in tertiary institutions or private practice, and those who reported higher overall confidence levels (51%-100%) were also more likely to order CBCT scans (P < .001). However, the number of CBCT scans per month was not associated with the type of dental practitioner nor the place of practice.

Adoption of best practices in CBCT usage

Among CBCT users, 88.6% ordered CBCT scans only after performing a clinical examination, and 70.8% do so after taking conventional 2D radiographs first. Less than half (43.8%) adapt the FOV for all cases, but most of them (84.5%) do so for at least half of their cases. Less than a third (27.9%) of CBCT users specify a resolution (voxel size) for the clinical indication in all their cases. When we looked at whether they specified voxel sizes for at least half of their cases, the proportion increased to 47.9% (Figure 3).

Adoption of best practices in cone-beam CT (CBCT) usage.

GPs with additional training were more likely to adapt FOVs compared to GPs and specialists (P = .024). Oral and maxillofacial surgeons and orthodontists were less likely to adapt FOVs compared to other specialists, although this was not statistically significant (P = .061). Endodontists were more likely to specify voxel sizes compared to other specialists (P = .001).

CBCT interpretation

Among the 219 CBCT users, almost half (47.9%) interpreted CBCT scans themselves; 37.9% referred some scans and 14.2% referred all their scans to a medical or oral maxillofacial surgeon, radiologist, or pathologist for interpretation.

When asked how frequently they would refer scans to a CBCT reading service if it was available, 33.3% responded “All the time” or “Almost always,” 37.5% responded “Frequently” or “Sometimes,” while 29.2% responded “Rarely” or “Never.” Female dentists, dentists who practiced for less than 20 years, and those who had fewer hours of training and were less confident in CBCT interpretation were more likely to refer all their CBCT scans for evaluation if a CBCT reading service was available (P < .01).

Of 219 dentists, 71.7% correctly stated that the entire CBCT scan should be evaluated; 12.8% and 9.1% of dentists felt that they were only responsible for the dentoalveolar region or only the region of interest, respectively, and 6.4% responded that they were not sure. Dentists who had previously undergone more than 2 h of training in CBCT interpretation (P = .01) and practiced for less than 20 years (P < .01) were more likely to be aware that the entire CBCT scan must be evaluated.

Discussion

As of 2023, there were a total of 2417 dental radiography machines licensed by Singapore’s NEA for dental imaging [Data provided by NEA, personal communications]. In NEA’s records, dental radiography machines are licensed under one of the following categories: “Dental x-ray,” “Dental x-ray (Intraoral),” “Dental x-ray (OPG/Ceph)” or “Dental x-ray (CBCT),” and these categories are selected by licensees during the registration process.

There were 222 records listed under the category: “Dental x-ray,” of which 210 records were reclassified by the study team into the 3 remaining categories based on the manufacturer and model information provided. For extraoral radiography models that were upgradable to CBCT, the study team classified them as “Dental x-ray (OPG/Ceph)” as it was uncertain whether the units had been upgraded. After accounting for 12 machines which could not be identified, there were 2405 machines, consisting of 1499 intraoral, 697 extraoral panoramic/cephalometric and 209 CBCT units. With 2065 registered dentists7 this represents a crude ratio of just over 1 dental imaging unit per dentist in the country, and approximately 1 CBCT for every 10 dentists.

Considering the high concentration of dental imaging apparatus in this country, it is important to evaluate if users adopt best practices in their use of 2D and 3D dental imaging. The objective of this survey was to identify current practices and knowledge gaps in dental imaging to determine training needs for dentists in Singapore.

IO imaging

Among our respondents, 88.9% use digital imaging exclusively for IO radiography, while an additional 6.6% use both digital imaging and film. This is similar to recent surveys in Canada (89%),8 Korea (89%),9 Belgium (90%),10 Sweden (98%),11 and Australia, (82.5%)12 and is higher compared to Hong Kong (65.4%)13 (Table S1).

Among the 34 dentists (11.1%) who still used film for some or all of their cases, the most frequent reasons for not using digital receptors were that they did not have control over how the clinic was equipped or how the radiograph was taken (n = 23), cost of converting to a digital set-up (n = 9), or no perceived need to convert to digital radiography (n = 6).

Only 17 dentists (5.6%) used RC. There were no identifiable trends among these respondents in terms of their years of practice, or whether they were GPs, residents, and specialists, although 7 out of 11 of the latter 2 categories were Endodontics specialists or residents.

The usage of RC in Singapore (5.6%) is lower than what was reported in Turkey (9.1%),14 Canada (10.2%),8 Hong Kong (13.0%),13 and Korea (19.0%),9 and similar to Australia (5%)12 (Table S1). The most frequent reasons stated among the 288 non-RC users were that they did not have control over how the clinic was equipped or how the radiograph was taken (n = 192), they did not know that RC existed (n = 115), or they had never undergone any previous training in using RC (n = 69). There were fewer concerns regarding associated technical challenges and imaging errors (n = 28).

In Singapore, RC was introduced in the country’s only dental school in 2019 and is now the default for bitewings and periapical radiography in the undergraduate clinic. The number of dentists using RC is expected to increase as more graduates are aware of the associated dose reduction benefits,15,16 and are familiar with its usage. However, the adoption of RC will still be dependent on whether it is made available at their workplaces.

CBCT scan acquisition

Clinicians are encouraged to adopt low-dose protocols aimed at balancing dose and image quality in an indication dependent and patient specific manner.17 Low-dose protocols involve adjusting FOVs, tube currents, voxel sizes, exposure times, and scanning trajectories.18 Other studies which evaluated FOV and voxel adjustments reported a wide range from 46.4% to 97% and 46% to 72%, respectively (Table S1).8–14,19–26

In our study, dentists reported lowest confidence levels in “Technical aspects” of CBCT imaging. This lack of confidence could account for the suboptimal adherence to low-dose strategies, where less than half (43.8%) of the CBCT users routinely adapt their FOV for all CBCT scans, and an even lesser proportion (27.9%) specify voxel sizes for all their cases.

When only considering the 123 CBCT users who had undergone training in technical aspects of CBCT, there were 61 dentists who did not adapt FOV for all their cases. The most reported reason for not adapting the FOV in this group was the concern that the region of interest (ROI) might be missed if the FOV was too small. The scout preview function of CBCT circumvents this problem as it serves as a useful tool for centralization of the ROI.27 This underscores the importance of training operators to use the scout view before exposing the patient to the full dose required for volumetric image acquisition.

While smaller voxel sizes allow for visualization of fine details, there is a trade-off of increased noise and mAs is increased to compensate for this.28 Voxel size should be carefully selected to produce diagnostically useful images while not subjecting the patient to increased radiation. Of the above 123 CBCT users, we found that 45.5% adjusted voxel size less than half of the time. The prevailing reason chosen was that their clinical indications only required standard voxel sizes, which comprised implant planning, wisdom teeth surgery, pathology evaluation, and orthognathic surgery planning. Several others were not aware that voxel size adjustment was possible or did not feel the need to do so. Conversely, all endodontists adjusted voxel sizes more than half the time, which is unsurprising given the need to visualize detailed information in endodontic CBCT scans. This was also reflected in a study conducted among endodontists in Australia and New Zealand (NZ), where most surveyed used voxel sizes smaller than 150 μm.19

In our study, oral and maxillofacial surgeons and orthodontists were less likely to adapt FOVs compared to other specialists. One possible explanation could be the use of CBCT for pre-orthognathic surgical evaluation, where the entire skull has to be imaged and collimation of the FOV is not required. Similarly, in clinical scenarios where multifocal pathoses are anticipated, a wider FOV may be necessary to capture all lesions present.

CBCT image interpretation

A systematic review in 2019 reported that the overall frequency of incidental findings on CBCT ranges from 24.6% to as high as 94.3%.29 The majority are found in areas beyond dentoalveolar perimeters, such as the airway, paranasal sinuses, temporomandibular joints, and calcified carotid arteries. Given the high incidence of “incidentalomas,” including malignancies in 1.4% of cases, a minimum standard of competency should be expected for CBCT interpretation.

So, who is responsible for findings within a CBCT scan? In Europe, the EADMFR guideline separates “the Prescriber” (the professional who makes the referral for imaging), from “the Practitioner” (the person who makes the radiation exposure), and the latter also reports all findings on the CBCT scan.30 In the United States, a 2008 AAOMR position statement that the person performing the scan must interpret the whole case, and they would be held to the same standards as specialists if any findings were missed.31

In Singapore, the Healthcare Services (Outpatient Dental Service) Regulations 2023 mandates that CBCT images must be interpreted by either a dentist who has been certified to be competent in reading CBCTs, or a medical radiologist.32 OMR is not a recognized specialty in Singapore and options to refer to OMR specialists for CBCT reporting are limited, unlike in countries such as Sweden and Norway, where a radiologist's report is required.20,21 Based on our survey, almost half of the CBCT users (47.9%) report on their own scans without referring for reporting, while 52.1% made referrals sometimes or always. Interestingly, in other countries where radiological reporting services are available, the proportion of users who referred some or all of their scans for reporting was 45.1% in the United Kingdom,22 and 73.1% among endodontists in Australia and NZ,19 indicating a range in the uptake of reporting services (Table S1). In Singapore, 70.7% stated that they would refer some or all their scans for reporting if such a service was available.

Despite legal differences across various jurisdictions, one commonality is the importance placed on CBCT interpretation, and the suggestion (and in some cases, mandates) to refer scans to medical radiologists or oral and maxillofacial radiology (OMR) specialists for formal reporting. The certification process for CBCT usage from acquisition to interpretation in Singapore is still being developed, and guidance may be taken from governing bodies and organizations in other countries (Table S2).30,33–39

Limitations of our survey

This study had a response rate of only 11.7%, although the survey was run for approximately 6 weeks and 2 rounds of emails were sent out. Due to selection bias, CBCT users (71.8% in our study) are likely to be over-represented among our respondents as non-CBCT users may have perceived that the survey was not relevant to them. It is possible that some findings, such as the proportion who have undergone CBCT training may be over-estimated. Other pertinent limitations raised in a similar survey include social desirability bias (answering what they think is correct rather than what they practice), nonresponse bias due to the voluntary nature of surveys and the inability to verify the accuracy of the responses or establish causality in the associations that were tested.8

Conclusions

This survey provides insights in conventional 2D and CBCT imaging that require increased educational efforts. For IO radiography, there should be more emphasis on RC. With respect to CBCT, dentists had the least amount of training and were least confident in image acquisition. They also reported interest in CBCT interpretation and the use of viewing software. Additionally, dentists should also be encouraged to use scout views and adapt FOVs and voxel sizes where appropriate. This training could be provided through continuing education courses through universities, dental societies, or industry partners, and considerations for course development have been presented in our Discussion.8,13,40,41 It also follows that more dentists in Singapore should be encouraged to pursue postgraduate specialist education in OMR to meet the clinical and training needs of the dental population, which will only become more urgent as the usage of CBCT continues to increase.
