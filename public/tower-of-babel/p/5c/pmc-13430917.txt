A pilot study assessing the feasibility of contrast-enhanced ultrasound for triaging fine-needle aspiration in lateral cervical lymph nodes of papillary thyroid carcinoma
Authors: Dai Pan, Yuanbing Xu, Yichao Tao, Fen Huang, Cuiling Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo explore the role of contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) in preoperative ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (US-FNA) of lateral cervical lymph nodes (LCLN) in patients with papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC).MethodsA total of 112 patients with 164 suspicious LCLN were enrolled into two cohorts based on whether they received CEUS examination before US-FNA. In the conventional cohort, 64 nodules from 42 patients underwent direct US-FNA, while in the CEUS-triage cohort, 100 nodules from 70 patients performed CEUS examination first. Among these, 28 homogeneously-centrifugal enhanced nodules were exempt from FNA, and 72 nodules proceeded to US-FNA. The effectiveness of FNA and the cumulative recurrence rate (CRR) during the follow-up period were compared between the two cohorts. The CEUS characteristics of metastatic LCLN were also summarized.ResultsThere was a significant difference in the rate of US-FNA performed between the CEUS-triage cohort and the conventional cohort (72.0% vs. 100.0%, p < 0.001). The CEUS-triage cohort had a lower uncertainty rate of FNA (6.9% vs. 18.8%, p = 0.04). All patients were followed up for an average of 38.4 months. There was no significant difference in the CRR between the two groups in the entire cohort and the surgical population (all p > 0.05). CEUS of metastatic LCLN in PTC showed centripetal enhancement, hypo-enhancement, heterogeneous enhancement, and perfusion defects (all p < 0.001).ConclusionsCEUS may have the potential to serve as a valuable triage tool for US-FNA of suspicious LCLN in PTC patients, potentially reducing unnecessary puncture rates and increasing the overall diagnostic yield of biopsies. Features such as centripetal enhancement, hypo-enhancement, heterogeneous enhancement, and perfusion defects observed on CEUS were more frequently linked to metastatic lymph nodes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16243-4.

Introduction

Papillary thyroid cancer (PTC), while often indolent, is frequently associated with cervical lymph node metastases (CLNM), which occur in 20%-80% of patients and represent the most common site of disease recurrence [1, 2]. The extent of surgical lymph node dissection is critically guided by preoperative imaging evaluation of the central and lateral neck compartments. Inadequate dissection risks regional recurrence, whereas overly extensive surgery increases morbidity [3, 4]. Although intraoperative ultrasound can provide supplementary information, accurate preoperative identification of metastatic lateral cervical lymph nodes (LCLN) remains a pivotal yet challenging task, as it directly impacts surgical planning and patient outcomes.

Conventional ultrasonography (US) suggestive of suspicious metastatic lymph nodes include enlargement, loss of the fatty hilum, a rounded rather than oval shape, hyper-echogenicity, cystic change, microcalcifications, and peripheral vascularity [5, 6]. However, the metastatic lymph nodes and other cervical lymph nodes diseases always appear overlapping characteristics, there is a degree of difficulty in the diagnosis using US. Ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (US-FNA) for suspicious cervical lymph nodes is widely recommended by experts, although the indications for FNA differ among various guidelines [7, 8]. Despite its widespread use, US-FNA is not without limitations, including diagnostic uncertainty and the potential for insufficient biopsy samples, which can compromise diagnostic accuracy and increase the likelihood of non-diagnostic punctures [9]. To address these limitations, many researchers advocate for the use of thyroglobulin (Tg) measurement in the eluate of the FNA needle (FNA-Tg) as an adjunctive diagnostic tool [10]. Elevated FNA-Tg levels are associated with a higher risk of lymph node malignancy. However, variations in detection reagents and methods can influence the diagnostic cut-off values for FNA-Tg, necessitating standardization for consistent clinical application.

CEUS is a practical imaging technique that utilizes intravascular microbubble-based contrast agents to improve blood echogenicity, allowing for the real-time assessment of tissue microperfusion. It has demonstrated significant value in evaluating metastatic lymph nodes [11, 12]. Some studies reported that CEUS may be a valuable technology in differentiating between benign and malignant lymph nodes [13, 14]. This diagnostic utility is supported by evidence across multiple cancers: in breast cancer, homogeneous high-signal enhancement on CEUS is used as a visual criterion for “negative” nodes [15]. In head and neck (oral) cancer, a majority (80.5%) of benign lymph nodes present with a homogeneous enhancement pattern [16]. And in thyroid cancer, a key study found that 82.8% of benign cervical lymph nodes exhibited homogeneous enhancement, with centrifugal perfusion as the predominant benign pattern [14]. This modality is particularly advantageous for evaluating micro-perfusion and reflecting the internal vascularity of lesions in real time, allowing for clearer visualization of abnormal areas compared to conventional US [17]. Therefore, CEUS has the potential to be used to guide US-FNA operations.

Building upon the established association of homogeneous centrifugal enhancement with benign pathology, we hypothesized that this specific CEUS pattern could serve as a reliable criterion to triage patients, thereby safely reducing unnecessary FNA procedures. To test this hypothesis, we conducted a retrospective cohort study. Patients with suspicious LCLN were allocated into two groups: a CEUS-triage cohort, in which lymph nodes exhibiting homogeneous centrifugal enhancement were assigned to active surveillance (FNA exemption), and a conventional cohort that underwent standard US-FNA management. The primary objective was to evaluate the safety of this CEUS-based triage strategy by comparing the long-term cumulative recurrence rates (CRR) between the two cohorts. Additionally, we assessed its potential clinical efficacy by analyzing the reduction in the rate of non-diagnostic or indeterminate FNA results. This study aims to determine whether CEUS can be utilized as an effective pre-FNA triage tool to optimize the diagnostic pathway for LCLN in PTC.

Materials and methods

Study population

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiaogan Central hospital (date: January 22, 2024, number: KY-2024012302) and this study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent prior to their examination. From January 2020 and January 2022, 164 suspicious lateral compartments (cervical lymph node levels II–V, scanning from the level of the submandibular glands to the clavicles, > 0.5 cm) from 112 PTC patients were enrolled. Phase 1 (Conventional cohort, 2020.01-2020.06, 64 suspicious lymph nodes): All eligible patients with suspicious LCLN underwent direct US-FNA as per standardized diagnosis and treatment at that time. Phase 2 (CEUS-triage cohort, 2020.06-2022.01, 100 suspicious lymph nodes): Based on promising pilot data from our early CEUS experience on LCLN, we prospectively applied the CEUS-based triage tool to all eligible patients (Table S1). In this phase, suspicious LCLN first underwent CEUS. Those with a homogeneous-centrifugal enhancement pattern entered surveillance group, while others underwent US-FNA (Fig. 1).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (ⅰ) all LCLN (level II-V) had at least one of the suspicious characteristics (cystic area, hyper-echogenicity, microcalcifications, peripheral vascularization, absence of echogenic fatty hilum), and all LCLN of CEUS-triage cohort were detected by CEUS; (ⅱ) FNA were performed within 2 weeks before thyroid surgery; (ⅲ) all of the patients obtained entire pathology results (cytological or histological pathology). Exclusion criteria included patients with tuberculosis or other head and neck malignancies or prior thyroidectomy for primary PTC, pregnancy and any contraindication to CEUS.

Conventional US examination and CEUS examination

EPIQ5 (Philips Medical System, Netherlands) equipped with a 5- to 12- MHz linear probe or Resona 7 (Mindray Medical System, Shenzhen, Guangzhou, China) equipped with a 5- to 14- MHz probe was used for both conventional US and CEUS examinations. The operator was a radiologist with 20 years of experience in US imaging and 10 years of practical experience in US-FNA. The detail sonographic features were discussed by two experienced radiologists.

All of the patients underwent a US examination of the cervical regions. Conventional US was used to assess the lateral cervical area in both transverse and longitudinal sections. All size measurements and subsequent CEUS evaluations were additionally performed on the longitudinal section of each lymph node [17]. LCLN with at least one of the following characteristics: cystic area, hyper-echogenicity, microcalcifications, peripheral vascularization or absence of echogenic fatty hilum were identified as suspicious metastatic LNs. The location and image characteristics of the suspicious LNs were recorded. The US images and dynamic photographs were saved.

The contrast agent used in this study was prepared by suspending SonoVue (Bracco, Milan, Italy) in 5 mL of normal saline and gently shaking to form a homogeneous microbubble suspension. A low mechanical index of 0.07 was selected, and 2.4 mL SonoVue suspension was rapidly injected through a three-way tap via the elbow superficial vein, followed by 5 mL saline flush. The perfusion process was continuously monitored for 2–3 min and dynamic images were saved to disk. The contrast-enhanced characteristics were registered as enhancement pattern, degree of enhancement, enhancement homogeneity and present or absence of a perfusion defect. The nodal enhancement pattern was defined as centrifugal perfusion, centripetal perfusion and chaotic perfusion according to the first filling position of the contrast agent. At peak enhancement, the degree of enhancement was assessed and classified as hypo-enhancement, iso-enhancement, hyper-enhancement, or non-enhancement in comparison to the adjacent tissue. According to the distribution of contrast agent at peak enhancement, enhancement homogeneity was classified as homogeneous or heterogeneous. Take note of whether or not there is a non-perfusion area present in the nodal. Centripetal or chaotic perfusion, hypo-enhancement or non-enhancement, heterogeneous enhancement and the presence of perfusion defect area were identified as suspicious metastatic LNs characteristics (Fig. 2).

Conventional ultrasound often fails to detect deep cervical lymph nodes in regions like the level IIb/VII area, retropharyngeal space, and upper mediastinum due to the bone-gas interface [18]. For patients with a high suspicion of cervical lymph node metastasis, it was recommended to implement a “second-look” ultrasound assessment of the suspected areas by combining contrast-enhanced CT imaging guidance, and performed FNA testing when feasible.

US-FNA technique

At our institution, US-FNA was performed in all patients by two senior sonographers with more than 10 years of experience. After CEUS examination and image analysis, FNA was performed under conventional B-mode ultrasound guidance. The needle was directed towards the target area (e.g., a region with perfusion defect or heterogeneous enhancement) that was identified during the prior CEUS scan. FNA was conducted using a 23G puncture needle (Hakko, Osaka, Japan) employing the lifting-thrusting technique. Each puncture involved 10–15 lifting and thrusting motions, with each lymph node being punctured an average of three times. To accommodate lymph node mobility and ensure adequate specimen collection, the operator adjusted the needle in multiple directions and angles. The puncture specimens were immediately smeared on glass slides and air-dried naturally. If the initial sampling was unsatisfactory, a second puncture was performed to ensure quality. Patients were instructed to apply pressure to the puncture site with tampons for at least 15 min post-FNA to prevent hematoma. All patient imaging data were meticulously preserved (Fig. 3).

FNA-Tg testing was performed at the request of clinician or at the discretion of cytopathologists. A total of 58 nodules in the CEUS-triage cohort were tested using FNA-TG, while 36 nodules in the conventional cohort were tested for Tg in the puncture eluate. The cut-off value for FNA-Tg was set to 1.0 ng/mL, < 1.0 ng/mL was defined negative, and ≥ 1.0 ng/mL was considered positive [19].

Surgical procedures and postoperative management

The cytological diagnosis results of LN-FNA can be divided into six grades, according to the Bethesda reporting system [19, 20]. Bethesda Class I-II LCLN were defined benign, automatically entered into the follow-up cohort. Bethesda Class III were considered uncertainty, and stratified according to FNA-Tg, follow-up is recommended if < 1.0 ng/mL, and upgrade to Class IV while ≥ 1.0 ng/mL. Lymph node biopsy was performed for nodes categorized as Bethesda Class IV. Suspicious lymph nodes were examined via frozen section analysis. If malignancy was confirmed, radical neck dissection was conducted. For patients with Bethesda Class V-VI, radical neck dissection was performed directly. The ultrasound reports included a detailed description of the anatomical location and adjacent structures of the LCLN that underwent FNA, which were also marked on the body surface. During the procedure, 0.1 ~ 0.2 mL of 1.0% methylene blue (Jiangsu Jumpcan Pharma-ceutical Co., Taixing, Jiangsu, China) was injected into the FNA LNs according to the FNA operator’s instructions. LNs dissection began immediately, with the surgeon locating and removing the blue-stained LNs with needle holes, which were then placed in separate specimen bags. The gold standard for final diagnosis was histologic pathologic results of paraffin-embedded sections (Fig. 4).

We conducted a focused review of all LNs in the follow-up cohort over a six-month period. A secondary US-FNA of suspicious LNs was performed if either of the following conditions was met: (i) the maximum diameter of lymph nodes increased by more than 20%, (ii) lymph node volume increased by 30%. All patients received postoperative thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) suppression therapy, and those at medium to high risk of recurrence were treated with radioiodine 131. Complete follow-up data were available for all patients. We calculated CRR of entire cohort and surgical population, defining disease recurrence as new lesions in the surgical area confirmed by pathology or distant organ metastases suggested by imaging or pathology.

Statistical methods

SPSS version 25.0 software package (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for all statistical analyses. Quantitative variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and median. The χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was performed to compare categorical variables. The cumulative recurrence risk was analyzed by Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate significance.

Results

Study population

A total of 164 lymph nodes (conventional cohort: 64 lesions and CEUS-triage cohort: 100 lesions) in 112 PTC patients were enrolled in this study (Table 1). Mean patient age in CEUS-triage cohort was 46.4 years (19 ~ 68). Of which, 72.9%were female, and 27.1% were male. The size of the primary thyroid tumor and suspicious lymph nodes were 14.9 mm and 16.0 mm in the CEUS-triage cohort, and 15.1 mm and 16.1 mm in the conventional cohort, respectively, with no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05). FNA-Tg was detected in 58.0% nodules in the CEUS-triage cohort and 56.3% nodules in the conventional cohort, the comparison of FNA-Tg and Serum Tg levels between the two cohorts showed no statistical significance (all P > 0.05). The ultrasound features of the two cohorts in the target lymph nodes, including fatty hilum, microcalcifications, cystic area, hyper echogenicity, and peripheral vascularization did not show a statistically significant difference (all P > 0.05).

US-FNA and surgical pathology

In the conventional cohort, 64 suspicious lymph nodes were directly treated with US-FNA. 16 nodules were determined to be cytologically benign, 12 nodules were uncertainty, and 36 nodules were determined to be malignant and underwent surgical biopsy or radical resection. The 16 benign nodules and 12 uncertainty nodules were enrolled into the follow-up cohort. Among them, 14 nodules showed abnormal changes and underwent a second US-FNA during the follow-up period. Of which 5 nodules were diagnosed to be malignant and subsequently received surgical treatment.

In the CEUS-triage cohort, 28 suspicious lymph nodes underwent CEUS examination and exhibited a homogeneous-centrifugal enhancement pattern. These were identified as non-metastatic nodules, and exemption from FNA, automatically entered into the follow-up cohort. The remaining 72 lymph nodes underwent US-FNA, with 19 nodules determined to be benign, 5 nodules uncertainty, and 48 nodules malignant, which subsequently underwent surgical biopsy or radical resection. The 19 benign nodules and 5 uncertainty nodules were enrolled in the postoperative follow-up cohort. Among them, 8 nodules underwent a second US-FNA due to abnormal changes during the follow-up period, and 2 of those were considered malignant and underwent surgical treatment.

Clinical translation of FNA under CEUS guidance

A total of 64 nodules (100.0%) in conventional cohort and 72 nodules (72.0%) in CEUS-triage cohort underwent US-FNA examination. The malignant, benign, and uncertainty rates in conventional cohort were 56.3%, 25.0%, and 18.8%, respectively, while those in CEUS-triage cohort were 66.7%, 26.4%, and 6.9%, respectively. There was no statistical difference between the malignant and benign diagnostic rates between the two cohorts (p = 0.21; p = 0.85). Notably, the CEUS-triage cohort had a lower FNA uncertainty rate (p = 0.04). 28.0% of the LCLN were exempted from FNA examination in CEUS-triage cohort, there was a statistically significant difference in the FNA exemption rate comparing with the conventional cohort (p < 0.001). In addition, the FNA effective rate in CEUS-triage cohort was 93.1%, and that in conventional cohort was 81.3%. There was a statistically significant difference between the two cohorts (p = 0.04). In the follow-up cohort, 14 nodules in conventional cohort and 8 nodules in CEUS-triage cohort underwent a second US-FNA. The malignant rate of conventional cohort was 35.7%, while that of CEUS-triage cohort was 25.0%, there was no significant difference (p = 0.49) (Table 2).

Analysis of prognosis

All patients received postoperative TSH suppression therapy. Postoperative radioiodine 131 therapy was administered to 26 cases (61.9%) in the conventional cohort and 40 cases (57.1%) in the CEUS-triage cohort. All patients were followed up after surgery, with the follow-up period ending on March 1, 2024. The mean follow-up time was 38.4 months (range: 14–51 months). There was total 20 cases of recurrence in two cohorts. In the conventional cohort, 5 cases were confirmed by a second US-FNA biopsy of suspicious lymph nodes in the lateral cervical region, 3 cases were residual thyroid recurrences, 1 case was lung metastasis (diagnosed by imaging), and 1 case was bone metastasis (diagnosed by imaging). In the CEUS-triage cohort, 2 cases were diagnosed as suspicious lymph nodes in the lateral cervical region by US-FNA, 6 cases were residual thyroid recurrences, and 2 cases were lung metastases (diagnosed by imaging).

We analyzed the entire cohort and the surgical population group separately. The CRR in both the entire cohort and the surgical population did not differ significantly between the two cohorts (p = 0.18 and p = 0.26, respectively) (Fig. 5).

CEUS features of metastatic lymph nodes

The rate of centripetal enhancement was 70.8% in metastatic LNs and 5.3% in non-metastatic LNs. The majority of non-metastatic LNs (84.2%) manifested centrifugal. The rate of hypo-enhancement in metastatic LNs was 75.0%, but only 10.5% in non-metastatic LNs. Hyper-enhancement was seen in 73.7% of non-metastatic LNs. Heterogeneous enhancement was present in 85.4% of metastatic LNs, while 21.1% in non-metastatic LNs. Perfusion defect was present in 66.7% of metastatic LNs, while 21.1% in non-metastatic LNs. The statistically significant CEUS features of metastatic LNs were centripetal enhancement, hypo-enhancement, heterogeneous enhancement, perfusion defect (all p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

CLNM in thyroid cancer are highly related with surgical options, local recurrence and distant metastasis [21]. As a result, it is critical to precisely evaluate suspicious CLNM prior to surgery. Since the traditional standard radical resection of PTC includes central cervical lymph node dissection, preoperative evaluation of LCLN is particularly important [22]. This evaluation is crucial as it impacts the prognosis of patients and significantly affects their postoperative quality of life.

Currently, contrast-enhanced CT is frequently used to evaluate lateral cervical lymph node metastasis in thyroid cancer. However, its high false positive rate limits its clinical application. CEUS can partially replicate the microvascular perfusion imaging capabilities of contrast-enhanced CT and offers unique advantages in assessing the characteristics of lymph nodes. In this study, all LCLN in the CEUS-triage cohort underwent CEUS examination. Huang et al. reported that the CEUS enhancement patterns of non-metastatic lymph nodes were predominantly homogeneous (82.8%) and centrifugal (68.2%). In our study, we utilized the homogeneous-centrifugal enhancement pattern as a stratification criterion for CEUS. Homogeneous-centrifugal enhancement of LCLN were identified as non-metastatic, and US-FNA was not recommended for those nodes. In the CEUS-triage cohort, 28.0% of LCLN were exempt from FNA examination, and FNA efficiency rate was higher under effective CEUS guidance. During a 3-year follow-up, none of the 28 nodules showed abnormal signs. These findings suggest that CEUS can effectively reduce the number of unnecessary FNA procedures.

CEUS patterns can help differentiate between metastatic nodes (centripetal enhancement, hypo-enhancement, heterogeneous enhancement, perfusion defect) and non-metastatic nodes (homogeneous-centrifugal enhancement). This is a critical clinical decision point that conventional ultrasound often cannot resolve. Notably, CEUS has the potential to reduce the rate of uncertainty in LCLN. Our study revealed that the uncertainty rate of FNA in the CEUS-triage cohort was significantly lower than that in the conventional cohort (6.9% vs. 18.8%, p = 0.04). To further clarify the reasons, we compared the baseline data from the two cohorts of uncertain cases and observed a different in the size of LCLN. The comparison suggested the importance of prioritizing CEUS assessment for cases with smaller suspected CLNM. Meanwhile, CEUS effectively reveals the microvascular perfusion patterns of nodules, differentiates between cystic and solid regions, and assesses the distribution of blood flow. which can provide more information for the subsequent puncture, such as the puncture path, puncture site, etc. All those help to reduce the uncertainty rate of FNA in the CEUS-triage cohort. Furthermore, we found that FNA-Tg in recurrent cases showed an increasing trend in the uncertainty cases. This implied that dynamic monitoring of FNA-Tg levels was an effective method for FNA indeterminate nodules. This study also observed that the CEUS-triage cohort exhibited a higher rate of malignancy detected by FNA, although the difference was not statistically significant. This trend may be attributed to the more precise targeting of the puncture area under the guidance of CEUS. Several studies have shown similar views [23, 24]. Yin et al. reported that CEUS improved specimen adequacy for FNA in high-risk thyroid nodules by avoiding unnecessary FNAs of non-enhancing nodules and accurately locating viable tissue for precise, real-time guidance [24].

Our study followed the entire cohort for an average of 38.4 months. In the CEUS-triage cohort, 28.0% of the nodules were exempt from FNA, and no definite recurrence was observed during the follow-up period. Recurrences in the conventional cohort were primarily regional lymph node recurrences, whereas the CEUS-triage cohort had more contralateral residual thyroid recurrences. This difference may be related to the surgical methods chosen, with more patients in the CEUS-triage cohort undergoing unilateral thyroid lobectomy (18.8% vs. 11.9%). Both cohorts exhibited low distant recurrence rates (4.8% vs. 2.9%). These follow-up data suggested that a CEUS-guided follow-up strategy is a relatively safe option.

Finally, we compared the CEUS features of metastatic and non-metastatic lymph nodes in the lateral cervical region. Metastatic lymph nodes predominantly exhibited a heterogeneous enhancement pattern (85.4%) and were mostly hypo-enhancing (75.0%). Some metastatic nodules also showed local hyperenhancement and peripheral ring hypo-enhancement, suggesting different regions of metastatic activity. These findings are consistent with those reported by Hong et al. [14]. However, it is important to note that the assessment of internal vascularity and enhancement patterns with CEUS can be technically challenging when the target lymph node is very small (e.g., < 5 mm in diameter) or contains extensive cystic components [17, 25]. Multiple biopsies and aspirations to obtain multipoint samples of each lymph node are advised to increase the FNA diagnostic rate [26]. Furthermore, heterogeneous enhancement regions should be biopsied as significant targets, and perfusion defect sites should be avoided.

CEUS provides critical vascular function information within nodules, aiding in the evaluation of “active areas” and facilitating the selection of FNA targets. CEUS is not only valuable for the targeted localization of FNA, more importantly, as a functional imaging tool, it offers supplementary information, such as blood perfusion, in addition to the anatomical details provided by traditional ultrasound, thereby effectively guiding clinical decision-making.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration when interpreting the findings. Firstly, its single-center, retrospective design with a limited sample size may impact the generalizability of the results and the ability to detect differences in long-term outcomes. Therefore, this study should be viewed as a hypothesis-generating, exploratory investigation rather than a definitive efficacy or safety trial. Secondly, due to the typically slow-growing nature of PTC, the median follow-up of 38.4 months, while offering initial insights, is inadequate for drawing conclusive assessments regarding very long-term oncological safety. These constraints underscore the necessity for further research but do not diminish the significance of this initial exploration in proposing a potential new diagnostic approach.

Conclusions

CEUS may have the potential to serve as a valuable triage tool for US-FNA of suspicious LCLN in PTC patients, potentially reducing unnecessary puncture rates and increasing the overall diagnostic yield of biopsies. Features such as centripetal enhancement, hypo-enhancement, heterogeneous enhancement, and perfusion defects observed on CEUS were more frequently linked to metastatic lymph nodes.
