KLF9 Inhibits Brain Metastasis of Non‐Small Cell Lung Cancer by Regulating Ceramide Synthase 1 Synthesis
Authors: Yingqiu He, Junfan Pan, Sufang Chen, Ying Lin, Peiwei She, Yusheng Chen, Nengluan Xu, Hongru Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe regulatory mechanisms driving brain metastasis (BM) in non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) are complex, with Ceramide synthase 1 (Cers1) playing a critical role. However, the upstream factors controlling Cers1 expression remain unclear. Additionally, Kruppel‐like factor 9 (KLF9) has been implicated as a potential tumor suppressor transcription factor (TF) in lung cancer. Our study aims to explore the physiological effects and molecular mechanisms of Cers1 upstream regulatory TFs in NSCLC BM, focusing on the link between KLF9 and Cers1. TFs regulating Cers1 expression were screened via GENECARD and UCSC databases. KLF9 expression and survival analysis in NSCLC were analyzed using TCGA and GEO data sets. Dual‐luciferase assays were conducted to investigate the specific binding site of KLF9 on the Cers1 promoter. KLF9 genetic modulation impact on NSCLC BM was assessed through in vitro assays for proliferation, migration, and invasion, along with in vivo orthotopic xenograft models to evaluate tumor growth and metastasis. Our study revealed a significant downregulation of KLF9 in vitro, correlating with poor prognosis. KLF9 has a direct positive transcriptional regulation on Cers1, particularly on its promoter region (−711 nt/+22 nt). In vitro, KLF9 overexpression inhibited the ability of cells to penetrate a blood–brain barrier model. Moreover, in vivo experiments demonstrated a marked suppression of BM tumor formation upon KLF9 expression. This study reports KLF9's direct control over Cers1 expression by acting on its promoter. KLF9 demonstrates inhibitory effects on NSCLC BM, presenting a promising therapeutic avenue for NSCLC BM treatment.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death worldwide [1]. Non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) covers approximately 80%–85% of lung cancers [2]. In patients with advanced NSCLC, the incidence of brain metastases (BM) can rise to 44%, with a higher prevalence observed among those diagnosed with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) [2]. Despite considerable advancements in therapeutic approaches, the outcomes remain far from satisfactory. Therefore, it is particularly important to explore novel driver genes associated with BM in NSCLC and investigate potential targeted drugs.

Ceramide (Cer) is a core component of sphingolipid metabolism, exerting significant control over crucial processes such as autophagy, cell death, and stress responses. Ceramide is synthesized via the enzymatic activity of six ceramide synthases (Cers1‐6), each with distinct substrate specificity [3]. Cers1, the first mammalian Cers identified, catalyzes the N‐acylation reaction of sphingosine to produce C18‐ceramide and has been shown to exert an anti‐tumor proliferation role in a variety of tumors [4, 5]. While previous reports have identified the role of Cers in brain metastasis, there is currently limited information available regarding this specific context. In our previous study, significant downregulation of Cers1 was observed in NSCLC cell lines and BM tissues [6]. Conversely, Cers1 increased expression was linked to improved prognoses, inhibiting BM progression by interacting with ubiquitin‐specific protease 14 (USP14) and downregulating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. However, the precise upstream regulatory mechanism governing Cers1 remains elusive.

During the metastasis process, tumor cells undergo a multistep biological transformation, with transcription factors (TFs) known to participate in carcinogenesis and the development of different cancers. The Kruppel‐like factor (KLF) family, a group of TFs, regulates diverse biological processes, including cancer, cardiovascular, and nervous system functions. Specifically, KLF9, also known as basic transcription element‐binding protein 1 (BTEB1), belongs to the specificity protein (SP) 1/KLF family and has been identified as a regulator of oncogenesis, exhibiting low expression levels in several tumor types such as colon, breast, gastric, and liver cancers [7]. In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, KLF9 was found to interact with the TCF‐4/β‐catenin complex, leading to the inhibition of WNT pathway activity and subsequent suppression of tumor progression [8]. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that in NSCLC, miR‐141 and miR‐20a‐5p promote cell proliferation and migration by targeting KLF9. Moreover, KLF9 overexpression was reported to counteract the proliferative and invasive effects induced by miR‐20a‐5p in A549 cells [9]. Overall, evidence suggests KLF9 as a potential tumor suppressor gene in lung cancer; however, its role in NSCLC remains insufficiently explored. Further research is needed to clarify KLF9's precise function in NSCLC and its potential role in BM, enhancing our understanding of its therapeutic implications in cancer management.

The present study aims to investigate the regulatory connection between KLF9 and Cers1 in NSCLC BM. Initially identified through bioinformatics analysis, KLF9's role as a potential transcription factor acting on the Cers1 promoter was explored through in vitro assays. Subsequent experiments evaluated KLF9's impact on LUAD BM, revealing downregulation in vitro and a positive correlation with Cers1 expression. Furthermore, KLF9 overexpression inhibited cell migration, invasion, and blood–brain barrier penetration in vitro, and suppressed BM tumor formation in vivo. These findings underscore the potential of targeting the KLF9–Cers1 axis as a promising therapeutic strategy for managing NSCLC BM.

Materials and Methods

Identification of Transcription Factor and Binding Sites in the Cers1 Promoter Through Genomic Analysis

To identify candidate transcription factors (TFs) regulating Cers1, the genomic sequence spanning from 2000 base pairs (bp) upstream to 200 bp downstream of the transcription start site (TSS) was retrieved from the University of California Santa Cruz (UCSC) Genome Browser database (http://genome.ucsc.edu/) and defined as the Cers1 promoter region. This Cers1 promoter sequence was subsequently scanned for potential TF binding sites using the integrated JASPAR Transcription Factors tool within the UCSC Genome Browser. Transcription factors associated with predicted binding sites exhibiting a JASPAR minimum score > 400 were compiled as List A. Concurrently, potential TFs implicated in Cers1 regulation based on published evidence or database predictions were identified by querying the GENECARD database (https://www.genecards.org/) with the search term “Cers1” AND “transcription factor”, resulting in List B. A refined set of candidate TFs (List C) was then generated by identifying the intersection between List A and List B. For each candidate TF in List C, the Cers1 promoter sequence was subjected to precise re‐scanning using the official JASPAR database online tool (https://jaspar.elixir.no/). Binding sites with a JASPAR predicted binding probability score > 8 (corresponding to a relative profile score threshold > 80%, indicating high binding probability) were identified. The core candidate transcription factors and their corresponding high‐confidence binding sites within the Cers1 promoter were ultimately determined.

Bioinformatics Analysis

Transcriptome data and clinical information for 598 LUAD samples and 59 adjacent normal tissues were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (http://cancergenome.nih.gov/) for analysis of KLF9 expression and overall survival (OS). The Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets (GSE10072, GSE31210) were used to analyze the expression level of KLF9 mRNA in LUAD samples. Additionally, the University of Alabama Cancer (UALCAN) portal (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/) was used to analyze the expression levels of transcription factors in both LUAD and adjacent normal tissues. Furthermore, the expression of KLF9 was examined in the UALCAN pan‐cancer analysis section.

Cell Lines and Reagents

The human normal bronchial epithelial cell line (BEAS‐2B) and the LUAD cell lines A549, H1299, PC‐9, and HCC827 were obtained from Procell (Wuhan, China). These cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and antibiotics (1% penicillin/streptomycin), all purchased from Meilun Biotechnology (Dalian, China). These cell lines were maintained in a humidified incubator containing 5% carbon dioxide.

Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay

The binding of transcription factors to the Cers1 promoter was analyzed by dual‐luciferase reporter gene detection experiments. The luciferase reporter gene pGL4‐Cers1, containing the Cers1 promoter, was constructed by Hanheng Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). PC‐9 and H1299 cells seeded in 12‐well plates were transfected with either pcDNA3.1 control or pcDNA3.1‐KLF9 plasmid, followed by co‐transfection with pGL4‐Cers1 and thymidine kinase‐renin vectors (50 ng; Promega). Cells were collected 48 h post‐transfection, washed twice with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), and then dissociated with 200 mL lysis buffer (Promega). Luciferase activity was assessed by a dual‐luciferase reporter system (Promega), with the firefly luciferase signal normalized to the renin luciferase signal.

Quantitative Reverse Transcription‐Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT‐qPCR)

RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and reverse transcribed using PrimeScriptTM RT Reagent Kit (RRO36Q; Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan). SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (RR820A; Takara Bio) was used for qRT‐PCR. Fold changes in gene expression were determined using the 2 − ΔΔCT method and normalized using β‐actin. The primers used in this study are shown in Table S1.

KLF9 Expression Modification in Stable NSCLC Cell Lines

KLF9 overexpression (KLF9‐OE), positive control (VE), KLF9 knockdown (shKLF9), and negative control (shNC) vectors were purchased from Shanghai Gene Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All lentiviral vectors carried the enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) reporter gene. H1299 or PC‐9 cells were seeded into 24‐well plates and transfected with 1 × 108 TU/mL lentivirus. Following incubation at 37°C and 5% carbon dioxide for 24 h, the medium was changed, and cells were further cultured for an additional 48 h. Successful transduction was confirmed by EGFP fluorescence under a microscope. Stable cell lines were selected using puromycin (2 μg/mL) and utilized for all in vivo and in vitro experiments. The sequence of KLF9 shRNA construction is detailed in Table S2.

Western Blotting

LUAD cell lines were used to analyze protein expression. Cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (Thermo, USA) containing protease inhibitors (Beyotime, China) and phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; Beyotime), and the protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Beyotime). Approximately 20–30 μg of protein from each cell lysate was separated by SDS‐PAGE. The proteins were then transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Sigma, USA) and incubated overnight at 4°C with specific primary antibodies. After incubation with the respective secondary antibodies for 1 h, targeted protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (Meilun), and band density was quantified using quantitative software (Bio‐Rad). The primary antibodies used were α‐Tubulin (Immunoway, China), KLF9 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), Cers1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), Bax (abcam, USA), Bcl (abcam, USA), Cleaved Caspase‐3 (Immunoway, China), Caspase‐3 (ABclonal, China), CDK4 (Cell Signaling Technology, USA), CDK6 (Cell Signaling Technology, USA), MMP2 (Proteintech, China) and MMP9 (Proteintech, China). The secondary antibodies used were anti‐rabbit and anti‐mouse polyclonal antibodies (Immunoway, China).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) Staining

IHC staining was performed on formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded nude mouse subcutaneous transplanted tumor tissues to detect the expression of KLF9 proteins. The antibodies used were KLF9 (Santa Cruz). Briefly, tissue sections were incubated with the corresponding primary antibody overnight at 4°C, with normal goat serum serving as a negative control. After washing, tissue sections were incubated for 1 h with biotinylated secondary antibodies (Santa Cruz), followed by incubation with streptavidin‐horseradish peroxidase (HRP) complex (Dako, CA, USA). The sections were then immersed in 3,3‐diaminobenzidine (DAB), counterstained with 10% Mayer's hematoxylin, dehydrated, and then mounted. The evaluation involved scoring based on the percentage of positively stained tumor cells and staining intensity, conducted independently by two experienced pathologists. Lastly, photographs were captured using a Leica light microscope.

Flow Cytometry of Cell Cycle

Cell cycle analysis was conducted using the Cell Cycle Assay kit (Meilun, China). A total of 1 × 106 cells were collected and resuspended in 1 mL PBS to obtain a single‐cell suspension. Subsequently, 3 mL of pre‐chilled absolute ethanol was slowly added to the cells, followed by an overnight incubation at 4°C. The cells were then centrifuged at 1000×g for 3–5 min, and the supernatant was carefully removed. After resuspension in an additional 1 mL of PBS, working solutions containing propidium iodide (PI) and RNase A were prepared for staining. Each cell sample was treated with 500 μL of staining solution, and the resulting red fluorescence, excited at 488 nm, was analyzed by an Accuri C6 flow cytometry after 30 min of incubation at 37°C in the dark.

Assessment of Cell Proliferation

Colony formation assay was used to determine cell proliferation ability. Transfected PC‐9 and H1299 cells (500 cells per well) were seeded in 6‐well plates and cultured under normal conditions for 2 weeks. Cell clones (> 50 cells) were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet (Beyotime), and the number of visible clones was counted under a microscope.

Transwell Migration and Invasion Assays

Migration and invasion assays were performed using transwell chambers with 8.0 μm pore size (Corning, USA). For migration experiments, 1 × 104 cells were resuspended in 200 μL serum‐free RPMI 1640 medium and added to the upper chamber, while 2 × 104 cells were placed in the matrix‐coated upper chamber for invasion experiments. In both experiments, the bottom chamber was filled with RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS. After 24 h, cells on the membrane were stained with 0.5% crystal violet to observe the migration and invasion of cells. Images were captured using a microscope, and cell quantification was performed using ImageJ software.

Construction of a Blood–Brain Barrier (BBB) Model

The in vitro BBB model was established following previously described protocols [6, 10]. Briefly, hCMEC/D3 cells and astrocytes were seeded on both sides of the culture plate. Specifically, 1 × 105 hCMEC/D3 cells were cultured in the upper chamber, while mouse CP‐M157 astrocytes were cultured in the lower chamber. Due to China's COVID‐19 policies at the time, human astrocyte lines were not available. After constructing the BBB model, 200 μL of 1 × 105/mL cells expressing EGFP were added to the upper chamber. After 24 h, cells in the lower chamber were gently washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 20 min. Then, cells were photographed under a fluorescence microscope, and the average number of EGFP‐positive migrating tumor cells was determined in 5 random fields.

Orthotopic Xenograft Model

The protocol for establishing the BM model of lung cancer followed previously described methods [6]. First, mice were anesthetized and a precise 1 mm hole was created in the skull using a syringe needle. Subsequently, 5 × 106 PC‐9 cells stably expressing luciferase were resuspended in 30 μL of serum‐free RPMI 1640 medium and slowly injected into the brain. Body weight was measured every 3 days after surgery. Bioluminescence imaging was performed at week 6 post‐surgery or upon the onset of clinical signs indicative of BM, such as inactivity, weight loss, or hunching. For imaging, each mouse received an intraperitoneal injection of 150 mg/kg luciferin (Peking University, USA) for 10 min, followed by quantification of the animal's region of interest (ROI) using the IVIS Lumina in vivo imaging system. The mice were subsequently euthanized, and their brains were isolated and sectioned into thin slices (2–3 mm). Sections were stained with hematoxylin–eosin (HE) for histopathological examination and stained with specific antibodies for IHC evaluation. The remaining mice were kept until death for survival analysis. All animal experiments were approved and conducted in strict adherence to the guidelines of the Animal Ethics and Use Committee of Fujian University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (FJTCM IACUC 2021188).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using the “Limma” package of R software (version 4.2.0) for TCGA data and SPSS (20.0) for experimental results. The unpaired T‐test was used to compare two groups, while the χ test assessed and compared distribution differences. One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to analyze differences within three or more groups. The Kaplan–Meier test was conducted to compare survival differences among groups. The results were reported as the mean ± standard deviation. A p‐value of ≤ 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

KLF9 Identified as a Potential Transcriptional Regulator Upstream of Cers1

In a previous study, Cers1 was established as a suppressor gene linked to NSCLC BM [6]. In order to further explore the upstream regulatory mechanism of Cers1, GENECARD and UCSC prediction software were employed to screen for TFs potentially targeting the Cers1 promoter region (chr19:18896097~18898096). A total of 81 TFs were identified by GENECARD, and 85 TFs by UCSC, resulting in 5 common TFs: CCCTC‐binding factor (CTCF), KLF9, MYC, early growth response protein 1 (EGR1), and GLI‐similar 2 (GLIS2) (Figure 1A, Table S3). Subsequent analysis of TF mRNA expression using the TCGA LUAD database revealed higher CTCF expression but lower expression levels of KLF9, MYC, EGR1, and GLIS2 in cancer tissues compared to para‐cancerous tissues (p ≤ 0.001) (Figure 1B–F, respectively). Further analysis using JASPAR software evaluated the potential of KLF9, MYC, EGR1, and GLIS2 to interact with the Cers1 promoter. Results indicated a high binding score for the KLF9 binding site (Table S4). Given its understudied role in NSCLC literature, we investigated KLF9 as a candidate upstream regulator of Cers1.

Cers1 upstream TFs prediction. (A) GENECARD and UCSC databases were used to predict TFs that might act on the Cers1 promoter. Expression levels are presented for CTCF (B), KLF9 (C), MYC (D), EGR1 (E) and GLIS2 (F) in the TCGA LUAD database (not significant (ns), *p < 0.05; ***p < 0.001).

KLF9 mRNA Found Downregulated in NSCLC

To confirm the correlation between KLF9 expression and clinical prognosis, we used TCGA and GEO databases to analyze the expression level of KLF9 in clinical samples. In the TCGA database, a significant downregulation of KLF9 mRNA expression in LUAD tissues compared with normal lung tissues was observed (Figure 1C). Further analysis of paired samples (57 cases) from the TCGA database showed a lower mRNA expression of KLF9 in LUAD tissues compared to para‐cancerous tissues (p ≤ 0.0001, Figure 2A). Additionally, lower expression of KLF9 was observed in LUAD tissues compared to adjacent tissues (p ≤ 0.001, Figure 2B) in the GEO dataset (GSE10072, GSE31210), while a significantly better prognosis was observed in patients with high expression levels of KLF9 compared to those with low expression levels (p ≤ 0.05, Figure 2C) using the TCGA database. Pan‐cancer analysis using the UALCAN database revealed that KLF9 mRNA expression was lower in cancer tissues relative to normal tissues across 24 tumor types, except for pheochromocytoma, paraganglioma, and sarcoma (Figure 2D). Moreover, lower expression of KLF9 was observed in LUAD cell lines compared to BEAS‐2B cells (Figure 2E). These findings collectively suggest that KLF9 may be implicated as a tumor suppressor gene in LUAD.

KLF9 expression is downregulated in LUAD. (A) Analysis of KLF9 mRNA expression in LUAD paired samples from the TCGA database. (B) Examination of KLF9 mRNA expression in LUAD samples using the GEO datasets (GSE10072, GSE31210). (C) Kaplan–Meier analysis of OS in patients stratified by KLF9 expression. (D) UALCAN database was used to analyze the expression levels of KLF9 mRNA in 24 types of tumors. (E) Western blotting was used to detect the protein expression levels of KLF9 in LUAD and BEAS‐2B cells. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

KLF9 Was Shown to Positively Regulate Cers1 Transcription

Previous findings of this study predicted that KLF9 might act as a transcriptional regulator upstream of Cers1. Of note, H1299 and PC‐9 cells have been established as models with a high tendency for BM, as supported by preliminary experiments showing their pronounced invasiveness and strong association with BM. Here, stable cell lines were established to either overexpress or knock down KLF9 using H1299 and PC‐9 cells (Figure 3A,B), with subsequent evaluation of Cers1 protein expression conducted via Western blot. The results showed that the expression level of Cers1 protein was significantly upregulated in cell lines overexpressing KLF9 but downregulated after KLF9 knockdown (Figure 3C). The dual luciferase reporter assay revealed that KLF9 overexpression enhanced the fluorescence activity of the Cers1 promoter, while KLF9 knockdown led to its reduction (Figure 3D), indicating a positive regulatory role of KLF9 on the Cers1 promoter.

KLF9 positively regulates Cers1 promoter transcription. (A, B) Construction of stably transfected and knockdown KLF9 cell lines. (C) Western blot was employed to detect alterations in Cers1 protein expression following up‐ or downregulation of KLF9. (D) Dual‐luciferase assay was used to detect the fluorescence activity of the Cers1 promoter. (E) Cers1 promoter recombinant plasmid was transfected to detect its fluorescence activity. (F) Effect of KLF9 binding site mutants on the fluorescence activity of the Cers1 promoter. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Subsequent JASPAR analysis identified potentially 10 binding sites (BS) of KLF9 on the Cers1 promoter (score > 8) (Table S4). Upon transfection of the full‐length Cers1 promoter and its truncated fragments into KLF9‐overexpressing PC‐9 cells, enhanced promoter activity was observed, particularly for pGL4‐711 (−711 nt/+22 nt) (Figure 3E). Further investigation revealed two KLF9 binding sites on pGL4‐711.

To explore the transcriptional activity of KLF9 binding to the specific sites on the Cers1 promoter, pGL4‐711‐KLF9mut1 and pGL4‐711‐KLF9mut2 recombinant plasmids were constructed and transfected into KLF9‐overexpressing PC‐9 cells. The Cers1 transcriptional activity was evaluated through fluorescence. The results showed that compared to pGL4‐711, the fluorescence activity of transfected pGL4‐711‐KLF9mut2 was not altered, while the fluorescence activity of transfected pGL4‐711‐KLF9mut1 was significantly reduced (Figure 3F). These findings confirm that KLF9 directly regulates the transcriptional activity of the Cers1 promoter, primarily through its association with the (−711 nt/+22 nt) region.

KLF9 Inhibited the Proliferation and Metastasis of NSCLC In Vitro

In vitro experiments were conducted to assess the impact of KLF9 on NSCLC proliferation. Notably, employing a KEGG signaling pathway enrichment analysis revealed a negative correlation between KLF9 and “DNA REPLICATION” (FDR q < 0.05, NES = −2.04) and “CELL CYCLE” (FDR q < 0.05, NES = −1.96) (Figure 4A), suggesting a potential role of KLF9 in influencing the proliferation ability of NSCLC. The results of colony formation experiments demonstrated that KLF9 overexpression significantly weakened cell proliferation, whereas KLF9 silencing produced the opposite effect (Figure 4B,C). Moreover, the protein expression levels of apoptosis‐related proteins—Bax, Bcl‐2, caspase‐3, and cleaved caspase‐3 (activated caspase‐3) – in the stable cell lines were further investigated. Western blot analysis revealed that KLF9 overexpression upregulated Bax and cleaved caspase‐3/caspase‐3 while downregulating Bcl‐2. Conversely, KLF9 knockdown downregulated Bax and cleaved caspase‐3/caspase‐3 and upregulated Bcl‐2 (Figure 4D).

KLF9 inhibits the proliferation of NSCLC cells. (A) Enrichment analysis of KLF9‐related pathways in NSCLC. (B, C) Colony formation assay showed that KLF9 inhibited cell proliferation. (D) Protein quantification by Western blot revealed that KLF9 promoted the expression of apoptosis‐related proteins. (E, F) Cell cycle‐related proteins were detected by flow cytometry and Western blot, indicating that KLF9 inhibited cell proliferation. **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.

To elucidate the effects of KLF9 on cell cycle dynamics, flow cytometry was used to detect the cell cycle. The experimental results showed that KLF9 overexpression increased the number of cells in the G0/G1 phase and decreased the number of cells in the S and G2/M phases, suggesting that overexpression of KLF9 can block the cell cycle and DNA synthesis, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation (Figure 4E). Subsequently, the expression levels of cell cycle‐related proteins, CDK4 and CDK6, were assessed by Western blot. It was observed that following KLF9 overexpression, a significant reduction in expression levels of CDK4 and CDK6 was evident in the H1299 group, while no significant difference was observed in the PC‐9 group. Conversely, upon KLF9 knockdown, a substantial increase in protein expression levels of CDK4 and CDK6 was present in both the PC‐9 and H1299 groups (Figure 4F). The above results suggest that KLF9 overexpression suppresses cell proliferation and metastasis.

KLF9 Inhibited the Transmigration of Human LUAD Cells Through Blood–Brain Barrier Model In Vitro

To investigate the impact of KLF9 on the permeability of BM in human LUAD, initial assessments were conducted to determine its effect on the invasion and migration abilities of NSCLC cells through transwell experiments. This study showed that cell migration and invasion abilities were weakened upon KLF9 overexpression, whereas knockdown had the opposite effect (Figure 5A,B). Furthermore, protein levels of invasion and metastasis‐related protein MMP2 and MMP9 were detected by Western blot and compared to the control group, revealing that KLF9 overexpression led to downregulation of MMP2 and MMP9 protein levels, while KLF9 knockdown resulted in significant upregulation of both protein levels (Figure 5C). These results indicate a negative correlation between KLF9 expression and tumor metastasis.

KLF9 inhibits the ability of NSCLC cells to penetrate the BBB. Transwell assay showed that KLF9 inhibited the migration (A) and invasion (B) of cancer cells. (C) Protein quantification by Western blot showed that KLF9 inhibited the expression of EMT‐related proteins, MMP2 and MMP9. (D) Fluorescence microscopy revealed that KLF9 inhibited lung cancer cells from crossing the BBB. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.

Subsequently, the impact of KLF9 expression on the ability of lung cancer cells to penetrate the BBB was evaluated by co‐culturing hCMEC/D3 cells and mouse astrocytes on one side of the transwell membrane. After PC‐9 cells were added and cultured for 24 h, the number of cells through the BBB model was observed under a fluorescence microscope. The results showed that the number of PC‐9 cells crossing the BBB model was significantly lower after KLF9 overexpression compared to the control group, while KLF9 knockdown had the opposite effect (Figure 5D), demonstrating that KLF9 overexpression inhibited the ability of cells to cross the BBB.

KLF9 Suppressed NSCLC BM In Vivo

The effect of KLF9 on NSCLC BM in vivo was further examined through the establishment of orthotopic xenograft tumor models using stably transfected KLF9 (KLF9‐OE) and control (VE) cells in the brain of mice. The results showed that KLF9‐OE significantly reduced the volume of BM (Figure 6A,B) and extended survival duration (Figure 6C) compared to control cells. Immunofluorescence examination of BM sections from these mice confirmed higher KLF9 expression in mice bearing KLF9‐OE cells compared to mice bearing control (VE) cells (Figure 6D), indicating that the KLF9‐OE stable cell line successfully colonized the brain metastases and exerted tumor‐suppressive functions.

(C) Kaplan–Meier survival curves for animals in different groups. (D) Immunofluorescence analysis of KLF9 expression in each group. **p < 0.01.

Discussion

NSCLC BM represents a clinical symptom closely associated with poor prognosis. Elucidating the molecular mechanisms of this disease is crucial for refining treatment strategies. Previous research has identified Cers1, a component of the ceramide pathway, as a suppressor of NSCLC BM by downregulating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway [6]. In this study, we delve into the upstream regulatory mechanism of KLF9 on Cers1 and its impact on NSCLC BM (Figure 7). Our findings reveal that KLF9 promotes apoptosis in NSCLC cells while hindering their proliferation, invasion, and migration through the BBB, exerting a negative regulatory effect. This investigation represents the first comprehensive exploration of KLF9's regulatory influence on Cers1 and its role in the context of NSCLC BM.

Schematic illustration of the molecular mechanism by which the TF KLF9 acts on the Cers1 promoter to regulate NSCLC BM.

An important hallmark of cancer initiation and progression is the increased invasiveness displayed by cancer cells. The transition from normal cells to cancer cells involves the expression and dysfunction of multiple transcription factors. KLF9, a member of the Spl/Kruppel‐like TFs family, participates in various biological processes including growth, differentiation, or apoptosis. KLF9 exhibits bidirectional effects on transcriptional regulation, either activating or inhibiting it, a trait influenced by the specific cancer type and its complex microenvironment. For instance, in breast cancer, KLF9 not only upregulates the expression of functional E‐cadherin by repressing MMP9 transcription but also directly binds to GT‐boxes in the E‐cadherin promoter, thereby upregulating the expression of functional E‐cadherin. This dual action enhances epithelial cell adhesion, consequently inhibiting breast cancer invasion [11]. Moreover, KLF9 positively regulates p53 expression and induces apoptosis in liver cancer cells [12]. However, in ovarian cancer, KLF9 knockdown significantly inhibits cell proliferation and arrests the cell cycle in the G0/G1 phase, indicating that KLF9 plays a distinct cancer‐promoting role in ovarian cancer compared to other cancers [13]. In NSCLC, KLF9 research focuses on downstream target genes. Of note, MiRNA‐20a‐5p targets KLF9 and inhibits the transcription of downstream genes regulated by KLF9, accelerating the proliferation and invasion of NSCLC [14]. Additionally, the miR‐660‐5p in the supernatant exosomes of NSCLC A549 and H1299 cells promotes tumor growth and metastasis by targeting KLF9 [15]. However, there is limited research on KLF9 as an upstream TF regulating downstream gene in NSCLC. In this study, KLF9 was found to inhibit NSCLC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro, suggesting that KLF9 may function as a tumor suppressor gene in NSCLC.

Previous studies using whole‐exome sequencing (WES) enabled the identification of key abnormal signaling pathways, among which activation of the ceramide pathway significantly inhibited BM and Cers1 negatively regulated the metastatic potential of NSCLC [6, 10]. Exploring the upstream regulatory mechanism of Cers1 is essential for the development of drugs targeting the ceramide pathway. Our findings, through in vivo and in vitro experiments, confirmed a positive correlation between the mRNA and protein expressions of KLF9 and Cers1. Particularly, dual‐luciferase experiments confirmed that KLF9 directly regulates the Cers1 promoter (−711 nt/+22 nt) region activity, and KLF9 overexpression suppresses the ability of cells to penetrate the BBB in vitro and in vivo. These findings suggest that KLF9 may act to inhibit the promoter of the NSCLC brain metastasis‐related gene Cers1, exerting an inhibitory effect on NSCLC BM. Nevertheless, our study has certain limitations. Firstly, the expression level of KLF9 in lung cancer tissues and lung cancer BM was not analyzed in clinical samples. Secondly, although the use of hCMEC/D3 cells and mouse astrocytes to construct the BBB model is straightforward and reliable, the two‐dimensional nature of this model restricts dynamic observation of the BM process. Furthermore, in the future, a spontaneous lung‐to‐brain metastasis model should be used. Finally, our study failed to modulate KLF9 and Cers1 simultaneously to elucidate their combined impact on lung cancer BM, a topic that will be further addressed in future studies.

In summary, our study elucidates the complex regulatory relationship between KLF9 and Cers1, revealing KLF9's direct modulation of Cers1 promoter activity, which consequently impacts Cers1 expression. Furthermore, our findings highlight KLF9's multifaceted role in inhibiting NSCLC proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro and orthotopic xenograft tumor formation in vivo, underscoring its potential as a tumor suppressor gene in NSCLC. These findings propose that targeting the KLF9‐Cers1 axis could represent a novel therapeutic strategy for combating NSCLC BM, emphasizing the need for further exploration of KLF9's role in NSCLC progression and metastatic processes.
