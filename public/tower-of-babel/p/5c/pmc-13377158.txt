Health Care Use and Outcomes in Heart Failure
Authors: Finlay A. McAlister, Chuan Wen, Jeffrey A. Bakal, Mohit Bhutani, Michael K. Stickland, Paul E. Ronksley, Jason Weatherald, Grace Y. Lam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhether there are residual impacts after the COVID-19 pandemic for patients with heart failure (HF) is unknown.ObjectivesTo explore HF-related health care use and outcomes between March 2018 and May 2024.MethodsRetrospective cohort study using interrupted time series (ITS) to compare monthly rates of mortality, hospitalizations, emergency department (ED) visits, and ambulatory care visits in patients with HF pre-pandemic (March 12, 2018-March 11, 2020), intra-pandemic (March 12, 2020-May 11, 2023), and post-pandemic (May 12, 2023-May 11, 2024), adjusted for age, sex, socioeconomic status, comorbidity burden, seasonality, and pre-pandemic trends.ResultsAlthough demographics and comorbidities were similar across all 3 periods (66,782 patients pre-pandemic, 77,473 intra-pandemic, and 82,072 post-pandemic), patients with HF presenting to an ED or being hospitalized had more severe Canadian Triage Acuity Scores during and post-pandemic than pre-pandemic (both P < 0.001). Over a median follow-up time of 2.9 years (IQR: 1.1-5.9 years), monthly outcomes were significantly lower during and post-pandemic for all-cause hospitalizations (incidence rate ratio: 0.83 [95% CI: 0.81-0.86] and 0.81 [0.78-0.84]), and all-cause ED visits (0.81 [0.78-0.84] and 0.84 [0.80-0.89]), even after adjusting for covariates and pre-pandemic trends (ITS P = 0.001, and 0.005, respectively). Non-COVID mortality rates were lower post-pandemic (incidence rate ratio 0.58 [0.53-0.63]) but ITS analysis revealed this was not a significant departure from pre-pandemic trends (P = 0.79).ConclusionsThe declines in hospitalizations and ED visits seen during the pandemic continued post-pandemic, severity of presentation increased, and the decline in non-COVID mortality rates was consistent with pre-pandemic trends.

Heart failure (HF) is a leading cause of death and disease burden worldwide, but the age-adjusted incidence and prognosis of HF appeared to be improving in most jurisdictions prior to onset of the COVID-19 pandemic.1 However, numerous studies demonstrated that the COVID-19 pandemic was associated with a decline in HF hospitalizations but increases in case severity and in-hospital mortality among hospitalized patients between March 2020 and the fall of 2022.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Whether the management patterns and outcomes for outpatients with HF were as profoundly affected during the pandemic and whether the patterns for both outpatients and hospitalized patients returned to pre-pandemic trends after the pandemic are unknown.

This is important to answer since the excess mortality rates seen after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic have not yet fully returned to pre-pandemic levels in many countries, especially for cardiovascular diseases.8, 9, 10, 11 There has been much speculation that the pandemic would have long-term negative sequelae for cardiovascular health due to its impact on the health care system (including clinician burnout, sustained workforce shortages, and backlogs in diagnostic testing and procedures), the cumulative impact of deferred preventive care during 2020 to 2023, and persistent changes in patient lifestyle and health-seeking behaviors.12

The World Health Organization declared the COVID-19 Public Health Emergency over on May 5, 2023. We designed this study to explore resource use (outpatient visits, emergency department [ED] visits, and hospitalizations) and mortality in patients with HF between March 2018 and April 2024 in Alberta, which has a universal access, publicly-funded health care system with complete capture of all health care interactions and no point-of-service charges for approximately 5 million people. Specifically, we compared trends in ambulatory, ED, and hospitalization utilization and mortality pre-pandemic (March 2018-February 2020), intra-pandemic (March 2020-April 2023), and post-pandemic (May 2023-May 2024).

Methods

Design and study sample

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adult Albertans living with HF using routinely collected data from 5 administrative health databases linked deterministically via each patient’s unique health care number. A diagnosis of HF was defined using International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-based case definitions previously validated in Alberta (ICD-10 code: I50.0, I50.1, I50.2, or I50.9 in ED or hospital visits or in at least 2 outpatient encounters [ICD-9 code 428.x]),13 across all encounters with the health care system from April 1, 2002, to May 11, 2024. As such, our cohort included both prevalent cases (diagnosed between 2002 and 2018 who were alive at the start of the study) and new incident cases (cases who first met the case definition criteria during the primary study window from March 12, 2018, to May 11, 2024) and was dynamic over time in that new incident cases joined the cohort as diagnoses were made and cases left the cohort when they died. We tracked event rates in this cohort over time and most patients were followed pre-, during, and post-pandemic. We excluded individuals younger than 18 years or those not registered with the Alberta Health Care Insurance Plan (these would have been mostly visitors to the province or patients from other jurisdictions transferred to an Alberta hospital for care, members of the military, or incarcerated individuals).

Data sources

The databases used included: 1) the Canadian Institute for Health Information Discharge Abstract Database (which records dates, the most responsible diagnosis, up to 24 other diagnoses, and procedures for all acute care hospitalizations); 2) the National Ambulatory Care Reporting System (which records dates for all patient visits to hospital-based physicians’ offices or EDs with coding for up to 10 conditions); 3) the HealthCare Provider Claims Database (which captures dates for all physician claims, including those shadow-billed by salaried physicians and whether the visit was virtual or in-person, and includes up to 3 diagnoses per encounter); 4) the Alberta Provincial Laboratory (which included results for laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 tests); and 5) the Alberta Health Care Insurance Registry, which includes patient postal code (which allows us to distinguish rural vs urban patients and estimate their material deprivation index quintile through linkage with Canadian census data).

Comorbidities

We interrogated our data sources to define each patient’s comorbidity profile using ICD-9 and ICD-10-CA codes from any hospitalizations, any ED visits, and any outpatient visits in the databases in the 1 year prior to and including the index visit using case definitions previously validated in Alberta.14

Outcomes

We examined monthly rates of ED visits, hospitalizations, outpatient visits (both in-person and virtual as Alberta Health introduced billing codes for virtual outpatients visits [mostly telephone] on March 17, 2020, that reimbursed at the same rate as in-person visits and this remained in place even beyond May 2024), and mortality (both all-cause and non-COVID-related [defined by excluding deaths within 30 days of a positive test for COVID-19])—for all outcomes we explored all-cause and HF-specific and expressed rates per 1,000 patients. Patient severity for ED presentations or hospitalizations was classified using the Canadian Triage and Acuity Scale (CTAS) assigned by a clinician at their ED presentation (CTAS 1 = requiring urgent resuscitation, CTAS 5 = nonurgent).

Statistical analysis

Given the overlapping populations within this dynamic cohort, baseline characteristics were evaluated using descriptive analysis and not statistically compared between periods. Patient demographics, comorbidities, severity scores, and health care utilization were summarized for each time period: continuous variables were reported as mean (SD) and median (Q1, Q3); categorical variables as counts and percentages. Presentation severity (grouped by CTAS scores) was compared across periods using standard Pearson chi-square tests. We calculated incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% CIs to compare the monthly event rates intra- vs pre-pandemic, post- vs pre-pandemic, and post- vs intra-pandemic.

We conducted interrupted time series (ITS) regression and AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) to predict outcome trajectories over time. The exact transition dates defining the segmented regression structure and interruption points were pre-pandemic (March 12, 2018, to March 11, 2020), intra-pandemic (March 12, 2020, to May 11, 2023), and post-pandemic (May 12, 2023, to May 11, 2024). While standard survival analyses are optimal for evaluating individual patient outcomes, ITS and ARIMA models are specifically designed to evaluate the longitudinal impact of structural shifts in health care delivery over time.15 ITS models estimate 2 parameters rather than a single averaged event rate:-Slope (trend): This estimate represents the underlying month-to-month trajectory of the event rate within a specific period. For example, a negative pre-pandemic slope indicates that event rates were already decreasing month-by-month before the pandemic began.-Change (level/step change): This estimate represents the immediate, acute shift in the event rate when a new period begins. For instance, a significant negative “COVID change” estimate indicates a sudden drop in hospitalizations during the first month of the pandemic, independent of the pre-existing downward trend.

Furthermore, as monthly health care data are naturally autocorrelated, the event rate in any given month is mathematically linked to the preceding month. ARIMA models are applied specifically to filter out this autocorrelation and seasonal variation. In this study, ARIMA modeling was performed alongside ITS to ensure that the observed changes in event rates represented true structural shifts in the health care system rather than statistical artifacts. To execute this, stationarity of the pre-pandemic data was assessed using the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test. Candidate models were iteratively evaluated using information criteria (including Akaike Information Criterion and Schwarz Bayesian Criterion), and final model orders (p, d, q) were selected by balancing optimal statistical fit, overall model stability, and clinical plausibility. Adequate model fit was confirmed by the absence of residual autocorrelation via the Ljung-Box Q statistic. The exact implemented model orders, goodness-of-fit statistics, and residual diagnostics for each outcome are detailed in Supplemental Table 7.

The primary ITS analyses were performed using SAS PROC AUTOREG to account for serial correlation. Contribution to model fit was formally evaluated at both the structural and variable levels. Specifically, the inclusion of seasonal parameters and aggregate clinical covariates (age, sex, and Charlson Comorbidity Index scores) was justified by comparing candidate and unadjusted (crude) models against fully adjusted models. Model fit during these stages was assessed using information criteria (Akaike Information Criterion, Schwarz Bayesian Criterion) and error metrics (mean squared error and mean absolute percentage error, with the adjusted models demonstrating consistently superior fit (Supplemental Tables 8 and 9). Within the final selected models, overall goodness-of-fit was evaluated using the total R-squared (R2) statistic, and the specific contribution of individual components (intervention variables, covariates, and seasonal indicators) was assessed via the 2-sided P values of their parameter estimates.

To determine if the pandemic's impact differed by sex, we conducted a secondary 2-series ITS analysis that included interaction terms for sex and pandemic period. We performed ARIMA modeling to ensure the robustness of the ITS results. Stationarity was evaluated visually and via statistical tests; first-order differencing was applied where appropriate. Model identification was based on autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation function plot patterns, and the Ljung-Box test. Analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4, and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. For both the ITS and ARIMA models, event rates were expressed per 1,000 patient-days to account for the dynamic nature of follow-up time.

Ethics and reporting

The University of Alberta Health Research Ethics Board approved this study (Pro00119420) with a waiver of individual patient consent, as the investigators were only provided with deidentified data. We followed the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected Data guidelines.

Results

The number of HF patients alive at the start of each of the pre-, intra-, and post-pandemic periods were 57,793, 66,786, and 77,485, respectively, incidence rates of newly diagnosed HF were relatively stable over time at approximately 900 to 1,100 new cases per month, and population-level point prevalence of HF in Albertan adults was 1.8% pre-pandemic, 1.9% during the pandemic, and 2.1% post-pandemic. Age, sex distribution, rural residency, and Charlson Comorbidity Index distribution were fairly similar in all 3 periods (Table 1), but patients presenting to an ED or being hospitalized were sicker during and post-pandemic than pre-pandemic (28.6% during and 29.9% post- vs 25.1% pre-pandemic had Canadian Triage Acuity Scores in the 2 most severe categories, both P < 0.001, Table 1). Monthly event rates and incidence rate ratios

The all-cause monthly mortality rate per 1,000 patients was not statistically significantly different during the pandemic (Figure 1A, Table 2) but was significantly lower post-pandemic than pre-pandemic or during the pandemic (Tables 2 and 3). On the other hand, the non-COVID mortality rate declined significantly both during and after the pandemic (Figure 1B, Table 2). All-cause and HF-specific hospitalizations and ED visits decreased significantly during the pandemic and remained significantly lower than pre-pandemic in the post-pandemic period (Figures 1C and 1F, Table 2). All-cause and HF-specific outpatient visits were stable from pre- to intra-pandemic (if virtual and in-person encounters were both included) but were significantly lower in the post-pandemic period than pre- or intra-pandemic (Figures 1G and 1H, Table 2). The declines post-pandemic compared to intra-pandemic were even greater for virtual visits than in-person visits, although both types of encounters declined markedly post-pandemic. All of these health care utilization patterns were similar in direction but larger in magnitude in patients with incident HF (Table 3) compared to prevalent HF patients (Supplemental Table 1). Although crude rates were different between the 3 periods (Table 2), after adjusting for covariates and pre-pandemic trends, our ITS analyses revealed that the declines in non-COVID mortality rates were not significantly different than would have been expected given pre-pandemic trends during (P = 0.65) and after (P = 0.79) the pandemic, but a higher proportion of deaths occurred at home (16.0% during vs 14.6% post- vs 12.1% pre-pandemic, Supplemental Table 4). On multivariable logistic regression, the strongest predictors of at-home non-COVID death were time period (22% higher during the pandemic and 11% higher post-pandemic than pre-pandemic) and male sex (37% higher risk than females after adjusting for all covariates) (Supplemental Table 5).

All-cause and HF-specific outpatient visits were stable from pre- to intra-pandemic in the crude and in the adjusted ITS analyses, but the apparent significant decrease in the post-pandemic period compared to pre- or intra-pandemic (IRR: 0.81 [0.77-0.85] and 0.78 [0.74-0.81]) was nonsignificant in the adjusted ITS analyses (P = 0.13 and 0.65, respectively) due to high month-to-month variability. The ARIMA modeling confirmed the ITS results (Supplemental Figures 1A to 1F), as did sensitivity analyses varying the pandemic end dates (Supplemental Table 6).

Discussion

We found that the declining trends in hospitalizations, ED visits, and outpatient visits in patients with HF seen pre-pandemic accelerated during and after the pandemic, both for HF and for all-causes, although patients presenting to an ED or being hospitalized were sicker (had more severe Canadian Triage Acuity Scores) during and post-pandemic than pre-pandemic. We also found that while non-COVID mortality rates for adults with HF were lower during and post-pandemic, they were not significantly different than was predicted from pre-pandemic trends after covariate adjustment, although a higher proportion of deaths occurred at home both during and after the pandemic.

While our results seem to contradict earlier data suggesting that the increase in cardiovascular mortality rates seen during the acute phase of the COVID-19 pandemic had persisted post-pandemic,9, 10, 11 we believe it is not surprising for 3 reasons. First, we focused on HF rather than all cardiovascular diseases and, unlike some other types of cardiovascular disease, there have been several disease-modifying therapies introduced for HF in the past decade which have markedly improved HF-related morbidity and mortality, particularly angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitors and sodium glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors, both of which only became publicly funded in Alberta within the past half decade. Second, there has been a marked increase in the prescription of guideline-recommended HF therapies since 2019 in Alberta16,17 and other jurisdictions, and users do demonstrate substantial reductions in hospitalizations, ED visits, and mortality.18,19 Third, as it is possible sicker patients with more advanced HF may have been more likely to contract COVID-19 and more likely to die during the pandemic than those patients with milder forms of HF, an element of survivor bias may be playing a role in our findings. For example, we saw slight declines in age and the proportion of patients with Charlson scores of 5 or greater as our cohort moved into the post-pandemic period, and the IRR for the post- vs pre/intra comparisons were of larger magnitude in patients with incident HF than prevalent HF. However, we also cannot rule out the possibility of changes in case capture over time (if, eg, post-pandemic there was earlier diagnosis of milder cases of HF, perhaps as a result of broader use of biomarkers and/or imaging in outpatients).

In support of our findings, a recent analysis of 715 U.S. hospitals (7.8 million hospitalizations) reported that although patient acuity (measured by case-mix index) was significantly higher in the first quarter of 2024 than in the fourth quarter of 2019, risk-adjusted hospital mortality was significantly lower compared to 2019, implying a return to pre-pandemic trajectories of improvement.20

The declining trends in outpatient encounters and acute care utilization we found may also reflect the increasing issues with access to care being documented across Canada since the onset of the pandemic and which have continued to worsen.21,22 While some of this may be due to residual patient reluctance to potentially expose themselves to infection during in-person health care visits, the current shortages of all types of clinicians and high rates of provider burnout also undoubtedly contribute.23 While we (and others) have previously shown that the rapid shift to a mix of virtual and in-person outpatient care during the COVID-19 pandemic did not negatively impact frequency of follow-up, prescribing, or short-term outcomes for patients with HF, there was a decline in laboratory monitoring and screening activities.24, 25, 26, 27, 28 To the extent that virtual visits may lead to less optimal monitoring of patients with HF, any adverse effects from the reduction in in-person outpatient encounters may be delayed. This is an area of substantial ongoing research activity.

Study Limitations

Although we captured all health care interactions in an entire Canadian province with universal access and no payment at the point of service, and used previously validated case definitions to define HF and comorbidities, there are some limitations to our study. First, we used administrative data and do not have access to clinician notes, imaging, medication use, or laboratory/biomarker data and thus cannot fully capture severity of HF or comorbidities, nor accurately distinguish heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) from heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) (the sensitivity of ICD-10 codes are in the range of 34% to 39% for HFpEF and 68% to 72% for HFrEF without inclusion of medication data).29, 30, 31 Second, our definition of non-COVID-related mortality was based on excluding any deaths within 30 days of a positive PCR-based SARS-CoV-2 test in the provincial registry. However, we did not have access to home test kit results, and although all patients presenting to an ED or hospital with suspected COVID-19 were formally tested in Alberta per protocol, we thus would have underestimated COVID-related mortality in those who died out-of-hospital. However, defining deaths within 30 days of a positive SARS-CoV-2 test as potentially COVID-related is standard in the literature.32 Finally, the Alberta Healthcare Registry captures all-cause mortality data and we were unable to determine how many of the deaths were specifically due to HF. However, we did use previously validated administrative data case definitions to define which ED visits, hospitalizations, and outpatient visits were for HF as the primary diagnosis.13,14

In conclusion, the declining trends in hospitalizations, ED visits, and outpatient encounters for patients with HF seen pre-pandemic in Alberta continued during and after the pandemic and non-COVID-related mortality rates were not significantly different than predicted by pre-pandemic trends and covariate mix after May 2023. While this may well be attributable to increasing use of guideline-directed medical therapy, including novel therapies like angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitors and sodium glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors, in patients with HF over the past decade, future studies should test this theory as we did not have access to medication data in this study. It is noteworthy that a higher proportion of deaths in patients with HF occurred at home both during and after the pandemic, and patients with HF presenting to an ED or being hospitalized were sicker during and post-pandemic than pre-pandemic—future studies should examine whether this reflects issues in accessing care, appropriate redirection of less acute presentations away from the ED or hospital, or changes in care-seeking behavior.
