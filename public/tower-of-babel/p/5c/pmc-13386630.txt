Increased 1-year mortality rates after hip fracture surgery in geriatric patients with skeletal muscle atrophy: a propensity score-matched study
Authors: Wanyun Tang, Xu Liu, Yuhao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate the relationship between skeletal muscle atrophy (SMA), assessed using chest CT-derived skeletal muscle index (SMI) at the T12 vertebra level, and 1-year mortality in geriatric patients following hip fracture surgery.MethodsAfter applying exclusion criteria, 1,243 patients (514 SMA) were analyzed. SMA was diagnosed by the skeletal muscle index at the T12 vertebral level (males: T12-SMI < 25.75 cm2/ m2; females: T12-SMI < 20.16 cm2/m2). The association between T12-SMI and 1-year mortality risk was assessed using multivariate logistic regression and propensity score-matched analyses. The predictive accuracy of T12-SMI for 1-year mortality rates was evaluated by ROC curves. The dose–response relationship between T12-SMI and 1-year mortality rates was examined.ResultsSMA was present in 41.4% of patients. The 1-year mortality rate was significantly higher in SMA patients compared to non-SMA patients (35.8% vs. 10.2%, p < 0.001). Lower T12-SMI levels were strongly associated with increased mortality, exhibiting a dose–response relationship. Multivariable-adjusted odds ratios for 1-year mortality decreased significantly across higher T12-SMI quartiles for both males and females.ConclusionsSMA, defined by low T12-SMI, is an independent predictor of 1-year mortality in geriatric hip fracture patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07163-w.

Introduction

Hip fractures are a major health concern among older adults, with an estimated annual incidence of over 1.6 million cases worldwide [1–3]. This number is projected to rise substantially due to population aging and increased life expectancy, with the global burden expected to reach 4.5 million by 2050 [3, 4]. Hip fractures are associated with significant morbidity, prolonged hospital stays, and high mortality rates, making them a critical public health challenge [5–7]. Among older adults who experience hip fractures, 1-year mortality rates range between 15 and 36%, underscoring the need for a deeper understanding of factors influencing these outcomes [4, 8]. Beyond the immediate physical trauma, hip fractures often lead to a cascade of complications, including functional decline, loss of independence, and long-term care needs, further exacerbating their societal and economic impact [9–11].

SMA, characterized by the progressive and generalized loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength, and function, affects approximately 10%–27% of older adults, with even higher prevalence reported in hospitalized and frail populations [12–14]. This condition has been increasingly recognized as a critical determinant of adverse outcomes in various surgical contexts [15–17]. Among geriatric patients, SMA contributes to reduced physiological reserve, impaired mobility, and heightened vulnerability to stressors such as surgery [18–20]. Studies have shown that the prevalence of SMA in patients with hip fractures generally exceeds 44%, varying depending on diagnostic criteria and population characteristics [21–23]. In Japan, based on the AWGS 2014 criteria, the prevalence was reported to be 81.1% in males and 44.7% in females [24]. A 2016 study from Korea further reported prevalence rates of 68.2% for males and 44.3% for females [25]. SMA patients are at a higher risk of postoperative complications, including infections, prolonged hospital stays, and delayed rehabilitation [20]. Moreover, their increased frailty and diminished functional capacity may predispose them to higher mortality rates, with some studies suggesting that SMA independently predicts 1-year mortality in geriatric populations [26]. For example, Shen et al. demonstrated that muscle mass (T12 SMI) and muscle quality indicators (T12 SMD and T12 IMAT), derived from chest CT, have the potential to serve as predictors of in-hospital mortality in older hospitalized patients [27].

Despite the clear association between SMA and adverse outcomes, data on its specific impact on long-term mortality following hip fracture surgery are limited and inconsistent. Furthermore, previous studies have often been constrained by small sample sizes, lack of multi-regional representation, and inadequate adjustment for confounding variables. To address these limitations, there is a need for robust research designs that combine large-scale datasets and advanced statistical methods, such as propensity score matching, to isolate the independent effects of SMA on postoperative outcomes.

By leveraging such methodologies and multi-regional data, this study aims to provide comprehensive insights into the relationship between SMA and 1-year mortality following hip fracture surgery, thereby contributing to improved risk stratification and tailored management strategies for this vulnerable patient population.

Methods

Data sources and patient

Patients aged 60 years or older people who underwent hip fracture surgery between January 2016 and December 2023 were retrospectively identified from institutional electronic medical records and clinical databases. Inclusion criteria were (1) availability of preoperative assessments required for SMA diagnosis, (2) documentation of hip fracture surgery using standardized surgical techniques, and (3) complete follow-up data for at least 1 year post-surgery. Exclusion criteria included patients with pathological fractures due to malignancy, those with incomplete baseline data, and patients who died within 48 h of surgery, as these deaths were considered unrelated to SMA-related outcomes [28].

Data collection

Data were collected from the hospital's health information system, covering demographic information such as gender, age, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. Comorbidities included hypertension, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cardiovascular disease, stroke, dementia, intracerebral hemorrhage, chronic liver disease, and chronic kidney disease. Surgery-related factors included fracture type, surgery type, intraoperative blood loss, transfusion, postoperative ICU admission, bedridden time, intraoperative time, and the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification. Laboratory findings encompassed white blood cell count (WBC), hemoglobin level (HGB), blood glucose, albumin, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine levels (Cr). Common complications such as deep vein thrombosis (DVT), urinary tract infection (UTI), and pneumonia were also documented. Additionally, SMA was evaluated using skeletal muscle index and its classification.

The most recent preoperative laboratory results were used for analysis. Data extraction was conducted independently by two researchers, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a senior investigator to ensure consistency and reliability.

Exposure and outcome

The primary exposure in this study was SMA assessed using chest computed tomography (CT) imaging performed on all patients aged 60 years and older undergoing hip fracture surgery. CT scans were obtained using a 64-slice helical CT scanner (Philips Healthcare) with a slice thickness of 5 mm. Imaging parameters included a voltage of 120 kV and a current of 250 mA, and the data were stored in the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) for further analysis.

The skeletal muscle cross-sectional area (SMCSA) at the mid-level of the 12th thoracic vertebra (T12) was calculated using ImageJ software (NIH ImageJ version 1.2c). According to previous research, skeletal muscle area was measured within an attenuation range of −29 to 150 Hounsfield Units (HU), which represents the density range for skeletal muscle tissue [29]. SliceOmatic software was used to analyze the unenhanced transverse CT images at the T12 vertebral level. The software automatically segmented and calculated the SMCSA for all visible skeletal muscles at this level.

To account for differences in body size, the skeletal muscle index (SMI), an established metric for muscle mass, was calculated using the following formula: SMI = SMCSA/Height2 (skeletal muscle index, calculated as skeletal muscle cross-sectional area [SMCSA, cm2] divided by height squared [m2], unit: cm2/m2) [30].

A higher SMI indicated greater skeletal muscle mass, while lower values were indicative of reduced muscle mass. In our study, the diagnosis of SMA was established by referencing sex-specific cut-off values for the Skeletal Muscle Index (SMI), which were derived from a previously validated sarcopenia diagnostic standard [31]. Specifically, the diagnostic thresholds we adopted were: < 25.75 cm2/m2 for males and < 20.16 cm2/m2 for females. This choice of criteria was driven by the current lack of dedicated, consensus-based cut-off values for SMA in existing research, making validated sarcopenia-related SMI thresholds a reliable and evidence-supported alternative for consistent assessment. Notably, a cross-sectional study conducted by Hu and colleagues—whose SMI cut-off values we referenced—also applied this sex-stratified metric (specifically using T12 vertebral level skeletal muscle area normalized by height squared) to evaluate sarcopenia in inpatients. Their findings revealed that 54.03% of the inpatient cohort met the sarcopenia diagnostic criteria (T12-SMI < 25.75 cm2/m2 for males and < 20.16 cm2/m2 for females), further validating the relevance and clinical applicability of this T12-SMI-based threshold system for assessing muscle-related conditions in hospitalized populations.

To evaluate the dose–response relationship between SMA severity and outcomes, patients were further divided into four quartile groups (Q1–Q4) based on SMI levels(Male: Q1 group (< 21.3 cm2/m2), Q2 group(21.3–26.5 cm2/m2), Q3 group(26.6–39.2 cm2/m2), Q4 group(≥ 39.3 cm2/m2); Female: Q1 group (< 17.2 cm2/m2), Q2 group(17.2–23.0 cm2/m2),Q3 group(23.0–31.8 cm2/m2), Q4 group(≥ 31.9 cm2/m2)), with Q1 representing the lowest muscle mass and Q4 the highest.

To minimize potential biases and ensure comparability between SMA and non-SMA groups, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed in a 1:1 ratio. Covariates included in the matching model were factors relevant to this study. This rigorous approach facilitated a robust analysis of the relationship between SMA and 1-year mortality following hip fracture surgery, while controlling for potential confounders. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality at 1 year following hip fracture surgery. The mortality data were obtained through follow-up visits and hospital electronic records.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and as counts with percentages for categorical variables. Statistical significance was evaluated using appropriate tests based on data type and distribution. The independent-samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test was applied for continuous variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test was employed for categorical variables.

Multivariate logistic regression models were utilized to assess the association between SMA and 1-year mortality, adjusting for potential confounders. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed in a 1:1 ratio (each patient with SMA matched to one without) using a nearest-neighbor algorithm without replacement. A narrow caliper width of 0.02 (based on the standard deviation of the logit of propensity scores) was applied to ensure close matching of baseline characteristics. Covariates included in the propensity score model were age, sex, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, COPD, cardiovascular disease, dementia), surgical factors (fracture type, surgery duration, intraoperative blood loss), and laboratory markers (albumin, hemoglobin)—all identified as potential confounders of the association between SMA and 1-year mortality. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated to evaluate the balance of covariates after PSM, with values less than 0.1 indicating adequate balance. To evaluate the predictive accuracy of T12-SMI for 1-year mortality, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed.

We stratified patients into quartiles (Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4) based on T12-SMI to accurately evaluate the dose–response relationship between SMI and mortality. To minimize bias from other confounding factors, we employed PSM. Univariate logistic regression analysis was then conducted to calculate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for the association between elevated T12-SMI and 1-year mortality.

SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp., Chicago, Illinois, USA) and R software version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Boston, Massachusetts, USA) were used to conduct all statistical analyses.

These findings indicate that higher T12-SMI is associated with better survival outcomes regardless of gender.

Figure 3c illustrates the 1-year mortality rates across four quartiles based by T12-SMI (Q1–Q4) for males. The mortality rates are as follows: Q1 (39.5%), Q2 (32.5%), Q3 (8.9%), and Q4 (6.5%), with a statistically significant trend (p < 0.001). Figure 3f shows the 1-year mortality rates across the same quartiles (Q1–Q4) for females. The mortality rates are: Q1 (34.2%), Q2 (23.5%), Q3 (11.2%), and Q4 (10.7%), also showing a statistically significant trend (p < 0.001). These findings indicate that both males and females in the lower T12-SMI quartiles have significantly increased 1-year mortality risks.

Table 4 further explores the association between T12-SMI quartiles and 1-year mortality using unadjusted, multivariable regression-adjusted, and PSM-adjusted models. In the male subgroup analysis, with Q1 (< 21.3 cm2/m2) as the reference, the odds ratios for 1-year mortality decrease significantly across higher quartiles. For Q3 (26.6–39.2 cm2/m2), the OR is 0.149 (95% CI: 0.073–0.305, p < 0.001) in the unadjusted model, 0.193 (95% CI: 0.092–0.407, p < 0.001) after multivariable adjustment, and 0.204 (95% CI: 0.071–0.585, p = 0.003) after PSM adjustment. Similarly, in the female subgroup analysis (with Q1 < 17.2 cm2/m2 as the reference), higher quartiles show significantly reduced ORs for 1-year mortality. For instance, Q3 (23.0–31.8 cm2/m2) and Q4 (≥ 31.9 cm2/m2) have adjusted ORs of 0.348 (95% CI: 0.193–0.629, p < 0.001) and 0.320 (95% CI: 0.175–0.583, p < 0.001), respectively, in the multivariable model, and 0.377 (95% CI: 0.200–0.714, p = 0.003) and 0.164 (95% CI: 0.072–0.372, p < 0.001), respectively, in the PSM-adjusted model. Dose–response relationship

A dose-dependent relationship between T12-SMI and 1-year mortality was observed in male patients, with lower T12-SMI levels associated with increased 1-year mortality risk. Specifically, female patients with T12-SMI < 26.6 cm2/m2 demonstrated a significantly higher risk of 1-year mortality compared to those with T12-SMI ≥ 26.6 cm2/m2 (Fig. 4a). The predicted probabilities and actual observed values for 1-year mortality based on T12-SMI (Fig. 4b) indicate a rising risk of 1-year mortality with decreasing T12-SMI. Furthermore, when analyzed as a continuous variable, higher T12-SMI consistently correlated with reduced 1-year mortality.. Collectively, these findings establish T12-SMI as an independent and dose-dependent risk factor for 1-year mortality in male geriatric patients following hip fracture surgery. Discussion

In this study, the 1-year mortality rate was significantly higher in patients with SMA compared to those without, both before and after propensity score matching (PSM). This is consistent with previous studies that have established SMA as a predictor of poor outcomes in various surgical contexts, particularly in geriatric populations. Our results extend these findings by providing robust evidence that SMA is an independent predictor of mortality following hip fracture surgery, even after adjusting for multiple confounding factors such as age, comorbidities, and surgery-related factors.

Our findings are generally consistent with those of other studies. For example, Lenchik et al., in the NLST study, found that higher baseline paraspinous skeletal muscle area (SMA) and skeletal muscle density (SMD) were associated with lower all-cause mortality risk over 6 years in men, with an HR of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.79–0.91; p < 0.001) for SMA and 0.91 (95% CI: 0.84–0.98; p = 0.012) for SMD. No significant associations were observed in women [32]. Zhang et al. included 6 studies with 1,494 participants in a meta-analysis, finding that SMA was significantly associated with higher all-cause mortality risk in nursing home residents (HR 1.86, 95% CI: 1.42–2.45, p < 0.001) [33]. Pierobon analyzed 10 studies with data from 2,811 pancreatic cancer surgery patients. The meta-analysis found that patients with low muscle mass had significantly worse overall survival (OS) compared to those with stable muscle mass (ROM 0.86; 95% CI: 0.81–0.91, p < 0.001) [34]. However, Wiegert included 13 studies involving 1,959 incurable cancer patients and found no statistically significant association between muscle mass loss and reduced overall survival [35].

Interestingly, the mortality rate for patients with SMA was significantly higher in both male and female subgroups, although males exhibited a slightly higher mortality risk than females. This gender difference may be due to the higher prevalence and greater severity of SMA in older men, as suggested by previous literature [36–39].

In addition, we identified different T12-SMI thresholds for predicting 1-year mortality based on gender: < 22.9 cm2/m2 for females and < 26.6 cm2/m2 for males. This difference in thresholds can be attributed to the distinct physiological characteristics between the sexes. Generally, males have higher baseline muscle mass due to greater levels of testosterone, which promotes muscle growth [40–42]. As a result, the threshold for SMA in males is set higher to reflect this greater muscle mass. In contrast, females typically have lower muscle mass, particularly as they age and experience a decline in estrogen levels post-menopause, which accelerates muscle loss [43–45]. Therefore, the lower threshold of 22.9 cm2/m2 for females aligns with the generally lower muscle mass observed in older women, while the 26.6 cm2/m2 threshold for males reflects the higher muscle mass typical in aging men.

The thresholds established in this study are different from those reported in some other research. Several factors may contribute to this discrepancy. First, differences in study populations could explain the variation. For example, our cohort might consist of a more frail or older people, where muscle mass is already significantly reduced, leading to lower thresholds for SMA. Second, variations in imaging protocols and muscle measurement techniques may affect the results. Our study used chest CT at the T12 level to measure skeletal muscle index (SMI), but other studies may use different anatomical levels or imaging methods, leading to different muscle mass estimations and thresholds. Erstine's study identified SMA SMI thresholds at L3 as 34.4 cm2/m2 for females and 45.4 cm2/m2 for males. When L3 is unavailable, alternative levels in order of priority are L2, L4, L5, L1, T12, T11, and T10 [46]. Moreover, Nemec's study assessed skeletal muscle mass measurements at two thoracic levels on chest CT to diagnose SMA. The cutoff values at T12 were 42.6 cm2/m2 for males and 30.6 cm2/m2 for females, while at T7, the cutoffs were 46.5 cm2/m2 for males and 32.3 cm2/m2 for females [47]. Furthermore, different studies may apply varying criteria to define SMA, such as incorporating additional factors like strength or functional performance, which could influence the cutoff values [47–49]. These discrepancies highlight the need for further research to refine SMA diagnostic criteria and establish population-specific thresholds that account for sex differences, age-related changes, and the clinical context in which SMA is being assessed.

The association between SMA and increased mortality risk is likely multifactorial. SMA patients typically have reduced physiological reserves, which makes them more vulnerable to complications following major surgery, such as infections, delayed rehabilitation, and prolonged hospital stays [50, 51]. Additionally, SMA is often accompanied by other age-related conditions, such as frailty, chronic inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation, all of which contribute to poor outcomes [17]. Furthermore, the loss of muscle mass can impair mobility and independence, further diminishing the ability to recover from surgery and increasing the likelihood of long-term care needs [52, 53].

The presence of SMA may also reflect a more generalized decline in health, suggesting that muscle mass could serve as a surrogate marker for overall frailty and vulnerability [54, 55]. This highlights the importance of considering SMA in the broader context of geriatric care, where interventions aimed at improving muscle mass and strength could have significant benefits in reducing postoperative complications and improving survival outcomes.

This study underscores the need for early identification and management of SMA in older adults, particularly those undergoing hip fracture surgery. Preoperative screening for SMA, using CT scans to measure T12-SMI, may offer valuable prognostic insights for 1-year mortality. Clinicians could use this information to guide individualized management plans, including the implementation of prehabilitation programs aimed at enhancing muscle strength and function before surgery. In the postoperative period, SMA patients may benefit from more intensive rehabilitation efforts to promote recovery and prevent further muscle loss.

Moreover, the dose–response relationship observed between T12-SMI and mortality suggests that even small increases in muscle mass could have meaningful effects on patient outcomes. This highlights the potential value of interventions designed to improve muscle mass, such as nutritional supplementation, resistance exercise, and physical therapy, in reducing mortality risk and improving the quality of life for older adults after hip fracture surgery.

The strengths of this study include its large sample size, multi-regional data, and the use of robust statistical methods, including propensity score matching, to control for confounding factors. These features enhance the generalizability and reliability of the findings. This study is the first to use chest CT to diagnose SMA in hip fracture patients, utilizing existing imaging resources efficiently. By measuring the skeletal muscle index (SMI) at the T12 level, chest CT provides accurate, objective, and cost-effective SMA assessment. This approach not only reduces diagnostic costs but also improves preoperative risk stratification and management in older people.

However, there are several limitations to this study. First, its retrospective design may introduce biases related to data collection and patient selection. Although propensity score matching was used to minimize selection bias, the observational nature of the study limits the ability to infer causality. Second, while T12-SMI is a well-established marker of SMA, it does not capture other aspects of SMA, such as muscle strength or physical performance, which could provide additional insights into the relationship between muscle function and mortality. Finally, the study population was limited to patients from high-volume clinical centers, which may not fully represent the broader geriatric population, particularly those in more rural or underserved areas.

Conclusions

SMA, as assessed by T12-SMI, is a strong predictor of 1-year mortality following hip fracture surgery in older people. This study provides compelling evidence that SMA is an independent risk factor for poor outcomes and underscores the importance of early identification and management of SMA in this vulnerable population. Future research should focus on prospective studies to confirm these findings and explore potential interventions to mitigate the impact of SMA on postoperative outcomes.
