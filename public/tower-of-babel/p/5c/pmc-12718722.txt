A novel audiogram model for predicting the prognosis of sudden sensorineural hearing loss
Authors: Hsia-Wei Tseng, Chien-Yeh Hsu, Chih-Hao Chen, Yuan-Chia Chu, Wen-Huei Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL) is an otological emergency involving hearing loss greater than 30 dB across three consecutive frequencies within 72 hours. Prognosis is influenced by the initial pure-tone audiogram (PTA), with ascending and U-shaped patterns showing better outcomes and descending patterns linked to poorer outcomes. Traditional audiograms are classified into four to seven types based on hearing thresholds at four to six frequencies, such as flat, ascending, or descending types. However, the definitions of these patterns are often ambiguous and not easy to apply in clinical practice. This study aimed to compare the prognostic value of the traditional seven-type audiogram model with a novel three-type audiogram model.Methods:This retrospective cohort study included SSNHL patients from 2012 to 2023. Hearing outcomes, measured by PTA, were classified using both the traditional seven-type audiogram model and a new three-type model based on the 4 kHz threshold (the best, intermediate, the worst). The traditional seven-type model classifies audiograms into flat, descending, ascending, tent-shaped, U-shaped, jagged, and profound types based on the threshold patterns at four frequencies (0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz). The novel three-type model classified audiograms according to the relative position of the 4 kHz threshold: (1) 4 kHz the best (lowest threshold), (2) 4 kHz intermediate (threshold in between), or (3) 4 kHz the worst (highest threshold). Complete recovery (CR) was defined as a final hearing threshold ≤25 dB HL. Multiple and stratified linear regression analyses assessed its association with the CR rate, defined as the proportion of patients achieving CR.Results:In total, 965 patients with SSNHL were recruited (51.5% men; mean age 50.8 ± 15.1 years). Left ear involvement was more common (53.3%). CR was achieved in 21.1% of patients. The three-type audiogram model classification was significantly associated with CR (overall p < 0.001, multivariable logistic regression). 4 kHz was the worst, the CR rate was 20%, intermediate, 36%, the best, 49%. In contrast, the traditional seven-type audiogram model did not demonstrate such predictive characteristics.Conclusion:The novel three-type audiogram model provides better prognostic predictions for SSNHL, with 4 kHz frequency being a key factor influencing recovery. Traditional audiogram classification is relatively complex, whereas the novel three-type audiogram model, which uses the 4 kHz threshold for classification, is simple, clear, and provides better prognostic prediction for SSNHL, making it more suitable for clinical application.

1. INTRODUCTION

Sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL) is a common otological emergency, defined as sensorineural hearing loss (HL) of 30 dB or greater across at least three consecutive test frequencies within a 3-day period. Most cases of SSNHL have an unknown etiology.1–6 The annual incidence in the United States is 5 to 27 cases per 100 000 people, resulting in approximately 6600 new cases each year.2 Prognostic factors include age, the degree of HL, the initial treatment period, the presence of vertigo, and the type of initial pure-tone audiogram (PTA).7–10 Previous studies have shown that the initial type of audiogram affects hearing recovery, with ascending and U-shaped audiograms being associated with better outcomes, whereas descending audiograms are linked to poorer recovery.8–12 Currently, there is no unified classification for PTAs. A review of various scholars’ definitions revealed at least six to nine types, with unclear and sometimes unclassifiable definitions.13 Audiograms have been classified into at least six to seven types in previous SSNHL studies,14–16 and complex definitions make it difficult to interpret or accurately categorize many audiogram results in clinical practice, posing challenges in applying research results to clinical settings. Traditional audiogram types do not provide very accurate predictions of hearing recovery. Previous studies have indicated that audiogram configurations with better prognoses, such as ascending and U-shaped patterns, tend to exhibit relatively mild hearing loss at 4 kHz, whereas descending audiograms—typically associated with poorer outcomes—often show more severe impairment at this frequency.17,18 These observations highlight the prognostic significance of the 4 kHz threshold in SSNHL patients. Building on this evidence, we developed a novel classification method that groups initial audiograms into three clearly defined and straightforward types, based on the 4 kHz threshold, to improve the accuracy and practicality of SSNHL prognosis prediction.

This study aimed to establish a new model for initial audiograms in SSNHL patients, considering 11 confounders (eg, age, sex, affected ear, tinnitus status, vertigo status, dizziness status, diabetes mellitus status, hypertension status, intratympanic steroid injections use, intravenous steroid use, hyperbaric oxygen therapy [HBOT] use, and pretreatment hearing grade), to compare the differences in the complete recovery (CR) rate between the traditional seven-type audiogram model (7-type audiogram) and the new three-type audiogram model (3-type audiogram). The results of this study will provide clinicians with a simpler, faster, and more accurate method to assess and predict SSNHL prognosis.

2. METHODS

2.1. Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Taipei Veterans General Hospital in Taiwan from February 2012 to April 2023. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Veterans General Hospital (IRB: 2022-03-003BC). The study included 965 patients (977 hospital admissions) diagnosed with SSNHL. These patients were admitted to the Department of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery on the day of diagnosis for treatment with systemic steroids and appropriate follow-up.

The inclusion criteria were defined according to the 2019 practice guidelines of the American Academy of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery Foundation (AAO-HNS), which require patients to exhibit SSNHL exceeding 30 dB across three consecutive frequencies within a 72-hour timeframe. Patients under 18 years of age or with known causes of HL, such as acoustic neuromas, were excluded.

According to the 2019 clinical practice guidelines from the American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery, the standard first-line treatment for SSNHL in our institution is systemic corticosteroid therapy, administered either orally or intravenously. Systemic treatment is initiated in all eligible patients who present within 14 days of symptom onset. For patients with contraindications to systemic steroids—such as uncontrolled diabetes mellitus or peptic ulcer disease—or for those presenting beyond the 14-day therapeutic window, intratympanic dexamethasone injections (5 mg/d) are offered as an alternative or salvage therapy. Intratympanic steroids may also be used as adjunctive treatment in cases with incomplete recovery following systemic therapy. HBOT is additionally available as a salvage or adjunctive option, preferably initiated within 2 weeks of onset or within 1 month when used for salvage purposes.2 However, due to the lack of reimbursement by Taiwan’s National Health Insurance, its application is limited to patients willing to cover the treatment cost. Supportive therapies, including plasma expanders (Rheomacrodex) to improve cochlear microcirculation, flunarizine as a vasoactive agent, and neurotrophic vitamin supplementation (folic acid, vitamin B6, and B12), are also commonly administered. Although these interventions are not part of guideline-recommended treatments, they are frequently used in clinical practice across our region to support inner ear recovery. Treatment strategies are tailored based on the patient’s clinical condition, comorbidities, timing of presentation, and preferences through shared decision-making. All patients with complete baseline audiometric data were included in the prognostic analysis of CR, regardless of treatment status. This approach allowed for an intention-to-treat (ITT) style analysis, capturing the prognostic implications of audiometric patterns—including the newly proposed 4 kHz-based model—even in patients who did not receive systemic or intratympanic steroid therapy. Most of these untreated patients either had contraindications to systemic corticosteroids—such as uncontrolled diabetes or peptic ulcer disease—or concurrently declined intratympanic injection due to its invasive nature. As such, their inclusion reflects real-world variability in clinical decision-making and enhances the generalizability of our prognostic model.

The main outcome assessed was the CR rate, with predictive factors including the 3-type and 7-type audiogram results. The confounding variables considered were age, sex, affected ear, tinnitus status, vertigo status, dizziness status, diabetes mellitus status, hypertension status, intratympanic steroid injections use, intravenous steroid use, HBOT use, and pretreatment hearing grade.

2.2. Hearing assessment and data collection

PTAs were conducted by experienced audiologists following the testing procedures recommended by the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA).19 An initial PTA was performed at the first admission (before treatment), and hearing was tracked with at least three to four PTAs or until the patient achieved CR. The treatment outcome was defined as the best hearing within 180 days post-treatment or CR. Hearing recovery was based on the modified Siegel’s criteria, with an average hearing threshold of ≤25 dB HL at 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz considered CR7 (Supplemental Material 1, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A355). To evaluate the prognostic implications of different audiometric patterns, two classification models were applied to the initial audiograms: a novel 3-type audiogram model developed in this study, and a modified version of Pittman’s traditional 7-type audiogram classification.

2.2.1. New 3-type audiogram model

We categorized the audiograms into three types based on the relative thresholds of 4 kHz compared with 0.5, 1, and 2 kHz:

2.2.2. Traditional 7-type audiogram model

In this study, we modified the original audiogram classification proposed by Pittman.20 Instead of using six frequencies ranging from 0.25 to 8 kHz as in the original scheme, we focused on four representative frequencies for classification: 0.5 kHz (low frequency), 1 and 2 kHz (mid frequencies), and 4 kHz (high frequency). To improve the specificity of the classification, we applied stricter numerical criteria, particularly for flat-type audiograms, which helped reduce over-classification and achieve clearer distinction between audiogram types. The definitions for each audiogram configuration are as follows:

Flat type: The difference between the hearing threshold values at each frequency is ≤10 dB, and the average hearing threshold value across the four frequencies is <90 dB HL.

Down-sloping: A downward slope from low to high frequencies is observed, with the threshold at 4 kHz being ≥15 dB HL greater than that at 0.5 kHz, where the threshold of higher frequencies does not fall below that of lower frequencies. Adjacent frequencies may have the same threshold; up to two to three of the four frequency thresholds may be the same, and the average hearing threshold value across the four frequencies is <90 dB HL.

The PTA results were verified by the team’s otolaryngologists and audiologists. Data integrity was assessed for issues such as separators and string errors via Excel, and the generated text files were uploaded into SPSS for analysis.

2.3. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted via SPSS statistical software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables are presented as the means, SDs, minimum values, and maximum values. Multiple regression analysis was employed to evaluate the correlation between the prediction models (the traditional seven-type audiogram model and new three-type audiogram model) and the CR rate, with adjustments made for potential confounders. The significance level was set at 0.05 for all the statistical tests. For both audiogram models, multivariate regression analyses were conducted using the audiogram category as a categorical predictor and CR as the binary outcome. The reference group for each model was set as the category with the worst hearing configuration—“4 kHz the worst” in the 3-type model and “Profound” in the 7-type model. Beta coefficients (β) and 95% CI were calculated to estimate the adjusted differences in CR rate between audiogram types. All models were adjusted for 11 confounders including age, sex, affected ear, tinnitus, vertigo, dizziness, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, intratympanic steroids, intravenous steroids, HBOT, and pretreatment hearing grade.

3. RESULTS

3.1. Characteristics and treatment modalities

Our study group comprised 965 individuals (977 hospital admissions) diagnosed with SSNHL, with an average age of 50.8 ± 15.1 years. The majority of the patients were men (51.5%), and the left ear was more commonly affected (53.3%). Among the 977 hospitalization records analyzed, 155 involved treatment with systemic steroid therapy only, 132 with topical (intratympanic) steroid therapy only, and 601 with both systemic and topical steroid therapy. An additional 89 admissions did not involve any steroid treatment. These numbers reflect treatment patterns across all included hospitalizations and were used for subgroup analyses in treatment-related outcome comparisons. A significant proportion of patients did not experience tinnitus (73.1%) or vertigo (89.5%). Diabetes was present in 6.6% of the patients, and hypertension was present in 13.3%. The most commonly used treatment method was intratympanic steroid injections (75.0%), followed by intravenous steroid injections (77.4%) and HBOT (5.9%). The CR rate was 17.4% in the systemic-only group, 18.2% in the topical-only group, 23.0% in the combined systemic and topical group, and 19.1% in patients who received no steroid treatment. Statistical comparison revealed no significant difference in CR rates among the four treatment groups (χ² = 3.40, p = 0.334). Using the seven-type audiogram model, profound type was the most prevalent type, accounting for 28.7% of cases, followed by flat type (16.8%), down-sloping and rising type (each at 14.7%), jagged type (8.7%), and both tent-shaped and U-shaped (8.2%). Under the three-type audiogram model, the most common classification was 4 kHz the worst, observed in 53.3% of patients, followed by 4 kHz the best in 33.3% and 4 kHz intermediate in 13.4%.

3.2. Post-treatment recovery and hearing loss grades

Post-treatment evaluation indicated that achieving full hearing recovery, characterized by an average hearing threshold of ≤25 dB HL, was relatively uncommon, with only 21.1% of patients exhibiting this outcome. Analysis of the distribution of hearing impairment severity before treatment revealed that Grade 3 hearing impairment was the most common (36.5%), followed by Grade 5 hearing impairment (27.4%), Grade 4 hearing impairment (21.8%), and Grade 2 hearing impairment (14.2%) (Table 1).

3.4. Performance of the 3-type audiogram model

The innovative three-type audiogram model demonstrated significant variations in the CR rate. Specifically, the CR rate for the 4 kHz frequency, categorized as least favorable, was 20%. In contrast, the most favorable 4 kHz category presented a 29% higher CR rate, with a value of 49%. The intermediate 4 kHz category had a CR rate that was 16% higher than that of the least favorable category, which was 36% (Fig. 3). These findings suggest that the three-type audiogram model provides a simpler and potentially more accurate method for predicting recovery outcomes in patients with SSNHL.

4. DISCUSSION

4.1. Novel insights into prognostic factors of SSNHL

The present study, which was conducted over an 11-year period from 2012 to 2023 at a specialized medical center, provides a detailed analysis of the prognostic factors associated with SSNHL. Our findings underscore the importance of the 4 kHz frequency in predicting treatment outcomes, a factor that has not been previously emphasized in the literature. The development and validation of the new three-type audiogram model (3-type audiogram) represents a significant advancement in the field, offering a more nuanced understanding of SSNHL prognosis than the traditional seven-type audiogram model (7-type audiogram).

Our results indicate that after adjusting for various confounders, the three-type audiogram model demonstrated a significant difference in the CR rate among its categories. This suggests that the model’s focus on the 4 kHz threshold, as opposed to the broader frequency range considered in the seven-type audiogram, may capture more relevant information regarding the potential for recovery. The stratification provided by the three-type audiogram model allows for a more personalized approach to patient care, potentially leading to tailored treatment strategies on the basis of the specific audiogram type.

4.2. Clinical utility and model simplification

The three-type audiogram model effectively quantifies CR rates and demonstrates a dose-response effect, enhancing its clinical utility. With its simplicity and objective approach, it allows clinicians to counsel patients on prognosis and make informed treatment decisions with speed, precision, and reliability. Unlike traditional models, it provides a streamlined classification: if the 4 kHz threshold is not the worst hearing loss, the prognosis for CR is more favorable; conversely, the worst 4 kHz threshold indicates a less optimistic outcome.

This model lays a foundation for further research into SSNHL pathophysiology and opens avenues for targeted treatments and improved patient outcomes. By refining our understanding of the relationship between SSNHL and audiogram patterns, this model could help improve CR rates. In summary, the three-type audiogram model marks a significant advance in SSNHL prognostication. Its effectiveness in predicting recovery rates and ease of use make it well-suited for clinical practice. As we continue to refine and explore its broader implications, we are optimistic about its potential to improve SSNHL patient outcomes.

4.3. Limitations and future directions

Despite the robustness of our study, several limitations must be acknowledged. The lack of a global standard for definitions of audiogram types may impact the generalizability of our findings. The definitions used in our study, while based on a comprehensive review of the literature, may not align with all clinical practices or research protocols. This variability in classification could lead to discrepancies in the interpretation of audiogram types and their associated predicted prognoses.

Additionally, the severe classification in the seven-type audiogram model may not fully encompass the spectrum of severe HL, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true prevalence and impact of this subtype. The criterion for CR, defined as an average hearing threshold of ≤25 dB HL, may not account for the recovery status at individual frequencies. This could introduce a cognitive bias in the assessment of treatment outcomes, as patients with partial frequency recovery may still be classified as having achieved CR.

The sample used in this study was derived from a single medical center, which may limit the diversity of the patient population and restrict the applicability of our findings to other settings. The absence of a goodness-of-fit test to compare our sample with the national population further restricts the ability to extrapolate our results to a broader patient base.

Future research should aim to validate the three-type audiogram model in a multicenter setting, ensuring a broader representation of patients and healthcare practices. Furthermore, the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying SSNHL, particularly in relation to the 4 kHz frequency, warrant further investigation. Understanding the biological basis for the observed associations between audiogram types and recovery outcomes could inform the development of targeted treatment strategies. Such research could also explore the potential for early intervention on the basis of audiogram classification, with the aim of improving CR rates and overall patient outcomes.

In conclusion, this study revealed the 4 kHz frequency as a pivotal factor in the prognostic evaluation of initial audiograms for patients with SSNHL, which is a novel concept in the literature. Using this insight, we introduced a new three-type audiogram model (three-type audiogram) that outperforms the conventional seven-type audiogram model (seven-type audiogram) in assessing the CR rate, both qualitatively and quantitatively.
