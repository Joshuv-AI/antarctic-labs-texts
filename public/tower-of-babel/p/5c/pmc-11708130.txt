Mortality After Pediatric Cardiac Surgery in Patients From Disadvantaged Neighborhoods
Authors: Mitchell C. Haverty, Rittal Mehta, Dita Niraula, Andrew T. Waberski, Yves d’Udekem, Jennifer H. Klein
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDisadvantaged socioeconomic status correlates with adverse outcomes for patients with congenital heart disease. We examined individual and neighborhood characteristics associated with adverse short-term surgical outcomes and investigated potential drivers of disparities.MethodsSingle-center retrospective analysis collected clinical and demographic information on cardiovascular surgery patients over a 15-year period (2007-2022) from the District of Columbia metropolitan area. Neighborhood socioeconomic status, determined by the Child Opportunity Index, compared disadvantaged neighborhoods with advantaged neighborhoods. A stepwise multivariable model examined operative mortality and major surgical complication outcomes.ResultsOf the 2562 patients, one-half (49.6%) resided in disadvantaged neighborhoods. In bivariable analysis, nonmodifiable clinical factors were associated with higher operative mortality. Patients from disadvantaged neighborhoods had higher odds of surgical mortality compared with patients from advantaged neighborhoods (odds ratio, 1.52; 95% CI, 0.993-2.315; P = .054), though this relationship was not present in multivariable analysis (odds ratio, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.52-1.58; P = .73). Surgical factors such as The Society of Thoracic Surgeons - European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (STAT) category, patient size, and genetic abnormalities predicted higher odds of operative mortality and major surgical complications.ConclusionsIncreased operative mortality experienced by patients from disadvantaged neighborhoods is influenced by nonmodifiable factors such as surgical complexity, patient size, and the presence of a genetic abnormality. To address these disparities, we must reconceptualize care for the preoperative patient.

Socioeconomic status (SES) is a well-recognized social determinant of health and major driver of health inequalities in the United States.1 SES describes the background of opportunity, agency, and capital that is often based on individual or community education, income, and other resources. Despite the breadth of research into the relationship between health and SES, disparities in health outcomes persist. There are limited data on the impact of SES on outcomes after pediatric cardiac surgery and little understanding of the possible confounders underlying these disparities.2,3

We investigated the impact of socioeconomic background on congenital cardiac surgery outcomes in patients in a single center with a predominantly disadvantaged patient population. We also investigated clinical characteristics that may influence disparities in surgical outcomes. We hypothesized that patients from disadvantaged socioeconomic backgrounds have higher rates of operative mortality and major surgical complications independent of clinical confounders.

Material and Methods

Single-center retrospective analysis was conducted on all patients who underwent an initial index operation for congenital cardiac surgery at Children’s National Hospital (Washington, DC) between January 1, 2007 and June 30, 2022. Patients undergoing minor, non–defect-repairing surgical procedures or living outside the Washington, DC metropolitan area (according to the US Office of Management and Budget) were excluded. A waiver of consent was obtained from the Institutional Review Board.

Demographic and clinical data were obtained from the electronic medical record. Genetic abnormality was defined as any abnormal genetic sequencing (whether a named syndrome or chromosomal abnormality). Operative mortality, as the primary outcome, was defined as death within 30 days of surgery or during the index hospital stay. Major surgical complication, as the secondary outcome, was defined by The Society of Thoracic Surgeons database. The Society of Thoracic Surgeons - European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (STAT) category, an in-hospital mortality risk index, was used to approximate procedural complexity and severity of illness.4

Patient address was geocoded to the US Census Bureau definition of neighborhood at the census-tract level. An associated metropolitan-normed overall Childhood Opportunity Index (COI) for a given census tract was obtained from the publicly available COI 2.0 data set.5 COI is derived from 3 SES domains of health/environment, social/economic, and education.6 COI ranks neighborhoods into 5 quintiles: “very low,” “low,” “moderate,” “high,” and “very high.” Study census tracts were categorized as “disadvantaged” (very low and low COI quintile) or “advantaged” (high and very high COI quintiles). The moderate quintile was ungrouped. Categoric variables were assessed by the χ2 or Fisher exact test, whereas continuous variables were evaluated using the t test or Kruskal-Wallis test as appropriate. Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression assessed factors associated with clinical outcomes. The Hosmer-Lemeshow technique, followed by a stepwise model selection, was used to find the best model associated with outcomes such as mortality. Accuracy of the model was assessed by C-statistic and area under the curve. All assumptions were met, and necessary transformations were done as needed. Significance level was set to α = 5%. Analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Notable differences in patient characteristics were identified among neighborhoods. Race varied significantly with COI designation (P < .001); Black patients accounted for 47.6% of patients from disadvantaged neighborhoods but just 12.4% of patients from advantaged neighborhoods. Patients from disadvantaged neighborhoods were more likely to have genetic abnormalities and to be smaller and younger at the time of surgery than patients from moderate or advantaged neighborhoods. There were no statistically significant differences in STAT category, noncardiac abnormalities, prematurity, or antenatal diagnosis by neighborhood COI.

Bivariable analysis was performed to explore associations with operative mortality as the primary outcome (Table 2). Notably, patients from disadvantaged neighborhoods had a considerably higher odds of operative mortality than patients from advantaged neighborhoods (odds ratio [OR],1.52; interquartile range [IQR], 0.99-2.32), although this was of borderline statistical significance (P = .054). In addition, individual factors of prematurity, postnatal diagnosis, and genetic abnormalities were all significantly associated with increased odds of operative mortality. Comment

Our central finding is that neighborhood disadvantage is associated with a 52% increased odds of operative mortality after congenital cardiac surgery. Although this result meets only borderline significance (P = .054), the clinical implication of a greater than 50% increased risk of operative mortality cannot be dismissed.

Previous single-center studies have shown increased mortality after congenital cardiac surgery for patients from disadvantaged socioeconomic backgrounds.2,7 Our work replicates these findings within a predominantly disadvantaged population that originates entirely from a single metropolitan region. Neighborhood advantage was associated with differences in genetic abnormality rate, but not other underlying health factors such as STAT category (reflecting cardiac complexity), prematurity, and noncardiac abnormalities. Possible explanations include differences in neighborhood rates of pregnancy termination for a genetic abnormality or actual variation in incidence of genetic abnormalities by neighborhood. Interestingly, age and size at surgery varied by neighborhood despite the overall lack of differences in underlying health status, thus pointing to a critical area for investigating disparities in surgical outcomes.

Multivariable stepwise analysis shows nonmodifiable factors, notably genetic abnormalities, to be significant for operative mortality and major surgical complications. The presence of a genetic abnormality cannot be altered to improve surgical outcomes and therefore offers a major avenue for future research. Although up to 35% of congenital heart disease has a genetic cause, diagnosed disorders account for only 2.5% of congenital heart disease cases, a finding suggesting that unidentified genetic abnormalities contribute to disparities in cardiac surgery outcomes.8

Identifying interventions to improve detection and management of genetic abnormalities is crucial. This may include research to identify causal genes for congenital heart disease, development of therapies to delay surgery to allow for patient growth, and exploration of placental health, maternal nutrition, and chronic stress in surgical outcomes. We encourage similar analyses at other centers of diverse size and patient population to identify area-specific disparities and opportunities in cardiac care.

In conclusion, early mortality odds after cardiac surgery in a diverse patient population were more than 50% higher for patients from disadvantaged neighborhoods as compared with advantaged neighborhoods. Inequity in surgical mortality may be influenced by disparities in genetic abnormalities and nonmodifiable surgical factors. We are compelled to rethink care for the highest-risk surgical patients. Optimization of surveillance and care for this vulnerable population of patients, who carry an additional burden of neighborhood disadvantage, may improve operative outcomes.
