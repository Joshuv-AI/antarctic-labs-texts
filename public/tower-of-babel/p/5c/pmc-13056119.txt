Convergence of external and internal stressors on a stress-responsive enhancer of the Sonic hedgehog gene to induce hair disorders
Authors: ZiYu Chen, XiaoQi Chen, KaiBin Yang, HouDong Zhou, YaFei Zhang, Zhen Huang, YanDing Zhang, Ralf Paus, ZhiCao Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceIt is conceivable that stress impacts our health through perturbation of the intrinsic regulatory molecular pathways. Here, we used the fast-growing hair follicles as a model, and showed that various stressors suppress the expression of Sonic hedgehog (Shh) gene, a key regulator of hair follicle growth and regeneration. The transcription factor Early growth response 1 mediates stressor-induced suppression of Shh gene expression. We also identified the long-range enhancer for this regulation, the hair stress-responsive enhancer (HSRE). The stressors analyzed include chemotherapy drugs, noise, aging, and obesity, both of internal and external origin known to disrupt hair growth. This work illustrates how the various stressors are integrated and converged on the regulation of Shh gene expression to impact organ growth and regeneration.

Hair dystrophy and hair loss are often associated with increased pressure in our daily life. The hair follicles (HFs) are highly proliferative, metabolically demanding, well-perfused and densely innervated, thus not surprisingly are sensitive to various stressors (1–8). The hair loss-promoting stress may be imposed by defined external stimuli such as chemotherapy drugs (9–11) and environmental noise (3, 7, 12, 13), or internal metabolic/epigenetic changes during aging (14–18) and obesity (19). The mechanisms proposed range from regulated cell apoptosis (9–11), neuronal-endocrine-inflammation (3, 7, 12, 13, 20, 21), insufficient stem cell maintenance (14–18), and local inflammatory responses (19).

It is unclear whether there might be a common molecular integrator system on which these various stressors converge to elicit coordinated responses. Decades of intensive research has established that hair development, growth, and regeneration are regulated by a small group of evolutionarily conserved signaling pathways such as wingless-related integration site, bone morphogenetic protein (BMP), fibroblast growth factor (FGF), Sonic hedgehog (SHH), and NOTCH signaling (8, 22–25). A plausible hypothesis would be that stress perturbs one of these key intrinsic signaling pathways, thus leading to hair disorders. Indeed, we found that the SHH signaling is specifically perturbed under chemotherapy, which then causes the damage responses within the HF (9, 10, 26). However, whether perturbation of the SHH signaling also occurs under other stress conditions, and how stress is linked to the regulation of Shh gene expression remains unknown.

The SHH signaling is essential for hair development (24, 27–29). SHH is also a major mitogen in the HF (9, 30) and controls HF cycling (31–34). The regulation of Shh gene expression is a paradigm of long-range enhancer function (35–40). While enhancers that specifically regulate Shh gene expression in the limb bud, neural tube, and other tissues and organs during embryonic development have been identified (35–40), enhancers that regulate Shh gene expression in the HF have not been characterized.

In our previous work using colorectal cancer cells as a model, we found that the SHH gene itself is not an immediate early gene (IEG), but rather is quickly suppressed by the IEG Early growth response 1 (EGR1) which acts through a chemotherapy-responsive enhancer 139 Kb upstream of the transcription start site (TSS) (41). Here, we started by mapping the potential enhancers that regulate Shh gene expression within the HF in mice. We hypothesize that various external and internal stressors induce HF damage by converging on the regulation of Shh gene expression. Our results suggest that an Egr1–Shh axis integrates very distinct chemical, noise, aging, and obesity-associated tissue stressors to impact hair growth and regeneration.

Results

Mapping the Potential Hair Stress-Responsive Enhancer (HSRE) of the Shh Gene within the HF.

To identify the potential enhancers that regulate Shh gene expression within the HF, we first mapped the open chromatin regions upstream of the Shh gene using Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin with high-throughput sequencing (ATAC-seq) (42). HF epithelial cells were freshly isolated from the C57BL/6J mouse back skin at 2-mo age after depilation to induce active hair growth (anagen VI) (43, 44). The properties of the separated cells were characterized by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), which contained about 99% E-cadherin+ epithelial cells, and 67% P-cadherin+ HF epithelial cells. ATAC-seq data indicated multiple peaks within 1 Mb upstream of the Shh gene (Fig. 1A), suggesting potential regulatory sites. The locations of these peaks were consistent with previous reports using sorted HF epithelial stem cells (45, 46). As an alternative method to identify open chromatin regions, we performed Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation (CUT-tag) experiments using the histone marker H3K27ac (47). The resulting peaks consistently overlapped with the ATAC-seq peaks (Fig. 1A). In previous work using cell culture models, we have identified the chemotherapy-responsive enhancer through which EGR1 suppresses SHH gene expression (41). EGR1 bound the 130 Kb upstream enhancer region of the Shh gene as revealed by Chromatin Immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (CHIP-seq) experiments (Fig. 1A), suggesting similar regulatory mechanism within the HF.

To characterize the activities of these potential enhancers within the HF, we cloned these peak regions into the reporter construct Hsp68-LacZ and made transgenic mice on the C57BL/6J background (48, 49). To this end, we analyzed the 130 K, 172 K, 333 K, 447 K, and 542 K upstream enhancers based on their peak locations in the ATAC-seq and CUT-tag data (Fig. 1A). A summary table of the enhancer mapping effort is shown in Fig. 1H. Overall, we performed 1,940 pronuclear injections, which delivered 263 live pups; among these, 33 pups were genotyped positive for LacZ, and 12 mice were confirmed to be LacZ positive in the skin by β-Galactosidase staining. Mapping the LacZ-reporter activities in skin revealed that the 130 K enhancer labeled the hair shaft/outer root sheath epithelium, showing some variations and partially overlapping with Shh gene expression (Fig. 1 C and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S2F), while the Shh gene is asymmetrically expressed in the HF epithelium (Fig. 1D). The 447 K enhancer, on the other hand, specifically labeled the sebaceous gland (Fig. 1 C and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S2G). While other enhancers were negative, the 542 K enhancer labeled the precortical hair matrix (Fig. 1G). Based on the EGR1 binding peak and reporter activity, we hypothesized that the 130 K enhancer is responsible for stress-induced suppression of the Shh gene within the HF, which we termed the HSRE. The 447 K enhancer may be termed the sebaceous gland enhancer.

We then constructed HSRE knockout mice on the C57BL/6J background using the CRISPR/Cas9 method to test its function in the HF (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 and Supplemental File 1). Homozygous HSRE knockout mice were viable, normally coated, and the HFs regenerated normally after plucking at 2-mo age, but the Shh mRNA level was 30 to 50% lower than their wild-type (WT) littermates when analyzed at anagen VI postdepilation, as judged by the FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) values from RNA-seq data and qRT-PCR analysis using whole-skin samples (SI Appendix, Fig. S2I). This is consistent with our previous results in cell culture models where multiple enhancers contribute to the endogenous SHH gene expression (41). In addition, we obtained the Egr1 knockout mice which was constructed on the C57BL/6J background. The expression level of the Shh gene in anagen skin in these mice was also reduced as compared with that in age-matched WT mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S2I). Again, this is consistent with our previous results in cell culture models where reduced EGR1 expression led to reduced SHH expression (41). With this combination of genetically manipulated mice on the enhancer and transcription factor that potentially regulate Shh gene expression, we tested their involvement in various stress-induced hair disorders.

The Egr1–Shh Axis Regulates Chemotherapy-Induced Alopecia (CIA).

We have shown in previous work that down-regulation of Shh gene expression is a key early event in CIA which involves the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway (9, 10, 26). It remains unclear how the down-regulation of Shh gene expression occurred in the HF. Here, we found that in Egr1−/− mice as well as in HSRE−/− mice, CIA is partially prevented: the HFs entered telogen at 5 and 10 d after chemotherapy in WT mice, whereas they remained in anagen in knockout mice (Fig. 2A). At 21 d after chemotherapy, the WT mice regenerated black hair through a new round of anagen initiation, while the knockout mice carried a white fur due to the rescue effect which rendered the HFs remained on the same growth cycle (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). These data resembled our previous results using the SHH protein or smoothed agonist (SAG) to rescue CIA (9), where the CIA response was switched from the dystrophic catagen pathway to the dystrophic anagen pathway due to the less severe damage within the HF (11, 23).

At the molecular level, chemotherapy suppressed Shh gene expression at 1 and 2 d after chemotherapy in WT mice; this down-regulation was less significant in Egr1−/− and HSRE−/− mice (80% reduction vs. less than 50% reduction; Fig. 2B). Egr1 expression was increased in WT mice as well as in HSRE−/− mice in the same timeframe (Fig. 2C). The expression change of EGR1 was further confirmed at the protein level by immunohistochemistry and western blot analysis (Fig. 2G and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). In addition, RNAscope analysis verified the expression change of the Shh gene within the HF (Fig. 2 H and I and SI Appendix, Fig. S3), which were further confirmed by western blot analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Please note that while the expression of the Shh gene within the HF was reduced in Egr1−/− and HSRE−/− mice, the reduction of gene expression after chemotherapy was less significant (Fig. 2 B and I). CHIP-qPCR experiments revealed increased EGR1 binding on the HSRE when the HF epithelial cells were analyzed before and 1-d after chemotherapy (Fig. 2D), suggesting the existence of an Egr1–Shh signaling axis in the mouse HF that regulates CIA.

We then tested whether the activator protein (AP)-1 inhibitors SR11302 and T-5224 which target the immediate-early responses could prevent the suppression of Shh gene expression in the HF, an effect shown in cell culture models in our previous work (41). Indeed, both SR11302 and T-5224 partially rescued CIA in the mouse model (Fig. 2A), prevented Egr1 up-regulation, and maintained Shh gene expression (Fig. 2 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Thus, chemotherapy up-regulated EGR1 expression, which then bound the HSRE and suppressed Shh gene expression; the up-regulation of EGR1 was prevented by the AP-1 inhibitors SR11302 and T-5224, leading to a rescue effect.

The Egr1–Shh Axis Regulates Noise-Induced Hair Dystrophy.

Noise-induced hair dystrophy has been modeled and characterized previously (12, 13, 50–53). The sonic stress is also widely used by gardeners as a commercial rodent repellent. The perifollicular neurogenic skin inflammation induced by sonic stress critically depends on functional mast cells, substance P, NGF, and likely involves other neuroendocrine factors and neuro-immune crosstalk (3, 12, 13, 50, 51). Here, we used a commercial device that emits 25 kHz ultrasound to treat the mice (Fig. 3A). Consistent with previous reports (12, 13, 50, 51), we found that after exposing the mice to noise for 2 d, the diameters of the hair bulb were reduced (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S4), and apoptosis in the hair matrix was increased as detected by active Caspase-3 staining (Fig. 3C). We also detected increased expression of NGF, TNFα and substance P in mouse skin after noise exposure for one or two days (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), demonstrating that the HFs showed the expected in vivo responses to noise.

Interestingly, we found a significant decrease of Shh gene expression, and increased Egr1 expression in skin after noise exposure (Fig. 3 D and E). These were further confirmed by western blot analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S4), immunohistochemistry (Fig. 3I), and RNAscope analysis (Fig. 3 J and K). Please note that while the Shh level was reduced in Egr1−/− and HSRE−/− HFs, the reduction was less severe after noise exposure (Fig. 3 D and K). Increased EGR1 binding on the HSRE in WT mice was also detected in the HF epithelial cells after noise exposure (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, the AP-1 inhibitors SR11302 and T-5224 both prevented Egr1 up-regulation and the down-regulation of Shh gene expression (Fig. 3 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Phenotypically, the reduction of hair bulb diameter and increase of active Caspase-3 positive apoptotic cells were also prevented in Egr1−/− and HSRE−/− mice, and after treatment by the AP-1 inhibitors SR11302 and T-5224 (Fig. 3 G and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Together, our data support a critical contribution of the Egr1–Shh axis in noise-induced HF dystrophy.

The Egr1–Shh Axis Contributes to the Retarded Hair Regeneration in Aged Mice.

Retarded hair regrowth after depilation in aged mice has been documented in previous studies (15, 16, 54). Although the mechanism governing this phenomenon is likely to be complex, increased expression of signaling inhibitors such as BMP, Follistatin, DKK1 and SFRP4 (14, 54), and impaired epithelial/dermal stem cell maintenance (15–18, 55) have been proposed. Given the critical role of SHH signaling in hair growth control, we wondered whether there are alterations in the SHH signaling during hair regeneration in aged mice.

We first confirmed that hair regrowth postdepilation was indeed retarded in aged mice: judged by skin color change (15, 16, 54, 56), hair regrowth was delayed for about 3.3 d in aged mice (18 to 24 mo) as compared with young mice (2 to 3 mo; 9.0 d vs. 12.3 d; Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A); judged by the criteria of HF cycling stages, the HFs in young mice entered anagen VI at Day 8 postdepilation, whereas they were in anagen IIIc or IV in aged mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). The final hair fiber length was similar, however, because the hair growth phase was slightly prolonged in aged mice: the HF entered telogen at 23 d postdepilation in young mice, while in aged mice the HFs entered telogen at 27 d postdepilation (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). In addition, we found only slightly reduced percentage of the zigzag hair type, and no obvious change of hair fiber diameters in aged mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S6), a result differs from the previous report where these parameters were compared between the first and second hair coat (57).

We then examined at what stage hair regrowth was retarded in aged mice. Depilation induced a cascade of molecular events that leads to hair regeneration including CCL2-mediated inflammation as the wound signal, and increased TNFα-secreting macrophages in the skin macroenvironment (58). We found similar Ccl2 expression at 24 h, and similar Tnfα expression at 4 d postdepilation (SI Appendix, Fig. S7), suggesting that hair regeneration was initiated similarly in aged mice.

We further quantified the expression of Shh mRNA by RNA-sequencing (FPKM) and qRT-PCR: similar levels of Shh mRNA were found at Day 4 postdepilation; however, at Day 8 postdepilation, the Shh mRNA level in aged mice was only about 20% of that in young mice (Fig. 4B). This was further verified by western blot analysis at the protein level, together with increased EGR1 protein expression in aged mouse skin (Fig. 4C). Immunohistochemistry and RNAscope analysis confirmed the increased EGR1 expression and decreased Shh mRNA in the hair matrix keratinocytes at Day 8 postdepilation, respectively (Fig. 4 D and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). When single cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) data was profiled and compared, we confirmed the increased Egr1 expression in the hair matrix keratinocytes in aged mice at Day 8 postdepilation (P = 2.2e-16; Fig. 4E; mapping details in SI Appendix, Fig. S9). A time-serial RNAscope analysis was conducted, which revealed the reduced Shh expression in aged HFs (Fig. 4G and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). This is followed by a time-serial CHIP analysis using the HF epithelial cells, which revealed increased EGR1 binding on the HSRE in aged mice (Fig. 4H). Collectively, our data support the notion that in aged mice, Shh gene expression was significantly reduced which may contribute to the retarded hair regeneration.

To probe the significance of reduced SHH signaling in hair regeneration in aged mice, we examined whether the AP-1 inhibitors SR11302 and T-5224 can promote hair regrowth. Indeed, both inhibitors as well as SAG promoted hair regrowth, as judged by mouse skin color change (Fig. 4I and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Furthermore, SR11302 treatment reduced Egr1 expression and increased Shh expression in aged mouse skin as determined by qRT-PCR analysis (Fig. 4J). Consistently, hair regrew faster in aged HSRE−/− mice than in age-matched WT mice (Fig. 4I and SI Appendix, Fig. S6): while Egr1 expression was increased in both aged WT and HSRE−/− mice, the down-regulation of Shh gene expression was not significant in aged HSRE−/− mice (Fig. 4K). Thus, the Egr1–Shh axis contributes to the retarded hair regrowth in aged mice, which can be targeted pharmacologically to promote hair regeneration.

The Egr1–Shh Axis Contributes to the Retarded Hair Regeneration in Obese Mice.

Retarded hair regrowth in obese mice has been reported (19) and is confirmed by our independent analysis: in average, hair regrowth was delayed for about 2.4 d postdepilation in ob/ob mice as compared with age-matched WT C57BL/6J mice when judged by skin color change (9.0 d vs. 11.4 d; Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). The HFs were in anagen IIIc or IV at Day 8 postdepilation, as compared with anagen VI in WT control mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Again, the final hair length is similar in WT and ob/ob mice, because the retarded regrowth was accommodated by a prolonged hair growth phase (SI Appendix, Fig. S6).

We then quantified the expression of the Shh gene in ob/ob and age-matched WT mouse skin by RNA-sequencing and qRT-PCR analysis: similar levels of Shh mRNA were detected at Day 4 postdepilation; however, at Day 8 postdepilation, the Shh mRNA level in ob/ob mice was about 35% of that in WT mice (Fig. 5B). The decreased SHH protein expression as well as increased EGR1 expression were confirmed by western blot analysis using whole skin lysates at Day 8 postdepilation (Fig. 5C). Immunohistochemistry and RNAscope analysis confirmed the increased EGR1 expression and decreased Shh mRNA level in the hair matrix (Fig. 5 D and F). Profiling scRNA-seq data confirmed the increased Egr1 expression in the hair matrix keratinocytes (P = 2.2e-16; Fig. 5E; mapping details in SI Appendix, Fig. S9). A time-serial RNAscope analysis was conducted, which revealed the reduced Shh expression in ob/ob mice (Fig. 5G and SI Appendix, Fig. S8). This is followed by a time-serial CHIP analysis using the HF epithelial cells, which revealed increased EGR1 binding on the HSRE in ob/ob mice (Fig. 5H). Thus, it appears the Egr1–Shh axis is also involved in the regulation of retarded hair regrowth in obese mice.

We functionally validated the significance of this Egr1–Shh axis in hair regrowth in obese mice. First, the AP-1 inhibitors SR11302 and T-5224 both promoted hair regrowth in ob/ob mice (Fig. 5I and SI Appendix, Fig. S8), just as effective as SAG which was reported previously (19). Then, SR11302 treatment reduced Egr1 expression and increased Shh expression in ob/ob mouse skin (Fig. 5J), confirming that the rescue is through the Egr1–Shh signaling axis. Finally, we used a high-fat diet to feed the HSRE−/− mice and age-matched WT mice. This led to delayed hair regrowth in WT mice, as reported previously (19); however, this did not occur in HSRE−/− mice (Fig. 5I and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Egr1 expression was increased in both WT and HSRE−/− mouse skin after high-fat diet feeding, however, the expression of the Shh gene was reduced in WT but not in HSRE−/− mouse skin (Fig. 5K). These results suggested that the Egr1–Shh axis regulates hair regrowth in obese mice.

Discussion

In this work, we demonstrate that a variety of internal and external stressors—chemotherapy drugs, noise, aging, and obesity—all promoted HF damage by converging on the regulation of Shh gene expression. The combination of enhancer (HSRE) and transcription factor EGR1—while essential for maintaining Shh gene expression in the HF under normal physiological condition—functions as the molecular switchboard that integrates very distinct stress stimuli to regulate Shh gene expression and impacts hair growth and regeneration (Fig. 6). Two chemical agents that target this Egr1–Shh axis alleviate the suppression of the Shh gene and rescue stress-induced hair disorders.

Convergence of external and internal stressors on the Egr1–Shh axis to induce hair disorders. (A) The HSRE is located at 130 Kb upstream of the TSS of Shh gene. The 447 K enhancer is active in the sebaceous gland, and the zone of polarizing activity regulatory sequence (ZRS) is about 900 Kb upstream which regulates Shh gene expression during limb bud development. (B) A common theme for stress-induced hair disorder is the convergence of medication (chemotherapy drugs), noise, aging, and obesity on the regulation of Shh gene expression. (C) HSRE together with EGR1 contribute to Shh gene expression within the HF. Under stress, increased EGR1 binding inhibited Shh gene expression. In the absence of HSRE, the expression of the Shh gene is reduced, however, the HF stress response was attenuated.

SHH signaling is among the small set of evolutionarily conserved key pathways that regulate hair development, growth, and regeneration (22–25). We have shown in previous research that the suppression of the Shh gene is among the earliest events in the HF after chemotherapy: down-regulation of Shh expression occurred within 6 to 12 h posttreatment, which was in the same timeframe of genomic DNA damage responses, the activation of p53 and MAPK pathways, and well before the onset of keratinocyte apoptosis in the hair matrix (9). In the colorectal cancer cell culture model, we found that an EGR1–SHH axis led to the suppression of the SHH gene in cancer chemotherapy (41). Here, we expand the in vitro work, and establish that a similar Egr1–Shh axis within the mouse HF mediates stress-induced hair disorders. Our results emphasize the critical role of SHH signaling in hair growth control (9, 29, 30), and suggest that the Shh gene is a quick, stress-responsive gene that impacts hair growth and regeneration.

It is likely that the various stressors trigger the expression of Egr1 through distinct mechanisms. For instance, chemotherapy drugs may activate Egr1 expression through DNA damage responses and/or cytokine induction (41, 59, 60). Noise-induced NGF, substance-P, and mast cell activation may trigger Egr1 expression (3, 12, 13, 50–53). In aging, the senescence-associated secretory phenotype as well as other intrinsic epigenetic alternations (61, 62) may contribute to the increased Egr1 expression within the hair matrix keratinocytes. And finally, inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and IL6 may contribute to increased Egr1 expression in obese mice (19). While the specific upstream factors may differ, the increased Egr1 expression nonetheless leads to the suppression of the Shh gene and impacts hair growth/regeneration. Consequently, the AP-1 inhibitors that target the immediate early responses to suppress Egr1 expression, which we showed in cell culture models (41), work well in the HF and rescue stress-induced hair disorders.

The regulation of Shh gene expression in embryonic development attracts extensive investigation (35–40). In this study, we found many potential regulatory sites of the Shh gene in the HF, and identified two enhancers that showed regional-specific activity: one labeled the sebaceous gland, and the other labeled the HF epithelium with some variations (HSRE). Variations in the reporter activity may arise from the random insertional nature of the transgenic assay, as described in previous work (63). HSRE is important for the regulation of Shh gene expression: its knockout leads to a 30~50% reduction of Shh mRNA level in the HF. However, the growth and regeneration of the HF appears normal in HSRE−/− mice, suggesting additional enhancers are involved to maintain a substantial level of Shh gene expression. The expression of the Shh gene was also reduced in Egr1−/− HFs. Given that high concentrations of growth factors including FGF2 and EGF suppressed Shh expression in the HF (64), we consider the Egr1–Shh axis is likely involved in regulating Shh expression in homeostasis. Importantly, our functional data established the stress-responsive nature of HSRE (Fig. 6C).

The SHH signaling is crucial for the development, homeostasis, and regeneration of many organs (65–67). It is possible that the Egr1–Shh axis also contributes to the perturbed homeostasis and regeneration of other organs under stress. For example, dementia is a common adverse effect in chemotherapy, and impaired memory is also associated with increased mental stress (68, 69); these may be due to the perturbed SHH signaling in the hippocampus where continuous neurogenesis is required for memory (70). However, the expression of the Shh gene is under the regulation of tissue-specific enhancers. Sequence alignment revealed that the HSRE only has homology in mammals, but not in chicken, Xenopus, or zebrafish (SI Appendix, Fig. S2E). Thus, the specific enhancer and transcription factor mediating stress responses in other organs remain an open question. Overall, our findings may establish a paradigm that links the various internal and external stressors to the key intrinsic signaling molecules to impact organ growth and regeneration.

Limitations of the Study.

This work is limited by our capability to specifically manipulate Egr1 expression within the HF. While the HSRE is tissue-specific because the 130 K upstream enhancer of the Shh gene is only mapped in the HF but not in other tissues (35–40), Egr1 is ubiquitously expressed. Given that K14 is not expressed in the hair matrix where the Shh gene is expressed, this promoter is not ideal. The ShhGFPcre mouse (30) is not ideal either, because one copy of the Shh gene is inactivated thus its endogenous expression in skin was reduced and showed high variation (SI Appendix, Fig. S2J). This awaits the availability of better tools in the future. The other enhancers that help to maintain Shh gene expression within the HF under physiological conditions and in the absence of HSRE also deserve further characterization, including the possibility of “hidden enhancers” without histone markers detectable by ATAC-seq/CHIP-seq (71).

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement.

All animal experiments were approved by the ethics committee of Shenzhen University and Fujian Normal University.

Experimental Animals.

Regular C57BL/6J mice were purchased from the Gempharmatech, Foshan, Guangdong, China. C57BL/6J mice were housed up to 18 to 28 mo when used as aged mice. Aged mice were wax-depilated to induce hair regeneration and synchronize hair growth (43). Usually two to three rounds of induction were required to achieve synchronized hair regrowth in aged mice (19). For other experiments, mice were used at 2 to 3 mo age (young mice). Egr1 knockout mice (# S-KO-01852, C57BL/6J background) were purchased from the Cyagen, Guangzhou, China. Ob/ob mice (# JKM000632, C57BL/6J background) were purchased from the Junke Bioengineering, Nanjing, China. Obese mice were delivered at 2-mo age and were housed for 1 to 2 wk before experiment. Ob/ob mice were used till 6-mo age. ShhGFPcre mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (# 005622, C57BL/6J background) and genotyped accordingly. Primer sequences are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1.
