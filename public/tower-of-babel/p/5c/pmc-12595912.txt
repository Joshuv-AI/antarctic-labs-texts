Land-based carbon neutrality efficiency in the European Union
Authors: Ming-Chung Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe European Union (EU) has promised to achieve net-zero emissions via carbon neutrality by 2050. The aim of this research is to evaluate carbon neutrality efficiencies on forest and agricultural lands in the EU.ResultsThe study finds that carbon neutrality efficiency can be computed by a multiplication of carbon emissions efficiency, carbon sink efficiency, and the target of carbon neutrality. We further divide the EU countries into a green group with continuous carbon emission reduction, a gray group with a continuous carbon emission increase, and a mixed group without a continuous carbon emission reduction or increase.ConclusionsThe findings are as follows. (i) Carbon neutrality management is less of a focus than carbon emission management. (ii) There is a trade-off relationship between forest carbon neutrality efficiency and agricultural land efficiency. (iii) Countries in the green group exhibit great heterogeneity in their GDP than those in the gray and mixed groups. (iv) The green group countries exhibit heterogeneous economic structures, and their carbon neutrality performance reflects the overall pattern observed in the EU.Keywords: Carbon neutrality, Forest, Agricultural land, EU

Background

Global warming and climate change are an unstoppable trend, spurring climate adaptation to be the main strategy of many governments and societies. Some important global climate cooperation actions include the first earth summit in 1992, the Kyoto Protocol adopted in 1997 and put into effect in 2005, and the Paris Agreement in 2015. The International Energy Agency (IEA) published a comprehensive study in 2021 about Net Zero, demonstrating things that need to be done in order for the world to reach net-zero emissions by the year 2050.

Carbon neutrality describes a state of net-zero emissions that can be achieved by technologies of carbon capture and storage (CCS) or natural carbon sink, which also covers negative carbon emissions for forests, agricultural lands, and wetlands. Those types of land have the function of removing carbon emissions in the atmosphere and are called green carbon, yellow carbon, and blue carbon, respectively. This research observes the amounts of CO2 emissions and carbon sinks of forests and agricultural lands in the European Union (EU)’s 27 member states from years 2010 to 2020 and presents their results as follows.

Figures 1 and 2 present that the amount of carbon emissions has a quadric increase with the gross domestic product (GDP), while the amounts of forests and agricultural land have a quadric decrease with GDP. This phenomenon means that the gap between carbon emissions and forest carbon sink and the gap between carbon emissions and agricultural land carbon sink become greater under an increase in GDP. The levels of GDP, carbon emissions, and carbon sinks of forests and agricultural land are the results of economy activity, but some worrisome phenomena appear from the economic and environmental data. In this study we want to target the efficiency of resource application in order to achieve the aims of net-zero emissions and carbon neutrality. Based on this line, the study first sets up a method to compute carbon neutrality efficiency and then measures the performances of carbon neutrality in the EU and its 27 member countries.

To clarify the conceptual distinctions among carbon emissions, carbon sinks, and carbon neutrality efficiency, we provide the following explanation: (i) carbon emissions refer to the amount of CO2 released as a result of human or economic activities; (ii) carbon sinks refer to the amount of CO2 absorbed or removed from the atmosphere by natural systems such as forests and agricultural land; (iii) carbon neutrality efficiency, as innovatively defined in this study, is an integrated measure that captures both the efficiency of emission reductions and the effectiveness of carbon sink operations. By decomposing this measure into emissions efficiency and sink efficiency, we can more effectively evaluate a country’s progress toward achieving net-zero emissions.

This study employs carbon sinks from forests and agricultural land referred to as green carbon and yellow carbon, respectively to offset CO2 emissions using the Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) approach. A key advantage of the DEA method is that it enables the calculation of carbon sink and carbon neutrality scores for forests and agricultural land without being affected by differences in units of measurement for green carbon, yellow carbon, or CO2 emissions. Moreover, the DEA approach identifies the necessary adjustments for forest and agricultural land carbon sinks, as well as CO2 emissions, to reach their efficient levels, i.e., the target values for forest and agricultural land carbon sinks and CO2 emissions. Data on the actual carbon sink capacities of forests and agricultural land, along with CO2 emissions, are obtained from the World Bank dataset. The units used are square kilometers (km2) for forest and agricultural land, and kiloton (kt) for CO2 emissions. However, this study excludes wetlands associated with blue carbon that also contribute to carbon sequestration, due to insufficient and inconsistent national-level data across EU countries in the EU database during the study period. Literature review

Carbon neutrality directly relates to carbon emissions and carbon removal. Indeed, greater carbon emission efficiency can reduce carbon emissions, while higher carbon sink efficiency can raise carbon removal, both of which achieve the aim of carbon neutrality. The promotion of carbon emission efficiency implies that carbon emissions are reduced to an optimal amount. Conversely, the promotion of carbon sink efficiency means that carbon sink capacity increases to an optimal amount. Because the research of Zhang and Deng [1] and Yin et al. [2] on carbon sink efficiency does not start from the idea of optimal carbon sink capacity, this paper extends this idea to propose that efficient carbon neutrality results from an optimal amount of carbon emissions and optimal carbon sink capacity.

For the carbon emission efficiency literature, Chang et al. [3] and Chang and Hu [4] take the ratio of optimal amount of carbon emissions to actual amount of carbon emissions as an indicator to measure carbon emission efficiency. Wang et al. [5] indicate that renewable energy development, which causes carbon emission reduction, has a positive influence on better emission efficiency. Low-carbon consumption and production behaviors also prompt carbon emission efficiency. Dubois et al. [6] explore the effect of a change in consumption behavior on greenhouse gas (GHG) reduction in high-income European countries. Rondoni and Grasso [7] review the literature about the effect of carbon footprint label on consumer behavior and discuss its influence on industry. Grainger and Smith [8] study the role of low-carbon and high-carbon materials and mention that metal is a kind of low-carbon material, whereas wood is a kind of high-carbon material. A slow technological change in low-carbon material application does not prompt carbon emission efficiency and further obstructs carbon neutrality achievement. Dong et al. [9] indicate that green technology innovation helps emission efficiency in regard to realizing carbon neutrality.

Aside from carbon emissions, carbon sink is also a critical factor for carbon neutrality. Zhang and Deng [1] investigate carbon sink efficiency in cities of China. They conclude that tertiary industry development, economic growth, foreign direct investment, technological progress, and environmental regulation setting can prompt carbon sink efficiency to achieve carbon neutrality. Some studies investigate carbon sink capacity, such as Bernal et al. [10] who claim that planted forests and woodlots have the highest carbon emission removal rate of between 4.5 and 40.7 tons per year per hectare during the first 20 years of tree growth. Koweek [11] find that the carbon emission removal rate is between 0.66 and 0.85 tons per square kilometer per year for microalgae in a tropical-to-subpolar ocean. Gogoi et al. [12] claim that there is a significant carbon sink difference among different forest types. Korosuo et al. [13] emphasize the importance of reversing the declining forest carbon sink in the EU and propose climate-smart forest management as a key solution. Fayet et al. [14] discuss the potential of abandoned agricultural land in Europe to contribute to climate and biodiversity goals, pointing to land-use policies as a major influence.

Luo et al. [15] highlight a clear trade-off between vegetation carbon sequestration and fossil-fuel CO2 emissions in the Greater Bay Area of China, underlining the importance of localized policy responses and inter-calibration methods to quantify carbon budgets. Rayer et al. [16] and Chen et al. [17] contribute conceptual and policy-based strategies for achieving net-zero targets, through rigorous definitions of offsetting and broad decarbonization frameworks respectively.

Data envelopment analysis is one approach to examine performance in an input–output process, and its benefit is that it does not concern the measurement unit of input and output factors. Because of different measurement units between the amounts of carbon emissions and carbon sink capacity, DEA is suitable for estimating carbon neutrality efficiency of decision-making units (DMUs). The initial DEA model traces back to the CCR model of Charnes et al. [18] and BCC model of Banker et al. [19]. Both models take a radical approach to adjust the input or output factors. The former assumes constant returns to scale, while the latter assumes variable returns to scale.

Tone [20] presents a DEA model that employs a slacks-based measure (SBM) to adjust the input and output factors for optimal amounts. Lu et al. [21] include the factor of forest carbon sink in the DEA model, but only explore the energy performances of European countries. Li et al. [22] apply three indicators (trade openness, export diversification, and renewable electricity output) to probe the achievement of carbon neutrality, but they do not use any factor for carbon sink. Our paper simultaneously involves the amount of carbon emissions and the capacities of forest and agricultural land carbon sinks in DEA model to investigate carbon neutrality efficiency in order to achieve net-zero emissions.

Methodology

We first apply the decomposition approach to establish an indicator to measure carbon neutrality efficiency. Second, we utilize a DEA model to compute the score of carbon neutrality efficiency.

Equation (4) shows that actual carbon neutrality efficiency is decided by carbon emissions efficiency, carbon sink efficiency, and target carbon neutrality efficiency. According to the structure of TCNE = TCS/TCE, when TCS ≤ TCE, TCNE falls within the range [0, 1], which also results in ACNE ∈ [0, 1]. The condition TCS ≤ TCE implies that the challenges of global warming and climate change remain unresolved under optimal conditions, preventing both TCNE and ACNE from reaching their optimal values. In contrast, TCS > TCE suggests that a viable solution to global warming and climate change is available under optimal conditions. Therefore, when TCS > TCE, we set TCS = TCE to achieve carbon neutrality, i.e., TCNE = 1. At this time, the ACNE score only depends on the CEE and CSE scores. We expect that ACNE ∈ [0, 1], because ACS ≤ ACE describes the facts of global warming and climate change caused by carbon sink capacity not being able to remove all amounts of carbon emissions. Furthermore, some carbon-negative countries, such as Bhutan, Suriname, and Panama, represent cases where ACS > ACE. Therefore, when ACS > ACE, we set ACS = ACE to achieve actual carbon neutrality efficiency, i.e., ACNE = 1.

The multiplicative formulation, , is adopted to reflect the joint performance of emission reduction, sink enhancement, and policy ambition. This approach is conceptually preferable, as the three components represent independent yet complementary dimensions of carbon neutrality: (i) CEE evaluates the efficiency of emission control, (ii) CSE measures the effectiveness of carbon sinks, and (iii) TCNE captures the level of national or policy ambition toward achieving carbon neutrality. Multiplying these components ensures that poor performance in any single aspect proportionally lowers the overall efficiency score, which aligns with the principle that achieving carbon neutrality requires balanced and simultaneous progress across all dimensions.

A DEA application on the estimation of carbon neutrality efficiency

To spur beneficial production performance, a desirable output should increase and an undesirable output should decrease. In the case of carbon neutrality, the firm’s product and carbon sink capacity should increase, and carbon emissions should drop. The objective function in model (5) uses the slack variables of desirable outputs, carbon sink capacities, and undesirable outputs (i.e., zyi0, zcj0, and zsk0) to compute DMU 0’s overall efficiency score (i.e., ρ0). This is a minimization problem, as the SBM-based objective function aggregates slack ratios across desirable outputs, carbon sinks, and undesirable outputs. Minimizing this function enhances overall efficiency in an output-oriented DEA framework. The symbols X, E, Y, C, and S in the budget constraints of model (5) respectively present the vectors of general input, energy input, desirable outputs, carbon sink capacities, and undesirable outputs. The variables x0, e0, yi0, sj0 and ck0 are DMU 0’s general input, energy input, ith desirable output, jth carbon sink capacity, and kth undesirable output, respectively, where i = 1…I, j = 1…J, and k = 1…K. The symbols wy, wc, and ws are respectively the weights of the desirable outputs, undesirable outputs, and carbon sink capacities, where wy + wc + ws = 1. The symbol ε in the weights denotes a slightly positive number in order to avoid a no meaningful value to appear in the weights. The symbol λ is the vector to connect all input and output variables by a linear combination. The constraints Xλ = x0​ and Eλ = e0​ ensure that the evaluated DMU₀ is compared to a convex combination of peers utilizing the same level of inputs. Although these constraints do not directly appear in the objective function, they are essential for maintaining consistency in input levels during output-oriented efficiency evaluation. All collected data and variables should be positive.

In simple terms, Model (5) evaluates how efficiently a country transforms its resources into economic outputs while minimizing carbon emissions and maximizing carbon sink capacity. The objective function consists of three components: (i) desirable outputs such as GDP, (ii) undesirable outputs such as CO2 emissions, and (iii) beneficial outputs such as carbon sinks from forest and agricultural land. The model adjusts each DMU’s outputs using slack variables to indicate how far it is from an ideal or efficient benchmark. The constraints ensure that the adjusted inputs and outputs remain within the bounds of the observed data, and that all weights and slack variables are non-negative. In other words, the model illustrates how much a country could improve its performance by reducing emissions and increasing carbon sinks if it were to operate like the most efficient peers in the sample. We list the features of model (5) as follows. (i) It is an output-oriented slacks-based measure DEA model and suitable for applying to a situation that simultaneously considers production, carbon sink capacity, and carbon emissions. (ii) The weights in the model connecting production, carbon sink capacity, and carbon emissions are endogenous variables instead of being based on any arbitrary decision. (iii) The slack variables of carbon sink capacities and carbon emissions coming from the model’s outcome can be used to estimate disaggregate efficiency; i.e., carbon sink efficiency, carbon emissions efficiency, and carbon neutrality efficiency. As with a usual DEA model, our model does not allow for vacancy data and negative numbers during the test period. We emphasize that model (5) in this paper differs from the SBM model of Tone [20] since the weights in our model are decided by slack variables. These slacks are used to adjust the actual outputs to their efficient frontier levels. Based on this, we derive the following efficiency measures: (i) CO2 emissions efficiency (CEE) is calculated as the ratio of efficient carbon emissions (ACE − zc) to actual carbon emissions (ACE), and is also equivalent to the ratio of target carbon efficient emissions (TCE) to actual carbon emissions (ACE), as defined in Eq. (2); (iii) Target carbon neutrality efficiency (TCNE) is defined as TCS/TCE = (ACS + zs)/(ACE − zc). These derivations link the DEA model presented in model (5) to the efficiency expressions in Eq. Country-level summary statistics

Combining emissions and carbon sink data reveals important insights into each country’s capacity to achieve carbon neutrality. High-emission countries with limited sink resources such as Italy and Spain face considerable challenges unless they significantly enhance mitigation technologies or expand carbon sinks. In contrast, countries like Sweden and Finland, with low emissions and vast forest areas, exhibit strong potential for reaching carbon neutrality. France stands out for its balanced profile, with both high emissions and extensive land-based sinks, indicating a feasible but demanding transition pathway. These differences highlight the need for country-specific strategies: heavily industrialized nations may require ambitious policy interventions and carbon offset programs, while naturally endowed nations should focus on preserving and optimizing their existing sink capacities.

Carbon neutrality efficiency analysis

Each efficiency indicator in Fig. 3 is calculated based on the slack variables obtained from model (5), where the overall efficiency score is derived from the model’s objective function. According to the formulas in Eq. (6), the CO2 emissions efficiency score (CEE) is calculated as CEE = (ACE – zc)/ACE. The actual carbon neutrality efficiency score (ACNE) is then calculated using Eq. (4) as ACNE = CEE × CSE × TCNE, where ACNE reflects the actual carbon neutrality efficiency of forest and agricultural land.

Efficiency analysis in the EU

Figure 3 presents the trends in overall efficiency, CO2 emissions efficiency, and the carbon neutrality efficiencies of forest and agricultural land. The rankings, from top to bottom, are CO2 emissions efficiency, overall efficiency, agricultural land carbon neutrality efficiency, and forest carbon neutrality efficiency. This pattern indicates that the EU has achieved better performance in managing CO2 emissions than in managing forest and agricultural land. One explanation is that CO2 emission management has been a focus since the adoption of the Kyoto Protocol. However, following the implementation of the Paris Agreement in 2015, attention has shifted toward land-based carbon removal strategies to meet the 2050 carbon neutrality target. As forest carbon neutrality efficiency lags behind that of agricultural land, improvements in forest management are necessary. This finding is consistent with Korosuo et al. [13], who concluded that the EU’s current forest management trajectory is deviating from its climate neutrality objectives. To enhance forest management, Korosuo et al. [13] suggest that the EU revise its land use regulations to encourage climate-unfriendly practices.

In addition, the relatively higher carbon emissions efficiency scores suggest that the EU has achieved better results in reducing emissions than in leveraging land-based carbon sinks. The disparity between forest and agricultural land neutrality efficiency scores further highlights the trade-offs involved in land use.

Carbon neutrality efficiency and environmental kuznets curve

The environmental kuznets curve (EKC) hypothesis states that an inverted U-shape relationship exists between income and pollution level (Grossman and Krueger, 1991). This paper uses a scatter chart in Fig. 4 to illustrate the correlation between carbon neutrality efficiency and GDP and a scatter chart in Fig. 5 for the correlation between agricultural land carbon neutrality efficiency and GDP. No matter for either figure, no high-GDP country has top carbon neutrality efficiency. This implies that a high-GDP country should take on more duties to remove carbon emissions via forest and agricultural land management. When we focus on top carbon neutrality efficiency, Figs. 4 and 5 show that the number of low-GDP countries with top efficiency is the most, followed by the number of middle-GDP countries, and then the number of high-GDP country is the fewest. Figures 4 and 5 also present that low-GDP countries have great heterogeneous performance on forest and agricultural land management since the numbers of countries with top efficiency and those with poor efficiency are almost the same. The EKC patterns illustrate how economic development influences land-based carbon neutrality efficiency, thereby helping to explain the differences in performance among EU countries.

The cluster analysis complements the EKC discussion by identifying common carbon reduction patterns among EU countries, providing a grouping perspective to interpret heterogeneous efficiency outcomes. In this study, we apply cluster analysis to investigate the carbon reduction pathways of the 27 EU countries. For the cluster result of the 27 EU members, Fig. 7 presents GDP on the horizontal axis and has the green group countries marked as number 3, the mixed group countries marked as number 2, and the gray group countries marked as number 1 on the vertical axis. In the figure there are 12 EU countries with continuous carbon reduction during the whole observation year: Austria, Belgium, Germany, Denmark, Spain, Estonia, France, Croatia, Hungary, Ireland, Italy, and Sweden. However, there are 7 EU countries with a continuous carbon increase during the whole observation period: Cyprus, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Latvia, Malta, Slovak Republic, and Slovenia. The other 8 countries are without either a continuous reduction or increase in carbon emissions during the whole observation period: Bulgaria, Czechia, Finland, Greece, Netherlands, Poland, Portugal, and Romania. The cluster result shows that the countries in the green group have higher heterogeneity in their GDP than countries in the mixed and gray groups, because of a great GDP difference in green group countries.

Cluster result of the 27 EU countries

In Fig. 7 the green group countries marked by number 3 are distributed from high GDP to low GDP, the mixed group countries marked by number 2 are distributed from middle GDP to low GDP, and the gray group countries marked by number 1 have low GDP. The results indicate that the high-GDP countries, including Germany, France, Italy, and Spain, always present continuous carbon reduction, and that the low-GDP countries, including Malta, Lithuania, Latvia, Cyprus, Slovenia, Luxembourg, and Slovak Republic, always have a continuous carbon increase. Based on the EKC concept, an inverted U-shape relationship exists between national GDP and the amount of carbon emissions, and Kacprzyk and Kuchta [27] find that the turning point of EKC is after carbon emissions start to fall when income rises. The results in Fig. 7 satisfy the EKC hypothesis in that low-GDP countries continuously increase their carbon emissions and high-GDP countries continuously reduce their carbon emissions. The contribution of this cluster analysis lies in its identification of structural patterns in carbon emission trajectories and their relationship to economic scale. Although the gray group countries tend to be smaller in population and geographic area, their consistent increase in carbon emissions highlights regions where policy support and targeted interventions may be most needed.

Efficiency analysis in cluster

In a previous section this paper divides the 27 EU countries into three clusters: green group, gray group, and mixed group. We now compare the scores of overall efficiency, carbon emission efficiency, carbon sink efficiencies of forest and agricultural land, and carbon neutrality efficiencies of forest and agricultural land in the EU as well as between the three groups.

Figure 8 shows that the EU and the three groups have higher carbon emissions efficiency than the other efficiency indicators, and the worst performance is forest carbon neutrality efficiency. This finding reveals that the EU and the three groups need to pay greater focus on forest carbon neutrality efficiency than on agricultural land efficiency. The high carbon emissions efficiency and low overall efficiency also denote that the reason causing low overall efficiency is not from a gap in carbon emissions, but instead from the carbon sink gaps of forest and agricultural land. The EU and the green group have similar efficiency scores for all efficiency indicators, illustrating that the green group can be representative of the EU. Taking the EU’s radar chart in Fig. 8 as a standard, the green group’s shape is the most similar to the EU’s shape, the gray group’s shape is the farthest from the EU’s shape, and the mixed group’s shape is in-between.

Efficiency score analysis among the various EU groups

Three special points in the gray group are its highest overall efficiency, highest forest carbon sink efficiency, and lowest agricultural land carbon neutrality efficiency compared to the EU and the other two groups. Luo et al. [28] claim that vegetation carbon sequestration is one accessible way to offset the carbon emissions coming from fossil fuels. One may neutralize a continuous carbon emission increase in the gray group by raising agricultural land carbon neutrality efficiency. Figure 8 reveals a trade-off between forest carbon neutrality efficiency and agricultural land carbon neutrality efficiency in the EU and its three groups, because high agricultural land carbon neutrality efficiency and low forest carbon neutrality efficiency exist at the same time. This result may be caused by that lands are a base of forest and agricultural land carbon neutralities, and that forest and agricultural land sizes are hard to change in a short time, thus causing a trade-off in carbon neutrality efficiencies for forest and agricultural land.

Discussion and policy implications

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of carbon neutrality efficiency for forest and agricultural land in the European Union, employing a decomposition approach alongside a slacks-based DEA model. The results offer valuable insights into current carbon management practices and highlight areas for potential policy enhancement.

Our analysis indicates that carbon emission efficiency in the EU is substantially higher than the efficiencies associated with carbon sinks from forest and agricultural land. This finding corroborates previous research which emphasizes the EU’s policy focus on emission reductions over carbon sequestration. Notably, forest carbon neutrality efficiency lags behind that of agricultural land, consistent with concerns raised by Korosuo et al. [13] regarding the difficulty of aligning EU forest management practices with climate neutrality goals.

The identification of three country clusters, i.e., green, gray, and mixed, further underscores the heterogeneity among EU member states in terms of both GDP and carbon management trajectories. Countries in the green group, in particular, exhibit significant economic diversity while maintaining carbon neutrality performance comparable to the EU average. This suggests that economic scale alone does not determine carbon neutrality outcomes; rather, tailored national strategies are essential.

Moreover, the observed trade-off between the carbon neutrality efficiencies of forests and agricultural land suggests that achieving optimal outcomes in one sector may constrain progress in the other likely due to land-use limitations, particularly in densely populated or agriculturally intensive EU countries. EU land-use policy, under frameworks such as the Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) and the Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry (LULUCF) Regulation, has historically treated the goals of agricultural productivity and forest conservation as separate policy domains. However, this separation may hinder the development of integrated carbon neutrality strategies. For example, prioritizing reforestation could reduce the availability of farmland, while agricultural expansion may lead to deforestation or a decline in forest carbon stocks. To address this challenge, policymakers should adopt cross-sectoral planning approaches that support multifunctional land use such as agroforestry, carbon farming, or rewilding while ensuring that carbon sequestration and food security objectives are balanced. This finding is consistent with the work of Bouma et al. [25] and Montanarella and Panagos [26], who emphasize the importance of territorial and soil management in sustainable development and climate policy.

From a policy standpoint, our findings underscore the need to allocate greater attention and resources toward enhancing carbon sink capacities, particularly within forest ecosystems. EU-level frameworks such as the European Green Deal and the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism should consider incorporating targeted incentives and regulations that promote both emission reductions and carbon removals to support balanced progress toward net-zero emissions.

To improve forest carbon neutrality efficiency, the EU should prioritize incentives for sustainable forest management and afforestation, particularly in countries with large land areas but low forest productivity. This includes revising the Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry Regulation to strengthen monitoring, enforce reforestation targets, and integrate carbon sequestration metrics into national forest strategies. In addition, harmonizing remote sensing and land-use reporting methods across EU member states could reduce measurement bias and support performance benchmarking.

For lower-GDP member countries, many of which fall into the gray or mixed carbon path groups, financial and technical assistance is essential. The EU could consider establishing a “Carbon Efficiency Support Fund” under the Green Deal framework to channel investment into carbon sink infrastructure such as forest rehabilitation and regenerative agriculture. Technical training, knowledge transfer, and cross-country partnerships should also be promoted to help close the carbon efficiency gap between high- and low-GDP countries.

This study has several limitations, including data availability and its exclusive focus on carbon sinks from forest and agricultural land. It does not incorporate additional carbon sink types, such as wetlands, to evaluate the effectiveness of emerging carbon offsetting mechanisms or to examine the socio-economic implications of different transition pathways toward carbon neutrality. These limitations highlight opportunities for future research to expand the scope of carbon sink analysis and assess broader environmental and socio-economic dimensions.

The findings highlight the complex and multidimensional nature of achieving carbon neutrality in the EU. They emphasize that a balanced, context-specific approach to managing both emissions and carbon sinks is crucial for reaching the 2050 net-zero target.

Conclusions

A clean development mechanism (CDM) and emission trading (ET) scheme were established in the framework of the Kyoto Protocol and were kept continuously working by the Paris Agreement. The functions of CDM and ET are to achieve carbon neutrality and realize net-zero emissions. The EU is formally push for the enforcement of the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism in 2026 with the goal of net-zero emissions before 2050.

This research theoretically explores a method to estimate carbon neutrality efficiency and finds that it can be calculated by a multiplication of carbon emissions efficiency, carbon sink efficiency, and optimal carbon neutrality efficiency. It empirically takes the 27 EU countries as observations to investigate their carbon emission efficiency, carbon sink efficiency, and carbon neutrality efficiency for forest and agricultural land. We divide the 27 EU countries into a green group of countries presenting a continuous reduction in the amount of carbon emissions, a gray group of countries presenting a continuous increase in the amount of carbon emissions, and a mixed group of countries that do not present a continuous reduction or increase in the amount of carbon emissions.

The empirical result finds that the EU has paid more attention on carbon emission management than carbon removal management, which can be seen by the carbon neutrality efficiencies on forest and agricultural land being weaker than carbon emission efficiency in the EU. Thus, forest and agricultural land carbon neutrality efficiencies should be paid the same attention as carbon emissions efficiency in order to successfully achieve the target of net-zero emissions. We do find that a trade-off phenomenon occurs between forest carbon neutrality efficiency and agricultural land carbon neutrality efficiency since the former is always weaker than the latter in our empirical result. This may be caused by the areas of forest cultivation and agricultural planting in a country’s territory being fixed or only showing little change. Hence, there is a crowding-out effect on forest area and agricultural area. Countries in the gray group exhibit the lowest agricultural land carbon neutrality efficiency in the three groups. We also find that the performance of the green group aligns with the EU average, which may be attributed to its internal diversity and the inclusion of several large economies. Although the gray group’s overall impact is less significant, it reveals pockets of persistent carbon growth that are critical to achieving EU-wide carbon neutrality.

After the 2015 Paris Agreement, the carbon offsetting scheme has become another way to achieve carbon neutrality and net-zero emissions. Rayer et al. [16] support the use of the carbon offsetting method to achieve carbon neutrality. A company or an organization can acquire carbon sink capacity through a scheme of certified emission reduction (CER) or voluntary emission reduction (VER). Chen et al. [17] mention that these two schemes are a kind of strategy to achieve carbon neutrality. Cong et al. [29] indicate the linkage between CDM and carbon offsetting, in that CDM will experience a decline in investments, but it makes the major host countries adhere to surplus capacity in carbon offsetting credits. One direction for future study is to evaluate how carbon offsetting efficiency can help achieve carbon neutrality. Additionally, the current study adopts a descriptive and exploratory approach, focusing on graphical trend analysis to visually illustrate the relationship between carbon–neutral efficiency and GDP per capita. Future research may pursue a more formal empirical investigation incorporating control variables, panel fixed effects, and robustness checks. Finally, our DEA model builds upon the SBM framework and assumes constant returns to scale (CRS) rather than variable returns to scale (VRS). Although VRS may better capture the heterogeneity among EU countries in terms of size, structure, and resource endowment, the CRS assumption enables us to control for scale effects and reduce the influence of outliers when comparing performance. This study also acknowledges several limitations inherent to DEA modeling. First, DEA is a non-parametric method and does not permit statistical inference, which limits the ability to conduct hypothesis testing. Second, while slacks are used to generate target values internally, they reflect relative rather than absolute performance and may vary depending on the dataset or input–output configuration. Future research may consider employing the VRS specification to better account for country-level heterogeneity, as well as adopting bootstrapped DEA or integrated SFA-DEA models to improve robustness and enable statistical inference.
