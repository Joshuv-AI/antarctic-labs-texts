Recognizing pellagra with Casal’s necklace: a fatal case of anorexia nervosa
Authors: Atsushi Yamada, Kana Unuma, Nobutaka Arai, Ryohei Kuroda, Naoka Umemoto, Takanao Matsumoto, Osamu Kitamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pellagra is a nutritional deficiency caused by the inadequate intake or metabolism of niacin (vitamin B3) and its precursor tryptophan. While pellagra is now rare in developed countries, individuals with eating disorders such as anorexia nervosa or chronic alcoholism remain at risk of developing this condition. We report a fatal case of pellagra in a woman with anorexia nervosa. Autopsy revealed severe emaciation, Casal’s necklace, and additional cutaneous findings, including Russell’s sign. Histopathological examination of the neck lesion revealed characteristic psoriasiform changes. Postmortem serum analysis revealed a markedly reduced tryptophan concentration (16.1 nmol/mL; reference range: 40.9–68 nmol/mL), consistent with the diagnosis of pellagra. This case highlights the diagnostic importance of integrated histopathology, cutaneous findings, and postmortem serum amino acid profiling in identifying fatal nutritional deficiencies. Furthermore, it emphasizes the need to consider pellagra in at-risk populations, even in modern forensic settings.

Introduction

Pellagra is a disease caused by a deficiency of niacin (vitamin B3) and its precursor tryptophan. This condition was once prevalent but is now rare in developed countries due to improved nutritional standards and the widespread availability of vitamin-enriched foods [1–5]. Historically, pellagra was common in populations with diets heavily reliant on maize and lacking adequate protein sources, or in individuals with impaired nutrient absorption [2, 3]. Clinically, pellagra presents with the classical triad of dermatitis, diarrhea, and dementia, and is fatal if untreated [2, 4]. A hallmark dermatologic sign of pellagra is “Casal’s necklace”, which is a symmetrical hyperpigmented rash around the neck that arises from the characteristic photosensitivity of pellagra [5–8]. This report presents a fatal case of pellagra in a young woman with anorexia nervosa who died of hypothermia due to malnutrition-induced immobility. Although pellagra is uncommon in well-nourished populations, individuals with eating disorders or chronic alcoholism remain at risk owing to their compromised nutritional intake [3, 4, 9–11]. This case highlights the diagnostic value of histopathology, cutaneous findings, and serum amino acid profiling in identifying pellagra, underscoring the importance of comprehensive evaluations in suspected nutritional deficiencies in forensic practice.

Case presentations

A woman in her 20s with a medical history of anorexia nervosa and hypomenorrhea was found deceased indoors, clothed in winter attire, with the heating system set to 16 °C. The deceased was a university student majoring in nutrition who adhered to a severely restricted diet, occasionally consuming only a raw egg and small amounts of yogurt. A judicial autopsy was performed approximately 48 h postmortem to investigate the role of malnutrition in her death.

The deceased measured 147 cm in height and weighed 25.4 kg with a body mass index (BMI) of 11.8 kg/m2, consistent with severe emaciation (Fig. 1a). Hyperpigmented, scaly, well-demarcated border lesions encircled the neck (Fig. 1b). Additional findings included reddish-brown calluses on the dorsal hand (Fig. 2a), erythematous lesions with crusted erosions, small vesicles, and a non-healing ulcer on the knee (Fig. 2b), and perniosis-like lesions of the toes with interdigital intertrigo (Fig. 2c). Her hair was unkempt but without alopecia, and the teeth and nails were intact. Subcutaneous fat was markedly reduced (0.3 cm thick). The heart weighed 136 g and showed subepicardial fat atrophy with a watery, gelatinous appearance (Fig. 3a). Brighter blood was observed in the left cardiac chamber, which may reflect underlying hypothermia. The weights of most major organs, including the heart, liver, and kidney, were approximately 60% of the average values for her age [12], with the exception of the brain (1,297 g). Wischnewski spots were present in the gastric mucosa (Fig. 3b). A skin sample taken from the margin of the neck lesion (Fig. 4a) revealed psoriasiform epidermal hyperplasia with acanthosis and hyperkeratosis, parakeratosis, loss of the granular layer, irregular elongation of the rete ridges, and basal hyperpigmentation with dermal melanin dispersion (Fig. 4b). Neuropathological examination revealed focal chromatolytic-like changes in limited areas of the amygdala and hippocampus, but no typical abnormalities associated with nutritional disorders, including vitamin B complex deficiency. Postmortem blood from the right atrial appendage was centrifuged, and the collected serum was stored at 4 °C. The vitamin concentrations were as follows: B1, 103 ng/mL; B2, 314.1 ng/mL; niacin (B3), 9.7 µg/mL. Vitamin B6 subtypes included pyridoxamine (231.8 ng/mL), pyridoxal (148.3 ng/mL), and undetectable pyridoxine (< 3.0 ng/mL). The concentrations of 3-hydroxybutyric acid and zinc were 85 µmol/L and 128 µg/dL, respectively. Serum amino acid concentrations were measured by outsourced providers (SRL, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) [13]. The tryptophan concentration was markedly reduced (16.1 nmol/mL; reference range: 40.9–68 nmol/mL [14]), supporting the diagnosis of pellagra (Fig. 5).

The cause of death was determined to be hypothermia owing to malnutrition-induced immobility in a cold environment.

Discussion

Anorexia nervosa is a recognized risk factor for pellagra owing to the restricted intake of niacin and tryptophan, which are precursors of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide synthesis [10, 11]. The presence of Casal’s necklace in this case provided a key diagnostic clue consistent with classic pellagra. Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide is essential for repairing UV-induced DNA damage; thus, pellagra-associated lesions predominantly occur on sun-exposed areas, including the face, neck, chest, upper back, forearms, and dorsal hands, and may progress to leathery hyperpigmentation [15]. Photosensitivity in pellagra is attributed to an increased kynurenic acid concentration and a decreased urocanic acid concentration. Niacin deficiency enhances kynurenic acid synthesis via the tryptophan–kynurenine–nicotinic acid pathway, while reducing urocanic acid in the stratum corneum, thereby diminishing UVB protection [5, 16]. The differential diagnoses include photosensitive dermatoses such as drug-induced photosensitivity, porphyria, lupus erythematosus, and dermatomyositis. Cutaneous findings in anorexia nervosa arise not only from malnutrition but also from behavioral and psychiatric comorbidities. Russell’s sign consists of knuckle calluses from repeated self-induced vomiting and is indicative of purging-type anorexia nervosa. The deceased in our case showed several skin findings associated with anorexia nervosa, including Russell’s sign, toe perniosis, interdigital intertrigo, paronychia, and delayed wound healing (Fig. 2a–c) [17]. The histopathological features of pellagra vary with disease progression and may resemble psoriasis [18, 19]. In our case, the characteristic findings of pellagra included epidermal hyperplasia with hyperkeratosis, parakeratosis, loss of the granular layer, and irregular elongation of the rete ridges (Fig. 4b). Furthermore, the lack of Munro’s microabscesses supported the differentiation from true psoriasis. Individuals with pellagra may also have subepidermal blisters resembling sunburn injury [20].

Amino acid profiling revealed severe tryptophan depletion, providing biochemical evidence of pellagra. Prior studies report that cases of alcoholic pellagra show similar reductions in amino acids, including decreased concentrations of tryptophan (36.5 ± 39.4 nmol/mL vs. 59.4 ± 166 nmol/mL in healthy controls), leucine, valine, lysine, and methionine [21]. Our findings in this case were compared with population-based data from healthy Japanese women (median age, 52 years; range, 24–80 years) [14]. Amino acid concentrations are significantly influenced by sex and age, while short-term lifestyle factors (e.g., fasting duration, time of day of blood sampling, season, physical activity, NSAID use, smoking, and temporal alcohol consumption) have minimal effects [22]; therefore, these indicators have low individual variability and are reliable for assessing nutritional conditions.

The postmortem biochemical changes in amino acid concentrations remain unexplored, and these effects must be considered before their application in forensic practice. In this case, most amino acid concentrations were markedly elevated compared with the concentrations in living individuals, likely due to a combination of hemolysis, tissue lysis, and potential bacterial biosynthesis (Fig. 5). A previous study revealed that leaving blood samples at room temperature for 6 h after collection increases the ornithine and glutamic acid concentrations, and decreases the arginine and cystine concentrations [23]; however, the tryptophan concentration shows only a mild increase of 7.5% [23]. In contrast, the deceased in our case had a markedly decreased tryptophan concentration, which cannot be explained by known pre-analytical variables alone. Further studies are needed to elucidate the postmortem alterations and improve the reliability of biochemical assessments in amino acid profiling as part of forensic evaluation.

Conclusion

This case demonstrated the diagnostic value of integrated skin observation, histopathology, and serum biochemical analysis for identifying pellagra in forensic practice. Casal’s necklace and features such as Russell’s sign and other dermatologic findings are indicators of broader nutritional deficiencies. The presence of these signs should prompt the targeted identification of fatal malnutrition. Additionally, integrating amino acid analysis into forensic practice may provide an objective tool for diagnosing fatal nutritional conditions; however, further research is warranted to elucidate the effects of postmortem changes on amino acid concentrations.
