Development and Validation of the Brief Support Group Leader Self‐Efficacy Scale Among Scleroderma Peer Support Group Leaders
Authors: Sabrina Provencher, Daphna Harel, Richard S. Henry, Marie‐Eve Carrier, Laura Dyas, Violet Konrad, Marie Hudson, Brett D. Thombs, Catherine Fortuné, Catherine Fortuné, Amy Gietzen, Karen Gottesman, Geneviève Guillot, Michelle Richard, Ken Rozee, Maureen Sauvé, Nancy Stephens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveOur objective was to develop a brief Support Group Leader Self‐efficacy Scale (Brief‐SGLSS) that maintains the core content and measurement properties of the full 32‐item SGLSS among systemic sclerosis (SSc) support group leaders.MethodsDevelopment and validation involved four steps conducted by researchers and a Support Group Advisory Team of people with SSc: (1) item content assessment and prioritization, including a Delphi process; (2) item analysis and preliminary selection, including analysis of SGLSS measurement properties using Scleroderma Patient‐centered Intervention Network–Scleroderma‐Support group Leader Education (SPIN‐SSLED) trial data and a data‐driven item resampling process; (3) advisory team input to select final Brief‐SGLSS items; and (4) comparison of convergent validity and trial change data of the Brief‐SGLSS and full SGLSS.ResultsItems were categorized into six content themes. Two Delphi rounds resulted in one item excluded from consideration. A 6‐item Brief‐SGLSS was selected at the consensus advisory panel meeting based on the resampling process and content and statistical considerations. The Pearson correlation of the Brief‐SGLSS and full SGLSS was 0.96. The standardized mean difference between intervention (n = 74) and waitlist (n = 72) groups in the SPIN‐SSLED trial immediately post intervention was 0.93 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.59–1.27) for the full 32‐item SGLSS and 0.93 (95% CI 0.58–1.27) for the Brief‐SGLSS. Convergent validity of the Brief‐SGLSS and full SGLSS with other measures was similar.ConclusionThe 6‐item SGLSS is shorter and more feasibly implemented than the full SGLSS and maintains similar measurement properties.

INTRODUCTION

Many people with autoimmune rheumatic diseases (ARDs) join support groups for emotional support, information, and education. 1 , 2 , 3 They are particularly important for many people with rare ARDs who cannot easily meet others facing similar challenges or access understandable disease‐related information. 4 , 5 Peer support interventions, including support groups, increase disease management self‐efficacy and may improve mental health and the adaptation of positive health behaviors. 6 , 7 , 8

Barriers to forming and sustaining support groups include a limited number of volunteer leaders, burden on peer leaders, and limited guidance on organizing and leading groups. 5 Commonly reported challenges include a lack of resources, poor coordination or insufficient support from sponsoring organizations, and difficulty managing complex group dynamics. 9 , 10

The Scleroderma Patient‐centered Intervention Network–Scleroderma Support group Leader Education (SPIN‐SSLED) Program is a 13‐week videoconference‐based group program designed to provide knowledge and skills to improve self‐efficacy for leading effective groups and to reduce leader burden. It is a general program that could be easily adapted for other medical conditions. The SPIN‐SSLED trial randomized SSc support group leaders to SPIN‐SSLED training (n = 74) or waitlist control (n = 74) and found that the program improved leader self‐efficacy substantially (standardized mean difference [SMD] effect size = 0.84, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.58–1.09), based on the Support Group Leader Self‐efficacy Scale (SGLSS). 11

The 32‐item SGLSS measures support group leader confidence to successfully perform leader tasks (eg, organizational skills), manage group and interpersonal interactions, and balance group leadership and self‐care needs. SGLSS items are measured with a six‐point Likert scale with response options from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree); higher total scores (possible range 32–192) reflect greater self‐efficacy. 12 The SGLSS was validated in two sets of SSc support group leaders from North America, Europe, and Australia and had good internal consistency reliability (n = 102, α = 0.96; n = 55, α = 0.95) and evidence of convergent validity. 12

The SGLSS length and content redundancy between some of the 32 items suggest the need to shorten the scale, which could reduce the burden of completing it without meaningfully affecting measurement properties. 13 The limited number of people in the target population of SSc support group leaders, however, poses a barrier to data‐intensive methods. Our objective was to use a series of methods that are feasibly applied in a context where it is not possible to include large numbers of participants to develop a Brief‐SGLSS that maintains core content and has similar measurement properties as the 32‐item full‐length SGLSS.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Methods were based on guidelines for shortening patient‐reported outcome measures, including (1) documenting original scale validity and the purpose of shortening, (2) considering the conceptual model of the measure, (3) preserving content validity, (4) preserving measurement properties, (5) documenting the justification for selecting items, and (6) validating the short form in an independent sample. 14 Our methods were designed to reflect the small number of SSc support group leaders internationally and, thus, the impossibility of obtaining a sample large enough to conduct data‐intensive analyses, including a factor analysis of a 32‐item measure.

Step 1 involved item content assessment and prioritization, including grouping items according to content themes to ensure item inclusion across important content areas (Step 1a) and obtaining support group leader input on prioritization (Step 1b). Step 2 involved item analysis and preliminary selection. In Step 2a, we used data from the SPIN‐SSLED trial to evaluate measurement properties. In Step 2b, we performed preliminary item selection based on a data‐driven resampling process. In Step 3, we incorporated analysis results and stakeholder input to select Brief‐SGLSS items. In Step 4, we compared intervention effect sizes from the SPIN‐SSLED trial and convergent validity of the Brief‐SGLSS and full SGLSS.

Results are reported in accordance with the COnsensus‐based Standards for the selection of health status Measurement INstruments reporting guideline for studies on patient‐reported outcome measurement properties. 15 The Research Ethics Committee of the Centre intégré universitaire de santé et de services sociaux du Centre‐Ouest‐de‐l'Île‐de‐Montréal (#2020‐1780) approved the study.

Step 1: Item content assessment and prioritization

Development of the full SGLSS began with identifying potential items from measures of group psychotherapy leader skills, a study of experiences of cancer peer support group leaders, and input from SSc support group leaders. 12 Items were selected jointly by a team of researchers, patient organization sponsors of SSc support groups, and SSc support group leaders. 12 Factor analysis was not conducted due to the small number of participants in validation studies and large number of items, but a high Cronbach's α suggested the viability of a total score approach. To ensure content validity of the Brief‐SGLSS, we conducted an item content grouping exercise, along with item prioritization via the Delphi method.

Step 1a: Item content assessment

We used thematic analysis results from a 2016 systematic review on support group leader skills, knowledge, and attributes (N = 49 studies) 16 and themes addressed in the SPIN‐SSLED training program 11 to generate an initial content framework. Themes included are “Group promotion and resourcing,” “Group and interpersonal processes,” “Group organization and management,” “Group member grief support,” “Environment and inclusiveness,” and “Healthy leadership practices.” A research team member (SP) assigned each of the 32 items to initial content themes, and then themes and preliminary item assignments were discussed among three researchers (SP, RSH, and BDT), and a consensus was reached on a preliminary framework.

Next, an expert panel comprised two SPIN‐SSLED support group leader trainers (LD and VK) and SPIN‐SSLED Support Group Advisory Team members provided input on preliminary themes and item assignments via the online survey platform Qualtrics (Supplementary Material 1). Panel members (1) reviewed content themes and preliminary item assignments; (2) provided feedback on whether the framework reflected the areas of knowledge and skills necessary for effective support group leadership and suggestions for adding, removing, or renaming content themes; and (3) indicated if they agreed with item assignments to content themes and, if not, alternative theme areas. We integrated expert panel input into a proposed final content framework and discussed each item with panel members in a consensus meeting where the content framework was finalized.

Step 1b: Item prioritization

The Delphi technique is used to obtain opinion consensus among group members and can be used to obtain input on item prioritization for scale reduction. 17 , 18 We conducted a Delphi process with support group leaders who had undergone SPIN‐SSLED training 11 but were not part of our expert panel to obtain feedback on the importance of including each SGLSS item in the Brief‐SGLSS.

The Delphi survey was hosted on Qualtrics (Supplementary Material 2). Participants provided sociodemographic information, SSc‐related characteristics, and support group leader experience. In each round, participants rated each item, without considering content area, on importance for inclusion. We used a 5‐point Likert scale with response options of 1 = not important, 2 = somewhat important, 3 = neutral, 4 = important, and 5 = very important. 19 , 20 Free‐text boxes were provided following each item and at the end of the survey for comments.

There is no consensus on the best sample size for Delphi panels, 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 and the ideal participant number may depend on the investigation topic, its complexity, and the heterogeneity of viewpoints and topic knowledge. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 We believed that a larger group with diverse viewpoints best supported study objectives. We, therefore, emailed invitations to all support group leaders who had completed the SPIN‐SSLED program (N = 191), including participants who were in the intervention group of the SPIN‐SSLED trial, received training after being on the trial waitlist, or received SPIN‐SSLED training post‐trial. Participants provided informed consent via Qualtrics.

Recommendations suggest that three Delphi rounds are sufficient for meaningful consensus, though fewer founds may suffice if consensus is reached or ratings are not changing meaningfully. 20 , 22 We conducted two rounds. In each round, participants had two weeks to provide responses, and we provided reminders one week and three days before the deadline. We launched the second round two weeks after the closing date of the first round. We reviewed results and incorporated feedback for consideration after each round. Items rated as important or very important by at least two‐thirds of participants were considered to have reached consensus to be considered for inclusion and those with two‐thirds who rated unimportant or somewhat important for exclusion. Items that did not reach consensus in the first round were rated again in the second round. We circulated reports summarizing results after each round, including summary statistics for each item (mean score, maximum and minimum score, interquartile range, median, and percentage ratings of 1–5 ranking for items).

Step 2: Item analysis and selection via resampling

Step 2a: Item analysis of all 32 SGLSS items

We used data from the SPIN‐SSLED trial (N = 148), which includes demographics, disease characteristics, support group leader experience and self‐reported outcomes, including SGLSS and Patient Health Questionnaire‐8 (PHQ‐8) 23 , 24 scores, and among leaders with experience leading groups, Oldenberg Burnout Inventory (OLBI) 25 , 26 and Volunteer Satisfaction Index (VSI) 27 scores. We examined interitem correlations between SGLSS items, across all items and within themes, to identify potentially redundant items and examined proportions of items with very low or high scores to identify items not contributing substantive information. Additionally, we evaluated change in item scores among those in the SPIN‐SSLED intervention group between baseline and immediately postintervention to identify items that are likely sensitive to change.

Step 2b: Preliminary selection of Brief‐SGLSS items via resampling

We identified subsets of items for the Brief‐SGLSS that included at least one item per content theme and composed a short scale with the highest possible correlation with the SGLSS using a resampling method described by Harel et al. 28 All SGLSS items were included in the resampling exercise. For each possible shortened scale length (eg, 6 items, 7 items, up to 31 items), we resampled the full list of eligible items 100,000 times with the content theme constraint in place such that 100,000 possible shortened scales of that length were considered to select which 6 or which 7 items, for example, would create the best form of that length. We then calculated the correlations between each of the candidate 100,000 shortened versions generated and the full‐length SGLSS and selected the shortened version of that length that maximized the correlation with the full‐length SGLSS. We then proceeded with the 26 shortened scales in consideration (the best scale of length 6, the best scale of length 7, etc). Because the correlation with the full‐length SGLSS is expected to increase as the number of items in the shortened form increases, we visually inspected the maximal correlations between each of the 26 shortened forms and the full‐length SGLSS to identify the number of items that would yield strong correlations and specific items to include. We used R (R version 4.1.2) to conduct resampling.

Step 3: Final Brief‐SGLSS item selection

We held a meeting via Zoom with expert panel members. During this meeting we reviewed, in lay‐friendly terms, steps taken, including the initial assignment of items to content themes, the Delphi process, and statistical analyses, which included item statistics and resampling, and results from each step. Because the resampling method (100,000) only samples a subset of all possible item combinations for each possible scale length, we relied on input from all analysis steps to determine candidate shortened scale versions. We then shared preliminary shortened scale versions of the SGLSS based on item content, stakeholder input, and statistical criteria and asked panel members for suggestions on improving the shortened version, including review of included items. Following discussion, we ensured there was consensus on the final Brief‐SGLSS.

Step 4: Comparison of convergent validity of Brief‐SGLSS and full SGLSS

We compared SMD intervention effect estimates from the SPIN‐SSLED trial based on the Brief‐SGLSS versus the full SGLSS among participants with postintervention data and compared convergent validities between the Brief‐SGLSS and full SGLSS with other trial outcome measures, including the PHQ‐8, OLBI, and VSI, also using post‐trial data from the SPIN‐SSLED trial. The PHQ‐8 was administered to all trial participants, and the OLBI and VSI were administered to trial participants who already led support groups at the time of trial enrollment.

The PHQ‐8 23 , 24 measures depressive symptoms over the last two weeks on a 4‐point scale, ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day) with higher scores indicating more depressive symptoms. The PHQ‐8 performs equivalently to the PHQ‐9, 23 which is a valid measure of depressive symptoms in people with SSc. 24 The PHQ‐8 is available in French and English.

The OLBI (16 items, 4‐point scale from 1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree) assesses exhaustion and disengagement from burnout and has been validated in diverse populations. 25 , 26 The OLBI was initially designed for work‐related burnout but has been adapted for numerous settings and in multiple countries and languages. 29 The OLBI has a two‐factor structure (exhaustion and disengagement) with good measurement properties. 25 , 26 , 29 Higher scores on each factor indicate higher levels of exhaustion and disengagement. Our research team revised the wording of each OLBI item in English and French 30 versions to reflect the support group environment rather than a work environment (eg, “I find my work to be a positive challenge” was revised to “I find my role as a support group leader to be a positive challenge”). Cronbach's α in a sample of 55 people with SSc was 0.84 for exhaustion and 0.80 for disengagement. 12

Leader satisfaction (participation efficacy) was measured using a modified version of the participation efficacy subscale of the VSI, which reflects one's belief that they are making a positive difference through their volunteer role. 27 The VSI was validated using a sample of volunteers from a nonprofit organization (N = 327) and was found to be reliable and predict intent to continue as a volunteer. 27 As in other studies, 31 , 32 we modified the wording of some of the items to reflect participants’ role as support group leaders. The participation efficacy subscale asks respondents to indicate their level of satisfaction on seven items using a 7‐point Likert scale from 1 (very dissatisfied) to 7 (very satisfied).

Involvement of people with lived experience

Members of the SPIN Support Group Advisory Team were involved in all stages of SPIN's research on support groups in SSc and in developing the SGLSS. 12 Team members initially contributed by reviewing qualitative interview data from support group leaders, members, and nonparticipants, followed by the design of the Scleroderma Support Group Survey, which informed SGLSS development by collecting information on the experiences and training needs of SSc support group leaders, priorities of SSc support group members, and reasons why people do not attend SSc support groups. 10 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 Team members participated in developing the SPIN‐SSLED program and validating the SGLSS, 12 which was administered in the SPIN‐SSLED feasibility trial 37 and was the primary outcome for the full‐scale trial. 11 For the present study, team members met with the research team and provided input at each stage of research. They were involved in interpretation of results, reviewed and provided comments on the manuscript, and are co‐authors.

Step 1b: Item prioritization

Delphi Round 1

Of the 191 support group leaders invited to take part in the Delphi study, 46 (24%) provided rankings on all items in Round 1. As shown in Table 1, among the 46 participants, mean age was 61.2 years (SD = 9.4), 43 (94%) participants were female, 37 (of 41 who reported race or ethnicity; 90%) participants identified as White, and 36 (of 44 who reported if they were diagnosed with SSc; 82%) participants had been diagnosed with SSc. Most participants were from the United States (n = 23, 50%) or Canada (n = 12, 26%). There were 35 (76%) experienced support group leaders with a mean of 7.3 years (SD = 4.9) leading an SSc support group.

Of the 32 items rated in Round 1, 28 (88%) reached consensus as important to include, and the other 4 (13%) items did not reach consensus and were included in Round 2. See the Delphi Summary report in Supplementary Material 3.

Delphi Round 2

Of the 46 participants in Round 1, 45 (98%) completed Round 2. Of the four items rated in Round 2, one (item 9) on member recruitment reached consensus as important to include. The other three items (item 1: finances and resources; item 17: helping members relate to others of different age; and item 31: helping members relate to others of different gender) did not reach consensus, and none moved closer to consensus than in Round 1. We decided to exclude one item (item 1: finances and resources) after Round 2 because its rating went down and it was close to the exclusion threshold. See Supplementary Material 3.

Step 2: Item analysis and selection via resampling

Step 2a: Item analysis of all 32 SGLSS items

We analyzed baseline SGLSS responses from all 148 SPIN‐SSLED trial participants. 11 Mean age was 55.4 years (SD = 11.9), 134 (91%) participants were female, 121 (of the 146 who reported race or ethnicity; 83%) participants were identified as White, and 130 participants had been diagnosed with SSc (88%). Participants were from the United States (n = 91, 61%), Canada (n = 22, 15%), and seven other countries (n = 35, 24%). There were 114 (77%) experienced leaders with a mean of 5.2 years (SD = 5.6) leading an SSc support group (See Table 1). Interitem correlations across content themes ranged from r = 0.16 to 0.87, including six interitem correlations that were >0.70. Interitem correlations within each of the six themes all ranged from r = 0.34 to 0.87 (see Supplementary Material 4, Supplementary Tables S1–S7). We did not identify any items with mean scores that were notably low or high compared to other items.

As shown in Table 3, means of all items increased from pre‐ to post‐training by 0.38 (SD = 0.77) to 0.72 (SD = 1.18) points.

DISCUSSION

We developed a Brief‐SGLSS using a novel series of methods that could be applied in a relatively small‐sample, rare‐disease context. The 32‐item SGLSS was shortened to a 6‐item Brief‐SGLSS. The SGLSS is the only scale designed to assess support group leader's confidence to successfully perform leader tasks, manage group and interpersonal interactions, and balance group leadership and self‐care needs. 12 We initially grouped items based on evidence and input from a Support Group Advisory Team and identified six key theme areas to include in the Brief‐SGLSS: “Group promotion and resourcing,” “Group and interpersonal processes,” “Group organization and management,” “Group member grief support,” “Environment and inclusiveness,” and “Healthy leadership practices.” We then asked trained support group leaders to provide input on the importance of each item for assessing support group leader skills and knowledge via the Delphi technique. The Delphi process underlined that almost all items were deemed important to include in the scale, but many items were repetitive, so we used a quantitative approach to identify items for inclusion. To do this, we used data from a leader education and training intervention trial 11 to evaluate item properties and find subsets of items that correlated highly with the full 32‐item SGLSS. We selected the final 6‐item version via a consensus process with our expert panel of people with SSc and support group leader trainers who incorporated content and statistical considerations and then compared convergent validity and amount of change based on the full SGLSS and the 6‐item Brief‐SGLSS. The full SGLSS and Brief‐SGLSS correlated highly (r = 0.96), and convergent validity and change magnitude were similar between the full SGLSS and Brief‐SGLSS.

Based on a 2016 systematic review on skills, knowledge, and attributes of support group leaders 16 and themes addressed in the SPIN‐SSLED program, 11 we identified key abilities and skills, not specific to SSc, that support group leaders need to effectively lead peer support groups. The present study found that a 6‐item Brief‐SGLSS measures these abilities and skills as well as the full 32‐item version. Researchers wishing to assess key skills and knowledge in support group leaders could use the Brief‐SGLSS, and those wishing to do a more in‐depth assessment of support group leaders’ abilities could use the 32‐item version of the SGLSS. The SGLSS items are not specific to SSc support groups, and the scale should be tested among support group leaders in other settings.

Strengths of this study include the novel methods that we employed, allowing us to have confidence in our results despite having a small participant pool. There are a small number of people who lead SSc peer support groups internationally 5 ; therefore, our ability to evaluate the SGLSS and Brief‐SGLSS in an existing database of 148 SSc support group leaders, although not a large sample in many diseases, was a strength in a rare‐disease context. Another strength is that people with SSc involved in support groups have been involved across all of our team's research on support group leader training and were involved in each step of the development and validation of the Brief‐SGLSS. Final item selection was based on their input after review of statistical considerations. There are also limitations. Even though the SGLSS measure can be used across diseases, our data and patient input came from individuals with SSc. They may have had specific perspectives related to this condition, and the SGLSS should be tested in other diseases. Additionally, none of the items in the full or Brief‐SGLSS address technological challenges, which may be important for some leaders, even though they were not prioritized by peer leaders involved in the development of the full SGLSS.

In conclusion, we identified a 6‐item Brief‐SGLSS that measures support group leader skills and knowledge and correlates highly with the original 32‐item SGLSS. The short 6‐item version is a valid measure of support group leader self‐efficacy that will reduce administration burden compared to the 32‐item SGLSS. The SGLSS has been used to evaluate the effectiveness of a training program designed to improve the effectiveness and sustainability of support groups by improving leader self‐efficacy in SSc. The SGLSS, though, is not specific to SSc and ideally would be tested in other patient groups and translated into other languages. Methods from our study provide an example of how to do rigorous scale development in small populations and samples.
