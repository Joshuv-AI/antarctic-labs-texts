Risk factors of incident lung diseases and the impact of DMARDs in rheumatoid arthritis patients: a longitudinal study
Authors: Guangcai Chen, Qing Zhang, Linyu Geng, Yaqi Zhang, Jun Liang, Ziyi Jin, Zhijie Qian, Xinqi Xu, Kexin Zhu, Qing Shu, Xiaojun Tang, Huayong Zhang, Xuebing Feng, Hongwei Chen, Dandan Wang, Lingyun Sun, Wei Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWe aimed to investigate the impact of clinical characteristics and therapy on rheumatoid arthritis (RA)-related lung diseases.MethodsThe retrospective cohort consisted of 1,207 inpatients at baseline. RA-related lung diseases included interstitial lung disease (ILD), bronchiectasis, pleural effusion, and pulmonary arterial hypertension. Kaplan-Meier method was used to measure the cumulative incidence curve. Cox regression was conducted to evaluate the associations between RA-related lung diseases and risk indicators. Logistic regression was employed to examine the impact of drugs.ResultsThe study identified 145 cases of RA-related lung diseases (34.2/1,000 person-years; 95% CI: 28.6–39.7) and 98 RA-ILD cases (22.5/1,000 person-years; 95% CI: 18.0–26.9) over 3.5 years. The cumulative incidence of RA-related lung diseases increased continuously over time, accelerating after 10 years of RA and age 55. RA-related lung diseases was independently associated with older age at RA onset (per 10 years, hazard ratio [HR] = 1.22, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.04–1.42), longer RA duration (per 10 years, HR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.15–1.77), higher Rheumatic Disease Comorbidity Index (HR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.08–1.39), prior pulmonary infections (HR = 2.26, 95% CI: 1.58–3.24), and systemic lupus erythematosus comorbidity (HR = 2.36, 95% CI: 1.35–4.13). After adjustment, ever-use of methotrexate demonstrated a significant protective association against both RA-related lung diseases (odds ratio [OR] = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.44–0.92) and RA-ILD (OR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.35–0.83). Similarly, biologic or targeted synthetic disease modifying antirheumatic drugs (b/tsDMARDs) were also found protective (OR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.35–0.99).ConclusionThis study identifies several clinically significant risk factors for RA-related lung diseases, while demonstrating protective effects of methotrexate and b/tsDMARDs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03915-3.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic inflammatory disease that can eventually lead to joint destruction and disability [1]. While RA primarily involves the joints, nearly 50% of RA patients experience extra-articular manifestations, such as rheumatoid nodules, pulmonary involvement, or vasculitis [1–3]. RA can have diverse manifestations throughout the components of the lungs, including the parenchyma (interstitial lung disease [ILD] or rheumatoid nodules), pleura (pleural inflammation and/or effusions), small and large airways, and pulmonary vasculature [3–5]. RA-related lung disease, particularly RA-related ILD (RA-ILD), is associated with significant mortality [4], and is directly responsible for more than 10% of the deaths in RA patients [5–8].

Most studies on RA-related lung diseases have focused on RA-ILD, with nearly 10% of RA patients reported to combine clinically apparent ILD, and 30–50% combined subclinical interstitial abnormalities [9]. The reported incidence of RA-ILD ranges from 1.8 to 6.7 per 1,000 person-years [10]. The pathogenesis of RA-ILD is mainly linked to abnormal immune reactions affecting the alveolar walls and lung parenchyma [4]. In RA, two potential mechanisms connect joint and lung involvement: either autoimmunity begins in the joints and affects the lungs, or immune dysregulation originates in the lungs and spreads to the joints. Both pathways are closely linked to immune responses against citrullinated proteins [4, 11]. Several previous studies have identified risk factors for RA-ILD, including older age, later onset RA, male, higher RA disease activity, ever smoked, longer RA duration, pulmonary comorbidities, rheumatoid nodule and elevated rheumatoid factor (RF) and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibody (ACPA) level [10, 12–17]. However, in addition to RA-ILD, other types of RA-related lung diseases (like bronchiectasis, pleural disease, and pulmonary hypertension) are also important and represent a serious threat to the prognosis. Studies exploring both the incidence of overall RA-related lung disease and potential risk factors are lacking.

RA-related lung diseases may arise directly from RA involvement or secondary to immune-modulating medications used to treat RA [3]. The routine treatment involves conventional synthetic disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (csDMARDs), and methotrexate (MTX) and leflunomide (LEF) are representative drugs that are reported to cause pulmonary toxicity [10, 17–22], and thus there is a sense of caution among rheumatologists and pneumologists. However, we observe the tendency that DMARDs like MTX and LEF may not increase the risk of RA-ILD in clinical practice, which have also been reported in some studies [23–28]. Evaluating the association between these DMARDs use and the risk of RA-related lung diseases, especially RA-ILD, in a longitudinal design in Chinese patients, and verifying our hypothesis, is essential.

Thus, this study has two primary objectives: (1) to comprehensively characterize the clinical profiles of RA patients while identifying potential risk factors for the development of RA-related lung diseases, and (2) to evaluate the associations between DMARDs therapy (including MTX and LEF) during disease progression and the occurrence of RA-related lung diseases. The findings are expected to provide clinically relevant evidence to guide the management of RA patients regarding lung involvement.

Methods

Patients and study design

The Chinese RA inpatients who consecutively received medical care from January 2013 to February 2023 at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital were enrolled in our study (Fig. 1). We conducted a retrospective cohort study among inpatients without RA-related lung diseases at baseline. All RA patients fulfilled the 2010 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) and/or 1987 ACR revised criteria for RA [29, 30]. Exclusion criteria were (1) The initial diagnosis was not RA or (2) previous and treated RA-related lung diseases, or (3) information missing at baseline. This study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (Approval No. 2023-541-01), with a waiver of written informed consent granted in accordance with institutional guidelines for retrospective research.

Study flow and the inclusion and exclusion diagram for the study subjects

We conducted two designs based on our research purposes: (1) a longitudinal cohort analysis for the association between RA-related lung diseases and potential risk indicators, and (2) a nested case-control study design to examine the effect of drugs on RA-related lung diseases. In the nested case-control analysis, all incident RA-related lung disease cases occurring during follow-up were selected as the case group. The control group comprised cohort participants who remained free of RA-related lung disease throughout the observation period.

RA-related lung disease and clinical data

Demographics, clinical features, laboratory tests, high resolution computerized tomography (HRCT) imaging and therapeutic management were extracted from the medical records system of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital. We defined the earliest hospitalization record with HRCT imaging as the baseline. The medical records of patients receiving consecutive care are regarded as our follow-up records. The follow-up period lasted from the date of baseline to that of developing RA-related lung diseases, or the last follow-up, or death. All diagnoses of lung diseases were based on HRCT, and those images were evaluated in a blinded manner by two experienced radiologists. RA-related lung diseases were defined as RA-ILD, bronchiectasis, pleural effusion, and pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). The diagnosis of RA-ILD was established according to the guideline recommendations [31]. Because the examination of right heart catheterization (RHC) is infrequently performed in China, PAH was defined as either a systolic pulmonary artery pressure > 40 mmHg by echocardiography or a mean pulmonary arterial pressure ≥ 25 mmHg at rest and pulmonary artery wedge pressure ≤ 15 mmHg by RHC [32].

RA duration was defined as the time from RA onset to baseline. Disease activity was evaluated by disease activity score in 28 joints (3-variable DAS28) based on the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) [33]. Swollen and tender joints count in 28 joints, and deformed joints were collected from the medical database. Comorbidities assessment at baseline was based on the Rheumatic Disease Comorbidity Index (RDCI) (ranges 0–9). RDCI was composed of eleven illness categories and was calculated according to the formula: 2 × lung disease + [2 × (heart attack, other cardiovascular diseases, or stroke) or 1 × hypertension] + fracture + depression + diabetes + cancer + (ulcer or stomach problem) [34]. The category definitions were referenced according to the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification codes (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/icd/icd9cm.htm). Additionally, several non-RA-related lung diseases (pulmonary infections, emphysema, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD]), occurred before baseline or during follow-up, were also recorded to evaluate their impact on the development of RA-related lung diseases [10, 17, 35].

Treatment assessment

Treatment patterns were systematically evaluated through comprehensive medical record review. We defined treatment exposure period as spanning from RA onset until either the development of RA-related lung diseases or the last documented follow-up. Medication exposure to MTX, LEF or biologic/targeted synthetic DMARDs (b/tsDMARDs) was categorized as ever-use (≥ 3 months of continuous prescription during the exposure period) versus never-use. Given the retrospective study design, both treatment duration and dosage data for MTX/LEF may be subject to measurement bias. Consequently, cumulative dose (treatment exposure period × dose) response were excluded from primary analyses due to reliability concerns and are presented solely in supplementary materials for reference. MTX and LEF were selected as the focus of csDMARDs analysis due to their status as first-line therapies, clinically significant yet inconsistent pulmonary safety profiles, and the need for population-level evidence to clarify their respective RA-ILD risk associations. Glucocorticoids (GCs) was presented as a prednisone-equivalent dose. GCs maintenance dose was defined as the minimum dose for ≥ 3 months, and maximum dose was defined as the maximum dose used.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages), and continuous variables with normal distributions were presented as means and standard deviations (SDs), otherwise as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). The distributions of continuous and categorical variables between two groups were compared by the Student’s t test (normal distribution) or Mann–Whitney U test (non-normal distribution) and the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, respectively. The incidence rate (per 1,000 person-years) was computed as cases divided by person-years at risk, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated via Poisson distribution. The cumulative incidence curve was measured using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using Log-rank tests.

To identify and quantify the associations between potential risk factors and RA-related lung diseases, multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression was conducted, and hazard ratio (HR) and 95% CI were estimated. Variable selection for multivariate Cox analysis was based on a priori knowledge, including 2023 ACR guidelines [36], predictors consistently reported in peer-reviewed literature, and biological plausibility. Collinearity was rigorously evaluated through Spearman’s rank correlation analysis (retention threshold: |rs| < 0.7) and variance inflation factors (VIFs) (all VIFs < 5), confirming no substantial multicollinearity among selected variables (Supplementary Figure S2). The proportional hazards assumption was checked using statistical tests based on the scaled Schoenfeld residuals (Supplementary Table S2, Supplementary Figure S3). The linearity assumptions of continuous variables were assessed using restricted cubic splines (RCS), implemented via the cph function (R rms package) and nonlinearity was tested by comparing the model fit with and without spline terms (Supplementary Figure S4).

To examine the effect of drugs in the nested case control design, multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed, and odds ratio (OR) and 95% CI were calculated. We performed the same analyses as above when only RA-ILD or bronchiectasis was the concerned event. To address potential confounding by indication, we conducted sensitivity analyses using 1:1 or 1:2 propensity score matching (PSM) with a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the propensity score. Propensity scores were estimated via logistic regression incorporating baseline covariates including sex, age at RA onset, RA duration, smoking status, drinking status, C-reactive Protein (CRP), ESR, Sero-positivity, RDCI, deformed joint, morning stiffness, Non-RA-related lung diseases, concomitant autoimmune diseases, GCs maintenance dose, GCs maximum dose and follow up time. Balance between matched groups was verified using standardized mean differences (SMD) (< 0.1 for all covariates). To assess the robustness of our findings, we performed another sensitivity analysis excluding all RA-related lung disease events occurring within the first 28 months of follow-up (median observation time).

Laboratory examination data with missing rates less than 5% were imputed using the multivariable imputation by chained equations. Statistical significance was determined for P-values less than 0.05 (two-tailed). All analyses were conducted with R 4.2.1 statistical software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics

Among the 1,723 RA inpatients evaluated at baseline, 516 (29.9%) presented with RA-related lung diseases. The clinical characteristics of RA inpatients with or without lung diseases were shown in Supplementary Table S1. Our longitudinal cohort comprised 1,207 RA patients without baseline pulmonary involvement, among whom 145 subsequently developed RA-related lung diseases during follow-up (Fig. 1). RA-ILD was the most prevalent form, accounting for 67.6% of 145 cases. Bronchiectasis (17.2%), pleural effusion (11.7%), and PAH (6.2%) followed. Two of the nine PAH patients were treated with medication (sildenafil), and one had received RHC. It was worth noting that some patients experienced more than one form of RA-related lung diseases.

Table 1 described the baseline characteristics of RA patients who developed RA-related lung diseases and those who did not. Patients with incident RA-related lung diseases had a longer duration of RA, older age at inclusion, and higher ESR levels. We found no statistical differences between the two groups regarding seropositivity or isolated RF/ACPA positivity. Patients with history of non-RA-related lung diseases, especially pulmonary infections, were more likely to develop RA-related lung diseases (33.1% vs. 11.3%, P < 0.001). Among other autoimmune diseases that coexist with RA, Sjögren syndrome (SS) and Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) were the two most common types, accounting for 26.2% and 4.6%, respectively, and these patients with autoimmune comorbidities were more likely to develop RA-related lung diseases (44.1% vs. 33.0%, P = 0.010). Although the median of RDCI was the same between two groups, there may be different distributions (higher quartiles in these developed RA-related lung diseases, P < 0.001) (Table 1).

Incidence of RA-related lung diseases

There was a total of 145 incident RA-related lung diseases during 4244.7 person-years of follow-up, with an incidence rate as 34.2 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI: 28.6–39.7). The incidence rate of RA-ILD was 22.5 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI: 18.0–26.9). The mean follow-up period was 3.5 (SD = 3.2) years, and the median was 2.3 years (IQR 0.8–5.6). The cumulative incidence of RA-related lung diseases increased continuously over time (Fig. 2), with a notable rise after 10 years of RA duration (Supplementary Figure S1A) and a marked increase from age 55 onwards (Supplementary Figure S1B).

Risk factors for RA-related lung diseases

Univariate and multivariable regression results were presented in Table 2. Incident RA-related lung diseases was independently associated with older age at RA onset (HR = 1.22 per 10 years, 95% CI: 1.04–1.42), longer RA duration (HR = 1.43 per 10 years, 95% CI: 1.15–1.77), higher RDCI scores (HR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.08–1.39), prior pulmonary infections (HR = 2.26, 95% CI: 1.58–3.24), SLE comorbidity (HR = 2.36, 95% CI: 1.35–4.13). Similar conclusions can also be drawn for RA-ILD, even though the significance of RDCI was no longer observed (Table 2). While the association between higher CRP and RA-related lung diseases was only marginally significant (HR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.00-1.07, P = 0.090), we observed a notable correlation between higher CRP and RA-ILD (HR = 1.05, 95% CI : 1.01–1.09). A relatively larger effect was seen for SLE comorbidity (HR = 3.15, 95% CI: 1.69–5.86) towards RA-ILD. We found that incident bronchiectasis was associated with longer RA duration (HR = 2.70 per 10 years, 95% CI: 1.63–4.49), smoking (HR = 4.02, 95% CI: 1.00-16.19), RDCI (HR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.00-1.72), prior pulmonary infections (HR = 2.49, 95% CI: 1.07–5.77), and emphysema (HR = 4.79, 95% CI: 1.44–15.94) (Supplementary Table S3).

Effect of DMARDs on RA-related lung diseases

In the analysis based on a nested case-control design, the proportion of LEF ever-use in patients with or without RA-related lung diseases was 37.9% and 40.9% (P = 0.558), respectively, while the proportion of MTX ever-use was 50.3% and 65.1% (P = 0.001), respectively (Table 3). MTX users tended to have a lower occurrence of RA-related lung diseases compared with MTX non-users (log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 3A). A negative association was found between MTX ever-use and RA-related lung diseases after adjusting for age at RA onset, sex, smoking, RA duration, LEF and b/tsDMARDs use (ORadj = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.44–0.92, Table 3). This association was a bit larger for RA-ILD (ORadj = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.35–0.83, Supplementary Table S4). There was also a significant difference in the occurrence of RA-related lung diseases between LEF users and non-users (log-rank P = 0.006; Fig. 3B), but LEF ever-use was not associated with RA-related lung diseases (ORadj = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.66–1.37, Table 3) or RA-ILD (ORadj = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.57–1.37, Supplementary Table S4).

Our stratified analysis further evaluated the association between MTX/LEF use and RA-related lung diseases (Supplementary Table S5-S6). Notably, MTX ever-use demonstrated a significant protective effect against RA-related lung diseases especially in patients with high DAS28 scores (OR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.19–0.90, P = 0.026), with a significant interaction observed (P for interaction = 0.024). However, no significant effects for either MTX or LEF were observed in other examined subgroups (all P for interaction > 0.05).

Supplementary Figure S5-S6 demonstrated the dose-response relationships between varying cumulative doses and exposure durations of MTX/LEF and the development of RA-related lung diseases. Multivariate-adjusted analyses revealed significant protective effects of MTX against overall RA-related lung diseases, with particularly strong risk reduction observed at both low (0–0.5 g cumulative dose: OR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.29–0.87) and high (≥ 2.5 g cumulative dose: OR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.17–0.70) exposure levels. These protective associations remained consistent in RA-ILD subgroup analyses across most dose tiers (0–0.5 g: OR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.18–0.73; 0.5–1.2 g: OR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.19–0.93; ≥ 2.5 g: OR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.16–0.83). Temporal analyses revealed durable protective effects of MTX against overall RA-related lung diseases for both short-term (< 1 year: ORadj = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.29–0.94) and long-term (> 3 years: ORadj = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.38–0.97) treatment durations. In contrast, LEF exposure demonstrated no significant association with pulmonary outcomes at any dose level or exposure duration.

Furthermore, a significant negative association was found for the use of b/tsDMARDs (ORadj = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.35–0.99) (Table 3). Among the b/tsDMARDs, tocilizumab and tsDMARDs were associated with a decreased risk of RA-related lung diseases (tocilizumab, ORadj = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.07–0.77; tsDMARDs, ORadj = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.14–0.91), whereas TNF inhibitors were not associated with RA-related lung diseases (ORadj = 1.11, 95% CI: 0.56–2.05) (Supplementary Table S4). No association was found between drug use and the development of bronchiectasis (Supplementary Table S4).

Sensitivity analyses

Our propensity score-matched sensitivity analyses corroborated the primary study findings, demonstrating robust protective associations for MTX and b/tsDMARDs. As detailed in Supplementary Table S7-S12, the 1:1 matched analysis (MTX: 413 users vs. 413 non-users; b/tsDMARDs: 263 users vs. 263 non-users) revealed significant risk reduction for both therapies (MTX: ORadj = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.42-1.00; b/tsDMARDs: ORadj = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.25–0.82). In contrast, LEF exposure (477 users vs. 477 non-users) showed no statistically significant association (ORadj = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.55–1.22). These protective effects were further supported by 1:2 case-control matched analyses (131 cases vs. 262 controls), which showed consistent directional trends for MTX (OR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.42–1.05) and b/tsDMARDs (OR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.22–0.74), reinforcing the primary study conclusions while accounting for potential confounding.

Our sensitivity analyses, which excluded early events occurring within the first 28 months of enrollment (leaving 78 incident RA-related lung diseases for analysis), yielded findings consistent with primary results. Several risk factors maintained statistically significant associations with consistent effect directions: RA duration (HR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.00-1.81), prior pulmonary infections (HR = 2.20, 95% CI: 1.33–3.65), and SLE comorbidity (HR = 3.15, 95% CI: 1.51–6.57). Although age at RA onset and RDCI score did not reach statistical significance in this reduced sample (likely due to diminished power), they exhibited consistent risk trends with the primary analysis (age at RA onset: HR = 1.21, 95% CI: 0.99–1.48; RDCI: HR = 1.17, 95% CI: 0.97–1.40). Notably, CRP levels emerged as a significant predictor in this analysis (HR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.01–1.10). MTX use continued to demonstrate protective effects with comparable magnitude (OR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.31–0.83). While b/tsDMARDs did not achieve statistical significance (OR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.34–1.34), their effect direction aligned with the primary findings. Complete results were available in Supplementary Table S13-S14.

Discussion

This longitudinal study of 1,207 RA patients demonstrates an incidence rate of 34.2 cases of RA-related lung diseases per 1,000 person-years, with RA-ILD representing the most frequent manifestation (22.5 per 1,000 person-years) over a mean follow-up of 3.5 years. Key risk factors include older age at RA onset, prolonged RA duration, elevated RDCI scores, prior pulmonary infections, and SLE comorbidity. Notably, MTX demonstrates protective effects against RA-related lung diseases, with similar trends observed for tocilizumab and tsDMARDs.

Existing literature had predominantly focused on RA-ILD [12–16, 37], with limited data addressing the full spectrum of RA-related lung diseases [38–40]. While reported prevalence of 7.7–28.4% in prior studies align with our findings (29.9%), our work extended this evidence by systematically evaluating risk factors across the full range of RA-related lung disease. We confirmed established RA-ILD predictors including late RA onset and prolonged duration, while providing complementary insights into CRP’s predictive value - demonstrating its significance at folds above the upper limit of normal (ULN) [12, 14]. Our study further identified three underrecognized risk indicators: RDCI scores (validated as mortality predictors in RA [34, 41]), prior pulmonary infections, and SLE comorbidity. The RDCI’s strong association highlights the cumulative burden of systemic inflammation, while pulmonary infections may contribute through both acute alveolar damage [42, 43] and chronic inflammatory priming [10, 17, 35]. Importantly, our exclusion of baseline ILD cases strengthened the temporal association between pulmonary infections and RA-ILD development. Our study intentionally included patients with overlap syndrome to evaluate the influence of concomitant autoimmune diseases - a population representing 34.3% of our cohort that would be inappropriate to exclude in real-world research. This approach differed from previous studies and provides important insights, particularly regarding secondary SS (prevalence 10–30% in RA [44–46]) and SLE (4.6% in our cohort). Notably, while SLE was less frequent in our population, its strong association with RA-related lung diseases aligns with established evidence showing 50–70% pulmonary involvement in SLE, including 4–5% of cases where respiratory symptoms precede diagnosis [47].

Our analysis yielded divergent findings regarding two commonly cited risk factors - smoking and autoantibody positivity. While smoking had established mechanistic plausibility for lung injury through cytokine-mediated epithelial damage and fibroblast activation [13], we observed only a non-significant trend despite higher smoking rates in affected patients. This may reflect methodological limitations in smoking assessment (routine clinical documentation rather than standardized interviews) or temporal variation of smoking behavior following RA diagnosis, or a potential threshold effect, as another study reported six-fold risk only above 30 pack-years [14]. For sero-positivity of RA autoantibodies, despite numerous studies demonstrating an association with elevated risk of RA-ILD [13, 37], bronchiectasis [48], and overall RA-related lung diseases [39], our analysis did not confirm this. This discrepancy likely stemmed from key methodological differences: whereas prior cross-sectional studies linked concurrent autoantibody levels to prevalent disease, our study assessed baseline RF and ACPA status in relation to incident RA-ILD during follow-up - a distinction supported by recent evidence showing combined RF/ACPA seropositivity predict existing rather than new-onset RA-ILD [49]. The dynamic nature of autoantibody levels and potential unmeasured confounders may further obscure these relationships in observational studies.

The association between csDMARDs and RA-related lung diseases remained controversial. While earlier studies raised concerns about MTX-induced pulmonary toxicity [18–20], accumulating evidence now suggests potential protective effects [23–26]. Recent multicenter [25] and multinational studies demonstrated an association between MTX use and delayed onset of RA-ILD (by 3.6 years in a five-country study [26]), while another study reported a reduced risk of respiratory failure in RA patients [24]. This protection may stem from MTX’s dual suppression of systemic and pulmonary inflammation [26]. For LEF, the evidence also remained conflicting [21, 22, 27, 28]. While North American and Asian studies identified increased risk primarily in patients with pre-existing ILD or concurrent MTX use [21, 22], or poor lung function [50], our analysis of MTX-treated patients found no significant association (Supplementary Table S6). This discrepancy may reflect our exclusion of baseline ILD cases - a key effect modifier identified in prior research. Recent meta-analytic evidence suggested LEF increases prevalent RA-ILD risk (OR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.08–1.87) [17], though another study paradoxically reported better outcomes in LEF-treated patients, the researchers conservatively attributed part of this association to changes in medication use after baseline [28].

Our findings further demonstrate a protective association between b/tsDMARDs use and reduced risk of RA-related lung diseases, aligning with emerging evidence of their safety profile in RA-ILD [38, 51, 52]. Meta-analytic data corroborated this protective effect against RA-ILD specifically [17], though further mechanistic studies were needed to clarify the underlying pathways.

Several important limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the retrospective design spanning over two decades of disease courses may have led to misclassification of cumulative drug exposures, while unmeasured confounders like occupational exposures, air pollution and pulmonary function testing results could affect the observed associations. Second, the single-center nature of our study, focusing on hospitalized patients with likely more severe disease, may limit generalizability to community RA populations. Third, our reliance on baseline HRCT for pulmonary assessment, while ensuring diagnostic accuracy, may have introduced selection bias by overrepresenting symptomatic cases, potentially leading to overestimated lung disease incidence. Case ascertainment challenges included potential under detection of subclinical disease and possible overestimation from incidental findings, though we mitigated this bidirectional bias through strict radiographic and functional criteria. Fourth, the relatively short follow-up, variable follow-up durations, and modest sample sizes for rarer conditions like bronchiectasis and PAH required cautious interpretation. Incomplete mortality data precluded competing risk analyses. These limitations underscore the need for future multi-center prospective studies with community-based recruitment, systematic HRCT screening, comprehensive information collection (including standardized drug exposure assessment, environmental and occupational exposures, dynamic measurement indicators, and systematic mortality tracking) and longer follow-up to validate our findings. Mechanistic studies were also needed to elucidate the protective effects of MTX and b/tsDMARDs observed in our analysis.

Conclusions

To conclude, this study identified several key risk factors for RA-related lung diseases, including older age at RA onset, longer RA duration, elevated RDCI scores, prior pulmonary infections, and SLE comorbidity. Importantly, we demonstrated protective associations for MTX and b/tsDMARDs, while finding no significant risk with LEF exposure. These findings improved our understanding of RA-related lung diseases and provide clinically actionable evidence for risk stratification, particularly supporting the safety and potential benefits of MTX in high-risk patients requiring close pulmonary monitoring.
