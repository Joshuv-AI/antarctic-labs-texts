Muir-Torre Syndrome: A Rare Case Report and Review of Literature
Authors: Zihao Chen, Yan Wan, Chen Zhou, Luying Wang, Ran Huo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMuir-Torre syndrome (MTS) is an uncommon autosomal dominant disorder characterized by germline mutations in DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes, resulting in microsatellite instability (MSI). It is regarded as a variant form of Lynch syndrome, characterized by the co-occurrence of at least one cutaneous sebaceous neoplasm and at least one visceral malignancy. Furthermore, studies have indicated that MTS may manifest in transplant recipients and in immunocompromised patients. The diagnosis and management of such cases necessitate a multidisciplinary approach. Here, we report a case of a Chinese patient with a history of rectal cancer who developed multiple sebaceous adenomas during immunosuppressive therapy six years after renal transplantation. This case report not only provides additional evidence for the inducing effect of immunosuppressants on MTS but also emphasizes the importance of a thorough diagnosis of MTS and the significance of preventing the development of potential skin tumors in patients with MTS.Keywords: muir-torre syndrome, cutaneous sebaceous neoplasm, DNA mismatch repair, immunosuppressive therapy

Introduction

MTS is a rare autosomal dominant genetic disorder regarded as a cutaneous manifestation subtype of Lynch syndrome (hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer, HNPCC). The hallmark of this syndrome is the concurrent or sequential occurrence of sebaceous gland neoplasms or keratoacanthomas with one or more visceral malignancies, frequently involving colorectal cancer, genitourinary tumors, breast cancer, lung cancer, among others.1 Traditionally, the Amsterdam criteria have been primarily applied to identify families with Lynch syndrome; however, their sensitivity for diagnosing MTS is limited. To address this limitation, the Mayo Clinic proposed a clinical feature–based MTS risk scoring system that integrates the number of sebaceous neoplasms, age at first onset, and the personal and first-degree family history of Lynch-associated malignancies. Previous studies have demonstrated that this scoring system exhibits good discriminative ability in identifying high-risk individuals and can facilitate the selection of patients for subsequent immunohistochemical and genetic testing.2

The pathogenesis of MTS is complex, with a widely accepted association between MTS development and MMR mechanisms. Its pathogenesis is characterized by mutations in MMR genes or promoter methylation–mediated inactivation of MMR, leading to genomic instability, rapid accumulation of mutations in specific oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes, and subsequent MSI, which ultimately drives tumor development. The altered MMR genes associated with MTS include Mutator L Homologue (MLH) 1, Mutator S Homologue (MSH) 2, MSH6, Postmeiotic Segregation Increased (PMS) 2.2 MTS is a rare disease. Its low incidence rate leads to a limited number of cases. Most clinical reports on MTS have focused on the white population in developed countries.3 This article presents a case of MTS in a middle-aged male renal transplant recipient from China.

Case Presentation

This study presents the case of a 45-year-old man with a medical history spanning 27 years. Twenty-seven years prior, the patient had undergone radical surgery for rectal cancer, followed by colostomy. Postoperative histopathological examination established a diagnosis of adenocarcinoma, and no subsequent adjuvant therapy, including chemotherapy, was administered. Sixteen years ago, the patient progressed to end-stage renal disease of unknown etiology, received maintenance dialysis, and eventually underwent renal transplantation. Then he had been maintained on long-term immunosuppressive therapy with tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and prednisone. Over the past decade, the patient developed multiple cutaneous nodules distributed extensively throughout the body, with a predominant concentration in the facial region. Pathology reports from three prior cutaneous tumor excisions (two on the face and one at the stoma site) confirmed the diagnosis of sebaceous adenoma. There was a significant family history of colorectal malignancies (both mother and maternal grandmother).

Physical examination revealed multiple scattered exophytic, lobulated nodules of varying sizes on the patient’s nasal dorsum and in the alar region. The lesions exhibited pink to erythematous surface changes with focal ulceration, crust formation, and partial hemorrhagic eschar formation. Multiple satellite-like papulonodular skin lesions were observed bilaterally in the zygomatic regions, perioral area, chin, and forehead. Lesions in the nasal region exhibited a tendency to coalesce, forming irregularly contoured, cauliflower-like masses that resulted in partial obstruction of the external nares (Figure 1B). Examination of the oral mucosa and conjunctiva revealed no abnormalities in the patient. Additionally, a large spherical mass adjacent to the patient’s stoma was observed (Figure 1C), the skin lesions were flesh-colored, with visible ulcers or scabs on the surface.

Following a comprehensive clinical evaluation, the patient underwent surgical intervention under local anesthesia at four anatomical sites: one on each of the bilateral cheeks, one on the forehead, and one on the right nasal ala (Figure 1D). Histopathological examination revealed marked hyperplasia of the sebaceous glands within the dermal tissue, accompanied by acute and chronic inflammation, consistent with a sebaceous adenoma (Figure 2A and B). Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis of DNA MMR proteins (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2) revealed intact nuclear expression of MLH1 and PMS2, whereas concurrent loss of nuclear immunoreactivity for MSH2 and MSH6 was observed. These findings indicate a deficiency in MMR protein expression, suggesting the presence of MSI.

Discussion and Conclusions

MTS is an uncommon autosomal dominant disorder that is a subtype of HNPCC. The clinical diagnosis of MTS is based on the synchronous or metachronous occurrence of at least one sebaceous skin neoplasm and at least one internal malignancy.3 Currently, there is no clear treatment method for MTS. Some scholars believe that low-dose isotretinoin can be recommended as a chemopreventive medication for skin malignancies in MTS patients.4 The pathogenesis involves germline mutations in MMR genes, which lead to genomic instability and the rapid accumulation of mutations in certain oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes. This results in MSI, ultimately triggering tumorigenesis.5 MSH2, MLH1, MSH6, and PMS2 are the principal molecular targets. Notably, approximately 33% of MTS cases mainly follow an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern, presenting as MSI-negative status, known as MTS type II (accounting for approximately 35% of cases), and this variant exhibits unique molecular characteristics.6 Given that MTS is rare but often accompanied by synchronous or metachronous malignant tumors, maintaining a high level of vigilance in clinical practice is crucial. In addition to evaluating the typical family history and personal tumor history, systematic IHC analysis is key to identifying MTS. In MMR protein deficiency models, the co-deficiency of specific combinations demonstrates a higher diagnostic value for MTS than isolated protein deficiencies. While the positive predictive values (PPV) of single defects vary (MSH2:55%, MLH1:88%, MSH6:67%), the combined deficiency of MLH1/MSH6 or the triple deficiency of MLH1/MSH2/MSH6 achieves a PPV of 100%, serving as the definitive molecular diagnostic criteria for MTS.7 Mutations in the MSH2 gene are the most prevalent cause of MTS, accounting for approximately 90% of cases globally.8 Based on the established clinical diagnostic criteria, specifically the co-occurrence of sebaceous neoplasms with visceral malignancies and evidence of MMR deficiency, this case fulfills the diagnostic criteria for MTS.

Currently, there are no definitive clinical management guidelines for MTS. Sebaceous gland tumors are relatively rare in clinical practice and can be the primary manifestation of MTS. The histological classification of sebaceous gland tumor lesions mainly includes sebaceous adenoma, sebaceous epithelioma, sebaceous carcinoma, and basal cell epithelioma with sebaceous differentiation. In this case, a visceral malignant tumor was the initial symptom. Literature indicates that Sebaceous gland tumors occur after visceral malignancies in approximately 56% of cases, before in 22%, concurrently in 6%, and without a temporal relationship in 16%.6 Therefore, clinical decision-making for MTS screening in individual patients remains challenging. Some experts have suggested conducting IHC and MSI analyses for all sebaceous gland tumors, even if the patient has no personal or family history related to MTS.9 Given that the absence of MMR protein expression can result from either germline or sporadic somatic mutations, it is necessary to identify germline mutations in MMR genes through genomic sequencing, which serves as a crucial basis for confirming MTS.1 If a patient is diagnosed with MTS upon the initial presentation of sebaceous neoplasms, it is essential to implement an annual cancer screening and prevention protocol. MTS-associated visceral malignancies predominantly include colorectal cancer, which is the most commonly observed, followed by genitourinary tract tumors, breast cancer, endometrial carcinoma, gastric cancer, lung cancer, and hepatobiliary carcinoma. Moreover, the Mayo MTS risk-scoring algorithm can be employed for assessment. Patients with a score of ≥2 points in this risk stratification tool should undergo IHC analysis of MMR proteins to facilitate the detection and treatment of skin tumors.8

MTS is particularly rare in the Chinese population. Before 2005, there was almost no information on this disease in Asian and African populations. To date, only a limited number of MTS cases involving individuals of Chinese ancestry have been documented in the medical literature. A documented case involving a Chinese patient with MTS was reported in 2021. The patient underwent renal transplantation at the age of 37 years, during which rapidly enlarging chest nodules were observed. Histopathological evaluation confirmed the diagnosis of sebaceous carcinoma. Subsequent systemic malignancy screening revealed synchronous sigmoid colon adenocarcinoma. IHC analysis demonstrated a complete loss of nuclear expression of MSH2 and MSH6, whereas MLH1 and PMS2 staining patterns remained intact. Genetic testing identified a c.229_230delAG (p.Ser77fs) frameshift mutation in exon 2 of the MSH2 gene within the lesion.10 To the best of our knowledge, this is the first documented case of MTS in a Chinese patient who received immunotherapy following renal transplantation. The patient developed multiple sebaceous tumors on the face and around the stoma site after six years of oral immunosuppressive therapy. Postoperative IHC revealed the loss of MSH2 and MSH6 expression.

Immunosuppressive therapy administered to organ transplant recipients has been recognized to accelerate the progression of skin tumors, a phenomenon observed not only in patients with MTS but also in individuals without a defined genetic predisposition to cancer. Calcineurin inhibitors (CNI), such as cyclosporine and tacrolimus, promote tumor invasion, progression, and metastasis. This mechanism involves the upregulation of transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) activity, along with the inhibition of nucleotide excision repair (NER) pathway function.11 In contrast, mTOR inhibitors, such as sirolimus and everolimus, can induce G1 pha se cell cycle arrest, suppress TGF-β signaling, and reduce the incidence of cutaneous tumors through VEGF-mediated anti-angiogenic effects and by diminishing endothelial cell responsiveness to VEGF receptors.12,13 Patients undergoing long-term immunosuppressive therapy commonly develop cutaneous malignancies. Compared to immunocompetent individuals, transplant recipients exhibit a significantly elevated risk of non-melanoma skin cancer.14 A hypothesis posits that immunosuppressive therapy unmasks the systemic presentation of MTS in patients.15 Other researchers have also documented this form of exposure (Table 1). The reported case in China provides additional support for this hypothesis. In renal transplant recipients with MTS, conversion from tacrolimus to sirolimus has been reported to effectively suppress the development of cutaneous malignancies.16

Summary of Cases with MTS-Associated Skin Tumors Following Transplantation

In summary, we present a case of multiple sebaceous adenomas in a renal transplant recipient undergoing immunosuppressive therapy, with the diagnosis aligning with MTS. MTS represents a rare clinical entity, necessitating a high index of suspicion among healthcare professionals, particularly in transplant recipients receiving immunosuppressive therapy. Our case further substantiates the hypothesis that immunosuppressive regimens may unmask latent MTS in transplant recipients and potentially accelerate neoplastic progression. In patients with a family history of visceral malignant tumors and immunosuppression, heightened vigilance is warranted for the development of cutaneous neoplasms.
