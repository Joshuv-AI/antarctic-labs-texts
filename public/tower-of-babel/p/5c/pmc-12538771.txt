Three-dimensional printed screw guide-assisted metatarsal osteotomy for the treatment of mild-to-moderate hallux valgus
Authors: Jiazheng Wang, Shaoling Fu, Cheng Wang, Shutao Zhang, Zhongmin Shi, Xin Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study was to analyse the efficacy of three-dimensional (3D)-printed screw guide-assisted percutaneous metatarsal transverse osteotomy (PMTO) for the treatment of mild-to-moderate hallux valgus (HV).MethodsA retrospective case analysis was used to collect the clinical data of 21 patients with mild-to-moderate HV admitted to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Affiliated Sixth People’s Hospital between June 2022 and April 2023, all of whom were treated with 3D-printed screw guide-assisted PMTO, and to compare the pre- and postoperative hallux valgus angle (HVA) and the 1st-2nd intermetatarsal angle (IMA). The duration of surgery and the shortening of the first metatarsal were recorded. The efficacy and function of the patients were assessed via the American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Surgery Society (AOFAS) forefoot score and visual analogue score (VAS) preoperatively and at the last follow-up. Statistical analysis included normality assessment and internal comparisons (t tests for normally distributed quantitative data and the Mann‒Whitney U test for nonnormally distributed data).ResultsAll patients underwent successful surgery with a mean follow-up of 24.1 ± 2.2 months without complications. The mean HVA and IMA were corrected to 11.2 ± 2.6° and 8.1 ± 1.4° from the preoperative values of 32.2 ± 5.0° and 12.6 ± 1.6°, respectively (P < 0.05). The mean shortening of the first metatarsal was 3.2 ± 1.0 mm. The average operative duration was 41.0 ± 2.3 min. The AOFAS forefoot and VAS scores improved from 59.7 ± 3.6 and 6.1 ± 1.3 preoperatively to 89.8 ± 4.3 and 0.7 ± 0.6 at the last follow-up, respectively (P < 0.05).ConclusionThe use of 3D-printed screw guides, improving the safety and effectiveness of PMTO and shortening the operation time, is an effective, efficient and secure way to correct HV deformities.Level of evidenceLevel IV, retrospective cohort study.Keywords: Hallux valgus, Minimally invasive surgery, 3D printing, Forefoot

Introduction

Three-dimensional printing, also known as 3D printing (3DP), constructs objects via layer-by-layer printing. As the application of 3DP in the field of foot and ankle surgery gradually intensifies, it can be used to manufacture custom implants or surgical guides and instruments [1], i.e., custom-made 3D printed guides for osteotomy and screw placement.

Hallux valgus (HV) is a common forefoot disorder referred to as an abnormality of the first metatarsophalangeal joint (MTPJ). It occurs when the hallux deviates outwards, forming an angle between it and the first metatarsal, known as the hallux valgus angle (HVA), which is greater than 15 degrees. Usually, it is accompanied by medial hallux capsulitis or pain on the first metatarsal head [2]. Surgical treatment for hallux valgus is mainstream. There are various surgical treatment methods for HV. The most used ones include Chevron osteotomy, Scarf osteotomy, Lapidus osteotomy, and Akin osteotomy for proximal interphalangeal valgus. Each of these methods has its own unique effect in correcting deformity. It has been reported that the Chevron osteotomy can provide a greater HVA correction than Scarf osteotomy, while the proximal Chevron osteotomy can provide a better IMA correction compared to distal Chevron osteotomy [3, 4]. In recent years, minimally invasive surgeries (MIS) have gradually come into the focus of surgeons, including percutaneous Chevron-Akin, minimally invasive Chevron-Akin, and percutaneous metatarsal transverse osteotomy (PMTO) [5]. MIS has gained wider acceptance among patients because of its advantages, such as smaller incisions, less trauma, and faster recovery [6–8]. However, problems such as longer operation times, more X-ray exposure and postoperative incision complications urgently need to be solved.

This study investigated 21 patients (22 feet) with mild-to-moderate HV and used a 3D-printed screw guide to assist in PMTO treatment. The efficacy of this technique was analysed to explore its application value in surgery.

Methods

Study design

Clinical and radiographic data, as well as general information, related to 21 patients (22 feet) with mild-to-moderate HV admitted to the Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Affiliated Sixth People’s Hospital between February 2020 and February 2022 were retrospectively analysed.

The study received approval from the institutional review board and adhered to the STROBE guidelines for observational studies.

Participants

Among the 21 patients (22 feet) with mild-to-moderate HV, there were 6 males (6 feet) and 15 females (16 feet). Ages ranged from 17–64 years, with an average of 43.2 ± 13.7 years. The main symptoms of the patients were pain on the medial side of the first MTPJ and deformity of the forefoot. When there is transferred metatarsalgia, callus formation and pain can also occur. On the basis of the results of the preoperative physical examination and imaging tests, as well as the classification of HVA and the intermetatarsal angle (IMA) between the first and 2nd metatarsals [9], all patients were divided into a mild group, with 8 patients (8 feet, 36.4%), and a moderate group, with 13 patients (14 feet, 63.6%). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) HVA ≤ 40°; (2) IMA ≤ 16°; (3) good patient compliance, received 3DP-assisted PMTO technique, and signed the informed consent form; and (4) complete follow-up data and a follow-up time of ≥ 12 months. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with rheumatoid arthritis or degenerative diseases of the first MTPJ; (2) patients complicated with severe cardiac or pulmonary dysfunction, peripheral vascular disease or poor glycemic control in diabetes; (3) patients with mental disorders unable to cooperate; and (4) patients with previous foot surgeries or trauma history.

Screw guide design

A preoperative CT scan was performed on the forefoot of the patient. DICM data were extracted and imported into Arigin 3D Pro software (Shanghai Xinjian, China) to reconstruct the 3D data of HVs. The reconstructed data were imported into surgical planning software for design.

The screw guides were designed based on the three-dimensional model after simulated reduction. Before simulating reduction, the bony spurs on the 3D model of the bone that might affect the reduction were removed to avoid interference during reduction. The angles and directions of the screw guide pin holes were determined through simulated metatarsal osteotomy, HV deformity reduction and screw placement. Considering the possible interference from other factors during the operation, the guide pin holes were set as a 7-hole honeycomb pattern to allow for fine adjustment during the operation. The final guide data were saved in STL format and imported into a 3D printer (Shanghai Xinjian) for printing.

Surgical technique

The patient was tilted to the healthy side, and the hip was elevated. A tourniquet was applied at the proximal end of the thigh (70 kPa). Two Kirschner wires were used to attach and fix the 3D-printed screw guide (Shanghai Xinjian) to the medial side of the foot through the positioning holes. Another Kirschner wire was pre-embedded into the first metatarsal through appropriate screw holes before osteotomy so that its pinpoint was confirmed under fluoroscopy to just reach the lateral side of the first metatarsal head as planned preoperatively. Then the Kirschner wire was retracted to make sure that pinpoint was inside the proximal lateral cortex at the head-neck junction. Then the screw guide was removed, but the Kirschner wire was retained. A 2 mm incision was made on the medial side of the first metatarsal head. A low-speed high-torque minimally invasive power tool (Swiss 3500, Shanghai Bojin) and a 2 mm * 8 mm drill were used to cut the first metatarsal along the metatarsal neck. The first MTPJ capsule was manually released. The pre-embedded Kirschner wire was further advanced to fix the first metatarsal head. Under fluoroscopy, the hallux alignment and the Kirschner wire was checked in a good position. A 4.5 mm absorbable screw (Inion, Finland) was placed for internal fixation. If the metatarsal osteotomy failed to completely correct the first MTPJ alignment, a minimally invasive Akin osteotomy was performed at the base of the first proximal phalanx via a micropower tool, and a 2.7 mm absorbable screw (Inion, Finland) was inserted. Finally, fluoroscopy was used to confirm the position of the screw and the correction effect of hallux valgus. For patients with tightness on the lateral side and difficulty in reducing the first MTPJ, a 50 ml syringe needle was used to release the MTPJ capsule. For patients with metatarsalgia, a minimally invasive distal metatarsal osteotomy (DMMO) was performed without internal fixation. For patients whose gastrocnemius aponeurosis was tight, minimally invasive gastrocnemius loosing was performed.

Data collection

The HVA and IMA were observed and compared before and after surgery, and the operation duration and degree of shortening of the first metatarsal bone were recorded. The American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) forefoot score [10] and visual analogue scale (VAS) score [11] were used before the operation, 3 months after the operation, and at the last follow-up to compare the surgical efficacy between the two groups of patients.

Ethical approval

All patients signed an informed consent form. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Affiliated Sixth People’s Hospital [2023‑KY‑008 (K)].

Statistical analysis

The data were statistically analysed via SPSS 26.0. When the data were normally distributed, they were expressed as (x̅ ± s), and an independent sample t test was used. When the data were nonnormally distributed, the rank sum test was used. P < 0.05 indicated statistically significant differences.

Results

Clinical outcomes

Twenty-one patients (22 feet) who underwent PMTO with 3D screw guide assistance were followed up completely after the operation. The follow-up period ranged from 18 ~ 26 months. The preoperative AOFAS forefoot score ranged from 53 ~ 66 points. Among them, 19 patients (19 feet) underwent lateral soft tissue release. Fourteen patients (14 feet) underwent minimally invasive Akin osteotomy. Two patients (2 feet) underwent second metatarsal DMMO, and 1 patient (1 foot) underwent simultaneous second and third metatarsal DMMO. One patient (1 foot) underwent minimally invasive gastrocnemius loosing surgery. The average operation duration was 41.0 ± 2.3 min.

Perioperative outcomes and complications

Postoperative complications occurred in 2 patients (2 feet). One patient (1 foot) presented with stiffness at the first MTPJ, which improved after visiting the rehabilitation clinic. The other patient (1 foot) experienced mild numbness around the incision. None of the other patients complained of postoperative discomfort. No complications, such as metatarsal head necrosis, non-union of the bone, or recurrence of deformity, occurred in this group.

Discussion

In this study, the authors employed 3D-printed screw placement guides to assist in performing PMTO for the treatment of mild-to-moderate HV deformity, achieving favourable clinical outcomes. The postoperative effects were satisfactory, and the observed complications were also relatively subtle. This is attributed to the precise preoperative planning and the implementation of MIS, which effectively avoided unnecessary trauma and reduced the probability of complications. The benefits of precision medicine for patients are considerable, at least reducing intraoperative thermal injury. And the advantages of MIS are as mentioned before, improving the prognosis of patients.

3D printing technology and surgery

When designing the 3D-printed percutaneous screw placement guide, authors used a series of 3 mm-layer images as the basic data, including the data of the foot skin and soft tissues within the measurement scope. The author’s team transparently rendered the skin in the Arigin 3D Pro software, performed simulated osteotomy and repositioning of the bone tissue, and inserted the screws. After all the simulated data and positions were determined, the guide plate was designed based on the shape of the foot skin, and the guide pin holes and angles were determined. The edge of the screw guide matched the protrusions or depressions of the skin. Therefore, when the 3D-printed percutaneous screw guide is fixed, it is necessary to ensure that each part of it and the anatomical positioning marks correspond one-by-one and keep the guide plate as close as possible in contact with the skin of the foot. The screw guide and the foot should not be moved until the screw placement process is complete. During screw placement, the team used the central hole first, which was the best position during the simulation. If there is a need to adjust, other holes can be used. This technique requires the surgeon’s surgical experience, including being able to handle problems such as loss of reduction and inaccurate guide positioning. This approach is difficult for junior surgeons and has a certain learning curve.

3DP technology can enhance doctors’ clinical skills as well as produce personalized custom-made orthoses or tools [12, 13]. Moreover, it can optimize the surgical process by customizing implant devices and surgical tools specifically for patients [1, 14]. For HV, accurate preoperative planning is extremely necessary, especially for revision surgeries, to try and identify the causes of failure and appropriately address these issues [15]. A team used 3DP technology to create two osteotomies and screw guides for HV correction. The osteotomy guide was used to fit the patient’s skin surface to determine the osteotomy site, and a Kirschner wire was inserted for positioning. The screw guide determines the angle and direction of screw insertion through the positioning Kirschner wire, and the guide allows the guide pin to pass through [16].

Surgical technique and internal fixation

In this study, the author chose to use only one absorbable screw for fixation without any complication observed, such as delayed union, un-union or fracture, which aligned with the result that some studies had already demonstrated that by reasonably designing the osteotomy angle, the mechanical strength of one 4.5 mm absorbable screw could also meet the requirements [17]. Compared with previous techniques, the third-generation minimally invasive HV correction technique has a more stable therapeutic effect and a wider range of applicable indications [5, 18–20]. In Bösch technique, it is unstable when fixed with only one Kirschner wire. It may cause dorsal deformity of the first metatarsal. Therefore, it is recommended to add a diagonal Kirschner wire to fix the metatarsal head [21]. Similarly, the osteotomy line of the first metatarsal in PMTO is approximately at the neck of the metatarsal bone. Different osteotomy angles cause a change in the length of the first metatarsal [22]. Other studies asserted 2 metal screws for fixation. The first metal screw should be parallel to the long axis of the first metatarsal shaft on the sagittal plane [23]. The second metal screw is used to prevent rotation, which helps to enhance the stability of the overall structure. There is no definite conclusion on when Akin osteotomy should be performed. Currently, it is reported that proximal distal phalangeal articular angle (PDPAA) less than 8° is considered an indication for performing Akin osteotomy, but whether there is a smaller threshold value is still under discussion [24]. MIS is a suitable surgical choice for correction of uncomplicated HV deformity, while the most common complication is recurrence of the deformity [25].

This study has several limitations as well, such as its small sample size, relatively short follow-up period and lack of a control group. The medium- and long-term therapeutic effects remain unknown. There is a lack of evaluation of the orthopaedic effect in patients with severe hallux valgus deformity. The quantity of patient-reported outcome measurements used in this study was insufficient. Considering the time and economic cost of screw guide production, only a few anatomical markers are included in the design process, and the screw guide cannot fully closely adhere to the skin during the operation, leaving a certain gap for movement, which introduces some errors to the screw placement process. Moreover, as this technology is still in the initial exploration and clinical trial stage at present, a control group has not been set up for this study. In the future, the author will make targeted improvements to address the limitations mentioned above to enhance the scientific nature of the research and the general applicability of the results.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the 3D-printed screw guide-assisted PMTO technique has a shorter operation time, better short-term prognosis, and can effectively correct lateral deviation deformities of the foot. It also leads to high clinical satisfaction and is a safe and reliable surgical method for treating mild-to-moderate HV.
