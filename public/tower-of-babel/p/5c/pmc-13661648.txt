Risk of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw during romosozumab treatment
Authors: Joshua Stokar, Auryan Szalat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) is a rare but clinically important adverse event in antiresorptive osteoporosis care. The most recently approved osteoporosis medication, romosozumab, has a dual mechanism, combining antiresorptive and osteoanabolic effects. Thus, concern exists regarding a possible risk for MRONJ with romosozumab, although current evidence is limited mainly to isolated case reports. We performed a retrospective new-user active comparator propensity score-matched cohort study in the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network comparing women treated with romosozumab vs. parathyroid hormone (PTH) analogs (teriparatide or abaloparatide). Women aged ≥ 18 years with no prior osteonecrosis, jaw inflammatory disease, oral/head-neck cancer, or radiation exposure and no recent exposure to chemotherapy were eligible. After 1:1 propensity score matching, 15,689 patients remained in each cohort. During 1 year of follow-up, MRONJ occurred in 11 romosozumab-treated patients and 12 PTH analog-treated patients (hazard ratio [HR] 0.89, 95% CI 0.39–2.01; log-rank p = 0.78). Absolute event rates were very low in both groups (0.070% vs. 0.076%). As expected, the positive control outcome of hypercalcemia occurred less often with romosozumab (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.58–0.75). Introduction

Medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) associated with antiresorptive therapy is uncommon in osteoporosis treatment, but it has substantial implications for patients and clinicians because it can complicate dental care, delay treatment sequencing, and influence therapeutic choice [1]. A recent TriNetX analysis of parenteral antiresorptive agents underscored that MRONJ is rare in non-cancer populations but remains a clinically relevant safety outcome, particularly when comparing agents with distinct antiresorptive potency and treatment patterns [2]. In contrast, there is no increased risk of MRONJ associated with the osteoanabolic parathyroid hormone (PTH) analogs, teriparatide and abaloparatide [1, 3]. Romosozumab, the most recently approved osteoporosis treatment, is a sclerostin antibody with both osteoanabolic and antiresorptive effects, creating uncertainty regarding its comparative MRONJ risk profile [1, 2]. Real-world adverse event reporting data and case-based literature have raised concern that romosozumab may also be associated with MRONJ [4–6].

In routine practice, romosozumab and PTH analogs are often chosen for patients at very high fracture risk, making the latter an appropriate comparator as they are similarly positioned as anabolic or bone-forming therapies for severe osteoporosis [7], and are not generally considered MRONJ-promoting drugs [1, 3]. We therefore used a large real-world network to compare the risk of MRONJ in women treated with romosozumab versus PTH analogs.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective new-user active comparator cohort study using the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network. TriNetX is a federated global research network that provides access to de-identified, longitudinal electronic health record data contributed by participating healthcare organizations. The Global Collaborative Network includes data from healthcare organizations (HCOs) across multiple countries and regions, with information on demographics, encounters, diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory results, and vital signs. Data are harmonized into the TriNetX common data model and mapped to standard terminologies, including ICD-10-CM, CPT/HCPCS/ICD-10-PCS, RxNorm, and LOINC. Patient-level data are de-identified or pseudonymized according to applicable privacy regulations, and participating HCOs remain anonymous within collaborative network analyses. At the time of analysis (May 2026) the Global Collaborative Network comprised 171 HCOs (67 from the United States, 31 from Europe, the Middle East and Africa, 31 from Latin America and 42 from Asia–Pacific) with data from ~ 180 million individual patients. To improve causal interpretability, cohorts were restricted to patients exposed to the treatment of interest and propensity score matching (PSM) was used to balance measured baseline risk factors for MRONJ. Statistical analyses were performed using the built-in analytic tools of the TriNetX platform, as previously described [8].

The treatment cohort included women aged 18 years or older with romosozumab exposure on or before April 15, 2025, to allow for a full year of treatment and follow-up. The control cohort included women aged 18 years or older with exposure to a PTH analog (teriparatide or abaloparatide) on or before April 15, 2025, without romosozumab exposure at or before index (see Table S1 for list of codes used). Patients with any history of osteonecrosis, inflammatory conditions of the jaws, malignant neoplasms of the oral/head-neck region, or radiation exposure before index treatment were excluded, as were those treated with chemotherapy or antiangiogenic agents in the prior year (see Table S2 for list of codes used). Follow-up was capped at 1 year because romosozumab is generally administered for a single 12-month treatment course [7], after which patients are commonly transitioned to an antiresorptive agent that is itself associated with MRONJ risk. To improve case capture and account for potential underreporting, both ‘osteonecrosis due to drugs’ (ICD-10 M87.1) and ‘inflammatory conditions of the jaw’ (M27.2) were combined as the composite primary outcome [2]. Hypercalcemia (defined as a composite of ICD-10 E83.52 ‘hypercalcemia’ or a measured serum calcium ≥ 11 mg/dL) was prespecified as a positive control outcome because it was expected to be more frequent with PTH analogs.

Baseline covariates from the electronic health record up to 1 year before the index date were included in the propensity score model, including age, race/ethnicity, osteoporotic fracture, diabetes mellitus, body mass index, smoking, long-term systemic glucocorticoid use, periodontal disease, dental caries, dental examination, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and prior exposure to antiresorptive medications. Medication covariates, including denosumab, bisphosphonates as a class, and the specific agents alendronate, risedronate, and zoledronic acid, were defined by the presence of the corresponding medication code during the baseline period in TriNetX and were not subject to a minimum number of prescriptions. In contrast, “long-term use of bisphosphonates” was defined using the ICD-10 code Z79.83, which reflects documented long-term current use in the electronic health record rather than a duration threshold predefined by the investigators.

Baseline characteristics were compared between treatment groups before and after propensity score matching using p values and standardized mean differences (SMDs). An SMD < 0.1 was considered indicative of adequate balance. Time-to-event analyses were assessed with Kaplan–Meier methods and hazard ratios generated within the TriNetX platform. Mean follow-up after matching was 335.3 days in the romosozumab cohort and 324.1 days in the PTH analog cohort, with median follow-up of 365 days in both cohorts.

Before matching, 17,793 women were identified in the romosozumab cohort and 42,428 in the PTH analog cohort. After PSM, 15,689 patients remained in each group. After matching, baseline characteristics were well balanced, with all standardized mean differences < 0.10 (Table 1).

During the 1-year follow-up window, MRONJ was rare in both groups. In the matched analysis, 11 romosozumab-treated patients and 12 PTH analog-treated patients met the composite jaw outcome definition, corresponding to absolute risks of 0.070% and 0.076%, respectively (Table 2). Kaplan–Meier analysis showed no significant between-group difference (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.39–2.01; log-rank p = 0.78). A sensitivity analysis limited to U.S.-based HCOs showed no evidence of increased MRONJ risk with romosozumab.

As expected, based on the known calcemic effects of PTH-based therapies, hypercalcemia occurred less often with romosozumab than with PTH analogs (394 vs 575 events; 2.51% vs 3.66%). This corresponded to an HR of 0.66 (95% CI 0.58–0.75; log-rank p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this propensity score-matched real-world study, romosozumab was not associated with a detectable increase in short-term MRONJ risk compared with PTH analogs in women treated for osteoporosis. The absolute rate of MRONJ was extremely low in both cohorts, and the confidence interval around the hazard ratio was wide, indicating that the present analysis is most informative in reassuring against a large excess risk rather than excluding small risk differences. Additionally, as MRONJ events were rare, we were unable to reliably analyze the composite MRONJ outcome separately by each individual diagnostic code included in its definition. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as applying to the composite MRONJ outcome rather than to any single diagnostic component.

Romosozumab-associated MRONJ appears to be rare but has been reported both in pivotal clinical trials and in post-marketing case reports. In the phase III FRAME trial, Cosman et al. reported two cases of MRONJ among patients exposed to romosozumab, including one occurring following romosozumab treatment in the context of ill-fitting dentures, and the other event occurred after 12 months of romosozumab treatment and one dose of denosumab after a tooth extraction and subsequent osteomyelitis of the jaw [9]. In the ARCH trial comparing romosozumab followed by alendronate versus alendronate alone, Saag et al. reported adjudicated MRONJ events in both groups during follow-up, with two cases occurring in the romosozumab-to-alendronate arm and one in the alendronate-only arm [10]. Regulatory safety reviews from the European Medicines Agency similarly summarized four MRONJ cases across the romosozumab development program, only one of which occurred during active romosozumab exposure, while the remaining cases developed after subsequent antiresorptive therapy [11]. Two additional published case reports developed after dental extraction and/or implant-related procedures, with outcomes ranging from resolution after romosozumab discontinuation and conservative therapy [4], to severe progressive disease requiring subtotal mandibulectomy and free fibula flap reconstruction [6]. However, causal attribution to romosozumab remains limited by local dental triggers and additional exposures such as subsequent denosumab or anti-VEGF therapy. Our results support the view that if romosozumab-associated MRONJ occurs in routine osteoporosis care, it is rare during the approved 12-month treatment window. These findings are also consistent with preclinical anti-sclerostin data showing that clinically relevant sclerostin antibody exposure did not induce ONJ-like lesions in rats with experimental periodontitis [12].

The comparison with PTH analogs is clinically relevant. Teriparatide and abaloparatide are osteoanabolic agents commonly considered alternatives for patients with osteoporosis at very high fracture risk, including some patients in whom antiresorptive-related adverse effects are a concern. The absence of a clear MRONJ excess with romosozumab suggests that potential jaw safety concerns alone should not automatically preclude its use when the expected anti-fracture benefit is high. The control outcome also behaved as expected: hypercalcemia was less frequent with romosozumab, consistent with the known calcemic effects of PTH-based therapies, supporting the internal validity of the analytic approach.

The 1-year follow-up window was intentionally chosen for clinical reasons. Romosozumab is generally limited to a single year of treatment, after which patients are typically transitioned to an antiresorptive therapy to maintain skeletal benefit. Because antiresorptives are themselves associated with MRONJ, extending follow-up beyond the romosozumab treatment period would make attribution of later jaw events substantially more difficult and could bias comparative safety estimates. Indeed, in both pivotal romosozumab trials, the few reported MRONJ events occurred after the 12-month romosozumab treatment period and during subsequent antiresorptive treatment with alendronate or denosumab [9, 10]. Whether romosozumab modifies MRONJ risk after subsequent antiresorptive therapy remains uncertain. Indeed, recent experimental results in a mouse model showed increased risk of MRONJ in mice treated with romosozumab followed by zoledronic acid compared with those treated with zoledronic acid alone [13]. Further studies should compare the risk of subsequent MRONJ after treatment with romosozumab and transition to different antiresorptive agents. Conversely, for patients who are not treatment naïve, the type and duration of the preceding antiresorptive treatment may also augment the risk of subsequent MRONJ following romosozumab. As only a minority of the patients in the current study had evidence of antiresorptive treatment preceding romosozumab, further studies enriched for such populations seem warranted.

This study has limitations. First, the analysis relied on routinely coded electronic health record data, and outcome misclassification or omission is possible, especially for a rare event such as MRONJ. Second, despite PSM to an active comparator, residual confounding remains possible. Third, because TriNetX includes data only from participating healthcare organizations, selection bias is possible and the findings may not be fully generalizable to all clinical settings. Fourth, heterogeneity across countries and regions in prescribing patterns, healthcare practices, dental care access, and diagnostic coding may have influenced both treatment assignment and outcome capture. Fifth, TriNetX does not provide detailed adjudication of dental procedures, oral examination findings, drug adherence, cumulative duration of prior antiresorptive treatment, or reasons for treatment selection. Sixth, although the 1-year follow-up window was clinically appropriate for isolating exposure to romosozumab itself, it may underestimate later events occurring after treatment sequencing changes. Finally, the analysis was restricted to women and may not generalize fully to men.

In conclusion, this large matched real-world analysis found no detectable short-term increase in MRONJ risk with romosozumab compared with PTH analogs among women with osteoporosis during the 12-month romosozumab treatment period. Because MRONJ was rare, continued pharmacovigilance and pooled analyses with longer follow-up are warranted.
