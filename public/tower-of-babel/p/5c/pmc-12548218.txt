Background

The illicit use of tramadol has become a significant public health challenge in Ghana, particularly among young commercial drivers. Tramadol, a centrally acting synthetic opioid analgesic, is primarily prescribed for moderate to severe pain but is increasingly being abused for its euphoric and stimulant-like effects [1]. Although tramadol is classified as a prescription-only medication in Ghana, its widespread illicit availability has led to its non-prescribed use among various populations, including commercial drivers who rely on it to enhance physical endurance and alertness during long working hours [2–4]. This growing trend has raised concerns regarding its impact on the individual’s health, road safety, and overall economic productivity [5].

Studies have demonstrated that tramadol misuse can lead to severe health complications, including dependency, respiratory depression, disorientation, and increased risk of seizures [6, 7]. Furthermore, chronic misuse has been linked to mental disorders such as anxiety, depression, and cognitive impairments, which may further compromise the decision-making abilities of commercial drivers [2, 8]. The adverse effects of non-medical use of tramadol extend beyond the individual user, contributing to increased road traffic accidents, reduced economic output, and heightened healthcare costs for both individuals and the state [5, 9]. In Ghana, commercial drivers constitute a high-risk group due to the physically demanding nature of their work, long hours on the road, and exposure to peer influence, which may encourage substance use as a coping mechanism [5, 10].

Several socio-demographic and environmental factors have been identified as contributors to non-medical use of tramadol among young drivers. Research suggests that lower levels of formal education may be associated with a higher likelihood of non-medical use of tramadol as individuals with limited knowledge of the drug’s risks may be more susceptible to misinformation and peer influence [11]. Additionally, peer pressure plays a significant role, as drivers often use tramadol within social networks where the drug is perceived as a performance enhancer rather than a harmful substance [12, 13] Awareness and knowledge of tramadol’s effects have also been linked to increased non-medical use, with individuals who are more familiar with the drug’s stimulant properties being more likely to engage in its excessive use [14].

Given the rising prevalence of tramadol’s non-medical use and its implications for road safety and public health, there is a need for targeted interventions that address the root causes of its inappropriate use. Strengthening regulatory frameworks to control the illicit sale of tramadol, increasing awareness campaigns, and providing alternative coping strategies for commercial drivers could help mitigate this crisis [9].

Available studies have either explored the motivations for tramadol use among commercial drivers [3, 15] or examined the predictors of tramadol use among commercial drivers [4, 12, 16].

There appear to be a lack of studies employing mixed methods approach to explore both the underlying reasons and the quantitative aspects of tramadol’s inappropriate use. This study aimed to adopt a concurrent triangulation mixed methods design to examine the key factors driving tramadol use among young commercial drivers in the Tamale Metropolis. It investigated their sources of supply, the dosages consumed, and their perceptions of the potential effects of tramadol use, providing a comprehensive understanding of the issue. The study sought to answer the following questions:

Methods

Study design

This study employed a concurrent triangulation mixed methods design to explore the factors influencing tramadol use among young commercial drivers in the Tamale Metropolis. This design enabled the simultaneous collection and analysis of both quantitative and qualitative data, allowing for the integration of findings to enhance the validity and depth of the study. By utilizing both approaches, the study leveraged the strengths of each method, ensuring a more comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon under investigation [17].

Study setting

The study was conducted in the Tamale Metropolis, northern Ghana’s largest urban and commercial centre. As a key transportation hub, the metropolis is home to a significant number of commercial drivers who operate under demanding working conditions that may contribute to substance use, including tramadol abuse. Data collection took place at various bus stations within the metropolis, where commercial drivers gather daily for work. Commercial drivers in the metropolis are registered at a specific station hence cannot crossover to other stations where they are not registered. These locations provided a conducive setting for engaging participants, as they represent the core operational areas where tramadol use may be prevalent among young commercial drivers.

Sampling technique

The study employed distinct sampling techniques for the quantitative and qualitative components to ensure a robust and comprehensive analysis.

Qualitative sampling

For the qualitative component, data were collected through Focus Group Discussions (FGDs). The focused groups were made up of 6–8 commercial drivers who used tramadol. The snowball sampling method was used to identify commercial drivers who use tramadol at the various stations. These persons were then put into the various focus groups at the various stations. In all, 7 groups were formed with a total of 50 commercial drivers. Each bus terminal was uniquely identified and categorized to facilitate data organization and analysis. Also, participants were identified using codes and the name of the bus station.

Quantitative sampling

A convenience sampling approach was employed to select participants for the quantitative component of the study. A total of 434 commercial drivers were recruited from seven bus terminals within the Tamale Metropolis. This sampling method was chosen due to its efficiency in accessing respondents within their natural work environment.

Quantitative data collection instrument and technique

A structured questionnaire was developed to collect quantitative data from participants. The questionnaire included closed-ended questions covering key areas such as demographic characteristics, reasons for tramadol use, dosages of tramadol use, pattern of use and sources of tramadol. The questionnaire was translated into Dagbani (local language) and back-translated into English by a professional linguist. The translated version was used to interview participants who were not proficient in English. To ensure clarity, relevance, and reliability, the questionnaire was pretested with a small sample of drivers (40 drivers) outside the study area before full-scale implementation.

Data collection was conducted by trained research assistants who administered the questionnaire in person at various transport stations. Research assistants approached participants who provided informed consent and conducted the interviews while they waited for their buses to be loaded. To ensure privacy and minimize distractions, the interviews were conducted in the office of the station manager by the research assistants, which provided a conducive environment for data collection.

Validity and reliability

The reliability of the study’s primary questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a score of 0.764—exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.70. This indicates that the questionnaire demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, confirming the reliability of the study’s constructs.

Variables of the study

Independent and dependent variables

Dependent variables include knowledge of tramadol’s inappropriate use, level of tramadol’s inappropriate use, population group, and the independent variables, which include age, religion, gender, and educational level.

Qualitative data collection instrument and technique

For the qualitative component of the study, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 50 participants from among the participants in the quantitative study to gain an in-depth understanding of their lived experiences with tramadol use. An interview guide was developed based on an extensive review of existing literature and was further refined by experts in addiction research to ensure that the questions were relevant and appropriate for exploring key themes such as motivations for use, peer influence, accessibility, and perceived consequences of tramadol abuse.

To ensure linguistic accuracy and cultural relevance, the interview guide was translated into Dagbani, the predominant local language spoken in the Tamale Metropolis and subsequently back-translated into English to maintain consistency in meaning. Interviews were conducted in either English or Dagbani, depending on the participants’ language preference. Each interview lasted approximately 30 to 45 min and was audio-recorded with participants’ informed consent.

Data collection was carried out daily by trained research assistants, who ensured that no participant was interviewed more than once at each bus terminal. The process continued systematically across the selected transport stations until the required sample size was achieved. The study was conducted from September to December 2024.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

Quantitative data were entered and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarize categorical variables. To determine the factors significantly associated with tramadol’s inappropriate use, binary logistic regression analysis was conducted. The results were presented in tabular form, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Qualitative analysis

The qualitative data were transcribed verbatim and analysed manually using thematic analysis, following the framework outlined by Braun and Clarke [18]. An inductive coding approach was used to identify recurring patterns and themes related to tramadol’s non-medical use. The data were manually coded and categorized into themes and sub-themes. The qualitative findings were then compared with the quantitative results to facilitate triangulation, enhancing the depth and validity of the study’s conclusions.

Ethical considerations

The study received ethical approval from the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (KNUST) Committee on Human Research and Publication Ethics, with approval number CHRPE/AP/1033/23. Additionally, permission was obtained from the Tamale Metropolitan Assembly to conduct the study at various bus terminals.

All participants were fully informed about the study’s objectives, methodology, potential benefits, and risks in a language they understood and respected. Participation was entirely voluntary, and individuals had the right to withdraw at any time or decline to answer any questions they found uncomfortable. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, while those unable to write provided thumbprints to signify their consent.

To protect participants’ privacy and maintain confidentiality, code names rather than real names were assigned to all participants. Additionally, all audio recordings were securely stored for future reference in a manner that ensured anonymity.

Rigor and trustworthiness

To enhance the reliability and validity of the study, several measures were implemented:

The quantitative instrument underwent pretesting to ensure clarity, consistency, and reliability before data collection.

The qualitative component adhered to Lincoln and Guba’s [19] criteria for trustworthiness, ensuring:

Credibility through member checking, that is allowing participants to review and validate the findings. Transferability was also ensured by providing rich, thick descriptions to enable broader applicability of the results. Through the maintenance of a detailed audit trail, documenting all research decisions and processes, dependability was ensured. Data triangulation, achieved by integrating quantitative and qualitative findings, strengthened the overall validity and depth of the study.

Sociodemographic characteristics of participants

A total of 434 commercial drivers participated in the study, with a mean age of 26.8 ± 2.95 years and a modal age of 27 years. The participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 35 years, with the majority (73.7%) falling between 24 and 29 years. Only one participant was female, reflecting the male-dominated nature of commercial driving in the Tamale Metropolis.

Regarding marital status, most participants (81.8%) were single, while 17.3% were married and 0.9% were divorced. The majority identified as Muslims (67.5%), followed by Christians (30.0%) and traditionalists (2.5%). Educational attainment varied, with 27.0% having no formal education, 51.4% completing basic education, 16.1% reaching secondary education, and 5.1% obtaining tertiary education.

In terms of housing, 47.2% lived in rented accommodations, 44.2% in family homes, 6.5% were squatting, and 2.1% owned private homes. When asked about drug use awareness, 70.7% reported having information on drug use and abuse, while 29.3% had no prior knowledge as shown in Table 1 below.

Discussion

The findings highlight a significant pattern of non-medical tramadol use among participants, with 60.1% reporting its use for recreational or self-prescribed purposes rather than medical treatment. This finding is similar to the higher prevalence (85.2%) reported in the Kano metropolis, Kano State in Nigeria among commercial drivers [20]. This high difference is likely due to the small sample size of 197 used in the Nigerian study. The prevalence is however higher than similar studies in the Accra Metropolis in Ghana which reported 24.7% prevalence of non-medical use of tramadol among commercial drivers [2] This aligns with global concerns regarding the misuse of prescription opioids, particularly in regions where access to healthcare is limited, and self-medication is prevalent [21]. This finding adds to the clarion call to curb tramadol’s ianppropriate use especially among the youth. The high prevalence of daily tramadol use (41.5%) may suggests a dependency pattern. The responses from the focused group discussions (FGDs) further corroborate the possible dependence, with participants expressing an inability to function without the drug. Statements such as, “Tramadol is now my daily dose; if I don’t take it, my body will react in some way”, illustrate the compulsive nature of tramadol use, suggesting that many users may already be experiencing withdrawal symptoms when they attempt to stop. This aligns with the literature on opioid dependency, where tolerance and withdrawal symptoms drive continued use despite negative consequences [22].

Reasons for tramadol use

The study indicates that while some participants use tramadol for pain relief (19.4%), the majority (39.2%) consume it to enhance physical performance and activity. This finding aligns with existing literature suggesting that tramadol misuse is common among labour-intensive workers, commercial drivers, and young adults in physically demanding jobs, as they believe the drug enhances stamina and endurance [3, 4, 12].

Peer influence (14.1%) emerged as one of the leading reasons for tramadol use, confirming the role of social networks in substance use behaviours. Social learning theory suggests that individuals are more likely to adopt behaviours, including drug use if they are exposed to peers who use and promote such substances [23]. In this context, peer pressure may create an environment where tramadol use is normalized, particularly among young adults who seek social acceptance or shared experiences. Additionally, increasing sexual desire (11.5%) as a motivation for tramadol use is consistent with anecdotal reports and some regional studies linking opioid use to perceived aphrodisiac effects [24]. The finding is further corroborated by a study conducted in the Accra Metropolis in Ghana which also reported sexual enhancement as the motivation for tramadol use [2].

Modes of tramadol use

The results indicate diverse methods of tramadol consumption, with 43.5% of users mixing the powdered form with energy drinks, 31.6% swallowing the capsule with water, and 12.4% combining it with other substances. The preference for mixing tramadol with energy drinks is particularly concerning, as this combination can amplify the drug’s stimulant effects, increasing the risk of cardiovascular complications such as tachycardia, hypertension, and arrhythmias [25, 26]. Additionally, co-administration with energy drinks may contribute to psychological dependence, as users continuously sip their mixture to sustain the drug’s effects throughout the day. Some users reported removing the capsule’s coating before ingesting the powdered tramadol to achieve a faster onset of action. This practice suggests that users are not only aware of tramadol’s pharmacokinetics but are also actively modifying their consumption methods to intensify its effects. The increased bioavailability of the drug in powder form can heighten the risk of overdose and adverse reactions [6].

Dosage patterns and tolerance development

Findings from this study indicate that commercial drivers consume tramadol at significantly higher doses than recommended for medical use. The average daily intake among users has been reported as 155.5 ± 91.60 mg, which exceeds therapeutic doses [27]. This is consistent with findings from opioid research, which highlight that repeated use of tramadol leads to reduced drug effects, necessitating higher doses to achieve the desired euphoria or enhancement in performance [16, 28]. The use of higher doses may be attributed to the fact that opioid receptors in the central nervous system become desensitized with prolonged use hence, individuals require higher doses to attain similar effects [22, 29]. The findings of this study align with the World Drug Report [30], which highlights that Ghana’s illegal drug markets are flooded with tramadol doses exceeding prescribed limits.

Sources of Tramadol

Even though there are several sources of tramadol including pharmacies, hospitals and others, the study findings show that the commonest source of tramadol is the black market (72.8%). This is an illegal source of tramadol where higher doses beyond the therapeutic range of prescription are sold raising concerns about its accessibility despite government regulations [31]. The predominance of illicit sources suggests that tramadol has shifted from its intended medical use as a prescription analgesic to a widely available and frequently inappropriately used street drug. This trend is in line with broader regional patterns in West Africa, where tramadol trafficking has escalated, contributing to significant public health challenges [32]. The accessibility of tramadol through informal markets complicates regulatory efforts aimed at controlling its consumption and preventing inappropriate use. Studies indicate that weak enforcement mechanisms and corruption within pharmaceutical supply chains facilitate the diversion of controlled substances into illicit markets [33, 34]. Given these challenges, it is imperative to strengthen regulatory frameworks and enhance surveillance systems to mitigate tramadol’s no medical use and its associated risks. The index finding is corroborated by Lasong et al., [12] who reported same source of tramadol among university students in Northern Ghana.

Strengths and limitations

This study has notable strengths, including its concurrent triangulation mixed-methods design, which integrates both quantitative and qualitative data for a comprehensive understanding of the inappropriate use of tramadol. The large sample size enhances the validity of the findings, while the study’s focus on motivations, consumption patterns, and black-market supply provides practical insights for policy and intervention strategies. However, limitations include selection bias due to convenience and snowball sampling, social desirability bias from self-reported data, and the cross-sectional design, which prevents establishing causal relationships. Additionally, the lack of biological assessments and the study’s geographical restriction to the Tamale Metropolis may limit the generalizability of the findings. Despite these constraints, the study offers valuable evidence to guide future research and policy interventions on non-medical use of tramadol.

Conclusion

The findings of this study highlight a significant prevalence of non-medical tramadol use among commercial drivers, revealing a pattern of misuse that aligns with broader global concerns about opioid dependenc. The high rates of daily tramadol consumption and self-reported dependence suggest an emerging public health crisis, particularly in regions where self-medication is prevalent due to limited access to healthcare. The motivations for tramadol use, including pain relief, enhancement of physical performance, peer influence, and sexual enhancement, indicate a complex interplay of social, occupational, and psychological factors driving misuse. Furthermore, the widespread availability of tramadol through illicit markets underscores the challenge of regulatory enforcement, making it imperative to strengthen policies aimed at controlling its distribution and access. The varied modes of tramadol consumption, including its combination with energy drinks and manipulation of its pharmaceutical form, further highlight the need for targeted interventions to address evolving patterns of drug use.

Implications for practice

The study’s findings have significant implications for healthcare providers, policymakers, and law enforcement agencies. Given the high prevalence of tramadol dependence, there is a need for increased awareness and education on the risks of opioid dependence. Healthcare professionals should be trained to recognize the signs of tramadol dependence, integrating screening measures into routine medical consultations, particularly for individuals in high-risk occupations such as commercial driving. Additionally, harm reduction strategies, including the provision of medically supervised withdrawal programs and access to evidence-based treatment such as opioid substitution therapy, should be explored as part of a comprehensive response.

From a policy perspective, regulatory frameworks should be strengthened to limit the availability of tramadol through illicit markets. Improved surveillance systems and stricter enforcement of pharmaceutical distribution laws are essential to reducing the diversion of prescription opioids into the black market. Public health campaigns should also be developed to educate at-risk populations about the dangers of non-medical use of tramadol, particularly in labor-intensive sectors where its use is widespread. Furthermore, the integration of community-based interventions, including peer education programs, could help reduce the influence of social networks in normalizing tramadol use.

Implications for future research

Given the high prevalence of tramadol misuse, future research should focus on understanding the long-term health consequences associated with chronic use, particularly cognitive and neurological impairments. Longitudinal studies are needed to assess the trajectory of tramadol dependence and the effectiveness of different treatment interventions. Additionally, qualitative studies exploring the lived experiences of people who use tramadol could provide deeper insights into the socio-cultural factors driving its non-medical use and inform culturally appropriate interventions.
