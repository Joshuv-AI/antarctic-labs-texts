Engineering human RNase 7 with an eosinophil RNase segment reveals determinants of cytotoxic and antimicrobial activity
Authors: Thi Thanh Quynh Tran, Sacha T Larda, Cecilia Nieves, Frédéric J Veyrier, Charles Calmettes, Nicolas Doucet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman ribonuclease 7 is a potent antimicrobial member of the RNase A superfamily with little cytotoxic activity, in contrast to the closely related eosinophil cationic protein (HsR3). To determine which structural elements underlie these divergent functions, we engineered a chimeric RNase 7 variant in which loop 4 to 5 residues 61 to 77 were replaced by the corresponding segment from HsR3. This chimera acquired cytotoxicity toward HeLa cells while retaining antibacterial activity against Escherichia coli and Mycobacterium smegmatis, identifying this segment as a key transferable determinant of RNase function. Consistent with this result, a peptide spanning HsR3 residues 61 to 77 also displayed antibacterial activity, supporting a direct contribution of this region to host-defense activity and functional modulation within the RNase scaffold. To define the structural basis of RNase 7 activity, we determined crystal structures of RNase 7 in apo and 5′-AMP-bound forms and combined these with NMR titrations and molecular docking. These analyses revealed a canonical RNase fold together with an unexpected adenine-binding pose in the B1 subsite. Complementary binding analyses showed that RNase 7 preferentially recognizes pyrimidines, yet can also accommodate purine-containing ligands such as UpA in more than one binding mode. Together, these findings establish loop 4 to 5 as a portable functional module that can reprogram biological activity within the human RNase scaffold and provide a structural framework for engineering multifunctional RNases with tailored properties.Keywords: ribonucleases, RNase 7, RNase 3, eosinophil cationic protein, chimeragenesis, multifunctional enzymes, antimicrobial peptides, cytotoxicity

Human ribonuclease 7 (HsR7) is one of the eight canonical members of the human RNase A superfamily. It was first isolated from healthy human skin-derived stratum corneum and has been shown to play defining roles in the innate immune system (1, 2). Along with eosinophil cationic protein (HsR3), HsR7 is active against many pathogenic microorganisms and has been identified as one of the most potent human antibacterial ribonucleases (2, 3, 4). The corresponding RNASE7 gene (5) was found to be expressed in various other epithelial tissues, including the kidney, and the respiratory, urinary, genitourinary, and gastrointestinal tracts (1, 5, 6). HsR7 was found to act as a broad-spectrum antimicrobial protein against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, as well as the yeast Candida albicans. It is also active against multidrug-resistant bacteria such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium (1, 5). Interestingly, an N-terminal fragment of HsR7 (residues 1–97) was implicated as a key domain for antibacterial activity, exhibiting higher activity than the full-length protein against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus saprophyticus (7). Similarly, the N-terminal fragment of HsR3 was also found to play a defining role in its antibacterial activity. This observation is supported by previous studies using an HsR3-derived peptide comprising residues 5 to 17 and 22 to 36 linked by a proline, which preserves key structural and functional features of the full-length protein (8). This peptide, previously designated RN3 (5-17P22-36), is referred here as HsR3(5-17P22-36).

The antimicrobial mechanism of HsR7 was shown to rely on its ability to disrupt the bacterial membrane. This activity is associated with the abundance of cationic residues on the protein surface, including a total of 18 lysine and 4 arginine residues (9). The cationic identity of HsR7 residues is in sharp contrast to that observed in HsR3, which harbors 1 lysine and 19 arginine residues. Microscope observations also revealed the ability of HsR7 to disrupt bacterial cell membranes in E. coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and E. faecalis (10). Both HsR7 and HsR3 exhibit high affinity for the membrane components of Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, including lipopolysaccharides and peptidoglycans (11). In contrast, each enzyme exhibits distinct mechanistic timing and behavior in their capacity to disrupt lipid bilayer vesicles. While HsR3 can trigger liposome aggregation before any significant cell content leakage can occur, HsR7 induces liposome content release before aggregation (12).

Although prior work was instrumental in defining the antimicrobial behaviors of both HsR3 and HsR7, evolutionary pathways defining their divergent activities and functional differentiation remain elusive. Our recent protein engineering study illustrated how leveraging ancestral sequence reconstruction and chimeragenesis within the RNase structural fold can be used as an efficient tool to predict functional changes, in addition to transitioning antimicrobial and/or cytotoxic activities between subfamily members (13). In the present work, we further expand our structural and functional analysis between RNase homologs to predict and modulate the antibacterial and cytotoxic activities of standalone peptides and chimeric proteins between HsR7 and HsR3, in addition to investigating their antimycobacterial potential against Mycobacterium smegmatis.

Comparative analysis of HsR2, HsR3, a reconstituted RNase2/3 ancestor (AncRNase), and the RNase 3 homolog from the crab-eating macaque in our previous study (13) with HsR7 revealed key differences in the loop 4 and loop 5 environments delineated by residues 61 to 77 (HsR3 numbering). Residues of loop 4 to 5 distinguish a cytotoxic RNase subgroup (HsR3 and MfR3) from a noncytotoxic subgroup (HsR7, HsR2, and AncRNase). By designing a HsR7/HsR3 chimera that swaps residues 61 to 77 of HsR3 within the HsR7 scaffold (ChimR73), we demonstrate that it is possible to preserve the ribonucleolytic and antibacterial properties of this scaffold, while at the same time inducing cytotoxic behavior in an otherwise nontoxic HsR7.

By using chemically synthesized designer peptides and analogs, we further deconvolute this activity to confirm that a standalone sequence of HsR3 (residues 61–77) contributes to its antimicrobial and cytotoxic activity, much like the previously determined role played by the two N-terminal HsR3 α-helices (8). Finally, we solved the first X-ray structure of HsR7 in its apo and 5′-AMP-bound forms, in addition to performing NMR titration and molecular docking experiments to clarify molecular interactions that define nucleotide base preferences in its ribonucleolytic function. While most RNase homologs exhibit preferential binding of pyrimidines at the B1 subsite and a strong preference for purine ligands at the B2 subsite (primarily adenine) (14), our results are consistent with binding of adenine at both the B1 and B2 subsites in HsR7, with the pyrimidine uracil likely binding exclusively to the B1 subsite.

Overall, this work describes the first engineered variant of HsR7 that gains cytotoxic activity through targeted loop 4 to 5 replacement, establishing this region as a transferable functional module within the RNase A superfamily. We further present the first ligand-bound crystal structure of HsR7—integrated with NMR and peptide functional analyses—to reveal how loop identity and nucleotide subsite specificity cooperate to shape antimicrobial and cytotoxic phenotypes. Together, these findings provide a new framework for rationally redesigning human RNases with tailored biological functions.

Results

Chimeragenesis and functional characterization of RNases and peptide variants

In addition to their ribonucleolytic function, human RNases play distinct roles as antimicrobial and cytotoxic agents (15, 16). For instance, eosinophil cationic proteins in human (HsR3) and the crab-eating macaque (MfR3) exhibit both antibacterial and cytotoxic activities, while the homologous eosinophil-derived neurotoxin (HsR2) does not (13). To deconvolute the molecular determinants that define these biological activities, we previously resuscitated an evolutionary ancestor between the HsR2 and HsR3 family branch (AncRNase) (13). Our results showed that this ancestor preserves high antibacterial activity against E. coli and no cytotoxic activity against HeLa cells. Similarly, we predicted and designed a minimal 4-residue variant that transforms HsR2 into a chimeric enzyme endowed with the antimicrobial and cytotoxic activities of RNase 3 members.

To investigate whether the close HsR7 homolog is amenable to similar functional modulation, we performed enzyme chimeragenesis by considering RNase sequence conservation and the respective cytotoxic and antimicrobial activities of homologs. Since only the RNase 3 family members (HsR3 and MfR3) show cytotoxic activities in our characterized panel (13), we hypothesized that the conserved HsR3 and MfR3 residues that differ in HsR2, AncRNase, and HsR7 might play important roles in their overall cytotoxic activity. Based on sequence differences between HsR7 and these closely related eosinophil RNases, we designed an HsR7 chimeric enzyme (ChimR73) by introducing the unique HsR3 sequence of residues 61 to 77 (loop 4–5) into the HsR7 core (Fig. 1A). This design generates a mostly preserved HsR7 primary structure (113/130 residues, i.e., 87% HsR7) that exposes a newly extended loop sequence extracted from the HsR3 homolog (17/130 residues, i.e., 13% HsR3) (Fig. S1).

To evaluate the functional modulation of this ChimR73 enzyme design, we characterized its antibacterial, cytotoxic, and antimycobacterial activities in parallel with the HsR7 and HsR3 parental sequences (Fig. 1, B–D). Our antibacterial assays show that HsR7 exhibits significant antibacterial activity, yielding only about 1% cell viability at 5 μM enzyme concentration, much like HsR3 and ChimR73 (Fig. 1B). This activity confirms that the ChimR73 design is properly folded and preserves the parental antimicrobial activity of HsR7 against E. coli DH5α cells. This observation is consistent with previous studies performed on the WT enzyme (10, 17). We also performed cytotoxicity assays to test the ability of these RNases to kill immortalized HeLa cells. Despite exhibiting high antibacterial activity, our results show that HsR7 is nontoxic to HeLa cells (Fig. 1, B and C). In contrast, HsR3 and ChimR73 both exhibit strong antibacterial activity and cytotoxic activities. This confirms that the HsR3 loop 4 to 5 sequence (residues 61–77) can effectively induce cytotoxicity in an otherwise noncytotoxic HsR7 structural background.

Finally, we also investigated the antimycobacterial activity of these enzymes alongside HsR3 and HsR5, which are known to be toxic to Mycobacterium species (4, 18). Our assays were performed with M. smegmatis, an ideal nonpathogenic and fast-growing species within the Mycobacterium genus, which contains several slow-growing human pathogens (19). As expected (4, 18), HsR7 exhibited high antimycobacterial activity against M. smegmatis, while HsR3 and HsR5 induced cell death at 80% and 90%, respectively (Fig. 1D). Interestingly, while parental sequences HsR3 and HsR7 can both induce high antimycobacterial activity, this capacity is significantly reduced in ChimR73, yielding only about 40% cell death at 10 μM enzyme concentration.

Of note, clustered arginine residues have been shown to be involved in antibacterial and cytotoxic activities in HsR3. Indeed, mutations at arginine positions were previously shown to reduce antimicrobial and/or cytotoxic activity in this enzyme (20). The efficiency of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) is also often attributed to their cationic nature, in great part due to the increased presence of lysine and arginine residues (21). Since the HsR3 (61–77) sequence can induce antibacterial and cytotoxic activity in our ChimR73 design, a question came up whether the HsR3 (61–77) peptide can independently act against E. coli and HeLa cells. Indeed, this sequence contains five neighboring arginine residues located within HsR3 loops 4 and 5 (Fig. 1A). To test this hypothesis, we synthesized the HsR3 (61–77) peptide and an analogous variant in which Cys62 was replaced by Ala, here designated HsR3 (61A63-77), to prevent intra or intermolecular disulfide bond formation. These two peptides were tested for their antibacterial and cytotoxic activities, alongside a positive control of the fused N-terminal HsR3 α-helices (HsR3 (5-17P22-36)) (8) (Table S1). All peptides were synthesized, purified, and identified using HPLC and MALDI-TOF analyses (Fig. S2).

Our results show that peptides HsR3 (61-77) and HsR3 (61A63-77) exhibit high antibacterial activities against E. coli DH5α at low micromolar concentrations, similar to the positive control HsR3 (5-17P22-36) (Fig. 1B). After 3 h of incubation with E. coli cells, cell aggregation was clearly observed for all three peptide sequences. Interestingly, the HsR3 (61-77) sequence shares many similarities with the HsR3 (5-17P22-36) control. These two peptides both have five arginine residues, they both exhibit a pI value of 11.8, and preserve a special “RFR” or “RWR” motif shared by many AMPs (22). However, HsR3 (61-77) and HsR3 (61A63-77) exhibited markedly reduced cytotoxicity toward HeLa cells, whereas HsR3 (5-17P22-36) retained substantial activity, albeit at slightly lower potency than full-length HsR3 (Fig. 1C). Since peptide activity can be influenced by their secondary structure in solution, we experimentally characterized their secondary structure by circular dichroism (CD) and predicted their 3D structure using AlphaFold 3 (23) (Fig. S3). Our results suggest that HsR3 (5-17P22-36) maintains a secondary structure similar to that found in HsR3, that is, two α-helices separated by one truncated loop. In contrast, the structures of HsR3 (61-77) and HsR3 (61A63-77) were determined to be primarily random coils, with some amount of helicity (Fig. S3).

Effect of chimeragenesis on enzyme kinetics

Much like other canonical members of the human RNase A superfamily, HsR7 was previously shown to be catalytically active against nonhomogenous tRNA substrates (1, 5). Its ribonucleolytic activity against yeast tRNA is about 50-fold higher than that of HsR3 (1). As demonstrated in the previous section, HsR7 was also shown to maintain broad spectrum antimicrobial activity against several potential pathogens (1, 5). However, in contrast to other biological functions promoted by homologous RNases, the antibacterial activity of HsR7 appears to act independently from its ribonucleolytic function. Indeed, a catalytically impaired variant of HsR7 generated by mutating catalytic residues H15A, K38A, and H123A exhibited similar antimicrobial activity to that of the WT enzyme against P. aeruginosa (9). Similarly, the ribonuclease-inactive H123A variant was shown to retain antibacterial activity against E. faecium and E. coli (24). These observations suggest that HsR7 might solely rely on its high cationic content to perform its antimicrobial action, much like the behavior observed with many AMPs.

To investigate the catalytic effect induced by chimeragenesis, we performed enzyme kinetics on ChimR73 and compared it with HsR3 and HsR7 using yeast tRNA as a substrate (Table 1, Fig. S4). Results show that all three enzymes display very similar apparent substrate affinity (Km-app), suggesting that chimeragenesis does not significantly perturb substrate recognition in ChimR73. Interestingly, even though the primary structure of ChimR73 is 87% conserved with HsR7, the ChimR73 catalytic turnover (kcat) is much closer to that of HsR3. Our results show that catalytic efficiency (kcat/Km-app) against yeast tRNA is roughly four times lower in HsR3 and ChimR73 relative to HsR7 (Table 1), demonstrating the importance of the Loop 4/5 domain (residues 61–77) in HsR7 catalysis.

3D structure of HsR7 in the free and 5′-AMP bound states

To provide additional atomic clues on the unique functional behaviors promoted by HsR3, HsR7, and ChimR73, we performed structural, biophysical, and dynamical comparison between these close homologs. The 3D structure of HsR3 has been extensively studied in the free and ligand-bound states using both X-ray crystallography and NMR spectroscopy (25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30). In contrast, the solution structure of HsR7 was only previously solved by NMR spectroscopy in its free state (9).

In the present work, we solved the X-ray structure of HsR7 at 1.77 Å resolution (Protein Data Bank (PDB) 9CSM), with electron density and anomalous signal consistent with bound divalent metal ions most likely corresponding to Zn. We also solved the first structure of a ligand-bound HsR7, that is, in the presence of 5′-AMP at 2.07 Å resolution (PDB 9CSN). Both crystals belong to the P1211 space group of the monoclinic system (Table S2). Overall, these two crystal structures demonstrate that HsR7 preserves the typical kidney-shaped RNase fold observed in other structurally resolved members of the human RNase superfamily (Fig. 2A) (15).

HsR7 has three alpha helices, six beta sheets, and seven loops, in addition to sharing common structural properties with homologous human RNases. These include the signature CKXXNTF motif, the His-Lys-His catalytic triad (His15, Lys38, and His123), and eight cysteine residues forming four disulfide bonds (Fig. S2A). HsR7 appears to crystallize with four bound metal ions per molecule. Based on the anomalous difference density contoured at the 5σ level and the presence of ZnCl2 in the crystallization solution, these ions were modeled as Zn, although other metals, including Cu, cannot be formally excluded from the diffraction data alone (31, 32, 33). Extracting backbone RMSD of C-alpha backbone atoms, we compared the apo structure of HsR7 with that of human RNases HsR1 to HsR6 (Table S3). As expected from the representation of an ensemble state that averages backbone flexibility, the NMR solution structures of HsR1 and HsR7 resulted in the highest RMSD values. However, owing to the preservation of core structural elements of this fold and despite showing varying degrees of sequence homology, all crystal structures show very low structural variability (RMSD values < 1 Å) (Table S3). This certainly contributes to the structural and functional tolerance observed in ChimR73, which hails from HsR3/7 parental sequences that only share 40% sequence identity (Fig. 2C). In fact, our own AlphaFold 3 model of ChimR73 predicts few overall structural variations relative to the WT parents, even predicting nearly identical conformation of the swapped residues 61 to 77 (loop 4–5) relative to the crystal structure of HsR3 (Fig. 2B, inset).

The 5′-AMP purine ligand can bind the B1 subsite of HsR7

The mechanism of action of pancreatic-type RNase superfamily homologs is tributary of nucleic acid base preferences that involve interactions with the three main residue subsites, typically labeled B1, P1, and B2 (Fig. 3A). These enzymes primarily interact with the nucleobases of a bound RNA substrate at the B1 and B2 subsites, with catalytic residues breaking down the scissile P-O5′ bond of the phosphoryl group located in the P1 subsite (14). Historical studies on BtRA have observed that the B2 subsite is the preferred binding site for purines, that is, primarily adenine (14, 34), whereas the B1 subsite tends to favor pyrimidines (35, 36). Complemented by kinetics studies, these observations were also confirmed in the crystal structures of homologous RNases such as BtRA, HsR2, HsR3, and HsR6 (Fig. 3, A and B). However, variations in the biological functions and cellular localization of these transphosphorylases are just a few of the many factors that influence broad catalytic efficiencies within the family, and consequently their subsite recognition profiles. To better delineate subsite preferences in HsR7, we tried to crystallize it in the presence of different ligand analogs, but could only solve the X-ray structure of a purine-bound 5′-AMP enzyme complex (Fig. 3C). This HsR7 holo structure (PDB 9CSN) consists of two chains (A and B), with 5′-AMP in the active site of chain A and a glycerol molecule populating chain B (Fig. S6). The latter was found to form salt bridges with two of three catalytic residues (H15 and K38), in addition to being hydrogen bonded to three water molecules. Overall, chains A and B have similar structures, except for a slight difference in the conformation of the catalytic H123. This histidine residue was shown to adopt different orientations among RNase A superfamily members, illustrating its involvement in substrate binding and product release (13, 37).

In chain A, the electron density map (contoured at 1.0σ) shows residues interacting with the 5′-AMP ligand, which is positioned deep in the active-site pocket (Fig. S6, B and C). Interestingly, 5′-AMP binds to the B1 subsite of HsR7 rather than the B2 subsite, contrasting with most RNase structures complexed with purine nucleotides (Fig. 3, B and C). Chain A shows interactions between 5′-AMP and residues Q14, H15, K38, L40, N41, T42, H123, L124, D125, and V127. K63 is located near the ligand-binding site, but its side-chain electron density is not sufficiently well defined to support a specific interaction with 5′-AMP (Fig. 3C). We observed polar contacts between the ligand phosphate group and residues Q14, H15 in α1, in addition to H123, L124 in β6 (the C-terminal β-strand). An additional water molecule (HOH21) participates in stabilizing the phosphate group of 5′-AMP. Finally, the ribose moiety of 5′-AMP forms hydrogen-bonding interactions with H15 (α1), K38 (loop 2), and L40, N41, and T42 (β1), whereas the purine ring interacts with D125 and V127 (β6) (Fig. 3C). The 5′-AMP-bound crystal structure of HsR7 illustrates a relatively rare instance of a B1 subsite purine preference among human ptRNase homologs.

NMR chemical shifts and ligand binding affinity suggest purine subsite promiscuity in HsR7

Ligand promiscuity was previously observed in human ptRNase homologs, suggesting that the affinity of more complex RNA substrates is defined by a combination of base selectivity, nucleotide position, and potentially stronger affinity of pyrimidines in the B1 subsite. To verify this hypothesis, we performed NMR titration experiments on 15N-labeled HsR7 in the presence of several small nucleotide ligands (Figs. S7–S9).15N-HsR7 showed nicely dispersed resonances on a {1H-15N}-heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC) spectrum, corresponding to a well-folded protein. We performed resonance assignments using a 15N-13C-labeled HsR7 and a combination of {1H-15N}-HSQC spectra with 3D experiments such as HNCACB and CBCA(CO)NH (38). Resonance assignments of {1H-15N} backbone amide vectors were achieved for 111 out of an expected total of 128 residues, corresponding to 86.7%. Unassigned residues included five proline residues, K1 at the N terminus, residues 34 to 38 in loop 2, and residues 87 to 92 in loop 6.

To identify the preferred binding sites of purine and pyrimidine mononucleotides in HsR7, we first performed NMR titrations experiments on 15N-labeled HsR7 using 5′-AMP and 3′-UMP. The pyrimidine and purine mononucleotides are known to bind to the B1 and B2 subsites of ptRNases, respectively (Fig. 3A). A list of residues displaying large chemical shift perturbations (CSPs, Δδ > 0.1 ppm) is provided in Table S4 and mapped on the structure of each protein in Figure 4. Binding of 5′-AMP resulted in large CSPs for 16 residues located throughout the protein, but primarily near residues of the B1 and B2 subsites (Figs. 4A, S7 and Table S4). These results contrast with BtRA and HsR2-4 homologs, whereby titrations with 5′-AMP resulted in residue perturbations primarily located near loop 4, in the purine binding site (B2 subsite) (39). The largest CSPs in HsR7 are observed in the C-terminal β-strand, including residues H123, L124, and D125 that directly interact with the phosphate group and purine ring of 5′-AMP in the HsR7 X-ray structure (P1 and B1 subsites) (Fig. 3C). Catalytic residue H15 also displays large CSPs, which is expected for a residue that directly interacts with a substrate phosphate group during catalysis. Although NMR results suggest that 5′-AMP may also interact with the B2 subsite in HsR7, they nevertheless confirm the crystallographic observation of the unique binding orientation in the B1 subsite.

As expected, binding of 3′-UMP pyrimidine resulted in large CSPs near the B1 subsite (Figs. 4B and S8). Ten residues displaying large chemical shift changes (Δδ > 0.1 ppm) are listed in Table S4, of which eight residues (Q14, H15, N41, H123, D125, L40, F43, and K63) are located in the P1 and B1 subsites. The remaining residues include L83 and D95, which are far from the active site and potentially suggest small conformational changes or dynamic variations upon ligand binding. These results are consistent with the pyrimidine subsite preference observed in homologous RNases.

Based on these NMR titrations, we hypothesized that B1 subsite preference for pyrimidines could still drive the binding of a purine to the B2 subsite of HsR7 in the context of polynucleotide ligands. To confirm this hypothesis, we performed NMR titration experiments with the deoxyribopentanucleotide ligand d(ACACA), mimicking a single-stranded RNA substrate analog. Our results indicate binding of this ligand to the active site of HsR7, with 19 residues showing large CSPs (Δδ > 0.1 ppm) (Figs. 4C and S9). These include residues located in both the B1 and B2 subsites (Table S4), that is, L40, F43, L44, K63, R126, V127 (B1 subsite) and N64, H123, D125, W10 (B2 subsite). Together with binding affinity measurements (see below), these findings provide strong evidence for synergistic interactions between the pyrimidine and purine subsites, facilitating the accommodation of longer single-stranded substrates within the HsR7 active site.

We used Isothermal Titration Calorimetry (ITC) and NMR line shape analysis implemented with the Quadratic10 equation of NMRViewJ to extract affinities of mono- and poly-nucleotide ligands binding to HsR7 (40). We extracted NMR Kd values of 1105 μM for 5′-AMP, 114 μM for 3′-UMP, and 10 μM for d(ACACA). These results respectively indicate a 9.7-fold and 111-fold stronger affinity for the 3′-UMP pyrimidine and d(ACACA) ligands relative to the 5′-AMP purine. We also confirmed these results by ITC, extracting similar Kd values of 850 μM for 5′-AMP, 294 μM for 3′-UMP, and 63 μM for d(ACACA) (Table S5). To identify binding affinity preferences in HsR7, we also used ITC to extract Kd values for a broader range of mono- and poly-nucleotide ligands, further comparing them to the prototypical BtRA homolog (Fig. 5 and Table S5). Overall, BtRA shows stronger binding affinity than HsR7 for all mononucleotide ligands, exhibiting Kd values ranging between 21 to 271 μM relative to 194 to 850 μM in HsR7. These results are in line with the previously observed higher ribonucleolytic activity observed in BtRA (41, 42). Oligonucleotides also exhibited overall stronger affinity than mononucleotides in both BtRA and HsR7, confirming the beneficial synergistic effects provided by multiple nucleotide moieties binding to the distinct subsite pockets of these enzymes. Interestingly, the nearly millimolar affinity of 5′-AMP is lower than all other mononucleotides tested in HsR7, including the 5′-GMP purine. This latter ligand is a notoriously low B2 subsite binder in most homologous RNases (14), suggesting that it may also bind to the B1 subsite in HsR7.

Protein-ligand docking

To complement the structural data and model ligand interactions not captured experimentally, we performed protein-ligand docking simulations of HsR7 with 5′-AMP, 3′-UMP, and the dinucleotide UpA. This approach enabled us to probe alternative subsite occupancy patterns and rationalize the unusual B1 purine binding in the context of canonical pyrimidine preferences. The UpA dinucleotide is a model RNA substrate that typically serves as a mimic of pyrimidine (U base, B1 subsite) and purine (A base, B2 subsite) preference in homologous RNases (14, 43). All docking runs were performed using chain A of the HsR7 crystal structure in complex with 5′-AMP (PDB 9CSN), after removal of the ligand and further preparation steps (see Experimental Procedures for details). We generated ten poses for every ligand model docked to the HsR7 (Fig. S12). Docking results are presented in Figure 6, displaying the binding modes for each ligand according to their convolutional neural network pose score output by GNINA (Table S6). We also illustrate possible hydrogen bonding interactions between the protein and each ligand for distances lower than 4.0 Å. The protein-ligand interaction profiler was also visualized using PLIP (44).

Our docking models of HsR7 with 5′-AMP suggest that the purine ring of 5′-AMP possibly binds to both the B1 and B2 subsites of HsR7 (Fig. 6, A and B). Indeed, we observed ten 5′-AMP poses, of which three poses preferentially bind to the B1 subsite, three poses to the B2 subsite, and four poses outside the active site (Fig. S12A). The top scoring binding orientation shows 5′-AMP in the B2 subsite (Fig. 6A), consistent with the binding orientation of 5′-AMP observed in other RNases (13, 45) and our own NMR titration experiments (Fig. 4). Figure 6A shows that 5′-AMP interacts with nine residues. The purine ring of 5′-AMP is stabilized by parallel π-stacking with H123, and hydrogen bonding interactions with N64, D66, N68, and D125. The ribose moiety forms a hydrogen bond with H123. Finally, the phosphate moiety of 5′-AMP interacts with Q14, V122 and L124 through hydrogen bonding interactions, and with K38 and H123 through salt bridges. The ninth pose (pose 9 rescored by GNINA), which has the lowest binding energy of the ten poses (Table S6), closely resembles the binding mode of 5′-AMP at the B1 subsite observed in our HsR7 crystal structure (Fig. 6B). Consistent with the crystallographic data, this pose preserves interactions between 5′-AMP and eleven residues including Q14, H15, K38, L40, N41, T42, K63, H123, L124, D125, and V127.

As expected, the docking of 3′-UMP to HsR7 indicates strong binding likelihood to the B1 and P1 subsites (Fig. 6C). Among the ten predicted poses, six poses occupy the B1 subsite, while the remaining four poses bind outside the active site (Fig. S12B). The top scoring pose shows interactions between 3′-UMP with Q14, H15, K38, N41, T42, H123, L124 and V127. Finally, we used docking to examine how a real dinucleotide substrate, UpA, could exploit binding subsites in the active site of HsR7. Interestingly, from a set of ten poses, we only identified five typical orientations where uracil was bound at the B1 subsite and adenine at the B2 subsite. The remaining poses did not adopt the orientation typically observed for bound substrates in the active site of RNases (Fig. S12C).

Interactions that most likely reflect crystallographically observed oligonucleotide substrate positioning in analogous RNases were observed in binding pose 2 (Fig. 6D). In this model, the positions of the ribose group of the adenine base is flipped relative to the docking model of HsR7 with 5′-AMP (Fig. 6, A–D), which may help prevent steric clashes with the uracil base. The purine ring still interacts with H123 through π-stacking interactions, and through hydrogen bonding interactions with N64, D66, and D125. The ribose group of the adenine base only forms hydrogen bonding interactions with H123. In comparison to the 3′-UMP docking model with HsR7, the pyrimidine ring of the uracil base shifts closer to T42, while the ribose group retains hydrogen bonding interactions with H15, K38, N41, T42, and L124 (Fig. 6, C and D). In the UpA structure, the scissile phosphate group retains salt-bridge interactions with K38 and H123 and forms hydrogen bonds with Q14 and H15. Notably, an additional hydrogen bond is observed between the phosphate group and W10, a feature absent in both the 5′-AMP (Fig. 6A) and 3′-UMP (Fig. 6C) docking models. Interestingly, the binding mode of UpA (pose 2) displays a similar orientation to that observed with 5′-AMP and 3′-UMP in the crystal structures of BtRA (PDB 1Z6S and 1O0N, respectively) (Fig. 6D).

Overall, our docking results corroborate NMR titration experiments with various ligands and align with the 5′-AMP binding conformation observed in the HsR7 crystal structure. A strong binding preference for 3′-UMP at the B1 subsite is supported, whereas 5′-AMP exhibits less preferential binding, potentially occupying both B1 and B2 subsites. In contrast, 3′-UMP and 5′-AMP tend to bind at the B1 and B2 subsites, respectively, in various ptRNases such as BtRA, HsR2, HsR3, and HsR4 (39). Notably, HsR7 demonstrates greater binding directionality with the dinucleotide UpA compared to mononucleotides.

Discussion

In the human RNase A superfamily, both HsR3 and HsR7 exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, with their bactericidal effects attributed to membrane destabilization (12). Previous studies have highlighted the critical role played by N-terminal residues in antibacterial function. Specifically, the N-terminal peptide of HsR3, comprising residues 5 to 17 and 22 to 36 connected by a proline, retained the biophysical properties of the full-length protein, including lipopolysaccharide binding and cell agglutination (8, 46). Interestingly, an HsR7 fragment spanning residues 1 to 97 exhibited even greater antibacterial potency than the full-length enzyme (7). Despite similar antibacterial efficacy, HsR7 showed reduced cytotoxicity toward HeLa cells compared to HsR3. Here, we identify loop 4 to 5 as a portable determinant of RNase function: by transplanting residues 61 to 77 from HsR3 into HsR7, we conferred cytotoxicity while preserving antibacterial activity, revealing a trade-off with antimycobacterial potency. Together with peptide assays, these observations support a “two-module” model in which an N-terminal α-helical module drives membrane engagement/agglutination, while a cationic loop 4 to 5 module tunes cytotoxicity.

Our engineered chimeric enzyme ChimR73 allowed us to investigate these functional differences by incorporating residues 61 to 77 from HsR3 into HsR7. This modification yielded an enzyme with enhanced antibacterial and cytotoxic properties. Furthermore, we intentionally synthesized a peptide that matched residues 61 to 77 from HsR3. The effectiveness of the HsR3 (61-77) peptide was notably lower than that of HsR3 and ChimR73, whereas the HsR3 (5-17P22-36) peptide maintained strong antibacterial and cytotoxic properties, comparable to the full-length protein. The shared chemical properties of these two peptides—including high arginine content, a pI of 11.83, and the presence of unique terminal motifs ‘RFR’ or ‘RWR’—likely contributed to their functional roles against E. coli and HeLa cells. Despite these similarities, structural differences were evident: the HsR3 (5-17P22-36) peptide primarily adopts an α-helical conformation, whereas HsR3 (61-77) assumes a random coil structure in solution. This potentially compromises its stability and efficacy compared to the conformation within the intact HsR3 enzyme. Collectively, these findings indicate that HsR3 possesses two key functional modules: the N terminus region and a secondary region encompassing residues 61 to 77 (loop 4–5), with both domains distinctively contributing to antibacterial and cytotoxic activities.

Like other RNase A superfamily members, HsR7 exhibits activity against RNA substrates but demonstrates variability in ligand recognition and substrate discrimination. Our analysis of HsR7 interactions with mono- and oligonucleotides, including 5′-AMP, 3′-UMP and d(ACACA), revealed distinct binding behaviors. Integrating crystal, NMR, and ITC data indicates a pyrimidine-favored B1 subsite in HsR7, with adenine able to occupy B1 or B2 depending on ligand context: (i) X-ray captures 5′-AMP at B1; (ii) NMR CSPs map to B1 and B2 for 5′-AMP but predominantly B1 for 3′-UMP; and (iii) binding analysis rank higher ligand affinity (lower Kds) for d(ACACA), followed by 3′-UMP, and finally 5′-AMP. In accordance with the integrative view described above, adenine is consistent with occupying B1 or B2 depending on context, whereas 3′-UMP binds predominantly at B1. Although we obtained the crystal structure of HsR7 in complex with 5′-AMP at the B1 subsite, NMR titration experiments and docking models with 5′-AMP, 3′-UMP, and UpA suggest a notable preference for uracil over adenine at the B1 subsite. This preference enables specific binding of UpA, with uracil occupying the B1 subsite and adenine binding at the B2 subsite. Docking was used here as a hypothesis-generating tool to rationalize binding modes we could not crystallize (e.g., pyrimidine-bound forms and dinucleotides) and to explore dual-occupancy models in a manner consistent with CSPs and Kd patterns.

Finally, to further characterize the binding properties of HsR7, we used ITC to evaluate its binding affinity with other mononucleotides, including 5′-GMP, 5′-CMP, and 5′-TMP. Surprisingly, while HsR7 displayed similar dissociation constants (Kd) for all tested mononucleotides except 5′-AMP, its affinity for 5′-GMP was significantly stronger than for 5′-AMP. Our results confirm that HsR7 exhibits unique ligand recognition and substrate discrimination properties between purine and pyrimidine bases at the B1 subsite relative to other RNase A homologs. These variations underscore disparities in RNase activity mechanisms that likely confer distinctly specialized functional roles to this member of the ptRNase superfamily. Overall, these findings support a modular, designable view of human RNases in which discrete surface elements tune subsite usage and biological outcomes.

In summary, this study introduces the first chimeric HsR7 variant with engineered cytotoxic activity, demonstrating that the loop 4 to 5 region functions as a transferable module capable of modulating biological properties across RNase family members. By combining chimeragenesis with high-resolution structural analysis, NMR, and peptide activity assays, we established a direct link between loop sequence identity, nucleotide subsite specificity, and the dual antimicrobial-cytotoxic profile of human RNases. These insights not only advance our understanding of structure-function relationships within the ptRNase superfamily, but also lay the groundwork for rational design of multifunctional RNases with therapeutic potential.

Experimental model and subject details

Cell lines and bacterial strains

HeLa cells (American Type Culture Collection CCL-2) were used for cytotoxicity assays and were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free before use. The bacterial strains used in this study were E. coli DH5α and M. smegmatis mc2 155. Growth media and culture conditions are described in the Method details section.

Methods details

Protein expression, refolding, and purification

All RNases were expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) using either the pJExpress414 vector (ATUM) for HsR5 and HsR7, or the pET22b vector (Novagen) for HsR3. Plasmid transformation, protein expression, RNase refolding and purification steps were performed as previously described, with slight modifications (13). The plasmids were transformed into competent E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells by heat shock (47). A single colony was picked and grown in LB medium overnight at 30 °C to create a preculture solution. Unlabeled proteins were expressed in LB medium while 15N- or {15N, 13C}-labeled samples were prepared by growing E. coli BL21(DE3) in a M9 minimal medium as described by Doucet et al. (48). A 1 L of growth culture was inoculated using 20 ml of preculture. Protein expression was induced by adding 1 ml of 1 M IPTG when OD600nm reached 0.8, which was maintained for 4 h at 37 °C. After expression, unfolded RNases were extracted from the inclusion bodies of the E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells and purified by Reversed-phase HPLC using C18-reversed phase silica gel (Vydac) packed into a 250 × 40 mm column. Fractions containing RNases were identified by MALDI coupled to time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF) (Bruker), as well as by analytical RP-HPLC using a CSC-Kromasil C18 column (100 Å, 5 μm, 4.6 × 250 mm).

To refold and obtain functional enzymes, the disulfide bridges were reformed in a refolding buffer (100 mM Tris-acetate, pH 8.5 (HsR3, HsR5) or 8.74 (HsR7), 0.5 M L-arginine, 3.2 mM reduced glutathione, 0.8 mM oxidized glutathione, 2 mM EDTA, and 5% glycerol) at 4 °C with slow stirring for 48 to 72 h. Refolded RNases were dialyzed against buffer A (15 mM sodium acetate, pH 5.0) and purified using a 5-mL cation exchange HiTrap SP HP column. SDS-PAGE was used to assess protein purity, and protein concentrations were determined using a NanoDrop ND-2000c UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) based on molecular weights of 15,650, 14,274, 14,501 Da and extinction coefficients of 17,460, 11,835, 8980 M−1 cm−1 for HsR3, HsR5 and HsR7, respectively, as estimated with ExPASy ProtParam. Secondary structure evaluation was carried out using a Jasco J-815 CD spectropolarimeter.

Peptide synthesis, purification, and characterization (HPLC/MALDI-TOF/CD)

Three peptides were synthesized in this study: a 17-aa peptide corresponding to residue sequence 61 to 77 of HsR3 (HsR3 (61-77)); an analogous peptide with a C62A mutation (HsR3 (61A63-77)); and a previously characterized positive control (HsR3 (5-17P22-36)) (8) (Table S1). Each peptide was synthesized using a solid-phase peptide synthesis strategy with Fmoc-chemistry on a CEM Liberty Blue automated synthesizer using 1.1 g of Rink amide AM resin from Matrix Innovation (0.3–0.8 mmol/g). The Fmoc group was first removed from the resin with 10 ml of 20% piperidine in 20 min. To form the peptide bond, the coupling reaction included 11 ml of 0.2 M Fmoc-amino acid, 5 ml of N,N′-Diisopropylcarbodiimide and 2 ml of 1 M Oxyma. All solutions were prepared in N,N-Dimethylformamide. The deprotection and coupling reactions were carried out sequentially and repeatedly until a complete peptide chain was obtained. The resin was washed three times with 10 ml of N,N-Dimethylformamide between each step. Full deprotection and cleavage from the resin was achieved using 88% TFA, 10% H2O, and 2% TIS at RT for 3.5 h. Resin beads were removed from the peptides by a filter. Crude peptides were obtained upon precipitation and extensive washing with cold ether. Targeted peptides were purified by HPLC using a C18 column (Phenomenex, 10 μm, 21.2 × 250 mm) eluted with 0 to 100% acetonitrile gradient in 0.1% aqueous TFA. Only HPLC fractions with purity higher than 90% were collected. Fractions were monitored by UV-absorption at 230 nm and screened by mass spectrometry (Bruker microflex MALDI-TOF spectrometer, using α-Cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid matrix). Fractions containing the peptide of interest were pooled, concentrated, and freeze-dried. Pure peptides were characterized by analytical HPLC using 0 to 100% acetonitrile gradient in 0.1% aqueous TFA, for 30 min, at a flow rate of 1 ml/min using CSC-Kromasil C18 column (100 Å, 5 μm, 4.6 × 250 mm), detection at 230 nm and mass spectrometry.

Antibacterial assays

Antibacterial assays were conducted using E. coli DH5α (DSMZ, German Collection of Microorganisms) based on the Miles-Misra method (49). All experimental steps were performed according to the previous procedure (13). RNase/peptide solutions were diluted in a 10 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.4 (PBS 1 X) to concentrations ranging between 1 μM and 50 μM, and mixed with bacterial suspension. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 3 hours, and then sequentially diluted 10-fold over the 100-10−5 dilution factor range. Twenty microliters of the three most diluted bacterial concentrations were placed in small drops on LB-agar plates. Each concentration was replicated four times to create four droplets. Agar plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C and the number of colony-forming units (CFUs) were counted in every droplet. To examine the findings, all CFUs were transformed into CFUs/ml and averaged for each sample. BtRA was used as a negative control because it does not show antibacterial activity.

Cytotoxicity assays

The cytotoxicity activity of RNases was tested on HeLa cells CCL-2 (American Type Culture Collection), as described previously (13). Cells were cultured in MEM media containing 10% fetal bovine serum. To begin with, cells were plated in 96-well plates at a concentration of 50,000 cells per well and were starved of serum for 6 h before being treated with RNases or peptides at concentrations ranging from 0.5 μM to 50 μM. Subsequently, cells were incubated for 36 h before assessing cell viability using the CellTiter Blue assay kit from Promega. Fluorescence intensity was measured using an Infinite M1000 pro plate reader with excitation at 560 nm and emission at 590 nm. BtRA and dimethyl sulfoxide were used as negative and positive controls, respectively.

Antimycobacterial assays (M. smegmatis)

Luciferase assays were performed using M. smegmatis mc2 155 harboring pOE-Lx (50). As previously described (51), bacteria were grown in 7H9 at 37 °C in presence of hygromycin until cultures reached an OD600 of 0.5. After centrifugation, the cultures were resuspended in PBS and used for luciferase assays. Plates of 96 wells (Becton Dickinson labware) were used to set up the experiments. Each well consisted of 2 × 106 bacteria and the protein of interest at final concentrations ranging from 1 μM to 10 μM in a total volume of 150 μl. As a no light emission control, WT (nontransformed) bacteria in PBS were included. Transformed bacteria in absence of protein served as a light-emitting control. Gentamicin at a final concentration of 20 μg/ml was used as an antimycobacterial activity control. Plates were incubated for 96 h. Immediately before measuring luminescence, 10 μl per well of 1% n-decyl aldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich) in absolute ethanol (luciferase substrate) was added. Light emission was measured in the Victor 3 TM Wallac 1420 Multilabel Counter (PerkinElmer) over a 10 s period. Each experimental condition consisted of three technical replicates, independently repeated three times. Luminescence output was expressed in log10(relative light unit).

Enzyme kinetics with yeast tRNA

RNase activity was assessed by following a previously published enzymatic procedure (13). Assays were performed with enzyme concentrations of 8.33 nM for HsR7 and 16.67 nM for ChimR73. The reaction mixture contained 5 μl of yeast tRNA with different concentrations ranging from 1 to 8 mg/ml in 160 μl of enzyme solution prepared in 40 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). Reaction was stopped at various intervals between 0 to 30 min by adding 130 μl of 20 mM lanthanum nitrate with 3% perchloric acid. The quenched reactions were cooled on ice for 15 min, followed by the removal of insoluble tRNA through centrifugation at 12,000 g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant samples, which contained soluble tRNA, were then transferred into UV half area 96-well plates from corning and analyzed at 260 nm using an Infinite M1000 pro from Tecan to quantify the hydrolyzed product. Initial velocities corresponding to different initial substrate concentrations were obtained from at least three experiments. Absorbance readings were converted to determine total RNA concentration in micrograms, where an A260nm value of 1.0 corresponds to 40 μg of RNA. tRNA concentration was calculated using the average molecular weight (Mr) of 28,100 Da (75–90 ribonucleotides/tRNA molecule x Mr 341/ribonucleotide) (52). GraphPad Prism was used to calculate initial velocity of the enzymatic reactions as well as Km and Vmax values. The turnover number (kcat) was determined by applying the formula kcat = Vmax/[E].

Crystallization, data collection, and structure refinement

Crystallization experiments were all performed at RT using the sitting-drop vapor diffusion technique.

HsR7

2 μl of 8 mg/ml HsR7 solution (in buffer A) was mixed at a 1:1 ratio with 0.01 M zinc chloride, 0.1 M MES, pH 6.0, 20% w/v PEG 6000. Crystal formation took place for 4 to 5 months. Crystals were cryo-protected by soaking in a solution of 0.01 M zinc chloride, 0.1 M MES, pH 6.0, 20% w/v PEG 6,000, 30% 2-methyl-2,4-pentanediol.

5′-AMP-bound HsR7

A mixture of 6 mg/ml HsR7 and 5′-AMP with a molar ratio of 1:10 was used for cocrystallization. In a crystallization plate well, 2 μl of the mixture was added to 2 μl of 0.1 M MIB (malonic acid:imidazole:boric acid = 2:3:3 M ratio), pH 7.0, 25% w/v PEG 1500 solution. Protein crystals grew after 4 to 5 months. Crystals were cryo-protected by soaking in a solution of 0.1 M MIB, pH 7.0, 25% w/v PEG 1500 solution, and 30% methyl-2,4-pentanediol.

All crystal diffraction data were collected at the Canadian Macromolecular Crystallography Facility Beamline 08B1-1 of the Canadian Light Source Synchrotron (53). Structure refinement was carried out using the PHENIX (54) and CCP4 (55) software suite. The NMR solution structure of HsR7 (PDB: 2HKY) was used as a template for molecular replacement method. All structural comparisons and visualizations were performed using the open-source PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 2.5.0.

{1H-15N} HSQC acquisition, NMR assignments, CSP analysis, and Kd estimation

15N- or {15N, 13C}-labeled protein samples were evaluated for purity and secondary structure by one- and 2D NMR spectroscopy on a 600 MHz Bruker Avance III spectrometer equipped with a 5 mm TCI cryoprobe at the Molecular Interactions and Materials Characterization Platform of the Centre Armand-Frappier Santé Biotechnologie Research Centre of INRS. Subsequent NMR experiments were carried out at 298 K on a Bruker 800 MHz NMR spectrometer (QANUC, McGill University). Samples were prepared at concentrations ranging from 140 μM to 400 μM in 15 mM sodium acetate buffer, pH 5.0 with 10% D2O. All spectra were processed using NMRPipe11.5 (56) and CcpNmr Analysis2.5.2 (57) on NMRbox (58).

To perform backbone resonance assignments for HsR7, 2D and 3D {1H-15N}-HSQC, HNCACB and CBCA(CO)NH spectra were recorded using 500 μl of protein sample at 400 μM. Titration experiments were performed using 5′-AMP, 3′-UMP, or deoxyribopentanucleotide d(ACACA) dissolved in the NMR buffer. {1H-15N}-HSQC spectra were recorded in the presence of 0, 0.174, 0.392, 0.697, 1.306, 2.714, 5.994, 12.004, 17.998, and 20.001 M equivalents of 5′-AMP or 3′-UMP as well as 0, 0.012, 0.027, 0.048, 0.090, 0.187, 0.413, 0828, 1.764, and 2.014 M equivalents of d(ACACA). 1H-15N compounded chemical shifts (Δδ) were calculated as described earlier (39). The dissociation constant (Kd) was determined using NMRViewJ (40) on NMRbox (58). The Kd represents the ligand concentration at which 50% of the changes in chemical shifts are observed.

ITC experiments

HsR7 was expressed and purified as described above, whereas bovine pancreatic ribonuclease A (BtRA) was obtained commercially (Bio Basic). Both proteins were prepared in 300 μl of 20 mM MES buffer at pH 6.4, with concentrations ranging from 80 μM to 100 μM. Each RNase sample was titrated with various ligands such as 5′-AMP, 5′-UMP, 3′-UMP, 5′-GMP, 5′-CMP, 5′-TMP, d(ACACA), and d(ATATA) at concentrations between 1 mM and 5 mM. This titration involved 24 injections of 2 μl for each ligand, with 220-s intervals between injections. Experiments were carried out three times independently using a NanoITC microcalorimeter (TA Instruments). The Kd values were obtained from the NanoAnalyze software v2.3.6.

Protein-ligand docking (software, parameters, and pose selection)

Chain A of the HsR7-5′-AMP cocrystal structure was used for docking (PDB 9CSN). The high-resolution crystal structure was prepared for docking first by removal of ligand, ions, and solvent followed by use of AutoDockTools-1.5.6 for addition of polar hydrogens and assignment of Gasteiger charges.

Rough 3D coordinates for the ligands were obtained by either drawing the desired structure in ChemDraw or downloaded from pubchem as sd files. The 5′-AMP ligand structure was obtained from the cocrystallized complex with HsR7 (PDB 9CSN). Ligands were subsequently prepared by removing all hydrogens, reassigning hydrogens according to standard protonation states and were saved as .mol2 files (59, 60). The ligands were then prepared as pdbqt files using AutoDockTools (61), using default settings (Gasteiger charges, default TORSDOF, etc.). Docking was performed using GNINA v1.1 (62) using a box size of 48, 40, 40, centered and encompassing the expected binding sites; a fixed seed of 1039587504; exhaustiveness = 64 (AMP and UMP) or exhaustiveness = 256 (for UpA), and default scoring options were used (GNINA rescore). A total of ten binding modes were generated for each ligand.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Assays were all carried out in at least three independent sets of experiments, and the data collected were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software. Results of antibacterial and cytotoxic tests were presented as percentage of the negative control (cells treated with 1 X PBS) and represent the mean ± SD. Significance was assessed using one-way ANOVA with ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
