Ordinary differential equation model of cancer-associated fibroblast heterogeneity predicts treatment outcomes
Authors: Junho Lee, Eunjung Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are key components of the tumor microenvironment (TME). CAF phenotypes are highly heterogeneous and exert anti- and protumorigenic effects. We present a mathematical model that describes cancer-immune-CAF interactions and exploits the heterogeneity of CAF phenotypes to predict cancer progression and treatment response. By simulating multiple treatment options, including targeted monotherapies alone, two different immunotherapies, and a combination of therapies, we have found that CAF composition can impact treatment outcomes, potentially resulting in comparable effectiveness of single-drug treatments and combinatorial approaches or even the ineffectiveness of multicombination therapies. These findings suggest that CAF composition can be a promising indicator, in some cases guiding the choice towards less invasive therapies without compromising effectiveness. Our model indicates that accounting for CAF characteristics might facilitate the matching of targeted treatments, supporting clinical decision-making.

Introduction

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is known to contribute to tumor progression (as reviewed in ref. 1). In particular, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are among the key components of the TME; they release various secretory factors, deposit extracellular matrix components and interact with tumor cells and other cells in the TME (as reviewed in refs. 2,3). CAFs exhibit significant phenotypic and functional heterogeneity4–7. Some CAF phenotypes can control the growth of tumor cells by either promoting8–10 or suppressing cancer progression11–13. CAFs can also modulate the immune landscape in the TME (as reviewed in refs. 14,15). Treatment outcomes are also influenced by specific CAF subtypes16,17. For example, CAFs with cancer-like phenotypes or extracellular matrix-related CAFs with low immune cell infiltration are correlated with poor patient survival. Conversely, CAFs associated with a proinflammatory TME contribute to improved survival in patients with cancer. Therefore, an assessment on the basis of a patient-specific CAF landscape could, in principle, guide more effective therapy choices. Even though previous studies have reported beneficial or detrimental CAF subtypes, the systematic prediction of treatment response and clinical outcomes on the basis of CAF phenotypes for individual patients requires the establishment of tailored algorithms and simulation models.

Mathematical modeling can be useful for predicting treatment outcomes and strategizing effective therapies (as reviewed in refs. 18–21). For combination therapies, designing a treatment strategy with experimental studies alone is challenging because of the extensive number of possible combinations of multiple drugs with different doses, timings, and orders. Various mathematical modeling approaches have been developed to investigate different types of combination therapies (as reviewed in ref. 22). In particular, several mathematical models describing cancer-immune dynamics have been developed to propose effective treatment strategies for immunotherapies alone23–25 or in combination with chemotherapy26,27.

To understand the role of CAFs in cancer progression and treatment responses, several mathematical and computational models have been developed. For example, Kraut et al. have developed a mechanistic mathematical model composed of a system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) that captures the dynamics between cancer cells, stromal fibroblasts, and therapeutic agents. Their model is designed to simulate stroma-induced drug resistance and to identify effective treatment strategies within a clinically informed cancer-stroma interaction framework28.

Chowkwale et al. have investigated mesenchymal transformation within the TME by developing a multiscale model29. However, these studies generally consider CAF populations as homogeneous. The impact of CAF phenotypic heterogeneity, a potentially critical determinant of treatment success, has not been investigated thoroughly.

To address this gap, we introduce a novel mathematical model that explicitly integrates the phenotypic diversity of CAFs. This model, constructed using ODEs, allows us to predict how this heterogeneity affects cancer growth and treatment response. We model the complex interactions among cancer cells, CAFs, effector T cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs), specifically considering four distinct CAF phenotypes: antiimmune, proimmune, anticancer, and procancer (CAF phenotypes are described in detail in Section 2.1). By systematically varying the proportions of these phenotypes in a simulated TME, we investigate cancer growth and responses to targeted therapy and two immunotherapy approaches (PD-L1 inhibitor and Treg inhibitor). On the basis of these simulations, we propose model-informed strategies for optimal treatment tailored to specific CAF phenotypic compositions. To construct and simulate our model, we use a combination of experimentally informed and theoretically motivated parameter values. Some parameters, such as PD-1/PD-L1 binding kinetics and drug decay rates, are drawn from prior biological literature, whereas others particularly those related to CAF phenotype influences are selected from biologically reasonable ranges and calibrated to qualitatively match known cancer-immune-CAF behaviors. This approach enables our model to generate mechanistic insights while remaining grounded in empirical data.

Results

Fibroblast phenotypes

This study focuses on the phenotypic differences in CAFs in the TME. The model assumes that a fixed number of CAFs exist in the TME and uses an indicator function to represent the cell population (F = 1 if CAFs are present in the TME, F = 0 otherwise). To systematically investigate the role of the different CAF phenotypes, we divide them into four categories, antiimmune, proimmune, anticancer, and procancer, on the basis of the experimental and clinical studies described below (Fig. 1).Fig. 1CAF phenotypes.CAF phenotypes are categorized into four groups: antiimmune, proimmune, anticancer, and procancer. CAF phenotypes.

CAF phenotypes are categorized into four groups: antiimmune, proimmune, anticancer, and procancer. The weight parameters (α, β, γ, δ ∈ [0, 1], α + β + γ + δ = 1) assigned to each category vary depending on the specific CAF phenotype.

The procancer phenotype is based on findings that CAFs can produce growth factors and cytokines that promote cancer progression30. For example, CAFs are a key source of Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), which facilitates cancer growth by activating phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) and protein kinase AKT pathway31. Conversely, we define as anticancer CAFs those phenotypes associated with the restraint of cancer progression, potentially through mechanisms such as the expression of apoptosis-inducing ligands like TRAIL, which has been shown to induce cell death in TRAIL-sensitive cancer cells. For instance, CAFs activated by TNF-α or nucleic acid fragments from apoptotic cells can secrete IFN-β and TRAIL, contributing to a cancer-suppressive microenvironment12,32.

T cells, or T lymphocytes, are white blood cells that play crucial roles in immune surveillance and cancer control. Among them, effector T cells directly eliminate cancer cells, whereas Tregs suppress immune activity to maintain homeostasis. The antiimmune phenotype of CAFs is defined by their immunosuppressive properties, including the upregulation of immune checkpoints and the recruitment of Tregs33–35. In particular, CAFs can express PD-L1 and FASL, which bind to PD-1 and FAS receptors on effector T cells, leading to functional exhaustion or apoptosis of these cells, thereby weakening the anticancer immune response36. In contrast, the proimmune phenotype of CAFs is less well characterized; however, emerging evidence suggests that certain subsets of CAFs may support the infiltration and activation of effector T cells within the TME, thereby contributing to cancer suppression37,38.

We assign four parameters (α, β, γ, δ ∈ [0, 1]) to represent the percentage of each CAF phenotype in the TME. The sum of all the parameters equals 1. This antiimmune effect α indicates that CAFs play a role in binding cancer cells’ programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) to effector T cell programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) to inhibit T-cell activity. α represents the recruitment of Treg cells by CAFs in the TME. The percentage of proimmune CAFs, β, represents the reactivation of effector T cells from exhausted effector T cells by CAFs. Furthermore, the anticancer effect (γ) involves cancer cell death by CAFs, and the procancer effect (δ) involves the activation of PI3K in cancer cells by CAFs to stimulate cancer cell growth (Fig.1).

Model parameters

We first nondimensionalize the model. The reference value for each parameter is listed in Table 1, and the detailed process of nondimensionalization is provided in the Methods. The reference dimension represents the unit value of each variable in the model. Our model is parameterized on the basis of literature sources when possible, as shown in Tables 2–3 (see the Supplementary Note for a detailed explanation of some parameterization and Fig. S1). Some parameters have not been measured experimentally or clinically. For these cases, we make a biologically reasonable order of magnitude approximations. To evaluate the robustness of our model predictions and identify key drivers of cancer progression and treatment efficacy, we conduct a sensitivity analysis (see the Methods and Fig. S2). By quantifying how small changes in individual parameters affect the model outputs, this analysis highlights which biological processes and CAF-related effects have the greatest influence on cancer cell dynamics. Initial conditions

The initial cancer cell density is set to 0.01 (C(0) = 0.01), representing a small cancer burden at the onset of the simulation. The initial density of activated effector T cell is 0.1 (Ta(0) = 0.1), allowing for a minimal preexisting immune presence. For PD-1/PD-L1 binding kinetics, both the ligand and receptor levels are initialized to 10 ([L](0) = 10, [R](0) = 10), allowing for a maximal complex level of 10. The PI3K level is initialized at 0.3 (P(0) = 0.3) to ensure dynamic responsiveness to CAF-derived stimuli. All other variables are initialized to zero. The system of ODEs is numerically solved using MATLAB’s ODE45 solver.

CAF phenotypes modulate cancer growth

In this section, to investigate the impact of the CAF phenotype on cancer growth, we simulate cancer growth for 27 months under various TME conditions. This time frame is chosen because the cancer growth rate estimated through parameter fitting is relatively low (Fig. S1); thus, a longer simulation period is required to observe meaningful changes in cancer dynamics, while ensuring that cancer cell density does not exceed the carrying capacity under protumorigenic conditions. In our model, TME conditions are defined by varying the phenotypic composition of CAFs using four parameters: the antiimmune rate (α), proimmune rate (β), anticancer rate (γ), and procancer rate (δ). These parameters represent the relative functional tendencies of the CAFs and are systematically varied under the constraint α + β + γ + δ = 1.

Prior to the cancer growth simulation, we check the local dynamics of PD-1 and PD-L1 (Fig. S3). In scenarios where all four CAF phenotypes are equally present (α = β = γ = δ), we observe increased cancer growth compared with that in a TME devoid of any CAFs (all CAF phenotypic parameters = 0). The activity of cytotoxic T cells is significantly reduced, resulting in a subdued immune response (Fig. 3A). Notably, exhausted effector T cells are absent when CAFs are not present. This is because, in our model, exhaustion of activated effector T cells occurs only through CAF-mediated antiimmune mechanisms, such as increased PD-1/PD-L1 complex formation. Without CAFs, this pathway is not triggered; thus, activated effector T cells do not become exhausted. This design emphasizes the role of CAFs in driving immune evasion via the PD-1/PD-L1 axis. Additionally, the recruitment of Tregs increases in the presence of CAFs (Fig. 3B), and the activity of PI3K in cancer cells is elevated in the presence of CAFs compared with that in the absence of CAFs (Fig. 3B). Phenotype of CAFs affects immunotherapy outcomes

CAF phenotypes can affect immunotherapy outcomes. For example, our 27-month simulations of two immunotherapy strategies (PD-L1 inhibitor and Treg inhibitor) demonstrate that, depending on the CAF phenotype, one treatment may effectively suppress cancer growth, whereas the other may not (Fig. 6). If cancer cells reside in a TME with 100% proimmune CAFs (α = 0, β = 1, γ = 0, and δ = 0), a Treg inhibitor can significantly inhibit cancer cell growth (Fig. 6A) owing to the effective restraint of Treg recruitment by therapy in combination with the sustained activity of cytotoxic T cells by proimmune CAFs. However, the effect of a PD-L1 inhibitor is negligible in this TME. The PD-L1 inhibitor can restrain cancer cell density by approximately 20% when cancer cells reside in a different TME with a proimmune knockout (i.e., α = γ = δ = 1/3; Fig. 6B). A more than 70% reduction can be achieved with the most favorable CAF phenotypes (e.g., a combination of high anticancer and low procancer CAFs). For example, therapy can reduce the cancer burden by approximately 70%, with a greater abundance of anticancer CAFs (γ = 0.8) and no procancer CAFs (δ = 0; Fig. 8 cross-section plot, with γ = 0.8). We also observe cancer cell density reduction in some cases where the TME lacks antiimmune CAFs (i.e., CAFs with proimmune, anticancer, and procancer effects only; Fig. S6).

The complex treatment response pattern highlights the complex interaction between CAFs and cancer cells under immunotherapy. Although either PD-L1 inhibitor or Treg inhibitor can be highly effective under favorable TME conditions, which depend on specific CAF phenotypes, their overall effectiveness is limited by the complex and dynamic interactions within the TME. Understanding these interactions is essential for improving the use of these therapies.

The model simulations demonstrate that CAF phenotypes can modulate anticancer therapy outcomes. The same therapy can be effective in one TME with particular CAF phenotypes but not in another TME with different CAF phenotypes. We seek to identify an effective treatment strategy on the basis of CAF phenotypes. The treatment is started when the cancer cell density reaches 2.5 times the initial density. The treatment duration is 6 months. Impact of CAF phenotypic heterogeneity on treatment efficacy under diverse cancer growth conditions

Thus far, we have assumed a single cancer cell growth rate. However, in reality, this rate can vary between patients. To address this variability and better capture the uncertainty inherent in cancer growth rates, we vary key model parameters to simulate a range of treatment outcomes. Specifically, we sample 500 growth rates (r ∈ [7.03e − 5, 1.5e − 3]) from a standard normal distribution, reflecting a 95% confidence interval centered on the median cancer cell growth rate42. Additionally, to capture the inherent stochasticity and interpatient variability in CAF phenotypes, we randomly generate 500 sets of CAF phenotype rates-α, β, γ, and δ-within the constraint α + β + γ + δ = 1.

We construct a dataset of 500 randomly generated CAF phenotype combinations and cancer growth rates, reflecting patient-to-patient heterogeneity in both TME composition and cancer proliferation dynamics. Under no treatment conditions, more than 70% of the simulated cases have progressed after 10 months, and approximately 90% have progressed after 27 months. Monotherapy with either a PD-L1 inhibitor or a Treg inhibitor slightly improves the probability (1% increase with a PD-L1 inhibitor & 3% increase with a Treg inhibitor). Notably, the application of a PI3K inhibitor alone significantly increases progression-free survival, increasing survival to 21%. The PI3K inhibitor strikingly improves early survival outcomes, increasing the 10-month survival rate to 60%, which is almost double the survival rate observed without any treatment (33.2%). Combining all three therapeutic agents–PD-L1 inhibitor, Treg inhibitor, and PI3K inhibitor–offers the most dramatic improvement. This combined treatment approach increases the 10-month survival probability to 72.2% and increases the 27-month survival probability to 60.8%.

Our previous simulations reveal that Treg inhibitor can lead to a wide range of treatment responses depending on the specific CAF phenotype (Fig. 8). In this simulation, we choose two CAF phenotypes, one that shows the least efficacy under inhibitor therapy (CAF1 in Fig. 8, where α = 0.2, β = 0, γ = 0, and δ = 0.8) and another that exhibits the highest efficacy (CAF2 in Fig. 8, where α = 0, β = 0.1, γ = 0.9, and δ = 0). In TMEs characterized by CAF1, the median survival time under Treg inhibitor treatment is only 6 months, whereas under PI3K inhibitor treatment, it extends to 11 months, indicating a more favorable outcome. However, for the CAF phenotype that responds best to Treg inhibition (CAF2), at 27 months, PI3K inhibitor treatment results in a survival rate of 38.8%, whereas Treg inhibitor treatment results in a higher survival rate of 66.4%. This finding suggests that while Treg inhibitor may take longer to manifest its full potential, it can provide superior survival benefit compared with that of PI3K inhibitor, particularly in patients whose CAF phenotype is optimally aligned with the mechanism of action of Treg inhibitor.

Discussion

Here, we develop a mathematical model based on ODEs, incorporating 11 state variables and 33 parameters, to simulate the impact of CAF phenotypic heterogeneity on cancer cell growth and treatment responses. By integrating different CAF phenotypes into the model, we are able to capture their distinct influences on cancer progression. CAFs with cancer cell growth-promoting phenotypes or immunosuppressive phenotypes increase the cancer cell growth rate. In contrast, an increased presence of anticancer or proimmune CAF phenotypes counteracts these effects, leading to suppressed cancer growth. These findings reflect the complex interplay between stromal and immune components in the TME and are consistent with recent studies showing that specific CAF subtypes are associated with worse survival outcomes in non-small cell lung cancer patients43.

PI3K inhibitor responses vary significantly depending on the CAF phenotypic composition. This finding supports recent experimental findings that colorectal cancer cells exhibit increased resistance to PI3K pathway inhibitors when exposed to fibroblast-conditioned media44. Similarly, our model predicts variability in immunotherapy responses, with more pronounced variability observed for a Treg inhibitor than for a PD-L1 inhibitor.

The broad variability in treatment response highlights the critical need for a tailored therapeutic approach. Currently, the immune landscape is considered to predict and identify patients responsive to immunotherapy; however, our findings indicate that CAFs play an important role in predicting the outcome of immunotherapy45.

Model-driven effective treatment strategies concerning specific CAF phenotypes suggest that a significant (at least 25%) cancer burden reduction can be achieved through either single-drug treatments or a combination of two involving PD-L1 inhibitor, Treg inhibitor, and PI3K inhibitor. This finding is particularly important, as monotherapies are often associated with reduced side effects, and identifying patients in which monotherapy is predicted to be as effective as combinatorial therapy might improve their quality of life, minimizing adverse events46. Notably, when the proportion of anticancer CAFs exceeds 60%, our model predicts that monotherapy alone can yield outcomes comparable to those of combination therapies. Some cancers in the TME with low to moderate levels of anticancer effects in combination with moderate levels of antiimmune CAFs require a combination of all three factors to reduce the burden. Conversely, a combination of high antiimmune, low proimmune, and low anticancer CAFs results in cancers that are resistant even to combination therapy with three drugs. These findings highlight the potential for CAF phenotype analysis to inform optimal therapy selection and underscore the possibility of targeting CAFs directly to overcome treatment resistance47. These predictions are experimentally testable. Recent advances in single-cell profiling techniques, such as multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) and mass cytometry, have allowed the phenotypic composition of CAFs to be quantified at single-cell resolution in patient biopsy samples48,49. Moreover, multiplex fluorescence immunohistochemistry (mFIHC) is a clinically applicable method for profiling CAF phenotypes in situ50. The quantitative outputs from these technologies can be directly incorporated into our mathematical modeling as input parameters, enabling individualized predictions of treatment outcomes on the basis of cancer-specific CAF profiles. In addition, model predictions can be validated in preclinical settings using 3D tumor organoid systems or patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, which recapitulate the complex stromal context of human tumors. Taken together, these experimental approaches provide a translational bridge between in silico modeling and clinically actionable precision oncology strategies.

Furthermore, this modeling approach may be translated into clinical practice by leveraging mFIHC, which enables high-resolution spatial profiling of CAF phenotypes in patient biopsy samples. The quantitative outputs of mFIHC can be used as input parameters for our mathematical model, allowing the prediction of individual treatment outcomes on the basis of the unique CAF composition within each TME. This approach supports the integration of model-driven simulations into clinical decision-making pipelines and paves the way for CAF-informed precision oncology strategies50.

This study also investigates the potential effects of variations in cancer cell growth rates on treatment outcomes. Considering the various parameter sets, the predicted progression-free survival under the PI3K inhibitor is significantly longer than that under either the PD-L1 inhibitor or Treg inhibitor. This variability suggests that both cancer-intrinsic factors and microenvironmental influences contribute to therapeutic success. Progression-free survival is, however, significantly affected by the CAF phenotype. In one CAF phenotype condition, progression-free survival under the Treg inhibitor is high, with almost 70% not progressing after 27 months of treatment, which is greater than that under the PI3K inhibitor (40% not progressing). In another CAF phenotype condition, only 10% does not progress under the Treg inhibitor after 27 months of treatment. These findings demonstrate the potential of CAF characterization for better personalized therapy. They also suggest that variability in CAF composition may be responsible for unexpected differences in treatment outcomes between patients.

Our current work presents a deterministic mean-field model describing cancer cell dynamics interacting with immune cells and CAFs with various phenotypes. This model can be extended to specific cancer types by integrating the relevant underlying biology of each cancer type. Several other factors, such as stochasticity and spatial heterogeneity, may affect treatment outcomes and can be investigated in future studies. In our mathematical model, we treat the population of CAFs as static owing to their long half-life, assuming stability within the TME. Although CAF populations are modeled as static parameters, we introduce variability in CAF phenotype parameters to mimic the effects of dynamic population changes. This approach allows for the exploration of phenotypic variations and their impacts on therapeutic outcomes, maintaining model simplicity while providing insights into cancer progression. However, this simplification may overlook specific transient dynamics that could be crucial under certain therapeutic conditions. Future models may include dynamic CAFs population and phenotypic variability to further assess their roles under different conditions.

An additional advantage of our mathematical modeling approach is that it enables the tracking of cancer and immune dynamics continuously across time. This approach allows for the assessment of treatment responses and microenvironmental effects at any desired time point, providing temporal resolution that may not be feasible in clinical or in vivo settings. However, a notable limitation of our model lies in its lack of spatial considerations, as it is constructed on the basis of ODEs. Consequently, the model does not account for spatial heterogeneity or cell–cell interactions that can be critical in shaping therapeutic outcomes, particularly in complex cancer architectures. Our modeling approach can be extended to incorporate spatial aspects using PDEs and agent-based modeling, facilitating the investigation of local microenvironmental cues, CAFs positioning, and the spatial coordination of immune–cancer interactions with increased fidelity.

Our results provide a theoretical understanding of the impact of CAF heterogeneity on cancer progression and treatment outcomes. Our model results emphasize the importance of CAF phenotypes in determining treatment outcomes. We also provide a rationale for personalized treatment choices that are finely tuned to the specific characteristics of the TME. Our model can contribute to the refinement of predictive models aimed at optimizing targeted therapy matching in precision oncology to ultimately maximize efficacy and minimize side effects. However, we acknowledge that the clinical application of this approach requires accurate identification of CAF phenotypic composition in individual patients. While this is not yet routinely feasible in current clinical settings, recent advances in single-cell sequencing and spatial profiling technologies offer promising avenues for capturing patient-specific CAF heterogeneity49,51. As such data become increasingly available, our model may serve as a computational platform to guide treatment design and hypothesis generation and can be used in synergy with experimental and clinical research to advance personalized cancer therapy.

Sensitivity analysis

We perform a sensitivity analysis of model parameters. Specifically, we utilize a model sensitivity analysis method developed by Krischner’s group, a general Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) scheme, and Partial Rank Correlation Coefficient (PRCC)52. The Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) scheme is a method used to efficiently sample input parameter spaces by ensuring that the entire range of each parameter is evenly represented. It allows for broad and systematic exploration of parameter combinations with fewer samples compared to simple random sampling. The Partial Rank Correlation Coefficient (PRCC) is a statistical technique used to evaluate the strength and direction of the relationship between an input variable and an output, while controlling for the effects of other variables. PRCC is based on rank correlations, making it suitable for detecting monotonic relationships even when the underlying relationship is nonlinear. This sensitivity analysis method has been used in several mathematical modeling studies53–55.

We investigate the sensitivity of densities of cancer cells (C), activated effector T cells (Ta), exhausted effector T cells (Te), regulatory T cells (Tr), level of PD-L1 ([L]), PD-1 ([R]), complex (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[\overline{L\cdot R}]$$\end{document}[L⋅R¯]) and PI3K (P) to these parameters at several time points. We choose a range for each of these parameters of interest and divide the ranges into 10,000 subintervals of uniform length for the calculation of the corresponding partial rank correlation coefficient (PRCC) value. A PRCC value is a real number in the interval [−1, 1] with the sign, where the sign indicates an increase (+) or decrease (-) of the given variable in response to an increase in the parameter at a given time.

Figure S2 shows the PRCC values of all variables at t = 1, 30, 810 days. The density of cancer cells is positively correlated to r, k, g2, a1, α, λRF, λR, k1, λP, λPG, δ but negatively correlated to g1, g3, rT, β, μR, k−1, μP, γ, b1. Especially, some parameters related to immune activity (rT, β, b1) show low PRCC absolute values on day 1, indicating a weak correlation with cancer cell density, but as time progresses, the absolute values of PRCC increase, demonstrating a stronger relevance.
