Clinical Outcomes of Photobiomodulation Therapy for Inferior Alveolar Nerve Injury: A Retrospective Analysis
Authors: Halenur Ates, Yavuz Tolga Korkmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis retrospective study evaluated the clinical outcomes associated with a photobiomodulation therapy (PBMT) protocol in patients with inferior alveolar nerve (IAN) injury following oral and maxillofacial surgery. The study specifically investigated whether the etiology of paresthesia, the time elapsed before treatment initiation, and the number of PBMT sessions were associated with variations in clinical outcomes.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 66 affected sides in 55 patients (39 female and 16 males; mean age 35.69 ± 14.49, range 15–80). The study sample consisted of patients with IAN injury due to maxillofacial surgery who were treated with PBMT and followed for at least 6 months. Patients were grouped based on the number of sessions (< 15 vs. ≥ 15), timing of treatment initiation (within vs. after 1 month), and surgical etiology (orthognathic vs. other procedures). Clinical neurosensory tests (two‐point discrimination, light touch, pinprick) and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) assessments were performed.ResultsImprovements in VAS scores and clinical neurosensory test results were observed during the follow‐up period (p < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were found between groups regarding overall outcomes. Although earlier treatment initiation and a higher number of sessions were associated with higher neurosensory scores, these factors did not reach statistical significance in multivariable analysis. Patients with orthognathic surgery‐related injuries demonstrated significantly lower neurosensory scores compared to other surgical etiologies (p < 0.05). Follow‐up duration was not significantly associated with clinical outcomes.ConclusionWithin the limitations of this retrospective observational study, PBMT was associated with improvements in neurosensory outcomes in patients with IAN injury. However, due to the absence of a control group and the heterogeneity of surgical etiologies, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and a causal relationship cannot be established. Further well‐designed controlled studies are needed to clarify the potential role of PBMT.

Introduction

Inferior alveolar nerve (IAN) injury is one of the most common complications after oral and maxillofacial surgery [1, 2]. These types of injuries can lead to considerable discomfort while performing everyday tasks such as chewing, eating, and talking [3]. Causes of IAN injury include [4] impacted tooth extraction [4], dental implant surgery [5], orthognathic surgery [6], endodontic treatment [7], cyst and tumor operations [8], and orofacial trauma [9]. When IAN injury occurs, it is crucial to quickly assess the cause, degree, and possible treatments, eliminate the cause, and start the necessary treatments as soon as possible [10].

In the literature, the incidence of impacted tooth extraction‐related IAN injury is 0.4%–23% [11], implant‐related IAN injury is 0%–40% [12] and bilateral sagittal split osteotomy‐related IAN injury is 85%–100% [13]. Neurosensory tests such as the two‐point discrimination test, light touch test, pinprick test, and thermal tests are used to detect, identify, and quantify the extent of IAN injury. Visual Analog Scale (VAS) is used to convert some values that cannot be measured numerically into numerical form [14].

Surgical treatment methods used in the treatment of IAN injury include decompression [15], neuroma resection [16], internal neurolysis [17], neurorrhaphy [18], nerve grafts, and intubation techniques [19], while non‐surgical methods include pharmacological treatment [20], acupuncture [21] and Photobiomodulation Therapy (PBMT) [2]. IAN injury to peripheral nerves is a pathology that challenges physicians clinically, and the chance of success after surgical treatment is generally limited [22]. The PBMT is a non‐invasive procedure that accelerates wound healing and is used in the treatment of IAN injury [23]. The PBMT has been shown to both reduce the production of inflammatory mediators of the arachidonic acid family from injured nerves and promote regeneration after injury [24].

The purpose of this study was to investigate whether the effectiveness of PBMT in patients with IAN injury is influenced by the underlying cause of paresthesia, the time elapsed before initiation of treatment, and the number of therapy sessions applied. Specifically, this study hypothesized that: (1) the etiology of paresthesia may affect treatment outcomes; (2) earlier initiation of PBMT may lead to better clinical improvement; and (3) increasing the number of treatment sessions may enhance the therapeutic efficacy.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Sample

A retrospective study design was used. In this study, the records of patients who applied to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Faculty of Dentistry, Karadeniz Technical University, between January 2019 and January 2023, with complaints of IAN injury resulting from maxillofacial surgery. Informed consent forms signed by all patients before starting treatment, including the causes of IAN injury, symptoms, diagnosis and treatment methods, the laser used, and application method, were obtained from records. None of the patients received adjunctive physiotherapy, speech therapy, or additional neurosensory‐targeted pharmacological treatment during the PBMT period.

The inclusion criteria for the study sample were patients with unilateral or bilateral IAN injury due to maxillofacial surgery who were treated with PBMT and followed up for at least 6 months. The diagnosis of the patients was confirmed by clinical neurosensory testing.

Exclusion criteria for the study were patients with IAN injury due to other reasons (pathology, infection, etc.). Additionally, patients with active infection (pain, increased fever, redness, edema, loss of function, etc.) or pathological findings (tumor, cyst, etc.) in the relevant area were excluded from the study. Patients with lingual nerve injury were also excluded from the study. Besides, patients with systemic neuropathies, systemic conditions potentially affecting nerve healing or study outcomes, previous nerve repair surgery, pregnancy, use of photosensitizing medications, or a history of head and neck malignancy were also excluded.

This retrospective study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Karadeniz Technical University (Protocol No: 8/2022) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Because the study was based on anonymized archival clinical data and involved no direct patient contact, the requirement for individual informed consent for publication was waived by the Ethics Committee. This clinical trial was officially registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov (Registration No: NCT06893497).

Study Variables

Patients were categorized according to three independent variables: number of PBMT sessions (< 15 vs. ≥ 15 sessions), surgical etiology (orthognathic vs. other procedures), and time to treatment initiation (≤ 1 month vs. > 1 month). All groups received identical laser parameters and application sites. The cutoff value of 15 sessions was selected based on sample distribution to ensure balanced subgroup sizes for descriptive comparison rather than to represent a biological threshold.

The primary predictor variable was the number of sessions in which PBMT was performed. The number of PBMT sessions ranged from 3 to 25. Patients receiving PBMT were divided into two groups: those who received fewer than 15 treatment sessions and those who received 15 or more treatment sessions.

The cause of IAN injury and treatment timing were evaluated as study variables. Patients were categorized into two groups according to etiology: orthognathic surgery and other surgical procedures. Other procedures included third molar extraction, implant surgery, cyst enucleation, and local anesthesia injection. The mean interval between surgery and initiation of PBMT was 6.86 ± 19.39 months (range: 0.23–120 months). Symptom onset was documented immediately postoperatively in all cases. Patients were also divided according to treatment initiation: within 1 month or after 1 month following injury. In cases where surgical notes indicated a high risk of intraoperative IAN injury (e.g., canal perforation, unfavorable fracture, traction, thermal damage, or nerve exposure), or where a definitive diagnosis of nerve injury had been established, PBMT was initiated within the first month. To ensure consistency, only patients without evidence of early spontaneous recovery were included. Absence of early spontaneous recovery was defined as no improvement in clinical neurosensory testing findings or patient‐reported symptoms within the first month after injury.

The outcome variable of the study was improvement in nerve injury. Neurosensory status was assessed before and after treatment using a modified Clinical Neurosensory Test (CNT) protocol based on the methodology described by Zuniga et al. [25] and subsequently applied by Miloro [2]. All patients underwent a standardized clinical neurosensory evaluation comprising Level A testing (two‐point discrimination), Level B testing (light touch), and Level C testing (pin‐prick nociception). Two‐point discrimination was assessed using a calibrated caliper. Distances of 15, 10, and 5 mm were tested, and the smallest distance at which the patient correctly identified two distinct points was recorded. Light touch sensation was evaluated using a blunt‐tipped instrument applied to 10 standardized points over the affected lower lip. The total number of correctly identified contact points (range: 0–10) was recorded. Pin‐prick nociception was assessed using a sterile sharp‐tipped forceps. The stimulus intensity was first standardized on unaffected tissue, when available, and then applied to the affected area at 10 predefined points. The number of sites at which the patient reported comparable pain sensation was recorded. All CNT examinations were performed by a single examiner who was not involved in the surgical procedures. Subjective neurosensory function was evaluated using a visual analog scale (VAS). The VAS consisted of a 100‐mm horizontal line ranging from 0 (complete absence of sensation) to 100 (completely normal sensation).

Results from clinical neurosensory testing were used to determine whether there were any changes in the patient's response to the same test before and after the administration of PBMT. The same tests after treatment were also used to determine the degree of improvement. These tests were performed before the PBMT (Time 0), and immediately after treatment (Time 1), and VAS was repeated in patients who were followed up for at least 6 months after treatment. (Time 2). Follow‐up duration refers to the post‐treatment observation period after completion of PBMT, not the duration of ongoing therapy.

The other variables studied were demographic data (gender and age) and the affected side.

PBMT Biostimulation Protocol

Photobiomodulation therapy (PBMT) was performed using a Ga‐Al‐As diode laser device (EzLase 940, Biolase Technology Inc., Irvine, USA) at a wavelength of 940 nm in continuous wave mode. The irradiation was delivered using a 1 × 3 cm handpiece corresponding to a 3 cm2 application area.

The output power was set at 0.7 W and applied for 60 s per point, resulting in a total energy delivery of 42 J per point. Energy density (J/cm2) was calculated based on the delivered energy and the irradiated spot area as provided by the manufacturer. Based on the irradiation area, the calculated fluence (energy density) was 14 J/cm2. Irradiance (Power Density) was 0.23 W/cm2. The treatment parameters are summarized in Table 1.

During application, the laser handpiece was positioned perpendicular (90°) to the irradiation site to minimize beam divergence and reflection. For intraoral and extraoral regions where direct access was feasible, the handpiece was applied in gentle contact with the mucosal or skin surface.

In the retromolar region, where direct contact was not possible due to anatomical limitations, irradiation was performed at an approximate distance of 1 cm from the mucosal surface while maintaining perpendicular orientation.

Extraoral application was performed along the lower lip, chin tip, mental foramen area, and the course of the inferior alveolar nerve by placing the handpiece in contact with the cheek skin. Intraoral irradiation was applied buccally in the region of the mental foramen and molar apices, and lingually toward the mandibular foramen area [2, 26, 27].

Treatment sessions were performed twice weekly with 3‐day intervals.

Data Collection Methods

All collected data (patients' age and gender, as well as clinical variables before and after PBMT) were accessed from recorded patient follow‐up forms. Treatment time was defined as the time from IAN injury occurrence to the time of the start of treatment.

All neurosensory testing was conducted by a trained surgeon (H.A.) with over 5 years of clinical experience in neurosensory evaluation, ensuring reliable and consistent assessment throughout the study. All PBMT applications were performed by a single surgeon (Y.T.K.) who has formal training and experience in this field, ensuring consistency and standardization of the procedure across all patients.

In this study, one of the surgeons collected data from the patient follow‐up form and performed neurosensory tests. The same surgeon performed sensory tests on all patients. The PBMT was performed independently by another surgeon. The PBMT planning for each patient was made by two surgeons.

Data Analysis

SPSS for Windows 17.0 program was used for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were given as median, min‐max, and frequency values. Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests were used to determine whether the data conformed to normal distribution. During the data analysis, the Friedman–Wilcoxon test and Bonferroni correction were used for changes over more than two time periods, the Wilcoxon test was used for comparisons between two time periods, and χ 2 tests were used for other comparisons. The number of sessions and the reason for paraesthesia were influential factors, and the optional and dependent variables were evaluated separately with the stepwise multivariate linear regression analysis method regarding their cause‐and‐effect relationships with final VAS. Because some patients contributed bilateral sides, the dataset had a clustered structure. To account for within‐patient correlation, a linear mixed‐effects model with a random intercept for patient ID was applied. Final VAS (VAS 2) score was included as the dependent variable, while etiology and number of PBMT sessions were entered as fixed effects. In addition to categorical subgroup comparisons, the number of PBMT sessions was also entered as a continuous variable in the linear mixed‐effects model. Differences at p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant, and the critical p‐value was recalculated when necessary.

Results

This study initially included 59 registered patients with nerve injury who underwent PBMT. However, 2 patients with lingual nerve injury and 2 with IAN injury secondary to dental infection were excluded. Consequently, 55 patients (39 female, 16 males; mean age 35.69 ± 14.49 years, range 15–80) with IAN neurosensory disturbance following oral and maxillofacial surgical procedures were analyzed. Eleven patients had bilateral involvement, resulting in 66 affected sides. Neurosensory assessment and laser application were performed independently for each side; therefore, analyses were conducted at the affected‐side level. Discussion

In this retrospective study, we evaluated whether the clinical outcomes of PBMT after IAN injury varied according to injury etiology, treatment timing, and number of treatment sessions. Overall, PBMT was associated with improvement in neurosensory outcomes, although recovery appeared to differ according to the underlying cause of injury. In particular, patients with orthognathic surgery‐related injuries demonstrated less favorable outcomes, whereas treatment initiation within 1 month and the number of PBMT sessions were not significantly associated with final recovery outcomes.

It has been shown in some studies that PBMT has a positive effect on nerve recovery objectively and subjectively in persistent cases that do not respond to pharmacological and surgical treatments [28]. The PBMT is a non‐invasive method, making it possible to stimulate injured nerves without surgical intervention [29]. The use of PBMT has been shown to improve long‐term sensory abnormalities in the IAN after injury [30]. Although PBMT shows promise, it is not currently FDA‐approved for treating nerve injuries in the United States, which limits clinical adoption.

The irradiation parameters were selected in accordance with previously reported PBMT protocols for peripheral nerve regeneration, ensuring biologically effective fluence levels. In the present study, photobiomodulation therapy applied at a wavelength of 940 nm with an output power of 0.7 W and a fluence of 14 J/cm2 was associated with measurable neurosensory improvement in clinical assessments. Previous studies have reported heterogeneous PBMT protocols for peripheral nerve injury. Pol et al. [26] reported the use of wavelengths between 904 and 910 nm with an output power of 0.5 W and a markedly higher fluence of 244.8 J/cm2, suggesting potential benefits in neurosensory recovery. Similarly, Ozen et al. [31] applied PBMT at 820–830 nm using a lower output power (0.05 W) and a fluence of 22.5 J/cm2, indicating possible improvement in sensory nerve injury. In contrast, Miloro and Criddle [2] employed a wavelength of 830 nm, an output power of 0.4 W, and a fluence of 20 J/cm2, but did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference compared with placebo. These variations in wavelength, power output, and fluence across studies highlight the lack of standardized dosimetric parameters in PBMT research. The fluence applied in the present study (14 J/cm2) falls within the commonly reported therapeutic window for neural photobiomodulation and was selected based on previously described biologically effective ranges. Nevertheless, the heterogeneity of protocols in the literature underscores the need for controlled dose–response studies to determine optimal PBMT parameters for peripheral nerve regeneration.

Timing of treatment initiation is considered a critical factor in PBMT protocols for peripheral nerve injury. Brugnera et al. [1] stratified patients into early (2–15 days) and delayed (30–365 days) treatment groups in cases of inferior alveolar and mental nerve injury following surgical intervention. Similarly, Miloro et al. [2] applied PBMT in patients treated between 3 and 12 months and beyond 12 months after nerve injury, reflecting variability in therapeutic timing across studies. In routine clinical management of inferior alveolar nerve injury, sensory function is typically monitored during the first month following injury, after which patients are reassessed for potential microneurosurgical intervention if recovery is inadequate [32, 33]. Based on this clinical framework and the biological rationale that early intervention may precede neuroma formation and progressive fibrotic changes, patients in the present study were stratified into two groups: those who initiated PBMT within 1 month of injury and those who began treatment after 1 month.

Neurosensory tests were performed to detect nerve injury, identify sensory injury, and assess the extent and extent of damage. Tests used in the literature for the detection and follow‐up of IAN injury after oral surgery include [34, 35]; VAS, pinprick test, light touch, two‐point discrimination, sharp‐blunt test, and electronic pain response test (ZGK‐1 electrometer). In this study, clinical neurosensory tests (two‐point discrimination test, pinprick test, and light touch test) and visual analog scale (VAS) were used together to evaluate the effects of PBMT on clinical parameters between patient groups. With the data obtained, it was aimed to determine the ideal PBMT procedure.

Seddon and Sunderland classification systems are widely utilized frameworks for grading peripheral nerve injuries based on anatomical and structural criteria [1, 36]. However, due to the retrospective design and lack of uniform intraoperative or electrophysiological data, these classification systems could not be applied. Therefore, functional neurosensory evaluation using validated clinical neurosensory tests (CNTs) was adopted as a pragmatic and clinically relevant assessment method [2, 25]. Additionally, IAN injuries in this cohort resulted from different maxillofacial surgical procedures, which may involve distinct mechanisms and severities of nerve damage. Such variability in surgical etiology, injury mechanism, and baseline severity may have influenced recovery trajectories independently of PBMT and therefore represents an important methodological limitation when interpreting the findings. However, the number of cases within individual surgical subgroups was limited, which precluded meaningful additional stratification to create more homogeneous groups. This limitation further restricts the ability to compare outcomes across patients and may have influenced the observed variability in recovery patterns.

In a study of 173 patients by Avendano et al. [37], it was observed that the incidence of complications in terms of IAN injury after extraction was higher in women than in men. In another study by Pogrel and Thamby [38], it was reported that dental neuropathic pain after surgery affected women more than men. In this study, similar to previously published studies, the number of female patients (%70.90) was more than that of male patients.

The age of the patients is also considered to be one of the determining factors. August et al. [39] found that patients' complaints regarding IAN function increased in direct proportion to age and reached the highest levels in procedures performed on patients over the age of 40. Macintosh [24] reported in his study that permanent damage is more common in people over the age of 40. In this study, IAN injury was detected in 40 affected sides under 35 years of age and in 26 affected sides aged 35 years and older.

Several studies in the literature have evaluated the potential role of PBMT in nerve healing. Previous reports have suggested favorable outcomes in patients with IAN injury following orthognathic surgery [14, 23, 40], third molar surgery [4], implant surgery [41], and other maxillofacial surgeries [8, 30, 42, 43, 44, 45]. Pol et al. [26] reported favorable neurosensory outcomes following superpulsed low‐level laser therapy in patients with inferior alveolar nerve paresthesia. Their findings provide additional support for the potential neuromodulatory effects of PBMT, including modulation of inflammation, enhancement of local microcirculation, and promotion of neural repair processes. This may partly explain the clinical improvements observed in the present study. However, given the retrospective design and lack of a control group, these associations should be interpreted cautiously [26]. However, Oliveira et al. [46] compared a control group with laser and electroacupuncture groups and reported no significant difference between the laser and control groups, whereas electroacupuncture was associated with faster recovery of neural sensitivity. On the other hand, in this study, when clinical neurosensory tests and VAS scores were evaluated before and after the PBMT, it was observed that there was a statistically significant improvement in 55 patients, and their complaints significantly decreased (p < 0.05). Besides, in 14 patients, it was observed that their symptoms completely disappeared, and their sensations returned to normal. However, given the absence of a control group, the observed improvements should be interpreted cautiously, as spontaneous nerve recovery may have contributed to the outcomes and and a causal relationship between PBMT and recovery cannot be established.

Several studies have examined the influence of treatment timing on inferior alveolar nerve recovery. Brugnera et al. [1] reported a 72.7% improvement rate in patients who initiated PBMT within 1 month, compared with 27.7% in those who began treatment between 1 month and 1 year after injury. In contrast, Miloro et al. [47] did not observe a statistically significant difference between patients treated within 3–12 months and those treated more than 12 months after nerve injury. In the present study, 31 affected sides initiated PBMT within 1 month of injury, whereas 35 affected sides began treatment after 1 month. Although no statistically significant difference was detected between the two groups in terms of neurosensory improvement (p > 0.05), patients treated within the first month demonstrated numerically higher clinical test scores. The lack of statistical significance may be partially attributable to limited statistical power in subgroup analyses. These findings suggest a possible influence of treatment timing; however, definitive conclusions cannot be drawn without larger, controlled studies. Although the overall sample size was acceptable for a single‐center study of this condition, subgroup analyses were limited by relatively small sample sizes, which may have reduced statistical power and contributed to nonsignificant findings in certain variables, such as treatment timing. Therefore, subgroup results should be interpreted cautiously and validated in larger, preferably multicenter studies.

Following injury to the inferior alveolar nerve, traumatic neuroma formation may begin within the first 30 days. Animal studies indicate that neuroma‐like tissue may appear 2 weeks after nerve transection [48]. Furthermore, clinical series on trigeminal and mandibular nerve injuries consistently show that delays in repair are associated with impaired sensory recovery [49]. Therefore, adopting a purely observational “wait and see” strategy lasting more than 30 days may not only reduce the likelihood of meaningful sensory recovery but also increase ethical concerns by exposing the patient to the risk of permanent neuropathic sequelae, such as pain associated with neuroma. Instead of passive observation, early clinical intervention may be considered; however, the specific contribution of PBMT to recovery remains uncertain.

Studies have been reported in the literature that applied PBMT with a different number of sessions [2, 14, 50]. For instance, Favaro‐pipi et al. [50] reported that recovery was faster when a single session of PBMT was applied compared to the control group. In addition, Faria et al. [14] evaluated orthognathic patients who underwent 10 sessions of the PBMT compared to the control group and reported better IAN healing. On the other hand, Miloro et al. [2] studied six patients with seven sessions of PBMT. They reported a significant acceleration in the magnitude of neurosensory return and in the time of return compared to patients who did not receive PBMT after orthognathic surgery. Unfortunately, there are not enough studies comparing the number of sessions. In the present study, 35 affected sides received PBMT for 6–15 sessions, whereas 31 affected sides received more than 15 sessions. Although clinical improvement was observed in both groups, no statistically significant difference was found between the groups regarding neurosensory recovery (p > 0.05). Furthermore, multivariate linear regression analysis demonstrated that the number of sessions was not an independent predictor of final VAS scores after adjustment for etiology. These findings suggest that increasing the number of treatment sessions alone does not necessarily enhance therapeutic efficacy.

Several studies have reported favorable outcomes with PBMT for IAN injury following orthognathic surgery [14, 40, 47, 51], and other surgical procedures [8, 44, 52, 53]. In the present study, recovery, reflected by final VAS scores, was significantly lower in patients who underwent orthognathic surgery compared with those treated for other surgical procedures (p < 0.05). This association remained significant in the linear mixed‐effects model analysis. These findings suggest that neurosensory recovery may be less pronounced in orthognathic surgery–related IAN injuries compared with injuries associated with wisdom tooth extraction, implant surgery, cyst enucleation, or local anesthesia injection

The differences observed in recovery patterns may be partially explained by the underlying mechanism of nerve injury. In orthognathic surgery, inferior alveolar nerve impairment is frequently associated with traction, stretching, or manipulation during osteotomy and mandibular repositioning. Such forces may result in neurapraxia or varying degrees of axonotmesis, potentially affecting a broader segment of the nerve. In contrast, nerve injuries related to implant surgery are more commonly compression‐ or contact‐induced, arising from direct mechanical pressure, cortical perforation, or implant proximity to the mandibular canal.

Compression‐type injuries may compromise intraneural microcirculation and induce localized ischemic changes, whereas traction‐related injuries may involve mechanical distortion and disruption of axonal transport mechanisms. These distinct pathophysiological processes can influence the extent of neural degeneration, inflammatory response, and regenerative dynamics. Consequently, variations in injury mechanism may contribute to differences in clinical recovery patterns and potentially to differential responsiveness to photobiomodulation therapy.

In the literature, studies evaluate the effect of gender and age on IAN injury and regeneration [24, 39]. There are not enough studies examining the effect of PBMT on demographic data. This study recorded data from 39 female and 16 male patients. It was observed that gender did not make a significant difference in terms of recovery after PBMT. When we evaluated the patients according to their age in this study, no statistically significant difference was found between patients aged 35 years and older and patients under 35 years of age (p > 0.05). Since the regeneration capacity is higher in younger patients, we expected a better recovery in patients under 35 years of age, but the effect was not sufficient to make a statistical difference. The low number of elderly patients may have caused this result.

From a practical perspective, PBMT may be considered an early adjunctive, non‐invasive option for patients with persistent IAN neurosensory disturbance, particularly when initiated within the first month after injury. However, the present findings do not indicate a clear additional benefit from increasing the number of treatment sessions alone. Patients with orthognathic surgery‐related injuries may demonstrate less favorable recovery and may therefore require closer follow‐up or consideration of alternative management strategies.

Conclusion

Photobiomodulation therapy (PBMT) appears to be associated with improvements in neurosensory function in patients with inferior alveolar nerve (IAN) injury, as reflected in both clinical neurosensory tests and VAS scores. Etiology was identified as a significant factor, with orthognathic surgery–related injuries demonstrating poorer recovery, while treatment timing and session number were not independent predictors, although earlier initiation showed a tendency toward better outcomes.

However, the absence of a control group limits causal interpretation, and spontaneous nerve recovery may have contributed to the observed improvements. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as associative, and a causal relationship between PBMT and recovery cannot be established.

Additional limitations include the relatively small sample size, the use of subjective assessment tools, and the analysis of bilateral cases as independent observations. Furthermore, the use of a single fluence value precludes conclusions regarding dose–response relationships, and variability in PBMT protocols across studies remains a significant methodological challenge.

Future well‐designed randomized controlled trials with standardized dosimetric parameters, multiple fluence protocols, and objective outcome measures are required to better clarify the potential role and optimal application of PBMT in IAN injuries.
