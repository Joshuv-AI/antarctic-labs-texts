CD4+ T-cell immunity to Bordetella pertussis
Authors: Jesse M Hall, Justin B Defalco, Purnima Dubey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite widespread immunization, Bordetella pertussis (B. pertussis) cases are resurging worldwide, prompting renewed interest in understanding the key mechanisms that underlie protective immunity. Similar to natural infection, immunization with whole-cell pertussis vaccines (wPV) generates long-lasting TH1/TH17 CD4 + T-cell responses that protect against B. pertussis infection. Additionally, wPV immunization generates robust tissue-resident memory T-cell responses (TRM) that limit both pertussis disease and transmission. In contrast, acellular pertussis vaccines (aPV) generate short-lived TH2-polarized immune responses that confer protection against severe disease but do not prevent B. pertussis colonization or transmission. Furthermore, the circulation of clinical strains with varied expression of aPV antigens also highlights the need for next-generation pertussis vaccines. Thus, coordinated innate and adaptive immune responses at the site of infection are required to effectively protect against B. pertussis colonization and persistence. This review integrates our current knowledge of natural and vaccine mediated T-cell immunity from both animal models and human studies to guide the development of improved pertussis vaccines capable of combating the resurgence of pertussis.Keywords: Bordetella pertussis, Whooping cough, T-cells, Adaptive immunity, Vaccines

Introduction

Bordetella pertussis (B. pertussis), the causative agent of whooping cough or pertussis, remains one of the most prevalent vaccine preventable diseases around the world. In 2024, approximately 89% of infants globally received at least one dose of diphtheria, tetanus, and pertussis containing vaccine, while 85% completed at least three immunizations [1]. Despite widespread vaccine coverage, B. pertussis continues to circulate with periodic outbreaks in immunized populations. This increased incidence became evident after the COVID-19 pandemic, with a substantial increase in global reported pertussis cases [2, 3]. There were 941,565 confirmed pertussis cases worldwide in 2024 [4]. This is a drastic increase from the 163,388 confirmed pertussis cases in 2023 [4].

Whole-cell pertussis vaccines (wPV) were introduced in the 1950s. Due to concerns over vaccine reactogenicity, in the late 1990 s many countries replaced wPV with an acellular protein-subunit pertussis vaccine (aPV) composed of B. pertussis virulence factors [5]. Since the implementation of aPV, cyclic increases in the number of pertussis cases have been observed worldwide. These trends suggest that current aPV protects against disease, likely through the induction of anti-PT antibodies [6, 7]. However, data from mice and nonhuman primate animal models show that aPV immunization fails to prevent nasal colonization, thereby permitting subsequent transmission of B. pertussis [8–11].

Multiple factors have contributed to the current resurgence of pertussis. Although B. pertussis was long considered a monomorphic pathogen [12], global surveillance has revealed ongoing genetic diversification. Recent clinical isolates frequently lack key virulence factors, including filamentous hemagglutinin (FHA), pertactin (Prn), and pertussis toxin (PT) [13–16]. These antigens are components of the current aPV, suggesting that vaccine-driven selection may contribute to these changes. Additionally, clinical isolates contain distinct single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and chromosomal rearrangements, although the functional implications of these alterations remain poorly understood [17]. Epidemiological data indicate that aPV-mediated protective immunity can decline substantially within four years after booster administration [18, 19], necessitating repeated booster doses. These findings raise significant concerns as aPV remains the predominant immunization strategy for expanding birth cohorts in many parts of the world. Improved surveillance and more sensitive diagnostics have increased pertussis case detection [20–22] and contribute to the increases in yearly cases. In addition, asymptomatic transmission may also contribute to the continued B. pertussis exposure in humans, especially as vaccine efficacy wanes over time [23]. Variable uptake of booster vaccines across all age groups along with uneven healthcare access may also contribute to pertussis resurgence [20].

It is now recognized that CD4 + T-cells are the central orchestrators of protective adaptive immune responses against B. pertussis [24–26], while CD8 + effector T-cells are not required for clearing B. pertussis from the respiratory tract [24, 26]. CD4 + T-cell responses differ substantially depending on whether immunity is induced through natural infection, aPV or wPV immunization [10, 27–29], and determines whether the bacteria will be cleared from the respiratory tract or will continue to persist and serve as a reservoir for transmission. These differing immune responses profoundly influence the functional outcome of B. pertussis infection with implications for future next-generation pertussis vaccine design.

Thus, there is an urgent need for next-generation pertussis vaccines that prevent B. pertussis nasal colonization and transmission. In this review, we synthesize the current understanding of CD4 + T-cell responses to B. pertussis with a focus on mucosal CD4 + T-cell responses and their implication for next-generation pertussis vaccine design.

CD4+ T-cell responses generated by B. pertussis infection and immunization

Natural infection elicits CD4+ T helper cell responses that are polarized towards TH1 and TH17 phenotypes [24, 30]. TH1 differentiation during B. pertussis infection is driven in part by IL-12 production from airway macrophages that are an early response to the infection [31], and are activated following the recognition of bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [31]. IL-12 also stimulates IFNɣ production, particularly from natural killer (NK) cells early during infection [32, 33], which further reinforces TH1 polarization. In mice, CD4 + T-cells are recruited to the lungs by day 7 post-challenge with B. pertussis [28]. IFNɣ produced by TH1 cells in turn polarizes murine airway macrophages towards the M1/classical state [34], which kill B. pertussis via nitric oxide dependent and independent mechanisms [35, 36]. Depletion of airway macrophages increased B. pertussis burden in the lungs highlighting the protective role of macrophages [37]. In parallel, B. pertussis can actively modulate macrophage function through the release of PT and adenylate cyclase toxin (ACT), which can alter macrophage pro-inflammatory gene expression via undefined inhibitory mechanisms [37, 38]. Beyond macrophage activation, IFNɣ stimulates the production of chemokines CXCL9 and CXCL10 by respiratory epithelial cells, which recruit leukocytes to the sites of infection [32]. IFNɣ is required for efficient clearance of B. pertussis in the lung [27], and IFNɣ dependent signaling pathways play a crucial role in preventing bacterial dissemination and severe pertussis disease [25, 39]. Furthermore, TH1 cell responses are critical for sustained immunity against pertussis, as B. pertussis challenged mice deficient in IFNɣ exhibit increased B. pertussis burden compared to wild-type mice [25].

TH17-mediated immune responses play a crucial role in pathogen clearance at mucosal barrier sites [40–45]. B. pertussis infection recruits T-cells to the nasal cavity that produce IL-17, and IL-17 induced responses are essential for clearing a B. pertussis infection from the nasal cavity [27]. B. pertussis components such as lipooligosaccharide (LOS), ACT and PT induce production of the TH17 polarizing cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, IL-21, and IL-23 by innate immune cells [30, 46–48] like dendritic cells [49] and macrophages [33]. Studies in mice and baboons showed that B. pertussis infection elicits IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-23 in the nasopharynx [50]. Mice deficient in either IL-6 [51] or IL-1β [52] exhibit impaired B. pertussis clearance underscoring the protective role of these TH17-polarizing cytokines during infection. Loss of IL-17 prevents B. pertussis clearance from the nose [53, 54], highlighting the importance of IL-17 mediated immunity in controlling B. pertussis colonization of the upper respiratory tract. TH17 cells promote the upregulation of effector chemokines CXCL1 and CXCL2 [53] as well as GCSF, IL-8, MCP-1, and MIP-1α [50] in nasopharyngeal cells, and leading to the recruitment of monocytes and neutrophils [53]. Siglec-F+ neutrophils are enriched in the nasal cavity and exhibit effector functions, including neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, reactive oxygen species production, and phagocytosis [53, 55, 56]. Consistent with their protective role, neutrophil depletion impairs B. pertussis clearance in convalescent mice [53]. B. pertussis virulence factors such as PT and ACT counter neutrophil activity through the accumulation of intracellular cAMP, inhibiting oxidative burst, preventing NET formation, and phagocytosis [57–59]. The importance of TH17 differentiation was also suggested by baboon studies, where stimulation of baboon PBMCs with heat-killed B. pertussis induced IL-6, IL-23, and IL-1β suggesting that these cytokines are key drivers in the innate immune responses associated with TH17 differentiation in this large animal model [50].

IL-17 elicits the production of key protective mechanisms in the mucosa by immune and epithelial cells. These include antimicrobial peptides like defensins and cathelicidins, which play a bactericidal role in protecting against pathogen infection [60, 61]. Work in human and animal models showed that B. pertussis infection upregulates levels of LL-37 and β-defensins that contribute to antimicrobial activity against B. pertussis [62–64]. Epithelial cell expression of mucin genes MUC2 and MUC5AC increased after B. pertussis infection [65]. Iron deprivation in B. pertussis enhances bacterial mucin binding, making bacterial cells more sensitive to mucin inhibition [66].

T-cell responses in the respiratory mucosa

While both systemic and mucosal CD4 + T-cell responses contribute to clearing a B. pertussis infection from the respiratory tract, increasing evidence points to the critical importance of tissue-resident memory T (TRM) cells in sustained protection against B. pertussis. Mouse models have been instrumental in defining the importance of tissue-resident CD4 + T cells. TRM are long-lived cells that are generated by natural infection and reside in tissues and do not circulate through the blood. Their presence within the respiratory tract provides a rapid response to reinfection and they serve as a crucial first line defense against pathogens [67]. In mouse models, CD4 + TRMs are detected in the lungs as early as 1–2 weeks post-infection and continue to colonize the lungs and nose after the bacterial load is eliminated [68, 69]. Human studies show that TRM populations in the lungs are replenished and maintained throughout aging via seeding by circulating T cells [70, 71], emphasizing the importance of this first line memory response. CD4 + TRMs produce primarily IFNɣ in the lungs [53, 68]. In parallel, CD4 + TRMs develop in the nose and produce primarily IL-17 [53]. TRMs are detected in the nose and lungs as long as seven months after the bacterial challenge is cleared from the respiratory tract [10]. Following reinfection, TRM cells rapidly expand locally [10], and are essential for decreasing B. pertussis burden [53, 68] from the lungs and the nose. The importance of TRM was demonstrated by treatment of convalescent mice with the sphingosine-1 phosphate receptor antagonist FTY720 prior to rechallenge. FTY720 retains circulating cells in the lymph nodes and prevents them from localizing to the infection site [72]. FTY720 treated mice showed an expansion of mucosal TRM cells and reduced B. pertussis bacterial load in the lungs, suggesting that tissue-resident immune cells alone can eliminate the bacteria [68]. Furthermore, adoptive transfer of TRM cells from convalescent mice into naïve irradiated mice decreased B. pertussis burden in the recipient challenge mice [68].

wPV consists of B. pertussis that is chemically inactivated by formalin and combined with diphtheria and tetanus toxoids to develop the DTP vaccine [5]. Intramuscular immunization with wPV induces TH1/TH17 polarized systemic immune responses [10, 28] that are drawn to the respiratory mucosa following B. pertussis challenge (Fig. 1). CD4+IFNɣ TRM cells are established in the lungs and CD4 + IL-17 + TRM are established in the nose. These TRMs are key to decreasing B. pertussis burden from the respiratory tract [10, 73], and provide strong and rapid protection against re-infection. Importantly, as with natural infection, B. pertussis bacterial load in wPV-immunized mice is reduced to the limit of detection in the nose. In addition, studies in the baboon model showed that wPV-immunized animals do not transmit the infection [8].

Immune response generated from wPV and aPV immunization. In wPV immunization, inactivated B. pertussis cells are taken up by dendritic cells, where bacterial components drive the induction of IL-6, IL-12, and IL-23 produced by innate immune cells. These cytokines drive differentiation of B. pertussis-specific T-cells into TH1 and TH17. IFNɣ and TNFα, produced by TH1 cells, activate and polarize M1 macrophages and promote IgG1 and IgG4 class switching in humans. IL-17 and IL-22, produced by TH17 cells recruit neutrophils, especially Siglec-F+ neutrophils, induce the production of antimicrobial peptides and mucin, and promote IgA class switching. DCs uptake aPV vaccine antigens while alum adjuvant activates the inflammasome. Together this promotes strong TH2 differentiation through IL-4 and weaker TH1 differentiation that produces IFNɣ. IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 production by TH2 cells promotes IgG1 and IgG3 class switching in humans. Additionally, the induction of antigen-specific T regulatory cells results in IL-10 production. Most of the mechanistic studies that contributed to our understanding of B. pertussis immunology were conducted in murine models. While these studies demonstrate the importance of both systemic immunity and protection at barrier sites that provide rapid responses to infection, it is important to confirm the importance of these findings in humans. The use of human mucosal tissues such as tonsils will be instrumental in validating animal studies in people.

In contrast to wPV, aPV immunization induces robust systemic TH2 T-cell responses (Fig. 1) [74]. TH2 differentiation is driven by IL-4 [75, 76] and drives IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 production by T-cells [75]. IL-4 also promotes IgG class switching in B cells [77, 78]. While both wPV and aPV generate strong IgG responses [79], studies have shown that children vaccinated with acellular alum-adsorbed pertussis toxoid show mainly IgG1 and IgG4 against PT, while natural infection and wPV immunization elicited IgG1 and IgG3 [79, 80]. While immunization with the acellular pertussis vaccine promotes antibody-mediated immunity [81], non-human primate and human studies showed that antibody titers and protective immunity wane more rapidly compared to wPV immunization [79, 82, 83]. One potential explanation is the difference in generation and maintenance of follicular T helper cells (TFH) responses after vaccination. Mice immunized with wPV generated more TFH and higher levels of CXCL13, than aPV immunization, and persisted longer in the lymph nodes. thereby supporting B cell recruitment to the follicles [84]. Conversely, aPV immunization produced robust anti-B. pertussis, anti-FHA, and anti-PT IgG antibodies that remained elevated 532 days post-boost, but TFH populations in the lymph nodes of aPV-immunized mice reduced drastically 60 days post-immunization [84]. In humans, T-cell responses induced following wPV immunization are more durable compared to aPV immunization [85]. Further studies in humans are needed to critically assess the longevity and effector function of CD4 + TRM cells following immunization. Additional studies are also needed to determine whether the magnitude, specificity and longevity of CD4 + T-cell responses can serve as correlates of protection against pertussis.

TREG cells induced by B. pertussis infection and vaccination

T cell effector function may be attenuated by regulatory T cells (TREG) in the lymph nodes and tissues. aPV immunization induces antigen-specific CD4 + TREG cells that produce IL-10, IFNɣ, and IL‐5 and suppress IL‐17 production by TH17 cells [86]. These CD4 + TREG cells expressed the transcription factor Foxp3 and markers CD49b, PD-1 and Lag3 [87]. Furthermore, aPV immunization also induced IL-10 expressing CD8 + TREG cells [87] that suppressed the activation of both TH1 and TH17 T-cells [87]. Blocking IL-10 following aPV immunization resulted in increased CD4 + TRMs expressing IL-17 [87]. These results suggest that the immune response generated by aPV is suboptimal and far less durable and may be the underlying cause of the inability of aPV to prevent B. pertussis colonization in the nasal cavity.

Emerging strategies to improve pertussis vaccines

In response to pertussis resurgence, current research efforts are directed towards creating improved pertussis vaccines that retain the safety of current aPV and overcome its limitations. The optimal vaccine formulation should protect against disease and reduce B. pertussis colonization of the upper respiratory tract. Active areas of investigation include identification of novel vaccine antigens, novel adjuvants that elicit TH1/TH17 polarized responses and immunization routes that generate tissue-resident memory T-cell responses.

Antigen discovery and strain variation

Current work focuses on improving the antigen composition of the aPV by selecting antigens that are highly conserved across diverse clinical isolates and are less susceptible to antigenic modulation. Prn-deficient strains continue to increase in circulation in countries that use aPV [88–91]. These strains have also been isolated in countries where wPV vaccination remains the standard of care [92, 93]. Thus inclusion of conserved vaccine antigens may prevent antigenic modulation and thereby improve vaccine mediated protection. Identification of novel antigens has been explored by whole-genome screening methods in animal models and in humans. A Tnseq screen in a nonhuman primate model identified antigens that are both immunogenic and essential for B. pertussis survival in vitro and in vivo [94]. Whole-genome mapping of B. pertussis specific CD4 + T-cell responses identified multiple potential vaccine antigens that were recognized by T-cells from humans immunized with wPV [95]. This study also showed that T cells from aPV immunized individuals responded to some of the novel antigens, suggesting that these individuals may have been exposed to B. pertussis. These findings suggest that asymptomatic infections contribute to shaping T-cell responses [95]. These data also suggest that wPV-immunized individuals may mount stronger responses to antigens encountered during vaccination and subsequent infection than aPV-immunized individuals.

In another study, in silico predictions identified some potential vaccine antigens that were recognized by memory T-cells from people immunized with wPV [96]. A reverse vaccinology approach also identified potential B. pertussis vaccine candidates [97]. These studies suggest that B. pertussis presents a large pool of potential antigens to the immune system. Inclusion of these antigens in next-generation vaccines may improve protection against circulating isolates and thereby decrease transmission of B. pertussis.

Novel adjuvants and mucosal delivery strategies

Adjuvants that elicit mucosal humoral and cellular responses will be a key component of novel subunit vaccines. Current efforts are focused on developing adjuvants that can polarize immune responses towards the desired TH1 and TH17 phenotypes. For example, an acellular formulation that used the TLR2 agonist lipoprotein LP1569 as an adjuvant induced TH1/TH17 mediated immune responses that decreased B. pertussis burden in the lower respiratory tract [98]. Formulations containing TH1/TH17 polarizing adjuvants are being tested by immunization routes that generate immune responses at the site of infection. We showed that adding the TLR4 agonist Bordetella colonization factor A (BcfA) [99] to an approved aPV decreased B. pertussis burden in the lungs and nasal cavity compared to aPV alone [100]. The vaccine was delivered by intramuscular priming followed by an intranasal booster. The decrease in bacterial burden was associated with an increase in TH1/TH17 CD4 + TRM cells in the upper and lower respiratory tract and an overall decrease in TH2 responses [100, 101]. The TLR9 agonist CpG ODN combined with alum and aPV vaccine antigens elicited TH1 immune responses and decreased B. pertussis burden in the lung [102]. A vaccine containing aPV antigens FHA, PT and Prn adjuvanted with LP1569 and the STING agonist c-d-GMP, delivered intranasally, decreased bacterial burden in the lungs and the nose [103]. Intranasal immunization with a vaccine where the adjuvant curdlan was added to an approved aPV decreased B. pertussis burden in the mouse respiratory tract [104]. aPV antigens combined with the OMV-based adjuvant T-vant delivered intranasally, generated a TH1/TH17 polarized immune response and cleared B. pertussis burden in both the lungs and nasopharynx [105]. An experimental acellular pertussis vaccine containing LP-GMP as the adjuvant along with antigens PRN, FHA and PT delivered intranasally, generated effector TH17 responses in the nose and decreased B. pertussis burden following challenge [87, 103]. Together these studies suggest that an acellular vaccine formulation that can generate a TH1/TH17 polarized immune response systemically and in the mucosa may elicit sustained immune responses that improve bacterial clearance [86, 106, 107]. Mucosal immunization strategies provide a platform for local delivery of both antigens and adjuvants, thus increasing protective immune responses at the site of infection. Together these strategies will generate sustained tissue-resident immunity and protection in the entire respiratory tract.

Next generation whole cell vaccines

Efforts are also underway to create a whole-cell vaccine that provides sustained protection without reactogenicity. The live-attenuated pertussis vaccine BPZE1 has been shown to elicit immune responses that closely resemble those induced by the reference wPV [108]. Intranasal BPZE1 immunization induced robust antibody titers and TH1/TH17 polarized cellular immune responses [108, 109]. In mice, protection was still observed after one year post immunization, suggesting an increase in the duration of immunity [108]. This observation was reinforced by baboon studies which also showed that intranasal delivery of BPZE1 reduced nasal colonization [8], highlighting the importance of mucosal vaccine delivery. The vaccine is safe [110] and ongoing clinical trials are evaluating immune responses and protective efficacy of this next-generation vaccine.

Summary and concluding remarks

Collectively, these studies highlight the need to move beyond traditional aPV vaccine formulations toward new strategies that elicit durable mucosal immunity, especially long-lasting TH1/TH17 TRM responses. The combination of conserved antigens, TH1/TH17 driving adjuvants and mucosal immunization delivery strategies provides the groundwork for future pertussis vaccines that address the limitations of current aPV. These vaccines must be safe and prevent severe disease, and most importantly, decrease nasal colonization and the likelihood of transmission. Such vaccines will provide protection for individuals across the age spectrum. Immunization of pregnant women with the aPV protects neonates prior to vaccination [111–115], and improved maternal vaccines will increase protection for this vulnerable population.

Experimental pertussis vaccines are tested primarily in mouse models as the high cost of baboon studies precludes testing of all vaccines in this large animal model. As in vitro and ex vivo models are developed to recapitulate and predict human responses to vaccines and infections [116, 117] validation of murine studies in these systems will further accelerate the development of next generation vaccines that prevent B. pertussis persistence and transmission.

Beyond immunogenicity, several critical considerations influence the development and deployment of intranasal vaccines, including the rigorous evaluation of safety following mucosal delivery, the feasibility and cost-effectiveness of large-scale production and distribution of novel formulations, and regulatory challenges associated with the approval of mucosal subunit vaccines.
