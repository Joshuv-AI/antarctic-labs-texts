Comparison of thyroid-specific quality of life after thermal ablation vs. thyroid lobectomy for benign thyroid nodules
Authors: Yan Li, Shengnan Huo, Ying Wei, Zhenlong Zhao, Lili Peng, Jie Wu, ShiLiang Cao, Na Yu, Wenjia Cai, Song Li, Shuqi Li, Ming-an Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo compare the thyroid-specific quality of life (QoL) in patients with benign thyroid nodules (BTNs) who underwent thermal ablation (TA) versus thyroid lobectomy (TL).MethodsPatients with BTNs who underwent ultrasound-guided TA or TL were enrolled in the present study. The Thyroid Cancer-Specific Quality of Life Questionnaire (THYCA-QoL) which focus on thyroid-specific symptoms that are also relevant to patients with BTNs was used to evaluate patients’ thyroid-specific QoL. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to control for confounding variables. Group differences in the THYCA-QoL scores were analyzed using t- tests.ResultsAfter PSM, 82 patients were included in each group. The median follow-up time for questionnaire administration was 3 (3, 3) months in the TA group and 6 (4, 8) months in the TL group. Compared with TL, the TA group reported significantly lower scores in voice, concentration, scar, tingling hands/feet, and weight gain (all p < 0.05), suggesting better thyroid-specific QoL. However, the TA group had higher scores on the “less interest in sex” scale, indicating more sexual interest-related concerns.ConclusionTA was associated with better thyroid-specific QoL than TL in several domains, though sexual interest-related concerns were slightly higher. These results suggest it may be a feasible alternative for patients with BTNs, with further studies needed to confirm long-term outcomes.Keywords: Benign thyroid nodules, Thyroid-specific quality of life, Thermal ablation, Thyroid lobectomy, Microwave ablation, Radiofrequency ablation

Introduction

Thyroid nodules are highly prevalent worldwide. The prevalence of thyroid nodules detected with ultrasound can be as high as 68% [1], of which over 90% are benign [2]. Thyroid lobectomy (TL) has been regarded as the conventional therapy for benign thyroid nodules (BTNs) [3, 4]. However, TL is inevitably traumatic, and often results in complications such as hypothyroidism, possible hoarseness, and scarring, which can negatively influence quality of life (QoL) [5]. In contrast, thermal ablation (TA) methods, including microwave ablation (MWA) and radiofrequency ablation (RFA), have recently gained acceptance as less invasive treatment options for BTNs. Previous studies have demonstrated that TA achieves comparable efficacy and safety with a lower complication rate [6–8].

QoL has become an important endpoint factor and a strong predictor of survival in evaluating the success of medical treatments, reflecting a shift in focus from survival to overall well-being [9–11]. BTNs are nonlife-threatening and generally have a favorable prognosis [2], previous studies have reported patients with BTNs maintain good overall QoL [12, 13]. Nevertheless, thyroid-specific symptoms such as voice changes, scar discomfort, tingling in the hands and feet, weight fluctuations, and impaired concentration can still significantly affect daily functioning [14].

To address this, the present study focused on thyroid-specific symptoms as the primary outcomes, aiming to compare thyroid-specific QoL between TA and TL in patients with BTNs.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and the principles of Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of China-Japan Friendship Hospital (No. 2023-KY-250). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Clinical trial registration: not applicable.

Eligible populations were patients with BTNs who underwent either ultrasound-guided TA or TL. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who had confirmed BTNs by pathology; (2) underwent TA (MWA or RFA) or TL; (3) were followed for more than 3 months; (4) had no major complications; and (5) were able to understand and complete the survey. The exclusion criteria were (1) serious primary diseases of the liver, kidney, hematopoietic, or endocrine system and (2) a history of psychiatric illness, personality disorders, cognitive impairments, or organic brain disease.

TA and TL procedure

MWA and RFA were conducted under ultrasound guidance with the hydrodissection technique based on fascial spaces surrounding the thyroid lobe. Continuous injection was applied as needed to maintain a separation distance of at least 0.5 cm [15, 16]. The operator was positioned at the patient’s head side, and normal saline was used for MWA while sterile distilled water was used for RFA. Representative images of the procedure are shown in Fig. 1.

The surgical strategy for TL followed the American Thyroid Association Management guidelines [3].

Measures

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The sociodemographic and clinical information was obtained by reviewing the hospital’s electronic medical record system. Data collected included age, sex, marital status, and educational background, as well as family history of thyroid cancer. Additional variables such as type of medical insurance, occupational status, and place of residence were also extracted.

Thyroid cancer-specific quality of life questionnaire (THYCA-QoL)

In this study, thyroid-related symptoms were assessed using the Thyroid Cancer-Specific Quality of Life Questionnaire (THYCA-QoL). This standardized instrument was originally developed for thyroid cancer survivors by Husson [17], and has been widely applied in subsequent clinical studies. The questionnaire comprises 24 items, including 7 symptom scales (neuromuscular, voice, concentration, sympathetic, throat/mouth, psychological and sensory problems) and 6 single items (problems with scar, felt chilly, tingling hands/feet, gained weight, headaches, interest in sex). Higher scores indicate greater symptom burden.

Although the THYCA-QoL questionnaire was originally developed for thyroid cancer survivors, its items focus on thyroid-specific symptoms that are also relevant to patients with BTNs. Given the absence of a validated thyroid-specific QoL instrument for benign thyroid conditions, THYCA-QoL was applicable as the most appropriate available tool for capturing these symptom domains in the present study.

The questionnaire was administered to the patients during their regular postoperative follow-up clinic visits. As this was a retrospective study based on clinical practice, the timing of questionnaire completion aligned with the specific follow-up protocols for each treatment modality (median follow-up: 3 months for the TA group and 6 months for the TL group).

Statistical analyses

To minimize baseline differences, propensity score matching was applied between the treatment groups. Continuous variables were summarized as mean values with standard deviations, while categorical data were expressed as counts and percentages. Group comparisons of demographic and clinical characteristics were performed using the chi-square test. For thyroid-specific QoL outcomes, independent t-tests were employed to evaluate score differences between the TA and TL groups. All analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided p value of less than 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Between July 2023 and June 2024, a total of 338 patients with BTNs who underwent either TA or TL were screened. After excluding 37 individuals who did not satisfy the eligibility criteria, 301 patients remained for analysis. Among them, 193 patients (64.1%; 31 men and 162 women) received TA, whereas 108 patients (35.9%; 20 men and 88 women) underwent TL. Following propensity score matching, 164 patients were retained, with 82 assigned to each treatment group (Fig. 2). The median follow-up time for questionnaire administration was 3 (3, 3) months in the TA group and 6 (4, 8) months in the TL group. Overall comparison of THYCA-QoL between the TA and TL groups

Statistical analysis revealed that the TA group had significantly lower scores than those in the TL group on the voice, concentration, scar, tingling hands/feet, and gained weight scales (all p < 0.05), indicating fewer thyroid-specific symptom complaints in the TA group. In contrast, the TA group had higher scores on the “less interest in sex” scale compared with the TL group (20.41 ± 20.19 vs. 8.75 ± 14.76; p < 0.001), reflecting greater concerns regarding sexual interest. Subgroup analysis of THYCA-QoL based on sex and age

To further evaluate factors influencing QoL, patients were stratified by sex (Table 3) and age (Table 4). Among females, TL was associated with significantly worse scores in concentration (p = 0.001), psychological aspects (p = 0.020), scar (p < 0.001), tingling hands/feet (p = 0.046), and weight gain (p = 0.001) compared to TA. Conversely, female TA patients only reported worse scores for “less interest in sex” (p = 0.002). In males, outcomes were largely comparable between treatments, except for a similarly impaired sexual interest in the TA group (p = 0.004).

Age stratification revealed that younger patients (< 55 years) undergoing TL suffered more from scarring (p < 0.001) and weight gain (p = 0.039). Older TL patients (≥ 55 years) exhibited significantly worse concentration (p = 0.006), psychological well-being (p = 0.002), and weight gain (p = 0.004). Notably, consistent with the sex subgroups, younger TA patients also experienced significantly less interest in sex (p < 0.001).

Clinical efficacy of TA

In the TA group, the mean initial nodule volume was 11.18 ± 18.77 mL. After a median follow-up of three months, the mean nodule volume significantly decreased to 4.36 ± 6.15 mL. The overall mean volume reduction rate (VRR) was 60.37 ± 22.01%, confirming the clinical efficacy of the TA procedure in our patient cohort.

Discussion

TL has been regarded as the mainstay treatment for BTNs. TA has rapidly developed because of its minimal invasiveness, safety, and efficacy [18–20]. QoL is now increasingly regarded as an essential outcome in therapeutic evaluation, as it reflects not only survival but also the broader impact of treatment on daily functioning [21]. Patients with BTNs generally maintain good overall QoL which means that generic QoL instruments may show ceiling effects and fail to detect subtle but clinically meaningful differences. For this reason, our study design emphasized thyroid-specific symptoms as the primary endpoints, using the THYCA-QoL to capture dimensions directly relevant to thyroid interventions. This approach allowed us to detect differences between TA and TL in domains such as voice, scar, tingling hands/feet, weight change, and concentration.

It is important to clarify the use of the THYCA-QoL in this context. Although the instrument was originally designed for thyroid cancer patients, it primarily assesses symptoms that are related to thyroid disease in general, including voice, swallowing, neck discomfort, and hormone-related complaints, making it relevant to patients with benign nodules as well. Nevertheless, the absence of a BTNs-specific validated QoL tool remains a limitation. Future research should focus on the development of questionnaires tailored specifically to benign thyroid disease.

Our findings revealed that patients treated with TA reported better thyroid-specific QoL across several symptoms domains, particularly regarding voice, concentration, scarring, tingling hands/feet, and gained weight. While our study primarily focuses on QoL, the substantial volume reduction rate of 60.37% observed in the TA group confirms that TA provides excellent clinical efficacy while preserving better functional and psychological well-being compared to TL. These benefits are most likely attributable to the minimally invasive nature of TA, which cases limited disruption to the skin, soft tissues, and thyroid gland. In contrast, TL can damage structures containing blood vessels, nerves, and parathyroid glands, resulting in sequelae such as visible neck scars, hoarseness due to recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, or hypocalcemia-related sensations in the hands and feet [22–24]. In addition, the application of conformal ablation technique for BTNs precisely targets the nodules under real-time ultrasound guidance, successfully confining the thermal field within the nodules. The use of hydrodissection further protects critical structures such as the recurrent laryngeal nerve and parathyroid glands, explaining the lower rates of hoarseness and hypocalcemia-related symptoms observed in the TA group. These results are consistent with previous studies reporting superior QoL outcomes for TA, especially regarding hypocalcemia, hoarseness, and scarring [25–28].

Preservation of thyroid function likely also contributes to the observed differences. TA selectively destroys nodules while leaving most of the thyroid parenchyma intact, thereby maintaining endocrine function in the majority of patients [8–31]. Although TL generally preserves sufficient residual tissue to sustain hormone production, a proportion of patients still develop subclinical or overt hypothyroidism and require levothyroxine replacement [32]. Moreover, even when the replacement prescribed, achieving stable hormone levels may be challenging, and fluctuations can result in symptoms such as weight gain or impaired concentration [33]. This functional advantage may explain, at least in part, the better outcomes in domains related to metabolism and cognition among patients treated with TA.

Our subgroup analyses provided crucial insights into the potential triggers affecting postoperative QoL, revealing that treatment impacts are highly dependent on patient demographics. First, sex and age appear to be major influential factors regarding cosmetic and metabolic concerns. Female and younger (< 55 years) patients are generally more active socially and professionally, making them more susceptible to body image alterations. This explains why scar complaints and weight gain were significantly more distressing for these subgroups following TL. The visible cervical scar and potential subclinical hypothyroidism (leading to weight gain) after lobectomy likely act as secondary triggers for the psychological burden and concentration issues observed predominantly in female patients. Second, age dictates different vulnerabilities. While younger patients focused more on physical appearance, older patients (≥ 55 years) demonstrated significantly worse concentration and psychological well-being following TL. This may be attributed to a reduced cognitive and emotional reserve in older individuals, making it harder for them to adapt to the physiological stress of surgery, general anesthesia, and subsequent thyroid hormone fluctuations compared to the minimally invasive nature of TA.

Interestingly, “less interest in sex” was consistently worse in the TA group across multiple subgroups (females, males, and younger patients).Thyroid hormones influence various aspects of well-being, including energy levels and sexual function [34, 35], subtle endocrine fluctuations during this recovery phase may contribute to the increased sexual distress observed in the TA group. One possible explanation is that, during the early postoperative period after TA, heat-induced inflammation and rapid absorption of the ablation zone may transiently affect thyroid function, potentially leading to subclinical hypothyroidism and reduced sexual desire. In contrast, patients in the TL group often receive postoperative levothyroxine supplementation, and transient subclinical hyperthyroidism during dose adjustment may partially explain relatively higher sexual interest in this group. However, because our follow-up was limited to 3 months, we could not determine whether this difference was transient or persistent. In addition, postoperative thyroid function was not analyzed longitudinally in relation to sexual outcomes. Future studies with longer follow-up are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Overall, our results indicate that TA provides several significant advantages over TL in preserving thyroid-specific QoL, particularly with respect to voice, scarring, neuromuscular symptoms, and weight control, although sexual interest-related concerns were slightly higher. These findings highlight the potential role of minimally invasive strategies for patients with BTNs and may provide useful information for individualized treatment planning.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and modest sample size may introduce residual selection bias despite propensity score matching. Second, the QoL evaluation timing differed (3 months for TA vs. 6 months for TL) reflecting real-world clinical routines. However, the fact that TL patients reported significantly worse QoL despite having a longer recovery period (6 months) actually further underscores the benefits of TA. Third, we lacked concurrent TSH measurements, which may have limited the interpretation of symptoms potentially associated with hypothyroidism. Fourth, the relatively high standard deviations in our results reflect the inherent subjectivity and high inter-individual variability typical of patient-reported outcomes. Finally, the overall follow-up was relatively short. Future multicenter, prospective studies with larger cohorts, longer follow-up, and objective neuroendocrine assessments are essential to validate these findings.

In conclusion, patients with BTNs who underwent TA reported better thyroid-specific QoL than those who underwent TL in several symptom domains, although sexual interest-related concerns were slightly higher. These findings suggest that TA may be a viable alternative to TL for selected patients, while highlighting the need for longer-term and larger studies to confirm these results.
