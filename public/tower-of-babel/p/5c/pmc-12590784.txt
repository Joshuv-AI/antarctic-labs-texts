Mesenchymal stem cells in sepsis-induced organ dysfunction: mechanisms and therapeutic potential
Authors: Tingyu Yang, Xinqi Xu, Jiapan An, Bin Li, Zhimin Dou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sepsis is a life-threatening condition characterized by organ dysfunction resulting from a maladaptive host response to infection and remains a leading cause of mortality in critically ill patients. Organ dysfunction frequently occurs as a sepsis complication, exacerbating systemic inflammation and contributing to increased mortality. Current treatment modalities for sepsis-induced organ dysfunction (SIOD) are limited to infection control, organ function support, and anti-inflammatory interventions. However, their effectiveness is limited. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) possess immunomodulatory and regenerative properties and offer a promising therapeutic approach for addressing SIOD syndrome through diverse mechanisms. This review elucidates the pathophysiological basis of SIOD, investigates the potential mechanisms and therapeutic applications of MSCs in the management of SIOD, and outlines existing challenges to underpin future MSC-based strategies for sepsis-induced organ injury treatment.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition characterized by organ dysfunction due to dysregulated immune responses to infections, and it is associated with high rates of mortality and morbidity [1–3]. In the intensive care unit (ICU), sepsis holds particular significance, impacting roughly 20–30% of patients, among whom 25–40% succumb to the condition prior to hospital discharge [4, 5]. Uncontrolled inflammatory responses in sepsis can lead to organ dysfunction syndrome, which predominantly affects the respiratory, cardiovascular, renal, neurological, hematological, hepatic, and gastrointestinal systems [6–12]. The mortality rate for septic patients diagnosed with multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) can be as high as 28–56% [13, 14].

Despite a more profound understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying sepsis, current therapeutic strategies remain predominantly focused on early identification, fluid resuscitation, antibiotic treatment, and organ support [2, 15]. However, these measures are not fully capable of halting the progression of organ dysfunction, especially in patients with severe disease. Therefore, an in-depth investigation into the pathogenesis of sepsis-induced organ dysfunction (SIOD) and the development of more effective preventive and therapeutic approaches is of importance for improving the prognosis of patients with sepsis.

In recent years, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), as a type of pluripotent stem cells with biological functions such as self-renewal, multi-differentiation, immune regulation, inflammation, oxidative stress inhibition, and tissue repair promotion and regeneration, have garnered extensive attention [16, 17]. Multiple studies have illustrated that MSCs can effectively improve SIOD, suggesting they may be a potentially effective strategy for treating SIOD [18–20].

This review aimed to elucidate the pathological mechanism of SIOD and the biological functional characteristics of MSCs, to summarize the preclinical research progress of MSCs in SIOD treatment, including their mechanisms of action in immunomodulation, antioxidative stress, cell death regulation, and vascular endothelium repair, to explore their clinical application prospects and provide theoretical support and practical reference for future MSC-based SIOD treatment strategies.

Pathological mechanisms of SIOD

The pathophysiological mechanisms of organ dysfunction induced by sepsis are complex and dynamic. They begin with the systemic inflammatory response triggered by pathogen invasion [21]. Pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) activate pattern recognition receptors on host cells, releasing large amounts of inflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [22]. These mediators spread via the bloodstream, causing systemic inflammatory response syndrome. As inflammation intensifies, oxidative stress increases, with excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species damaging cells [23, 24]. Inflammation and coagulation factors also impair microcirculation, causing endothelial cell injury, thrombus formation, and tissue hypoperfusion [25]. This process leads to tissue hypoxia and nutrient supply insufficiency, disrupting cellular function, particularly mitochondrial dysfunction, which causes energy metabolism disorders and apoptosis or necrosis [26, 27]. Additionally, sepsis causes immune system hyperactivation, followed by immune suppression, with immune cell dysfunction and immunoparalysis, increasing susceptibility to secondary infections [28]. These pathophysiological changes interact, forming a vicious cycle, resulting in cell, tissue and organ damage and ultimately leading to MODS.

Given the distinct structures and functions of different organs, their mechanisms of injury also vary. Therefore, gaining an in-depth understanding of the specific injury mechanisms of each organ is crucial for developing targeted and personalized therapeutic strategies. Research has shown that in SIOD, key organs such as the liver, heart, lungs, kidneys, and brain are frequently affected [29, 30].

Sepsis-induced lung injury

In the pathological process of sepsis, the lungs are the most severely affected organ, leading to acute lung injury (ALI) [31, 32]. In sepsis, pro-inflammatory mediators generated by systemic inflammatory responses can be disseminated to the lungs via the circulatory system. These inflammatory mediators activate and recruit a substantial number of neutrophils and macrophages, which in turn release additional pro-inflammatory cytokines and ROS, thereby exacerbating local inflammatory responses and ultimately causing injury to both alveolar epithelial cells and pulmonary capillary endothelial cells (Fig. 1) [33, 34]. Damage to alveolar epithelial cells impairs their function in clearing alveolar fluid, leading to the accumulation of fluid within the alveoli and consequently worsening pulmonary edema. Concurrently, injury to pulmonary capillary endothelial cells disrupts the vascular endothelial barrier, increases vascular permeability, and facilitates the extravasation of fluid into the pulmonary interstitium and alveolar spaces, thereby exacerbating ventilation-perfusion mismatching. Moreover, damage to alveolar epithelial cells also interferes with the synthesis and secretion of pulmonary surfactant, resulting in alveolar collapse and atelectasis, which further compromises gas exchange. In critically ill patients, sepsis-induced ALI can develop into acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), life-threatening severe lung damage involving hypoxemia, and pulmonary infiltrates [33].

Sepsis-induced kidney injury

The kidney is a primary target organ in sepsis [35]. Sepsis, the systemic inflammatory response releases inflammatory mediators that can directly damage renal epithelial cells and vascular endothelial cells (Fig. 2). These mediators also recruit and activate immune cells, prompting them to secrete additional inflammatory mediators, ROS, and proteases. These substances further exacerbate the injury to renal tubular epithelial cells and endothelial cells [36]. Endothelial cell damage activates the coagulation cascade, exacerbating intrarenal thrombotic microangiopathy and leading to microcirculatory dysfunction [35, 37–39]. This dysfunction is characterized by uneven blood flow distribution in the renal capillaries, with some areas experiencing blood flow stasis while large vessel blood flow may remain normal [40]. Such a situation not only directly results in direct tubular epithelial injury but also potentially results in patchy areas of ischemia and loss of autoregulation, aggravating tubular epithelial cell injury and dysfunction (Fig. 2) [41–44].

Moreover, during sepsis, renal tubular epithelial cells undergo metabolic reprogramming, shifting metabolism toward glycolysis [45]. Metabolic reprogramming is an adaptive response aimed at reducing energy consumption and prioritizing cell survival (Fig. 2) [46]. However, this metabolic shift may also affect the function and viability of renal tubular epithelial cells [47]. Although the kidneys possess some self-repair capabilities, if the injury is severe or persistent, the renal self-repair mechanism may not be able to fully restore the damaged renal tissue, ultimately leading to irreversible renal functional damage and even progression to chronic kidney disease or renal failure.

Sepsis-induced liver injury

The liver, as a key immune organ in the body, plays a vital role in sepsis. According to clinical classification, sepsis-induced liver injury can be categorized into inflammatory liver injury, hypoxic liver injury, cholestatic liver injury, and secondary sclerosing cholangitis (Fig. 3).

In sepsis, hepatic sinusoidal endothelial cells engulf pathogens entering the liver, presenting them to hepatic stellate, kupffer, and other intrahepatic immune cells [48, 49]. After activation, immune cells in the liver can release TNF-α, IL-6, and other inflammatory mediators, further recruiting peripheral immune cells into the liver. This process can trigger a cytokine storm, exacerbate hepatic inflammation, and ultimately result in injury or death of hepatic cells, including hepatocytes, biliary epithelial cells, and endothelial cells, resulting in inflammatory liver injury [49, 50].

Endothelial cell damage not only causes vascular tension and peripheral vasodilation but also activates the liver coagulation system, causing microvascular congestion and microthrombosis, and significantly reduces liver blood perfusion, leading to liver ischemia and hypoxia [49]. Simultaneously, pathogens and inflammatory factors can directly cause mitochondrial dysfunction and interfere with oxygen metabolism in liver cells [51–53]. However, body metabolism is enhanced during sepsis, requiring the liver to eliminate many metabolites while simultaneously synthesizing substances, such as acute-phase proteins, leading to increased oxygen demand. If a patient develops septic shock, liver ischemia, and hypoxia are further aggravated. The decrease in liver oxygen supply and increase in oxygen consumption eventually lead to liver cell death, functional impairment, and hypoxic injury, also known as shock liver [49, 54, 55].

Damage to hepatocytes and biliary epithelial cells may cause downregulation of hepatic transport system expression, alterations in transporter protein transport, reorganization around the microtubules of the cytoskeleton, and disruption of tight junctions between hepatocytes that impact bile secretion, eventually leading to cholestasis [49, 56, 57]. Cholestasis results in heightened oxidative stress, increased cell membrane permeability, and hindered regeneration of bile transport proteins [58, 59], which may lead to compromised glucose and lipid metabolism, suppressed immune responses, and vascular dilation, exacerbating liver injury [60]. Additionally, damage to biliary epithelial cells can also diminish the secretion of protective neutralizing agents, such as phosphatidylcholine and bicarbonate [61]. This reduction allows bile can directly impact the compromised bile duct epithelium, leading to epithelial cell necrosis, shedding, and bile duct blockage. This sequence exacerbates the inflammatory response near the bile ducts and compromises the barrier [55]. Furthermore, the compromised biliary microenvironment creates a conducive setting for bacterial colonization, culminating in intricate and treatment-resistant infections that progress to chronic cholangitis [62]. Prolonged inflammation can induce fibrotic changes in the bile ducts, potentially resulting in strictures in both the intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts [63]. Inflammatory damage persists, disease progression continues, and biliary cirrhosis may ultimately ensue.

Sepsis-induced cardiac injury

Heart, as the pump organ, plays a key role in the pathology of sepsis and septic shock. During sepsis, the systemic inflammatory response releases a large amount of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [64]. These pro-inflammatory cytokines further activate immune cells within the heart, including macrophages and monocytes, which in turn release a large amount of inflammatory mediators, triggering a cascade of inflammatory reactions [65]. Inflammatory mediators directly act on cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells, exacerbating oxidative stress and leading to cellular damage and death [64].

Cardiomyocyte damage is predominantly linked to mitochondrial dysfunction. During sepsis, inflammatory responses and oxidative stress can disrupt mitochondrial morphology, damage mitochondrial DNA, and downregulate the expression of genes encoding mitochondrial proteins [66–68]. Downregulated genes related to mitochondrial changes are associated with hormonal alterations from the well-recognized ‘low T3’ syndrome in sepsis [69–71]. These changes impair ATP production, further exacerbated by ROS and nitric oxide (NO) inhibiting the respiratory electron transport chain complexes (notably complex I and IV) [72–74].

Endothelial cell injury leads to myocardial microvascular thrombosis and increased permeability, resulting in the leakage of fluid and albumin into the interstitial space. These processes result in an imbalance between the microvascular fluid filtration rate and the lymphatic fluid removal rate, leading to myocardial edema [75]. This edema not only leads to pulmonary arterial hypertension and pericardial effusion but also dramatically reduces energetic efficiency, impairing both contraction and relaxation [76, 77].

Moreover, the damage to endothelial and cardiomyocytes, as well as the activation of immune cells, can also cause dysregulation in the production of endogenous vasoactive substances such as arachidonic acid and prostaglandins. This further exacerbates endothelial dysfunction and worsens coronary microvascular dysfunction, leading to cardiac ischemia and affecting cardiac function [78]. In summary, sepsis-induced cardiac injury is the result of the concerted action of multiple mechanisms, involving the release of inflammatory mediators, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, endothelial cell damage, and dysregulation of endogenous vasoactive substances (Fig. 4). The interplay of these factors ultimately leads to cardiac dysfunction. Sepsis-induced brain injury

Brain dysfunction is one of the most perilous forms of organ dysfunction caused by sepsis [79]. Sepsis, pro-inflammatory cytokines released by the systemic inflammatory response can activate microglia, astrocytes, pericytes, and neutrophils, which in turn produce a large number of inflammatory mediators and ROS, triggering a cascade of inflammatory reactions [80]. These inflammatory mediators and ROS downregulate tight junction protein expression and alter their structure and function, thereby compromising the blood-brain barrier’s integrity and function [81, 82]. Additionally, they directly damage the blood-brain barrier’s microvascular endothelial cells, inducing apoptosis or necrosis [83]. This damage accelerates microthrombus formation and further exacerbates blood-brain barrier permeability [84].

Blood-brain barrier dysfunction disrupts the relative isolation of the central nervous system, which facilitates the entrance of leukocytes and inflammatory mediators into the brain parenchyma [85, 86]. These substances infiltrate the central nervous system through the compromised blood-brain barrier, exerting direct toxic effects and activating microglia and astrocytes [87, 88]. Microglial activation leads to the release of ROS, NO, and glutamate [89], while activated astrocytes produce excessive amounts of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including C-X-C motif chemokine ligand (CXCL) and C-C motif chemokine ligand (CCL). Astrocyte-secreted CCL11 promotes microglial migration and activation [90], while microglia-secreted cytokines (IL-1α, TNF-α, C1q) induce reactive astrocytes, which secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines and neurotoxic lipids, ultimately leading to neuroinflammation [91]. The significant quantities of inflammatory mediators resulting from neuroinflammation lead to neuronal dysfunction and cognitive impairments.

Moreover, endothelial cell damage can also lead to cerebral circulatory dysfunction, including reduced cerebral flow, hypotension, and compromised autoregulation. Cerebral circulatory dysfunction can lead to insufficient energy supply and mitochondrial dysfunction, triggering various neurological and electrophysiological changes, including neuronal anoxia, apoptosis, and cerebral ischemia [92–94].

These complex pathophysiological mechanisms act in concert, ultimately resulting in brain injury. If the inflammation persists, it may also lead to long-term cognitive dysfunction (Fig. 5).

Notably, SIOD is characterized by intricate pathophysiological mechanisms, and the injuries to various organs are significantly interrelated. These interrelations exacerbate disease progression. For instance, hepatic dysfunction can lead to a reduced capacity for endotoxin clearance, resulting in elevated circulating endotoxin levels. This, in turn, further intensifies systemic inflammatory responses and triggers more extensive organ damage. Moreover, hepatic insufficiency also affects drug metabolism and the synthesis of coagulation factors, increasing the risks of bleeding and infection. Renal injury leads to the accumulation of toxins and metabolic waste, causing uremia. It also disrupts electrolyte and acid–base balance, imposing additional burdens on the heart and nervous system. Renal failure can further cause fluid retention, heightening the risks of pulmonary edema and heart failure. The interrelated organ injuries render the clinical management of sepsis extremely complex and lead to poor efficacy of current treatment modalities. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel and efficacious therapeutic interventions.

Biological function of MSCs

MSCs have garnered significant interest in treating diverse diseases. These adult stem cells possess various advantageous traits, such as diverse origins, robust self-renewal capacity, low immunogenicity, and the ability to differentiate into multiple cell lineages. Additionally, they exhibit biological features, including immune modulation, homing, antimicrobial and antioxidant properties, and regulation of programmed cell death (PCD) (Fig. 6). These attributes enable MSCs to facilitate tissue repair and regeneration through diverse mechanisms. A thorough understanding of the biological properties of MSCs is crucial for elucidating their therapeutic role in disease management and enhancing treatment outcomes.

Multilineage differentiation potential

MSCs originate from the mesoderm during early embryonic development. Their capacity for multilineage differentiation is contingent on the expression of distinct cell cycle regulatory proteins and transcription factors, including octamer-binding transcription factor 4, Nanog, and SRY-box transcription factor 2 [95]. Besides, the differentiation potential of MSCs is influenced by factors such as tissue source, local microenvironment, and specific cell subtypes, enabling them to differentiate into various cell lineages [96–98].

MSCs exhibit lineage differentiation preferences based on their tissue source. Adipose-derived MSCs tend to differentiate into adipocytes, while synovial-derived MSCs primarily differentiate into chondrocytes. Conversely, umbilical cord-derived MSCs exhibit significant osteogenic and chondrogenic differentiation potential [98]. The microenvironment is critical in guiding MSCs’ differentiation. For instance, bone morphogenetic protein-2 prompts osteoblastic differentiation, whereas dexamethasone and insulin favor adipogenic differentiation [99, 100]. MSCs can transdifferentiate across the germ layers under specific conditions. For instance, they can differentiate into ectodermal neuron-like cells in response to brain-derived neurotrophic factors and retinol or endodermal hepatocyte-like cells upon exposure to hepatocyte growth factor and fibroblast growth factor 4 [101–103]. In addition, distinct MSC subtypes exhibit varying differentiation capabilities. Single-cell transcriptome analysis of human bone marrow MSCs has identified three subgroups: Stem, functional, and proliferative. The stem subgroup possesses self-renewal and differentiation potential, whereas the functional subgroup illustrates enhanced osteogenic differentiation capacity [104].

Immunoregulation

When exposed to inflammatory stimuli, MSCs can influence immune responses through direct cellular interactions and paracrine signaling pathways. Through direct contact, MSCs can regulate the activity, proliferation, and differentiation of immune cells [105, 106]. Specifically, MSCs have been shown to engage with T cells via programmed death ligand expression, leading to the suppression of effector T cell proliferation and activation, promotion of CD4 + CD25 + regulatory T cells (Treg) and CD8 + Treg production, and maintenance of forkhead box P3 + expression on Treg cell surfaces [105, 107]. Moreover, MSCs can secrete various immunoregulatory factors through paracrine signaling, which act directly on immune cells to modulate their activity and functionality, thereby exerting immunomodulatory effects. These factors include soluble cytokines, such as prostaglandin E2, IL-10, transforming growth factor-β, growth factors, and extracellular vesicles (EVs) [108, 109].

MSCs exhibit a bidirectional immunoregulatory capacity that is modulated by the inflammatory microenvironment. MSCs primarily exert immunosuppressive effects during intense inflammation, mitigating inflammatory damage by dampening exaggerated immune responses. Conversely, MSCs can stimulate immune responses under mild inflammatory conditions and bolster immune defense mechanisms. This dynamic regulatory interplay allows MSCs to maintain immune homeostasis within an organism [110].

Homing

MSCs can detect and react to injury signals, prompting their migration to specific locations through interactions between signaling molecules and their respective surface receptors. The homing process of MSCs can be categorized into local and systemic homing, each following distinct pathways [111, 112].

During local homing, MSCs are administered directly into the target tissue, where they initially adhere to the extracellular matrix before accurately migrating to the injury site. Chemokine concentration gradients, such as Stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1), facilitate migration and guide the MSCs [111]. Notably, this mechanism operates independently of blood circulation, emphasizing the crucial role of MSCs’ interactions with the specific tissue microenvironment.

MSCs are introduced into the bloodstream through peripheral vascular injection during systemic homing. Initially, they engage with selectins on the endothelial cell surfaces, facilitating their rolling along the vascular endothelium. Subsequently, upon activation by chemokines, MSCs adhere to endothelial cells and transmigrate into tissues. MSCs adhere to the extracellular matrix within tissues and migrate through tissues towards injury sites, guided by chemokine gradients [113]. This complex process involves MSCs’ transportation in the bloodstream and their sequential interactions with vascular endothelial cells and extracellular matrix within tissues.

Antimicrobial function

MSCs exhibit wide-ranging antimicrobial capacity, demonstrating inhibitory effects on Gram-positive bacteria [114] and bactericidal properties against Gram-negative bacteria [115–118], mycobacteria [119], viruses [120], and parasites [114].

MSCs express various toll-like receptors on their surfaces to detect microorganisms and respond to their antigens [121]. Upon activation, MSCs produce and release antimicrobial peptides, including cathepsin LL-37, lipocalin-2, beta-defensin-2, and hepcidin [122]. These peptides have direct antimicrobial activity, disrupt biofilm formation, and remove existing biofilms, thereby preventing microbes from host immune evasion and enhancing the bactericidal function of phagocytes [123]. Furthermore, MSCs secrete cytokines and chemokines that facilitate microbial clearance by recruiting and improving the phagocytic activity of monocytes/macrophages and neutrophils at the infection site [124]. This antimicrobial role of MSCs not only mitigates tissue and organ damage caused by microorganisms and limits microbial-induced harm to healthy tissues [125].

Antioxidative stress

MSCs can counteract oxidative stress and mitigate oxidative stress-induced tissue injury through various mechanisms. These mechanisms involve bolstering the antioxidant defense system of the host, suppressing inflammation, and enhancing energy metabolism within impaired cells [126].

Upon inflammatory stimulation, MSCs not only secrete antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) directly but also enhance the expression and functionality of SOD, CAT, glutathione peroxidase (GPX), and glutathione reductase in host cells [126, 127]. These antioxidant enzymes play a crucial role in scavenging oxygen-free radicals generated during cellular respiration, thereby preserving cellular redox homeostasis [128]. Moreover, MSCs can impede the recruitment and activation of immune cells, such as neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages, leading to the downregulation of ROS-generating enzymes, such as NADPH oxidase and myeloperoxidase, in immune cells. Consequently, this modulation curtails ROS production by immune cells in response to respiratory bursts [126]. Additionally, MSCs contribute to the enhancement of energy metabolism in damaged cells through various mechanisms. For instance, they can restore the mitochondrial membrane potential via paracrine signaling and ameliorate mitochondrial function. Moreover, MSCs facilitate the transfer of functional mitochondria and mitochondrial protective proteins to impaired cells using cellular structures, such as tunnelling nanotubes (TNTs) and EVs, thereby enhancing cellular energy metabolism and diminishing ROS generation [129–132].

Regulating PCD

PCD refers to an active and orderly process of self-destruction regulated by specific cell genes [133]. Common PCDs include apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis [134]. MSCs can regulate PCD through both paracrine and direct contact [135].

The soluble factors and EVs secreted by MSCs can improve the microenvironment of damaged tissues by regulating immunity and promoting angiogenesis, thereby promoting the survival of damaged cells and indirectly regulating PCD. For example, paracrine IL-10 from MSCs can inhibit hepatocyte pyroptosis [136]. EVs derived from MSCs can inhibit pyroptosis [137, 138]. Additionally, MSCs can selectively transport ions, proteins, polypeptides, microRNAs (miRNAs), and mitochondria to target cells through gap junction proteins, TNT, and other intercellular communication structures, regulate intercellular signal transduction, and directly regulate PCD [135, 139]. For example, MSCs target mitochondria to ischemic cardiomyocytes via TNTs, improving cardiomyocyte energy metabolism and inhibiting cardiomyocyte apoptosis [140]. MSCs contact target cells through connexins 43 (Cx43) and Cx45 gap junctions, regulate the secretion of SDF-1, and inhibit apoptosis of hepatic stellate cells [141]. These studies suggest that MSCs regulate PCD through multiple mechanisms and play an important role in tissue repair and disease treatment.

MSC-exosomes

Exosomes (exos) are small extracellular vesicles released by MSCs in both quiescent and activated states [142]. Owing to their selective assembly, targeted delivery, efficient tissue repair capabilities, high safety profile, robust structural integrity, production ease, and storage, exosomes are increasingly emerging as a viable alternative for MSC-based therapy [143–146]. As crucial signal transduction mediators, exosomes can selectively package proteins, lipids, and miRNA, facilitating their effective transport to the target cells. This process modulates gene expression, key enzyme functions, and signaling pathways within the target cells, fostering tissue repair and regeneration [147, 148]. Notably, exosomes lack cell proliferation capacity, rendering them more adaptable and secure for engineering purposes. Through engineering, the functions and properties of exos can be precisely manipulated [149]. For instance, chemical modification using 1-Ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide/N-Hydroxysuccinimide to covalently attach carboxylic acid-functionalized LJM-3064 aptamers to amine groups on exosome surfaces has exhibited efficacy in treating multiple sclerosis [150]. The therapeutic benefits and customizable nature of exos underscore their significance in MSC therapy. These findings offer a foundational framework for advancing the understanding of the biological functions of MSCs, elucidating their action mechanisms, and propelling the development of exosome-based therapeutic interventions.

In summary, MSCs regulate the pathological processes of diseases through diverse mechanisms, exerting therapeutic effects. It is essential to emphasize that MSCs’ functions are interconnected and mutually reinforcing, culminating in a functional network that exerts their efficacy. Specifically, homing MSCs serves as a prerequisite for their therapeutic actions, such as immunomodulation and antioxidation. The intimate correlation between antioxidants and immunomodulation constitutes a pivotal aspect of this functional network. MSCs regulate immune homeostasis to dampen inflammatory reactions and mitigate cellular oxidative stress by curtailing inflammatory cell infiltration and immune mediators. Conversely, MSCs mitigate oxidative stress by suppressing the inflammatory mediators and cytokines secretion, thereby alleviating inflammation. Moreover, the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of MSCs are instrumental in regulating PCD. These functions synergistically contribute to the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs in various diseases.

Mechanistic studies of MSCs in SIOD

As mentioned above, the pathological mechanisms of SIOD are complex and involve multiple interrelated processes. Effective treatment of SIOD can only be achieved by adopting a multi-target collaborative intervention strategy to break down interrelated mechanisms. Consequently, an ideal therapeutic strategy should be capable of precisely targeting the diverse pathological processes involved in sepsis and enabling organ-specific interventions. Basic studies have demonstrated that MSCs, as stem cells with multiple functions such as regulating immune homeostasis, relieving oxidative stress, and regulating PCD, can regulate inflammatory response, oxidative stress, and other pathological processes of SIOD at multiple targets, and are promising candidates for treating SIOD (Fig. 7).

MSCs regulate immune homeostasis in SIOD

Owing to their immunomodulatory properties, MSCs can inhibit the release of inflammatory factors and attenuate inflammatory cell infiltration in a site-specific manner during sepsis, thereby alleviating organ injury [105].

In the lung, studies have shown that MSCs can reduce the number of inflammatory cells in the alveolar lavage fluid of septic mice and inhibit the release of inflammatory factors, such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, thereby correcting the immune imbalance [151–153]. MSCs can reduce the number of CD14+ and CD68+ T cells and the levels of IL-6 in the lungs during acute lung injury in sepsis, thereby attenuating the inflammatory response [154]. Additionally, MSC-exos can regulate the number of alveolar macrophages and the release of IL-27, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, thereby reducing pulmonary inflammation [155].

For the kidney, research indicates that MSCs are capable of reducing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as CXC, CCL, and IL-17) and inhibiting the migration of neutrophils to the kidneys, alleviating inflammatory response [156]. MSCs can suppress the expression of Nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) as well as the release of IL-1 and IL-6, thereby improving renal tubular function in sepsis [157]. Furthermore, MSC-exos can modulate renal inflammation by reducing the release of several pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α, while restoring the level of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 [158, 159].

Mounting studies have provided convincing evidence that MSCs can inhibit the release of TNF-α, IL-6 and monocyte chemotactic protein (MCP)-1, and upregulate the anti-inflammatory factors IL-10 and IL-4 to correct immune imbalance in liver injury. MSCs can suppress the migration and activation of neutrophils in the liver, thereby alleviating liver damage caused by sepsis [160–162]. Furthermore, MSCs can also secrete soluble TNF receptor 1, which alleviates the liver injury and inflammatory response [163]. In addition, MSCs and their exos can promote the polarization of macrophages towards the M2 phenotype, thereby improving immune homeostasis in liver injury [164, 165].

Several studies have reported that MSCs attenuate inflammatory cell infiltration and reduce inflammatory cytokine release in cardiomyocytes during sepsis. For instance, MSCs can reduce the expression of TLRs; inhibit the NF-κB signaling pathway; and decrease the levels of inflammatory factors, such as IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α, in the plasma and myocardium of septic mice [166]. Additionally, MSCs have been shown to secrete exosomes enriched with miRNA-223, which inhibits myocardial inflammatory responses and mitigates myocardial injury [167].

Similarly, previous studies have also verified the regulatory effects of MSCs on the immune response in sepsis-induced brain injury. For example, MSCs can inhibit the secretion of IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α, and the proliferation and activation of microglia, consequently attenuating the inflammatory response in the brain [168–170]. In addition, MSC-exos can ameliorate neuroinflammation in sepsis-induced brain injury by reducing inflammatory cell infiltration and microglial activation in the hippocampus [171].

Taken together, MSCs can mitigate the inflammatory response and alleviate organ injury in sepsis by inhibiting inflammatory signaling pathways and secreting exosomes.

MSCs attenuate oxidative stress in SIOD

Antioxidative stress of MSCs against SIOD aims to modulate key targets for inhibiting oxidative stress and inflammation and ameliorate oxidative tissue damage (especially of the lungs, liver, and kidneys) [172].

MSCs have been found to reduce ROS production by upregulating the expression level of the nuclear factor erythrocyte 2-related factor 2 in hepatocytes, while also attenuating sepsis-induced liver injury by inhibiting NF-κB signaling [173, 174]. MSCs can also scavenge excessive ROS by paracrine secretion of glutathione, thereby alleviating sepsis-induced injury in the heart, liver, lungs, and kidneys [175–177]. MSCs-exos-derived glutathione peroxidase 1 can reduce ROS production by upregulating extracellular signal-regulated kinases 1 and 2 and B-cell lymphoma 2 expression and downregulating the IκB Kinase β/NF-κB/caspase-9/-3 signaling, mitigating hepatic oxidative damage [178]. Moreover, MSCs-exos-derived miR-26a-5p can also scavenge excess ROS in the liver by inhibiting metastasis-associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript1 and promoting the expression of CAT and GPX [179].

These findings illustrate that MSCs can ameliorate ROS production and oxidative stress through various mechanisms, alleviating organ injury.

MSCs regulate PCD in SIOD

MSCs regulate PCD in sterile inflammatory SIOD through diverse mechanisms involving intricate signaling pathways and molecular targets that safeguard cells and organs from damage by regulating PCD.

Research has found that MSC transplantation in sepsis-induced lung injury can reduce the number of apoptotic cells in the lungs and distal organs [180, 181] and protect alveolar macrophages from apoptosis induced by endotoxins in part by inhibiting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [182]. Additionally, MSCs-derived exosomes mitigate lung tissue injury by delivering miR-125b-5p to inhibit Keap1, thereby upregulating the nuclear factor E2-related factor 2/GPX4 pathway to reduce ferroptosis in microvascular endothelial cells, and by modulating signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 expression to attenuate pyroptosis in macrophages [183, 184]. Moreover, MSCs inhibit the apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells in lung injury through the CXCL12/CXCR4 signaling axis [185].

It has been reported that MSC-exos can alleviate cellular apoptosis in sepsis-associated kidney injury by activating autophagy and the silent information regulator 1 (SIRT1) signaling pathway [186, 187]. In addition, MSCs can also promote mitophagy and inhibit apoptosis and pyroptosis of renal tubular epithelial cells in kidney tissues by upregulating SIRT1/Parkin, thereby ameliorating sepsis-induced kidney injury [188].

For the liver injury, research indicates that MSCs can improve mitochondrial membrane potential in macrophages via paracrine activity, scavenge excessive ROS, and consequently impede macrophage pyroptosis in the liver [175]. Additionally, MSCs can hinder hepatocyte pyroptosis by suppressing NLR family pyrin domain-containing protein 3 inflammasome activation [189]. Moreover, MSC-exos mitigate hepatocyte apoptosis by inducing hepatocyte autophagy [190]. MSC-exos also transport autophagy-related protein 2 homolog B to macrophages, inducing mitochondrial autophagy by suppressing the intracellular stimulator of interferon genes signaling [191]. In summary, MSCs can alleviate sepsis-induced liver injury by modulating cell death within the liver.

In the cardiac, MSCs can inhibit apoptosis and pyroptosis in cardiomyocytes, macrophages, and other cells, thereby promoting cell survival and alleviating sepsis-induced cardiac injury. For example, MSCs-EVs carrying miR-223 can prevent myocardial cell apoptosis and suppress the inflammatory response by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway, which in turn can help to prevent sepsis-induced cardiac injury [167]. MSCs-EVs enriched with miR-146a-5p can promote cell proliferation and survival by regulating Myb-like protein 1 expression, thus protecting septic myocardial cells or tissue [192]. Additionally, the delivery of miR-223-3p by MSCs-EVs can suppress sepsis-induced cardiac pyroptosis and dysfunction [193].

In the brain, the regulatory effect of MSCs on PCD has also been observed. For instance, exosomes derived from MSCs provide neuroprotection in sepsis-associated encephalopathy by regulating hippocampal pyroptosis via the NOD-like receptor protein 3/caspase-1/gasdermin D pathway [171]. The delivery of miR-140-3p by MSCs-exos ameliorated cognitive impairment in CLP mice by improving microglial pyroptosis and S-lactoylglutathione metabolism by targeting high mobility group box 1 [194]. Moreover, studies have suggested that MSCs can alleviate neuronal dysfunction by promoting the inhibition of neuronal autophagy [195].

These observations suggest that MSCs potentially coordinate PCD regulation in SIOD through multiple targets, fostering cell viability and thereby mitigating organ damage.

MSC-mediated restoration of the vascular endothelial barrier in SIOD

MSCs repair the vascular endothelium of the injured organ through diverse mechanisms, thereby effectively enhancing its microcirculation. First, MSCs can differentiate into endothelial cells and integrate into damaged areas, directly repairing the vascular endothelial barrier [196]. Moreover, MSCs enhance vascular endothelial function by mitigating inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, consequently reducing vascular endothelial cell damage and death [196–199]. MSCs secrete various pro-angiogenic cytokines such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), epidermal growth factor, and fibroblast growth factor, along with exosomes, facilitating endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and lumen formation. This coordinated action regulates intercellular and intracellular signal transduction and metabolic processes, ultimately enhancing organ microcirculation [200, 201]. For instance, MSCs have been demonstrated to mitigate sepsis-induced vascular endothelial injury by promoting nitric oxide synthase expression and stimulating VEGF and hepatocyte growth factor production [157, 202, 203]. MSC-exos can target endothelial Abl tyrosine kinase 2 to deliver miR let-7-5p, thereby attenuating p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase activation and reducing LPS-induced endothelial glycocalyx degradation and vascular leakage [204]. Moreover, research has revealed that MSC-exos could promote vascular endothelial barrier recovery by inhibiting microvascular inflammatory cytokine levels and alleviating microvascular permeability, thereby improving sepsis-induced kidney, lung, liver, and other organ injury [205–210]. These combined mechanisms synergistically alleviate organ injury by augmenting capillary density in ischemic tissue and fostering collateral angiogenesis, thereby enhancing blood supply to the organ [211, 212].

In summary, MSCs and their exos exhibit regulatory effects on the pathological progression of sterile inflammation-SIOD through multiple target modulation, thereby serving as a therapeutic intervention for SIOD. The increasing focus on MSC-exos in SIOD treatment is attributed to their advantageous features, including high histocompatibility, precise targeting, and low immunogenicity [213, 214]. These results suggest that MSCs can exert their corresponding therapeutic effects against different types of SIOD. With further research, precise SIOD treatment may be possible by modifying MSCs, especially their exosomes. These results provide a theoretical basis for the potential of MSCs in SIOD treatment and lay the foundation for further clinical trials.

Clinical prospects of MSCs in SIOD treatment

Although preliminary research has confirmed the promising use of MSCs in treating SIOD, their safety and efficacy require full validation through clinical trials before they can be considered for clinical implementation. However, there is a lack of specific clinical data regarding SIOD. To gain insights into the potential clinical use of MSCs in SIOD, it is essential to closely monitor the progress of clinical research on MSCs and innovative therapeutic approaches utilizing MSCs.

Clinical research status of MSCs in sepsis

There have been numerous clinical trials utilizing MSCs in both acute and chronic conditions, including myocardial infarction, heart disease, ARDS, and kidney injury with all trials demonstrating well-tolerated and trials demonstrating mixed efficacy results [215–218]. Overall, there have been 7 completed, 3 active, and 1 unknown status MSC clinical trials for sepsis (Table 1). Overall, studies have demonstrated the favorable safety and efficacy profile of MSCs and their exos in sepsis, particularly in sepsis-related ARDS [219, 220]. These conclusions are supported by evidence-based medicine focused on ARDS [221], offering valuable insights into the clinical utilization of MSCs and their exosomes in SIOD.

Mode of administration of MSCs

Multiple routes can be utilized for administering MSCs, such as local, intravenous, arterial, and intralesional injections, with the selection typically based on the treatment target and injury location [222]. For instance, intracoronary injection is commonly employed for heart disease treatment [223, 224], whereas intravenous injection is preferred for liver disease [225, 226]. Presently, intravenous injections are the most prevalent administration method because of their simplicity, low invasiveness, high repeatability, and capacity to disperse MSCs widely throughout various organs, including the lungs, liver, and spleen [213]. Nonetheless, intravenously delivered MSCs tend to accumulate in the lungs, potentially leading to suboptimal dosages reaching the intended organs and impacting MSCs’ efficacy [227]. Hence, further investigations are imperative to refine the administration strategy.

MSCs-based combination therapy

Besides optimizing their function, strategies of MSC-based combination therapies to enhance MSC efficacy are a promising option. Studies have shown that MSCs can achieve better therapeutic effects when combined with other drugs. For instance, combining MSCs-exos with antibiotics has demonstrated promise in further reducing sepsis-induced liver damage [228, 229]. Moreover, pairing MSCs with antioxidants has enhanced their effectiveness significantly [177, 230]. Additionally, utilizing natural compounds from plants and microorganisms in conjunction with MSCs can help modulate inflammatory responses and mitigate organ inflammation [231, 232]. This combined approach leveraged the benefits of such as antioxidants and antibiotics to mitigate oxidative stress and inflammation, creating a more conducive microenvironment for MSCs to thrive and function optimally. Nonetheless, further experimental studies are imperative to elucidate the precise mechanisms and to identify the most effective combinations for such therapies.

Challenges in MSC-based therapy for SIOD

Despite the therapeutic potential of MSCs in treating SIOD, their clinical utilization encounters obstacles stemming from the intricate pathological processes of SIOD and the inherent limitations of MSCs.

The pathogenesis of SIOD involves many aspects, such as inflammatory reaction disorder, microcirculation dysfunction, mitochondrial dysfunction, and metabolic disorders, with interplay among the injuries to different organs [22, 29]. This complex pathological process, mediated by multiple factors, makes it difficult to fully cover all the pathological links of SIOD with MSCs alone, and it is difficult to achieve an ideal therapeutic effect.

MSCs have exhibited inherent limitations. First, variations in cell characteristics, differentiation potential, immunomodulatory capacity, and secretory function among donors, tissue sources, and culture conditions lead to inconsistent therapeutic outcomes, hindering standardized production and clinical application [233, 234]. Second, prolonged in vitro expansion diminishes the proliferation, differentiation, and immune regulatory abilities of MSCs, potentially culminating in malignant transformation, thereby compromising their therapeutic potential [234]. Third, systemic administration of MSCs is impeded by their limited in vivo homing capacity, hindering efficient localization to injury sites. Even after successful homing, survival rates may decline due to hypoxia and oxidative stress, jeopardizing long-term efficacy [235, 236]. Last, while native MSCs possess low immunogenicity, exposure to inflammatory mediators in vivo can heighten their immunogenicity post-infusion, provoking immune rejection and potentially undermining therapeutic effectiveness [234].

Discussion and prospects

SIOD is a leading cause of mortality among critically ill patients and those outside ICUs; its pathological mechanism is complex and involves inflammatory reaction disorders and the cell death process. Existing therapeutic approaches exhibit restricted effectiveness, underscoring the pressing need for novel treatment modalities. MSCs possess diverse biological capabilities, including multilineage differentiation, immunomodulation, and tissue regeneration enhancement. By modulating the pathophysiological progression of SIOD, MSCs have the potential to confer protective effects, offering a promising avenue for addressing SIOD.

However, MSCs encounter challenges in clinical applications, such as differences in cell characteristics, limited homing capacity, and immunogenicity concerns. With the in-depth study of the biological functions of MSCs, it is expected that the efficacy and safety of MSCs can be improved by optimizing the cell source, improving the production technology of MSCs, pretreatment strategies, and combination therapies [149, 237, 238]. Moreover, MSC-exos, serving as crucial mediators for the therapeutic effects of MSCs, provide another option for treating SIOD due to their favorable attributes, including high histocompatibility, low immunogenicity, precise targeting, and absence of risks associated with live cell therapies.

In conclusion, existing studies indicate that MSCs have good application prospects in treating SIOD. Future research will focus on exploring the specific mechanisms and targets of MSCs in SIOD and optimizing their efficacy and safety. This will promote the clinical transformation of this therapy, make it an essential strategy for managing SIOD in the future, and provide new ideas for improving the prognosis of patients with sepsis.
