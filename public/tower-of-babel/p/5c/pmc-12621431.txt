Differences in Severity and Prognosis Between Bicuspid and Tricuspid Severe Aortic Stenosis
Authors: Taiji Okada, Takeshi Kitai, Takeshi Morimoto, Chisato Miyakoshi, Tomohiko Taniguchi, Mitsuhiko Ota, Takao Kato, Kenji Ando, Nobuhisa Ohno, Norio Kanamori, Koichiro Murata, Yuichi Kawase, Tatsuhiko Komiya, Makoto Miyake, Yoshio Arai, Chisato Izumi, Tadaaki Koyama, Yutaka Furukawa, Takeshi Kimura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBicuspid aortic valve (BAV) often leads to aortic stenosis (AS). However, current severity assessment and treatment strategies do not differentiate between BAV and tricuspid aortic valve (TAV) patients.ObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to investigate differences in echocardiographic indices used to evaluate disease severity and to compare long-term outcomes between BAV and TAV.MethodsWe performed a post hoc analysis of 3,815 patients with severe AS enrolled in the CURRENT AS (Contemporary outcomes after sURgery and medical tREatmeNT in patients with severe Aortic Stenosis) registry. Echocardiographic parameters and clinical outcomes were compared between patients with BAV and those with TAV. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. Sensitivity analyses included propensity-score matching and estimation of HRs for BAV relative to TAV. The median follow-up period was 36.9 months (IQR: 18.2-51.8).ResultsPatients with BAV had higher jet velocity (maximal aortic jet velocity: 4.65 ± 0.87 vs 4.09 ± 0.91 m/s; P < 0.001) and mean pressure gradient (51.6 ± 20.3 vs 39.9 ± 19.3 mm Hg; P < 0.001), with similar aortic valve area (0.70 ± 0.21 vs 0.72 ± 0.18 cm2; P = 0.288) compared to those with TAV. Discordant severity grading was less frequent in BAV than in TAV (34% vs 58%; P < 0.001). Linear regression indicated larger aortic valve area in BAV for equivalent maximal aortic jet velocity and mean pressure gradient values. Five-year all-cause mortality was lower in BAV than in TAV (6.5% vs 45.9%; P < 0.001), with matched-cohort analysis confirming a survival benefit (HR: 0.46; 95% CI: 0.28-0.77; P = 0.003).ConclusionsPatients with BAV have unique hemodynamic characteristics distinct from TAV. Tailored evaluation criteria for BAV patients may influence both prognosis and treatment strategies.Key words: aortic stenosis, aortic valve replacement, bicuspid aortic valve

Bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) is a prevalent cardiac anomaly that affects 0.5% to 2% of adults1, 2, 3 and is a cause of aortic stenosis (AS). Current guidelines define severe AS by any of the following cutoff parameters; maximal aortic jet velocity (Vmax) >4.0 m/s, mean pressure gradient (mPG) >40 mm Hg, or aortic valve area (AVA) <1.0 cm2.4, 5, 6 This concept assumes that progressive AS leads to increasing transvalvular jet velocity, and the chosen thresholds reflect their demonstrated prognostic value. However, when AVA is near 1.0 cm2, discordance may occur between Vmax of 4.0 m/s and mPG of 40 mm Hg, underscoring the complexity of severity assessment. Moreover, the relationship between jet velocity and orifice area depends not only on the area's absolute size but also on geometric shape. Given the differing valve-opening geometries in BAV vs tricuspid aortic valve (TAV), the relationship between Vmax and AVA may vary. Nevertheless, comparative data between BAV and TAV regarding the echocardiographic severity indices remain limited.

In patients with BAV, the onset of AS occurs 5 to 10 years earlier than in patients with TAV.3 Studies of BAV cohorts have demonstrated that 12% to 37% of patients either had or developed moderate-to-severe AS.7, 8, 9, 10 Previous studies have reported improved survival following surgical aortic valve replacement (AVR) in patients with BAV,10 however, these cohorts were predominantly younger. Moreover, comparative data on clinical outcomes between patients with BAV and those with TAV, whether managed conservatively or with surgical AVR, remain scarce. This knowledge gap persists despite recent investigations that have explored comparative outcomes between BAV and TAV following transcatheter AVR.11,12

The aim of this study was to investigate differences in echocardiographic indices used to evaluate disease severity and to compare long-term outcomes between BAV and TAV.

Methods

Study design and patient population

The study design of the CURRENT AS (Contemporary outcomes after sURgery and medical tREatmeNT in patients with severe Aortic Stenosis) registry was described elsewhere in detail.13 In brief, CURRENT AS is a retrospective, multicenter registry that enrolled 3,815 consecutive patients with severe AS from 27 centers in Japan between January 2003 and December 2011. Echocardiographic examinations were performed by the experienced cardiac sonographers and/or cardiologists with all measurements adhering to the criteria of the American Society of Echocardiography.14 The protocol received independent approval from the Institutional Review Board or ethics committee at each participating center. Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study, and no patient refused participation when contacted for follow-up. Patients intended for surgical AVR as their initial treatment strategy, regardless of actual surgery timing, were classified into the initial AVR group.13

Assessment of aortic valve morphology and severity of AS

The aortic valve morphology, including the number of cusps, was determined based on surgical inspection, or preoperative and/or intraoperative echocardiography. Thirty-two patients with unicuspid, quadricuspid, or indeterminate cusp number were excluded (Figure 1). Among the remaining 3,783 patients, 254 patients had BAV; 211 by surgical inspection and 43 by echocardiography. AS severity was assessed by echocardiography specialists at each site. Vmax and mPG were calculated using the simplified Bernoulli equation, and AVA was calculated using the standard continuity equation.15

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was all-cause death, and the secondary outcome was the composite of aortic valve–related death or hospitalization for heart failure (HF). Aortic valve–related death included aortic valve procedural death, sudden death, and death due to HF possibly related to AS. HF hospitalization was defined as hospitalization for worsening HF requiring intravenous drug therapy. Other outcomes included cardiovascular death, sudden death, and noncardiovascular death. The causes of death were classified according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium definitions and were adjudicated by a clinical event committee. Sudden death was defined as unexplained death occurring in patients who were previously in stable condition. Follow-up data were primarily collected through a review of hospital charts or via direct contact with patients, relatives, and/or referring physicians. This was done using mailed questionnaires regarding survival, symptoms, and subsequent hospitalizations.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test. Continuous variables are expressed as the mean and SD or median and IQR. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank sum test, depending on distributions. In both the BAV and TAV groups, linear regression models evaluated the association between AVA and Vmax or mPG. Clinical outcomes were compared in the entire study population and within the initial AVR and conservative-management strata. Cumulative incidence curves were generated by the Kaplan-Meier method and compared by the log-rank test. To adjust for baseline differences, multivariable Cox proportional hazards models estimated HRs with 95% CIs for BAV vs TAV patients, incorporating 23 clinically relevant covariates (Table 1).13

As a sensitivity analysis, we performed 1:1 propensity score matching. Propensity scores for BAV patients were derived from a logistic regression model including 16 variables,13 with age and Vmax treated as continuous covariates (C-statistic = 0.877). A greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm on the logit of the propensity score matched each BAV patient to a TAV patient; outcomes were then compared using Kaplan-Meier estimation and Cox models to calculate HRs (95% CIs).

All analyses were conducted in SPSS v28.0 (IBM) or JMP v10.0.2 (SAS Institute). All tests were 2-tailed, with P < 0.05 denoting statistical significance.

Results

Baseline clinical and echocardiographic characteristics

Comparisons of baseline characteristics between patients with BAV and TAV were shown in Table 1. Patients with BAV were younger, predominantly men, and less frequently had comorbidities such as obesity, hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation or flutter, anemia, renal failure, malignancy, and coronary artery disease. In contrast, patients with BAV exhibited a higher prevalence of a history of smoking and chronic lung disease than those with TAV. The age distribution among patients with BAV and TAV is shown in Supplemental Figure 1 for both the entire study population and the propensity score–matched cohort. Additionally, patients with BAV compared to those with TAV were less likely to present with HF symptoms at baseline and had lower surgical mortality risk scores, including the logistic European System for Cardiac Operative risk Evaluation score, European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation II score, and Society of Thoracic Surgeons score. Comparisons of baseline characteristics between the 2 groups after propensity score (PS) matching were shown in Supplemental Table 1.

Differences in echocardiographic indices regarding as severity between BAV and TAV

There were no significant differences regarding the AVA between the groups (0.70 ± 0.21 vs 0.72 ± 0.18 cm2; P = 0.288). In contrast, patients with BAV had a significantly higher Vmax and mPG compared to those with TAV (4.65 ± 0.87 vs 4.09 ± 0.91 m/s; P < 0.001, and 51.6 ± 20.3 vs 39.9 ± 19.3 mm Hg; P < 0.001, respectively). The rate of discordant grading in severity such as AVA ≤1.0 cm2 despite Vmax <4 m/s, or AVA >1.0 cm2 despite Vmax ≥4 m/s16 was significantly lower in BAV than TAV (34% vs 58%; P < 0.001). In the linear regression analysis between Vmax and AVA, the AVA corresponding to the Vmax of 4.0 m/s was 1.0 cm2 in BAV, but it was 0.75 cm2 in TAV. Similarly, the AVA corresponding to the mPG of 40 mm Hg was 1.0 cm2 in BAV, but it was 0.75 cm2 in TAV (Figure 2).

Comparisons of clinical outcomes between patients with BAV and TAV

The median follow-up was 36.9 months (IQR: 18.2-51.8). Follow-up completeness at 3 years, assessed by the modified Clark’s C method, was 69% overall (94% in TAV, 67% in BAV). During follow-up, 1,444 patients died and 807 were hospitalized for HF. At 5 years, patients with BAV had a significantly lower cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality than those with TAV (6.5% vs 45.9%; P < 0.001). Likewise, the composite of aortic valve–related death or HF hospitalization was lower in BAV vs TAV (12.5% vs 37.7%; P < 0.001) (Figure 3). After multivariable adjustment, BAV remained associated with lower all-cause mortality (HR: 0.54; 95% CI: 0.34-0.84; P = 0.007) but not with the composite outcome (HR: 0.80; 95% CI: 0.53-1.22; P = 0.301) (Table 2).

Survival and Composite Events in the Propensity Score–Matched Cohort

Kaplan-Meier curves compare (A) all-cause mortality and (B) the composite of aortic valve–related death or HF hospitalization between BAV and TAV patients after 1:1 propensity score matching. In this matched cohort, BAV was associated with significantly lower 5-year all-cause mortality (4.7% vs 25.6%; P < 0.001) and a reduced composite event rate (6.3% vs 18.2%; P < 0.001), confirming the survival benefit observed in the overall population. Abbreviations as in Figures 1 and 2.

When stratified by treatment strategy (Supplemental Figure 2), patients with BAV had lower 5-year all-cause mortality than those with TAV in both the initial AVR group (4.7% vs 25.6%; P < 0.001) and the conservative-management group (9.2% vs 53.8%; P < 0.001) (Supplemental Figure 3). Similarly, the composite of aortic valve–related death or HF hospitalization was lower in BAV vs TAV for both strategies (initial AVR: 6.3% vs 18.2%; P < 0.001; conservative: 21.6% vs 46.3%; P < 0.001) (Supplemental Figure 4). After multivariable adjustment, BAV remained associated with reduced all-cause mortality in both strata (initial AVR: HR: 0.50; 95% CI: 0.32-0.79; P = 0.003; conservative: HR: 0.45; 95% CI: 0.24-0.85; P = 0.014), but not with the composite outcome (initial AVR: HR: 0.72; 95% CI: 0.47-1.08; P = 0.114; conservative: HR: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.48-1.36; P = 0.421) (Table 2). Among patients initially managed conservatively, 536 patients underwent AVR during follow-up (477 in the TAV group and 59 in the BAV group; P < 0.001). The median time to AVR was comparable between the groups (20.7 [11.7-35.5] vs 25.2 [10.2-38.5] months; P = 0.475). In the propensity-matched cohort, 40 TAV and 56 BAV patients underwent AVR on follow-up (P = 0.010); median time to AVR was similar (TAV: 27.6 vs BAV: 31.6 months; P = 0.513). Outcome analyses in this matched cohort were consistent with the multivariable Cox models (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we conducted an extensive comparative analysis of echocardiographic indices related to AS severity and assessed prognostic differences between patients with BAV and those with TAV in the largest contemporary severe AS cohort. The main findings are as follows: 1) patients with BAV exhibited significantly higher Vmax and mPG than those with TAV, even when having similar AVA; 2) an AVA of 1.0 cm2 corresponded to a Vmax of 4.0 m/s in BAV but only 0.75 cm2 in TAV; and 3) BAV was independently associated with lower mortality risk than TAV, and this association remained significant even after adjusting for confounders (Central Illustration).

BAV morphology, characterized by unequal cusp size, a raphe, and smooth cusp margins, creates an eccentric, often elliptical orifice that disrupts leaflet motion and generates turbulent flow. These features potentially contribute to early degeneration.17, 18, 19 Hemodynamically significant AS increases impedance and resistance to left ventricular ejection.20,21 In this study, we found that patients with BAV had higher jet velocity than those with TAV, despite comparable AVA. These differences likely reflect variances in orifice geometry between the 2 groups. Echocardiographic estimation of orifice area relies on the simplified Bernoulli equation, which was originally derived for small, circular openings. A narrowed aortic valve orifice can take diverse shapes, particularly between BAV and TAV, so differences in flow velocity are expected even when anatomical AVA is the same.22 Moreover, because BAV often coexists with dilation of the ascending aorta, the impact of pressure recovery on gradient interpretation should be considered when interpreting transvalvular gradients.23 Conversely, the measurement of AVA by planimetry may overestimate the actual anatomic AVA in patients with BAV,24 thereby limiting the accuracy of severity assessment based on AVA. Higher gradients in BAV may also reflect preserved stroke volume in younger, less comorbid patients. As our data set lacked direct flow measurements, future studies incorporating flow parameters, such as stroke volume index, are needed to clarify the interplay between flow status and transvalvular pressure gradients in BAV.

The prognosis of patients with BAV has often been regarded as relatively benign, but comparative data vs TAV are limited. In our cohort, patients with BAV demonstrated more favorable survival than those with TAV. Consistent with the previous report,25 patients with BAV were younger and had lower atherosclerosis risk profiles, likely contributing to reduced all-cause mortality and cardiovascular events. Interestingly, despite higher Vmax and mPG for a given AVA,26,27 BAV remained independently associated with lower mortality after multivariable adjustment and in propensity-matched analyses.

In summary, in a large contemporary cohort with severe AS, BAV exhibited higher Vmax and mPG than TAV despite comparable AVA. These findings highlight the importance of hemodynamic assessment and suggest that in patients with BAV, AVA should be interpreted with particular attention alongside velocity, gradient, pressure recovery, and flow state. Further confirmatory studies are warranted to determine whether the same echocardiographic criteria for grading AS severity can be applied equally to both valve morphologies, or whether morphology-specific thresholds should be considered.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it lacks detailed anatomical classification of BAV such as the presence of raphe or the portion of fused cusps. Second, echocardiographic measurements were performed locally without a central core laboratory, potentially introducing intersite variability. Third, most BAV diagnoses were made intraoperatively, limiting the preoperative applicability of tailored AVA/gradient thresholds. However, recent advances in cardiac computed tomography have enabled more accurate preoperative identification of BAV and measurement of AVA, offering the potential to improve diagnostic precision and support the future application of tailored severity thresholds in clinical settings.28 Fourth, the study period largely preceded the availability of transcatheter AVR in Japan, so our findings may not fully generalize to current TAVR practice. Finally, incomplete flow data, including missing stroke volume indices, precluded analysis of discordant AS phenotypes. Additionally, ascending aortic dimensions and morphology were also not systematically evaluated, limiting assessment of pressure recovery and BAV-associated aortopathy. The impact of mild AR on transvalvular gradients remains unclear. Future studies incorporating detailed flow measurements, systematic aortic assessment, and AR severity gradation may provide further insights.

Conclusions

In severe AS due to BAV, the relationship between AVA and Vmax differs from that in TAV. These hemodynamic differences suggest that the current criteria for grading AS severity may not be equally applicable to both valve morphologies and that morphology-specific cutoffs may need to be considered between patients with BAV and TAV.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Current guidelines for evaluating AS do not differentiate between BAV and TAV morphologies. This study highlights significant hemodynamic differences between BAV and TAV patients. BAV patients have higher Vmax and mPG despite similar AVAs. Additionally, BAV patients show lower all-cause mortality. These findings suggest that tailored evaluation criteria for BAV patients could lead to more accurate severity assessments and improved clinical outcomes.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Future research should focus on prospective multicenter studies to validate these findings and explore the impact of personalized treatment strategies for BAV patients. Further investigations into the underlying mechanisms driving these differences could provide deeper insights and enhance the effectiveness of clinical interventions.
