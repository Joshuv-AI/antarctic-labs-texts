Kuwait Atrial Fibrillation Management Analysis—Insights From a Tertiary Center in Kuwait
Authors: S. AlKharji, S. A. Almoosawy, J. AlBalool, E. Shenouda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundReal‐world data on atrial fibrillation (AF) contemporary management from the Gulf region are scarce and nonexistent from Kuwait. Despite recent guideline updates favoring early rhythm control, clinical characteristics, treatment strategies, and outcomes remain poorly characterized in this region. This study aimed to evaluate AF management patterns at a tertiary center in Kuwait, focusing on rhythm vs. rate control strategies and direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) prescribing practices.MethodsAn observational study on adult patients diagnosed with AF at Al Dabbous Cardiac Center between 2013 to 2022 was conducted. Demographic data, relevant clinical characteristics, and investigations were extracted. The primary outcome was the prevalence and predictors of rhythm control. Secondary outcomes included all‐cause and cardiovascular‐related hospitalizations. The appropriateness of DOAC dose‐reduction was assessed based on guideline‐based criteria.ResultsOf 558 records screened, 457 patients with non‐valvular AF were included. Rhythm control was pursued in 28.9% of cases, most commonly via direct current cardioversion (47.3%). Patients selected for rhythm control were more likely to be younger (mean age 64 vs. 70 years, p < 0.001), male (61.1% vs. 38.9%, p = 0.024), and followed in electrophysiology clinics (33% vs. 5%, p < 0.001). Rhythm control strategy was independently associated with greater odds of cardiovascular‐related hospitalizations (adjusted OR = 2.89, 95% CI: 1.12–7.48, p = 0.028). Among patients receiving reduced‐dose DOACs (68.8%), 11.6% of patients were underdosed despite the absence of criteria for dose reduction.ConclusionRhythm control was underutilized in our study population, falling short of international benchmarks and guideline recommendations. Disparities in rhythm control utilization based on sex and referral patterns were observed. Additionally, among patients on dose‐reduced DOACs, a significant proportion of them were underdosed. These findings highlight the need to enhance awareness of evidence‐based AF management and to promote adherence to guideline‐directed therapy.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common cardiac arrhythmia, and its prevalence is expected to rise in the coming years, leading to increased healthcare utilization and associated costs [1, 2]. Consequently, the management of AF has evolved to incorporate early rhythm control as a major component [1, 2]. Recent studies, notably EAST‐AFNET4 [3], demonstrated benefits of early rhythm control in reducing cardiovascular death, stroke, hospitalization for heart failure or acute coronary syndrome compared to rate control alone. Rhythm control strategies include electrical or pharmacological cardioversion, as well as catheter ablation. Catheter ablation offers superior rhythm control and symptom improvement compared to other rhythm control modalities [1, 2, 4, 5, 6]. In addition, catheter ablation spares patients from the systemic and proarrhythmic side effects associated with antiarrhythmic drugs (AAD) [3, 4, 7].

Despite favorable evidence supporting rhythm control, real‐world registries report lower adoption rates ranging between 35%–52% [8, 9, 10, 11]. Moreover, despite the established advantages of catheter ablation, it constituted less than 10% of rhythm control strategies in a large multinational registry [8, 11]. Such historically low rates also highlight the slow adoption of the strategy and challenge the gap between evidence and real‐world practice. Data on AF management patterns in the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) region, particularly concerning rhythm control, remain limited [12], despite the rapid evolution of rhythm control strategies and growing availability of catheter ablation technologies. Importantly, Gulf populations, particularly Kuwait, are characterized by a high prevalence of obesity [13](40%), diabetes [13](22%), and chronic kidney disease [14] consequently. These comorbidities may significantly impact rhythm control selection and anticoagulation strategies [2]. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the adoption of rhythm control strategies for AF at a large tertiary center in Kuwait and evaluate how local practices compare with international standards.

Methods

We conducted an observational study on all adult patients (≥ 18 years) with a diagnosis of AF who were admitted to or followed as an outpatient in Al‐Adan hospital between 2013 and 2022. Al Dabbous Cardiac Center (DCC) is a large tertiary center affiliated with Al‐Adan Hospital, a large 600‐bed tertiary hospital serving the population of the Al‐Ahmadi health area.

Eligible patients were identified through hospital electronic medical records (EMR) by screening for the presence of any anticoagulation therapy. Subsequently, patient charts were reviewed for confirmation of AF based on one or more of the following criteria: (1) a documented diagnosis of AF using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems 10th Revision (ICD‐10) codes [12], (2) documentation of an irregular pulse on physical examination, (3) electrocardiographic evidence of AF or (4) echocardiography reports noting an AF rhythm. Datapoints collected included: Demographic information, medical comorbidities including CHA2DS2VASc score, medications including anti‐arrhythmic drugs (amiodarone, sotalol, flecainide or propafenone), rate control agents including beta‐blockers, non‐dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers and digoxin, and oral anticoagulants, relevant laboratory investigations, specialty clinic follow‐up (EP clinic was covered by a regionally acknowledged board‐certified electrophysiologist and other clinics included general cardiology, internal medicine or other subspecialties than electrophysiology), Electrocardiogram (ECG) and Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) findings. In addition, insurance status was recorded as “complete health coverage” vs. other. In Kuwait, nationals have universal government‐funded healthcare, while non‐Kuwaiti residents rely on employer‐based or private insurance and often require out‐of‐pocket costs. In addition, DCCV and catheter ablation were performed at the discretion of the treating cardiologists and/or electrophysiologists, in line with international guidelines; detailed procedural parameters were not systematically collected for this analysis. A structured chart review was conducted to identify post‐treatment hospitalizations for heart failure, atrial fibrillation, or stroke following implementation of rhythm or rate control strategies.

The primary objective of this study was to determine the prevalence of rhythm control in the management of AF and to describe patients' characteristics compared to regional and international populations. In addition, all‐cause mortality, hospitalization, and hospitalization due to heart failure, AF, or stroke were evaluated for different subgroups.

Paroxysmal, persistent, and long‐lasting persistent AF were classified according to the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and American College of Cardiology (ACC) guidelines [1, 2]. Paroxysmal AF was defined as AF that terminates spontaneously or with intervention within 7 days of onset. Persistent AF was defined as continuous AF that is sustained beyond 7 days, including episodes terminated by cardioversion, either pharmacological or electrical. Lastly, longstanding persistent AF was defined as continuous AF lasting longer than 12 months [1, 2]. Furthermore, heart failure (HF) was defined as any clinical diagnosis of HF or objective evidence of cardiac dysfunction with an ejection fraction less than 40% [2]. A patient was classified as having hypertension or diabetes mellitus if they were receiving medical therapy for the respective disease [2]. Vascular disease was defined as a documented history of myocardial infarction, peripheral arterial disease, or aortic plaque [2]. Similarly, chronic kidney disease (CKD) was based on creatinine clearance [15] or documentation of end‐stage renal disease (ESRD) in the medical record.

Left atrial volume index (LAVI) was measured and indexed to body surface area. The degree of severity was categorized according to the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines: Normal (≤ 34 mL/m2), mild enlargement (35–41 mL/m2), moderate enlargement (42–48 mL/m2), and severe enlargement (> 48 mL/m2) [16].

Patients who were prescribed reduced doses of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) were identified through the EMR and subsequently categorized as receiving a recommended dose or an underdose. The recommended reduced dose was based on the ESC guidelines [15]. For Apixaban, dose reduction is recommended if two of the following criteria are met: (1) body weight ≤ 60 kg, (2) serum creatinine ≥ 133 μmol/L (≥ 1.5 mg/dL) or (3) age ≥ 80 years [1, 2, 15]. Additionally, a lower dose of apixaban was deemed appropriate if patients had a creatinine clearance between 15 to 29 mL/min. Moreover, dose reduction for rivaroxaban is recommended for creatinine clearance between 15 to 49 mL/min [1, 2, 15].

For exploratory analysis, patients were stratified into two study periods (2012–2020 vs. 2021–2022) to assess temporal trends in rhythm control utilization. The cutoff year of 2021 was selected to reflect the maturation of our EP service.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data visualizations were created using SPSS and Canva. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were reported as mean and standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on data distribution. Comparisons between normally distributed continuous variables were performed using an independent t‐test, and non‐normally distributed variables using the Mann–Whitney U test. Chi‐square test was used to compare categorical variables. A two‐tailed P‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Univariate logistic regression was conducted to evaluate the association between baseline characteristics and adopting a rhythm control strategy, with results expressed as crude odds ratios (OR) with a corresponding 95% confidence interval (95% CI). Variables with clinical relevance and/or significance in univariate analysis were incorporated in multivariable logistic regression analysis using forward selection; p ≤ 0.2 to avoid omitting potentially important predictors.

Results

A total of 558 cases were reviewed, of which we excluded 101 patients with valvular AF. Of the 457 patients included in this study, 131 (28.9%) underwent a rhythm control strategy for management of AF, while 311 (71.1%) received rate control. Among those managed with rhythm control, 47.3% underwent electrical cardioversion, 26.0% received pharmacological cardioversion, and 26.7% underwent catheter ablation. Rhythm Control vs. Rate Control

Rhythm control was more commonly pursued in younger patients (mean age 64 vs. 70 years, p < 0.001) and males (61.1%). Patients who received rhythm control therapy were more likely to be male patients, have paroxysmal AF, and have a lower CHA2DS2VASc (Table 1). DOACs were prescribed to 51.9% of patients in the rhythm control group compared to 40.4% in rate control (p = 0.014). LAVI was significantly larger in the rate control group (49 mL/m2, IQR: 37–61) compared to the rhythm control group (38.9 mL/m2, IQR: 32–51, p = 0.021). One third of patients in the rhythm control group were followed in an electrophysiologist clinic compared to only 5.3% in the rate control group (p < 0.001).

Predictors of Selecting a Rhythm Control Strategy

In the univariate analysis, age ≤ 65 years, male gender, follow‐up in the electrophysiology clinic, and absence of chronic kidney disease were associated with the adoption of rhythm control (Table 2). In the multivariate analysis, follow‐up in the electrophysiology clinic was the strongest independent predictor of choosing rhythm control (adjusted OR of 6.99; 95% CI: 3.55–13.79, p < 0.001). Additionally, male gender (adjusted OR = 1.70; 95% CI: 1.10–2.65, p = 0.017) and absence of CKD (adjusted OR = 1.82; 95% CI: 1.02–3.28, p = 0.044) were also independently associated with selecting rhythm control. Other variables such as age, BMI, LAVI, insurance status, and DOAC dosing were not significantly associated with the selection of a rhythm control strategy.

Subgroup Analysis of Rhythm Control Strategy

Patient characteristics stratified by rhythm control modality are summarized in Table 3. Those who underwent catheter ablation were significantly younger, had lower CHA2DS2‐VASc scores (median: 2, IQR: 1–3.75, P = < 0.001), smaller LAVI (median of 33.9 mL/m2; p = 0.047), and were followed up in EP clinic (48.6% compared to 19.4% and 8.8% in DCCV and AAD groups, respectively; p = 0.002). AAD therapy was more commonly used in patients aged ≥ 65 years (73.5%) and was associated with a higher mean BMI (36 kg/m2; p = 0.014). History of stroke was significantly more common in patients treated with DCCV (21%) compared to those in the ablation (5.7%) or AAD (5.9%) groups.

Predictors of Rhythm Control Strategy

A multivariate logistic regression (Table 4) was performed using catheter ablation as the reference category. Patients followed in EP clinics were significantly more likely to receive AAD therapy rather than undergo catheter ablation (adjusted OR = 10.83; 95% CI: 1.70–68.76, p = 0.012). Male gender and absence of heart failure were also significantly associated with AAD use (adjusted OR = 7.09; 95% CI: 1.29–38.46 and 4.88; 95% CI: 1.29–18.18, respectively). No statistically significant associations were observed between different rhythm control modalities and the risk of all‐cause mortality or hospitalizations in adjusted analyses.

Impact of Rhythm Control on Hospitalization

Rhythm control strategy was significantly associated with hospitalization due to heart failure, atrial fibrillation, or stroke (adjusted OR = 2.89; 95% CI: 1.12–7.48, p = 0.028). Notably, in the subgroup analysis of different rhythm control modalities, all stroke events reported occurred in the DCCV group. In addition, patients who underwent DCCV had the highest burden of cardiovascular‐related hospitalizations (77.8%), including admissions for heart failure, AF, or stroke. However, these associations were not statistically significant.

Temporal Trends in Rhythm Control Modalities

When stratified by study period, the overall prevalence of rhythm control remained stable (27.8% in 2013–2020 vs. 29.7% in 2021–2023; p = 0.67). However, the composition of rhythm control strategies changed markedly after 2021. In the early period, nearly all rhythm control consisted of DCCV. In contrast, the later period (2021–2023) showed diversity in rhythm control modalities to include DCCV (46.5%), catheter ablation (27.1%), and AAD (26.4%). Although these differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.32), the descriptive shift highlights the increased availability and adoption of catheter ablation and AAD following the maturation of EP services. EP clinic follow‐up remained low across both periods, with a non‐significant increase from 9.9% in 2013–2020 to 11.3% in 2021–2023 (p = 0.63).

Underdosing of DOAC

Among 159 patients prescribed low‐dose Apixaban or Rivaroxaban, dosing eligibility could be assessed in 77 individuals. Of these, 53 patients (68.8%) were underdosed. This represents 11.6% of the total DOAC‐treated population. Underdosing was significantly more common among female patients (p = 0.005) and those with diabetes mellitus (p = 0.005) (Supplementary Table 6).

In multivariate analysis (Supplementary Table 7), creatinine clearance ≥ 50 mL/min (OR = 4.78; p = 0.005) and body weight > 60 kg (OR = 11.76; p = 0.039) were significantly associated with DOAC underdosing. Other variables were not statistically significant. Additionally, there was no significant association between DOAC underdosing and the risk of all‐cause mortality or cardiovascular‐related hospitalizations.

Discussion

This study provides the first real‐world analysis of AF management from a single tertiary center in Kuwait, offering valuable insights into local practice patterns and prescribing behaviors in the region. Rhythm control was employed in less than one‐third of patients, consistent with international trends that continue to favor rate control in routine clinical practice. Our findings highlight key clinical factors and the role of specialist follow‐up in influencing the selection of rhythm control strategies. Additionally, the study identified a high prevalence of underdosing of DOACs, raising important concerns regarding anticoagulation quality and safety in this population.

Our study population shares several similarities, as well as notable differences, with previously reported registries from the GCC region. Hypertension was the most prevalent comorbidity in our population, consistent with other GCC‐based studies. Compared to GULF‐SAFE [17], the largest AF registry in the region, our cohort was older and exhibited a higher burden of comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, obesity, stroke, and heart failure. This reflects a regional shift towards aging populations and the rising prevalence of chronic diseases.

While our patient cohort shares many characteristics with other AF populations in the Gulf region, some notable differences emerged. The prevalence of rhythm control strategies in our cohort was approximately 29%, slightly higher than rates reported in regional studies, where reported rates have ranged between 12% and 22% [18, 19]. However, this remains lower than rates observed in large international registries such as GARFIELD‐AF [20], ORBIT‐AF [10], and EORP‐AF [9]. This finding underscores the continued predominance of rate control strategies, consistent with both regional practice patterns and earlier global trends (Table 5). It also highlights the potential influence of clinical conservatism, awareness, and disparities in access to electrophysiology (EP) services, all of which may impact rhythm control utilization.

Several factors were found to be associated with the selection of a rhythm control strategy, including male gender, EP clinic follow‐up, and the absence of CKD. These findings align with previously published data demonstrating that younger age, lower comorbidity burden, and EP involvement are associated with a greater likelihood of rhythm control interventions [8, 9, 10]. Conversely, lower rates of rhythm control adoption among women are consistent with prior reports of sex‐based disparities in AF management [20, 21, 22]. The underlying reasons for this remain unclear and have not been previously evaluated in AF populations within the GCC region, underscoring the need for further research in this area. Potential contributing factors described in other cohorts include delayed diagnosis or referral due to atypical symptoms, perceived higher procedural risk, underrepresentation of women in major clinical trials, and bias or systemic inequities in healthcare delivery [21, 23, 24].

EP clinic follow‐up was strongly associated with rhythm control utilization, consistent with findings from the ORBIT AF [10] and EORP‐AF [9]. In our study, approximately 10% of the patients were followed in EP clinics, compared to 15%–25% in ORBIT‐AF I [10] and II [30]. Early referral to EP has been associated with a significantly shorter time to ablation, as well as reductions in heart failure symptoms, hospitalization, and emergency room visits compared to usual care [25]. Our analysis showed that despite the maturation of our EP service after 2021, the proportion of patients followed in EP clinics remained low and did not significantly increase. This suggests constraints at the level of referral pathways or physician practice patterns that continue to limit rhythm control adoption in our center.

In the subgroup analysis of patients treated with a rhythm control strategy, DCCV (47.3%) was the most used modality, followed by catheter ablation (26.7%) and AAD (26%). The use of catheter ablation in 26.7% of patients in our cohort exceeded reported rates in other Gulf registries [18]. This may reflect growing confidence in procedure efficacy, increased availability of catheter ablation technology with wider implementation of recent ESC updates recommending catheter ablation as first‐line therapy in selected patients [2, 4].

The presence of heart failure was associated with a lower likelihood of undergoing catheter ablation, and instead, a greater reliance on AAD or DCCV, suggesting a more conservative rhythm control approach in this subgroup. This may reflect hesitancy to pursue ablation in patients perceived as higher risk or with advanced structural heart disease. However, there is mounting evidence supporting catheter ablation in patients with AF and heart failure with reduced ejection fraction [26, 27]. Multiple randomized trials and meta‐analyses have demonstrated significant reductions in both mortality and cardiovascular hospitalizations in this population [26, 27, 28, 29, 30].

Our study showed a higher rate of hospitalizations for HF, AF, or stroke in the rhythm control group. This was primarily driven by the DCCV subgroup, which exhibited the highest burden of cardiovascular‐related admission (77.8%). These findings may be due to the limited long‐term efficacy of DCCV in maintaining sinus rhythm. Additionally, patients in the DCCV group had a higher median LAVI of 47 mL/m2 (IQR 43–55), reflecting more advanced atrial remodeling that may have contributed to recurrent hospitalizations and thromboembolic events despite initial rhythm control. Overall, these findings highlight the potential limitations of relying solely on electrical cardioversion for rhythm control. However, due to the small number of events, our results should be interpreted carefully and validated in larger prospective studies. These findings align with prior registry data [9, 10]. These emphasize the need for careful patient selection and follow‐up after electrical cardioversion.

In our study, 11.6% of patients receiving DOACs were underdosed despite meeting criteria for full dosing. This rate is notably higher than reported in U.S‐based registries such as ORBIT‐AF II [28] (9.4%), but lower than reported from a real‐world Saudi Arabian AF cohort [29] (26.8%). Underdosing can significantly impact patient outcomes, potentially to a similar extent as overdosing [28]. Although our study did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in outcomes across dosing categories, the sample size may have been insufficient to detect such an association. The observed trend of underdosing may stem from overly cautious prescribing habits, overestimation of bleeding risk, lack of physician awareness, and absence of region‐specific clinical guidelines [30]. While previous studies demonstrated that underdosing is significantly more common among non‐electrophysiology specialists [10, 28], this association was not observed in our cohort. It should be noted that dabigatran was prescribed in a very small number of patients during the study period, and the sample size was insufficient for meaningful assessment of dose‐appropriateness. Therefore, these cases were not included in the underdosing analysis. This represents a limitation of our study.

Comparison With Regional and International Registries

To place these findings in a broader context, we constructed a comparative summary of Kuwait AF management to regional and international cohorts (Table 5). Our analysis showed that rhythm control utilization remains intermediate in comparison to Gulf registries [31]. The proportion of patients undergoing catheter ablation in our center also exceeded that of prior GCC cohorts, reflecting increased availability and adoption of ablation in recent years, yet below contemporary benchmarks. Likewise, the prevalence of comorbidities such as obesity, diabetes, and CKD was higher in our population compared to Western registries. This highlights the unique demographic challenges in the Gulf. Finally, the rate of DOAC underdosing in our analysis (11.6%) was greater than that reported in ORBIT‐AF‐II [28] but lower than that observed in a recent Saudi cohort (26.8%) [30]. This positions our findings between global [8, 9, 10, 28, 32, 33] and regional [31] experiences. These comparisons highlight how Kuwait shares the comorbidity burden of Gulf populations while demonstrating evolving adoption of rhythm control strategies that, although improving, still fall short of international benchmarks. Despite the increasing availability of DOACs, our study showed a relatively high reliance on warfarin. This reflects drug availability and cost barriers, particularly among non‐nationals. Recent formulary updates beyond the study time‐frame have broadened DOAC access for all residents.

Overall, while the rhythm vs. rate control debate has been extensively addressed in global trials and registries, there is a scarcity of region‐specific data from the GCC. Our study is the first to contribute novel insights by examining the real‐world application of rhythm control strategies within a tertiary center in Kuwait, including patient selection, adherence to dosing guidelines, and access to electrophysiology services. These findings are especially relevant given the unique demographic and healthcare characteristics of the region, including higher comorbidity burden and limited EP infrastructure in certain areas. By identifying key gaps, such as underutilization of catheter ablation and DOAC underdosing, we provide actionable information that may inform local practice improvement and guideline adaptation in the GCC context.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design introduces inherent biases and potentially incomplete datasets. Second, this study was conducted at a single tertiary care center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings, particularly those with different healthcare structures, referral patterns, or patient populations. As such, our results should be interpreted with caution when applied to broader or more diverse populations. Future multicenter studies across the GCC region would be valuable to validate and expand upon these findings. Third, the reliance on electronic medical records introduces variability in data quality, as some patient encounters occurred during a period where medical records were paper‐charted. Consequently, certain clinical variables, such as the exact date of diagnosis, may not have been uniformly documented. Additionally, while inappropriate underdosing of anticoagulants was explored in this study, the rationale behind physician decision‐making could not be fully ascertained. Finally, patient outcomes are limited to a one‐year follow‐up period; thus, clinical events beyond this timeframe could have been missed. Assessment of long‐term sinus rhythm maintenance was not among the predefined objectives of this study, and these data were not systematically collected. Therefore, we were unable to assess the durability of rhythm control across different modalities. Future prospective studies with standardized rhythm follow‐up are needed to address this question.

Conclusion

In this single‐center study from Kuwait, rhythm control strategies for AF were underutilized compared with international standards. Patients receiving rhythm control were generally younger, had lower CHA2DS2‐VASc scores, and were more likely to be managed with electrophysiology specialist involvement. Female patients were less likely to undergo rhythm control, reflecting sex‐based disparities in care that have not been previously described in Gulf populations. In addition, a significant proportion of patients were prescribed DOACs at reduced doses. These findings carry important clinical implications, emphasizing the need for increased physician education on evidence‐based AF management and raising awareness around the benefits of adopting early rhythm control, as endorsed by clinical guidelines Figures 1, 2.
