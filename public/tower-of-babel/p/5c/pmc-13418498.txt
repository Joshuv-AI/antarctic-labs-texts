Outcomes of Helicobacter Pylori eradication on lipid profiles and cardiovascular disease risk: a systematic review, meta-analysis, and meta-regression
Authors: Coana Sukmagautama, Christopher Daniel Tristan, Erlangga Masykur Kynaya, Matthew Aldo Wijayanto, Annisa Aghnia Rahma, Jauza Athifah Hanun, Fathu Thaariq Baihaqy, Irnizarifka Irnizarifka, Sally Aman Nasution, Ari Fahrial Syam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aim:To evaluate the impacts of successful Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) eradication on lipid profiles and cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk.Background:Recent evidence suggests that H. pylori infection increases the risk of lipid impairment and CVD. However, the extent to which H. pylori eradication can reduce these risks remains inconclusive.Methods: Searches were conducted across databases (PubMed, Scopus, and ScienceDirect), along with website and citation searching, to identify studies before March 15, 2025. Studies assessing the effect of H. pylori eradication on lipid profiles and CVD risk were included. Meta-analysis with subsequent subgroup, meta-regression, and sensitivity analysis was performed using RStudio.Results: A total of 24 studies were included. Successful H. pylori eradication significantly improved LDL levels (MD: -2.46 mg/dL; -4.70 to -0.22; p = 0.03) and HDL levels (1.83 mg/dL; 0.51–3.16; p = 0.01). Meta-analyses of TC and TG changes yielded comparable results; however, meta-regression revealed that age was a significant covariate, revealing a trend toward significance in younger patients. Comparison analysis showed that significant results were only achieved in HDL changes between successful and unsuccessful eradication. Qualitatively, H. pylori eradication appeared to confer a beneficial effect on CVD risk, although this was likely indirect and influenced by patient-specific characteristics, including age and comorbidities. Conclusion: H. pylori eradication was associated with modest improvements in lipid profiles, particularly HDL and LDL, and may contribute to reduced CVD risk, especially in younger individuals. Successful eradication may therefore be considered as one of the efforts to enhance lipid profiles and reduce CVD risk.

Introduction

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection is one of the leading causes of global gastric pathologies, affecting nearly half of the population worldwide, with an estimated crude prevalence rate of 48.9% in adults (1). Its impact extends extended beyond localized infections and ulcers, as it has a significant association with malignancies, particularly mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma (2–4). Beyond that, recent studies have also indicated a link between H. pylori infection and an elevated risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), especially coronary heart disease (CHD) (5, 6). This association is hypothesized to involve the expression of the gastric cancer-related cytotoxin-associated gene A (CagA), as evidenced by the presence of anti-CagA antibodies in patients with CHD (7, 8). Additionally, chronic subclinical inflammation associated with H. pylori infection may contribute to the pathogenesis of CVD by causing dysregulation of lipid metabolism, endothelial dysfunction, and compromised plaque stability (8).

Therefore, it is essential to conduct a comprehensive systematic review on the effect of H. pylori eradication on lipid profiles, as these parameters are substantial independent predictors of CVD (9). A recent systematic review by Watanabe et al. assessed changes in lipid profiles following H. pylori eradication (10). However, its results are confounded by the inclusion of studies that did not confirm the success of eradication after treatment and/or did not use standard antibiotic regimens. These limitations may lead to bias in the true impact of confirmed eradication on lipid profiles. Moreover, despite growing evidence supporting a strong association between H. pylori infection and an increased risk of CVD, there is a lack of comprehensive reviews conclusively investigating whether H. pylori eradication may attenuate CVD risk (11). To address these existing gaps, this study sought to evaluate the impact of confirmed successful H. pylori eradication on lipid profiles and CVD risk, thereby providing clearer insights into the cardiovascular benefits of H. pylori eradication.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were based on the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) (12) with the identifier number CRD420251054764.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was carried out on March 15, 2025, using PubMed, Scopus, and Science Direct to identify studies that met the eligibility criteria, with search terms detailed in Table S1. A pair of authors (CDT and EMK) performed title-abstract and full-text screening independently, with any disputes to be arbitrated by a third party (CS). Subsequently, identification via website and citation search was conducted to identify any additional relevant studies.

Definition of H. pylori eradication

Successful eradication was defined as negative H. pylori status based on repeat testing follow-up after receiving the eradication regimen. Unsuccessful eradication was defined as persistent H. pylori seropositivity following eradication therapy, and the non-eradication group consisted of those with H. pylori-seropositivity who did not undergo any eradication regimen. Short-term studies were defined as those that conducted post-eradication follow-up within 12 months of completion of the eradication regimen. In contrast, long-term studies were defined as those with follow-up durations of 12 months or longer.

Eligibility criteria

We conducted a comprehensive search without restrictions on year of publication to identify any studies that met the following inclusion criteria: (1) Randomized controlled trial (RCTs) or observational studies, including cohort, case-control, cross-sectional, or pre-post studies/quasi-experimental studies that investigated CVD risk and lipid profiles in adult participants (aged ≥18 years) following eradication therapy for H. pylori infection; and (2) studies must confirm H. pylori eradication after the antibiotic regimen was employed in the lipid profiles study. In contrast, exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies that administered antibiotic regimens to H. pylori-seropositive patients but did not confirm eradication status with repeat testing during the follow-up period in the lipid profile study; (2) non-observational studies such as letters to the editor, reviews, and case reports/series; (3) irretrievable full text; and (4) non-English studies.

Data extraction and outcomes

The extraction process was conducted by a pair of authors (CDT and EMK). The following data were extracted: (1) first author and year of publication; (2) study site and countries; (3) study design; (4) mean age; (5) modality used in follow-up eradication testing; (6) follow-up duration; and (7) outcomes. The outcomes of this study were divided into two segments. The first segment involves assessing lipid profiles, including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and high-density lipoprotein (HDL). Effects on lipid profiles were evaluated using both pre–post analyses and comparative analyses. In pre–post analyses, changes were assessed by comparing baseline and follow-up lipid values within individuals who achieved successful eradication. In comparative analyses, lipid profile changes were compared between participants with successful eradication and those with unsuccessful or non-eradicated groups. The second segment examines whether H. pylori eradication modifies overall CVD risk.

Quality assessment

The data-reporting quality of the included studies was evaluated using two tools: the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for RCTs and observational studies (13), and the quality assessment tool from the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NIH) for pre-post study designs (available at https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health-topics/study-quality-assessment-tools). Studies assessed with the NOS were categorized as having low (0–3 stars), moderate (4–6 stars), or high (7–9 stars) methodological quality. The NIH tool comprises fourteen items designed to identify potential sources of bias. Each item was rated as “Yes,” “No,” or “Other,” with “Other” encompassing “Not Applicable” (NA) and “Not Reported” (NR). A final quality score was calculated for each study. Studies were rated as good quality if more than 80% of the responses were “Yes,” fair if 50–80% were “Yes,” and poor if less than 50% received a “Yes” rating. Quality assessments were conducted by a pair of independent authors (MAW and AAR), with any discrepancies resolved by the verdict of a third party (II).

Statistical analysis

All lipid measurements were standardized to mg/dL to ensure comparability across studies. Values reported in mmol/L were converted using established conversion factors. Measurements reported in mmol/L were transformed using standard conversion factors (×38.67 for TC, LDL, and HDL; ×88.57 for TG). For studies reporting multiple follow-up time points, we predefined the use of the longest available follow-up after eradication to capture the most sustained metabolic effect.

Statistical analysis was done using RStudio version 4.4.1 (Posit PBC, Boston, United States) using the packages ‘meta’ and ‘metafor’. Considering the variability between studies, a random-effects model was employed. All meta-analyses, subgroups, and meta-regressions used the Paule-Mandel estimator, Hartung-Knapp adjustment, and z-type confidence interval distribution. The generic inverse-variance method was employed, with the mean difference (MD) as the primary effect size for the pre-post lipid alteration. If a study reported an MD with its confidence interval (CI), the standard error was calculated from the CI using the standard formula (SE = (upper limit - lower limit) / (2 x critical value)). If only baseline and follow-up MD and standard error (SE) were available, SE (change) was computed using the change-score formula with an imputed baseline–follow-up correlation of r = 0.6.

A pairwise meta-analysis using MD effect sizes was performed to compare the change from baseline to follow-up between the successful eradication group and the unsuccessful or non-eradicated groups, for estimating a difference-in-differences effect. Heterogeneity across all meta-analyses was assessed using I2, with values <25%, 25-50%, and >50% indicating low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. An exploration of heterogeneity was conducted using subgroup analysis and meta-regression. Potential confounders meeting the Cochrane criteria were analyzed. Subsequent sensitivity analyses, including leave-one-out and cumulative effect analyses, were employed.

Results

Search results, baseline characteristics, and risk of bias

From the initial 960 records, title-abstract screening and duplicate removal yielded 33 full-texts. Thirteen were excluded for not including eradication follow-up, having incomplete data, having duplicated populations, reporting incorrect outcomes, not using a standard antibiotic regimen, and including populations with unsuccessful eradication. Subsequently, additional website and citation searching resulted in the inclusion of four additional articles. In total, twenty-four articles were included in the present analysis (5, 14–36), with a detailed flowchart in Figure 1. Across these studies, mean ages ranged from 32.8 to 70.2 years, encompassing high-risk CVD patients (type 1 DM, severe hyperlipidemia, hemodialysis), patients with gastrointestinal disease, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, and general check-up populations.

Twenty-one studies (8,023 patients) contributed to the quantitative analysis of lipid profiles (14–20, 22, 23, 25–36). The non-invasive diagnostic tests included the urea breath test, stool antigen test, or serological antibody testing. Invasive testing was performed using an endoscopy-based modality, utilising either the rapid urease test, histological examination of a stained gastric tissue sample, or bacterial culture using a gastric tissue sample. The effect of H. pylori eradication on lipid profiles

On the pre-post effect across four surrogate markers of lipid profiles, the statistical differences were observed only for LDL and HDL. H. pylori eradication was associated with a significant reduction in LDL (MD: -2.46 mg/dL; 95% CI: -4.70 to -0.22; p = 0.03; Figure 2). This effect appeared to be notably influenced by the inclusion of the study by Gen et al., as omitting this study in the leave-one-out analysis attenuated the significance (Figure S15) (36). H. pylori eradication also significantly increased HDL (MD: 1.83 mg/dL; 95% CI: 0.51–3.16; p = 0.01; Figure 3). On cumulative sensitivity analysis, both the LDL- and HDL-related effects were reversed in the study with the largest sample size (Wang et al.) (Figure S16 and S22) (29).

In the comparative meta-analysis, H. pylori eradication was associated with a significant increase only in HDL levels within the successfully eradicated group when compared to the unsuccessful group (MD: 10.18 mg/dL; 95% CI: 3.27–17.08; p = 0.01). However, leave-one-out analysis indicated that this effect was largely driven by the studies of Kanbay et al. (20) and Adachi et al. (14), suggesting potential confounding by individual study influence. The summary of the meta-analysis was available in Table 3.

Subgroup analysis and meta-regression of lipid profile outcomes

The explanatory analysis of heterogeneity with subgroup and meta-regression of lipid profiles revealed several significant findings. In country-level covariates, studies from Turkey showed greater changes in LDL and HDL, while studies from South Korea demonstrated stronger effects on TC, LDL, and HDL. On the HDL effect, the long-term subgroup found an alteration of significance, implying that the impact of H. pylori eradication on HDL may be transient and limited to shorter follow-up durations.

Meta-regression incorporating four covariates (follow-up duration, mean age, body mass index (BMI), and male proportion) was performed to explore potential sources of effect attenuation. Among these covariates, statistically significant associations were observed only for patient age for changes in TC (β = 0.40; p = 0.01) and TG (β = 0.91; p = 0.01). Younger patients exhibited greater reductions in TC and TG, whereas older patients demonstrated an attenuated effect. This finding suggests that although the initial pooled pre–post effects on TC and TG appeared comparable, age was a modifying factor, with significant benefits observed in younger populations. The complete summary of subgroup analysis and meta-regression was available in Tables S5 and S6, and a bubble plot of significant meta-regression was depicted in Figure 4.

The effect of H. pylori eradication on CVD risk

H. pylori eradication effects on CVD risk were reported in four studies (Table 2) (5, 21, 24, 34). Wang et al. reported a lower 11-year pooled incidence of CHD in patients receiving eradication therapy compared to the non-eradicated group. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed significantly lower cumulative CHD rates in patients under 65 who received eradication therapy (p = 0.03), while no benefit was seen in older patients (p = 0.19) (5). Regarding the effect on recurrence of CHD, Elizalde et al. found that non-successful eradication was associated with a higher rate of recurrence compared to successful eradication at 6 months (35% vs 10%; p = 0.01) and 12 months (55% vs 10%; p = 0.01). Multivariate Cox proportional hazards analysis revealed that unsuccessful eradication was an independent predictor of recurrence (RR 3.07; 95% CI: 1.35–6.98; p = 0.007), highlighting that successful eradication plays a critical role in reducing CHD risk (34).

However, a contrasting result was observed by a prospective cohort from Korea, which reported no statistically significant reduction in CVD risk (defined as angina pectoris or myocardial infarction) following successful eradication compared to unsuccessful eradication (OR 1.21; 95% CI: 0.67–2.19; p = 0.51). Multivariate analysis revealed that age, sex, body mass index, smoking status, and TG levels were key confounding factors influencing this relationship (24). For long-term follow-up (>10 years) cardiovascular death outcomes, risk reduction was observed, although it did not reach statistical significance, as reported by Kim et al. (HR 0.76; 95% CI: 0.57–1.01; p = 0.058) (21).

Overall, although there appeared to be a beneficial impact of H. pylori eradication on CVD risk, an apparent discrepancy was observed in the findings of four studies. These findings suggest that the potential CVD risk reduction of H. pylori eradication is likely indirect and may depend on patient characteristics, including age and comorbidities.

Discussion

A growing body of evidence suggests that H. pylori infection may increase lipid profile impairment and the risk of CVD (37, 38). However, the extent to which H. pylori eradication can improve lipid profiles and reduce the risk of CVD remains inconclusive (10, 11). Our study provides comprehensive pooled evidence indicating that the cardio-lipid effect of H. pylori eradication was modest, with significant value for LDL and HDL.

A plausible explanation for the association between H. pylori infection and dyslipidemia involves a chronic inflammatory response that disrupts lipid metabolism (39). The presence of lipopolysaccharides in the cell walls of H. pylori triggers the release of high levels of cytokines, particularly tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin-6, which inhibit lipoprotein lipase activity, thereby reducing peripheral TG clearance (40). In parallel, these cytokines promote hepatic very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) synthesis, further contributing to hypertriglyceridemia and elevated LDL levels. Collectively, these mechanisms result in an atherogenic lipid profile characterized by reduced HDL and increased total cholesterol, LDL, and TG (39). Therefore, it was logical that lipid improvement would occur following eradication; however, our analysis demonstrated that such benefits were modest and strongly influenced by study heterogeneity and population-specific factors. Importantly, these modest biochemical improvements may still have downstream clinical relevance, given the central role of dyslipidemia and inflammation in atherogenesis. Reductions in atherogenic lipoproteins and pro-inflammatory cytokine signaling may contribute to improved endothelial function and plaque stability, thereby potentially attenuating atherosclerotic progression.

Our analysis also captured country-based disparities in TC, LDL, and HDL response following eradication. In subgroup analyses of TC, LDL, and HDL, studies from South Korea appeared to demonstrate a more favorable lipid response, which may be explained by several factors. First, the effect was thought to be related to regional dietary pattern as South Korea characterized by a relatively low-fat dietary pattern compared with Western populations (41); consequently, the beneficial effects of H. pylori eradication on lipid parameters appeared more pronounced during follow-up. Second, studies have demonstrated that CagA-positive H. pylori strains play a pivotal role in escalating impairment of lipid profiles and CVD risk through intense inflammatory responses (42). In a subgroup of South Korean population studies, we hypothesized that it may be driven by a relatively lower prevalence of CagA-positive strains. Although direct evidence on CagA prevalence in South Korea is limited, H. pylori seropositivity there is around 40–50%, and may include fewer virulent strains compared to populations with higher CagA prevalence (43, 44).

Another notable confounding factor was the timing of follow-up lipid testing. In the subgroup where the effect was more pronounced than in the pooled meta-analysis, the follow-up period in the studies within that subgroup was shorter. In particular, in the HDL and LDL subgroup analysis, both Turkish studies conducted lipid testing at 3 months, resulting in a more pronounced effect (20, 36). Moreover, although follow-up timing was a non-significant variable on meta-regression, subgroup analysis of HDL changes found a loss of significance in long-term follow-up (>12 months) studies. This discrepancy may reflect the differential influence of time-dependent factors on lipid dynamics. Specifically, longer follow-up periods are more likely to capture the cumulative effects of lifestyle interventions, aging, and evolving comorbidities, including changes in diet, body weight, medication use, and overall metabolic status. As these influences accumulate over time, the initial lipid improvements observed shortly after H. pylori eradication may become diluted. This also explains why Wang et al. shifted the HDL and LDL changes: their study evaluated lipid levels at a mean follow-up of 17.1 ± 8.4 months in a large population, which might have shifted the initial significance (29).

While the pre–post study analysis demonstrated statistically significant results, the between-group comparison revealed that only HDL levels showed a significant increase in the successful versus unsuccessful eradication group. However, this finding was not robust, as influenced by Adachi et al., which may stem from a significantly higher proportion of smokers in the successful eradication group compared with the unsuccessful group (14).

Both analyses of lipid parameter alteration, provided by meta-regression and CVD risk reduction, confirm the greater cardiovascular benefit in younger individuals following H. pylori eradication. These results stemmed from the pathophysiological differences in vascular aging. In populations under 65, the endothelium retains better elasticity and function, allowing more effective recovery from inflammation-related damage. In contrast, older adults often exhibit irreversible vascular changes due to aging, which limits their responsiveness to infection clearance, thus resulting in a less beneficial impact (5, 24, 45, 46). This explanation may also explain the leave-one-out effect of LDL change, whereas excluding Gen et al., which corresponds to the youngest mean age (32.8 ± 6.5 years), attenuated the initial significant value (36).

Comorbidities may also confound the observed age–benefit relationship. Kim et al., who observed a comparable trend in results of CVD risk reduction, were known to include all hypertensive patients at baseline. This result may not be Generalizable, since the propensity of developing CVD was higher (21). As older individuals are more likely to have accumulated comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, the net cardiovascular benefit from eradication may be attenuated in this group (5, 24). Therefore, younger patients with fewer irreversible vascular changes and lower comorbidity burden are more likely to benefit following eradication.

This review was the first systematic review to specifically examine the impact of H. pylori eradication on CVD risk, filling a critical gap in the existing literature with large-scale data, allowing for a broad and diverse pool of studies. Additionally, we employed a comprehensive methodology, carefully considering various confounding factors, to provide a more nuanced understanding of the effect of H. pylori eradication. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we were unable to perform a quantitative assessment of CVD risk, which limits the ability to draw statistical conclusions regarding the effect of H. pylori eradication on cardiovascular outcomes. Second, we were unable to fully explore potential confounders in the comparisons between successful and unsuccessful eradication and between successful and non-eradicated cases, which restricts the depth of this analysis. Third, important biological modifiers such as H. pylori virulence factors (e.g., CagA status) could not be evaluated due to limited reporting across included studies. Fourth, substantial heterogeneity was observed in several analyses, which may reflect differences in study design, populations, follow-up duration, and background metabolic risk, thereby limiting the generalizability of the pooled estimates. Finally, potential publication bias cannot be excluded, as the majority of included studies were conducted in East Asian populations, and several cohorts lacked a randomized design, which may introduce selection bias and limit causal inference.

Looking ahead, H. pylori screening should be mandatory, as recommended by ACG (3). Following initial screening, eradication therapy should be prioritised for H. pylori-positive individuals in high-risk CVD groups following the established treatment regimens outlined in current guidelines. Future studies should explore the impact of H. pylori eradication on specific CVDs, such as the incidence of heart failure, peripheral arterial disease, and other CVDs. Studies are also needed to determine the effect of eradication across different racial groups, particularly those of Black African ancestry, where the prevalence of CagA-seropositive strains is higher (47). Additionally, the cost-effectiveness of early H. pylori screening and eradication as part of routine CVD risk management should also be evaluated, especially in terms of its potential to reduce long-term healthcare costs related to CVD. Addressing these research gaps will provide strong evidence for incorporating H. pylori eradication into one of the critical efforts in lowering CVD risk.

Conclusion

Our meta-analysis showed that H. pylori eradication substantially improves lipid profiles, particularly HDL and LDL levels, and may contribute to a reduction in CVD risk. The magnitude of benefit appeared partly indirect and was influenced by age and the burden of comorbidities, suggesting important patient-specific factors. Nevertheless, H. pylori eradication should therefore be considered as one of the efforts to improve lipid profiles and CVD risk in H. pylori-seropositive individuals, especially those at high CVD risk.
