Exploring the Perception of Urinary Catheter Use in Older Surgical Patients: ADLs Matter
Authors: Hao‐Mei Tung, Chuan‐Hsiu Tsai, Anthony Jeng, Chien‐Hui Ou, Chia‐Ming Chang, Fang‐Wen Hu, Tzu‐Jung Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study aims to assess the perception of urinary catheter use in older patients undergoing urologic and orthopaedic surgery and to explore the associated factors, specifically functional independence and knowledge.DesignA cross‐sectional research design.MethodsPatients were eligible if they were aged 65 and older, had received urological or orthopaedic surgery, were able to communicate independently, and had a urinary catheter. Data collection included demographics, voiding function history, health conditions, and the knowledge and perception of urinary catheter use. Multiple regression analysis was employed to investigate the variables associated with the perception of urinary catheter use.ResultsA total of 204 older patients were enrolled. The mean score of perception was 41.8 ± 2.7 (range 13–65). Lower perception scores (indicating a preference for catheterization) were observed for items related to mobility difficulties and incontinence. Multiple regression analysis revealed that older patients with greater independence in activities of daily living (ADLs) and better knowledge were significantly more likely to have a positive perception (i.e., recognizing the benefits of catheter removal and its risks).ConclusionOlder surgical patients' perceptions of urinary catheter use are significantly shaped by their ADL status and knowledge levels. Although catheters are often viewed as a convenience for mobility and incontinence, such perceptions frequently misalign with evidence‐based safety standards. To optimize perioperative care, it is imperative to implement structured preoperative education and shared decision‐making frameworks that prioritize function‐based urinary management.Patient or Public ContributionThis study incorporates older patients' perspectives by assessing their perceptions and knowledge of catheterization. The findings advocate for a transition toward function‐based management, where catheter alternatives are tailored to individual ADL status. By aligning clinical practice with patients' functional needs and informed preferences, healthcare providers can empower patients and reduce the risk of iatrogenic harm.

Introduction

Urinary catheters are widely used in acute care hospitals, with catheterization rates ranging from 18.6% to 26.0% in hospitalized patients (Gardner et al. 2014; Magill et al. 2014; Shackley et al. 2017). Older patients (aged 65 and older) are more likely to use urinary catheters and are at higher risk of having them placed without clear medical indications, such as neurogenic bladder dysfunction, bladder outlet obstruction, or a need for accurate measurement of urinary output (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2024). Among these patients, 38.3%–52.8% of catheterizations are considered inappropriate (Hu, Chang, et al. 2015; Hu, Yang, et al. 2015). The use of urinary catheters in hospitalized patients is now recognized as a significant risk factor for the development of infectious and noninfectious complications, which may lead to increased morbidity and mortality, especially in older patients (Hu, Chang, et al. 2015; Hu, Yang, et al. 2015; Saint et al. 2018).

Eliminating unnecessary urinary catheterization is the most important goal in preventing catheter‐associated adverse outcomes. However, insufficient attention has been given to surgical patients. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines, the use of perioperative urinary catheters should be limited to surgeries involving the urologic system or nearby structures of the genitourinary tract. Catheters may also be indicated for procedures with anticipated prolonged duration, those requiring large‐volume infusions or diuretics, or when intraoperative monitoring of urinary output is necessary (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2024; Meddings et al. 2014). Schnetz et al. (Schnetz et al. 2025) further noted that urinary catheters were routinely used in patients undergoing total joint arthroplasty in earlier practice, primarily to facilitate fluid management and ease postoperative micturition. However, catheter use was found to be associated with a high incidence of urinary tract infections. Notably, a systematic review reported that 80.0%–87.8% of postoperative urinary tract infections were attributable to urinary catheter use, making them the third most common type of postoperative infection (Mukantwari et al. 2019). Therefore, recent studies have employed the RAND Corporation/University of California Los Angeles (RAND/UCLA) appropriateness method to highlight clinical scenarios for determining the appropriateness of urinary catheterization during and after urologic and orthopaedic surgeries. For instance, in the case of bilateral total knee arthroplasty, catheter removal on postoperative day 1 is deemed appropriate, while delaying removal until postoperative day 2 or later is considered inappropriate for routine hip or knee arthroplasties (Meddings et al. 2019; Skolarus et al. 2019). Meanwhile, understanding older surgical patients' knowledge and perception of urinary catheter use is crucial to developing appropriate interventions that could further limit unnecessary use (Greer et al. 2011; Laan et al. 2020; Quast et al. 2016; Safdar et al. 2016). However, patient perception is not merely a reflection of convenience; it is a complex construct influenced by functional status and health literacy. In the context of perioperative nursing, understanding these perceptions is crucial for advancing shared decision‐making (SDM) and patient‐centered care. According to the Health Belief Model (HBM), an individual's perception of a clinical device can significantly mediate their health‐related behaviours and recovery outcomes.

A few studies have generally shown a positive perception of urinary catheter use in medical patients. Specifically, 89% of patients believed that catheters were not overused, while 68% recognized benefits such as convenience and comfort. Many patients also preferred catheter placement over alternatives like bedside commodes, bedpans, or diapers (Greer et al. 2011). However, these studies highlighted that patients were often unaware of the associated risks and lacked knowledge about alternatives to catheter use (Greer et al. 2011; Laan et al. 2020). Notably, previous studies have focused primarily on medical patients. Given the differences between medical and surgical populations, their findings may not be directly applicable to our target group. Our study specifically examines older patients undergoing urological and orthopaedic surgeries—a population that is generally more vulnerable in terms of mobility and bladder function, and may therefore have distinct perceptions and needs regarding catheter use. The surgical period presents a unique physiological and psychological stressor where the desire for mobility independence—represented by activities of daily living (ADLs)—may conflict with the perceived safety or convenience of a catheter. Moreover, the factors influencing these perceptions remain largely unexplored, and limited research has addressed this issue in older surgical patients. The trend of an aging population has significantly impacted Taiwan's healthcare system, particularly in orthopaedic and urologic surgeries, which together account for 52% and 12% of all surgeries performed on older patients, respectively (Ministry of Health and Welfare 2019). Therefore, this study aims to explore the perceptions of urinary catheter use among older surgical patients and the factors influencing these perceptions, thereby establishing a conceptual foundation for developing targeted preoperative nursing interventions and function‐based urinary management strategies.

Methods

Research Design and Participants

This study employed a cross‐sectional study design and received approval from the Institutional Review Board of National Cheng Kung University Hospital (IRB No. A‐ER‐109‐233). The written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. Participants were recruited from the urological and orthopaedic wards of a tertiary‐care medical center in southern Taiwan. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they were 65 years old or older, had undergone urological or orthopaedic surgery (the most prevalent surgical procedures among older adults in Taiwan), were capable of independent communication, and had a urinary catheter in place. Patients who had a urinary catheter placed before admission were excluded. The sample numbers were calculated by using G power 3.1.9.7. Since there were no relevant studies for reference, the median effect size was set at 0.15. Other estimation parameters included the number of predictors (14), the probability of TypeIerror (0.05) and power 0.95. It was estimated that at least 194 older patients were required for this study.

Measures and Procedure

A research nurse, trained by the corresponding author before data collection, was responsible for data collection. He visited the urological and orthopaedic wards from Monday through Friday from July 2021 to February 2022 to recruit eligible patients. After obtaining written consent, the research nurse conducted the data collection within 48 h after admission including medical records reviewed, and the patients interviewed at bedside or in a private room (depending on the patient's preference). The medical record provided demographic data (age, biological sex, and education level), voiding function history (genitourinary disease and surgery history), and health conditions (treatment diagnosis and the Cumulative Illness Rating Scale for Geriatrics (CIRS‐G)). During the interview, patients answered a question about previous experience with urinary catheter use (Yes/No) and completed the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), the Katz Index of Independence in Activities of Daily Living (Katz ADL), the 5‐item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS‐5), the Short Portable Mental Status Questionnaire (SPMSQ), the Short‐Form Mini‐Nutritional Assessment (MNA‐SF), and the knowledge and perception of urinary catheter use questionnaire. On average, the entire process took approximately 15–20 min per patient.

Cumulative Illness Rating Scale for Geriatrics (CIRS‐G)

The CIRS‐G measures the burden of current and chronic illnesses in older adults. It is an organ‐system‐based rating scale, with the most severe condition occurring in each of 14 organ systems assigned a severity score from no problem (0) to extremely severe (4). The final score is ranging from 0 to 56 (Miller and Towers 1991). The CIRS‐G score has been reported as a predictor for mortality after discharge (Soh et al. 2020).

Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS)

The CFS was originally published in 2005, and it contains seven descriptions to represent different levels of frailty (Rockwood et al. 2005). Later, the CFS was expanded from a seven‐point scale in 2007 to the present nine‐point scale. Patients are graded on a scale from 1 to 9 (score 1 = very fit, 2 = well, 3 = managing well, 4 = vulnerable, 5 = mildly frail, 6 = moderately frail, 7 = severely frail, 8 = very severely frail, and 9 = terminally ill). Preliminary evidence for the good psychometric properties of the CFS has been demonstrated (Pulok et al. 2020). The Chinese translation of the CFS demonstrated satisfactory inter‐rater reliability and criterion validity in older adults in Taiwan. Furthermore, it showed significant correlations with various geriatric assessments, including functional disability, physical performance, hand grip strength, comorbidity, cognition, depression, and nutritional status (Chou et al. 2022).

Katz Index of Independence in Activities of Daily Living (Katz ADL)

The Katz ADL is used to measure the functional levels of ADLs by measuring an individual's ability to perform six daily activities: bathing, dressing, using the toilet, getting up out of a chair, eating, and using incontinence materials. Each activity scores from 0 to 2, with 0 indicating extensive assistance needed, 1 indicating limited assistance, and 2 indicating independence. A total score ranges from 0 to 12, with higher scores indicating more independence in performing ADLs (Katz et al. 1963). The Cronbach's alpha of the Katz ADL was 0.84–0.94 (Reijneveld et al. 2007), and the Chinese version has been used widely in the Taiwanese population.

The 5‐Item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS‐5)

The depression level of older adults was assessed by GDS‐5. The scores of the five items are totaled, and a higher score indicates a higher level of depression (Hoyl et al. 1999). It had satisfactory internal consistency (α = 0.83–0.90) and had a sensitivity of 0.94, specificity of 0.81, positive predictive value of 0.81, negative predictive value of 0.94, positive likelihood ratio of 4.92, and negative likelihood ratio of 0.07 (Rinaldi et al. 2003). The GDS‐5 has been tested in Taiwan and demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, with strong item loadings within the same construct of depression (Li et al. 2021). A cutoff GDS‐5 score ≥ 1 indicated depressive symptoms in this study.

Short Portable Mental Status Questionnaire (SPMSQ)

It contains 10 questions to measure the cognitive function of older adults, with each wrong answer scored as 1 point. Intact mental function is indicated by fewer than two errors, and the scoring is adjusted for education. If a patient has a grade school education or less, one more error is allowed. If a patient has an education beyond the high school level, one less error is allowed in the scoring (Pfeiffer 1975). The Cronbach's alpha of the Chinese version of the SPMSQ was 0.70 (Chou 2003). Haglund and Schuckid (Haglund and Schuckit 1976) was examined the correlation between the SPMSQ scores and the diagnosis of brain damage or dysfunction; the result showed criteria validity was 0.63.

Short‐Form Mini‐Nutritional Assessment (MNA‐SF)

The MNA is composed of 18 items, and it limits usefulness for screening (Vellas et al. 1999). Rubenstein (Rubenstein et al. 2001) developed a six‐item MNA‐SF total score ranging from 0 to 14, and scoring for each part categorizes patients in the following manner: normal or possible malnutrition. The MNA‐SF scores and MNA scores were strongly correlated (r = 0.95); it had a sensitivity of 0.97, a specificity of 1.00, and a diagnostic accuracy of 0.98 for predicting undernutrition. Moreover, the MNA‐SF is effective for classifying the nutritional status of elderly Taiwanese. It shows high consistency with the full MNA and demonstrates strong predictive ability for follow‐up mortality, a key variable reflecting nutritional status (Tsai et al. 2013).

Knowledge and Perception of Urinary Catheter Use

The questionnaire was developed by the research team and modified from previous studies (Greer et al. 2011; Laan et al. 2020; Safdar et al. 2016; Darbyshire et al. 2015). It was developed using a theory‐driven approach based on the HBM (Rosenstock et al. 1988) and aligned with the Knowledge–Attitude–Practice (KAP) framework (Tabatabaei‐Jafari 2022). A construct‐to‐item mapping process was conducted during item generation to ensure theoretical alignment. Items in the knowledge domain were developed to assess patients' understanding of indications for catheterization, potential complications (e.g., catheter‐associated urinary tract infection), self‐care practices and alternative management strategies for urinary catheter use. For the perception domain, items were derived from key constructs of the HBM. Items addressing satisfaction, convenience and preference reflected perceived benefits. Items related to discomfort, restriction and embarrassment represented perceived barriers, whereas items assessing awareness of infection risk reflected perceived severity. Additionally, items regarding communication with physicians about catheter removal were conceptualized as cues to action. This structured mapping process ensured conceptual consistency between theoretical constructs and measurable items.

The scale comprised two domains: knowledge and perception of urinary catheter use. The knowledge domain included 8 items on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The score ranges from 8 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge. The perception domain consisted of 13 items rated on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with 8 negatively worded items reverse‐scored. The total score ranges from 13 to 65, with higher scores indicating a more positive perception of urinary catheter use. Content validity was established through expert panel evaluation by five specialists including two gerontological nursing professors, one urology clinical nurse specialist, one geriatrician and one urologist. The CVI was 0.95, and the scale demonstrated good internal consistency (α = 0.81).

Statistical Analysis

The first author and corresponding author examined the quality and accuracy of the collected data independently before processing and analysing it. The SPSS for Windows, Version 23.0 (IBM Inc., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis. All p values were two‐tailed; p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Descriptive analyses were used to evaluate the demographic factors, voiding function history, health conditions, and urinary catheter use of older surgical patients. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of the data. To measure the variables associated with perception of urinary catheter use, multiple regression analysis was performed. Five key assumptions essential to multiple regression models were tested and met: linearity, homoscedasticity, independence of errors, normality of residuals, and independence of predictor variables. Finally, all variables were entered into the model for adjustment.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 212 older surgical patients were eligible for the study. Six patients declined to participate, and two patients who had a urinary catheter placed prior to admission were excluded. Ultimately, 204 older surgical patients were included in the final analysis. The mean age of the patients was 73.3 ± 5.6 years old, 53.4% (n = 109) were male, and 54.9% (n = 112) had an education level below elementary school. Of the 204 patients, 100 (49.0%) had a genitourinary disease history, and 61 (29.9%) had a genitourinary surgery history. Most of them (54.9%) had previous experience with urinary catheter use. Regarding their health conditions, 101 (49.5%) had urological and 103 (50.5%) had orthopaedic treatment diagnoses. On average, their CIRS‐G total score was 7.7 ± 3.9, indicating a moderate level of severity of chronic illnesses. The CFS level was 5.0 ± 1.0, indicating mild frailty. Their Katz ADL score was 10.0 ± 3.1, indicating a high level of independence. Moreover, 15 (7.4%) patients had depressive symptoms, 17 (8.4%) patients had cognitive impairment, and 52 (25.5%) patients were at risk of malnutrition. Table 1 also reports their knowledge and perception of urinary catheter use. The mean knowledge score was 28.5 ± 4.4, which was at a moderate to high level. Their mean perception score was 41.8 ± 2.7 (with the total score of perception of urinary catheter use ranging from 13 to 65).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that older patients undergoing urologic and orthopaedic surgery had a mean perception score of urinary catheter use of 41.8 ± 2.7 (total score range 13–65). Lower perception scores were observed in items such as (1) I prefer a urinary catheter to a bedpan or diaper when I have difficulty in mobilization, (2) I prefer a urinary catheter to alternative methods, and (3) I prefer a urinary catheter when I have urinary incontinence. Importantly, we also found that older patients' ability to perform ADLs and knowledge of urinary catheter use were associated with their perception, suggesting that perception is not merely a passive reflection of knowledge deficits but represents a modifiable target within perioperative care.

Patients with better ADL performance may perceive urinary catheterization differently because greater functional independence is often accompanied by higher self‐efficacy. According to Bandura's self‐efficacy theory, individuals who believe they are capable of performing health‐related tasks are more likely to feel confident managing toileting needs without relying on invasive devices (Bandura 1977). This correlation warrants a more nuanced interpretation beyond mere functional status. It is plausible that patients with higher functional independence possess greater self‐efficacy and are more actively engaged in their recovery trajectory, viewing the catheter as a manageable clinical tool rather than a source of dependency. Conversely, those with lower ADL scores may perceive the catheter as a compensatory necessity for their mobility limitations, potentially masking the associated risks. This finding suggests that patient activation and functional baseline are critical determinants in how surgical patients weigh the convenience of a catheter against its potential complications. In contrast, patients with limited ADL capacity may view catheterization as a compensatory strategy that reduces physical burden, thereby shaping their perception of its necessity.

From a shared decision‐making perspective, clinical decisions should involve bidirectional information exchange and active patients' participation (Charles et al. 1997; Elwyn et al. 2012). Patient's understanding of the risks, benefits, and alternatives of urinary catheter use is essential to meaningful engagement in perioperative decision‐making. When patients lack adequate knowledge, their participation may be limited, potentially leading to passive acceptance of catheterization rather than informed choice. Previous studies have also shown that many patients are unaware that catheters can cause urinary tract infections and are not informed about alternative methods for managing urinary output (Greer et al. 2011; Laan et al. 2020; Quast et al. 2016). Similarly, Safdar et al. (Safdar et al. 2016) found that 75% of patients felt they had not received adequate education regarding the consequences of urinary catheter use. Additionally, 100% of patients reported that alternative methods were not discussed, and 65% felt they had not been adequately informed about the risks associated with urinary catheters. These findings highlight the need for improved patient education regarding urinary catheter use, its risks, and available alternatives.

Consistent with prior research, our results indicate that although overall preference scores were modest, patients may still perceive catheterization as a convenient option under conditions of mobility limitation or incontinence. This suggests that situational factors, rather than generalized preference, may influence acceptance of catheter use. Surgery induces a physiological stress response, including catabolic hormone secretion, decreased anabolic hormone levels, impaired pulmonary function, increased cardiac workload, fatigue, pain, and immunosuppression (Desborough 2000), all of which contribute to post‐operative functional decline therefore older patients are at higher risk of ADL deterioration (Zhang et al. 2020), particularly in tasks such as rising from a chair, toileting, and managing incontinence. A key clinical challenge is to identify personalized urinary management alternatives (e.g., portable urinals, bedside commodes, adult diapers, bedpans, or external devices such as condom catheters) that align with individual ADL capacities.

These findings have clear implications for perioperative care. Specifically, structured catheter education should be integrated into preoperative nursing assessments. Functional status assessment, such as ADL performance, may serve as a practical screening tool to guide individualized urinary management strategies rather than relying solely on surgical diagnosis. To bridge the gap between perception and evidence‐based practice, nursing protocols should transition toward function‐based urinary management—prioritizing catheter alternatives (e.g., portable urinals or bedside commodes) tailored to a patient's ADL status rather than surgical diagnosis alone. Furthermore, at a system level, integrating patient‐centered metrics such as informed preference and shared decision‐making into catheter quality programs will ensure that perioperative care is both clinically safe and aligned with the functional needs of the older surgical population, providing a more comprehensive evaluation framework for health system leaders and policymakers.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single hospital, which may limit generalizability. Future research should be conducted across different clinical settings, countries, and cultures to enhance the external validity of the results. Second, due to the cross‐sectional design, causal relationships cannot be established, and changes in perception over time could not be captured. The duration of catheterization and the timing of survey administration relative to the surgical procedure may influence perceptions; while data were collected within 48 h of admission to maintain consistency, the potential influence of cumulative catheter days on patient perception remains a factor for further investigation. Third, the use of a single numerical value to represent patients' perceptions of urinary catheter use may reduce the depth of interpretability. Due to limitations in the study design and the available data, a more detailed analysis or stratification of perception scores was not feasible. Further studies are recommended to adopt a mixed‐methods approach to explore the multidimensional meanings behind perception scores, specifically investigating psychological barriers to accept catheter alternatives and provide richer, context‐specific insights. Fourth, some variables (e.g., voiding history) were extracted from medical records without standardized validation, introducing potential misclassification bias. Finally, given the limited prior research on determinants of urinary catheter perception, additional factors may not have been captured. Future research could examine whether improving knowledge shifts perceptions or reduces catheter duration and investigate how clinician communication and education strategies influence patient acceptance of alternative urinary management methods. Longitudinal and mixed‐methods designs would provide richer insights into how perceptions evolve and inform interventions to enhance patient‐centered perioperative care.

Conclusion

This study underscores that older surgical patients' perceptions of urinary catheter use are significantly shaped by their functional independence and knowledge levels. While catheters are often viewed as a convenient solution for mobility and incontinence challenges, such perceptions frequently misalign with evidence‐based safety standards. To optimize perioperative care, it is imperative to implement structured preoperative education and shared decision‐making frameworks that prioritize function‐based urinary management. By aligning clinical practice with patients' functional status and informed preferences, healthcare providers can reduce unnecessary catheterization and mitigate the risk of iatrogenic harm in the older surgical population.
