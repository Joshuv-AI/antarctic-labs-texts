Gender and age differences in hand disinfection behaviour during the COVID-19 pandemic: field data from Swiss retail stores
Authors: Frauke von Bieberstein, Anna-Corinna Kulle, Stefanie Schumacher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo inform about behavioural gender and age differences in compliance with hand hygiene recommendations during the COVID-19 pandemic in Switzerland.DesignObservational study (24–25 June and 1–3 July 2020); validation study (4 December 2020); gender and age group estimated by observers.SettingObservations occurred in five supermarkets.ParticipantsAll customers entering the supermarkets during a 3-hour period per day (n=8245 main study) and during 1 day (n=1918 validation study).Main outcome measuresWe observed whether people disinfected their hands.ResultsThe study reveals considerable differences with respect to gender and age: For women, 58.7% (95% CI 57.3 to 60.0%) were disinfecting their hands vs 50.4% (95% CI 48.6 to 52.2%) of men. With respect to age, we identified a steep increase across age groups up to the age of 60 years and older with 35.5% (95% CI 30.7 to 40.5%) of youth (12-17 yrs) disinfecting their hands, 50.5% (95% CI 49.1 to 51.9%) of adults (18-59 yrs) vs 69.0% (95% CI 67.1 to 70.9%) of the golden age group (60-74 yrs) and 67.2% (95% CI 61.1 to 72.9%) of people older than 75 years. The validation study confirmed these differences.ConclusionsThe study documents substantial differences in hand hygiene compliance between gender and age groups. This should be considered in the design of protective measures to ensure clean and safe hands.Keywords: INFECTIOUS DISEASES, COVID-19, Public health, Observational Study, PREVENTIVE MEDICINE

Introduction

There have been over 775 million confirmed cases of and more than 7 million deaths globally from the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) as of July 2024.1 Since the onset of the pandemic, the WHO has recommended, among other important procedures, hand hygiene as an effective low-cost measure for the public to reduce the spread of SARS-CoV-2.2 As a result, governments worldwide issued hand hygiene recommendations to fight the pandemic.3,6 High levels of compliance are necessary for these measures to be effective and researchers have developed interventions to promote hand hygiene.7 However, survey evidence suggests substantial differences in self-reported compliance with hand hygiene guidance depending on gender and age.8 9 Understanding these demographic factors that influence compliance remains vital today not only for preventing future pandemics but also for designing effective measures during emerging threats.

Thus far, we only have limited knowledge of actual hand hygiene behaviour for different age groups and genders during the pandemic. Survey research on self-reports of hand hygiene during the COVID-19 crisis can provide important indications: Two large representative surveys conducted in the United States found that men reported less frequent hand hygiene during the COVID-19 pandemic compared with women.8 9 This difference is mostly in line with survey evidence from other countries.10 11 With respect to age, it became clear early on in the pandemic that the risk of severe COVID-19 infection rises significantly with increasing age.12 13 The empirical evidence on potential compliance differences between age groups is less clear, but first studies revealed important distinctions too, with younger people reporting less frequent hand hygiene compared with older individuals8 9 and older people reporting a higher agreement with different general preventative measure recommendations.14

These demographic differences align with broader research on preventive health behaviours. Engagement in screenings and physician check-ups generally increases with age,15 16 while younger adults tend to exhibit more risk-taking behaviours16 and less preventive health behaviours.17 Women typically report higher engagement in preventive care than men,15 18 though this gap narrows when underlying health conditions are considered.18 Together, these findings highlight that demographic factors systematically shape health behaviours and underscore the importance of examining age- and gender-specific patterns in hand hygiene compliance during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Furthermore, although surveys represent an important way to advance knowledge, research in fields such as patient behaviour suggests that respondents tend to overestimate their own readiness to perform (socially) desirable behaviours.19 20 Related studies on compliance in the context of COVID-19 have indicated a similar pattern.21 22 Therefore, it is important to complement survey research with studies that observe actual behaviour. Most studies of actual behaviour conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic stem from hospital settings. Two studies on actual hand hygiene behaviour based on data from US hospitals showed that hand hygiene first increased substantially during the pandemic and later decreased.23 24 However, as these studies used automated measurements by disinfection dispensers, they could not report on gender and age. A study in Germany that investigated disinfection behaviour by healthcare workers also showed an increase in hand hygiene during the pandemic.25 In this study, most participants were female, and gender and age differences were not reported. A study in the UK observed hand hygiene pre and post ‘freedom day’ in supermarkets, bus stops and other transport locations and found the rates to be highest in supermarkets.26 Gender and age differences were not reported. A field experiment with the goal to increase hand hygiene among hospital visitors noticed in one of their treatments a large gender difference with 53.3% of the women using the hand sanitizer compared with 39.7% of the men.27

The aim of this study is to provide insights into compliance with hand hygiene regulations during the COVID-19 pandemic for different age groups and genders. We present the results of a field study conducted in June and July 2020 in five stores of a large Swiss retail chain. Unobtrusive observers captured the actual hand disinfection behaviour of customers entering the store, including estimates of the customer’s gender (inferred from gender expression), and age group. Furthermore, we present the results of a validation study conducted in December 2020 in one of the five stores.

Methods

Study setting and design

Following government regulations, hand disinfection dispensers were installed in the entrance areas of supermarkets and all other stores in Switzerland in March 2020. At the time of the study, customers visiting a supermarket were encouraged to disinfect their hands through a poster by the Swiss Federal Office of Public Health, recommending increased hand hygiene as one measure to contain the crisis. We collected data on customers’ hand hygiene behaviour in five Swiss supermarket stores over 5 days in June and July 2020 (June 24, June 25, July 1, July 2, July 3) during a 3-hour period per day. At the point in time of the measurement, the pandemic was in a relatively mild state, with most lockdown restrictions in Switzerland loosened and generally low infection and death numbers.28

Patient and public involvement

The headquarters of the supermarket chain was involved in the selection of the stores and the communication with the store managers. Patients were not involved.

Ethics

The project was carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Business Administration, Economics and Social Sciences of the University of Bern (approval obtained 3 June 2020 – serial number 072020). The Ethics Committee waived informed consent, as otherwise the study could not have been conducted in supermarkets. Hand disinfection was already in place in all stores prior to our study and customers remained free to decide whether to use it. No personally identifiable information was collected.

On-site data collection

Unobtrusive incognito observers captured customers’ hand disinfection behaviour. The stores for this study were, among other criteria, selected so that there was a spot for unnoticed unobtrusive observation, only one entrance to observe and a maximum average number of customers for any given point in time so that the observers would be able to capture all incoming customers. The observer manually captured hand disinfection behaviour during a 3-hour period per day on a paper sheet for each hour, indicating the incoming person’s behaviour (disinfecting hands, yes or no) and estimating the person’s gender and age. Gender estimates were derived from gender expression, as interpreted by observers. Gender was captured as binary (female or male). Age was also estimated based on visual appearance and recorded in four categories: youth (12–17 years), adult (18–59 years), golden (60–74 years) and old (75 years and older). In addition, the day of the week and the store were recorded as control variables. The observation data captured on paper were transferred to an Excel spreadsheet. The correct transfer was checked by three people independently.

All six observers took part in a 90 min preparatory session. This session included going through detailed run-sheets, reviewing the layout of each store and practising how to fill in the observation sheet. In addition to the preparatory session, the observers were visited on site by the same person from our research team, who joined them for 30–60 min of observation. In case of questions, observers were able to call or text the research team.

Validation study

To ensure the validity of the study’s findings over time, data were collected a second time, in December 2020 on 1 day for 9 hours in one of the five stores in the study sample. At that point in time, Switzerland was in a more severe pandemic state compared with the summer, with lockdown measures in place and a high and increasing number of daily COVID-19 infections and related deaths.28 29

Statistical analyses

All analyses were conducted using STATA. As all variables in this study were categorical, differences between the variables were investigated using two-sided χ2 tests. As the outcome variable is bounded [0,1], we report results from logistic regression analysis. Age group, gender, a control variable for day of the week and store dummies were included as independent variables. For ease of interpretation, we report average marginal effects (AMEs), which can be directly interpreted as changes in predicted probability (0–1 scale). A full description of the econometric model is provided in the supplementary materials. We conducted a separate analysis for the observations of the validation study. The results are robust to the inclusion of the additional data from December 2020 in the main data set.

Results

Descriptive statistics

In total, 8245 customers were observed across the five field days of the main study. Of these customers, 62.8% were classified as female. Regarding age, 63.6% of observations were classified in the age group adult, 28.5% in the age group golden, 4.7% in the age group youth and 3.1% in the age group old. The female share was similar in the age groups youth and adult, with 60.9% each, and somewhat higher in the age groups golden and old, with 66.7% and 69.5%, respectively.

Non-parametric analyses

The hand disinfection rate across the sample was 55.6%. There were statistically significant differences in hand disinfection behaviour regarding the gender and age groups. As shown in table 1 and figure 1, the overall hand disinfection rate for women is +8.3 percentage points higher than that for men (p<0.001, two-sided χ2 test).

With regard to age, we observed substantial differences in hand disinfection behaviour between the four groups. As table 1 and figure 2 show, people in the two oldest age groups disinfected their hands significantly more often than younger adults (+18.5 percentage points for the golden age group; +16.7 percentage points for the old age group, p<0.001 two-sided χ2 tests) and youth (+33.5 percentage points for the golden age group; +31.7 percentage points for the old age group, p<0.001, two-sided χ2 tests), revealing a steep increase from youth to older adults. Pairwise comparisons between the age groups revealed that all but the difference between the golden and old age groups was highly statistically significant (p<0.001, two-sided χ2 tests).

Assessing the gender difference for each age group, table 1 shows that the gender difference in hand disinfection behaviour is greatest and highly statistically significant, with + 9.4 percentage points (p<0.001, two-sided χ2 test) in the largest age group adult. The gender differences in the age groups golden and old have the same direction, but are only statistically weakly significant for the golden age group (statistically significant at the <5% level if observations from December are included), and not significant for the age group old. There is no observable difference between the two genders in the age group youth.

When comparing age groups within each gender, all pairwise differences were highly statistically significant (p<0.001, two-sided χ22 tests) except for the comparison between the golden and old age groups, which was not significant for either gender. Among men, the differences between youth and adults (p=0.026, two-sided χ2 test) and between adults and the old age group (p=0.003, two-sided χ2 test) were significant at the 5% level.

Results of multiple logistic regressions for predicting hand disinfection behaviour confirm these findings (see online supplemental table S1a). AMEs (online supplemental table S1a, Model 4) show that women were about 8 percentage points (pp) more likely to disinfect their hands compared with men, and the likelihood of disinfecting their hands increased by 20 pp for adults, 37 pp for the golden age group and 37 pp for the old compared with youth. Additional analyses with interaction terms between gender and age group (see online supplemental table s1b) indicate that the gender gap in hand disinfection varies by age group, with the most pronounced difference among adults and smaller or no differences in the other groups.

Validation study

To examine prevailing age and gender differences at a later point in the pandemic, we gathered additional data in December 2020. The age differences were even more pronounced when the winter validation data were compared with the summer data only from the store of the validation study (see table 2). Results of multiple logistic regressions for predicting hand disinfection behaviour confirm these findings (see online supplemental table S2a). AMEs (Model 2, online supplemental table S2a) show the same pattern as in the main study: women were about 9 percentage points more likely to disinfect than men, while the likelihood of disinfecting their hands increased by 22 pp for adults, 39 pp for the golden age group and 45 pp for the old compared with youth. Additional analyses with interaction terms between gender and age group (see online supplemental table S2b) show that the gender gap was statistically significant only among adults, while no significant gender differences were observed in the other age groups.

In addition, the validation study showed that overall hand disinfection behaviour may have dropped over the 6 month period. We observed an average hand disinfection rate of 44.4% in winter 2020 compared with 55.6% across stores and 61.9% in the store of the validation study in summer 2020 (see table 2). The decline was highly statistically significant (p<0.001, two-sided χ2 tests) for all subgroups except for youth, where the difference was significant at the 5% level (p=0.002, two-sided χ2 test), and for the oldest age group, where no significant difference was observed. However, the reduction in hand disinfection rates over time should be regarded as a preliminary insight that needs further verification, as in the validation study, behaviour was observed only in one store on 1 day.

All our results were robust when we combined the data from the main study (June/July 2020, five stores) and the validation study (December 2020, one store) into one dataset and included an indicator variable for the December wave (see online supplemental tables S3a,b).

Discussion

Hand hygiene was a key element in most national COVID-19 prevention programmes and continues to be very important in preventing the spread of other infectious diseases.30 31 While it is only one of several measures—alongside, for example, the very important measures of mask wearing and self-isolation—it was strongly emphasised by health authorities at the time of our study. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first observational study to capture actual hand hygiene behaviour with a focus on age groups during the COVID-19 pandemic and also the first study with respect to gender in a non-hospital field setting.

For women, we found a statistically significantly higher rate of hand disinfection behaviour of +8.3 percentage points compared with men. This difference in actual behaviour is in the same direction, but even higher compared with differences identified in surveys from the United States. There, +5.6 percentage points more women stated that they often or always use hand sanitisers compared with men8 and +5.3 percentage points more women stated that they wash their hands before eating at a restaurant compared with men.9

With regard to age, we observed a steep increase in the disinfection rate from youth (12–17 years) to younger adults to people aged 60 years and older. People aged 60 years and older disinfect their hands significantly more often than youth (>+ 31.7 percentage points) and younger adults (>+16.7 percentage points). One potential reason for the more compliant behaviour in the age groups 60 years and older might be an accurately higher perceived risk of contracting a severe case of COVID-19.12 13 32 Our findings are consistent with broader evidence showing that preventive health behaviours generally increase with age and that women tend to display higher compliance than men.15 16

The validation study conducted 6 months later identified similar age and gender differences as the main study at a later point in the pandemic. In addition, although the dataset is considerably smaller, it provides some indication that overall hand disinfection behaviour declined for both genders and in all age groups, except the age group 75 years and older, over the course of the pandemic, although the state of the pandemic was more severe in winter 2020 than in the summer of the main study.

These observed differences can be better understood when viewed through established health behaviour frameworks. The Health Belief Model33 34 helps explain age differences, as younger individuals may perceive lower susceptibility and severity of illness, reducing the perceived benefits of hand disinfection. The Theory of Planned Behaviour35 complements this perspective by clarifying how attitudes, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control shape intentions and actions. Higher compliance among women may thus reflect not only stronger risk perceptions but also more positive attitudes toward prevention and stronger normative expectations. Together, these theories underline how risk assessments and socio-psychological factors may interact to produce systematic age and gender differences in health behaviour.

The main limitation of the present study is that all data were recorded manually, which makes the process more error-prone than the automatic measurement of hand disinfection. A large part of literature studying hand disinfection relies on observations27 36 and, thus, faces the same challenges. Manual recording was chosen as this was the only option available at the time of the study that allowed for capturing estimates for gender and age. Gender and age were estimated based on visual appearance. This procedure may have led to misclassification, as gender expression does not necessarily reflect self-identified gender, and age is difficult to estimate reliably, especially near group boundaries. Even for those in the middle of an age range, perceived age may not reflect the actual age. Furthermore, in the large age group of adults (18–59 years), substantial heterogeneity may exist, as health perceptions and routines can differ markedly between younger and older adults. Future research should use a setting where exact ages and genders of a large, representative sample are recorded to enhance accuracy and validity.

Our results also have implications for hygiene promotion. Current campaigns mostly address broad audiences. Tailoring not only the content but also materials and channels may increase effectiveness—for instance, younger people may be better reached via social media (e.g., WHO’s TikTok “Safe Hands Challenge”37).

To keep the recording simple and accurate, the observers did not distinguish between customers entering alone and in groups. Each incoming customer was recorded as an independent observation; therefore, we cannot assess potential group behaviour. Moreover, the study was limited to five stores in the German-speaking region of Switzerland, and there may be geographic differences that cannot be accounted for. It is important to consider that, while our sample represents a broad range of shopping customers, it is not a representative sample. We were also not able to capture additional demographic characteristics such as socio-economic or educational status, which would allow for a more nuanced understanding of compliance differences and support the design of more targeted public health interventions. In addition, we assume that people who did not use the hand disinfection provided at the store entrance are non-compliant. However, it is possible that some of them could have opted to use their own hand disinfection gel before entering the store, which would result in an underestimation of true compliance. In sum, our study makes a statement about observable behaviour when entering a supermarket, and the striking difference observed between genders and age groups needs to be examined in another context to broaden the picture.

The study findings open up several avenues for future research. First, further research would benefit from testing whether the gender and age differences observed in this setting are present in other settings and/or other cultural and geographic environments. Second, future studies could explore the specific mechanisms behind the observed differences. Third, as the findings suggest that compliance with hand hygiene can be improved most in younger age groups and in men, it is important to test how communication can be tailored to increase compliant behaviour in these groups. In conclusion, these findings help understand how hand hygiene compliance differs across gender and age group and thus can help to better design and communicate hand hygiene recommendations.
