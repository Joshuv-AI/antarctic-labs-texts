Prevalence of falls and associated risk-factors among Georgian older adults
Authors: N Pitskhelauri, M Kareli, T Dochviri, N Chikhladze, C Hamann, D Dulf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFalls are one of the leading causes of injury-related morbidity and mortality among older adults worldwide, with over 680,000 fatalities annually. In Georgia, falls are the second leading cause of unintentional-injury related mortality and account for more than half of injury-related hospitalizations. In 2018, a total of 25,103 patients were hospitalized due to injuries in Georgia, and more than half of these cases were fall-related. The mechanisms and risk factors associated with falls are complex. In our study we aimed to identify the key risk factors for falls among community-dwelling Georgian adults aged 65 and older.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included community-dwelling adults aged 65 and older, who visited primary care services in Tbilisi, Georgia, between September 1, 2022, and February 14, 2023. The study employed a two-stage sampling approach. Initially, primary care (PC) services were randomly selected. Subsequently, within the selected PC services, eligible patients were recruited using a convenience sampling method. A structured questionnaire was administered by trained operators. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS v23. Pearson Chi-Square tests were used to assess associations between categorical variables, and binary logistic regression was performed to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsThe study included 1000 participants (Female = 500). Over one-third had fallen at least once in the past 12 months. Females were more likely to report falls (p = 0.01). Participants with neurological diseases had a higher likelihood of falls requiring medical care (OR: 1.39, 95% CI 1.03–1.90). Those with depression, arthritis, imbalance, and muscle weakness were 1.4, 1.6, 1.7, 1.8, and 1.6 times more likely to report a fall. Participants whose homes were crowded with furniture and had carpets were 1.52 (95% CI 1.05–2.34) and 1.43 (95% CI 1.06–1.94) times more likely to fall.ConclusionOur study found that the frequency of falls among older adults of Tbilisi, Georgia aligns with global estimates. Our findings show that being a woman, having medical comorbidities, and exposure to hazardous home environments are associated with a higher risk of experiencing falls, emphasizing the multidimensional nature of the fall risk-factors. Introduction

Falls are a major public health concern and one of the leading causes of injury-related morbidity and mortality among older adults globally. A fall is defined as ‘an unexpected event in which the participants come to rest on the ground, floor, or lower level’’ [1]. Globally, falls are the second leading cause of unintentional injury-related deaths, accounting for approximately 684,000 fatalities each year, over 80% of which occur in low- and middle-income countries, predominantly affecting individuals over the age of 60 [2].

The risk of falling increases significantly with age. Among adults aged 65 and older, the risk of falling at least once a year is approximately 30%. Moreover, 10% of older adults fall at least twice annually, and those who have fallen once have a threefold increased risk of falling again [3, 4]. In addition to fatal outcomes, falls frequently lead to serious non-fatal injuries and long-term disability. In 2021, an estimated 558.8 million people worldwide experienced falls, contributing to 43.8 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and approximately 800,000 deaths [5].

In the European Union, approximately 35,848 fall-related death are reported annually in older adults aged 65 and more, making falls one of the leading causes of unintentional injury-related mortality in this age group [6]. Similar trends have been observed in Georgia, a country in the Caucasus region, where in 2023, falls were also the second leading cause of unintentional injury-related deaths, primarily affecting individuals aged 55 and older [7].

In addition to the health burden, falls impose a significant economic cost. In 2014, 2.8 million people visited the United States emergency departments due to fall-related injuries, with one-third requiring hospitalization [8]. The annual medical cost of fatal and non-fatal falls in the United States is estimated to exceed $50 billion [9]. In Georgia, the incidence of falls is also substantial. A 2018 retrospective study reported an overall injury incidence of 3,042.3 per 100,000 population and 25,103 hospital admissions due to injuries, more than half of which (13,932; 55.5%) were fall-related [10].

The majority of falls occurs in older adults over the age of 65, making them an exceptionally vulnerable age group and confirming that age is one of the major risk factors for falls [10, 11, 12]. Besides the financial burden and the need for long-term treatment, falls in the older adult population have their own peculiarities, such as post-fall anxiety syndrome, which limits a person’s mobility and activity due to the constant fear of falling again, [13, 14] leading to further weakness, abnormal gait, and social deprivation, which in turn increases the risk of future falls, creating a vicious cycle [14] and increasing public health burden of falls.

The mechanism and risk factors related to falls are complex. Scientists suggest dividing fall-related risks into two groups: intrinsic risk factors such as balance and gait abnormalities and extrinsic risk factors such as trips or slips [3]. Others classify risk factors as sociodemographic (age, sex, education), medical (history of falls, poor visual equity, sedentary lifestyle), and environmental (domestic hazards) [15]. In most cases, falls are caused by several identifiable risk factors. A person with clinically confirmed gait or balance abnormalities, including muscular weakness has twice the risk of falling, [4] moreover, studies show that older age, female sex, [16, 17] presence of several comorbidities, [18] usage of multiple medications, [17] depression and other mood disorders [16, 19] are strong predictors of falls among older adults. Other risk factors include dizziness and delirium, decreased visual acuity, cognitive impairment, orthostatic hypotension, arrhythmia, alcohol intake, anemia, severe osteoporosis, decreased daily activity, usage of certain medications such as antihypertensive and anti-inflammatory drugs, drugs-acting on the central nervous system: antidepressants, antipsychotics, anxiolytics, 1st generation antihistamines [14, 15].

Despite the global evidence on the multifactorial nature of fall risk factors, there is a notable lack of region-specific data from Georgia. While several studies have emphasized the importance of identifying and addressing health-related, behavioral, environmental, and social determinants of falls, [14, 20, 21] especially in aging populations, the Georgian context remains understudied. A retrospective epidemiological study from 2018 highlighted that more than half of all injury-related hospitalizations in Georgia were due to falls, with older adults being particularly vulnerable [22]. However, the risk-factors and attitudes towards falls were not assessed. To address this critical knowledge gap, the present study aimed to explore the prevalence and risk-factors of falls in community-dwelling Georgian adults aged 65 and older.

Methods

This was a cross-sectional study conducted between 1 September 2022 and 14 February 2023 among adults aged 65 and older who visited Primary Care (PC) service in the capital city, Tbilisi. Older adults frequently visit PC services for routine check-ups and chronic disease management, making these facilities a practical location to engage with participants aged 65 and older. Conducting interviews in a medical setting ensures a comfortable environment for elderly participants, minimizing stress and maximizing the reliability of their responses.

The study utilized a two-stage sampling approach. On the first stage PC services in each urban area were selected through simple random sampling to ensure geographic representativeness across the city. There are 10 districts in the Tbilisi City.

The list of all PC by districts of city was requested from the Department of Statistics of National Center of Disease Control and Public Health. In Tbilisi there are 203 PC services, in total they serve 1.2 million population which represents about 32.5% of Georgia’s population (as of 1 January 2022). PC Services in each urban district were selected randomly. In selected PCs number of patients ranged from minimum 3,196 to 191,258 patients yearly. These patients, including older adults, typically visit primary care services for a variety of health needs, such as general medical consultations and routine health checks. Secondly, within the 10 selected PC services, eligible patients were recruited using a convenience sampling method.

Study population

The study included patients of 65 years of age and older who visited primary care service in Tbilisi City between 1 of September 2022 and 14 February 2023.

For sample size calculations we used the following formula: N= [Z²XPX(1-p)]/d². This formula was used separately for male and for the female population. For male we used lower precision compared to female, as the proportion of hospitalization is less in male compared to female. For male the precision is ± 1.2% and for female is ± 1.5%. Consequently, the required sample size was estimated to be 500 individuals for each sex.

Participants were stratified into five age groups (65–69, 70–74, 75–79, 80–84, and 85 years and older), with a target sample of 200 respondents per group. A convenience sampling approach was adopted to enable the recruitment of the required number of participants within the available settings. Once the target of 200 individuals in a particular age group was reached, no further participants from that group were included, to ensure equal group sizes and maintain comparability across age groups.

Research instruments

The questionnaire was developed based on a review of the relevant literature and existing validated instruments addressing falls and associated risk factors. (English Version of questionnaire - Annex 1 in supplementary materials). Several questions were replicated from previous surveys which examined the prevalence of falls among older adult populations globally. The questionnaire captured data on socio-demographic characteristics (age, sex, marital status, education level, employment status, living arrangement) and self-reported information on chronic diseases (cardiovascular diseases, neurological diseases, diabetes, anemia), mental health (depression), mobility and functional problems (loss of balance, muscle weakness, arthritis), and sensory impairment (visual impairments). The occurrence and history of falls were evaluated through a series of questions: “have you fallen in the past year?’’, “How many times have you fallen in the past year?”, “have you been injured as a consequence of those falls?’’. The questionnaire also included items assessing self-perceived risk of falling and the presence of environmental risk factors within the home setting.

To assess its face and content validity, three subject matter experts (two geriatricians and one public health specialist) reviewed the items for completeness, clarity, and appropriateness. In addition, the questionnaire was pilot-tested in August 2022 with ten older adults to assess its clarity, comprehensibility, and appropriateness. Pilot respondents understood and answered all questions. No new issues emerged after the pretest, suggesting that the instrument was well understood and acceptable to the target population. However, minor modifications were made to further refine the questionnaire such as adjustments to word order and shortening of certain items, to enhance clarity and improve flow, rather than in response to problems identified during pilot testing. Feedback from both the expert review and the pretest was used to refine and finalize the questionnaire prior to its use in the main study. 10 medical and public health students were trained to administer the questionnaire and collect participants’ consent forms. Each student was assigned to a specific urban district.

Data collection

Data were collected by research staff and trained students; the data collection and interview protocols were standardized. Patients were approached, explained the purpose of the study, asked their age and eligible participants were then invited to participate in a survey at the end of their medical check-up. Individuals who did not speak Georgian (n = 37) and patients with cognitive impairment (n = 16) were excluded. Cognitive status was evaluated by the interviewer during the initial interaction. Trained interviewers assessed participants’ ability to comprehend questions, adhere to instructions, and provide coherent, context-appropriate responses. Those who exhibited notable difficulty in understanding or recalling information, or in following the interviewer’s instructions, were deemed to have cognitive impairment, and were excluded from the study. Although residence in institutional settings was defined as an exclusion criterion, no individuals assessed for eligibility were institutionalized. As anticipated, all participants were community-dwelling older adults, which aligns with the setting of PC services selected for the survey.

Data were collected through interviewer-administered surveys conducted in different urban districts, with interviewers rotating to a new PC service each week. Interviewers read each question to the participants, and each interview lasted approximately 25 min. Interviewers provided explanations as necessary. Data collection was conducted simultaneously at all ten PC services once a week between 9 a.m. and 12 p.m. Data quality was reviewed on a weekly basis, and questionnaires with incomplete responses resulting from participants’ recall difficulties (n = 38) were excluded from the analysis.

Ethical approval

Patients were recruited on a voluntary basis. Written consent was obtained from all the participants. Interviews were conducted in a private atmosphere in the room. Anonymity and confidentiality were ensured by collecting non-identifying aggregate measures. This study was approved by IRB of National Center of Disease Control and Public Health of Georgia (IRB # 2022-073).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 23. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using Pearson’s Chi-Square test, with a p-value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Crude odds ratios (ORs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using binary logistic regression analysis. For this analysis, each independent variable was entered individually into a univariate logistic regression model to assess its association with the outcome variable (having experienced at least one fall in the past 12 months). Categorical variables were dichotomized or converted into dummy variables, as appropriate, with one category used as the reference group. Variables examined included socio-demographic factors: sex, age group, marital status, education level, employment status, and living arrangement; health-related conditions: cardiovascular disease, neurological disease, arthritis, diabetes, visual impairment, anemia, depression, balance impairment, and muscle weakness; and environmental fall-related risk factors at home: presence of carpets, slippery floors, overcrowded furniture, and inadequate lighting.

Results

Participant socio-demographic and fall-related characteristics

The analysis incorporated data from 1,000 completed questionnaires (500 female), out of which more than one-third 319 (31.9%) reported falling in the past 12 months. The majority of participants who fell were female (n = 186, 58.3%) aged more than 75 years old. Among all study participants who reported falling, nearly half were married (n = 137, 42.9%), had university degree (n = 138, 43.3%), absolute majority were actively employed (n = 277, 86.8%) and did not live alone (n = 261, 81.8%) (Table 1).

Out of 319 study participants who reported falling 203 (63.6%) had fallen once, 91 (28.5%) twice, 18 (5.6%) three times and 7 (2.3%) four or more times (Fig. 1) respectively. Regarding fall circumstances, more than half of the falls occurred at home (55.2%), with the bathroom being the most common specific location (31.6%). The majority of falls happened during the day (75.9%). The most frequent activity at the time of falling was walking (51.1%), followed by turning (29.8%), with no significant differences by sex for fall location, time, or movement performed (all p > 0.05) (Table 1).

The majority of study participants sustained mild injuries (n = 180, 56.4%) as a result of fall and there was no statistically significant difference of the severity of injury between men and women (p value = 0.44) (Table 1).

Comparison of fall prevalence and frequency of falls in female and male study participants

Females were more likely to report having fallen than males (Fig. 1), with 37% of female study participants reporting a fall during the last 12 months, while only 27% of male study participants reporting a fall during the same time period (Table 1). Perceived risk of falling

The perceived risk of falling did not differ among the study participants who fell and who did not. Moreover, the number of falls did not significantly influence the perceived risk of falling. However, perceived risk of falling was significantly influenced by the domestic risk factors that increased the risk of falling in all study participants. Most of the study participants who had risk factors at home that increased their risk of falling perceived their risk of falling as medium or high (p value = 0.01), similarly having risk-factors at the workplace was significantly associated with the severity of perceived risk of falling. The majority of participants who reported having fall risk-factors at work evaluated their fall risk as medium or high rather than low (p value < 0.001), while majority of those who reported not having risk factors at their workplace evaluated their risk as low (p value < 0.001) (Table 4).

Factors associated with reported falls

Female participants were 1.64 times more likely to fall compared to males (Odds Ratio 1.64, 95% CI 1.25–2.14). Participants living alone were 1.91 times more likely to experience at least one fall compared to those not living alone (Odds Ratio 1.91, 95% CI 1.31–2.78). Married participants were 0.70 times as likely to fall compared to those who were not married (divorced, widowed, or never married) (Odds Ratio 0.70, 95% CI 0.53–0.91) (Table 5).

Regarding comorbidities, participants with neurological diseases were 1.39 times more likely to fall compared to those without (Odds Ratio 1.39, 95% CI 1.03–1.89). Similarly, those with musculoskeletal conditions, such as arthritis, imbalance, and muscle weakness, were 1.74 (Odds Ratio 1.74, 95% CI 1.32–2.29), 1.84 (Odds Ratio 1.84, 95% CI 1.35–2.50), and 1.62 (Odds Ratio 1.62, 95% CI 1.16–2.25) times more likely, respectively, to have reported a fall compared to those without these conditions. Participants with depression were 1.60 times more likely to fall compared to those without depression (Odds Ratio 1.60, 95% CI 1.10–2.33) (Table 5).

For home environmental risk factors, participants whose homes were crowded with furniture were 1.53 times more likely to fall compared to those without such conditions (Odds Ratio 1.53, 95% CI 1.05–2.23). Similarly, participants with carpets in their apartments were 1.43 times more likely to report falls compared to those without carpets (Odds Ratio 1.43, 95% CI 1.06–1.94) (Table 5).

Discussion

In line with these global estimates, our study found the prevalence of falls in older adults (aged 65 or older) of Georgia to be 32%. Similar results have been observed in the USA, where 27.6% of older adults fall at least once a year, [23] and in European countries where annual fall incidence ranges from 20% to 40% [24]. Specifically, in the United Kingdom and France, the rate is about 30%, and in Germany, it ranges from 25% to 35% [25]. Significant differences in fall incidence, mortality, and the burden of falls across European countries can be influenced by factors such as healthcare systems, reporting practices, and demographics. Overall, the incidence of falls has an increasing trend worldwide largely due sociodemographic, environmental, and medical factors [26].

Older adults, especially those over the age of 75, are generally at a higher risk of falling [26]. However, our study did not find a significant difference in fall prevalence across different age groups. Individuals aged 75 or older were 1,23 more likely to have reported a fall in the past year, but the result was not statistically significant (95% CI 0.93–1.62), which might be due to the limited sample size potentially missing smaller effects. Gender is also an important contributing factor affecting the fall prevalence, globally falls are more common in women, likely due to higher rates of osteoporosis in postmenopausal women, and decreased muscle strength and balance [16, 17, 27]. In line with the global results, our study found that the falls were 1.64 times more likely (95% CI 1.25–2.14) to be reported among women compared to men, confirming that gender is an important factor related to fall occurrence.

Our study confirms the importance of social support, showing that marriage acts as a protective factor against falls among older adults. Participants who were married had 30.5% reduced odds of falling (95% CI 0.53–0.91), compared to those who were divorced, widowed, or never married. This is in line with global findings, Bloch and colleagues, in their systematic review, found that married individuals are less likely to fall [28]. Moreover, in our study the prevalence of falls was also influenced by the number of family members, older individuals who lived alone were almost twice as likely to have reported a fall within the past year compared to individuals living with family members. Bloch and colleagues also mention that the lower fall rates among married individuals or those living with family members could be partly due to increased engagement in physical activities, which increases muscle strength and improves balance, as well as better social support providing regular assistance to older adults [28].

Other well-established factors contributing to increased prevalence of falls in older adults are medical comorbidities. Older adults with multiple chronic health conditions, balance problems, and mobility impairments are at a higher risk of falling [16, 18, 19]. Consistent with these findings, our study indicated that individuals with underlying neurological diseases, depression, arthritis, muscle weakness, and balance impairment were more likely to have reported falling within the past year. Among these conditions, balance impairments, arthritis, and muscle weakness were found to nearly double the odds of having reported a fall within the past year. These results highlight the need for appropriate treatment of underlying medical conditions to decrease fall occurrence among older adults.

The third group contributing to the increased prevalence of falls in older adults is environmental factors and domestic hazards [15]. Key hazards include slippery floors, poor lighting, clutter and obstruction with furniture and objects, carpets, uneven surfaces, stairs and ramps, and lack of handrails [29, 30]. In line with these, in our study we found that individuals whose apartments were overcrowded and who had carpets inside their house had almost 1,5 times increased odds of falling (95% CI 1.06–1.94), confirming that environmental factors play a significant role in the prevalence of falls. Similar results were observed in a study by Rosen, Karin, and Noonan, which found that frequent fall injuries at home occurred at the transition between carpet-rug and non-carpet rug [31].

Limitations and Future Directions

There are many limitations in our study. The interviews were conducted exclusively among community-dwelling older adults residing in Tbilisi, the capital city of Georgia, excluding individuals with cognitive impairment. This limitation may affect the generalizability of the findings to the broader older adult population across the country. Future research should aim to include individuals with cognitive impairment, as well as those living in institutional or assisted-living settings and in rural areas, to ensure a more comprehensive evaluation of fall risk and prevalence within Georgia’s aging population.

In this study, cognitive status was assessed by interviewers as a pragmatic approach to ensure participants were able to provide informed consent and reliable responses. While appropriate for the study context, this method may lack precision. Future research should incorporate standardized cognitive screening tools to more accurately evaluate cognitive function and account for its potential influence on fall risk and related outcomes.

Although formal psychometric testing was not conducted, the instrument was developed through a comprehensive review of relevant literature and evaluated for content and face validity by subject matter experts, as well as pretested with the target population. However, further validation may be necessary to ensure its applicability across diverse settings, urban areas, and population groups in future research.

The reliability of this study depended upon self-reported experience of falls, medical comorbidities, and environmental hazards so it is possible that some of these things may have been under- or over- reported. Future research should consider integrating objective clinical assessments, where feasible, to validate self-reported data and enhance the robustness of findings. Moreover, employing validated screening instruments such as the PHQ-9 or the Geriatric Depression Scale would ensure a more accurate identification of depressive symptoms in older populations.

Moreover, home environmental factors were evaluated in a general manner. Future research employing more comprehensive assessments of the home environment may provide a deeper insight into its relationship with fall risk.

Conclusions

The results of our study showed that the prevalence of falls among older adults of Tbilisi, Georgia aligns with global estimates. The results recapitulate the multidimensional nature of falls in older adults, emphasizing the interplay between sociodemographic, medical and environmental factors. Our findings confirm that women, individuals with several medical comorbidities, and those exposed to hazardous home environments were more likely to have experienced a fall within the past year compared to those without comorbidities or hazardous home environments. Moreover, our study indicated the importance of social support in decreasing fall occurrence. In our study we did not find a significant difference between different age groups, which could be due to the low number of study participants. Future studies with increased numbers of study participants may help to depict any age effects. Falls remain a major public health concern with significant global prevalence, addressing these factors through targeted interventions, including managing chronic health conditions, improving home safety, and enhancing social support, as well as developing fall prevention strategies is essential.
