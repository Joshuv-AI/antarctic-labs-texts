Intergenerational educational expectation discrepancies and adolescent mental health: evidence from China
Authors: Yongxiang Wang, Shiqian Wang, Xinyi Ling, Wenjie Wang, Weiqi Chen, Zeyang Wu, Daili Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn the context of increasingly fierce educational competition and growing concern over adolescent mental health, discrepancies in educational expectations between parents and children have become an important factor in understanding adolescent psychological development. Although previous studies have confirmed the influence of parental educational expectations on adolescent academic and psychological outcomes, few have systematically examined intergenerational discrepancies in educational expectations and their potential nonlinear effects.MethodsUsing baseline data from 19,487 students in the China Education Panel Survey (CEPS), this study constructed an indicator of intergenerational educational expectation discrepancy and examined its association with adolescent mental health. Quadratic regression, U-test procedures, mediation analysis, moderation analysis, and polynomial regression with response surface analysis were used to assess nonlinear effects and underlying family mechanisms.ResultsThe results revealed a significant inverted U-shaped relationship between intergenerational educational expectation discrepancies and adolescent mental health. Psychological well-being was highest when parent–child educational expectations were closely aligned, particularly when adolescents’ own expectations were slightly higher than perceived parental expectations. Excessive discrepancies in either direction were associated with poorer mental health. Family conflict, family interaction, and parental confidence partially mediated this relationship, while parental control amplified the adverse effects of expectation misalignment.ConclusionsThis study highlights the dynamic role of educational expectations within family systems and provides empirical evidence that balanced parent–child educational expectations are important for adolescent mental health. The findings offer new insights for educational sociology, developmental psychology, and family-based interventions aimed at promoting adolescent psychological well-being.Keywords: Parent–child expectation discrepancy, Adolescent mental health, Nonlinear relationship, Family conflict and interaction, Parental control and confidence

Introduction

Mental health issues among adolescents have emerged as a critical challenge confronting both public health and educational sectors globally. A report by the World Health Organization (WHO, 2022) indicates a persistent rise in emotional disorders such as depression and anxiety within the adolescent population. The social consequences of these issues not only affect academic performance but may also extend into adulthood, leading to significant economic and health costs. In East Asian cultural contexts, as exemplified by China, education has long been regarded as a primary channel for families to achieve social mobility [23]. Consequently, parental educational expectations occupy a central position in family education and are considered a key factor influencing adolescents' personality development, academic performance, and psychological well-being [13].

However, in recent years, adolescents' educational experiences have undergone significant changes. With the universalization of compulsory education, the expansion of secondary education, and the rapid transformation of social competition structures, adolescents have developed increasingly clear judgments about their own educational trajectories. Consequently, their educational expectations have gradually evolved into an independent psychological variable distinct from parental judgments [3]. In this context, parental and adolescent educational expectations are not always highly aligned. The discrepancies may reflect not only differences in family resources and values but also intergenerational asymmetries in the understanding of social opportunity structures. Although disparities in educational expectations are highly prevalent in real-world settings, their potential psychological implications and familial significance have not yet received sufficient research attention.

Existing research primarily focuses on two directions. One stream emphasizes the "motivational effect" of parental educational expectations, which can enhance adolescents' academic motivation and developmental aspirations [50]. Another body of research highlights the potential risks of "excessive" parental expectations, such as cumulative stress, diminished self-efficacy, and increased anxiety [30]. However, both lines of inquiry share a common limitation: they treat "parental educational expectations" as a unidirectional force imposed from above, overlooking the fundamentally interactive nature of educational expectations in intergenerational relationships. Parental expectations do not exist in isolation,adolescents' own expectations also contribute to the construction of meaning. The discrepancy between the two may be more explanatory of adolescents' psychological states and developmental trajectories than either set of expectations alone.

A limited number of studies have begun to explore the impact of discrepancies in educational expectations on adolescents' emotional well-being, yet significant gaps remain. For instance, Rutherford [36] found that parent–child differences in educational expectations are associated with emotional distress among adolescents, but the study did not differentiate the magnitude of such discrepancies nor uncover the underlying psychological mechanisms. Furthermore, existing research has yet to address a more fundamental question: might the influence of educational expectation discrepancies be nonlinear? In other words, could a moderate level of discrepancy serve a motivational function, while only excessive differences lead to significant psychological risks? Research on educational motivation and stress has indicated that academic pressure often exhibits an inverted U-shaped relationship [34]. However, this framework has not yet been systematically applied to the study of intergenerational differences in educational expectations.

The contributions of this study are threefold. First, it re-examines the classic variable of educational expectations from an intergenerational interaction perspective, emphasizing “expectation discrepancies” rather than “parental expectation levels,” thereby enriching theoretical discussions in the sociology of education regarding family-student interactions. Second, by introducing nonlinear analysis methods, the study empirically reveals the “optimal range” of educational expectation discrepancies and the psychological risk thresholds, providing a new analytical paradigm for research in this field. Third, this study systematically demonstrates how educational expectation discrepancies translate into adolescent mental health outcomes within family relationships by examining key mechanisms such as family conflict, family interaction, and parental confidence, offering more targeted intervention pathways for educational practice.

Theoretical basis and research hypotheses

Research on educational expectations

The classic Wisconsin model of status attainment explicitly posits that educational expectations have long been regarded as a core social-psychological mechanism linking family background to intergenerational status mobility [39]. From the perspective of Bourdieu's theory of cultural capital, educational expectations are seen as an internalization of class-specific "habitus," reflecting the family's mastery of the rules of the educational system and their subjective anticipation of social opportunities [5]. Meanwhile, the assumption of "relative risk aversion" in rational choice theory suggests that parents' educational expectations are typically shaped by the desire to maintain their existing social standing and to avoid downward social mobility [7].

In addition to intergenerational transmission at the family level, adolescents' own educational expectations are also profoundly shaped by the contemporary environment. In recent years, scholars have gradually integrated macro-structural and micro-dynamic factors, revealing the complex network underlying the formation of adolescents' self-educational expectations. At the macro level, contemporary social adversities, such as social stratification, economic pressure, and inequality in educational resources, constitute the realistic framework within which adolescents assess their future opportunity structures, directly shaping their independent expectations regarding their own educational trajectories [4, 8, 35]. At the micro level, adolescents are not merely passive recipients of family expectations,rather, parenting styles, family investment in education, and the signals adolescents receive regarding their academic abilities during development collectively and dynamically adjust their self-expectations at the level of individual experience [1, 21, 38]. Thus, adolescents' self-educational expectations represent a dynamic process shaped independently by macro-level social adversities, micro-level family interactions, and individual experiences, providing a foundational basis for understanding intergenerational discrepancies in expectations.

Based on the complex mechanisms underlying expectation formation as discussed above, due to the distinct intergenerational positions from which parents and children perceive external environments and internal feedback, their educational expectations often fail to naturally align in reality. Although existing research generally acknowledges that higher educational expectations can promote adolescents' academic performance, it has largely been confined to the perspective of "parental expectations alone" [16, 50], failing to adequately examine the consequences of intergenerational expectation discrepancies. In fact, multiple recent studies, drawing on perspectives such as emotional intelligence and social exclusion, have indicated that adolescents are highly sensitive to the "expectation structure" within their families. Excessively high or misaligned expectations readily trigger negative emotions such as anxiety and depression, exacerbate parent–child conflict [18], and become a psychological burden for adolescents. Collectively, these studies suggest that the impact of educational expectation discrepancies should not be viewed merely as a singular academic stressor, but rather understood within a broader "sociopsychological structural framework." Therefore, this study adopts Identity Control Theory (ICT) as its core theoretical framework, emphasizing the conceptualization of intergenerational expectation discrepancies as expressions of "identity discrepancy," and seeks to explain the mechanisms through which educational expectation discrepancies influence adolescents' mental health from the perspective of identity consistency.

Identity control theory

Identity Control Theory was systematically proposed by Burke and Stets [9]. Its core premise lies in the idea that individuals, during social interactions, continuously compare their internal "identity standards" with the "reflected appraisals" received from significant others, striving to align the two in order to maintain the stability and coherence of their self-identity. When these two elements are congruent, individuals experience more positive emotional states and smoother social interactions. Conversely, when a mismatch occurs, it triggers identity threat, leading to negative reactions such as anxiety, distress, avoidance, or resistance. If such identity inconsistency remains unresolved, it may result in sustained psychological distress and relational tension.

In line with the logic of Identity Control Theory (ICT), the direct source of individuals' emotional and psychological responses is not the objective evaluations of others, but rather the reflected appraisals processed subjectively by the individual. Accordingly, in examining intergenerational expectation discrepancies, this study focuses on the misalignment between adolescents' perceptions of their parents' expectations and their own self-expectations. Compared with parents' unexpressed actual expectations, adolescents' subjective interpretations of parental expectations constitute the key mechanism triggering "identity threat" and psychological distress. The variables and theoretical constructs in this study correspond clearly, as illustrated in Table 1 below. Adolescents' educational expectations correspond to the identity standard, perceived parental educational expectations correspond to reflected appraisals, intergenerational expectation discrepancy represents the degree of identity discrepancy, and mental health serves as the psychological outcome of identity verification. Meanwhile, family conflict, family interaction, and parental confidence can be understood as the relational and emotional manifestations of identity discrepancy, representing key mechanisms within the identity control process.

In the educational context, adolescents first establish their "identity standards" based on their own educational expectations. Subsequently, they perceive parental educational expectations as "input signals" from significant others. These two sets of expectations are continuously compared through a "comparator," which generates an "error signal." The magnitude of this error signal is determined by the extent of the discrepancy between the two. Although the direction of the discrepancy may vary, with parental expectations being either higher or lower than the adolescent's expectations, it does not alter its fundamental implication within the identity system: any deviation signifies hindered identity verification, thereby creating identity threat. According to ICT, when the error signal is minimal, adolescents achieve identity verification. This leads to emotional stability, enhanced self-efficacy, and smoother family interactions. Conversely, when the error signal increases, it triggers a series of negative responses, including emotional reactions such as anxiety, frustration, and self-doubt, as well as relational outcomes like heightened parent–child conflict, reduced interaction, and diminished parental confidence. If this error signal persists and remains unregulated, ICT posits that it will accumulate in subsequent cycles, ultimately exerting a detrimental impact on adolescents' mental health. Thus, intergenerational educational expectation discrepancies serve as a quantifiable representation of this identity error signal. Their mechanisms of influence align with the cyclical identity processes outlined in ICT, providing a theoretical foundation for the nonlinear effects and relational pathways proposed in this study (Fig. 1).

Research hypotheses

Since the direction of the discrepancy, whether parental expectations exceed those of the adolescent or vice versa, triggers distinct emotional responses, the resulting psychological consequences exhibit significant qualitative differences. It is therefore essential to further analyze three typical scenarios within this framework.

In intergenerational relationships, when an adolescent's educational expectations are significantly higher than those of their parents, the error signal is interpreted by the adolescent as "I aspire to achieve more, but my family does not believe I am capable of doing so." This signal signifies that the adolescent's educational aspirations lack parental validation, leading to feelings of being undervalued, experiencing a lack of support, and emotional isolation. Over time, this undermines self-efficacy and may contribute to anxiety and depression [28].

In another typical scenario, where parental educational expectations are significantly higher than the child's own expectations, the primary risks adolescents face stem from high pressure and identity burden. Parents impose a predefined, higher educational identity onto their child, but the child does not internalize this goal, creating a substantial gap between external parental expectations and the adolescent's internal identity standards. Such elevated expectations are often accompanied by intensified monitoring, comparison, and normative demands. As adolescents are continuously "pushed toward higher positions," they are likely to experience suppressed autonomy, increased self-doubt, and heightened anxiety about failure, all of which contribute to a decline in mental well-being [28].

The third typical scenario involves a moderate discrepancy between parents' and adolescents' expectations. Traditional linear perspectives often assume that complete alignment represents the optimal state of family parenting [20]. However, from the perspective of Identity Control Theory, while absolute "zero discrepancy" avoids direct conflict, it may also imply a lack of flexible space within the family system to provide a buffer for individual development. In contrast, a moderate expectation deviation, particularly when adolescents' self-identity standards are slightly higher than their parents' reflected appraisals, often yields the greatest motivational benefits and sustains optimal psychological well-being. In such a state of moderate discrepancy, adolescents possess stronger intrinsic achievement motivation, reflected in higher self-expectations, while relatively lower external expectations from parents precisely avoid the imposition of oppressive, high-pressure intervention [33, 41]. This moderate buffer space not only grants adolescents considerable room for autonomous exploration but also prevents the identity verification failures and anxiety that can arise from excessively high external standards [45]. Therefore, the impact of intergenerational educational expectation discrepancies on adolescents' mental health exhibits an optimal range of moderate difference. It is only when discrepancies exceed this moderate range and diverge excessively in either direction, meaning that parental expectations are either too high and create excessive pressure or too low and result in a lack of support, that the accumulation of error signals becomes detrimental to mental health. Based on the above analysis, this study proposes the following research hypotheses:

H1: The impact of intergenerational educational expectation discrepancies on adolescent mental health demonstrates an inverted U-shaped relationship.

When the educational identity standards of parents and children persistently deviate, the most direct external manifestation of the resulting error signal is an increase in parent–child conflict. Conflict essentially functions as an inefficient corrective strategy wherein both parties attempt to forcibly align their standards through adversarial communication: parents may resort to criticism, blame, or heightened demands in an effort to "push" the child, while children may respond with defiance, resistance, or emotional outbursts to "defend" their own standards. Such high-intensity negative interactions not only deplete the emotional resources of both sides but also directly elevate adolescents' stress levels and emotional distress, ultimately harming their mental health [11].

When the cost of conflict becomes too high or repeated confrontations prove ineffective, both parents and children often resort to "interaction withdrawal" as an alternative corrective strategy. Parents may reduce discussions on educational topics and cut back on shared activities, while children may avoid educational communication with their parents or even withdraw from overall closeness and interaction. The marked decline in positive interactions severs the emotional support and identity-validating signals adolescents typically receive from their families, leading to feelings of loneliness and a lack of belonging, which in turn heightens the risk of depression and anxiety [6].

When the error signal cannot be effectively corrected, parents may internally adjust their original reflected appraisals, leading to shaken confidence or even outright disappointment in their child's educational capabilities. This decline in confidence itself constitutes a new, more negative reflected appraisal, which further amplifies the error signal. Simultaneously, parental hesitation in educational investment and a reduction in resource support tangibly deprive adolescents of the external conditions necessary to achieve their identity standards, thereby becoming a significant source of mental health risks [22].Based on the aforementioned analysis, the following research hypotheses are proposed:

H2a: Family conflict mediates the relationship between intergenerational educational expectation discrepancies and adolescent mental health.

H2b: Family interaction mediates the relationship between intergenerational educational expectation discrepancies and adolescent mental health.

H2c: Parental confidence mediates the relationship between intergenerational educational expectation discrepancies and adolescent mental health.

The level of parental control may significantly influence how the "identity error signal" is transmitted within the family, thereby moderating the impact of expectation discrepancies on adolescent mental health. According to ICT, when individuals lack autonomy and regulatory space in the face of identity threats, error signals are more likely to accumulate and solidify into psychological stress [9]. In high-control households, parents tend to resort to psychological control, punitive supervision, and excessive intervention to correct their children's educational goals, thereby diminishing adolescents' sense of autonomy and amplifying feelings of shame, tension, and failure stemming from expectation discrepancies [42]. Particularly within East Asian cultural contexts, high parental control often exacerbates the chain transmission of stress, making adolescents more vulnerable to the emotional harm caused by expectation discrepancies [31]. Conversely, in low-control or more supportive family environments, parents are more likely to provide emotional support, room for negotiation, and open communication, thereby buffering the negative effects of identity error signals and improving adolescents' emotional well-being and relational quality. Based on the analysis above, the following research hypotheses are proposed:

H3a: Parental control positively moderates the U-shaped relationship between intergenerational educational expectation discrepancy and family conflict (Fig. 2a).

Hypothesized moderating effect of parental control

H3b: Parental control positively moderates the inverted U-shaped relationship between intergenerational educational expectation discrepancy and family interaction (Fig. 2b).

H3c: Parental control positively moderates the inverted U-shaped relationship between intergenerational educational expectation discrepancy and parental confidence (Fig. 2b).

Research framework

Research design

Data source

This study utilizes baseline data from the China Education Panel Survey (CEPS), a nationally representative survey conducted by the National Survey Research Center at Renmin University of China. The baseline survey was launched in the 2013–2014 academic year, initially targeting seventh- and ninth-grade junior high school students. Using average regional educational attainment and the proportion of mobile population as stratification variables, the study randomly selected 438 classes across 112 schools within 28 county-level units nationwide. Respondents included students, their parents, homeroom teachers, subject teachers, and school administrators. After addressing missing values through mean imputation, the final analytical sample consisted of 19,487 observations.The CEPS data are available for academic research through the official CEPS website and are indexed in the Chinese National Survey Data Archive (CNSDA).

Variable definitions

Dependent variable

Adolescent Mental Health: Following Yang & Dai [52], this variable was measured using five items reflecting depressive or anxious feelings over the past week (e.g., “felt too depressed or lethargic to concentrate”). Each item was rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”), yielding a total score between 5 and 25. To intuitively reflect positive mental health status and facilitate the interpretation of regression coefficients, this study applied standard reverse coding to all items (with a score of 1 recoded as 5, and 5 recoded as 1), and subsequently summed the items to calculate a total score. Following this transformation, the variable ranges from 5 to 25 points, with higher scores indicating better mental health among adolescents and milder symptoms of psychological distress.

Independent variable

Intergenerational Educational Expectation Discrepancy. Referring to the study by Zhang [54], this study operationalized parental educational expectations using the survey item "What is your parents' educational expectation for you?" and adolescents' self-educational expectations using the item "What level of education do you hope to attain yourself?" The response options were coded as follows: "does not matter" was assigned a value of 0,"do not want to continue schooling now" was assigned 1; "junior high school graduation" was assigned 2; "secondary specialized school/technical school," "vocational high school," and "regular high school" were assigned 3; "junior college" was assigned 4; "university undergraduate" was assigned 5; "master's degree" was assigned 6; and "doctoral degree" was assigned 7. The intergenerational educational expectation discrepancy was calculated by subtracting adolescents' self-expectations from their perceptions of parental expectations, yielding a variable ranging from -7 to 7.

Mediating variables

Family conflict: Drawing on the research of Wei and Ma [48], family conflict was measured using the variables "How is your relationship with your mother?" and "How is your relationship with your father?". Responses are coded as follows: "Not close" is assigned a value of 3, "Average" is assigned a value of 2, and "Very close" is assigned a value of 1. A less close parent–child relationship typically indicates greater communication barriers and conflicts in parent–child interactions, thereby serving as a valid indicator of the extent of family conflict. In this study, the scores of these two items were summed and averaged, with higher scores indicating a less close parent–child relationship.

Family Interaction: Based on Dou et al. [14], three items were selected: frequency of having dinner with parents, visiting museums/zoo/science centers, and attending movies/performances/sporting events together. Responses were standardized and aggregated.

Parental Confidence: Following Yang [51], parental confidence was measured measured via “How confident are your parents about your future?”. Options ranged from “not confident at all” (1) to “very confident” (4), with higher scores indicating greater parental confidence.

Moderating variable

Parental Control: Drawing from Xie [49], six aspects of control were assessed: homework/exams, school performance, friendships, clothing, internet use, and TV time. Each was rated on a 3-point scale: 1 (“no control”), 2 (“some control”), and 3 (“strict control”). A total score was computed and standardized.

Control variables

Control variables were selected from three levels: adolescent characteristics, parental characteristics, and family characteristics. Adolescent characteristics include gender, grade, age, whether they are an only child, number of siblings, and hobbies and interests. Parental characteristics include parents' educational level and parents' occupational status. Family characteristics include family economic status.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata 18.0. Given the stratified sampling design of the CEPS data, school-level cluster-robust standard errors were applied in all regression models to reduce the influence of within-school correlation on statistical inference. The analytical strategy included correlation analysis, tests of U-shaped relationships, mediation analysis, moderation analysis, polynomial regression, and response surface analysis.

To test whether intergenerational educational expectation discrepancy had an inverted U-shaped effect on adolescent mental health, this study estimated a quadratic regression model including both the linear and squared terms of the discrepancy. Because the significance of the squared term alone is insufficient to establish a U-shaped or inverted U-shaped relationship, we further applied the U-test procedure proposed by Lind and Mehlum [27]. Specifically, we first examined the sign and significance of the squared term to preliminarily identify the direction of the curve. Second, we calculated the turning point using the coefficients of the linear and squared terms and assessed whether the turning point fell within the observed range of the independent variable. Third, we estimated the marginal slopes at the lower and upper bounds of the observed range and examined whether the two slopes were statistically significant and opposite in sign. Only when the squared term was significant, the turning point lay within the observed data range, and the endpoint slopes were significant with opposite signs did we conclude that a statistically meaningful U-shaped or inverted U-shaped relationship existed.

The mediating effects of family conflict, family interaction, and parental confidence were examined using a bootstrap-based mediation analysis. Given that the main relationship identified in this study was nonlinear, the squared term of intergenerational educational expectation discrepancy was treated as the key explanatory variable in the mediation models. First, we estimated the effects of the squared discrepancy term on each mediator, including family conflict, family interaction, and parental confidence. Second, each mediator was entered into the outcome model to examine whether it significantly predicted adolescent mental health after controlling for both the linear and squared terms of expectation discrepancy as well as other covariates. Third, the indirect effects of the squared discrepancy term on adolescent mental health through each mediator were estimated using 5,000 bootstrap resamples. A mediating effect was considered statistically significant when the 95% bootstrap confidence interval of the indirect effect did not include zero.

Moderation models were specified by adding the interaction between the linear discrepancy term and parental control, as well as the interaction between the squared discrepancy term and parental control. A significant interaction involving the squared discrepancy term indicates that parental control moderates the nonlinear association between intergenerational educational expectation discrepancy and the mediating variables. Marginal effect plots were also produced to show the conditional marginal effects of expectation discrepancy on family conflict, family interaction, and parental confidence at different levels of parental control.

Empirical analysis

Common method bias test

To assess common method bias, Harman's single-factor test was conducted. The results showed that the first factor accounted for 18.718% of the variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40%. This indicates that common method bias is not a serious concern in this study.

Mediation effect analysis

The results in Columns (2) to (4) of Table 4 show that the squared term of expectation discrepancy has a significant positive effect on family conflict, indicating a U-shaped relationship (Fig. 5b), whereas it has significant negative effects on family interaction and parental confidence, indicating inverted U-shaped relationships (Figs. 5c and 5d). As shown in Table 5, the U-tests indicate that the turning points for these three mediating variables are -0.234, -0.367, and -0.975, respectively, with significant slopes of opposite signs on the two sides of each turning point. When adolescents' expectations are moderately higher than parental expectations, the family system reaches its most favorable state: family conflict is minimized, whereas family interaction and parental confidence peak. Columns (5) to (7) of Table 4 then enter the mediators separately. Family conflict has a significant negative effect on adolescent mental health (β = -1.012, p < 0.01), whereas family interaction (β = 0.107, p < 0.01) and parental confidence (β = 1.238, p < 0.01) exert significant protective effects. Analysis of moderating effects

To test the moderating effect of parental control, this study introduced the interaction term between the squared term of intergenerational expectation discrepancy and parental control into the models (Table 7). The results indicate that the interaction term exerts statistically significant nonlinear effects on family conflict (β = 0.001, p < 0.1), family interaction (β = -0.005, p < 0.01), and parental confidence (β = -0.001, p < 0.05). To visually illustrate this moderating mechanism, this study plotted moderation effect graphs under different levels of parental control (Figs. 6a–6c). As the level of parental control increases, the curvature of the nonlinear curves for each indicator becomes significantly more pronounced, and the peaks become more prominent. Specifically, high-intensity parental control not only strengthens the U-shaped effect of expectation discrepancy on family conflict but also exacerbates its inverted U-shaped effects on family interaction and parental confidence. This suggests that strict parental control amplifies the sensitivity of the family system to expectation misalignment, making the characteristic of "moderate discrepancy being optimal" more evident. Thus, Hypotheses H3a, H3b, and H3c are all supported. To further verify the robustness of the results, this study plotted marginal effect graphs following the method proposed by Haans et al. [19] (Figures 7a and 8c). The results similarly confirm that parental control does not merely exert a simple linear shifting effect,rather, it intensifies the nonlinear characteristics of family mechanisms by significantly strengthening the influence of the squared term of intergenerational discrepancy.

Discussion

Major research findings

The core finding of this study is that by quantifying the discrepancy between parental educational expectations and adolescents' self-expectations, it validates an inverted U-shaped relationship between educational expectation discrepancies and adolescent mental health. Specifically, adolescents' mental health is optimal when parental and self-expectations are aligned or closely matched, whereas psychological distress increases when either parental expectations significantly exceed those of the adolescent or vice versa. This result also aligns with the balance of expectations theory. Statistical modeling identifies a tipping point that reveals the critical threshold of expectation discrepancy, beyond which imbalances pose significant risks to mental health. Furthermore, polynomial regression and response surface analysis (RSA) indicate that under conditions of high educational expectation congruence (X = Y), mental health improves as expectation levels rise. Conversely, under conditions of expectation incongruence (X = − Y), the magnitude of the discrepancy, rather than its direction, is what affects mental health: larger discrepancies correspond to poorer mental well-being. This confirms that the inverted U-shaped structure is methodologically robust and not an artifact of difference scores but reflects a genuine pattern in educational expectation misalignment. This finding offers a new explanatory framework for long-standing scholarly debates on the effects of parental educational expectations.

Existing research has shown that high parental educational expectations can, under certain conditions, positively influence adolescents' academic performance [16, 20], while also enhancing their learning motivation and self-efficacy [50]. However, numerous studies also highlight that excessively high parental educational expectations may increase adolescents' risk of anxiety and depression [25], and in some contexts, even lead to rebelliousness and academic burnout [10]. The inverted U-shaped model presented in this study reveals that parental expectations are neither "the higher, the better" nor "the higher, the worse",instead, there exists an optimal range of alignment. This finding not only reconciles the contradictions between positive and negative conclusions in prior research but also, through the statistical measurement of the inflection point, provides empirical thresholds for "expectation congruence" for the first time. This insight contributes to the ongoing scholarly debate regarding the "optimal level of educational expectations" [46].

Further analysis reveals that family conflict, interaction, and parental confidence play crucial mediating roles. When a significant discrepancy exists between parental and adolescent educational expectations, it is likely to trigger parent–child conflict and relational tension [17], thereby increasing adolescents' psychological stress and emotional distress. Conversely, high-quality family interaction and strong parental confidence can help mitigate the negative effects of expectation imbalances and enhance adolescents' psychological resilience. This finding aligns with the perspective of family systems theory, which posits that the family functions as an interconnected whole, and that expectation discrepancies indirectly impact individual mental health through relational dynamics [12].

Moreover, parental control exerts a magnifying effect on the relationship between expectation discrepancies and adolescent psychological outcomes. The findings indicate that in families characterized by high levels of parental control, the psychological consequences of expectation imbalances are more severe. Conversely, in families with lower levels of parental control, adolescents possess greater autonomy, which helps buffer the negative effects of expectation mismatches. This observation aligns with existing research on parenting styles, which suggests that authoritative or supportive parenting is more conducive to adolescents' psychological development [43, 47]. However, this study advances the understanding by elucidating the interactive effect between parental control and expectation discrepancies, offering new insights into how different parenting styles moderate the influence of educational expectations.

It is noteworthy that the psychological significance of expectation discrepancies may vary across cultural contexts. Chinese society is deeply influenced by Confucian culture, where education is viewed as a vital pathway to family honor, self-cultivation, and upward social mobility [26]. Parental expectations often carry normative and family responsibility attributes, and the cultural emphasis on filial piety amplifies adolescents' sensitivity to parental evaluations, making expectation discrepancies more likely to be experienced as emotional stress or relational tension [53]. Within this cultural logic, the inverted U-shaped relationship observed in this study may be particularly pronounced. In contrast, individualistic cultures, such as those in the United States and Europe, tend to place greater emphasis on autonomy, independent goal-setting, and personal choice [37]. Parent–child expectation discrepancies are often viewed as a normative part of the self-differentiation process rather than a violation of family obligations, potentially resulting in lower emotional intensity. Consequently, the inflection point of the inverted U-shaped relationship may emerge later or exert weaker effects in such contexts [32]. Additionally, research from Japan and Korea suggests that East Asian cultures generally share high levels of academic pressure and parental authority, making adolescents more susceptible to psychological distress due to expectation discrepancies [24]. Collectively, these studies indicate that while the inverted U-shaped effect exhibits cross-national consistency within East Asian societies, its sensitivity range may vary depending on cultural norms.

Theoretical contributions

First, Contribution to Educational Expectation Theory. Unlike the view that high parental expectations are always positive [13] or the assertion that they are necessarily harmful [25], this study emphasizes that the role of expectations is characterized by a threshold effect. This revises and expands educational expectation theory: expectations are not merely a unidimensional source of motivation or stress but a complex variable exhibiting nonlinear effects at different levels of discrepancy.

Second, Deepening Family Systems Theory. Traditional family systems theory posits that interactions and emotional quality among family members play a crucial role in adolescents' mental health [29]. By introducing conflict, interaction, and parental confidence as mediating variables, this study reveals how family relationships specifically moderate the link between expectation discrepancies and mental health. Notably, conflict is not the sole negative mechanism,positive interaction and parental confidence can act as buffers. This provides a more nuanced explanatory pathway for family systems theory.

Third, Enriching Parenting Style Theory. Research on parenting styles typically explains psychological outcomes through dimensions such as supportiveness, authoritativeness, and permissiveness [2, 43]. By examining the moderating effect of parental control, this study reveals that, in the context of educational expectation discrepancies, varying levels of parental control significantly shape mental health outcomes. This finding not only broadens the scope of parenting style theory but also suggests that researchers should pay greater attention to the interaction between parenting styles and educational expectations, rather than examining them in isolation.

Fourth, an Interdisciplinary Contribution to Mental Health and Cultural Capital Theory. The impact of educational expectation discrepancies extends beyond the psychological level and is closely linked to social mobility and the transmission of cultural capital [5]. This study finds that expectation congruence not only enhances mental health but may also facilitate individuals' smooth navigation within social structures. This interdisciplinary perspective introduces educational sociology and cultural capital dimensions into mental health research, thereby enhancing the comprehensiveness of theoretical frameworks.

Practical implications

This study carries significant practical implications for families, schools, and policy-making. For families, the research underscores the importance of parents aligning educational expectations with their children's actual abilities and developmental aspirations. By fostering open, egalitarian communication and reducing unidirectional, high-pressure demands, parents can achieve a more reasonable match between their expectations and their children's realities. At the school level, as a crucial support system connecting families and adolescents, schools can provide parents with more accurate informational feedback through college and career guidance, parent-teacher meetings, and daily communication channels. This would help parents develop more realistic educational assessments. Furthermore, school-based psychological counseling and early identification systems can detect students experiencing excessive pressure due to expectation discrepancies and offer targeted interventions to mitigate associated psychological risks. Evidence from international studies also indicates that school-led communication and support mechanisms can effectively enhance the coordination of educational goals between parents and children [44]. On a broader policy level, the findings suggest that policymakers should integrate "parent–child expectation congruence" into frameworks for family education and adolescent mental health policies. Drawing on international experiences, governments can offer systematic educational guidance to parents through family education programs, school-based mental health services, and community support networks. Such measures would facilitate collaborative interventions between schools and families, establishing a more comprehensive support system across the family-school-community nexus. By transforming educational expectations from a private family matter into a policy-supported public issue, these efforts can ultimately foster a healthier developmental environment for adolescents.

Limitations and future research directions

Although this study systematically analyzes educational expectation discrepancies and their underlying mechanisms, it has several limitations that warrant further refinement in future research. First, the study relies on cross-sectional data, which limits the ability to establish temporal sequence or causal direction between expectation discrepancies and mental health. Future research could utilize longitudinal panel data, such as from the CEPS, to apply cross-lagged panel models to examine whether expectation discrepancies predict subsequent changes in mental health over time. Additionally, incorporating individual fixed-effects models could help control for time-invariant traits, thereby enabling estimation of the causal effects of within-individual changes in expectation discrepancies on mental health outcomes.

Second, this study is based on a sample situated within the Chinese cultural context. Parental educational expectations are deeply influenced by Confucian values, intergenerational obligations, and collectivist norms, which may differ in their formation mechanisms and psychological significance from cultures emphasizing autonomy and individual choice, such as Western societies. Therefore, future research should conduct cross-cultural comparative studies to examine the universality and cultural adaptability of the inverted U-shaped relationship, as well as to explore the moderating role of family structures across different cultural backgrounds.

Third, the core variables in this study rely primarily on self-reported questionnaires, which may be subject to subjective biases and social desirability effects. Future research could integrate multi-source data, such as teacher evaluations, behavioral tasks, longitudinal big data (e.g., learning behavior records), or psychophysiological indicators, to enhance the objectivity and robustness of the measurements.

Additionally, the measures of family conflict and family interaction used in this study are derived from items widely employed in national surveys such as the China Education Panel Survey. However, as these items are not standardized psychological scales, they may not fully capture the dynamic nature of conflict behaviors or the multidimensional structure of interaction quality. Similarly, the measurement of mental health in this study primarily focuses on depressive symptoms and does not encompass broader psychological dimensions such as anxiety or subjective well-being. Future research should incorporate validated family relationship scales, such as the Parent–Child Relationship Inventory and the Conflict Behavior Questionnaire, along with multidimensional mental health indicators to enhance construct validity and comparability across studies.

Finally, while this study focuses on mechanisms within the family system, the formation and impact of educational expectation discrepancies are also influenced by school environments and broader societal structural factors. Future research could adopt multilevel models to incorporate school climate, teacher expectations, resource availability, and policy dynamics, thereby constructing a more comprehensive family-school-society multilevel framework to better understand the interplay of these factors.

Conclusions

This study shows that intergenerational educational expectation discrepancy is nonlinearly associated with adolescent mental health in China. Moderate alignment between parental and adolescent expectations is linked to better mental health, whereas excessive discrepancies in either direction are associated with poorer psychological outcomes. Family conflict, family interaction, and parental confidence partly explain this association, while parental control further shapes the strength of these mechanisms. These findings highlight the importance of balanced parent–child educational expectations and supportive family communication for adolescent mental health.
