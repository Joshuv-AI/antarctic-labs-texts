Antiplatelet Therapy Tailored by Ethnicity
Authors: Joon Ho Ahn, Min Chul Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Over the last several decades, the potent P2Y12 inhibitors ticagrelor and prasugrel have been the treatments of choice instead of clopidogrel for the management of acute coronary syndrome (ACS). Recent guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology and the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association recommend ticagrelor over clopidogrel on the basis of the major randomized clinical results of the PLATO (Platelet Inhibition and Patient Outcomes) trial.1,2 However, the efficacy and safety of cardiovascular drugs may vary among ethnicities, with resulting geographic differences in the doses of approved drugs, preferred treatment options, and drug labels. Data suggest that East Asian patients may respond differently to P2Y12 inhibitors than Caucasians in terms of the incidence of thrombotic and bleeding events after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and in the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles of antiplatelet drugs.3

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Loh et al4 report a large-scale, population-based, propensity score–matched study evaluating the bleeding outcomes of ticagrelor vs clopidogrel in a multiethnic cohort of Southeast Asian patients with ACS. The current study analyzed 14,812 patients and revealed that ticagrelor was associated with a significantly higher risk than clopidogrel of Bleeding Academic Research Consortium type 3 (adjusted HR: 1.33; 95% CI: 1.04-1.69).4 In contrast to the PLATO trial, which showed no significant difference in PLATO-defined major bleeding between ticagrelor- and clopidogrel-treated patients, these study results suggest that ticagrelor may have a greater bleeding risk in Southeast Asian patients than previously appreciated.

The current study data support the result of the TICAKOREA (TICAgrelor Versus Clopidogrel in Asian/KOREan Patients with ACS Intended for Invasive Management) trial,5 which reinforces the “East Asian paradox.” According to the concept of the East Asian paradox, compared with Caucasians, East Asian patients exhibit higher bleeding events while having similar or fewer ischemic events despite elevated platelet reactivity. The possible mechanisms underlying the results observed in Southeast Asian patients warrant closer attention. Unique demographics factors (eg, low body weight), comorbidities, and disease patterns in this population may have contributed to the higher bleeding rates.

Furthermore, the higher plasma concentrations of ticagrelor reported in East Asians compared with Caucasians could also have accentuated the bleeding risk among Southeast Asian patients.6 Because potent P2Y12 receptor inhibitors are commonly used in daily clinical practice, the unique risk-benefit tradeoff in the East Asian population provided a cornerstone in ethnicity-tailored antiplatelet therapy.3 The current study identifies several independent predictors of bleeding: age ≥65 years, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, hyperlipidemia, anemia, severe chronic kidney disease, previous bleeding within 6 months, and concurrent oral anticoagulant use. Ethnicity was considered a crucial determinant; Indian and other ethnic groups demonstrated significantly lower bleeding odds than Chinese and Malay patients.

These findings highlight the complexity of bleeding risk stratification in Southeast Asian patients and suggest that ethnicity should be considered alongside traditional clinical factors when selecting P2Y12 inhibitors. However, not all previous studies demonstrated increased bleeding with ticagrelor in East Asian patients. In the randomized clinical trial conducted among Japanese, Taiwanese, and South Korean patients with ACS after PCI (the PHILO trial),7 no significant difference in major bleeding events was observed between ticagrelor and clopidogrel. A balanced perspective is therefore needed to better guide clinicians on when and how to consider the impact of ethnicity in antiplatelet therapy.

Loh et al4 used propensity score matching and sensitivity analyses (including a PCI-only cohort). However, the limitations inherent in retrospective database studies remain, such as potential residual confounding, reliance on diagnostic codes for bleeding events, and lack of data on ischemic events and ticagrelor dose or P2Y12 switching. Most importantly, ischemic outcomes such as myocardial infarction, stent thrombosis, and stroke were not evaluated. This omission limits the ability to fully assess the net clinical benefit of ticagrelor compared with clopidogrel. Moreover, although the HR of 1.33 for bleeding reached statistical significance, the modest effect size warrants careful consideration of its true clinical relevance in everyday clinical practice. Nonetheless, Loh et al4 provided strong justification for the integrity of their findings, including consistent median follow-up and minimal missing data (<0.4%).

This study has several important clinical implications. First, the results emphasize that individualized antiplatelet strategies should be considered for Southeast Asian patients. Given the elevated bleeding risk associated with ticagrelor in this cohort, clinicians may consider clopidogrel, particularly in Chinese and Malay patients, and ethnic background could potentially inform the degree of caution required. Second, this study challenges the widespread application of Western-derived guidelines in non-Western settings. Current Academic Research Consortium–High Bleeding Risk criteria do not include ethnicity as a risk factor. Along with the result from TICAKOREA trial,5 the study results presented here further suggest that interethnic differences in bleeding susceptibility are both measurable and clinically relevant. Future iterations of global consensus statements should consider incorporating ethnicity into the bleeding risk algorithms. The development of an Asia-focused bleeding risk score incorporating ethnicity, comorbidities, and dynamic treatment variables (eg, drug switching and renal function) is a logical next step.
