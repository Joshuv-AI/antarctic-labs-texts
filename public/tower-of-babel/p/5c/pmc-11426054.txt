Remote data collection of infant activity and sleep patterns via wearable sensors in the HEALthy Brain and Child Development Study (HBCD)
Authors: Nicolò Pini, William P. Fifer, Jinseok Oh, Camille Nebeker, Julie M. Croff, Beth A. Smith, the Novel Technology/Wearable Sensors Working Group
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The HEALthy Brain and Child Development (HBCD) Study, a multi-site prospective longitudinal cohort study, will examine human brain, cognitive, behavioral, social, and emotional development beginning prenatally and planned through early childhood. Wearable and remote sensing technologies have advanced data collection outside of laboratory settings to enable exploring, in more detail, the associations of early experiences with brain development and social and health outcomes. In the HBCD Study, the Novel Technology/Wearable Sensors Working Group (WG-NTW) identified two primary data types to be collected: infant activity (by measuring leg movements) and sleep (by measuring heart rate and leg movements). These wearable technologies allow for remote collection in the natural environment. This paper illustrates the collection of such data via wearable technologies and describes the decision-making framework, which led to the currently deployed study design, data collection protocol, and derivatives, which will be made publicly available. Moreover, considerations regarding actual and potential challenges to adoption and use, data management, privacy, and participant burden were examined. Lastly, the present limitations in the field of wearable sensor data collection and analysis will be discussed in terms of extant validation studies, the difficulties in comparing performance across different devices, and the impact of evolving hardware/software/firmware.

Introduction

The HEALthy Brain and Child Development Study (HBCD), a multi-site prospective longitudinal cohort study sponsored by several NIH Institutes, Centers, and Offices, will examine human brain, cognitive, behavioral, social, and emotional development beginning prenatally and planned through early childhood (Nelson et al., 2024, Volkow et al., 2024). The HBCD Study will enroll over 7000 participating families across the 27 participating research sites in the United States and follow them from pregnancy through early childhood. The long-term goal of the HBCD Study is to advance our understanding of how child development is affected by exposure to substances and other environmental, social, and biological factors and experiences. The HBCD Study will be, to our knowledge, the most detailed study of early brain and child development ever conducted. It will produce baseline normative developmental data to be utilized by a variety of stakeholders to rigorously investigate physical and mental health outcomes and subsequently evaluate potential targets for early interventions (Anunziata et al., 2024, Cioffredi et al., Jul 14 2024, Cole et al., 2024, Dean et al., 2024, Edwards et al., 2024, Fox et al., 2024, Gurka et al., 2024, Harden et al., 2024, Hillard et al., 2024, Kable et al., 2024, Kingsley et al., 2024, Murray et al., 2024, Nelson et al., 2024, Pini et al., 2024, Si et al., 2024, Sullivan et al., 2024, Volkow et al., 2024). In the context of the HBCD Study, the Novel Technology/Wearable Sensors Working Group (WG-NTW) 1) investigated the opportunities and challenges of utilizing wearable and remote sensing technologies at scale and 2) implemented measurements derived from wearable and remote sensing technologies, with the goal of advancing non-invasive data collection outside of laboratory settings to enable exploring, in more detail, the associations of early experiences with brain and child development.

In recent years, advancements in portable consumer technologies have led to a notable increase in the development of noninvasive, consumer-grade devices, often referred to as wearables, for sensing human biosignals across the lifespan (Huhn et al., 2022). Wearables have the characteristic of enabling the collection of a wealth of multimodal and multidimensional physiological parameters and environmental/contextual information in naturalistic settings with minimal disruption to individuals’ typical activities. In fact, wearable devices are often described as being generally unobtrusive, comfortable to wear, and of minimal burden to participants (Booth et al., 2019). Given the advantages offered by these novel technologies, several research fields and diverse communities have spearheaded the implementation and deployment of wearables outside of laboratory settings, enabling the capture of data for health research in large study populations – often referred as studies in the wild. Moreover, wearables afford the opportunity of collecting data for prolonged periods of time to achieve long-term data gathering in the naturalistic environment (Booth et al., 2019, Gustafsson et al., 2021).

In this paper, we describe the theoretical background and decision-making framework supporting the practical implementation of the collection of two types of data: infant activity (by measuring leg movements) and sleep (by measuring heart rate and leg movements) via wearable devices selected for use in the HBCD Study. Derivatives extracted from these two data biosignals will enable the unprecedented characterization of normative trajectories of development of infant leg movement, physical activity patterns,sleep patterns, and heart rate and respiration underlying sleep patterns. Moreover, derivatives extracted from wearable devices (as well as the raw data) will be made available to the scientific community (see Data Releases) in conjunction with the data collected across a multitude of modalities in the HBCD Study (Nelson et al., 2024, Volkow et al., 2024, Murray et al., 2024, Kingsley et al., 2024, Harden et al., 2024, Hillard et al., 2024, Cole et al., 2024, Si et al., 2024, Dean et al., 2024, Fox et al., 2024, Gurka et al., 2024, Sullivan et al., 2024, Pini et al., 2024, Cioffredi et al., Jul 14 2024, Cioffredi et al., 2024, Edwards et al., 2024, Kable et al., 2024, Anunziata et al., 2024). Biosensor data collected via wearable devices will provide essential data to address research questions across multiple research domains. Researchers will be able to use the data collected as part of the HBCD Study Protocol to characterize the developmental evolutions of infant activity and sleep over time as well as exploring how they relate to experiences, learning opportunities, and environmental factors. We will illustrate the domains of interest with respect to study design and data collection (see HBCD Study Protocol) using wearable technologies in the HBCD Study protocol. Specifically, we will discuss the consideration in terms of operability and implementation of piloting and launching a study of this scale and complexity. Finally, we will elaborate on the collaborative structure of the WG-NTW, and the discussions regarding the role of wearable devices for Diversity, Equity, Inclusion (DEI), specifically the challenges of using wearable devices with respect to practices that promote inclusion of diverse populations and such that data may produce generalizable knowledge (Murray et al., 2024).

Novel tech/wearables within the context of the HBCD study

Scientific background supporting the collected measures

The collection of infant activity (by measuring leg movements) and sleep (by measuring heart rate and leg movements) via wearable devices was included within the broader scope of the HBCD Study (see HBCD Study Protocol) to capture the longitudinal development of these key developmental processes in the early stages of life. Derivatives extracted from these biosignals (and the raw data), and their associated constructs will be available for conducting analyses in a stand-alone manner or in combination with other data modalities (Nelson et al., 2024, Volkow et al., 2024, Murray et al., 2024, Kingsley et al., 2024, Harden et al., 2024, Hillard et al., 2024, Cole et al., 2024, Si et al., 2024, Dean et al., 2024, Fox et al., 2024, Gurka et al., 2024, Sullivan et al., 2024, Pini et al., 2024, Cioffredi et al., Jul 14 2024, Cioffredi et al., 2024, Edwards et al., 2024, Kable et al., 2024, Anunziata et al., 2024). As an example, measures of infant activity and sleep health dimensions may serve as predictors, moderators, mediators, and outcomes to investigate multiple complex and nested relations across domains collected in the HBCD Study.

Seminal work by Esther Thelen and colleagues described the developmental trajectory of infants’ spontaneous leg movements across the first year of life and explained how alternating kicking is a precursor to walking in typically-developing infants (Thelen, 1979, Thelen et al., 1981, Thelen et al., 1983, Thelen and Fisher, 1983, Thelen and Fisher, 1982). This work has two important aspects: 1) it describes trajectories of the emergence of neuromotor control as the nervous system develops in typical development and 2) it highlights the relationship between early motor experience and later functional motor skills. There are known differences between neuromotor control in infants with typical development and infants with atypical development. Cross-sectional studies have shown that kicking has altered characteristics in infants born preterm, (Geerdink et al., 1996) and in infants with periventricular brain injuries, (Droit et al., 1996, Fetters et al., 2004, Barbosa et al., 2005) very low birth weight, (Jeng et al., 2002) Down syndrome, (Ulrich and Ulrich, 1995, McKay and Angulo-Barroso, 2006) and myelomeningocele (Rademacher et al., 2008, Smith et al., 2011). For example, an inability to dissociate intralimb joint couplings during kicking can be observed as early as one month of age in preterm infants with white matter disorder (Fetters et al., 2004). This supports the ability of early spontaneous leg movement patterns to differentiate between typical and atypical developmental trajectories.

Data collected in the HBCD Study will create a normative sample of typical development; this is of crucial importance for being able to identify atypical developmental trajectories early in life. Further, knowledge gained as part of the HBCD Study will allow us to link early motor behavior with later developmental outcomes; this has implications for early intervention.

There is well-documented evidence for the role of sleep in infant's health and physical development, including guiding central and autonomic nervous system development, regulation of growth hormone (Takahashi et al., 1968) and immune function (Besedovsky et al., 2012). The early stages of life are characterized by dramatic neurobehavioral changes (Jiang, 2019). Sleep is one of the primary activities of the brain during early development (Tham et al., 2017). In infancy, sleep is essential for growth and development, including the maturation of the central and autonomic nervous systems (CNS and ANS) and the emergence of regular circadian rhythms (Lange et al., 2010, Mirmiran et al., 2003, McGraw et al., 1999, Heraghty et al., 2008). Moreover, a wealth of multidisciplinary literature has demonstrated the associations of multidimensional sleep measures of sleep health with children's attention and alertness, (Waldon et al., 2018) mood, (Hall et al., 2012) behavior control, (Hairston et al., 2016) learning and memory, (Dewald et al., 2010) executive function, (Cremone et al., 2017) and cognitive performance (Paavonen et al., 2010). For example, brain circuits underpinning both sleep and attention are closely linked (Humphreys et al., 2014, Hvolby, 2015, Verhoeff et al., 2018). However, little is known about the timing in the emergence of differences in sleep trajectories or the underlying ANS pathways. Additionally, measures abstracted from parent-reported questionnaires are of limited value in predicting later sleep health in infancy and childhood (Tomisaki et al., 2018). In fact, most studies solely investigate qualitative sleep measures, which cannot capture the complexity of the underlying physiological mechanisms responsible for differences in sleep health (Bernier et al., 2013, Sadeh et al., 2015, Geva et al., 2016). The manifestation of sleep disturbances may begin in infancy and is thought to be associated with trajectories of structural and functional brain development. In the HBCD Study an equitable approach to the collection of sleep physiology is made possible via unobtrusive wearable device able to capture multidimensional measures of sleep in the home environment.

Data collected in the HBCD Study will create a normative sample of typical development of both sleep and ANS function. This research is relevant to public health because sleep is an underrecognized, yet modifiable risk/resilience factor foundational for positive health. Findings could have implications for early intervention in young infants aimed at reducing future risk of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes.

In sum, given the focus on early exposures and experiences in the HBCD Study, the infant activity (by measuring leg movements) and sleep (by measuring heart rate and leg movements) data collected via wearable devices and designed by the WG-NTW will provide an unprecedented opportunity for the examination of individual differences in movement and sleep patterns before differences in physical and mental health outcome become evident. Our approach is designed to be feasible, inclusive, and scalable. Within this open science framework, research will afford the opportunity to quantify the relative contribution of experiences, learning, and environmental factors in shaping such trajectories.

Data in relation to specific HBCD areas of investigation/questions

Infant activity (by measuring leg movements)

Infants (0–9 months) will wear custom leg warmers with a pocket that securely holds a sensor (tri-axial accelerometer and tri-axial gyroscope) at each ankle for 72 continuous hours (see HBCD Study Protocol). Sensor data will be used to estimate intensity of physical activity (sedentary, light, moderate-to-vigorous), sleep/wake cycles (duration), and quantity (number of movements/hour of awake time), variability (entropy), duration (s), average acceleration (m/s2), and peak acceleration (m/s2) of leg movements (Smith et al., 2015, Smith et al., 2017, Trujillo-Priego et al., 2020, Abrishami et al., 2019). Infant sleep (by measuring heart rate)

Infants (0–9 months) will wear a wireless watch-like and upper-arm-worn wearable device (see HBCD Study Protocol). This device is fastened to the infant’s upper arm via a soft-textile band. The photoplethysmography (PPG) sensors (one emitting green light PPG and one emitting red light PPG) embedded in the device allow for the collection of three biosignals, namely pulse rate signal (in beat per minute), blood oxygen saturation signal (SpO2) (in percentage), and respiratory rate signal (in breath per minute). These estimates of such biosignals are sampled at 1 Hz. The data collection is planned for 72 continuous hours. See Fig. 2 for an example of a 15-minute trace. The sensor data will be used to estimate measures of average and variability (short- and long-term) for the three collected signals. Moreover, sleep states will be derived from these vital signs data (Pini et al., 2022). Specifically, sleep states will be scores as either awake (W), active sleep (AS), quiet sleep (QS), or indeterminate (I) (Fifer et al., 2005, Sahni et al., 1999, Sahni et al., 1995). From the scored sleep traces, sleep/wake cycles for each state (duration and variability) will be derived utilized to address the following objectives of HBCD: 1. characterize typical neurodevelopmental trajectories of ANS and sleep health; 2. assess the influence of early life experiences in shaping the evolution of such trajectories; 3. assess the role of these trajectories in predicting subsequent neurodevelopmental outcomes; 4. investigate risk and resilience factors mediating these relationships.Fig. 2Fifteen minutes of processed heart rate sensor data from one infant. In the trace displayed, from minute 0 to minute 10, the infant was asleep, and the sleep period was scored as active sleep (AS), whereas from minute 10 to minute 15, the infant was asleep, and the sleep period was scored as quiet sleep (QS).Fig. 2

The derived measures of infant activity and infant sleep can be used to investigate several questions. Within-person, age-dependent variation in derived measures can be used to estimate individual developmental trajectories and assess the factors that may influence them, e.g., using mixed-effects models. A key example for HBCD is assessing the effects of pre- and post-natal exposure to opioids, marijuana, alcohol, and/or other substances on neurodevelopmental trajectories.

Decision making framework

The design of a robust, feasible, and actionable protocol for the collection of wearable sensor data in a study of the complex as HBCD was informed by a rigorous and extensive decision making framework to help account for the numerous challenges associated with the utilization of novel wearable technologies in research studies. Studies conducted in the wild encompass numerous challenges which are unique to digital health research.

In the HBCD study, the WG-NTW is co-led by Drs. Beth A. Smith and William P. Fifer. Members of the WG-NTW meet every other week to discuss and reevaluate the scientific constructs, share information on the status of devices and data collection, and reach out to various other WGs within and outside of the HBCD Study. In addition to the large group meetings, a sub-group was formed. The activity of this sub-group is the design, implementation, and testing of the data processing pipelines. To ensure integration of the wearable data collection in the context of the HBCD Study, the WG-NTW Co-Chairs meet every other week with the leadership of all the WGs. Conversations within and across WGs are also supported and overseen by the HBCD Consortium Administrative Core (HCAC) and HBCD Data Coordinating Center (HDCC) (Nelson et al., 2024).

Here we describe the criteria informing the decision on which wearable sensors to use in the HBCD Study. The WG-NTW also consulted the Digital Health Checklist (DHC) and Framework (Nebeker et al., 2020). The DHC (see Fig. 3) is a decision support tool that was developed to assist researchers who are designing studies that will involve a wearable sensor technology (Nebeker et al., 2020). The framework undergirding the DHC consists of four intersecting domains: 1) Access & Usability, 2) Risks & Benefits, 3) Privacy, and 4) Data Management. The four domains are grounded by commonly accepted ethical principles of respect for persons, beneficence, justice (United States. National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects of Biomedical, & Behavioral Research, 1978) and respect for law and public interest (Bailey et al., 2012). The DHC supports ethical decision making from the early stages of research design by prompting researchers to carefully consider what features might impact a participant’s access to and use of the device as well as how to think through related risks and benefits, including privacy expectations and managing volumes of granular personal health data. In this section we will briefly expand on some of the key DHC constructs. The WG-NTW has devoted particular attention to the implementation and procedures for monitoring issues that map to 1) Access & Usability, 2) Risks & Benefits, 3) Privacy, and 4) Data Management in the HBCD Study.

Specific to Access and Useability, one of the foremost challenges is the logistical burden of deploying and ensuring wearable devices are correctly maintained. Considerations of Access and Useability are relevant to both research participants and staff. For example, large-scale studies like the HBCD Study must consider the impact of sensor failure and provide research staff with appropriate knowledge for detecting different possible sensor failures. Staff should also be equipped with tools, including checklists, for the rapid detection of sensor malfunction and have protocols for reporting to minimize data quality issues. Research teams have limited direct control over the environment for studies in the natural environment to support the tracking of the status of the devices at scale, semi-automated tools and quality control (QC) checklists should be available to the research staff members. With respect to the Data Management domain, similar considerations apply. For example, strategies that allow for the automatic and timely assessment of the quality of data collected via wearable sensing technologies are critical. The WG-NTW has implemented a variety of semi- and fully automated QC tools with the goal of minimizing data loss. While data loss is likely to occur at multiple stages of the data collection pipeline, the WG-NTW, in close collaboration with HCAC and HDCC, has implemented several strategies in order to mitigate these risks. These encompass strategies discussed at the WG-NTW level and implemented at the level of the participating sites. Examples are the utilization of identifiers to ensure that the linkage between a given study participant and the assigned device is unique, and timely communications between the individuals responsible for assessing data quality and feedback on data quality provided to staff members.

HBCD study protocol

The primary consideration driving the selection of the timepoints for data collection was the identification of development windows to efficiently capture meaningful variation of infant activity (by measuring leg movements) and sleep (by measuring heart rate and leg movements). Taking into account the extensive schedule of assessment implemented in the HBCD Study Protocol, the WG-NTW recommended the biosensor data collection (see Fig. 4) at Visit 2 (0–1 month of age) and Visit 3 (3–9 months of age). This protocol implemented for the main study was designed with the goal of balancing the burden on participating families and research staff with the desire to collect as much data as possible and incorporate considerations regarding inclusivity and accessibility (see DEI Considerations). Moreover, the procedures concerning data quality assessment, data quality reports, and logistical coordination are centralized and supervised by members of the WG-NTW.Fig. 4Graphical summary of the HBCD Study Protocol. Infant movement wearable sensor data are collected at Visit 2 (0–1 month of age) and Visit 3 (3–9 months of age). The research staff set up 2 infant movement wearable sensors (tri-axial accelerometer and gyroscope) before the participant arrives. Settings are: sampling frequency = 25 Hz, accelerometer range = ± 16 g, gyroscope = ± 2000 dps. A 60-second calibration file is performed for each sensor, 10 seconds of the sensor sitting flat on each of its 6 surfaces. Next, each sensor is placed in a casing to make it larger than a choking hazard. Finally, each sensor is placed into a secure sleeve on a custom-made legwarmer with a pocket. One sensor is placed on each ankle of the infant, just above the foot. The sensors are programmed to start recording at 10 am eastern/9 am central/8 am mountain/7 am pacific the following morning. They record for 72 hours while the families go about their typical activities. Data are stored on the sensor and downloaded off of the sensor via USB cable once they are returned to the study team.

Heart rate wearable sensor data are collected at Visit 2 (0–1 month of age) and Visit 3 (3–9 months of age). Upon verifying that the systems for data collection are fully charged, the research staff set up 1 heart rate sensor by scanning a unique QR code to link a given heart rate sensor to a study participant. The information regarding the serial number (S/N) of the system, the date and time of system configuration, and the expected end date and time of the recording are logged in an online “Set-up Form”. Next, the sensor is placed on the upper arm of the infant study participant. The sensors are programmed to start recording at 10 am eastern/9 am central/8 am mountain/7 am pacific the following morning. The data collection period is set to a duration equal to 72 hours. The data collected in this period of time, are temporarily stored on the sensor and transmitted (via Bluetooth) to a tablet when in range of the heart rate wearable sensor. The data is downloaded off of the tablet and uploaded to an online cloud repository when a system is returned to the study team. Upon retrieving a system, the information regarding the S/N of the system, the date and time of system retrieval, and any annotation on status of the device (e.g., damages) are logged in an online “Return Form”.

Caregivers receive a brief survey via text message and/or email 3 times while their infant is wearing the sensors. The surveys are sent at the 24, 48, and 72-hour mark of the recording and ask about the preceding 24 hours. They ask whether or not the infant wore the sensors most of the 24 hours, if the 24 hours were fairly typical behavior for the infant (and if not, why not), how much time the infant spent in devices (car seats, swings, etc.), about sleeping, and about swaddling.

Data releases

A subcommittee of the WG-NTW was tasked with the development of the protocols for data transfer, preprocessing, and generation of derivatives. This subcommittee decided on the standardization of all acquired datasets into the Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS). A custom instance in the Longitudinal Online Research and Imaging System (LORIS) platform was developed to host the collected biosensor data. Once data are uploaded to LORIS, files collected via infant and heart rate sensors are processed through dedicated pipelines which are implemented by the high-performance computing (HPC) facility at the University of Minnesota through CBRAIN – a web-based software that integrates LORIS with the HPC.

For the annual data releases, the HBCD Study will provide access to processed variables in addition to raw data files.

For the infant movement sensor, 4 Continuous Wave Accelerometer files (CWA) for each 72-hour collection (1 calibration file for each leg and 1 72-hour data file for each leg) are generated. The CWA files are uploaded into LORIS. Following BIDS motion extension, (Jeung et al., 2024) an extension to the BIDS for motion data, files are converted to Tab Separated Values (TSV) and associated metadata are generated using JavaScript Object Notation. Organized raw data files following BIDS as well as processed derivatives (listed in Infant Activity (by Measuring Leg Movements)) will be released in data releases, along with sensor questionnaire data. The code used to obtain the processed derivatives from the raw data will also be released. The raw data contain the tri-axial accelerometer and gyroscope data, along with timestamps and metadata.

For the heart rate sensor, 1 comma-separated values (CSV) file for each 72-hour data collection is generated. The CSV files are uploaded into LORIS. Following BIDS physiological and other continuous recordings extension, (Jeung et al., 2024) files are converted to Tab Separated Values (TSV) and associated metadata are generated. Raw data in BIDS format and processed derivatives (listed in Infant Sleep (by Measuring Heart Rate)) will be released in data releases, along with sensor questionnaire data. The raw data contain the heart rate (HR), the blood oxygen saturation (SpO2), and the respiratory rate (RR), along with their respective measures of reliability, timestamps and metadata.

DEI considerations

The WG-NTW has incorporated DEI consideration and guidelines in the study design and protocol in accordance with the overall principles of equitability and accessibility characterizing the HBCD Study (WG-DEI).

We acknowledge that, from an historical perspective, data collection via wearable devices has been preferentially conducted in individuals who are disproportionately non-Hispanic White, located in high-income urban setting, and of higher-socio-economic status (Zinzuwadia and Singh, 2022, Cho et al., 2022, Bent et al., 2020). Historically, the lack of representative and diverse study populations has produced identifiable bias in wearable devices and sensors used in human biosignals (Perez et al., 2019, Webb et al., 2022). In fact, some racial and ethnic communities – especially populations who have faced historical oppression and marginalization due to their race, ethnicity, gender and/or biological sex – are not only disproportionately excluded from research studies, but have also been directly or indirectly harmed by biases in the clinical guidelines derived from data collected in research studies (Cox, 2023, Washington et al., 2023). As an example, in 2021, a report issued by the FDA warned that at-home, pulse oximeters used in the home environment might not recognize severe hypoxemia in non-White people (Sjoding et al., 2020, Fawzy et al., 2022, Gottlieb et al., 2022).

The WG-NTW has committed time, training, and implementation resources towards ensuring that families could participate in the HBCD Study and the biosensor data were collected in an equitable, respectful and inclusive manner. To achieve these goals, we worked closely with the Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion WG (WG-DEI) and continue to incorporate feedback from a wide range of consortium members. These implementation strategies aim to make study participation more acceptable to diverse communities, and ultimately generate evidence that is generalizable, hence truly translatable to the general population (Hamideh and Nebeker, 2020).

The criteria informing the WG-NTW decision on which wearable sensors to use in the HBCD Study were informed by the DHC (see Decision Making Framework) (Nebeker et al., 2020). The following section provides concrete examples of implementation of DEI principles. Grounded in accepted ethical principles of behavioral and biomedical research, the DHC has identified several constructs for collectively shaping responsible and ethical practices in the conduct of digital health research. In fact, the ethical principles foundational to the DHC (and its four domains) are grounded in the inspiring values of Diversity, Equity, Inclusion, and Belonging.

The DHC domain of Access and Useability prompts the researcher to evaluate selection of a wearable sensor based on whether the technology has been tested and validated with the populations eligible for study inclusion (Hamideh and Nebeker, 2020). As previously discussed, inaccuracies in HR detection may be detected in scenarios where the devices (and their algorithm for the extraction of derivatives) utilized were not tested nor validate in diverse populations (Bent et al., 2020). The HR sensor used the HBCD Study underwent a series of systematic validations to ensure the accurate and reliable detection of biosignals across the full range of skin tones (manuscript in preparation). Lastly, members of the WG-NTW monitor and discuss the study survey responses and feedback provided by the study participants and research team members, respectively. This qualitative data provides an invaluable insight to better understand the opportunity around acceptability and usability for advancing equitable data collection across racial and ethnic groups. The process of combining qualitative and quantitative data have the potential of informing innovative holistic approaches for conducting large-scale research studies in the wild for the years to come (McElwain et al., 2024). As the HBCD Study continues to grow and evolve, the presented DEI framework will continue to inform and promote a closer integration of the feedback provided by research participants into the HBCD Study Protocol.

Conclusions

The members of WG-NTW have spearheaded the design, deployment, and data QC and processing pipelines of the biosensor data collection in the HBCD Study. The membership includes experts in analytical, contextual, procedural, and technical aspects of remote data collection and includes Principal Investigators, Co-Investigators, Post-doctoral fellows, Project Coordinators, and Research Staff. As a result, this interdisciplinary team brings multiple perspectives and expertise which are key for monitoring a study of this large scale and involving such a diverse representation of study participants. At present, researchers and staff are finalizing the implementation of the data processing pipeline that will generate derivatives to be shared with researchers in the scientific community in the upcoming data releases.

It is worth noting that the utilization of novel and wearable technologies in a large observational study encompasses a variety of anticipated and unanticipated challenges which require special consideration from a logistical as well as analytical perspective. Algorithms and data pipelines need to evolve hand-in-hand with the updates in terms of hardware/software/firmware to guarantee the reliability and reproducibility of the results. This aspect is crucial to ensure researchers are equipped with high-quality data with the goal of deriving normative data and investigating relationships across a wealth of data domains, dimensions, and constructs. As a result, the constructs presented in this work may be subject to change upon evaluation of the status of data collection and quality. Nonetheless, it is worth highlighting that the decision making framework illustrated in this work may inform the design of future studies involving scalable solutions for the collection of remote wearable data.

At present, several challenges continue to limit the widespread adoption of wearable technologies in large research studies. A primary limiting factor is the lack of wearable devices with large and extensive validation studies and systematic evaluation of the generated metrics. Information on the wearable devices and/or their performance are often only available from the manufacturers, thus lacking objective and independent validation by a third-party entity. For many devices, validity testing is only conducted in small trials and cohorts of individuals not representative of different racial and ethnic groups. Further, most metrics have been created and validated in adults and are often not appropriate for use with infants. As a result, researchers often face difficulties at the decision making stage of comparing the validity and usability of different technologies for the collection of biosignals. Lastly, in the context of large studies, it is worth noting that the rapid growth and development of wearable solutions has the shortcoming of dealing with hardware/software/firmware updates, which may contribute additional burden to the research staff and require agile coordination among the different entities involved in the data collection and analysis workflows.
