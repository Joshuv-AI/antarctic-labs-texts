The expression and clinical significance of ARHGAP25 in osteosarcoma based on bioinformatics analysis
Authors: Xiaoqian Liu, Siyuan Zhang, Dong Wang, Kaili Lv, Yonggui Wang, Lei Peng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractARHGAP25, a member of the ARHGAP family, encodes a negative regulator of Rho-GTPase that is important for actin remodeling, cell polarity, and cell migration. ARHGAP25 is down-regulated in a variety of solid tumors and promotes cancer cell growth, migration, and invasion. However, nothing is understood about ARHGAP25’s biological function in osteosarcoma. This work used qPCR and WB to confirm the expression of ARHGAP25 in osteosarcoma following the initial analysis of its expression in pan-cancer. For GO and KEGG analysis, we have chosen 300 genes from the TARGET osteosarcoma data that had the strongest positive correlation with ARHGAP25, and we created nomogram and calibration charts. We simultaneously overexpressed ARHGAP25 in osteosarcoma cells to examine its impact on apoptosis and proliferation. By using MSP, we determined their methylation status in osteosarcoma cells and normal bone cells. We observed that ARHGAP25 was significantly downregulated in a range of malignancies, including osteosarcoma, and was associated with poor patient outcomes. The decrease of ARHGAP25 expression in osteosarcoma is related to DNA methylation. Overexpression of ARHGAP25 induced apoptosis and inhibited the proliferation of osteosarcoma cells in vitro. In addition, ARHGAP25 is also associated with immune-related pathways in osteosarcoma. These findings suggest that ARHGAP25 is a valuable prognostic biomarker in osteosarcoma patients.Keywords: Osteosarcoma, ARHGAP25, Methylation, BiomarkerSubject terms: Cancer, Oncology

Introduction

Adolescence is the peak period of risk for osteosarcoma, the most common cancer of the bones with a high potential for local invasion and metastasis. In most osteosarcoma cases, pulmonary metastasis is the most common cause of death, but its origin is not fully understood. Patients with unresectable or recurrent osteosarcoma have a poor prognosis and require combination treatment in addition to thorough surgical excision of all detected lesions. Thus, a greater understanding of osteosarcoma metastasis is crucial for finding more reliable and effective biomarkers to aid in early diagnosis, survival evaluation, and treatment.

ARHGAP25 (Rho GTPase Activating Protein 25) is a negative regulator of the Rho family of GTPases1. Recent research has demonstrated that ARHGAP25 boosted the proliferation, migration, and invasion of cancer cells in solid tumors such as colorectal cancer (CRC), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), and lung cancer2–6. The p.G218R unique heterozygous missense mutation in ARHGAP25 alters the function of bone cells, most likely leading to poor bone resorption and turnover and, as a result, increased skeletal fragility and fracture risk7. In vitro, the overexpression of ARHGAP25 prevented osteosarcoma cells from growing, migrating, and undergoing epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT)8. However, little is known about how ARHGAP25 works biologically in osteosarcoma. As a result, research into the function of ARHGAP25 will help us comprehend the molecular processes underlying the advancement of osteosarcoma.

To methodically convey our findings, we created a flow chart (Fig. 1). According to our findings, in numerous kinds of cancers, including osteosarcoma, ARHGAP25 is dramatically downregulated and is linked to a bad outcome for patients. There was a link between low ARHGAP25 expression and worse total survival and metastasis. Additionally, a reduction in the expression of ARHGAP25 in osteosarcoma is linked to CpG methylation. It was also predicted that ARHGAP25 played a role in controlling cancer and immune response pathways. These results indicate that ARHGAP25's low expression is a useful biomarker for disease prognosis in osteosarcoma patients.

Results and discussion

ARHGAP25 expression in pan-cancer

It was found that ARHGAP25 expression was high in 11 types of cancer in TCGA and GTEx pan-cancer data, including Lymphoid Neoplasm Diffuse Large B-cell Lymphoma (DLBC), Esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), Head and Neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), Kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), Kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), Acute Myeloid Leukemia (LAML), Brain Lower Grade Glioma (LGG), Skin Cutaneous Melanoma (SKCM), and Stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD). However, its expression in Bladder Urothelial Carcinoma (BLCA), Breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), Colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), Kidney Chromophobe (KICH), Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), Lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), Pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), Prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), Rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), and Uterine Corpus Endometrial Carcinoma (UCEC) was minimal (Fig. 2A,B). Subsequently, we further detected the mRNA expression of ARHGAP25 in osteosarcoma by GEO data, we found that ARHGAP25 was low expressed in GSE36001, GSE16088 osteosarcoma cells, and tissues (Fig. 3A,B). Western blot and qPCR analysis revealed that ARHGAP25 mRNA and protein were markedly low-expressed in the human osteosarcoma cells compared to hFOB 1.19 cells (Fig. 2C,D). The results indicate that human osteosarcomas exhibit downregulation of ARHGAP25.

ARHGAP25 expression is related to patient prognosis in cancer

The association between ARHGAP25 expression and overall survival was examined in the TCGA and TARGET cohort to determine the value of ARHGAP25 in predicting the prognosis of cancer patients. A lower ARHGAP25 expression was associated with worse overall survival in TARGET-osteosarcoma (P = 0.037, Fig. 3C), TCGA-LUAD (P < 0.001, Fig. 3E), and TCGA-SKCM (P < 0.001, Fig. 3F). On the contrary, a higher ARHGAP25 expression was related to poorer overall survival in TCGA-LGG (P < 0.001, Fig. 3D). This indicates that the expression of ARHGAP25 mRNA has varying effects on prognosis in different tumors.

Models for individual prognostic prediction

Many malignancies can be prognostically assessed better with nomograms than with conventional TNM staging9. We constructed a prognostic nomogram (Fig. 4A) based on all statistically significant prognostic factors in Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis and drew a calibration curve to test the efficiency of the nomogram. The nomogram includes metastasis, age, and ARHGAP25 to predict the total survival period, and its C index is 0.703 (0.661–0.744). The calibration curve makes an ideal prediction for the nomogram of clinical results at 1, 3, and 5 years (Fig. 4B). The nomogram, in general, is a model to forecast the long-term survival rate of osteosarcoma patients.

Correlation and enrichment analyses

Based on TARGET-osteosarcoma data, we analyzed the connection between ARHGAP25 and other genes to predict ARHGAP25 function, including associated pathways. The hottest 20 genes with the highest positive and negative correlations were shown in Fig. 4C. To create a PPI network, the top 300 genes that are most strongly correlated with ARHGAP25 were chosen (Fig. 4D). Then, depending on the first 300 genes, we used the Metascape database to investigate the function of ARHGAP25, including associated pathways. Regulation of cell activation, positive regulation of immune response, leukocyte activation, regulation of defense response, inflammatory response, cellular response to cytokine stimulus, negative regulation of immune system process, side of membrane, regulation of response to biotic stimulus, adaptive immune response, negative regulation of immune response, T cell activation, leukocyte migration, regulation of cell killing, response to bacterium, regulation of B cell proliferation, myeloid leukocyte activation, regulation of interleukin-12 production, and endocytic vesicle were the gene terms associated with ARHGAP25, as determined by functional enrichment and Gene Ontology analyses (Fig. 4E). Additionally, the top 300 genes in B cell receptor signaling pathway, Staphylococcus aureus infection, Yersinia infection, Primary immunodeficiency, Neutrophil extracellular trap formation, Cytotoxicity caused by natural killer cells, Migration of leukocytes across the endothelium, Pertussis, Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis, Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, Signaling pathway for C-type lectin receptors, Activation of platelets, Malaria, Fc epsilon RI signaling pathway, Pathways in cancer, African trypanosomiasis, Acute myeloid leukemia, Ferroptosis, and Human immunodeficiency virus 1 infection showed enrichment and crosstalk according to the KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 4F). Based on these results, ARHGAP25 may have an immune-related pathway in osteosarcoma.

ARHGAP25 expression is associated with DNA methylation

A common epigenetic process that promotes cancer growth is DNA methylation. Normal methylation patterns are typically disturbed in tumor cells, where global hypomethylation coexists with localized hypermethylation. Similar to deletions or mutations, hypermethylation inside the promoter of tumor suppressor genes silences the gene and gives cells an edge in growing10. As a result of the methylation of CpG in endothelial cells in arteries, CpG-SNPs might alter ARHGAP25 expression11. Based on the EWAS Data Hub, Osteosarcoma tissue has a considerably greater methylation level of ARHGAP25 than normal bone tissue, and the prognosis is poorer the higher the methylation level (Fig. 5A–D). According to MSP results, hFOB1.19 cells showed partial methylation of the ARHGAP25 promoter, while MG63 cells and HOS cells had complete methylation (Fig. 6A). Together, our findings demonstrated that the expression of the ARHGAP25 gene in osteosarcoma is regulated by promoter methylation.

Overexpression of ARHGAP25 induced apoptosis and inhibited proliferation of osteosarcoma cells in vitro

We transfected MG63 and HOS cells, which produce low amounts of endogenous ARHGAP25, the null expression vector was used as a control to detect the biological effects of ARHGAP25 on osteosarcoma. By qPCR and Western blot, we confirmed the ARHGAP25 level in these cells (P < 0.05, Fig. 6B,C). An analysis of flow cytometry data showed a significant increase in apoptotic cells after ARHGAP25 overexpression (P < 0.05, Figs. 6D and 7A). EdU-555 cell proliferation assay confirmed that ARHGAP25 overexpression inhibited the proliferation of osteosarcoma cells (P < 0.05, Fig. 7B). It appears that overexpression of ARHGAP25 promotes apoptosis in osteosarcoma cells and suppresses proliferation.

Discussion

The disorder of Rho/Rac/Cdc42-like GTPases caused by the genetic changes in the ARHGAP family of genes makes them cancer-associated genes1. ARHGAP22 is the main molecule that regulates Rac and Rho signaling, which causes melanoma cells to metastasize12–14. ARHGAP25 and ARHGAP24 are homologous to these molecules. ARHGAP24 has a good anti-tumor effect on a variety of solid tumors15–18. Previous research has indicated that ARHGAP25 has an impact on solid tumor cell proliferation and invasion2–6,19. In addition, ARHGAP25 may control phagocytosis by altering the actin cytoskeleton locally20. In comparison to osteoblasts, macrophages, and osteoclasts had a greater relative expression of ARHGAP257. Its pattern of expression and biological importance in osteosarcoma, however, is yet unknown. There was a significant reduction in ARHGAP25 protein and mRNA expression in osteosarcomas. This led us to further explore the significance of ARHGAP25 in osteosarcoma.

Our research using TCGA and GTEx data from UCSC Xena revealed that ARHGAP25 was highly expressed in DLBC, ESCA, GBM, HNSC, HNSA, KIRC, KIRP, LAML, LGG, SKCM, and STAD, whereas low expression was observed in BLCA, BRCA, COAD, KICH, LUAD, LUSC, PAAD, PRAD, READ, and UCEC. In addition, ARHGAP25 was a low expression in osteosarcoma in the GSE36001 and GSE16088 datasets. The varying levels of ARHGAP25 expression in various tumor types may be a reflection of various underlying processes and functions. We also discovered that the expression and prognosis of ARHGAP25 were different in different tumors, such as osteosarcoma and low-grade gliomas. In osteosarcoma and LUAD, its poor expression was related to a bad outcome. According to these results, ARHGAP25 may be used to assess cancer patients' prognoses.

There is a significant alteration in the methylation landscape of cancer cells, which affects both DNA methylation and post-translational modification of histones. This alteration results in altered gene expression that controls tumor characteristics21. Research has indicated a connection between the methylation status of ARHGAP25 and the emergence and progression of various diseases22–24. According to our research, osteosarcoma cells have considerably more methylated ARHGAP25 than osteoblasts. Osteosarcoma tissue has a considerably greater methylation level of ARHGAP25 than healthy bone tissue, as well as the prognosis is poorer the higher the methylation level. In addition, the DNA methyltransferase inhibitor decitabine reverses the epigenetic silencing of tumor suppressor genes and inhibits the progression of osteosarcoma25–27. Therefore, we speculate that the low expression of ARHGAP25 in osteosarcoma is related to DNA methylation and that the application of DNA methyltransferase inhibitors may reverse the expression of ARHGAP25 in osteosarcoma.

A close association was found between ARHGAP25 and immune-related terms in GO, including positive regulation of immune response, leukocyte activation, cellular response to cytokine stimulus, negative regulation of immune system process, adaptive immune response, negative regulation of immune response, T cell activation, leukocyte migration, regulation of cell killing, regulation of B cell proliferation, myeloid leukocyte activation, and regulation of interleukin-12 production. The KEGG analysis revealed that ARHGAP25 contributes to the signaling pathway involving B cells, Primary immunodeficiency, Natural killer cell mediated cytotoxicity, Leukocyte transendothelial migration, and Human immunodeficiency virus 1 infection. The immune system can detect and eliminate aberrant cells in the body while constantly monitoring them28. Cancer cells with high immunogenicity may evade immunological eradication by compromising immune system components that would otherwise eradicate them29. Most osteosarcoma specimens may not contain enough immune infiltration to elicit meaningful responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) alone due to the immunogenomic landscape in osteosarcoma being characterized by genomic complexity and significant clinical heterogeneity30. ARHGAP25 was found to negatively influence immune cell phagocytosis and neutrophilic granulocyte transendothelial migration20,31. This suggests that up-regulating the expression of ARHGAP25 in osteosarcoma may contribute to immune cell infiltration.

Our studies have shown that ARHGAP25 methylation levels in osteosarcoma cells and tissues are significantly higher than in healthy bone cells and tissues and that higher methylation levels are associated with a worse prognosis. Histone DNA methylation and post-translational modification will be significantly impacted by changes in the methylation landscape of cancer cells, which will influence the expression of genes that regulate tumor features21. The methylation status of ARHGAP25 has been linked to the emergence and progression of several disorders22–24, according to research. ARHGAP25 is a differentially methylated gene in osteosarcoma32, which is consistent with the results of this study. According to reports, DNA methyltransferase inhibitor decitabine induces DNA demethylation of many proapoptotic genes (such as GADD45A), leading to apoptosis in osteosarcoma cell lines (U2OS and MG63) and tumor cell models in tumor-bearing mice27. Unbalanced cell growth and cell death suppression both contribute to the development of cancer33, while overexpression of ARHGAP25 can inhibit osteosarcoma cell proliferation and promote apoptosis. This suggests that overexpression of ARHGAP25 can treat osteosarcoma by having an impact comparable to that of DNA methyltransferase inhibitors.

Conclusions

ARHGAP25 expression in osteosarcoma is regulated by promoter methylation and affects immune cell infiltration. Overexpression of ARHGAP25 induces apoptosis in osteosarcoma cells and suppresses cell proliferation. ARHGAP25 could be a therapeutic target for osteosarcoma.

Materials and methods

Data collection

The UCSC Xena database was used to obtain the TCGA total cancer data, ARHGAP25 expression, and clinical information. A combined set of normal tissues and tumor tissues was obtained from the TCGA and GTEx databases to evaluate the ARHGAP25 mRNA level. GEO database was used to retrieve osteosarcoma microarray data, including GSE36001 (platform: GPL6102) and GSE16088 (platform: GPL96).

Correlation and enrichment analyses

TARGET-Osteosarcoma data were used to calculate the value of the Pearson correlation value of ARHGAP25 and other mRNAs. To evaluate how ARHGAP25 functions, we selected 300 genes with the greatest positive correlation to it for enrichment analysis. GO and KEGG were analyzed using the Metascape database34.

Epigenome-wide association study data hub (EWAS data hub)

In the latest version of the EWAS Data Hub, 75,344 DNA methylation array data samples are incorporated and a normalization technique is utilized to eliminate batch effects between samples. Reference DNA methylation profiles are offered by EWAS Data Hub for a variety of situations, including 81 tissue/cell types (including 25 distinct types of blood cells and brain tissue), six ancestry groups, and 67 disorders (including 39 cancers)35.

Cell lines and culture

Procell Life Science and Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) was where the MG63, HOS Osteosarcoma, and hFOB 1.19 cells (Fetal limb tissue from spontaneous abortion) were acquired. MG63, HOS, and hFOB 1.19 cells were cultured according to the manufacturer's requirements. 0.25% trypsin was used to remove the cells every 72 h for passage, and the medium was changed every 24 h.

Cell transfection

6 well plates were seeded with MG63 and HOS cells at a density of 3105/well. We transfected MG63 and HOS cells with overexpression of ARHGAP25, and transfected cells with empty expression vectors were used as controls. We used Lipofectamine 2000 for transient transfection.

qPCR

RNA was extracted according to the reagent manufacturer's requirements and reverse-transcribed into cDNA, followed by qPCR. Table 1 lists the amplification primers and sequence information.

Western blot

After combining Phenyl methyl sulfonyl fluoride with Western and IP cell lysis buffer, the total protein was recovered by dividing the cells on ice. The Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit was used to assess each sample's protein level. A PVDF membrane was used to divide a total of 30 g of protein using SDS-PAGE. ARHGAP25 antibody was used for a Western blot study. The internal benchmark was provided by the β-actin antibody.

DNA extraction and methylation specificity PCR (MSP)

DNA was extracted according to the requirements of the reagent manufacturer. A kit for DNA bisulfite conversion was used to purify 2 g of DNA. The specific methylation PCR primer of the ARHGAP25 promoter region was constructed (Table 2). Electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel was applied to identify the PCR products.

Flow cytometry analysis

After being cleaned in ice-cold PBS, cells removed from culture dishes were fixed in ethanol in cold PBS. Cells were resuspended in chilled PBS after being pelleted in a chilled centrifuge. The cells were treated with 2 mg/ml of bovine pancreatic RNAase and 20 mg/ml of propidium iodide for 20 min at room temperature following 30 min of incubation at 37 °C. FLOWJO software was used to analyze data from 5 × 104 cells analyzed on a BD FACSCanto.

EdU staining

EdU staining was performed using the BeyoClickTM EdU Cell Proliferation Kit with Alexa Fluor 555, according to the manufacturer's instructions. 6-well plates were used to seed the transfected osteosarcoma cells. Incubation was carried out at 37 °C for 2–4 h after the addition of EdU working solution to each well after 16 h. Incubation with PBS containing 0.3% TritonX-100 for 10–15 min followed by staining with Click Additive Solution was completed after washing the cells in PBS and fixing them with 4% paraformaldehyde. DAPI was then used to stain the cells.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed in R (version 3.6.3). Unpaired samples were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test and Wilcoxon rank-sum test to determine the expression of ARHGAP25. The log-rank test was assessed using the surviver package and the survival package to evaluate the impact of ARHGAP25 on the overall survival rate.
