High fever, low GCS, and high lactate: independent risk factors for progression of influenza-associated encephalopathy to acute necrotizing encephalopathy in children
Authors: Dongmei Zhang, Yuting Yang, Zhong Chen, Dan Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfluenza-associated encephalopathy(IAE), as the most severe neurological complication of influenza virus infection, has become an increasingly severe public health challenge in the global pediatric field. Acute necrotizing encephalopathy(ANE) is a malignant subtype of IAE and currently shows a significant upward trend. Therefore, early identification of independent risk factors for the progression of IAE to ANE is crucial.MethodA retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of children diagnosed with IAE who were hospitalized at Wuhan Children’s Hospital from January 2020 to January 2025. According to whether ANE occurred during hospitalization, the children were divided into the ANE group and Non-ANE for comparative study. SPSS was used to analyze the risk factors of ANE.ResultsAmong 55 children diagnosed with IAE, 25 progressed to ANE. Compared with Non-ANE group, the risk factors for progression to ANE were peak fever, GCS score, and lactate. The areas under the curve (AUC) for distinguishing IAE and ANE using peak fever, GCS score, and lactate were 0.751, 0.675, and 0.765, respectively. The sensitivity and specificity of a peak fever ≥ 39.7℃ were 60% and 83.33%, respectively; for a GCS score ≤ 5, the sensitivity was 44% and specificity 96.67%; for lactate > 2.87, the sensitivity was 60% and specificity 83.33%. When the three risk factors were combined to diagnose ANE, the AUC was 0.912, with sensitivity and specificity of 72% and 96.67%, respectively.ConclusionThe combination of high fever peak, low GCS score, and high lactate level can serve as early predictors of ANE; however, due to the limitations of retrospective single-center small sample data, further confirmation from multi-center large sample studies is needed in the future.Keywords: High fever, Levels of consciousness impairment, High lactic acid, ANE

Acute Necrotizing Encephalopathy (ANE) is the most severe neurological complication of influenza virus infection, characterized by the rapid progression of seizures, consciousness disturbances, and symmetrical thalamic necrosis following sudden high fever [1]. This rare but highly fatal disease [2] shows a significant age preference among influenza-related neurological complications—children under 5 years old account for 71.6% of all cases [3]. Global data indicate that the incidence of ANE among children with influenza is approximately 0.006‰ to 0.029‰ [4–6], but the mortality rate reaches as high as 30%. Among survivors, only 10% fully recover, while over 60% are left with permanent neurological damage such as spastic paralysis, cognitive impairment, or epilepsy [7]. Notably, in recent years, the incidence of ANE in China has been on the rise. During the 2022–2023 influenza season, the incidence rates in southern and northern China increased by 3.8 times and 3.0 times respectively during peak influenza activity, with the peak occurring later but rising more rapidly [8]. Research at Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center shows that the incidence of neurological complications in severe influenza cases is as high as 35.8%, with ANE being the primary fatal neurological complication [9]. This severe situation highlights that ANE has become a “hidden killer” of children’s health. Therefore, early identification of risk factors for the progression of influenza-associated encephalopathy to acute necrotizing encephalopathy is crucial.

Most domestic and international reports on influenza-related encephalopathy progressing to acute necrotizing encephalopathy in pediatric intensive care units focus on inflammatory markers and cerebrospinal fluid proteins after the child’s admission. These test results take time to obtain, and this period may be especially critical for children whose influenza-related encephalopathy progresses to acute necrotizing encephalopathy. This study investigates the risk factors for the progression of IAES to ANE, aiming to achieve early identification and intervention. The risk factors in our study can be obtained immediately, which saves more time for these children.

Materials and methods

Research subjects

This study is a retrospective case-control study. Data of 6,647 pediatric influenza patients treated at Wuhan Children’s Hospital Affiliated to Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology from January 2020 to January 2025 were collected. We enrolled 55 influenza patients diagnosed with influenza-associated encephalopathy, all of whom had no evidence of co-infections and received oseltamivir after diagnosis, with Nonsteroidal Anti-inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs) administered for fever management during the febrile phase. This study complied with the standards of medical ethics, and the current study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, which was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Wuhan Children’s Hospital (Approval No.2024R044-E01) without the need for written informed consent.

Research methods

Clinical data includes baseline information (sex, age, weight, height), symptoms and signs (fever peak, number of convulsions, DFOC, duration of convulsions, body temperature during convulsions, nasal congestion, runny nose, cough, wheezing, vomiting, diarrhea, GCS, respiration and heart rate), medical history (febrile seizures, epilepsy), invasive treatment methods (mechanical ventilation (MV), blood purification), laboratory tests upon admission (pathogen detection (Influenza A, Influenza B), complete blood count (white blood cells(WBC), neutrophils(NEU), lymphocytes(LYM), monocytes(MONO), red blood cells(RBC), hemoglobin(Hb), platelets(PLT)), inflammatory markers (procalcitonin(PCT), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein(hs-CRP)), Blood lactate(Lac), organ function indicators (liver function, myocardial enzymes, kidney function, electrolytes, coagulation function), C3, C4, immune function monitoring (CD3 + CD4 + T, CD3 + T, NK, CD19 + B, CD3 + CD8 + T, CD4+/CD8 + ratio), cytokines (IL-6, IL-10, IL-2, TNF-a, IL-4))

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 software. Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test whether the data followed a normal distribution. Measurement data conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and compared with Student’s t-test. For non-normal data, they used Mann-Whitney U (reporting median [IQR]), and for categorical data χ² or Fisher (frequencies and percentages). Variables with statistically significant differences in univariate analysis (p < 0.05) were included in multivariate logistic regression analysis. The correlation and strength between the predictor variables in the regression model were measured using the variance inflation factor (VIF), in which VIF < 10 indicated “free of covariance”. The stepwise regression method was used to screen the independent risk factors for ANE in children with influenza-associated encephalopathy. The maximum Youden index for continuous variables was calculated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to determine the cutoff values and subsequently converted to dichotomous variables based on these cutoff values. GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 statistical analysis software (GraphPad, California, USA) was used to plot forest plots. P < 0.05 is considered a statistically significant difference.

Multivariate analysis of risk factors

The clinical data of the two groups of children were compared, and a stepwise regression method was adopted. Variables with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) in the baseline tables (Table 1 + Table 2) were subjected to univariate logistic regression analysis, and the meaningful indicators (P < 0.05) were then included in the multivariate Logistic regression analysis to identify independent risk factors. Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that for every 1 °C increase in peak temperature, the risk of IAE progressing to ANE increased by approximately 3.55 times (OR = 3.549, 95% CI: 1.55–8.1, P = 0.003), for every 1-point increase in GCS score, the risk of IAE progressing to ANE decreased by 34% (OR = 0.663, 95% CI: 0.494–0.889, P = 0.006), and for every 1 mmol/l increase in Lac, the risk of IAE progressing to ANE increased by 3.2 times (OR = 3.203, 95% CI: 1.332–7.702, P = 0.009) (Table 3; Fig. 1).

Predictive value of fever peak, GCS score, and Lac in differentiating ANE

The areas under the ROC curve (AUC) for fever peak, GCS score, and Lac in differentiating ANE were 0.751 (95% CI: 0.616–0.886, P = 0.0003), 0.675 (95% CI: 0.523–0.828, P = 0.0244), and 0.765 (95% CI: 0.639–0.892, P < 0.0001), respectively. According to the corresponding cutoff values at the maximum Youden index, when fever peak ≥ 39.7℃, the sensitivity and specificity for ANE occurrence were 60% and 83.33%, respectively. When the GCS score ≤ 5 points, the sensitivity and specificity were 44% and 96.67%, respectively. When Lac > 2.87, the sensitivity and specificity were 60% and 83.33%, respectively. The AUC of the combined diagnosis of ANE by the three risk factors was 0.912 (95% CI: 0.839–0.985, P < 0.0001), with sensitivity and specificity of 72% and 96.67%, respectively (Table 4; Fig. 2).

Discussion

IAE is a severe neurological complication of influenza virus infection, which occurs in both adults and children. The incidence rate in children is higher than that in adults, and ANE is its most devastating manifestation [10]. ANE is characterized by bilateral thalamic, brainstem, and sometimes cerebral white matter necrosis, accompanied by high morbidity and mortality (30%) [7]. Despite advances in critical care, early identification of risk factors for IAE progression to ANE remains critical for timely intervention. In this investigation, we identified three independent prognostic factors associated with the progression of IAE to ANE: peak fever temperature, GCS score, and Lac level. These associations were derived from a retrospective cohort analysis of IAE patients. Below, we contextualize these findings within the pathophysiology of influenza virus infection, integrate them with recent evidence, and discuss their clinical implications.

Our results demonstrate that a higher fever peak (≥ 39.7℃) independently increased the risk of ANE progression (OR = 3.549, 95%CI: 1.55–8.1, P = 0.003). This is consistent with the view proposed in Song’s research [6] that the ANE group is more prone to high fever. Hyperthermia in IAE is not merely a symptom but a key mediator of neuroinflammation and tissue damage. Influenza viruses infect respiratory epithelial cells, triggering innate immune activation and massive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [11] and pyrogenic mediators (prostaglandin E2) [12]. However, in severe IAE, this “cytokine storm” becomes dysregulated, crossing the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and amplifying neuroinflammation [13]. After influenza virus infection, the massive release of inflammatory factors causes the loss of ZO-1 in the blood-brain barrier, which in turn damages tight junctions (TJs) and leads to the leakage of serum proteins into parenchymal brain cells (neurons and glial cells) [14].Cerebral metabolic demand increases by 5–13% per 1℃rise in temperature, In ANE, the thalamus and brainstem—regions with high metabolic activity—are particularly vulnerable to hypoxia when oxygen supply fails to meet demand.

A lower GCS score (indicating impaired consciousness) was strongly associated with increased ANE risk (OR = 0.663, 95%CI: 0.494–0.889, P = 0.006). This is consistent with the view proposed in Song’s research [6] that the proportion of patients with disturbance of consciousness is higher in the ANE group. GCS reflects the integrity of cortical and brainstem functions, which are early targets of influenza-associated neuroinflammation. The protective effect of higher GCS may be attributed to: After influenza virus infection, the body’s immune system releases a large number of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α), forming a “cytokine storm.” These factors enter the central nervous system through the bloodstream, attacking the endothelial cells of brain blood vessels, leading to blood-brain barrier disruption. Subsequently, cytokines directly infiltrate brain tissue, causing cerebral edema, neuronal necrosis, and glial cell activation. Regions such as the thalamus and brainstem, where the blood-brain barrier is weaker, are more easily affected, presenting as symmetrical necrotic lesions, directly impacting consciousness regulation functions [15].

Elevated lactate (Lac > 2.87 mmol/L) was identified as an independent risk factor for ANE progression (OR = 3.203, 95%CI: 1.332–7.702, P = 0.009). Lactate accumulation in IAE reflects a mismatch between cerebral oxygen supply and demand, driven by multiple overlapping mechanisms: Cytokines damage the blood-brain barrier, causing increased vascular permeability and microthrombosis, leading to cerebral ischemia and hypoxia. Hypoxia further activates glycolysis, significantly increasing lactate production [15]. Lactate accumulation can cross the blood-brain barrier and enter brain tissue; excessive lactate buildup increases the brain’s acidic environment, thereby affecting neuronal function and brain metabolic balance [16]. Lactate directly influences Pyruvate kinase isoform M2 (PKM2) gene expression through histone lactylation, accelerating the glycolytic pathway [17]. Additionally, through the cooperation of Lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) and Lactate dehydrogenase B (LDHB), lactate production is further increased [18]. During the neuroinflammatory response phase, the continuous release of inflammatory factors triggers oxidative stress and increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction. Mitochondrial impairment directly affects the energy supply to nerve cells, further reducing the efficiency of neural signaling [19]. Mitochondrial dysfunction and energy deficiency may underlie blood-brain barrier disruption and cerebral edema in ANE.

Although this study provides valuable insights for the early prediction of IAE progressing to ANE, it also has some limitations. Due to the small sample size, the results of this single-center retrospective study may lack generalizability. The retrospective design is inherently prone to selection bias and limits the ability to establish causal relationships between risk factors and acute necrotizing encephalopathy. Future research with larger sample sizes and standardized protocols in multicenter prospective studies is needed to validate these findings, strengthen causal inference, and enhance statistical rigor.

Conclusion​

In summary, fever peak, GCS score, and lactate level are independent predictors of IAE progression to ANE, reflecting interconnected pathophysiological processes—viral neuroinflammation, neural reserve, and metabolic crisis. Clinically, these factors enable early risk stratification and guide targeted interventions to reduce ANE-related morbidity and mortality. As influenza seasons persist globally, integrating these metrics into routine care could transform outcomes for this vulnerable population.
