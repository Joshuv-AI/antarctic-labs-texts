Detecting fa leptin receptor mutation in Zucker rats with tetra-primer amplification-refractory mutation system (ARMS)-PCR
Authors: Xinyun Xu, Xinge Hu, Guodong Ma, Tiannan Wang, Jayne Wu, Xiaojuan Zhu, Guoxun Chen, Ling Zhao, Jiangang Chen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractDue to the genetic mutation (fa) in the gene encoding for leptin receptor, homozygous Zucker rats (fa−/−) develop excessive adiposity and become an experimental animal model in obesity and metabolic-related diseases research. Based on tetra-primer amplification refractory mutation system-polymerase chain reaction (ARMS-PCR), we developed a method to quickly genotype Zucker rats with a mutated fa allele from their wildtype littermates. The three genotypes are clearly discriminated on 2.0% agarose gel. Our method can be used as a reliable tool to set up and maintain the breeding colony in animal facilities as well as assign animals to control and treatment groups based on their genotypes for animal studies.Keywords: fa mutation, Leptin receptor, Tetra-primer amplification-refractory mutation system (ARMS), Zucker rats, Obesity

Introduction

Obesity remains a worldwide health crisis [1]. Obesity is second only to smoking as the leading cause of preventable death in the United States [2]. The accumulation of excess body fat negatively impacts health and quality of life, and substantially increases the risks of metabolic diseases, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes (T2DM), and cardiovascular diseases [3]. The aggregate medical cost due to obesity among adults in the United States was estimated at over $260 billion in 2016 [4].

Mounting evidence demonstrates obesity is heritable and multiple genes associated with body weight gain have been identified [5]. Leptin, secreted by adipose tissue, serves as a signal of satiety [6]. The leptin receptor gene (lepr), also known as obesity receptor (Ob-Rb), contains 17 common exons and several isoforms due to alternatively splicing at 3′ exons [7]. Among the leptin receptor isoforms, Ob-Rb is the only isoform with full signal transduction capabilities [[8], [9], [10]]. Leptin binding of leptin receptor (LRb) activates the intracellular LRb-associated Jak2 molecules triggering the activation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway to control food intake and subsequent energy expenditures [11,12], while short isoforms of LR are unable to mediate leptin action due to the absence of intracellular residues 31–36 [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. Major targets of leptin are located in the arcuate nuclei of the hypothalamus. Neurons of the paraventricular, ventromedial, and dorsomedial nuclei of the hypothalamus also have leptin receptors [[16], [17], [18], [19]]. Ob-Rb is heavily glycosylated with a molecular weight of ∼130 kDa [20]. The full-length Ob-Rb isoform contains 816 amino acids in the extracellular ligand-binding domain, 34 amino acids in the transmembrane domain, and 303 amino acids in the intracellular domain [21,22].

Impaired leptin signaling due to homozygous mutation of lepr results in hyperphagia, delayed sexual maturation, infertility, reduced growth hormone, and thyrotropin secretion, as well as early onset of obesity [23]. Animal models are valuable tools for the discovery of innovative strategies for leptin resistance and obesity. Zucker strain and its derivatives, Zucker Fatty (ZF) and Zucker Diabetic Fatty (ZDF) rats, are among the most preferred rodent models to study obesity, T2DM, and leptin signaling [24,25]. A missense A to C (fa mutation) in the lepr gene on chromosome 5 causes a Gln to Pro substitution at amino acid position 269 in its extracellular domain in all the identified isoforms of Ob-Rb protein, short and long forms [26,27]. Consequently, Zucker rats homozygous for the fa allele spontaneously develop obesity, hypercholesterolemia, and hyperlipidemia with normal to mild hyperglycemia [26]. The obese male Zucker rats have severely limited reproductive capacity, partially caused by compromised sperm production during puberty and increased sperm DNA fragmentation [28,29]. The obese Zucker female rats are infertile [30]. Genotyping therefore is essential to differentiate wildtype from heterozygous offspring for breeding, maintaining a colony, and identifying the wildtype or homozygous (fa−/−) rats for obesity and metabolic disorders related experiments [31,32].

Only a few studies have reported genotyping fa mutations in Zucker rats [25,[33], [34], [35], [36], [37]]. Phenotypic identification is one of the preferred methods. However, reliable body weight gain in Zucker fa−/− rats only becomes apparent around 4 weeks of age [37] compared to their lean and heterozygous littermates [25,37], which may not suit investigations requiring early interventions. Alternatively, daily mean core temperature (Tc) was considered an early predictor for fa−/− phenotype. This approach, however, is time-consuming and stressful, therefore, may not be practical [35,38]. Similarly, inguinal fat pad weight plotted against body weight was proposed for genotype identification in Zucker rats [34]. However, identifying the genotype of the pups with the inguinal fat pad will require the euthanasia of the animals or surgical lipectomy [34]. Several molecular approaches have also been developed to identify fa mutation. Because fa mutation creates a restriction site for Msp I, PCR-Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism (PCR-RFLP) can be used to detect the fa genotype [26]. However, the cutting of PCR amplicons by Msp I is not consistently complete, which may lead to genotype misclassification [33]. Durham et al. modified PCR-RFLP method by introducing a single base substitution (C to G) to create a Pvu II restriction site flanking the fa site. The newly created Pvu II site only exists in the wildtype allele. Subsequently, the amplified 101 bp of DNA fragment was digested by Pvu II and then visualized in 3% or higher 3:1 high-resolution blend agarose gel [33,36].

Here, we developed a method using tetra-primer-ARMS-PCR leptin receptor fa mutation identification using the genomic DNA of Zucker rats with Taq DNA polymerase. Compared to other thermophilic DNA polymerases, Taq DNA polymerase does not carry the 3′-5′ exonuclease activity; therefore, it has lower proof-reading activity [39]. In addition, the capacity of Taq DNA polymerase to elongate primers is dramatically handicapped if there is a mismatch at the 3′-end of the primer [[39], [40], [41]]. Tetra-primer ARMS-PCR takes advantage of these limitations of Taq DNA polymerase and turns it into a useful tool to facilitate the identification of single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) [32,[42], [43], [44], [45]].

Materials and methods

Animals

Four heterozygous Zucker rats (two males, two females at age of six weeks, Strain code, 380 fa+/−) were purchased from Charles River (Wilmington, MA, USA). They were paired as one male to one female per cage to obtain offspring. The breeding colony was maintained at Mossman Animal Facility. Body weights of male offspring with different genotypes born to the breeding heterogenous Zucker rats were monitored weekly from the age of 3 weeks old. Animal protocol (Protocol number 1256, June 09, 2020) was approved by the University's IACUC committee.

Genomic DNA extraction

Genomic DNA from the ears or liver tissues of Zucker rats was extracted as described [32,46]. Briefly, biological tissue samples were treated by 180 μL of lysis buffer (50 mmol/L NaOH), incubated at 95 °C for 10 min and then placed on ice. Subsequently, 20 μL of Tris-HCl (1 mol/L, pH 8.0) was added to neutralize the lysis buffer. After brief centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 3 min, the supernatant was ready for PCR reaction. Total genome DNA collected from ear tissue of three male Zucker rats with known genotypes were used to develop tetra-primer ARMS-PCR method. In addition, total genome DNA from archived liver tissues was extracted from 12 male Zucker rats and used in the study to validate our methods with results from PCR-RFLP. The genotypes of these 12 Zucker rats were kept blind to the tetra-primer ARMS-PCR operators. Ear tissue samples of rats aged 14 days and up to 21 days were collected through ear punching. Liver tissues were collected after euthanasia.

Tetra-primer ARMS-PCR was carried out with the Eppendorf Mastercycler X50 (Eppendorf North America, Enfield, CT). The stock concentration of primer is 10 μM. PCR reaction contained 0.25 μL of each primer (therefore the final concentration of each primer is 0.2 μM), 6.25 μL of DreamTaq 2 × PCR master mix (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 2.5 μL of crude extraction supernatant (approximately 250 ng of DNA), and 2.75 μL of water. The PCR was conducted as follows: 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 33 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min. The final elongation step was 72 °C for 7 min. The electrophoresis was carried out on a 2.0% agarose gel and ethidium bromide was used to stain the amplicons. The PCR products were separated on the gel under 110 voltage for 35 min using 1X TAE (Tris-acetate-EDTA) buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Bio-Rad ChemiDoc XRS+ with Image Lab was used for image acquisition (Image LabTM Software, Version 5.2.1, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Sequencing

We used FI and RO pairs to amplify the “A” allele (201 bp), and FO and RI pairs to amplify the “C” allele (263 bp). Amplicon bands were then isolated from the gel with Zymoclean Gel DNA Recovery Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). Genomics Core Facility at the University of Tennessee Knoxville conducted amplicon sequencing on Applied Biosystem 3730 DNA Analyzer (Waltham, MA, USA).

Genotyping with PCR-RFLP

The genotypes of Zucker rats were also verified by PCR-RFLP method, as reported previously [33]. Briefly, PCR primers were designed to generate an amplicon of 101 bp flanking the fa mutation, followed by Pvu II digestion (New England Biolabs, Beverly, MA) for 1.5 h at 37 °C. The results were electrophoresed and visualized on 4% agarose gels with ethidium bromide staining.

Results

We reported a tetra-ARMS-PCR assay to amplify allele-specific genome DNA coding for leptin receptor fa mutation in Zucker rats. In the tetra-primer ARMS-PCR assay, the last nucleotide at the 3-prime end of the inner primer is designed complementary to the target nucleotide. An extra mismatch nucleotide typically within the 3 to 5 bases near single mutation site at the 3-prime end can also be introduced [47,48] to improve the assay's specificity [32,40,48,49]. However, in our study, introducing the second mismatch at position-2 from the 3-prime end in the Reverse Inner (RI) primer led to a dramatical reduction of efficiency of PCR amplification for 263 bp amplicon (Fig. 2); the 263 bp band was discernible; however, the density of the band on the gel cannot be further improved (Fig. 2). Although the exact reason is unknown, it has been reported that mismatch extension can vary up to 100-fold depending on the sequences surrounding the mismatch [40,[50], [51], [52]]. Therefore, in this report, the second mismatch at position −2 from 3-prime end was not introduced in the RI primer.

Results of gradient PCR reaction with the introduction of second mismatch nucleotide at position-2 from the 3-prime end of the RI primer. As shown in the figure, although tetra-primer ARMS-PCR successfully discriminates the different genotype patterns on the gel, the introduction of second point mutation (5′-GATTGTAGAATTCTCTAAATATTTCATCG-3′, “T”, in red) in the RI primer, significantly reduced the efficiency of DreamTaq to generate amplicons from heterozygous (fa+/−) rat genomic DNA as indicated by the low density of the 263 bp band. fa+/+: wildtype; fa+/−: heterozygous, and fa−/− homozygous Zucker rats. Different sizes of PCR amplicons were indicated by red arrows.

To further optimize the PCR reaction parameters, we tested the impact of annealing temperature on the efficiency of the PCR reaction. The annealing temperature is most critical for the success of tetra-primer ARMS-PCR reaction [49]. As shown in Fig. 3, within the annealing temperatures range between 48.3 and 58.6 °C, our method was able to detect the outer band with the anticipated size. The allelic discrimination was also clearly demonstrated on the gel. Therefore, the annealing temperature of 55 °C was selected for the subsequent PCR reactions. The derived PCR products in our tetra-primer ARMS-PCR comprise a 409 bp amplicon from the common DNA region outside the fa mutation site. The appearance of the 409 bp band, was used as the internal control, reflecting the overall quality of genomic DNA extracted from rat tissues [53]. Two additional bands correspond to either heterozygous or homozygous genotypes, respectively. For wildtype (fa+/+), a 201 bp PCR product is amplified by the FI and RO primers (Fig. 3). For homozygotes of the mutant allele, we expect a 263 bp amplicon generated by the FO and RI primers. Two bands of PCR amplicons of 201 bp and 263 bp are expected from heterozygotes (fa+/−). These PCR products differ in length, therefore, can be differentiated easily by agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig. 3).

Patterns of amplicons generated from the tetra-primer ARMS-PCR reaction on agarose gel. DNA samples collected from three Zucker rats with known genotypes (wildtype (fa+/+), heterozygous (fa+/−), and homozygous (fa−/−) were analyzed in a gradient PCR (annealing temperatures from 48.3 to 64.9 °C) to identify an optimal annealing temperature for the assay on the 2.0% agarose gel. Different sizes of PCR amplicons were indicated by red arrows.

Next, our method was used to genotype 12 Zucker rats using genomic DNA extracted from archived liver tissues. The genotype of each Zucker rat was previously identified with PCR-RFLP and was kept blind to the tetra-primer ARMS-PCR operators. As demonstrated in Fig. 4a, three different amplicon patterns were generated which represented wildtype (fa+/+), heterozygous (fa+/−), or homozygous (fa−/−) genotypes, respectively. The patterns of amplicons are in agreement with the expected sizes shown in Fig. 1 and matched the genotyping results with PCR-RFLP (Fig. 4b).

We further verified the genotypes with the body weights of these rats. As expected, the fa−/− homozygous rats were substantially heavier than the wildtype (fa+/+) and heterozygous (fa+/−) rats between the age of 3 and 7 weeks (two-way ANOVA with Student-Newman-Keuls post hoc test; F (2,45) = 9.319, p < 0.001 by genotype; F(4, 45) = 112.585, p < 0.001 by week; F(8, 45) = 0.774, p = 0.628, by genotype and week interaction) (Fig. 5).

Homozygous mutation of fa−/− gene is detrimental to the reproductive function of Zucker rats. Therefore, reliable genotyping methods are crucial for breeding and colony maintenance. Quick and timely identification of desired genotypes are also essential for experiments in which early intervention may be the key for obesity and diabetes prevention or treatment. An ideal genotyping method also needs to be easily developed from sequence information [54]. Philips and colleagues discovered that the fa mutation creates a restriction site for Msp I, which was used to detect the fa genotype [26]. However, Msp I does not consistently cut amplicon completely, which can lead to genotype misclassification [33]. Durham and Truett subsequently improved the method by creating an alternative Pvu II restriction site using a site-specific mutagenesis technique. As a result, only the amplicon from wildtype alleles contains Pvu II restriction site while the amplicon from the mutant allele does not [33]. This method, however, still leaves much room for improvement. It involves multiple steps, requiring both PCR and enzymatic incubation and digestion, and takes about 5 h to complete the assay. In addition, Pvu II-treated PCR amplicon produced an 80 bp band, while the mutant genotype produced a 101 bp band, the small size of Pvu II treatment created thus requires 3% or higher agarose gel for better resolution/visualization. An easy and rapid genotyping method should also be able to produce reliable results even working with suboptimal DNA samples [54]. We developed a tetra-primer ARMS-PCR to genotype Zucker rats in a single step reaction and the assay takes less than 3 h. Our method is simple and robust, using crude DNA extracted by the most common chemical reagents found in chemical/biological laboratories and the genotypes identified by our method agree with sequence analysis. In this report, the length difference of amplicons containing A and C is 62 bp. The A containing amplicon (201 bp) is at the marker of 200 bp DNA electrophoresis ladder. The C containing amplicon of 263 bp is between the 200 bp and 300 markers, and the outer band (409 bp) is at the marker of 400 bp. The different genotype patterns were easily differentiated under a 2.0% agarose gel, therefore suitable for settings with limited research resources.

Conclusions

Here, we developed a method to identify three different Leptin receptor fa genotypes in Zucker rats with tetra-primer ARMS-PCR. The method is simple, robust, and reliable to perform without the need for DNA purification or restriction enzyme digestion, which is suitable for research exploring the etiology, gene-environment interaction, as well as implementing appropriate intervention approaches in the areas of obesity and metabolic diseases.
