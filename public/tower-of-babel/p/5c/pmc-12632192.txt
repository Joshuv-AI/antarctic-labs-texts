Systematic analysis of full-sibling identification by adding genetic markers and reference relatives
Authors: Qianqian Kong, Shubo Wen, Zhenmin Zhao, Man Chen, Weijie Teng, Liqin Chen, Ran Li, Hongyu Sun, Xiling Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFull-sibling (FS) identification is a crucial aspect of kinship analysis. However, current FS identification methods need to be more accurate and effective for routine FS identification. In this study, the optimal cut-off values for FS identification by incorporating both the identical by state (IBS) and likelihood ratio (LR) methods under four different levels of error rates were assessed. The corresponding sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and effectiveness were calculated for different numbers of short tandem repeats (STRs), ranging from 19 to 55. The identified cut-off values were validated using authentic pedigrees from the Han population in East China. It was determined that the cut-off values derived from the IBS method and LR method with an error rate below 0.01% were dependable and practical. The study also explored the impact of half-sibling relationships and the inclusion of reference relatives in FS identification. Currently, an updated technical specification for FS identification in China was released, serving as a valuable resource for FS testing in forensic applications.

Introduction

Biological full siblings (FSs) are offspring who share the same biological parents. According to the Mendelian law of heredity, there are no obligatory identical alleles between FSs, making the FS kinship identification challenging and complex. Different studies and countries have varying thresholds or standards for FS identification. The likelihood ratio (LR) method is a commonly used approach for kinship analysis, with reported cut-off values differing across studies [1, 2]. For example, the American Association of Blood Banks (AABB) sets the cumulative full-sibling index (CFSI) cut-off at 10 for FS identification [3]. A general LR threshold of 500 is suggested by Inoue et al. [2] for any kind of kinship analysis. Evett et al. [4] proposed an LR value of 10 000 for kinship identification, including FS. Additionally, a CFSI threshold of >1999 has been suggested for identifying FS relatives [5, 6]. In Carboni et al.’s study [7], the detection system containing 41 short tandem repeats (STRs) demonstrated greater individual discrimination power compared to the system with 15 STRs. In addition to LR, the identical by state (IBS) method, which directly counts identical alleles, is also utilized for kinship and FS analysis [2, 8–10]. The computation procedure for the IBS method is convenient and independent of the allele frequency of the STRs. Ge et al. [11] recommended an IBS threshold of ≥17 to distinguish FSs from unrelated (UR) individuals. The IBS method was utilized in the initial version of the FS identification specification released in China in 2014, under the accession number SF/Z JD0105002–2014 [12]. This specification outlines the threshold and corresponding system efficiency for three genotyping systems used in FS identification, which include 19 STRs, 29 STRs, and 39 STRs, respectively [12]. A subsequent study by Li et al. [13] calculated the system efficiency for a genotyping system encompassing STRs ranging from 13 to 50 for FS identification. Furthermore, a national standard for identifying biological siblings, GB/T 43641–2024, has recently been established [14]. It has to be noted that despite the importance of FS identification, further research is needed to determine how to define the threshold and system efficiency at various systems [7, 15, 16].

In current forensic casework, there is often confusion between half-siblings (HSs) and FSs, necessitating the identification of biological FS relationships for reasons such as privacy protection. This complexity poses challenges in accurately determining FS relationships, leading to potential risks and uncertainties. Differentiating between biological FS and HS remains a significant challenge in forensic science.

Genetic markers commonly used for kinship identification include STRs, single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), and insertion/deletion polymorphisms (InDels) [17–24]. Due to their high level of polymorphism and strong discrimination power, STRs are the preferred choice for forensic identification. Commercial STR kits based on capillary electrophoresis (CE) typically consist of 15–23 STRs with a high level of discrimination for paternity identification [25–28]. However, as the number of genetic markers is limited, a single CE kit may not suffice for distant relationship analysis [29]. To address this issue, a combination of multiple kits is often utilized to increase the number of STRs or combine STRs with SNPs in order to improve the discrimination power for kinship identification [30]. In addition, for complex kinship identification, a study by Pinto et al. [15] suggests that incorporating appropriate reference relatives can be more effective than only adding genetic markers [15, 31]. In order to improve the identification capabilities, the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) included seven additional autosomal STR loci (D2S1338, D19S433, D1S1656, D12S391, D2S441, D22S1045, and D10S1248) to the existing 13 CODIS STR core loci, creating the expanded FBI CODIS Core STR loci [32]. Therefore, further exploration into enhancing the discriminatory power of kinship identification through the addition of more genetic markers or the inclusion of other reference relatives is worthwhile.

In this study, we investigated the optimal cut-off value for FS identification and assessed the impact of HS using both IBS and LR methods. A total of 55 STRs from three different CE kits were selected, including SiFaSTR™ 23plex kit (Academy of Forensic Sciences, Shanghai, China), Goldeneye® DNA ID 22NC kit (Peoplespot Inc., Beijing, China), and AGCU NC 21 + 1 PCR amplification kit (AGCU ScienTech Inc., Wuxi, China) [33–36]. By considering that IBS scores for FS pairs, HS pairs, and UR pairs all follow a binomial distribution, we calculated IBS cut-off values for genotyping systems with varying numbers of STRs across four levels of error rates. Subsequently, we conducted simulations involving 100 000 pairs of FS and 100 000 randomly selected individuals to determine cut-off values, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and efficiency for FS identification using LR and IBS methods. Furthermore, we collected true pedigree samples from the Han population in East China and genotyped them using the three aforementioned CE kits to validate our theoretical and simulated observations. The study also examined the impact of HS and the inclusion of reference relatives in FS identification. Based on our results, an updated technical specification for FS identification in China was proposed, serving as a valuable resource for FS testing in forensic practice.

Materials and methods

Sample collection, DNA extraction, and quantification

Peripheral blood samples were obtained from 83 volunteers belonging to nine Chinese Han families in Zhejiang province (Supplementary Figure S1), as well as 98 UR healthy Han Chinese volunteers from East China, all with written informed consent. DNA extraction was carried out using a QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and quantified with a Qubit® 2.0 fluorometer using the Qubit® dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Academy of Forensic Science, Ministry of Justice, P. R. China (accession number 2020-01-12).

STR typing

DNA samples from the 181 individuals were amplified using the SiFaSTR™ 23plex kit, Goldeneye® DNA ID 22NC kit, and the AGCU NC 21 + 1 PCR amplification kit on a 9700 PCR Amplifier (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). After removing overlapping loci among the kits, a total of 55 STRs were obtained, including 19 CODIS STRs contained in the SiFaSTR™ 23plex kit, 39 STRs contained in two kits (SiFaSTR™ 23plex kit and Goldeneye® DNA ID 22NC kit), and 55 STRs in all three kits. 9947A DNA control and nuclease-free water were used as the positive and negative controls, respectively. PCR products were genotyped using ABI 3130xl (Thermo Fisher Scientific). GeneMapper ID-X (Thermo Fisher Scientific) software was used to analyze the electrophoresis data. The allele frequency of the 55 STRs from the Han population in East China was utilized for downstream analysis [36–38].

FS pair confirming

The parent–child relationships between individuals in each family were verified through paternity testing, with a combined parentage index (CPI) exceeding 10 000 and a minimum 15-year age gap. FS pairs were identified among the children from the same parents. A total of 32 FS pairs and 175 pairs of UR individuals were collected in the study.

Forensic parameter calculation

The markers in the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) were calculated using 133 UR healthy Han Chinese volunteers with written informed consent, including 35 and 98 individuals collected from Zhejiang province and East China, respectively [36–38]. The linkage disequilibrium (LD) between marker pairs was estimated using the expectation–maximization (EM) algorithm with Arlequin software 3.5.2 based on the information on https://cmpg.unibe.ch/software/arlequin35/ [39]. The power of discrimination (DP) values was calculated based on the 133 UR healthy Han Chinese volunteers.

Sample simulation

In total, 100 000 pairs of FSs, 100 000 pairs of HSs and 100 000 pairs of random selected individuals were generated with Familias 3.2.2 [40, 41], using allele frequencies obtained from previous studies [36–38].

Identical by state and LR calculation

IBS scores were calculated by directly counting the number of shared alleles [16]. LR values were calculated according to the formula in previous studies [45–47]. Frequency data of the Han population from East China were used for the LR calculation [36–38]. Sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and effectiveness were calculated to evaluate the system's performance with varying numbers of markers at different error rates [48]. Sensitivity refers to the proportion of samples correctly identified as positive [TP/(TP + FN)]. Specificity is the proportion of samples correctly identified as negative out of all negative samples [TN/(TN + FP)]. Accuracy is the ratio of correctly predicted samples to the total samples identified as FS or UR [(TP + TN)/(total samples)]. Effectiveness measures the proportion of samples correctly classified across total samples [(TP + TN)/total samples]. The terms “TP”, “FP”, “TN”, and “FN” represent “true positive”, “false positive”, “true negative”, and “false negative”, respectively. Four error rate cut-off thresholds of ≤0.001%, ≤0.01%, ≤0.05%, and ≤0.1%, regarded as high, low, adequate, accepted levels, respectively, were used when employing the IBS and LR methods for determining FS relationships. For instance, using the IBS method, an FS relationship can be supported if the combined IBS (CIBS) ≥ upper limits (t1), excluded if CIBS ≤ lower limits (t2), and indeterminable if t2 < CIBS < t1.

Results

Forensic parameters of the STRs

A total of 133 UR healthy Han Chinese volunteers from Zhejiang Province and East China were genotyped at 55 STRs using three different kits, the SiFaSTR™ 23plex kit, Goldeneye® DNA ID 22NC kit, and AGCU NC 21 + 1 PCR amplification kit. No deviations from the HWE was observed for any of the 55 STRs tested in this population (χ2 test with Bonferroni correction, adjusted P-value > 0.05/55) (Supplementary Table S1). There was no significant LD found among markers on the same chromosome, indicating they were in linkage equilibrium (LE) (adjusted P-value > 0.05/148 5, Supplementary Table S2). Additionally, DP values were further calculated, ranging from 0.981 5 for Penta E to 0.746 2 for TPOX (Supplementary Table S3).

The cut-off values for FS identification based on IBS method

To investigate the impact of varying numbers of STRs on FS identification, we analyzed the distribution across three marker groups, including 19 STRs (one kit), 39 STRs (two kits) and 55 STRs (three kits). In order to establish the threshold values for FS identification using the IBS method, we initially calculated the CIBS for FS and UR individuals through both theoretical calculations and direct counting in simulated individuals.

Theoretically, the probabilities of IBS scores for FS pairs and UR pairs, calculated using the allele frequency of 55 STRs, are shown in Supplementary Table S4. As the probability of a2 decreased with increasing DP values of STR in both FS pairs and UR pairs, the average DP value 0.910 7 of 55 STRs was used to estimate the cut-off values for FS identification. The mean CIBS values ranged from 24.38 (19 STRs) to 70.56 (55 STRs) for FS pairs and from 12.95 (19 STRs) to 37.48 (55 STRs) for UR pairs in theoretical calculations, varying based on the number of STRs tested. The overlap between FS pairs and UR pairs decreased as more markers were tested, from 66.37% to 1.48% as the number of STRs increased from 19 to 55 (Supplementary Table S5; Figure 1A). Upper limits (t1) and lower limits (t2) were then estimated to define FS (CIBS ≥ t1) and UR (CIBS ≤ t2) using the IBS method under error rates of ≤0.001%, ≤0.01%, ≤0.05%, and ≤0.1%. Sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and effectiveness were calculated to assess the system power with varying numbers of markers under each error rate. For example, under an error rate of ≤0.01%, theoretical calculation showed the sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and effectiveness values of 0.489 9, 0.581 4, 0.999 8, and 0.535 6, respectively, for 19 STRs; 0.960 8, 0.948 9, 0.999 9, and 0.954 9, respectively, for 39 STRs; and 0.997 0, 0.997 1, 0.999 9, and 0.997 1, respectively, for 55 STRs (Supplementary Table S5; Figure 1A).

Furthermore, the CIBSs were calculated by directly counting in simulated individuals. The average CIBS values ranged from 23.94 (19 STRs) to 70.58 (55 STRs) for FS pairs and from 12.00 (19 STRs) to 37.50 (55 STRs) for UR pairs in the simulation. The overlap between FS pairs and UR pairs decreased as more markers were tested, ranging from 70.28% (19 STRs) to 0.26% (55 STRs) (Supplementary Table S6; Figure 1B). According to the simulation results, the sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and effectiveness were 0.810 8, 0.581 5, 0.999 8, and 0.696 1, respectively, for 19 STRs; 0.995 3, 0.993 2, 0.999 9, and 0.994 3, respectively, for 39 STRs; and 0.999 7, 0.999 7, 0.999 9, and 0.999 7, respectively, for 55 STRs under an error rate of ≤0.01% (Supplementary Table S6; Figure 1B).

Theoretical calculations and direct counting both showed improvements in all parameters as the number of markers increased, although t1 and t2 are relatively higher in theoretical calculations compared to those in simulation. In theoretical calculations, cut-off values of t1 = 25 and t2 = 13 were set, while in simulations, cut-off values of t1 = 23 and t2 = 13 were set for 19 STRs with an error rate of ≤0.01% (Supplementary Tables S5 and S6). When analyzing 39 STRs, CIBS ≥43 indicates support for an FS relationship; CIBS ≤33 excludes this relationship; if 33 < CIBS <43, it is not possible to determine if the individuals are FS or UR. In the case of 55 STRs, the cut-off values are t1 = 57 and t2 = 51, with an error rate of ≤0.01%. The difference in cut-off values between theoretical calculations and direct counting may be due to the variations in the DP values of STRs.

The cut-off values for FS identification based on LR method

To determine the threshold values for FS identification using the LR method, we initially calculated the LR for FS and UR in simulated individuals. The mean log10LR values ranged from 5.55 (19 STRs) to 14.70 (55 STRs) for FS pairs, and from −4.35 (19 STRs) to −11.78 (55 STRs) for UR pairs in the simulation. With an increase in the number of markers tested, the overlap between FS pairs and UR pairs decreased from 45.36% (19 STRs) to 0.11% (55 STRs) (Supplementary Table S7; Figure 1C). The sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and effectiveness of FS identification were assessed using four different sets of threshold values. For 19 STRs, these four parameters were 0.731 2, 0.593 8, 1.000 0, and 0.662 5, respectively; for 39 STRs, the values were 0.984 7, 0.976 9, 1.000 0, and 0.980 8, respectively; for 55 STRs, the values were 0.997 2, 0.995 7, 1.000 0, and 0.996 5, under the condition of setting 4 and −4 (log10LR) as the threshold values to confirm and exclude FS kinship (Supplementary Table S7; Figure 1C).

Effectiveness steadily improved as additional markers were tested, nearing a value of 1 in both the IBS method and LR method (Figure 2). Additionally, the DP of a system with an equal number of STRs would be enhanced with a higher error rate or by allowing more lenient cut-off values in the theoretical and simulated tests (Figure 2). When implementing a 19 STRs system for FS identification, adjusting the threshold of log10LR from t1 > 1, t2 < −1 to t1 > 4, t2 < −4 resulted in a decrease in system effectiveness from 0.971 2 to 0.662 5. However, the accuracy improved from 0.997 7 to 1.000 0. On the other hand, when using 55 STRs, increasing the LR threshold led to a decrease in system effectiveness from 0.999 9 to 0.996 5, with accuracies remaining 1.000 0. Therefore, it was concluded that cut-off values obtained from the IBS method with an error rate below 0.01% and the LR method with a log10LR threshold of −4 and 4 could be considered reliable and practical. Based on these observations, the recommended threshold for error rate in identifying FS relationships is <0.01%, a criterion included in the latest national standard GB/T 43641–2024 in China [14].

Verification of true sample for FS identification

To assess the robustness of the cut-off values observed in simulated individuals, CIBS scores and CFSI values were calculated in true pedigree samples. Significant differences were found between FS pairs and UR pairs in true pedigree samples for both CIBS scores and CFSI values (independent-samples t-test, P < 0.0001). The mean CIBS scores in true pedigree samples calculated theoretically and through direct counting for 19 STRs in FS pairs were 23.65, 24.38, and 23.94, respectively (Supplementary Table S8; Figure 1). When using 39 STRs, the mean CIBS scores were 48.35, 50.04, and 48.89 for FS pairs. With 55 STRs, the mean CIBS scores were 68.39, 70.56, and 70.58 for FS pairs. A notable rise in the CIBS value was noted as the number of STRs increased. Additionally, the theoretically calculated CIBS values were higher than those obtained through direct counting for 19 STRs but similar for 55 STRs. This difference may be attributed to the average DP value being lower for the 55 STRs (0.910 7) compared to the 19 CODIS STRs (0.925 1) (Supplementary Table S4). The mean log10LR values in true pedigree samples and simulated individuals were 5.89 and 5.55 for FS pairs, and −4.52 and −4.35 for UR pairs using 19 STRs. For 39 STRs, the values were 11.39 and 11.47 for FS pairs, and −9.35 and −9.03 for UR pairs. With 55 STRs, the mean values were 13.67 and 14.70 for FS pairs, and −12.18 and −11.78 for UR pairs (Supplementary Table S8; Figure 1).

To further test the robustness of the cut-off values for FS identification as defined in simulated individuals, the effectiveness was calculated in true pedigree samples. The effectiveness was 0.535 6 for 19 STRs in the theoretical calculation of CIBS, which is comparatively lower than the effectiveness of 0.753 6 observed in true pedigree samples under the cut-off values of 25 and 13 (the CIBS cut-off values in the theoretical calculation). Similarly, the effectiveness was 0.696 1 for 19 STRs in simulated counting of CIBS, consistent with the effectiveness of 0.686 0 observed in true pedigree samples under the cut-off values of 23 and 13 (the CIBS cut-off values in direct counting). Moreover, the effectiveness was 0.662 5 for 19 STRs in simulated individuals based on the LR method, matching the effectiveness of 0.676 3 observed in true pedigree samples under the cut-off values of 4 and −4. Utilizing the cut-offs based on both IBS methods resulted in nearly perfect sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and effectiveness for 55 STRs in true pedigree samples. The verification, theoretical, and simulated results collectively indicated that the DP was stronger and the difference in CIBS score and CFSI for FS pairs and UR pairs was greater with an increasing number of STRs.

Introduction of a reference relative for LR method

For FS identification, despite detecting 55 STRs, the system efficiencies were 0.997 1, 0.999 7, and 0.996 5 based on the IBS method in theoretical calculation, the IBS method in direct counting, and the LR method, respectively. To further enhance the system efficiency of FS identification, reference relatives were incorporated. Three types of reference relatives were included (Supplementary Figure S2), with corresponding models defined as ABC, ABD, and ABE. ABC was introduced as an uncle for FS identification, ABD as an FS brother, and ABE as a grandmother. It can be observed that under the same detection system, the mean log10LR values of FS relationship identification models in descending order are: ABD > ABC ≈ ABE > AB, while the mean log10LR values of corresponding UR relationship identification models in descending order are: ABD < ABE ≈ ABC < AB (Supplementary Table S9). The overlapping region between FS pairs and UR pairs was negatively correlated with the number of STRs detected in the same model. The introduction of relatives in the models resulted in higher log10LR values compared to the case where only two individuals were identified (Figure 3). A slight increase in LR was noted for FS identification with the inclusion of an uncle or grandparent, whereas a significant increase was observed with the addition of an FS.

To further evaluate the impact of adding a third undoubted relative for FS identification, the sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and effectiveness of four models of FS identification were calculated using different cut-off values (Supplementary Table S9). The results suggest that including additional relatives led to higher system efficiency compared to identifying only two individuals. The effectiveness of the four models for FS identification, listed in descending order, are: AB < ABC ≈ ABE < ABD. Based on these results, it is recommended to prioritize the FS of the tested individual for FS identification, followed by an uncle or a grandparent. Using a log10LR cut-off of −4/4 for FS identification, it was observed that adding more markers to the tested relatives with 19 STRs did not result in an LR increase greater than adding markers to 39 STRs without relatives included (log10LR = 11.47 in AB model with 39 STRs; log10LR = 6.48, 9.60 and 6.50, respectively, in ABC/ABD/ABE models with 19 STRs). However, testing an FS relationship with 39 STRs in the ABD model yielded an LR higher than the LR in the AB model with 55 STRs (log10LR = 19.73 in the ABD model with 39 STRs; log10LR = 14.70 in the ABD model with 55 STRs). Uncles and grandparents showed similar LRs (log10LR = 13.21 in the ABC model with 39 STRs; log10LR = 14.70 in the ABD model with 55 STRs). In conclusion, for FS identification, it is advisable to first consider adding markers when only one kit with STR markers is tested, followed by adding closely related individuals.

The effect of HS in FS identification

In forensic practice, HSs are sometimes mistaken for FSs during FS identification, leading to an increased risk of misidentification. To assess the probability of misidentifying HSs as FSs, IBS scores and LR values were first calculated in HS pairs. The mean CIBS values ranged from 15.43 (19 STRs) to 44.66 (55 STRs) in theoretical calculations and from 17.44 (19 STRs) to 52.50 (55 STRs) in direct counting for HS pairs among simulated individuals (Supplementary Table S10). The mean log10LR values ranged from 0.229 9 (19 STRs) to 0.376 0 (55 STRs) for HS pairs in simulated individuals. The probability of misidentifying HSs as FSs was calculated using various cut-off values. When using LR >10 000 as the threshold for confirming FS kinship, as the number of markers increased, the probability of misidentifying HSs pairs as FSs pairs also increased. The probability of misidentification reached 0.135 8 for 55 STRs (Supplementary Table S10 and Supplementary Figure S3). This phenomenon may occur because an increase in the number of STRs raises the likelihood of verifying an FS relationship. However, as the number of STRs increases, so does the number of shared alleles resulting from the one-fourth shared blood between HS, leading to an increased probability of misclassifying an HS as an FS. These results suggested that HS pairs may not be adequately differentiated from FS pairs and UR pairs even using 55 STRs, and the risk of misidentification increases with a higher number of markers tested.

Additionally, we firstly calculated the CIBS for FS pairs and HS pairs through theoretical calculations and simulations. According to the theoretical calculations, the sensitivity, specificity, and effectiveness were determined as 0.282 0, 0.226 4, and 0.254 2, respectively, for 55 STRs at an error rate of ≤0.000 1% (Supplementary Table S11). Similarly, these parameters were found to be 0.508 9, 0.429 6, and 0.469 3, respectively, for 55 STRs at an error rate of ≤0.01%. A total of 10 000 pairs of sibling kinship were simulated using allele frequencies collected from previous studies [36–38]. The mean CIBS scores for FS pairs and HS pairs ranged from 23.84 (19 STRs) to 70.46 (55 STRs) and from 17.40 (19 STRs) to 52.36 (55 STRs), respectively (Supplementary Table S12). The overlap of CIBS scores between FS pairs and HS pairs decreased with an increase in the number of markers tested (Supplementary Table S13). Results from the simulated and theoretical data were consistent for FS pairs under the same detection system (paired-samples t-test, P > 0.05). However, the overlap between FS pairs and HS pairs decreased as more markers were tested [from 94.10% (19 STRs) to 40.81% (55 STRs) for IBS scores] and even with 55 STRs, HS pairs could not be fully distinguished from FS pairs (Figures 4 and 5, Supplementary Tables S12 and S13). In the simulation, the sensitivity, specificity, and effectiveness for distinguishing HS pairs from FS pairs were 0.666 5, 0.517 2, and 0.591 9, respectively, using 55 STRs with an error rate of ≤0.001% (Supplementary Table S12). These values increased to 0.732 7, 0.604 9, and 0.668 8, respectively, using 55 STRs with an error rate of ≤0.01%. The theoretical values were relatively higher than the simulated values for FS pairs, while they were lower for HS pairs in terms of CIBS. This difference may be attributed to the average DP value for the 55 STRs (0.910 7) being lower than that of the 19 CODIS STRs (0.925 1) (Supplementary Table S4).

Schematic representation of the two competing kinship hypotheses (H1 and H2) evaluated in the simulation study.

To further assess the ability to distinguish between FS pairs and HS pairs, we conducted a simulation study involving genotypes from 10 000 families by adding relatives for FS identification using Familias (Figure 6). Various genotypic configurations (AB, ABC, ABD, ABE, ABF, ABG, and ABM) were analyzed independently with different numbers of STRs. The mean log10LR values and overlapping regions between FS pairs and corresponding HS pairs were compared across different numbers of STRs, ranging from 19 to 55, in each model (Supplementary Table S14). The results revealed that under the same detection system, the mean log10LR values for FS relationship identification models ranked in descending order as follows: ABD > ABE ≈ ABC ≈ ABM > ABF ≈ ABG > AB, while the mean values for the corresponding HS relationship identification models ranked in descending order as: ABD < ABE ≈ ABC ≈ ABM < ABG ≈ ABF < AB. Our results showed that the average log10LR of FS pairs increased with the number of STRs, whereas the average log10LR of HS pairs decreased within the same model. In addition, the overlapping region between FS pairs and HS pairs exhibited a negative correlation with the number of STRs detected in the same model. Furthermore, the log10LR values obtained when introducing relatives were higher compared to identifying only two individuals (Figure 5). These results suggest that increasing the number of STRs and introducing reference individuals can enhance the effectiveness of the system for sibling identification.

To assess the impact of increasing the number of STRs and the presence of a third undoubted relative on sibling identification, four groups of cut-off values [log10LR = 4 (confirm)/−4 (exclude), 3 (confirm)/−3 (exclude), 2 (confirm)/−2 (exclude), and 1 (confirm)/−1 (exclude)] were analyzed. Sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and effectiveness were calculated for each model using these cut-off values (Supplementary Table S14). The results revealed a positive correlation between the system efficiency of sibling relationship identification and the number of STRs examined within the same model. In addition, including relatives in the analysis enhanced system efficiency compared to analyzing only two individuals. As expected, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and effectiveness improved as more markers were included, leading to reduced error rates. The results demonstrated that the effectiveness of seven common models for sibling relationship identification ranked in descending order as: AB < ABG ≈ ABF < ABM ≈ ABC ≈ ABE < ABD (Supplementary Table S14). Based on these results, it is recommended to prioritize full siblings for sibling identification when necessary.

The effect of DF value for FS identification

The probability of a2 decreased with increasing DP values of STR in both FS pairs and UR pairs, particularly in relation to the strengthening of IBS score. The discrepancy between results obtained from theoretical calculations and simulations could be attributed to the varying average DP value of STRs in this study. A mean DP value of 0.910 7 from the 55 STRs was utilized to establish the cut-off values for FS identification. This discrepancy lessened as the number of markers used increased, with no discernible difference when all 55 STRs were employed. Subsequent calculations of cut-off values for FS identification were conducted using varying numbers of markers and their average DP values. Theoretical calculations revealed a negative correlation between average DP values and IBS scores (Figure 7), as higher DP values of STRs led to decreased allele sharing probabilities and smaller IBS scores for both FS and UR pairs.

Discussion and conclusions

The requirement for FS kinship analysis is on the rise in forensic casework. Various methods have been developed to address this issue, including reconstructing family relationships using multiple known relatives or utilizing different DNA markers [7, 8, 15, 30, 44, 49, 50]. With the advent of next-generation sequencing (NGS), both STRs and SNPs can now be detected in a single reaction. The ForenSeq™ DNA Signature Prep kit underwent evaluation for its effectiveness in kinship analysis [30, 44, 50]. In subsequent studies, Li et al. [44] and Tao et al. [50] highlighted this panel’s ability to accurately determine paternity and FS relationships. Zhang et al. [30] also demonstrated that the 40 STRs + 91 SNP markers set achieved a sensitivity and specificity of 100% in FS testing. A study comparing 55 STRs from the CE platform to 40 STRs and 94 SNPs from a CE kit and an NGS-based kit showed that the efficiency was nearly equivalent for second-degree and third-degree kinship analysis [48]. Therefore, using 55 STRs alone can effectively distinguish FS kinship from UR pairs without the need for SNPs in routine casework.

The system efficiency of FS relationship identification in the LR method improved with the addition of a third individual and/or by increasing the number of STRs. Despite detecting 55 STRs in this study, the system efficiency for FS kinship identification did not match that of paternity tests. Previous studies have shown that selecting appropriate relatives can be more effective than simply adding more markers for sibling analyses in certain scenarios [16, 31]. Similar results were obtained in this study, where the system efficiency was 0.996 5 with two individuals tested using 55 STRs but increased to 1.000 0 when genotypes of the FS were included using 39 STRs in the LR method (Supplementary Table S9). These results suggest that the preferred order for introducing relatives should be the FS, followed by the grandparent and then the uncle.

The IBS method is utilized for determining FS kinship by calculating the total number of common alleles between two individuals. In comparison to the LR method, the IBS method is relatively simpler to calculate. The LR method, which is employed to address this issue, relies on LR computation. The theoretical framework of both IBS and LR methods is rooted in the concept of identity by descent (IBD) [45, 51]. This study incorporated both methods and observed a consistent increase in effectiveness with the testing of more markers, eventually nearing 1 for both the IBS and LR methods at the same error rate. For the IBS method, the effectiveness determined through simulated tests at four error rates was 0.427 0, 0.696 1, 0.861 7, and 0.926 0 for 19 STRs, and 0.998 6 and 0.999 7 for 55 STRs at error rates of ≤0.001% and ≤0.01%, respectively. On the other hand, the LR method yielded effectiveness values of 0.662 5, 0.819 4, 0.920 4, and 0.971 2 for 19 STRs, and 0.996 5, 0.998 5, 0.999 5, and 0.999 9 for 55 STRs under four groups of cut-off values, respectively (paired samples t-test P < 0.05 between the effectiveness calculated by the IBS method and LR method for 19 STRs). The disparity between the IBS and LR methods diminished as more markers were employed. In comparison to the IBS method, the LR method exhibited higher system efficiency when the same number of STRs were detected. The results were validated using true pedigree samples, particularly by including more relatives in sibling testing.

In routine casework, there is a possibility of misidentifying HS pairs as FS pairs for privacy protection or other reasons. The likelihood of this misidentification increases with the number of markers tested (Supplementary Tables S10 and Figure 3). The results of this study indicate that distinguishing FS relatives from HS relatives can be challenging. However, adding a third confirmed relative in sibling identification may help reduce this risk.

For the identification of FS in China, the initial version of the specification, assigned the accession number SF/Z JD0105002–2014 [12], was published in 2014. However, the threshold table in this version was only compatible with detection systems utilizing three specific combinations (19 STRs, 29 STRs, and 39 STRs), resulting in a relatively low detection system efficiency of up to 95%. To address these limitations, the FS identification specification was revised in 2024 under the accession number GB/T 43641–2024 [14]. The updated version includes threshold tables based on IBS and LR methods, outlining cut-off values and system efficiency with an accuracy of no <99.99%. The scope of application in the revised version has expanded from three fixed systems to 19 STRs to 55 STRs. Consequently, the updated standard for biological FS identification has enhanced both accuracy and applicability. Following the implementation and dissemination of this standard, there has been a significant enhancement in the capability of biological FS identification, contributing to the Ministry of Public Security of China's “Tuan Yuan” Operation (Family Reunion Initiative) by facilitating the reconnection of many long-lost relatives.
