AerialIRGAN: unpaired aerial visible-to-infrared image translation with dual-encoder structure
Authors: Decao Ma, Juan Su, Shaopeng Li, Yong Xian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Due to the high cost of equipment and the constraints of shooting conditions, obtaining aerial infrared images of specific targets is very challenging. Most methods using Generative Adversarial Networks for translating visible images to infrared greatly depend on registered data and struggle to handle the diversity and complexity of scenes in aerial infrared targets. This paper proposes a one side end-to-end unpaired aerial visible-to-infrared image translation algorithm, termed AerialIRGAN. AerialIRGAN introduces a dual-encoder structure, where one encoder is designed based on the Segment Anything Model to extract deep semantic features from visible images, and the other encoder is designed based on UniRepLKNet to capture small-scale patterns and sparse patterns from visible images. Subsequently, AerialIRGAN constructs a bridging module to deeply integrate the features of both encoders and their corresponding decoders. Finally, AerialIRGAN proposes a structural appearance consistency loss to guide the synthetic infrared images to maintain the structure of the source image while possessing distinct infrared characteristics. The experimental results show that compared to the existing typical infrared image generation algorithms, the proposed method can generate higher-quality infrared images and achieve better performance in both subjective visual description and objective metric evaluation.

Introduction

Infrared images are an important source of information for applications such as the detection1, positioning2, recognition3, and tracking4 of low-altitude unmanned aerial vehicles due to their high sensitivity to temperature changes, strong ability to penetrate fog, and robustness under low-light conditions. However, obtaining aerial infrared images of specific targets is extremely difficult due to the high cost of equipment and limitations in shooting conditions5. Since visible images are more accessible and cost-effective, researchers are increasingly turning to the task of deriving corresponding infrared images from visible images.

Traditional methods for generating infrared images based on visible images generally require three-dimensional modeling of the scene, classification and segmentation of the target materials, and then simulation based on the characteristics of infrared radiation6–8. This process is intricate, and the resulting infrared images often exhibit unnatural textures, hindering the effective execution of large-scale infrared image simulations.

In recent years, image translation tasks based on Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)9 and diffusion models10,11 have achieved significant progress. The task of translating visible images to infrared images also falls within the realm of image translation tasks, aiming to convert visible images into their infrared counterparts while preserving the structural information of visible images. The vast majority of current algorithms for visible-to-infrared image translation are based on the GANs method and have shown significant progress12–16. With the outstanding performance of diffusion models in image generation, some researchers have begun to explore their application in visible-to-infrared image translation17. However, these models encounter challenges, including lengthy training times and substantial hardware demands18.

In the aviation field, the task of visible-to-infrared image translation presents new challenges to existing algorithms19. Firstly, some infrared image generation algorithms based on Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have high data requirements. These algorithms typically require paired datasets of visible and infrared images12,13,15,20, which are extremely rare in the aviation field. Secondly, algorithms capable of handling unpaired data often struggle to effectively represent the infrared characteristics of the target. Moreover, these methods are generally based on CycleGAN21 and require two sets of generators and discriminators, leading to higher training costs. Lastly, current infrared image generation algorithms cannot meet the requirements of the diversity and complexity of aviation infrared targets. For instance, algorithms tested on the KAIST22 and VEDAI23 datasets have relatively simple scenarios and have only been experimentally verified in the fields of autonomous driving and remote sensing.

To address these challenges, we propose a one side unpaired aerial visible-to-infrared image translation algorithm called AerialIRGAN, which does not require paired data. Figure 1 illustrates the objectives of visible-to-infrared image translation. The goal is to obtain difficult-to-acquire infrared images from easily obtained visible images at a low cost. By integrating traditional infrared image simulation theory and according to the Stefan-Boltzmann Law, material properties are crucial for determining the amount of radiation emitted, as emissivity is directly related to them, and the temperature of an object is indirectly influenced by these properties. Therefore, we use deep semantic information from SAM to compensate for the lack of material information. In addition, to better facilitate the model’s recognition of the target object, we employ large convolution technology to encourage the model to have a larger receptive field. To address the edge distortion issues faced in the current infrared image generation process, we introduce a structural appearance consistency loss to encourage the learning of target image features.

Specifically, we first explicitly decouple the structure and appearance of the images and distinguish between structural and appearance information during the translation process. We calculate the similarities of the two types in the deep feature space to construct a structural appearance consistency loss. This loss constrains the structural similarity between the generated and source images, as well as the appearance similarity between the generated and target images. We also construct a bridging module that integrates semantic features from the SAM, encoded features using UniRepLKNet24, and decoded features. Lastly, we also employ a multi-scale contrastive loss to enhance the fine granularity of the generated image. Unlike CUT25 and DCLGAN26, this contrastive loss requires only one set of encoders and decoders. It does not necessitate re-encoding the target image, thus avoiding an increase in training costs.

Related work

Paired visible-to-infrared image translation

The paired visible-to-infrared image translation algorithms are primarily based on the Pix2Pix framework27. ThermalGAN12 and LayerGAN28 generate infrared images through multimodal data. ThermalGAN12 generates infrared images in two steps: first, it generates the target’s average temperature infrared image using visible images and temperature vectors, and then it generates a more refined infrared image using the target’s average temperature infrared image and the visible image. LayerGAN28 includes two methods for generating infrared images: one method uses temperature vectors, semantic segmentation images29, and thermal segmentation images to generate infrared images; the other method uses visible images, semantic segmentation images, and thermal segmentation images to generate infrared images. Pix2Pix-MRFFF13, InfraGAN14, and IR-GAN15 translate visible images into corresponding infrared images. The main advantages of these algorithms are reflected in three aspects. In terms of generative network design, they enhance network performance by adopting ideas such as residual blocks, UNet architecture, and attention mechanisms, such as ResNet30, UNet31, and channel and spatial attention modules32. In the adversarial network, they use networks with strong image fine-grained perception capabilities, such as PatchGAN33, MultiPatchGAN34, and UNetGAN35. In addition, WGAN36 and LSGAN37 are also employed for stable network training. Regarding the loss function, beyond L1 and GAN losses, they often incorporate additional types of losses aimed at enhancing edge information in infrared images, such as Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) loss14 and gradient vector loss15. However, these algorithms require strict data registration, as the quality of registration significantly impacts the generation of infrared images.

Unpaired visible-to-infrared image translation

The unpaired visible-to-infrared image translation algorithms are primarily based on the CycleGAN framework21. SIR-GAN38 takes traditional infrared simulation data as input to produce infrared images rich in texture information. DAGAN39 employs a novel dual-attention mechanism to predict flashover in compartment fires through visible-to-infrared translation. CVIIT40 introduces a contrastive loss to ensure content consistency between the generated image and the source image. Edge-guided Multi-domain RGB-to-TIR image Translation (EMRT)41 ensures consistency of edge information between the generated image and the source image, thereby preserving more detail information in the infrared image. These methods require two sets of generative and discriminative networks, and usually one set of networks is unnecessary, which increases the training cost. To address the issue of high training costs, DistanceGAN42 and GcGAN43 achieve one-way translation by introducing implicit distances. F/LSeSim44 extracts structural information of the network through a specific network. EnCo45 proposes a contrast loss different from CUT25 and DCLGAN26, requiring only a set of encoders and decoders, thereby reducing the training cost of the network. Although these methods have performed well in some image transformation tasks, they have limitations in the task of translating visible images to infrared images in aviation. Furthermore, some diffusion-based methods46–50 can achieve image-to-image translation through multiple denoising steps.

Additionally, with the rapid development of diffusion models in image generation, UNIT-DDPM51, DDIB52, BBDM53, Cycle-Diffusion54, VI-Diff55, and CycleGAN-Turbo56 have achieved significant performance in various image translation tasks based on diffusion models. However, these algorithms have not been applied to the task of translating visible images to infrared images in aviation, and they suffer from slow inference speed and high equipment requirements. CycleGAN-Turbo, which is based on a one-step diffusion model, has lower equipment requirements, and we have experimentally verified its effectiveness in the task of translating visible images to infrared images in aviation.

Segment anything model

Segment Anything Model (SAM)57 specifies the content to be segmented in an image by providing images and visual cues such as bounding boxes, points, masks, etc. After training on 11 million images with over 1 billion masks, SAM has a powerful zero-shot learning ability, enabling it to effectively apply to downstream visual tasks. To address the speed issues of SAM during training and inference, FastSAM58 replaces the Transformer architecture in SAM with a traditional convolutional neural network. MobileSAM59 uses a decoupled distillation method to build a lightweight image encoder that performs comparably to SAM. SAM has been excellently applied in tasks such as style transfer60, object detection61, and image editing62, but its application in the task of translating visible images to infrared images in aviation has not yet been effectively developed.

Method

This paper proposes an infrared image generation algorithm based on Conditional Generative Adversarial Networks (CGAN)63, incorporating methods from SAM and contrastive learning. The overall algorithm framework is shown in Fig. 2. The visible image is encoded by the SAM image encoder and the encoder based on UniRepLKNet, and the resulting features are fused and subsequently decoded to generate the infrared image. Discriminator

This paper utilizes PatchGAN33 as the discriminator D, which serves both to assess image authenticity and to supply an attention map for contrastive learning. PatchGAN achieves the extraction and representation of local image features, allowing the model to focus more on image details, which is beneficial for generating higher-quality images. The main reason for choosing PatchGAN in this paper is that it places a smaller burden on network training while ensuring, to a certain extent, the fine granularity of the generated images.

Experiments and results

We describe the metrics and datasets used in the study and present the results. All experiments were conducted using an Intel Core i9-10980XE CPU running @ 3 GHz on two Nvidia RTX 3090 GPUs.

Datasets

The Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) Visible-to-Infrared Image Dataset (UAV-VID) original data was collected using the DJI Matrice M200 V2 drone as the platform, with the DJI Zenmuse XT2 thermal imaging gimbal camera. The dataset includes visible images with a resolution of 4000\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×3000 and infrared images with a resolution of 640\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×512. To facilitate model learning, we perform a rigid transformation on the visible images to ensure they have the same resolution as the infrared images. The UAV-VID data includes targets such as buildings, radars, generators, cooling towers, ships, airport runways, cars, and bridges. In our experiment, all images were resized to a scale of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$256\times 256$$\end{document}256×256 pixels, with 8000 image pairs used for training and 880 image pairs for testing.

The Aerial Visible-to-Infrared Image Dataset (AVIID)19 is a dataset specifically designed for the task of translating aerial visible images into infrared images, created by researchers at Northwestern Polytechnical University. The dataset comprises more than 3000 pairs of matched visible and infrared images, intended to provide researchers with a valuable data resource for assessing and improving the performance of algorithms for visible-to-infrared image translation in the aerial domain. In our experiment, we selected the more complex subset known as AVIID-3, and all images were resized to a scale of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$256\times 256$$\end{document}256×256 pixels, with 1024 image pairs used for training and 256 image pairs for testing.

Image quality evaluation metrics

Two evaluation metrics were used to measure image quality, including Fréchet Inception Distance (FID)76 and Kernel Inception Distance (KID)77. FID is derived from the field of machine learning, which measures the distance between two statistical distributions of features extracted from the Inception-V3 Network, providing insight into the quality of generated images in a generative model. KID is a metric similar to the FID, the Kernel Inception Distance, to be the squared MMD78 between Inception representations and has a simple unbiased estimator. Correspondingly, a lower KID means a better performance. To better compare the results, the KID evaluation results were magnified by a factor of 1000.

Implementation detail

The improvements of the proposed method are based on the EnCo framework. All experiments were conducted using the public code and datasets for training and testing in the same experimental environment to provide a fair comparison. We selected 8 representative unpaired image-to-image translation methods for comparison with our approach. These include CycleGAN21, UNIT79, MUNIT80, EMRT16, CUT25, DCLGAN26, FSeSim44, EnCo45, BBDM53, and CycleGAN-turbo56. The AerialIRGAN method follows the same training setup as CycleGAN, CUT, DCLGAN, FSeSim, and EnCo methods. The Adam optimizer was employed, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _{1}$$\end{document}β1 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _{2}$$\end{document}β2 were set to 0.5 and 0.999, respectively. A total of 200 training epochs were performed to ensure model convergence. The learning rates of the generative and adversarial networks were fixed at 0.0002 and 0.000002, respectively, in the first 100 epochs, and then, they decreased linearly to zero. Discussion

The AerialIRGAN algorithm demonstrates superior performance in the generation of infrared images, notably surpassing current methods in preserving the structural integrity of the source images and in the vivid portrayal of infrared characteristics. It addresses the limitations of existing algorithms, which often result in structural degradation (as shown in the results of MUNIT in Figs. 7 and 8) or misrepresentation of infrared features (as shown in the results of EMRT in Fig. 7). AerialIRGAN’s design, with its SAC loss function, explicitly decouples the structural and appearance aspects of image, enhancing realism while maintaining fidelity to the original. The incorporation of a dual-encoder structure not only harnesses SAM’s advanced zero-shot learning abilities but also refines the model’s focus on the intricate details within images. This has enabled the model to grasp essential infrared attributes, such as the darker appearance of trees and water bodies due to their lower radiation values, as evidenced in the AerialIRGAN outcomes depicted in Fig. 8. Benefiting from these designs, as shown in Table 1, AerialIRGAN has achieved the best results on two aerial datasets. In our future work, we are considering adopting the SAM approach, utilizing points, bounding boxes, or masks to designate specific areas within infrared images, followed by fine-tuning further to enhance the generation quality of the infrared images.

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a one side end-to-end unpaired visible-to-infrared image translation algorithm (AerialIRGAN). Our dual encoder structure extracts semantic and sparse features from visible images, fusing them via the BSAM module. By incorporating the SAC loss, the structural features of the generated images are maintained, and the resulting infrared images exhibit distinct characteristics. Through our extensive experiments, our AerialIRGAN demonstrates its ability to generate realistic and detailed infrared images, particularly in aerial applications of visible-to-infrared image translation. However, AerialIRGAN is unable to adapt to inputs of different scales due to the influence of the SAM image encoder, making it difficult to generate high-resolution infrared images. Moreover, AerialIRGAN cannot fine-tune specific areas of the generated images. In future work, we will expand the application of AerialIRGAN to the generation of high-resolution infrared images and draw on the methods of SAM for fine-tuning specific areas of the generated images.
