Experimental evolution under different nutritional conditions changes the genomic architecture and virulence of Acinetobacter baumannii
Authors: Sohyeon Yun, Jihyeon Min, Sunyong Han, Ho Seok Sim, Se Kye Kim, Jun Bong Lee, Jang Won Yoon, Jinki Yeom, Woojun Park
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study uncovers the molecular processes governing the adaptive evolution of multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens without antibiotic pressure. Genomic analysis of MDR Acinetobacter baumannii cells cultured for 8000 generations under starvation conditions (EAB1) or nutrient-rich conditions (EAB2) revealed significant genomic changes, primarily by insertion sequence (IS)-mediated insertions and deletions. Only two Acinetobacter-specific prophage-related deletions and translocations were observed in the EAB1 strain. Both evolved strains exhibited higher virulence in mouse infection studies, each with different modes of action. The EAB1 strain displayed a heightened ability to cross the epithelial barrier of human lung tissue, evade the immune system, and spread to lung tissues, ultimately resulting in cellular mortality. In contrast, the EAB2 strain strongly attached to epithelial cells, leading to increased synthesis of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines. The genomic alterations and increased virulence observed in evolved strains during short-term evolution underscore the need for caution when handling these pathogens, as these risks persist even without antibiotic exposure.

Introduction

The dynamics of bacterial genome evolution during experimental adaptation have garnered significant attention in academic and industrial fields. These research fields aim not only to understand the molecular mechanisms of bacterial evolution but also to establish the scientific basis for harnessing the biotechnological potential of bacterial cells to produce valuable bioproducts1,2. In a short-term (~840 generations) experimental evolution with mutS-deleted hypermutator Pseudomonas aeruginosa, a higher frequency of nucleotide substitutions and intergenic insertions and deletions (INDELs) mutations, mainly in DNA homopolymeric tracts, resulted in an increased rate of phase variation and remarkable phenotypic heterogeneities in the evolved P. aeruginosa strains3. Homopolymer tracts exhibit a high concentration of INDELs due to replication slippage during DNA replication, leading to nucleotide additions or deletions within the tract4. These frequent INDEL mutations contribute to phase variation and can impact gene regulation in bacterial genomes4. Salmonella enterica underwent short-term pathoadaptive evolution through ~130 generations of serial passages in mice, resulting in substitutions and INDELs mutations, particularly in the global regulator genes phoQ and stpA, which are also known to influence the expression of another master regulator, RpoS5.

Even in a stable environment, single cells within a bacterial population accumulate mutations despite high-fidelity polymerases with meager spontaneous error rates [e.g., Escherichia coli case: (9.6 ± 0.1)×10−4 base pair substitution per generation]. These genomic mutations induced by polymerases due to DNA replication errors in individual cells can also contribute to phenotypic heterogeneity in a population by providing selective advantages over neutral mutations6,7. Furthermore, stress-mediated translesion synthesis (TLS) by error-prone polymerases can lead to DNA replication errors, resulting in heritable changes in chromosomes and phenotypic traits that enhance survivability under the same stress conditions8. Notably, a high mutation rate in a population experiencing near-lethal stresses could increase the likelihood of creating more adaptable variants under such high-risk conditions, which made the adapted cell dominant in a population9. Gene conversion with and without cross-overs play a role in the genomic evolution of bacterial cells, inducing genomic rearrangements through the actions of several crucial proteins such as RecA, RubA, and RecG10. RecA-dependent and -independent DNA recombination during DNA replication, considered major drivers of bacterial evolution, have been observed in various species, including S. enteria11.

Furthermore, marked alterations in bacterial genomic architecture are also associated with the mobile genetic elements (MGEs), such as transposons, IS elements, and prophages12. These MGEs can alter global gene expression patterns, leading to different phenotypic responses under harsh conditions13,14. In evolution experiments with E. coli REL606, a substantial number of genomic rearrangements occurred, primarily mediated by IS elements, leading to significant deletion events, which enhanced bacterial fitness under experimental conditions15. These changes in genome architecture eliminated regions, such as the rbs operon and prophage-related genes, which were not essential for the survival of the evolved strains in the given environment15. Interestingly, large prophage regions in the bacterial genome can be deleted within short generation times, benefiting the host by minimizing the metabolic burden16,17. Significant variations in the number and activity of transposase-possessing IS elements are evident even within the same bacterial species (e.g., A. baumannii, E. coli)18,19. Furthermore, the prevalence of IS element-mediated DNA recombination and INDEL events in DNA segments has been observed in the chromosome landscapes of experimentally evolved strains such as A. baylyi ADP1 and E. coli REL60620,21. In the genome of laboratory-evolved A. baylyi ADP1 after 1000 generations, high copy numbers of IS elements (particularly IS1236) and IS1236-mediated homologous recombination led to genomic rearrangements, including ~24 kb deletions20. Outward-directed strong promoters of transposition IS elements can impact the expression of neighboring genes, as seen in several carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii strains harboring ISAba1 adjacent to the blaoxa-51 gene22. Comparative genomics of A. baumannii (>1000) revealed an average of ~33 IS elements per genome with varying copy numbers of the same IS elements (e.g., IS26, ISAba1, ISAba13, and ISAba125), which presumably contributed to genome size variations during their evolution (~3.7–4.5 Mb)19,23.

Many pathogenic A. baumannii strains, including MDR A. baumannii NCCP 16007, harbor multiple IS elements, prophage regions, and externally acquired AR genes24,25. On average, only 52% of the genes encoded in the genome of Acinetobacter isolates, including A. baumannii and A. nosocomialis, belong to the core gene set of the genus26. The present study conducted laboratory evolution experiments with MDR A. baumannii NCCP 16007, a clinical isolate from a patient with a urinary infection. Our experiments, which spanned 8000 generations under different nutritional conditions, examined genomic changes associated with phenotypic shifts and high virulence using multi-omics approaches and mouse infection studies. Our data suggest that prophages and IS elements mediated significant genomic plasticity in the NCCP 16007 strain while preserving AR genes during short-term evolution, implying that the loss of AR genes may necessitate longer evolutionary periods. Our study also underscores the need for caution in handling these pathogens due to their significantly altered genomes and increased pathogenicity.

Results

Large-scale genomic rearrangement in the laboratory-evolved A. baumannii strains

In the laboratory evolution experiment, the MDR A. baumannii NCCP 16007 strain, isolated from a patient with a urinary infection with a genome size of ~3855 kb, served as a control and underwent 8000 generations of adaptation. The objective was to generate adaptively evolved A. baumannii strains (EAB) under starvation or nutrient-rich growth conditions without antibiotic pressure. Two variants were obtained: EAB1, which evolved under starvation conditions, and EAB2, cultivated under nutrient-rich conditions. The WT strain possessed several genes likely acquired through horizontal gene transfer, including 13 antibiotic resistance (AR) genes from other pathogens, two prophage regions, and a plasmid insertion (8.7 kb) (Figs. S1A, S2, and S3A). During the adaptive evolution experiments conducted under both nutritional conditions, we anticipated that there would be a loss of these elements or other non-essential genes (Fig. S1B)27. As the evolutionary experiments under normal nutrition conditions did not induce significant changes due to the absence of selective pressures, they were excluded from this study7. This experiment aimed to test whether a continuous subculture of bacteria on an agar medium under these two conditions would lead to the loss of unnecessary genes, potentially enhancing bacterial fitness through an increased mutation rate. Conversely, it was anticipated that antibiotic resistance and pathogenicity might decrease due to genomic evolution (Fig. S1B). As expected, the evolved strains (EAB1 and EAB2, represented by colored lines) exhibited improved growth rates under each tested condition. EAB1 demonstrated a growth rate increase of 13.24%, while EAB2 showed a growth rate increase of 15.35% (Fig. 1A). These growth rate improvements were significant when compared to the growth rate of the WT strain (1.2 ± 0.03 h- under starvation; 4.9 ± 0.10 h- under nutrient-rich conditions) (Fig. 1A). However, the CFUs of EAB2 in LB broth were less than those of WT and EAB1, approximately a 10-fold difference at a 24 h time point (Table S1). The population dynamics of the competing WT strain and the evolved strains were observed during the laboratory evolution of MDR WT cells, which were serially subcultured for 8000 generations. The evolved strains, with elevated growth rates, gradually outcompeted the WT strain, indicating their fitness advantages. As a result, our final evolved strains became prevalent due to the accumulation of mutations. Our comparative genomics analyses revealed that genomic evolution driven by nutritional stress resulted in substantial DNA rearrangements, including two Acinetobacter-specific prophage-related deletions and translocations in the EAB1 strains (Fig. 1B). These changes led to a reduction in their genome sizes: EAB1 had a size of ~3822 kb, with three deletions and two insertions, while EAB2 had a size of roughly 3846 kb, with two deletions, two insertions, and two point mutations, respectively (Fig. 1B). PCR assays were then conducted using gene-specific primer pairs (labeled as F and R in Fig. S2) to confirm the presence of deletion and insertion events in the genomic regions. The EAB1 strain underwent significant genomic alterations, notably a substantial deletion and translocation of two Acinetobacter-specific prophage regions (deletion_2 and deletion_3, Figs. S2A and S3B). A nine-bp deletion was also observed in the filE gene, which encodes a putative type 3 filamentous fimbriae component. These findings suggest that the removal of specific genes, such as filE, ata, and bamE, within the deleted regions, likely led to diverse phenotypic changes, encompassing alterations in biofilm formation, motility, and pathogenesis, known to be influenced by these genes (Table S2). Bacterial cells undergoing nutrient deficiency and slow growth have adapted to reduce their genetic load by losing unnecessary or prophage-derived genes while preserving essential genes related to biosynthetic pathways28. The evolved EAB1 strain under nutrient starvation conditions appears to have followed this pattern to alleviate metabolic burdens (deletion_2, Fig. S2A). Notably, the deleted region 3 underwent genomic rearrangements and was relocated to a different position on the chromosome (indicated as insertion_1 in Figs. S2A, S3A, and S4A). The recombination activity of the ISAba52 gene product present in the same region may have facilitated this relocation. The GC ratio of this moved region (50%–52%) differs from the overall chromosome background GC ratio (39%). Although the underlying mechanism remains unclear, our proposed scenarios for reintegration of prophage region 2 include DNA recombination of CpG islands (DNA homopolymeric tracts) within ISAba52 or excision/incorporation of prophage during replication and repair processes (Fig. S4)4,29,30.

During evolution in nutrient-rich conditions, bacteria may conserve energy for biosynthesis of cellular components by potentially losing genes linked to anabolic pathways31. This shift could enable them to rely more on external resources rather than internal synthesis31. Unexpectedly, the EAB2 strain, during its short-term evolution period of 8000 generations, retained its biosynthetic pathways for amino acids and other macromolecules. However, there were significant changes in its genome architecture, marked by removing two large DNA segments containing important genes such as phaC, ybaB, and bap. These genes play crucial roles in cellular functions such as carbon storage, gene regulation, and biofilm formation (Fig. S2B). Notably, one of the three immunoglobulin-like domains with multiple repeated sequences in the bap gene was eliminated, possibly through a recombination event, rendering the bap gene functional through a frameshift mutation, which was also confirmed by our PCR assay amplifying the corresponding recombinant regions (deletion_2, Fig. S2B). The occurrence of two IS-elements (ISAba58 for insertion_1 and ISAba1 for insertion_2 in EAB2)-mediated transposition events also contributed to the alteration of genomic structures in the EAB2 strain, potentially changing the expression levels of downstream genes (hcaT and bap genes, encoding a putative 3-phenyl propionic acid transporter and a biofilm-associated protein, respectively) by the outward promoters of incorporated IS-elements (Fig. S2B)22. Only two missense mutations (the epsL, 28 V > A; the adeI, 336 V > I) were present in the evolved EAB2 strain, and their roles in promoting faster growth rate need further exploration, particularly with exopolysaccharide production and the function of multidrug efflux pumps (Fig. S2B). Our results suggest that rapidly growing cells can accumulate mutations, even with proofreading and repairing enzymes, under optimal nutritional conditions. Therefore, populations adapted through dramatic genomic rearrangements with a growth advantage could become highly enriched during our sampling1.

Alterations in global gene expression patterns in the EAB strains

While both evolved strains exhibited the optimal growth in their originally recovered media (EAB1 in starvation conditions and EAB2 in nutrient-rich conditions), this enhanced growth was not observed in LB media (Fig. 1A). Our transcriptome analysis revealed distinct expression profiles in a non-optimal condition (LB media) to compare gene expression under the same conditions. Both evolved strains showed up-and down-regulation of many metabolic genes and modulation of several genes related to phenotypic traits (EAB1, 12.8% up and 6.5% down-regulation; EAB2, 4.2% up and 3.9% down-regulation) (Fig. S5). Both evolved strains displayed altered expression levels of genes associated with biofilm formation (e.g., bap, ata, bamE, fimA, and pilA), along with a gene involved in oxidative stress (katE) (Fig. S5 and Supplementary Data 1). The EAB1 strain exhibited significant changes in the expression of AR genes, with marked up-regulation of genes such as aph(6)-I, aphE, and blaoxa-133, as well as down-regulation of genes such as nepA and aac2-I. These distinct AR gene expression patterns were specific to the EAB1 strain. The observed changes in gene expression in both evolved strains prompted further investigation into their corresponding phenotypic alterations in subsequent steps. The reduced expression of genes such as ata and bamE in the EAB1 strain, which are involved in biofilm formation and outer membrane protein-folding, respectively, could be linked to genetic loci changes and genomic rearrangement (deletion_3 and insertion_1, Figs. 1B and S2A) during its evolutionary history. Deletion of the ata gene and subsequent transposition of the remaining 13 genes, including bamE, to other chromosomal positions caused changes in gene expression and led to phenotypic alterations in the EAB1 strain (see the deletion_3 & insertion_1 in Fig. S6A). It is important to note that the decrease in bamE gene expression, responsible for assembling outer membrane proteins, and the deletion of the ata gene may have shifted the cellular lifestyle from biofilms to planktonic cells. The insertion of IS elements (ISAba40 and ISAba58) into other genetic loci also affected the expression of multiple downstream genes, including a hypothetical gene potentially encoding a membrane protein in the EAB1 strain and the hcaT gene, responsible for a putative 3-phenyl propionic acid transporter in the EAB2 strain (marked as insertion_2 in Fig. S6A and insertion_1 in Fig. S6B). The upregulation of the biofilm-related bap gene (5-fold) in the EAB2 strain occurred due to the insertion event of ISAba1 containing outward-directed strong promoters (Pout)22. The insertion of ISAba1 with a strong promoter in the EAB2 strain (Fig. S7A Pout) significantly increased the expression of the bap gene, which was confirmed by creating a transcriptional fusion construct with the ISAba1 promoter region upstream of the GFP gene in A. baumannii ATCC 17978 (Fig. S7B–F). This data suggests that the ISAba1 region contains a functional and robust promoter (Pout), which could be attributed to higher bap gene expression (Fig. S7).

Different degrees of phenotypic changes in the evolved strains

Losses of the AR gene through the evolution of multidrug-resistant bacteria were predicted in the absence of antibiotic selection pressure, as carrying all AR genes imposes a significant fitness cost on bacterial cells32,33. However, this scenario was not observed in our evolved strains, as all AR genes remained in their genomes. The evolved strains exhibited decreased alterations in their antibiotic susceptibilities, notably towards polymyxin B (PMB), while retaining the pmrC gene (Table S3). Cell surface charge analysis using a zeta potential analyzer revealed an average negative charge, with the EAB2 strain having a negative charge that was approximately three times lower [EAB2, (−22.3 ± 1.2) mV] compared to the WT strains [(−7.8 ± 1.0) mV] in the initial exponential phase (OD600 = 0.4). Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images using dansyl-PMB confirmed stronger binding affinity and increased fluorescence of PMB specifically to the EAB2 strain (Fig. S8A, B). In the WT strain, PMB resistance is known to result from lipid A modification by incorporating phosphoethanolamine (PEtN), mediated by the pmrC gene, which encodes a PEtN transferase31. However, our transcriptome and qRT-PCR analyses demonstrated that changes in pmrC gene expression could be negligible, suggesting that other unknown mechanisms must be associated with the PMB resistance of the evolved strains (Fig. S9). Additionally, lipid A analysis using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI–TOF MS) showed m/z 2033 peaks (representing lipid A and one PEtN residue) with no significant change in the evolved strains. In contrast, the PMB-susceptible WT strain did not exhibit the m/z 2033 peaks (Fig. S8C). The mechanism behind the increased PMB sensitivity of the evolved strains remains unclear. However, in the EAB2 strain, there was a noticeable increase in PMB binding affinity to the cell membrane. This suggests that the higher PMB sensitivity may be conferred by changes in cellular surface charges mediated by unknown systems rather than the PmrC mechanism.

Genomic and transcriptomic analyses revealed remarkable changes in biofilm-associated genes (e.g., the ata and bap genes), leading to differences in biofilm-forming capabilities between evolved and ancestor strains. Our scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images displayed varying degrees of biofilm formation and outer membrane vesicles (OMV) production, which also influenced their membrane integrities in the evolved strains (Fig. 2A). Additionally, several other biofilm-related assays were conducted, including crystal violet-based quantification of biofilm formation (Fig. 2B), quantification of extracellular polysaccharides (EPS) in cell culture supernatants (Fig. 2C), binding of Congo red to cellular surfaces (Fig. 2D), quantification of OMV using the lipophilic dye FM4-64 (Fig. 2E), and confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) images using SYPRO Ruby for biofilm matrix staining (Fig. 2F). The absence of the ata gene and the down-regulation of biofilm-related genes (e.g., bamE, fimA, and pilA) in the EAB1 strain resulted in lower levels of biofilm formation, along with reduced amounts of other biofilm-associated factors such as EPS and OMV (Supplementary Data 1). The biofilm-forming phenotype of EAB1 strain was consistent with that of a population that had evolved for 6200 generations (Fig. S11). As a result, the ISAba1-mediated overexpression of the bap gene in the EAB2 strain led to robust biofilm formation, with biofilm formation rates increasing by ~30% compared to the WT strain (Fig. 2B). A mouse infection model was employed to assess the in vivo lethality of the evolved A. baumannii strains (Fig. 3A). A group of 45 mice (C57BL/6) received intraperitoneal injections of each A. baumannii strain [~2×108 colony forming units (CFUs)], resulting in varying levels of lethality within one week [WT: 25%, EAB1: 95%, EAB2: 85%)] (Fig. 3B). The WT strain exhibited lower lethality in mice compared to the other two evolved strains. The EAB1 strain was the most lethal among them, resulting in 95% mortality among the infected mice within a day. Interestingly, the lethality of mice infected with the EAB2 strain at the end of the observation period was comparable to that of the EAB1 strain, although there was a delay in mortality during the initial two days. Despite alterations in genomic architecture and gene expression within the evolved strains, a clear understanding of their pathogenicity remains elusive. Different levels of H2O2 sensitivity and catalase activities, resulting from changes in katE gene expression (high expression, EAB1; low expression, EAB2), matched well with our phenotypic tests in both evolved strains (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Data 1). Bacterial cells with high catalase activity may have a higher chance of survival inside the host by mitigating oxidative stress, which could be one of the contributing factors to the higher pathogenicity of the EAB1 strain exhibiting upregulation of katE expression (EAB1, 2.08-fold; EAB2, 0.43-fold) in mouse infection tests34,35. In summary, our laboratory research has uncovered a significant association between virulence phenotypes and genomic changes in our multidrug-resistant strains, which underwent 8000 generations of evolution under either starvation (EAB1) or nutrient-rich (EAB2) conditions. Tracking bacterial evolution time points for determining DNA rearrangement events

To track the major genomic and physiological changes, including large-scale genomic rearrangements, insertions, and deletions, in both evolved strains collected at various generation time points, PCR assays with gene-specific primers were conducted (EAB1, Fig. 4A; EAB2, Fig. 4B, and Supplementary Data 2). Genotypic heterogeneity generated by random mutations during evolutionary periods gives rise to the high survivability of small populations with beneficial mutations under challenging nutritional conditions, employing a bet-hedging strategy, which could lead to the dominance of the final mutant cells36. The coexistence of mutant cells with WT strains resulted in three PCR-amplified products from cells collected at 2500 generations, indicating the presence of the deletion_3 region in the mutant cells. Subsequently, the mutant cells became dominant, as evidenced by a reduction to one band at 3100 generations (Fig. 4A, deletion_3). Our data revealed that the genome-rearranged strain obtained an evolutionary advantage compared to the ancestral strain and dominated the cell population. Furthermore, the absence of the ata gene is likely associated with a decrease in biofilm-forming capacity and alterations in PMB susceptibility (as depicted in Fig. 4A). Moreover, the confirmation that gene expression, as assessed through RT-PCR, remained consistent even after 3100 generations for the EAB1 strain indicates that the decline in PMB resistance was not attributable to pmrC (as shown in Fig. S9B). Notably, the WT Δata strain exhibited a marked decrease in biofilm formation capability (~2.5-fold reduction) and PMB resistance (~2.0-fold reduction), demonstrating a phenotype closely resembling that of the EAB1 strain (as indicated in Supplementary Data 1 and Fig. S10C–E). Furthermore, the enhanced biofilm formation capacity observed in the EAB2 strain was attributed to the up-regulation of the bap gene resulting from the insertion of ISAba1 (Fig. S7). Additionally, the biofilm-forming phenotypes of EAB2 are identical to those in populations that evolved for 7200 generations under nutrient-rich conditions (Fig. S11). However, it is important to note that no significant change in biofilm formation capacity was observed during the period corresponding to this genetic mutation (800–1600 generations, as shown in Fig. 4B). This intriguing discovery underscores the complexity and multifaceted nature of biofilm formation, suggesting that there are likely additional factors, beyond just the overexpression of the bap gene, that play a substantial role in this phenomenon. The ISAba58-induced gene inactivation (the hcaT gene encoding a putative 3-phenyl propionic acid transporter) and large-scale deletion of the ~10 kb identified at 7200 generations in EAB2 might affect several tested phenotypes (biofilm, cell surface charge, growth rate, etc.). The physiological adaptation of the evolved strains enhanced their growth rates under each evolutionary condition (EAB1, starvation; EAB2, nutrient-rich), but the growth defect of the EAB1 strain in rich media suggested that the abundance of nutritional level could be a stress-inducing factor to a population adapted to starvation condition (see the growth curves in Fig. 4). Genome rearrangement events and other accumulated mutations in the EAB1 cells collected from a population at a 2500 generation time might be linked to a severe reduction in their biofilm formation, which could be more effective for taking up nutritional resources from the environment and give rise to evolutionary dominance in a population with their enhanced growth rates under starvation conditions. Our findings suggest that mutational accumulation, including changes in genomic architecture and associated alterations of phenotypes during laboratory evolution, could provide evolutionary advantages to the mutant cells over competitive populations, which were the cases of both evolved strains under nutrient-rich conditions. Moreover, identical PCR and phenotypic assays were conducted on five additional replicate strains recovered from our final evolutionary time point, confirming that all replicates displayed the same major genomic and phenotypic changes as those observed in EAB1 and EAB2 (Fig. S12). These consistent trends suggest uniform adaptive patterns within each nutritional condition, underscoring the emergence and prevalence of a singular predominant strain well-adapted to these environments.

Phenotypic variations and their differential impact on pathogenesis

The adhesion and invasion capabilities of the evolved strains on epithelial cell lines were also assessed. This was prompted by our observations in the mouse infection model, which revealed varying levels of pathogenicity among these strains (Fig. 3A). A. baumannii, a respiratory pathogen, can attach to the surface of lung epithelial cells39 and potentially penetrate the epithelium, leading to bacteremia and sepsis39,40. In line with this notion, it appears that A. baumannii can transform into a highly virulent pathogen under nutrient-deprived conditions, as exemplified by EAB1. This transformation is characterized by reduced bacterial adhesion and enhanced bacterial penetration of the host barrier during epithelial cell infection (Fig. 5A–C). Bacterial penetration of the host barrier triggers an immune response and damages tissue integrity (Fig. 5C)41. Moreover, the traits displayed by the ata mutant strain resemble those observed in EAB1 (Fig. 5A–C), supporting the idea that many phenotypic transformations in EAB1 result from genomic structural rearrangements, primarily the deletion of the ata gene. Interestingly, transcriptome analysis revealed that ompA gene expression is upregulated in the EAB1 strain, which may enhance its ability to cross the epithelial barrier compared to other strains (Supplementary Data 1)41. Consequently, A. baumannii can evolve into a highly virulent strain by enhancing the ability to cross the epithelial barrier to invade blood vessels in response to nutrient scarcity. In contrast, an evolved strain thriving in nutrient-rich conditions (EAB2) exhibits increased biofilm formation (Fig. 2), influencing its physiology and pathogenesis during infections. EAB2 promotes the aggregation of its cells with host epithelial cells (Fig. 5D and Supplementary Video 1–4). This capability arises from EAB2’s adaptation to produce EPS favorable for biofilm formation (Fig. 2). Furthermore, the ability of the EAB2 strain to cross the epithelial barrier was decreased compared to the wild type and EAB1 strains (Fig. 5C). By contrast, the observed effect is not attributable to differences in growth yield within the cell culture medium, as the final growth yields in the medium were found to be equivalent (Fig. 5E). Innate immune cells in the host, such as macrophages and dendritic cells, respond to pathogenic bacteria that have invaded the host epithelium by producing cytokines and chemokines42,43. To verify this, we infected a porcine alveolar macrophage 3D4/31 cell line with A. baumannii strains and subsequently assessed cytokine and chemokine expression in the 3D4/31 cells post-infection using qRT-PCR (Fig. 6). Consistent with the infection of A549 cells, we observed a significant aggregation of bacteria, particularly in EAB2, when using the alveolar macrophage 3D4/31 cell line (Fig. S13). Remarkably, infection with EAB2 led to the expression of cytokines and chemokines in 3D4/31 cells, including IL-1A, IL-6, IL-8, LIF, and CCL2 (Fig. 6). In contrast, infection with EAB1 did not induce the production of the tested cytokines and chemokines, unlike infection with the WT strain (Fig. 6). Discussion

Bacterial survivability and adaptability in various challenging environments can be associated with changes in expression patterns, metabolic shifts, and genomic mutations. These changes can even occur differently within the same species in a population, resulting in higher-fitness individuals becoming dominant and better adapted under continuous cultivation9,44,45. Our laboratory evolution experiments with MDR A. baumannii NCCP 16007 over 8000 generations under nutritional stress conditions showed that genome rearrangements mediated by prophages and IS elements were the primary drivers of adaptive strategies over the short-term evolutionary history (Fig. 1 and S2)46. The precise causal relationship between nutritional conditions and evolutionary patterns remains unclear and requires further elucidation in future studies. Nevertheless, all replicates of the final evolved strains exhibited identical evolutionary patterns, indicating the emergence of a single predominant strain under each nutritional condition. Similar to the EAB1 strain under the starvation condition, a continuous subculture of E. coli B strain under glucose-limited media also revealed that large-scale chromosome rearrangements and IS element-mediated transposition through homologous recombination between IS elements led to genome reduction and conferred genetic adaptation for increased fitness in the evolved population15. Interestingly, the deletion of Acinetobacter-specific prophage region 1 and translocation of prophage region 2 in the EAB1 strain were likely due to the strain’s adaptive fitness under nutrient-deficient habitat (Fig. S2A)47. The two Acinetobacter-specific prophages (YMC11/11/R3177 and Bphi_B1251) were identified as the most prevalent prophages in Acinetobacter species, although their lifecycles have not been experimentally demonstrated (Figs. S2 and S3)48,49. The RecA-dependent SOS response is known to activate the antirepressor of prophage regions, which can derepress the repressor of prophages, leading to their excision and reduction of bacterial genome50–52.

The insertion of IS elements (IS903 and IS981) upstream of the silent tetA and the ldhB genes induced the expression of both the tetA and the ldhB genes, thereby conferring tetracycline resistance and lactate degradation to cells53,54. Consistent with these findings, the integration of the IS element (ISAba1) with a strong promoter upstream of the bap gene resulted in the upregulation of the bap gene (5-fold), which may be linked to enhanced biofilm formation in our evolved EAB2 strain (Figs. S2 and 2). Nevertheless, during the timeframe spanning from 800 to 1600 generations, corresponding to the ISAba1 insertion_2 event, there was no discernible change in the biofilm formation of the EAB2 strain. The loss of the ybaB gene in the EAB2 strain might have impacted the expression of many metabolic process-related genes, as speculated based on the case of Borrelia burgdorferi B31, where overexpression of the ybaB gene led to changes in the expression patterns of many genes (i.e., ~1/4 of all genes exhibited altered expression levels)55. While increasing the expression of the bap gene (5-fold) in the EAB2 strain might seem like a straightforward intervention, it is crucial to consider the complex context involving the type I secretion system (T1SS)56. The expression levels of T1SS machinery genes (tolC, hlyB, and hlyD) in the EAB2 strain, ranging from 1.0 to 1.2-fold, did not exhibit the same up-regulation observed in the bap gene. This lack of up-regulation could potentially hinder the efficient folding and localization of the Bap protein to the outer membrane. Therefore, our data suggest that the Bap protein alone may not be solely responsible for the heightened capacity for biofilm formation observed in the EAB2 strain, underscoring the need for further genetic investigations to elucidate the mechanisms underlying enhanced biofilm formation in the EAB2 strain (Fig. 3).

The EAB1 and ata mutant strains, which showed reduced biofilm formation, demonstrated increased invasion abilities in epithelial cells and exhibited elevated cytotoxicity (Fig. 5B, C). The decreased biofilm formation and enhanced penetration efficiency of the epithelial barrier in the EAB1 and ata mutant strains could allow evasion of the immune response. This may explain why the EAB1 strain exhibits high virulence in mouse infection studies (Figs. 3B and 5C). This shift in lifestyle could offer advantages to the starvation-evolved EAB1 strain by aiding its evasion of the host immune response57,58. Notably, the dispersion phase enables bacteria to explore fresh nutrient sources, leading to a decrease in the dispersion rate from mature biofilms in nutrient-rich environments59–62. The EAB2 strain exhibited enhanced biofilm formation and cellular aggregation during infection due to increased EPS production (Fig. S13). The absence of either BdlA or DipA was associated with non-dispersing lifestyles and appeared to decrease the pathogenicity of P. aeruginosa63. The compromised dispersal capability could account for the EAB2 strain’s diminished penetration effectiveness to the epithelial barrier (Fig. 5). Conversely, the biofilm produced by the EAB2 strain intensified host mortality by triggering a cytokine storm marked by elevated levels of circulating cytokines and heightened immune cell activity (Fig. 6). In an in vivo mouse IP infection model, the evolved strains were found to be more virulent than the WT, and differences in the patterns of lethality between EAB1 and EAB2 suggest that the mechanisms underlying their lethality may differ. This notion is supported by the in vitro A549 and 3D4/31 infection assays, as the observed cellular responses and bacterial phenotypes differ between EAB1 and EAB2 (Figs. S13 and S14). Similarly, continuous selective pressure by the surrounding environment has been reported to reduce A. baumannii adherence to human epithelial cells and internalization by THP-1-derived macrophages64. Macrophages exhibited a rapid expression of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines when exposed to EAB2 but not to EAB1, a response typically seen in reaction to A. baumannii and its extracellular components, such as EPS and OMV65–67. In a previous study, toll-like receptor 4-deficient mice showed a decrease in A. baumannii clearance and could not control bacterial dissemination, correlated with low IL-6 and CXCL2 levels66. Conversely, persistent upregulation of the inflammatory response can lead to a cytokine storm and septic shock68.

The respiratory epithelium is crucial as the initial barrier and immune response regulator against pathogens. When pathogens compromise this epithelial barrier, the epithelial cells initiate an immune response. This prompts the release of cytokines by the epithelium, which triggers inflammatory reactions and attracts various immune cells69. However, the influx of these immune cells can inadvertently damage the tissue further, especially during their movement across the epithelial layer as they combat pathogens. These breaches in the epithelial defense can allow pathogens to penetrate more deeply. Supporting this idea, a comparison of CCL2 and CCL5 expression levels in A549 cells revealed notably higher values in EAB2 than in the wild type and EAB1 (Fig. S15). CCL2 and CCL5, produced by the epithelium, are pivotal in attracting monocytes and neutrophils, which can lead to severe damage to epithelial barriers70. Our findings suggest that EAB2 has a greater potential for harm in mouse survival tests, even though it releases less LDH than the wild type and EAB1 (Figs. 3B and 5C). This may be attributed to its enhanced CCL2 and CCL5 production, driving epithelial damage through a more robust immune cell response. Our hypothesis regarding the virulence mechanisms of the evolved strains is as follows: EAB1 evades host immune responses, replicates rapidly, and spreads into host organs, leading to host functional failure and mortality, whereas EAB2 adheres, aggregates, secretes EPS and OMVs, triggering a hyperinflammatory response in the host. This results in a cytokine storm, and the host may either recover or succumb to septic shock. The specific genomic factors responsible for these distinct virulence phenotypes in the evolved strains remain unclear, and further research is needed to uncover the correlation between genomic evolution and the molecular mechanisms underlying the virulence of A. baumannii.

In an evolution experiment conducted under antibiotic-free culture conditions, the MDR E.coli W5-6 strain experienced the loss of the pMCR_W5-6 plasmid (~240 kb) carrying many AR genes (e.g., mcr-1, cmlA1, and intl1) after 40 serial subcultivations71. However, the impact of serial transfers and subsequent laboratory incubation on genome structure and gene expression in recent clinical isolates is often overlooked. Contrary to expectations, both laboratory-evolved EAB strains retained all AR genes throughout 8000 generations, indicating that AR genes on the chromosomes are more stable than anticipated during our evolutionary experiments. In addition, even when exposed to stress conditions like a starvation environment, A. baumannii has been reported to maintain AR genes related to virulence and high pathogenicity, indicating that AR genes are located in stable genomic regions72,73. While the loss of AR genes may have necessitated longer evolutionary periods, our study observed genomic alterations and increased pathogenicity, highlighting the need for caution when managing these pathogens. Our findings illustrate how a single clinical isolate can evolve into a more virulent strain, offering insights that may apply broadly to other clinical A. baumannii strains and potentially to other bacterial species as well. This highlights the need for increased caution when handling bacterial isolates from healthcare settings and conducting serial propagation in laboratory culture media.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and evolution experiment

A. baumannii ATCC 17978 obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) was used as the reference strain and was maintained in the laboratory. A. baumannii NCCP 16007 (WT) was supplied by the National Culture Collection for Pathogens (NCCP) in the Republic of Korea. The EAB strains were derived from the WT through generation time evolution experiments. EAB1 was induced through evolution under starvation conditions, while EAB2 was cultured under nutrient-rich conditions. For the starvation condition, a 1.5% agar plate was prepared using an M9 minimal medium containing 0.1% glucose (JUNSEI, Japan) and 0.1% casamino acids (NEOGEN, USA). For the nutrient-rich condition, a 1.5% agar plate was prepared using Reasoner’s 2 A (R2A) medium supplemented with casamino acids (NEOGEN, USA). The WT populations were propagated at 25 °C for 7800 generations in starvation agar medium, with entire colonies selected and transferred to fresh agar medium every 3 days. Alternatively, they were propagated at 37 °C for 8000 generations in eutrophic agar medium, with fresh agar medium serially passaged every 2 days. This evolution experiment spanned a total of 210 days. Samples from each population were collected at 500–8000 generation intervals and stored at −80 °C with a 50% glycerol (JUNSEI, Japan) concentration.

Growth measurement

Growth curve analyses were conducted to detect changes in fitness resulting from evolution. To assess the fitness of each generation strain throughout the evolutionary process, EAB1 was cultured in LB broth and under starvation conditions [M9 minimal broth containing 0.1% glucose (JUNSEI, Japan)], whereas EAB2 was cultured under nutrient-rich conditions [1% casamino acids (NEOGEN, USA) was added to R2A broth]. The OD600 (optical density at 600 nm) of each strain was automatically measured at 1-hour intervals using a spectrophotometer (TECAN, SUNRISE, Switzerland), and the exponential growth rates (OD600 increase per unit of incubation time) were determined to measure fitness. The CFUs of all A. baumannii strains were measured at 3 and 24 h post subculture by serial dilution and plating on LB agar plates.

H2O2 sensitivity and catalase activity test

All strains were assessed for their sensitivity to hydrogen peroxide (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) using the disc inhibition assay as follows. Exponential phase cultures (OD600 = 0.5) were spread onto LB agar plates, and sterilized 8 mm paper disks (ADVANTEC, Japan) were placed on the agar surface. Subsequently, 10 μL of various concentrations (0.2, 0.5, 1, and 2 M) of H2O2 (freshly diluted in DW) were spotted on each disc. After overnight incubation at 37 °C, the diameters of the growth inhibition zones were measured. The catalase activity test relied on the formation of a stable and colored carbonate-cobaltate (III) complex ([Co (CO3)3]Co)74. Briefly, the culture in mid-exponential phase (OD600 ~ 0.7) was incubated at 37 °C with 10 mM hydrogen peroxide for 2 min, then rapidly mixed with a cobalt-bicarbonate reagent composed of 100 mL of cobalt (II) solution (Alfa Aesar, USA), 100 mL of sodium hexametaphosphate solution (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and 1800 mL of sodium bicarbonate solution (JUNSEI, Japan). The mixture was incubated in the dark for 10 min at room temperature. A standard was measured in DW instead of cell culture, and a blank was measured in DW only. Finally, the 440 nm peak was used to evaluate catalase activity.

Mouse intraperitoneal challenge and lethality assay

Animal experiments using C57BL/6 N mice were conducted at Kangwon National University. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. All experimental and animal care procedures received approval from the Kangwon National University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees (Approval Number: KW-220418-2) and were conducted in compliance with standard guidelines. Female C57BL/6 N mice, aged four weeks, were procured from Koatech Inc (Pyeongtaek, Korea) and were acclimated for a minimum of five days. A. baumannii strains grown O/N were diluted into 50 mL LB broth and incubated at 37 °C with continuous agitation at 180 rpm for 4 h until they reached the exponential phase (OD600 = 0.5). Bacterial cells were collected, washed, and resuspended in PBS. Following acclimatization, the mice were intraperitoneally challenged with ~2.0 × 108 CFUs (volume adjusted to 100 μL) of one of the three A. baumannii strains: WT, EAB1, or EAB2. As a control, mice were treated with 100 μL PBS. The challenged mice were observed for up to seven days and were humanely euthanized if they displayed apparent symptoms. After 7-day observation, mice were euthanized by CO2 asphyxiation.

Bacterial infection of the human lung epithelial cell

Human lung epithelial cell A549 cells were obtained from the Korean Cell Line Bank (KCLB), South Korea. A549 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (WELGENE, Korea) supplemented with 10% FBS (WELGENE, Korea) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment. For bacterial infection experiments, 2 × 105 cells were seeded in a 24-well cell culture plate (SPL Life Sciences, Korea) or 1 × 105 at the apical side of trans-well in the 24-well cell culture plates (SPL Life Sciences, Korea) and incubated for 20 h to establish confluent monolayers. A. baumannii strains were used for infection at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100 for adhesion and penetration, and an MOI of 1 for time-lapse imaging, Congo red binding assays, and qRT-PCR. The bacterial O/N culture was diluted in 5 mL of LB broth and incubated until it reached the exponential phase (OD600 = 0.5) at 37 °C with continuous agitation at 180 rpm. Bacterial cells were suspended in prewarmed RPMI 1640 medium (WELGENE, Korea) supplemented with 10% FBS (WELGENE, Korea) and then added to the A549 cell monolayer. The plate was incubated for infection at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment after centrifugation at 700×g for 10 min except for the penetration test.

Adhesion and penetration assay to human epithelial cells

A549 cells were infected with A. baumannii strains at an MOI of 100, as described previously (See the method ‘Bacterial infection of the human lung epithelial cell’), after which the plate was incubated for 6 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment. For adhesion assay, to determine the total number of bacteria interacting with A549 cells, after the incubation, each well was washed five times with prewarmed Dulbecco’s PBS (DPBS) (WELGENE, Korea) to remove planktonic bacteria. Subsequently, A549 cells were lysed using DPBS supplemented with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment for 30 min. Following the incubation period for bacterial invasion, prewarmed RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 250 μg/mL polymyxin B (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added for 1 h to remove the attached bacterial cells. Subsequently, the wells were washed twice with prewarmed DPBS and lysed as described above. For penetration assay, after incubation for 6 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment, trans-wells were gently removed and culture media at the basal side was collected. To determine the CFUs of adhesion or penetration, the lysate or cell culture media from the basal side was serially diluted with PBS and plated onto LB agar. The CFU number of adhesions was calculated by subtracting the number of invasive bacteria from the total number of bacteria.

Bacterial infection of porcine alveolar macrophage 3D4/31

Porcine alveolar macrophage 3D4/31 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment. For bacterial infection, 3.5 × 105 or 3.5 × 106 cells were seeded onto a 24-well or a 6-well cell culture plate (SPL Life Sciences, Korea) for 24 h before the infection experiment. A. baumannii strains were used for infection at a MOI of 10 for qRT-PCR, ELISA, and adhesion/invasion tests. The bacterial overnight culture was diluted in 50 mL LB broth and incubated at 37 °C with continuous agitation at 180 rpm for 4 h until they reached the exponential phase (OD600 = 0.5). Bacterial cells were collected, washed with PBS, and resuspended in prewarmed RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. The bacterial cells were then added to the 3D4/31 cell culture dish and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment.

Adhesion and invasion assay for 3D4/31

3D4/31 or A549 cells in the 24-well plate were infected with A. baumannii strains at a MOI of 10 or 100, respectively, as previously described, and then incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment for 8 h. To determine the total number of bacterial cells interacting with 3D4/31 cells (adhered + internalized), each well was washed five times with prewarmed PBS to remove planktonic bacteria. Subsequently, 3D4/31 cells were lysed using PBS supplemented with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment for 30 min. The bacterial cells were then diluted, plated on LB agar plates, and incubated at 37 °C for 19 h. The total number of interacting bacterial cells was counted directly. Regarding the internalized A. baumannii, each well was washed five times with PBS, and a prewarmed RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 250 μg/mL polymyxin B (Sigma-Aldrich) was added. The plate was then incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment for 1 h to eliminate adherent bacterial cells. Following this incubation period, the wells were washed three times with prewarmed PBS and lysed with 0.1% Triton X-100. The bacterial cells were subsequently diluted, plated on LB agar plates, and incubated at 37 °C for 19 h. The number of adherent A. baumannii cells was determined by subtracting the number of internalized cells from the total number of interacting cells.

Statistics and reproducibility

The statistical analyses of data and sample size are detailed in the relevant subsections of the Methods and Results and are also included in the corresponding Figure legends. GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 was used for data analysis and visualization. For multiple comparisons, one-way or two-way ANOVA was applied, as appropriate.
