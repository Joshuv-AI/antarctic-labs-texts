Declining incidence of hypoglycaemia in diabetes secondary to chronic pancreatitis: A nationwide cohort study from 2003 to 2022
Authors: Line Davidsen, Morten H Jensen, Asbjørn M Drewes, Filip K Knop, Frederico G S Toledo, Søren S Olesen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsPopulation‐based studies have consistently shown that individuals with diabetes secondary to chronic pancreatitis (pancreatic diabetes) have a high risk of hypoglycaemia. We aimed to investigate whether this risk has declined over recent years following the introduction of modern glucose‐lowering medications.Materials and MethodsIn this Danish nationwide population‐based cohort study, we included all adults with new‐onset diabetes between 1998 and 2022 and classified them as having pancreatic diabetes, type 1, or type 2 diabetes. We examined annual incidence rates of hypoglycaemia leading to hospitalisation, trends in dispensed glucose‐lowering medications, and associations with hypoglycaemia risk.ResultsAmong 583 220 individuals with new‐onset diabetes, 3383 (0.6%) were classified as pancreatic diabetes, 12 383 (2.1%) as type 1 diabetes, and 567 454 (97.3%) as type 2 diabetes. Comparing 2003–2006 to 2019–2022, the incidence of severe hypoglycaemia declined within each diabetes subtype: by 69% in pancreatic diabetes (incidence rate ratio (IRR) 0.33, 95% CI 0.25–0.40), 57% in type 1 diabetes (IRR 0.43, 95% CI 0.38–0.49), and 65% in type 2 diabetes (IRR 0.35, 95% CI 0.33–0.37). This coincided with a shift from human to analogue insulin and an increase in the use of metformin in individuals with pancreatic diabetes. Metformin use was associated with a lower risk of severe hypoglycaemia (adjusted hazard ratio 0.36, 95% CI 0.26–0.48). All types of insulin were associated with higher hypoglycaemia risk; insulin analogues carried a lower risk than human insulin.ConclusionHospitalisation for hypoglycaemia in pancreatic diabetes has declined substantially over time, alongside notable changes in treatment patterns.Keywords: chronic pancreatitis, glucose‐lowering medications, hypoglycaemia, secondary diabetes, time trends

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes secondary to chronic pancreatitis, also known as pancreatic diabetes, is increasingly recognised as a distinct and clinically challenging condition. 1 , 2 As the burden of chronic pancreatitis rises, so does the prevalence of pancreatic diabetes. 3 , 4 Unlike type 1 and type 2 diabetes, pancreatic diabetes occurs in the context of irreversible damage to both the endocrine and exocrine components of the pancreas; this damage leads to insulin and glucagon deficiencies, impaired insulin response to incretins, and nutrient maldigestion due to exocrine pancreatic insufficiency. 5 , 6 These pathophysiological characteristics contribute to glycaemic variability and an elevated risk of hypoglycaemia. 7 , 8 , 9 Accordingly, population‐based studies have consistently shown that individuals with pancreatic diabetes face a highly elevated risk of severe hypoglycaemia compared to those with other diabetes subtypes. 7 , 8 , 9

In type 1 and type 2 diabetes, rates of hypoglycaemia have declined over the last decades, 10 , 11 , 12 likely due to advances in insulin treatment, the introduction of newer non‐insulin glucose‐lowering medications (e.g., glucagon‐like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RA) and sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors), and the introduction of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM). 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 While decreasing trends in hypoglycaemia have been well documented in type 1 and type 2 diabetes, corresponding data for pancreatic diabetes are lacking. 12 Similarly, evidence on temporal changes in glucose‐lowering medications in this group is sparse. 19

In a population‐based setting, utilising national Danish health registries, we investigated (i) time trends in hypoglycaemia leading to hospitalisation among individuals with pancreatic diabetes, type 1 diabetes, and type 2 diabetes, (ii) the use of glucose‐lowering medications in pancreatic diabetes, and (iii) the association between glucose‐lowering medications and the risk of first‐time hypoglycaemia leading to hospitalisation in pancreatic diabetes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and data sources

We conducted a nationwide retrospective cohort study encompassing all incident cases of adult‐onset diabetes in Denmark from 1 January 1998, to 31 December 2022. In Denmark, each resident is assigned a unique civil registration number, facilitating the linkage of personal data across various national health registries. For this study, data were obtained from 6 registries. The Civil Registration System, established in 1968, records sex, date of birth, migration dates, and vital status for all permanent residents in Denmark. 20 The Danish National Patient Registry (DNPR), operational since 1977, includes records of all hospital admissions and, since 1995, outpatient and emergency visits, with diagnoses coded using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition (ICD‐10) since 1994. 21 The Danish Cancer Registry, used since 1943, tracks all cancer diagnoses. 22 The Danish National Prescription Registry, established in 1995, maintains accurate records of prescribed medications dispensed at pharmacies. 23 The Clinical Laboratory Information Registry includes data on biochemistry. 24 The Danish Education Registry provides data on educational attainment. 25

Study cohort

Individuals with new‐onset diabetes were identified based on either a prescription for glucose‐lowering medications (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) code A10) recorded in the Danish National Prescription Registry or the presence of a diabetes‐related ICD‐10 diagnosis code (E10.x, E11.x, E12.x, E13.x, E14.x, G63.2, H28.0, H36.0, M14.2, O24, or R73) in the DNPR. This strategy, based on previously validated algorithms, captures diabetes diagnoses from both primary care and hospital visits. 12 , 19 , 26 The onset of diabetes was defined as the first registered occurrence of either an ATC or an ICD‐10 code. We excluded individuals with a prior diabetes diagnosis (from 1996 to 1998), those younger than 18 years at the onset of diabetes, and those with a diagnosis of pancreatic cancer between 1 January 1996, and the date of diabetes onset.

Individuals were initially classified as having either type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Type 1 diabetes was defined by the presence of both an insulin prescription (ATC A10A) and a diagnosis code (ICD‐10 E10.x). All others were classified as having type 2 diabetes. 7 , 12 , 26 Individuals with a diagnosis of chronic pancreatitis (ICD‐10 K86) at least 3 months prior to their diabetes diagnosis were reclassified as having pancreatic diabetes, based on established criteria. 27 The final cohort comprised three subgroups: pancreatic diabetes, type 1 diabetes, and type 2 diabetes.

Outcomes

We examined trends in severe hypoglycaemia from 1 January 2003, to 31 December 2022. Severe hypoglycaemia was defined as events leading to hospitalisation, including both emergency department visits and inpatient admissions. Events were identified using data from DNPR, based on emergency department visits or hospital admissions with a primary or secondary ICD‐10 code of E159, E160, E161, or E162, recorded after the diabetes diagnosis. Outpatient contacts were identified and excluded via an algorithm proposed by the Danish Ministry of Health and the Elderly. 28

We investigated trends in the use of glucose‐lowering medications between 2003 and 2022 in individuals with pancreatic diabetes. Prescription data were categorised using ATC codes from the Danish Prescription Registry, excluding medications that were rarely represented. Intermediate‐acting isophane human insulin was grouped with long‐acting insulin to ensure analytical consistency, in line with previous studies. 12 A complete list of medication groups and corresponding ATC codes is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

We explored the association between glucose‐lowering medications and the risk of first‐time severe hypoglycaemia in individuals with pancreatic diabetes. To account for the dynamic nature of exposure to glucose‐lowering medications and to mitigate the risk of immortal‐time bias, we modelled the medication exposure as time‐varying. This allowed individuals to be classified as either exposed or non‐exposed to medications at different time points. Dispensing of a prescription was used as a proxy for treatment initiation, with individuals considered exposed from the time their prescription was dispensed and for the following 180 days. If a new prescription was dispensed within this period, exposure was extended for another 180 days. If no further prescriptions were dispensed, individuals were reclassified as non‐exposed until a new prescription was issued, an event occurred, or the end of the follow‐up period.

Patient characteristics

Baseline data were obtained from 1 January 1996, until the date of diabetes diagnosis. Information on age and sex was collected from the Danish Civil Registration System. Comorbidities and medication use were identified through DNPR and the National Prescription Registry. We captured diagnoses of obesity and chronic kidney disease, and medications involving pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy, antidepressants, antithrombotics, anxiolytics, and statins (Supplementary Table S1). The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was calculated using ICD‐10 codes from DNPR and the Danish Cancer Registry (Supplementary Table S2). 29 Smoking status was defined from tobacco‐related ICD‐10 diagnoses and prescriptions for smoking cessation or obstructive pulmonary disease in individuals aged 40 or older. 26 , 30 Alcohol abuse was assessed through diagnoses or prescriptions indicative of alcohol‐related conditions. Information on biochemistry data was reported for individuals with biochemical information available within a window of 90 days before to 7 days after the diabetes diagnosis, using the measurement closest to baseline. Biochemical information, extracted from the Danish Clinical Laboratory Information, included haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), total cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, and triglycerides. Information on the highest completed education level was obtained from the Danish Education Registry and classified as high school or lower versus above high school. An outline of the study design is provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics are summarised as medians (interquartile ranges [IQR]), means (standard deviations [SD]), or counts (%). For each diabetes subtype, the total number of hypoglycaemic episodes is presented, along with the distribution of individuals experiencing one, two, or more episodes. The incidence of severe hypoglycaemic episodes over the 20‐year period is expressed as the incidence rate (IR) per 100 person‐years, presented annually. Poisson regression models were used to analyse trends in severe hypoglycaemic episodes across defined year intervals, including 2003–2006, 2007–2010, 2011–2014, 2015–2018, and 2019–2022. The number of episodes was the dependent variable, the year interval was included as a categorical independent variable, and models were adjusted for the log of person‐years of exposure. The interval 2003–2006 served as the reference, and incidence rate ratios (IRRs) were derived from the model coefficients.

The annual proportion of patients with pancreatic diabetes who dispensed prescriptions for each glucose‐lowering medication from 2003 to 2022 was presented to illustrate trends over time. To examine the association between selected glucose‐lowering medications and the risk of first‐time severe hypoglycaemia, Cox regression models were applied. Insulin was categorised into four types: human long‐acting, human fast‐acting, analogue long‐acting, and analogue fast‐acting insulin. Glucose‐lowering medications were considered time‐varying exposures. For metformin and sulfonylureas, the models were adjusted for sex, age (as a time‐varying covariate), education, alcohol abuse, heavy smoking, CCI, chronic kidney disease, and insulin use (time‐varying). For insulin, models were adjusted for sex, age (time‐varying), education, alcohol abuse, heavy smoking, CCI, and chronic kidney disease. Follow‐up started on 1 January 2003, or the date of diabetes diagnosis if it occurred later, and continued until the occurrence of severe hypoglycaemia, emigration, death, or the end of follow‐up (31 December 2022), whichever occurred first. 26 Hazard rate ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p‐value <0.05. Data management and statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute), Stata 17.0 (Stata Corp), and R 4.3.2 (R Development Core Team).

RESULTS

Between 1 January 1996, and 31 December 2022, we identified 811 728 individuals diagnosed with diabetes. After excluding 216 247 with prevalent diabetes, 10 320 individuals below 18 years, and 1951 with a history of pancreatic cancer prior to their diabetes diagnosis, the cohort comprised 583 220 adults with new‐onset diabetes diagnosed between 1 January 1998, and 31 December 2022. Among these, 3383 (0.6%) had pancreatic diabetes, 12 383 (2.1%) had type 1 diabetes, and 567 454 (97.3%) had type 2 diabetes. A flowchart is shown in Supplementary Figure S2.

Detailed baseline characteristics by diabetes subtype are provided in Table 1. Patients with pancreatic diabetes and type 2 diabetes were comparable in terms of age (mean age 57.9 and 58.2 years), while those with type 1 diabetes were younger (mean age 45.4 years). Pancreatic diabetes was more prevalent in men (68.8%), and these patients had a higher prevalence of comorbidities, higher rates of smoking and alcohol abuse, and a lower education level compared to individuals with type 1 and type 2 diabetes.

Hospitalisation for hypoglycaemia from 2003 to 2022

During a median follow‐up of 4.9 (IQR 1.7–10.0) years, 373 (11%) patients with pancreatic diabetes experienced 721 hospitalisations due to hypoglycaemia. Of these, 234 (6.9%) had one episode, 64 (1.9%) had two episodes, and 75 (2.2%) had more than two episodes (Table 1). Among individuals with type 1 diabetes, 1475 (11.9%) individuals experienced 2563 severe hypoglycaemic episodes over a median follow‐up of 7.6 (2.6–15.0) years, while 13 445 episodes were registered in 10 461 (1.8%) individuals with type 2 diabetes over a median follow‐up of 6.6 (2.0–12.5) years (Table 1).

The annual IRs of severe hypoglycaemia across pancreatic, type 1, and type 2 diabetes are illustrated in Figure 1. In 2003, the incidence of severe hypoglycaemia in pancreatic diabetes was approximately 10 episodes per 100 person‐years, compared with 4 episodes in type 1 diabetes and 0.6 episodes in type 2 diabetes. All three subtypes reached their highest IR in 2003, followed by a steady decline thereafter. The reduction was most pronounced in pancreatic diabetes, with IR falling to 2.2 episodes per 100 person‐years by 2022, compared to 1.4 and 0.2 episodes per 100 person‐years in type 1 and type 2 diabetes, respectively. The IRRs for each diabetes subtype calculated over predefined year intervals (2003–2006, 2007–2010, 2011–2014, 2015–2018, and 2019–2022) are presented in Table 2, along with the corresponding number of hypoglycaemia events and person‐years of follow‐up. Comparing the periods 2003–2006 and 2019–2022, the incidence of severe hypoglycaemia decreased by 69% in pancreatic diabetes (IRR 0.33, 95% CI 0.25–0.40), 57% in type 1 diabetes (IRR 0.43, 95% CI 0.38–0.49), and 65% in type 2 diabetes (IRR 0.35, 95% CI 0.33–0.37).

Prescription of glucose‐lowering medications from 2003 to 2022 in pancreatic diabetes

The annual proportion of patients with pancreatic diabetes who dispensed a prescription of glucose‐lowering medications between 1 January 2003, and 31 December 2022, is presented in Figure 2, stratified as non‐insulin glucose‐lowering medications (Figure 2A) and insulins (Figure 2B). Sulfonylureas were the most dispensed non‐insulin glucose‐lowering medication from 2003 to 2007, after which metformin became predominant. From approximately 2018, an increase in the dispensation of GLP‐1RA and SGLT2 inhibitors was observed.

From 2003 to 2009, long‐acting insulin prescriptions were primarily composed of human insulin. Hereafter, the use of human insulin began to decline, coinciding with an increase in the use of insulin detemir and insulin glargine. From 2015 onward, the use of insulin degludec increased. Between 2009 and 2016, during the transition from human to analogue long‐acting insulins, combination therapies containing insulin aspart and insulin aspart protamine represented a significant proportion of prescriptions dispensed. For fast‐acting insulins, prescriptions for insulin aspart steadily increased from 2009, while the use of human fast‐acting insulins and combination therapies gradually declined.

Glucose‐lowering medications and the risk of severe hypoglycaemia in pancreatic diabetes

The association between prescriptions of metformin, sulfonylurea, human insulin, and insulin analogues and first‐time severe hypoglycaemia leading to hospitalisation in individuals with pancreatic diabetes is presented in Figure 3. Metformin was associated with a lower risk of severe hypoglycaemia (adjusted HR 0.36, 95% CI 0.26–0.48) when compared with those not receiving metformin. In contrast, insulin was associated with an increased risk, with the highest HR observed for long‐acting human insulin (adjusted HR 2.82, 95% CI 2.25–3.53) and the lowest for fast‐acting insulin analogues (adjusted HR 1.63, 95% CI 1.24–2.14). There was no difference in the risk of severe hypoglycaemia between patients receiving and those not receiving sulfonylureas (adjusted HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.64–1.37).

DISCUSSION

The incidence of severe hypoglycaemia leading to hospitalisation in Denmark has declined markedly over the last 20 years across all diabetes subtypes, with the most pronounced reduction observed in pancreatic diabetes. Individuals with pancreatic diabetes continued, nevertheless, to have the highest hypoglycaemia incidence rate in the period from 2019 to 2022. In parallel, treatment patterns in pancreatic diabetes have shifted notably, including a transition from human insulin to insulin analogues, a reduction in the use of sulfonylureas, and an increase in the use of metformin, followed more recently by the introduction of GLP‐1RA and SGLT2 inhibitors. Finally, among patients with pancreatic diabetes, treatment with metformin was associated with a reduced risk of severe hypoglycaemia, whereas all insulin types were linked to an increased risk, with the lowest risk observed for insulin analogues.

Temporal changes in hospitalisation risk for hypoglycaemia

Previous retrospective studies have shown an elevated risk of severe hypoglycaemia in pancreatic diabetes, with reported incidence rates ranging from 23 to 49 per 1000 person‐years and adjusted risk estimates 2.1 to 5.3‐fold higher than in type 2 diabetes. 7 , 8 , 9 However, these studies reflect average risks over time and do not capture temporal trends. In contrast, our study is the first to assess annual incidence rates of severe hypoglycaemia in pancreatic diabetes, enabling evaluation of changes over time. We observed a nearly 70% reduction in hypoglycaemia‐related hospitalisations from 2003–2006 to 2019–2022 in pancreatic diabetes, alongside a 57% reduction in type 1 diabetes and a 65% reduction in type 2 diabetes. These trends in type 1 and type 2 diabetes are consistent with prior studies reporting declining severe hypoglycaemia rates. 10 , 11 , 12 Despite the overall improvement, pancreatic diabetes continued to have the highest incidence of hypoglycaemia‐related hospitalisation in 2022.

Glucose‐lowering medications and the risk of severe hypoglycaemia in pancreatic diabetes

The observed decline in the incidence of hypoglycaemia‐related hospitalisations in pancreatic diabetes likely reflects changes in glucose‐lowering treatment patterns. We observed a substantial increase in metformin use, which was also associated with a reduced risk of first‐time hospitalisation due to severe hypoglycaemia. A previous population‐based study has similarly reported a reduced risk of severe hypoglycaemia with metformin treatment in pancreatic diabetes, 26 and metformin is recommended as the first‐line treatment for pancreatic diabetes. 31 Concurrently, we observed a decline in the use of sulfonylureas. Although sulfonylureas are well known to increase the risk of hypoglycaemia in type 2 diabetes, 12 , 32 , 33 we did not observe a similar signal in pancreatic diabetes. This may reflect impaired beta cell function in this group. 34 Moreover, a substantial rise in the use of GLP‐1RA and SGLT2 inhibitors was observed from around 2018. In pancreatic diabetes, their glucose‐lowering effects and safety remain uncertain. Concerns about an increased risk of acute pancreatitis with GLP‐1RA are based on limited and conflicting evidence. 35 , 36 , 37 For SGLT2 inhibitors, reduced beta cell function or mass may increase the risk of euglycemic ketoacidosis, warranting caution. 38 Therefore, studies investigating the risks and safety of GLP‐1RA and SGLT2 inhibitors in pancreatic diabetes are of great interest. In the present study, limited treatment duration and small patient numbers precluded a formal analysis, including their association with severe hypoglycaemia.

Shifts in insulin types likely also contribute to the decline in severe hypoglycaemia incidence. Since the introduction of insulin analogues, we observed a gradual increase in their use among patients with pancreatic diabetes, largely replacing human insulin over time. These newer insulins have more stable pharmacokinetics, including more predictable absorption and duration of action. They are generally associated with a lower risk of hypoglycaemia than human insulin, as demonstrated in previous studies of type 1 and type 2 diabetes. 13 , 14 , 16 , 39 Although we did not directly compare individual insulin types in this study, the risk estimates suggest that analogue insulins may be associated with a lower risk of severe hypoglycaemia than human insulins. The use of analogue insulin also aligns temporally with the decline in severe hypoglycaemia incidence, suggesting a potential contribution. Our findings suggest that insulin analogues may represent a safer option for treating pancreatic diabetes than human insulin formulations.

In addition to changes in prescription patterns of glucose‐lowering medications, other factors may have contributed to the decline in severe hypoglycaemia. First, CGM was introduced in Denmark around 2016, although access has remained limited. Until March 2023, CGM was recommended only for selected adults with type 1 diabetes, and patients with type 2 diabetes are still required to cover a substantial portion of the CGM costs, introducing socioeconomic bias. 40 Moreover, patients with pancreatic diabetes are often misclassified as having type 2 diabetes, raising concerns about whether they are appropriately considered for CGM. While CGM improved glycaemic control in pancreatic diabetes and tended to reduce hypoglycaemia in a recent randomised controlled trial from our group, 41 systematic registration of CGM use in Danish health registries has only recently begun and remains incomplete and unvalidated for research purposes. Therefore, we were unable to study the impact of CGM use on the risk of severe hypoglycaemia in the present study. Second, unmeasured factors, such as improved patient education and increased awareness of diabetes, may also have contributed to the declining incidence of severe hypoglycaemia. Third, improved nutritional support and pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy could contribute to reducing hypoglycaemia risk by mitigating malnutrition and stabilising glucose metabolism, 42 but are unlikely to fully explain the decline in severe hypoglycaemia. Finally, shifts in the demographic profile of the chronic pancreatitis population may affect the risk of severe hypoglycaemia. In recent years, a growing proportion of individuals are diagnosed at earlier disease stages. 4 Hence, the phenotype of the chronic pancreatitis population may have shifted, with more patients diagnosed prior to the development of pancreatic diabetes or at a disease stage where hyperglycaemia can be managed without insulin. 4 This may also contribute to the lower risk of severe hypoglycaemia.

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS

This study has several strengths. It is based on high‐quality, nationwide data from Danish health registries, which are prospectively maintained and allow for near‐complete long‐term follow‐up. The large, population‐based design ensures generalisability and provides strong statistical power. However, various limitations should be considered. First, we relied on hospitalisation data to identify episodes of hypoglycaemia, which most likely underestimates the true burden, as not all hypoglycaemic events lead to hospitalisation. While changes in healthcare‐seeking behaviour or coding practices could theoretically influence trends over time, we consider this unlikely to have substantially biased our findings. Second, dispensed prescriptions were used as a proxy for exposure to glucose‐lowering medications, which may not fully capture actual use or adherence. Third, other rare forms of diabetes, including drug‐induced diabetes, were not excluded and were most likely classified within the type 2 diabetes cohort. However, these subtypes represent only a small proportion of all diabetes cases and are therefore unlikely to have influenced the results. 34 Fourth, certain glucose‐lowering medications may be prescribed for non‐diabetes indications (e.g., SGLT2 inhibitors for heart failure or GLP‐1RA for weight loss). Therefore, we focused primarily on associations between severe hypoglycaemia risk and metformin, sulfonylurea, and insulin exposure. Fifth, we lacked data on hypoglycaemia awareness and the use of diabetes technologies such as CGM and insulin pumps. These factors may influence trends in severe hypoglycaemia risk. Sixth, limited treatment duration and the small number of hypoglycaemia events prevented analyses of GLP‐1RA and SGLT2 inhibitors and their risk of severe hypoglycaemia. Finally, although we observed a decline in the incidence of severe hypoglycaemia, we could not assess whether this occurred at the expense of glycaemic control.

CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS

Despite the persistently higher risk of severe hypoglycaemia in pancreatic diabetes compared to other diabetes subtypes, our findings demonstrate a clear decrease in incidence over time. This trend aligns with recent clinical trials reporting lower‐than‐expected time spent in hypoglycaemia based on CGM data, pointing to a broader, temporal decline in hypoglycaemia risk. 41 , 43 , 44 Such trends have significant implications for the design of future clinical trials and evolving treatment strategies. While the pathophysiology of pancreatic diabetes remains complex and requires careful consideration when glucose‐lowering medications are initiated or adjusted, the observed decline in hypoglycaemia risk may support a more individualised approach with tighter glycaemic targets in selected patients.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study offers valuable insights into the temporal trends of hypoglycaemia‐related hospitalisations in pancreatic diabetes. While pancreatic diabetes still carries a high risk of severe hypoglycaemia, the incidence has decreased substantially over the past two decades. Evolving treatment strategies, including the increased use of metformin and a shift from human insulin to safer insulin analogues, likely have influenced this decline.
