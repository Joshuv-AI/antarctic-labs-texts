The ginger-derived nanovesicles-coated albumin nanoparticles induce cell death and epigenetic regulation to treat colorectal cancer
Authors: Shuai Li, Wei Tian, Pengfei Li, Jingyuan Zhao, Yao Yao, Hong Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDue to the limitations of conventional cancer chemotherapy, including low bioavailability, limited indicators of therapeutic improvement, and unclear side effects, numerous laboratories have been actively engaged in the development of drug delivery systems. Here, we designed and synthesized a plant-derived ginger exosome-coated albumin nanoparticle drug delivery system (GEBSS) loaded with Shikonin (SHK) and STM2457 (a METTL3 inhibitor) and probes into the mechanism of antitumor.MethodsWe prepared and characterized GEBSS nanoparticles and evaluated their in vitro cellular uptake and targeting capabilities. The in vitro antitumor efficacy was assessed by measuring cell viability, clonogenic formation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, and apoptosis markers; biosafety was confirmed via a hemolysis assay. Furthermore, the ability of GEBSS to induce ICD was validated through Western blotting, ATP detection, and immunofluorescence assays, while its role in epigenetic regulation was elucidated using Dot Blot, MeRIP-qPCR, and RNA stability experiments. Finally, the in vivo antitumor effect of GEBSS was verified by intravenous administration in a nude mouse subcutaneous tumor model.ResultsA subsequent characterization revealed that GEBSS exhibited a concentrated size distribution around 142 nm, were efficiently absorbed by colorectal cancer (CRC) cells, and demonstrated inhibitory effects on tumor cell proliferation. In vivo experiments demonstrated excellent tumor-targeting ability, anti-tumor efficacy, and biocompatibility of GEBSS. Mechanistically, GEBSS induced apoptosis and immunogenic cell death (ICD) in tumor cells. Moreover, at the epigenetic regulation level, GEBSS suppressed cell proliferation by reducing the m6A methylation levels of immune checkpoint genes PD-L1 and CD47.ConclusionThis study explored the feasibility of producing naturally derived nanocarriers and, for the first time, employed a combination of SHK and STM2457 for CRC treatment, offering novel strategies and insights for nanomedicine in CRC treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04649-9.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Immunogenic cell death, Epigenetic, Ginger-derived exosomes, BSA

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer worldwide, with a global incidence of 10.6% and a mortality rate of 9.4%, ranking second among all diseases [1]. Since the mid-1990s, the incidence of colorectal cancer in adults under 55 years old has been increasing by approximately 1%-2% annually [2]. Chemotherapy is the most common treatment [3] but lacks specificity and has significant toxic side effects, underscoring the need for more effective, targeted therapies.

Shikonin (SHK), a red naphthoquinone compound abundant in the roots of Lithospermum erythrorhizon [4], exerts potent anti-tumor effects by inducing tumor cell apoptosis, immunogenic cell death (ICD), and other pathways [5–10]. ICD is a specific form of regulated cell death driven by stress stimuli. SHK can disrupt calcium homeostasis in cells, triggering endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS), which serves as a critical initial step in promoting ICD. Endoplasmic reticulum stress further induces the exposure and release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as the translocation of calreticulin (CRT) to the cell surface and the release of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) [11, 12]. Subsequently, pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on the surface of dendritic cells (DCs) recognize these DAMPs, promoting DC maturation and enhancing their antigen-presenting capacity. Activated DCs then present tumor antigens to CD8+ T cells, inducing their proliferation and differentiation into cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), which ultimately mediate specific anti-tumor immune responses.[13] However, the poor bioavailability and significant toxic side effects of SHK have severely limited its clinical application [14–16]. Furthermore, the SHK-induced immune-related antigens were limited [17], yet combining SHK with other anticancer drugs can enhance or even synergistically inhibit tumor growth [18–20]. Therefore, in this study, we chose to co-administer SHK with STM2457 to modulate the tumor microenvironment (TME) by altering m6A methylation levels, enhancing anti-tumor immunity, thus enhancing the anti-tumor activity of SHK.

As everyone knows, m6A modification is the most common internal chemical modification in eukaryotic mRNAs [21, 22] and can regulate mRNA stability, splicing, and translation [23–25]. Among these, m6A methyltransferase is a crucial enzyme regulating m6A methylation, with its core component methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) playing a significant oncogenic role in the development of CRC [26, 27]. For example, it promotes CRC progression through mechanisms dependent on m6A-IGF2BP2 [28], as well as by regulating the miR-1246/SPRED2/MAPK classical pathway to accelerate CRC invasion and metastasis [29]. In 2021, Yankova et al. [30] first reported STM2457, a METTL3 inhibitor with in vivo activity, and demonstrated that STM2457 modulates m6A level by inhibiting METTL3 enzyme activity, affecting the translation of METTL3 target genes, thereby significantly inhibiting acute myeloid leukemia progression. In CRC, STM2457 has been found to inhibit cancer development in CRC cells [31] However, as a small molecule drug targeting epigenetic regulation, its selectivity and safety profile require further improvement.

Albumin, as an endogenous nanocarrier, possesses excellent biocompatibility, non-antigenicity, stability, and biodegradability, making it a safe and non-toxic carrier. However, the targeting ability of albumin is relatively limited, we need to identify an alternative substance to enhance the targeting ability of the drug. Ginger-derived nanovesicles (GDNVs), which are a type of exosome isolated from ginger, share similarities with mammalian exosomes. As naturally derived entities, they exhibit low immunogenicity and high biocompatibility [32–34]. GDNVs possess bioactivity, targeting specificity, and regulatory functions, making them suitable as efficient drug delivery carriers [35] Compared to traditional synthetic carriers, GDNVs are more readily accepted by the body due to their natural origin, resulting in fewer side effects [36, 37]. As a drug delivery system, GDNVs demonstrate excellent delivery capabilities. They can cross biological barriers such as the blood–brain barrier, thereby enhancing drug delivery efficiency [33, 34]. For instance, GDNVs have been reported to improve the intraocular penetration of drugs for glaucoma treatment [38]. GDNVs also exhibit excellent targeting specificity and tissue affinity. Studies have shown that they possess inherent targeting abilities, enabling selective uptake by gut microbiota through specific lipids or amino acids, thus achieving precise delivery [39]. Concurrently, due to their organotropism, GDNVs can optimize drug enrichment in target tissues, such as tumors or the nervous system [37, 40]. Research has further reported on the anti-tumor potential of GDNVs. GDNVs loaded with 10-hydroxycamptothecin can enhance therapeutic efficacy while reducing systemic toxicity [41]. They can also improve the effectiveness of anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy in melanoma [39], promote intestinal repair, and prevent chronic colitis and colitis-associated carcinogenesis, demonstrating strong targeting capabilities. Moreover, GDNVs offer advantages such as high stability and yield. Up to 17.5 mg of exosomes can be extracted per kilogram of ginger, making them suitable for large-scale production [42]. Their bilayer membrane structure protects the encapsulated contents and extends the drug’s half-life [34]. Thus, we loaded SHK and STM2457 into albumin (Scheme 1), and then encapsulated them using GDNVs, furthermore, we have conducted a series of experiments to perform preliminary verification of the in vitro and in vivo effects as well as the mechanisms of action of GEBSS.

Materials and methods

Preparation of GDNVs

The GDNVs used in this study were all isolated from fresh ginger. The specific extraction steps are as follows: First, the ginger tissue was homogenized and centrifuged at 4 °C and 3000 × g for 30 min to remove tissue debris and large particulate impurities. The supernatant was carefully collected and subjected to another centrifugation at 4 °C and 10,000 × g for 60 min to further eliminate larger cell fragments and vesicles. Subsequently, an ultracentrifuge was used to pellet the exosomes by centrifugation at 4 °C and 100,000 × g for 120 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the exosome pellet was immediately washed with PBS to remove residual impurities and microvesicles. Finally, the purified exosome pellet was collected, resuspended in a small volume of PBS, and stored at − 80 °C for future use.

Preparation of BSS

30 mg bovine serum albumin (BSA) purchased from Solarbio was dissolved in 2 ml of water, and the pH was adjusted to 8.0–9.0 with 3 mol/L of NaOH solution (The total volume of the prepared solution was 10 mL). SHK (MedChemExpress) and STM2457 (MedChemExpress) were dissolved in 8 mL anhydrous ethanol. This solution was then added dropwise to the BSA solution at a rate of 0.2 ml/min (drug to BSA mass ratio of 1:15), followed by the addition of anhydrous ethanol (water to anhydrous ethanol volume ratio of 1:4) with continuous stirring (600–800 rpm). 3 mg N,N′-Bis(acryloyl)cystamine (BAC, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.) solution dissolved in anhydrous ethanol was added, maintaining a BSA to BAC mass ratio of 10:1. The mixture was stirred continuously at 50 °C for 24 h, ethanol was evaporated, and the product was purified by centrifugation to obtain BSS.

Preparation of GEBSS

To 10 mg of BSS, 2 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) was added and ultrasonically dispersed evenly. Then, 1 mL of GDNVs was added, mixed well, and sonicated on ice to disrupt the membranes (2 kHz, 10%, 3 min, 3 times, with 2 min intervals). After sonication, the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was washed three times with PBS to obtain GEBSS, which was then lyophilized for storage.

In the following text, BSS will be used to denote the drug-loaded nanoparticles without GDNVs coating, Blank NPs will refer to the blank nanoparticles coated with GDNVs but without drug loading, and GEBSS will represent the drug-loaded nanoparticles coated with GDNVs.

Characterization of GEBSS

The morphology of nanoparticles prepared in BSS and GEBSS solutions was examined using a Thermo transmission electron microscope. Following an established protocol, 10 µL of each sample was applied to an EM grid, stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid for 1 min, and air-dried. Imaging was performed at an accelerating voltage of 100 kV.

The particle size (expressed as hydrodynamic diameter), polydispersity index (PDI), and Zeta potential of the nanoparticles were assessed using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument via dynamic light scattering (DLS), with each sample appropriately diluted prior to measurement to ensure optimal scattering intensity.

Uptake experiment

HCT-116 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in a 24-well plate pre-coated with cell climbing slides. Subsequently, 300 μL of medium containing the fluorescently labeled drug at the desired concentration was added to each well, followed by incubation in the dark. At the desired time points, the fluorescence dye was aspirated, and the cells were washed three times with PBS solution. Then, 300 μL of 4% paraformaldehyde solution was added to each well and incubated for 15 min for fixation. After aspiration of the paraformaldehyde solution, the cells were washed with PBS solution. Hoechst 33342 nuclear staining solution (diluted 1:1000) was added to each well, followed by incubation in the dark for 15 min. After aspiration, the cells were washed with PBS solution. Finally, the cell climbing slides were taken out and mounted on glass slides with an appropriate amount of Fluoromount-G® Anti-Fade (Shenzhen Sinbobio). The slides were then observed under a confocal microscope (Leica).

RNA sequencing and differential expression analysis in cell lines

Sample Processing and Sequencing: Total RNA was extracted from GEBSS-treated and control cells using TRIzol reagent. RNA quality was assessed, and qualified samples were sent for library construction and sequencing by Azenta Life Sciences. Poly(A) mRNA was enriched, fragmented, and used to synthesize cDNA. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads.

Bioinformatic Processing: Raw sequencing reads were quality-trimmed and adapter-filtered using Cutadapt (v1.9.1). Cleaned reads were then aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 (hg38) using HISAT2 (v2.0.1). Gene-level read counts were quantified based on the alignment files using HTSeq (v0.6.1).

Differential Expression Calling: Differential expression analysis between treatment and control groups was performed using the DESeq2 R package (V1.26.0). Genes with an adjusted p-value (FDR/Padj ≤ 0.05) and an absolute log2 fold change (|log2FC|≥ 1) were defined as statistically significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs). This threshold corresponds to a twofold change in expression.

Analysis of public transcriptomic data from TCGA

Functional Enrichment Analysis: Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed on the identified DEGs using established methods (GOseq for GO, hypergeometric test for KEGG). Terms and pathways with an FDR ≤ 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Results

Characterization and uptake of GEBSS

We assessed the expression of exosome marker proteins CD9 and CD63 in BSS and GEBSS, which confirmed the successful encapsulation of GDNVs in GEBSS (Fig. 1A). TEM analysis further revealed the morphological features of both nanoparticles (Fig. 1B). Subsequently, the particle size of GEBSS was measured, and as shown in Fig. 1C, the nanoparticles exhibited a concentrated size distribution centered around 142 nm. The zeta potential of GEBSS was determined to be approximately − 30 mV, demonstrating that the nanoparticles are capable of maintaining stable dispersion in aqueous solution over prolonged periods. The polydispersity index (PDI) was found to be 0.187, indicating a relatively uniform size distribution. The encapsulation efficiency (EE) for the dual drugs (SHK and STM2457) in GEBSS reached 56%, with a drug loading (DL) capacity of 3.7%, validating the successful co-loading of both therapeutic agents into the nanoplatform.

Nanoparticles crosslinked with BAC contain disulfide bonds that respond to elevated levels of reduced glutathione (GSH) in tumor cells [44]. GSH can cleave these bonds, facilitating drug release at the tumor site. To simulate this, GEBSS was incubated in a 10 mM GSH solution for 1 h. Figure 1D shows significant disintegration under high GSH conditions.

Then, we evaluated the cellular uptake of BSS and GEBSS. Nanoparticles labeled with FITC were observed after 6 h of incubation. Figure 1E indicates that HCT-116 cells had higher uptake of GEBSS than BSS, with nanoparticles mainly localized in the cytoplasm, suggesting that GEBSS has greater therapeutic potential. Here, we hypothesize that the observed differences between BSS and GEBSS may be attributed to both the high targeting specificity and the ability to extend half-life conferred by GDNVs.

Subsequently, the drug loading capacity of the synthesized BSS and GEBSS nanoparticles was determined to quantify the concentrations of encapsulated SHK and STM2457. The UV–Vis scanning spectrum of the SHK standard in ethanol showed a maximum absorption peak at 516 nm (Fig. S1 A); therefore, 516 nm was selected as the detection wavelength for SHK. Linear regression analysis of the absorbance (A) versus concentration (C) of the SHK standard in ethanol yielded the regression equation: A = 0.020C – 0.011 (R2 = 0.9990), with the standard curve shown in Fig. S1 B. The concentration of STM2457 was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Fig. S1 C shows the peak position of the STM2457 standard, and Fig. S1 D presents the peak positions of other components excluding the nanoparticles. The nanoparticle samples were then analyzed, and the peak area corresponding to STM2457 was identified based on the retention time of the standard (Fig. S1 E), allowing calculation of the STM2457 concentration.

Induction of apoptosis and ICD by GEBSS

To determine whether GEBSS exhibits a superior toxic effect on tumor cells compared to SHK alone, STM2457 alone, the combination of SHK and STM2457, or BSS, we assessed the effects of different drug treatments on cell proliferation and viability using the CCK-8 assay. CCK-8 assay was applied after 24 h of treatment with SHK alone, STM2457 alone, a combination of SHK and STM2457, Blank NPs, BSS, and GEBSS (Fig. 2A), with STM2457 tested at concentrations of 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100, and 200 μM, and SHK at concentrations of 1.08, 2.17, 4.34, 8.68, 17.35, and 34.7 μM (BSS and GEBSS were confirmed to have equivalent drug loading concentrations through the above experiments, consistent with the corresponding concentrations of the control groups). Blank NPs did not affect cell viability, demonstrating excellent biocompatibility. The STM2457 group did not exhibit significant cytotoxicity, while both the BSS and GEBSS groups showed markedly higher cytotoxicity compared to the SHK and SHK + STM2457 groups, indicating a stronger antitumor capability.

Furthermore, a colony formation assay was performed to assess and compare the inhibitory effects of GEBSS and other formulations on the clonogenic survival of tumor cells. After 7 days of treatment with the respective drugs, the results (Fig. 2B) showed that the Blank NPs group did not inhibit cell proliferation compared to the control group. The SHK, SHK + STM2457, BSS, and GEBSS groups all significantly inhibited single-cell proliferation. Notably, unlike the results shown in Fig. 2A, the STM2457 group also demonstrated a significant inhibitory effect, suggesting that this small molecule inhibitor may require a longer duration to exert its effects.

The above results clearly demonstrated the cytotoxic effects of the GEBSS on tumor cells. However, it was necessary to further evaluate whether GEBSS could overcome the safety issues associated with SHK and STM2457. Therefore, a hemolysis assay was conducted to assess the safety of GEBSS. As shown in Fig. S2, only the highest concentration of GEBSS caused slight hemolysis, while no hemolysis was observed at other concentrations, confirming its good biosafety (The hemolysis rates at the tested concentrations were 3.83%, 1.68%, 1.40%, and 1.07%, respectively, with 0% for the DMEM group and 100% for the Triton group).

Mitochondrial membrane potential assays using the JC-1 probe indicated apoptosis in GEBSS-treated cells (Fig. 2C), with increased green fluorescence signaling a decrease in mitochondrial potential. Notably, SHK appears to be the primary component inducing apoptosis, STM2457 did not directly induce apoptosis. Additionally, DCFH-DA detected elevated ROS levels in GEBSS cells, also suggesting a link to apoptosis (Fig. 2D). Western blotting showed increased Bax and cleaved-Caspase3 and decreased Bcl-2 and c-Myc in the GEBSS group (Fig. 2E), indicating enhanced apoptosis in HCT-116 cells. Overall, GEBSS induces apoptosis and inhibits tumor cell proliferation in CRC.

We detected the expression levels of several cell immunity-related proteins using the Western blotting. NF-κB p65, a member of the NF-κB family, acts as a multifunctional protein transcription factor. Numerous studies have shown that NF-κB serves as a central hub in complex biological signal transduction networks, including regulating the transcription of relevant genes in response to immune stimuli. CD47 protein, a widely expressed cell receptor, aids in maintaining immune tolerance of benign cells under physiological conditions to prevent inappropriate phagocytosis of self-cells. Many types of cancer cells overexpress CD47 to mediate immune evasion by cancer cells [45], and CD47 commonly regarded as a checkpoint of the innate immune system. Programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1), expressed on the surface of tumor cells, interacts with programmed cell death receptor 1 (PD-1) on the surface of T cells. The binding of PD-L1 with PD-1 induces T cell apoptosis, dysfunction, exhaustion, resulting in the suppression of activation, proliferation, and anti-tumor function of tumor antigen-specific CD8+T cells, leading to tumor immune escape [46, 47]. PD-L1 generally functions as a checkpoint of the adaptive immune system. Following GEBSS treatment, we observed a significant downregulation of NF-κB p65, CD47, and PD-L1 protein expression levels (Fig. 2F), demonstrating that GEBSS not only induces tumor cell apoptosis, but also has the potential to modulate the tumor microenvironment, thereby enhancing antitumor immune responses. However, the therapeutic effect of only regulating the immune checkpoint is limited. If calreticulin (CRT) can be exposed to the surface of tumor cells by inducing the incidence of ICD, up-regulate the “eat me” signal, and improve phagocytosis with CD47, it can further enhance the immunomodulatory ability of immune-related proteins. Given the ICD-inducing role shown by SHK in other studies, we here examined the levels of ICD in tumor cells after GEBSS treatment. The characteristics of ICD in tumor cells include the translocation and exposure of CRT on the cell membrane surface, extracellular secretion of high mobility group protein 1 (HMGB1), extracellular release of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) molecules, and the expression of heat shock proteins (Hsp70, Hsp90) [48]. As shown in Fig. 2G, the surface expression of CRT on tumor cells in the GEBSS group was significantly increased. The intracellular ATP levels were assessed (Fig. 2H), revealing that extracellular ATP secretion was markedly enhanced in the GEBSS group, leading to a significant reduction in intracellular ATP levels. Furthermore, Western blotting of Hsp70 expression levels (Fig. 2I) demonstrated a significant upregulation of Hsp70 protein in the GEBSS group compared to the control group. These findings collectively indicate that GEBSS induces ICD in tumor cells.

GEBSS exerts anti-tumor effects through epigenetic regulatory mechanisms

Next, we performed transcriptome sequencing on cell samples treated with the GEBSS to validate the mechanism of action of GEBSS. As illustrated in Fig. 3A, following GEBSS treatment, a total of 796 genes were upregulated, while 342 genes were downregulated. Functional enrichment analysis of these differentially expressed genes revealed their predominant enrichment in metabolic pathways (Fig. 3B). In the Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed their predominant enrichment in macromolecule biosynthesis, RNA synthesis and metabolism (Fig. 3C), suggesting that GEBSS may exert its anti-tumor effects through the modulation of metabolism, gene transcription, and translational modifications.

Then we conducted a comparative analysis of METTL3 gene expression levels in CRC patients versus healthy individuals using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). The results showed that METTL3 is significantly overexpressed in CRC across different stages (Fig. S3), validating the rationale for selecting STM2457 as a therapeutic agent. As mentioned earlier, STM2457 can regulate the m6A modification levels by inhibiting METTL3. Therefore, we first examined the m6A modification levels of total RNA through Dot Blot assay. As illustrated in Fig. 3D, after 24 h of treatment, the m6A level of RNA in the GEBSS group was significantly reduced compared to the control group, this is consistent with the mechanism of action of STM2457. Next, we conducted meRIP-qPCR assay to detect the m6A modification levels of four genes, PD-L1, CD47, Bcl-2, and c-Myc which showed changes in expression levels after GEBSS treatment, to analyze the mechanism of GEBSS in epigenetic effects. As shown in Fig. 3E, compared to the control group, the m6A modification levels of PD-L1 and CD47 in the GEBSS group were significantly decreased, while there was no significant changes of Bcl-2 and c-Myc. This result may indicate that the decrease in Bcl-2 and c-Myc at the protein level is likely only related to the apoptotic effect induced by SHK. Furthermore, STM2457 was able to reduce the m6A modification levels of PD-L1 and CD47. m6A methylation is generally believed to impact RNA stability; therefore, we performed actinomycin D experiments to validate the changes in RNA stability induced by GEBSS. Actinomycin D is a polypeptide antibiotic produced by actinomycetes that intercalates with double-stranded DNA and binds to guanine bases, interfering with the transcription process and inhibiting mRNA synthesis. The results showed that the RNA half-life in the GEBSS group was significantly shortened compared to the control group (Fig. 3F), indicating that GEBSS treatment leads to an accelerated degradation rate of the corresponding mRNA. In summary, we propose that the epigenetic regulatory mechanism underlying the anti-tumor effect of GEBSS is achieved by reducing the m6A methylation modification levels of immune-regulatory-related genes (IRGs).

We also examined changes in METTL3 levels following GEBSS treatment and observed a decrease in METTL3 at the protein level compared to the control group (Figure S4). However, after reviewing the literature, we found no reports reasonably explaining a significant STM2457-induced reduction in METTL3 levels; on the contrary, some studies indicate that STM2457 may upregulate METTL3 protein expression [30, 49]. Therefore, the observed decrease in METTL3 protein levels may be attributable to an additional biological effect produced by the GEBSS nano-system within the context of CRC.

GEBSS can effectively target tumors and inhibit their growth

To investigate the tumor-targeting delivery efficacy of GEBSS in the in vivo environment, we used ICG dye to label GEBSS to observe the targeting effect of GEBSS at different time points (1 h, 5 h, and 24 h). As shown in Fig. 4A, GEBSS effectively distributed to the tumor at the 5-h and 24-h time points, which was confirmed by ex vivo imaging, indicating significant nanoparticle accumulation at the tumor site at both the 5-h and 24-h time points (Fig. 4B). These results demonstrate the pronounced tumor-targeting capability of GEBSS in vivo, along with a longer retention time in vivo.

To evaluate the anti-tumor effect of GEBSS in vivo, 9 HCT-116 tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into 3 groups (n = 3). Starting from day 7, they were intravenously injected with saline, SHK + STM2457, and GEBSS (10 mg/kg). After 4 consecutive treatments, the tumor volume growth in the SHK + STM2457 group and GEBSS group was significantly inhibited compared to the control group, with a more pronounced inhibitory effect observed in the GEBSS group (Fig. 4D). Figure 4C shows that during the treatment period, there were no significant changes in the body weight of the mice in each group, suggesting that SHK + STM2457 and GEBSS did not exhibit apparent toxic effects.

To further investigate the toxicity of the drugs on major organs, histopathological observations of the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys of the three groups were conducted using HE staining. As shown in Fig. 4F, no significant damage was observed in the organs of each group. In addition, serum biochemistry tests were performed to assess liver and kidney function by measuring levels of AST, ALT, ALB, UREA, CRE, and LDH. The results showed no significant differences in liver and kidney function among the three groups (Fig. S5). These results demonstrate the high biocompatibility of GEBSS in vivo, with almost no damage to normal tissue organs.

The detection of CRT and alterations in immune checkpoint molecules within the in vivo tumors (Fig. 4G) suggests a potential immunomodulatory role; however, further validation in immunocompetent models is required to confirm functional immune activation.

Discussion

In this study, we co-loaded SHK and STM2457 into BSA and encapsulated them with GDNVs to construct GEBSS. GEBSS had superior uptake in vitro compared to BSS. In a nude mice xenograft experiment, GEBSS demonstrated excellent tumor targeting and anti-tumor activity. GEBSS has the potential to effectively address issues related to the bioavailability, selectivity, and low safety of SHK and STM2457. In terms of mechanism of action, we confirmed that GEBSS exerts its anti-tumor effects by inducing apoptosis, triggering immunogenic cell death (ICD),. Also, GEBSS reduces the m6A methylation levels of PD-L1 and CD47 RNA, thus regulating epigenetics and modulating the tumor microenvironment (TME).

Grapefruit-derived exosome-like nanovesicles have been reported multiple times and can deliver chemotherapeutic drugs, microRNAs, and proteins for the treatment of diseases [50–54]. Encouraged by these excellent results, we also hoped to find a plant-derived carriers, and we successfully found GDNVs. Except for the safety, GEBSS shows a longer drug half-life. Longer circulation increases a carrier’s chance to penetrate tumor tissues via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, making prolonged accumulation of GEBSS in tumors vital for efficacy and specificity. And in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed GEBSS safety, reinforcing our confidence in its targeting and safety profile.

When tumors occur, immune cells and related stromal components recruited and activated by tumor cells can form an anti-tumor inflammatory microenvironment during the early stages of tumor settlement or growth, inhibiting the development of tumors. However, after persistent stimulation of tumor antigens and immune activation reactions, the effector cells in the microenvironment are in a state of exhaustion or reshaping, unable to perform normal functions or even promoting malignant characteristics of the tumor, leading to the creation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment [55]. Therefore, regulating TME to an active state is an effective strategy for treating cancer. There is a mutual relationship between m6A methylation and TME, where m6A modifications regulate tumor immunity by modulating the activity of immune cells in the tumor immune response, cytokine distribution, and immune checkpoints promotion or suppression of tumor development [55]. GEBSS significantly reduces RNA m6A methylation and protein levels of immune checkpoints CD47 and PD-L1 in HCT116 cells, leading to their rapid degradation and modulating the tumor microenvironment (TME). Additionally, GEBSS influences immunogenic cell death (ICD), stimulating the dysfunctional anti-tumor immune response. This indicates that GEBSS inhibits tumors by regulating m6A methylation and ICD, providing a targeted immunotherapeutic strategy to reshape a positive anti-tumor immune microenvironment.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not directly manipulate METTL3 or perform rescue experiments involving PD-L1/CD47 to establish causality. Consequently, the conclusion that GEBSS reduces m6A levels and the proposed mechanistic link between PD-L1 and CD47 are primarily inferred based on existing literature. Future studies will involve more comprehensive mechanistic investigations. Second, the nude mouse xenograft model used in this study is T‑cell deficient, which limits the direct assessment of adaptive immune responses induced by immunogenic cell death (ICD). Further validation in immunocompetent syngeneic mouse models representing distinct CMS subtypes is warranted. Third, the small sample size (n = 3 per group) in this in vivo study limits the generalizability of the findings; therefore, the results should be interpreted as preliminary observations that require confirmation in larger cohorts.

In summary, we successfully synthesized GEBSS, a tumor-targeting nanomedicine capable of effectively delivering the anticancer drugs SHK and STM2457. This innovation shifts the current paradigm of drug delivery from artificial synthesis and cell membrane coating to utilizing natural food-derived nanocarriers, providing new strategies and insights for targeted nanomedicine therapy for CRC.
