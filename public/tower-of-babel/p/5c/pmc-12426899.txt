Precision in Action: Using Intracardiac Echocardiography for Targeted Removal of a Large Lead‐Related Vegetation in a Patient With Infective Endocarditis
Authors: Muhammad Memon, Mohamad Sabra, Arfaat A. Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLead‐related infective endocarditis is a serious complication of implantable cardioverter‐defibrillators (ICDs), especially in patients with advanced heart failure who are poor surgical candidates. Management of large lead‐associated vegetations remains a clinical challenge.MethodsWe present the case of a 54‐year‐old woman with ischemic cardiomyopathy and recurrent ICD complications who developed bacteremia and infective endocarditis with vegetations on the aortic valve and a 1.5 × 1.3 cm mass on the right ventricular lead. Given her poor surgical candidacy due to worsening heart failure, a percutaneous approach was pursued.ResultsThe patient underwent successful intracardiac echocardiography (ICE)‐guided catheter‐based vegetation removal using a vacuum‐assisted aspiration system, followed by transvenous lead extraction. The procedure was well tolerated, and the patient demonstrated clinical improvement post intervention.ConclusionsThis case illustrates the utility of ICE‐guided percutaneous aspiration and lead extraction for managing large lead‐related vegetations in patients with infective endocarditis who are not candidates for surgery.

Background

Lead‐related infective endocarditis (IE) is a serious complication in patients with cardiovascular implantable electronic devices [1]. In particular, vegetations located on intracardiac device leads increase the risk of embolic events, particularly in right‐sided endocarditis. Larger vegetations, especially those over 1 cm, traditionally require surgical removal due to the elevated risk of embolization during transvenous lead extraction. However, advancements in catheter‐based techniques, such as the use of vacuum‐assisted debulking and intracardiac echocardiography (ICE)‐guided approaches, provide alternatives for patients who are ineligible for surgery.

Transvenous lead extraction is a procedure that patients with bacterial endocarditis and cardiovascular implantable electronic devices often require, and this clinical task requires precision and expertise due to its complexity and associated risks. Critically, bacterial infections are one of the most common and urgent reasons for lead extraction, and device‐related infections such as pocket infections, endocarditis, and sepsis require the complete removal of all hardware to eliminate the source of infection and prevent further complications. But beyond infection, several non‐infectious situations can also necessitate lead extraction, including lead malfunctions such as electrical failure, insulation breach, and conductor fractures, as well as redundant or nonfunctional leads.

Despite the clear indications for lead removal, the procedure has significant risks. Major complications, including cardiac tamponade, vascular injury, hemothorax, and pulmonary embolism, occur in 2%–4% of patients having a lead removed, even at institutions with high levels of experience in this procedure [2]. However, minor complications such as pocket hematomas and superficial infections are more common and less consequential. Also, incomplete removal of leads has been associated with markedly higher complication rates, and importantly, incomplete extraction has been linked to an increase inhospital mortality. In patients with prosthetic heart valves, the stakes are even higher, with an inhospital mortality rate of 9.8% for infected lead extraction. Despite these risks, complete lead removal is often curative and is strongly recommended when infection is confirmed, and performing timely interventions while adhering to established guidelines can significantly mitigate complications and improve patient outcomes.

Here we present the case of a 54‐year‐old woman with ischemic heart failure and inoperable lead‐related IE who underwent a successful percutaneous procedure for vegetation and lead removal. This case characterizes a hallmark example of how ICE‐guided catheter‐based vegetation removal and lead extraction is valuable for patients who cannot undergo surgical intervention and outlines the key elements of this complex process.

Patient Presentation

A 54‐year‐old woman with an implanted automatic cardioverter‐defibrillator and a medical history of heart failure, reduced ejection fraction of about 25%–30%, right bundle branch block, and complications requiring several cardiac device replacements, including left ventricular lead dislodgement with subsequent insertion of a new lead, was taken by a family member to the Emergency Department, where she was found to have leukocytosis and hypotension. The patient developed septic shock requiring pressor support. She was found to have bacteremia, with blood cultures positive for vancomycin‐resistant Enterococcus faecium and Streptococcus gallolyticus.

She was started on broad‐spectrum antibiotics prior to transfer to a tertiary care facility.

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) revealed vegetations on the aortic valve and a 1.5 × 1.3 cm vegetation on the right ventricular (RV) lead (Figure 1A, Video 1 and 2), severe aortic insufficiency.

Although the patient required immediate care, her worsening heart failure and existing comorbidities deemed her operable risk too high for full surgical intervention, which would likely have required at least three valve surgeries, lead extraction, and epicardial lead placement. Also, options for temporary mechanical circulatory support to facilitate surgery were limited and challenging. Therefore, 10 days after her initial presentation, she was transferred to a tertiary care center for transvenous lead extraction with ICE‐guided vacuum‐assisted debulking of the vegetations and electrophysiology intervention.

At the tertiary care center, the patient was prepared for complete extraction of all transvenous hardware and aspiration of the RV vegetation after obtaining informed consent from the patient with a family member present. A 9 Fr sheath for ICE was placed in the left femoral vein, and a 26 Fr sheath was placed in the right femoral vein under ultrasound guidance with sequential dilation. A 20 Fr AlphaVac system was placed and directed into the right atrium with ICE and fluoroscopic guidance. The aspiration cone was exposed from the outer sheath and positioned overlying the large RV lead vegetation. The vegetation was then manually aspirated off the RV lead, and removal was confirmed per ICE imaging (Figure 1B, Video 3). The lead and AlphaVac system were then removed, and ICE was used to confirm no pericardial effusion. A 5‐cm incision was made to access and remove the generator and all three leads—RA, RV, and LV—using manual traction after freeing the suture sleeves. The three leads included a Boston Scientific 7841 Ingevity + MRI lead in the right atrium, a Boston Scientific 4671 Acuity X4 Straight lead in the left ventricle, and a Boston Scientific 0673 Reliance 4‐Front S lead in the right ventricle. The leads were newly implanted on March 13, 2023, and were extracted on November 6, 2023, resulting in an implantation duration of 8 months. The patient was stable after the procedure, and at outpatient follow‐up 1 month later, she showed no signs of any post‐procedural complications.

Discussion

Lead‐related IE poses a significant risk for embolization, particularly when vegetations are large, and patients with IE within the context of a cardiac implant may have compromised heart function, making them ineligible for surgical intervention. Notably, vegetations greater than 10 mm carry a very high risk of embolism, with studies reporting pulmonary embolism in up to 55% of patients undergoing transvenous lead extraction for vegetations larger than 1 cm.

It is important to note that this cutoff is somewhat arbitrary and based on observational data with variable quality. The risk of embolization increases progressively with vegetation size, and there is no clear inflection point at which risk dramatically escalates. Thus, vegetations between 1 and 2 cm (1.5 cm in our case) are associated with increased risk, but not an absolute indication for surgery [3, 4].

The percutaneous aspiration device used was the AlphaVac system. The AlphaVac system is a vacuum‐assisted aspiration device designed for percutaneous removal of intravascular and intracardiac material, including thrombi and vegetations. The system consists of a large‐bore aspiration catheter, a vacuum pump, and a collection canister (Figure 2). The catheter is introduced percutaneously, typically via the femoral or internal jugular vein, and advanced under imaging guidance to the right atrium, right ventricle, or pulmonary arteries [5]. The maximal retrievable size is fundamentally limited by the internal diameter of the aspiration cannula, the consistency and friability of the vegetation, and the ability to safely maneuver the device within the right heart. Published case series and reviews of vacuum‐assisted aspiration systems report successful removal of vegetations up to 4 cm in maximal dimension, with most series describing vegetations in the 20–40 mm range as being amenable to percutaneous aspiration [6, 7].

The AlphaVac system is cleared by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) under the 510(k) pathway for the removal of thrombi or emboli. It is also CE marked in the European Union for similar indications. However, neither the FDA nor European regulatory authorities have approved the device specifically for the removal of vegetations due to infective endocarditis. The device's labeling remains focused on thrombus and embolus removal, and its use for vegetectomy in right‐sided infective endocarditis is considered off‐label [6, 7].

The Heart Rhythm Society strongly endorses the use of intraprocedural imaging during lead extraction. Intraprocedural imaging has become a cornerstone element of modern lead extraction, offering critical support for both procedural safety and success. Advanced imaging modalities such as TEE and ICE allow clinicians to navigate complex procedures with greater precision, helping them monitor for potential complications in real time. However, ICE offers significant advantages over TEE within the context of lead extraction procedures because of its operational benefits and advanced image quality of certain cardiac structures. Notably, ICE directly provides high‐resolution images of right‐sided cardiac structures, facilitating superior visualization during lead extraction. This capability is crucial for the accurate detection of vegetations and lead‐adherent echo densities. A previous study highlighted this advantage, showing that ICE had superior sensitivity (100%) for detecting IE compared to TEE, which had a sensitivity of about 73% [8]. Additionally, a prospective study involving 162 patients further highlighted the ability of ICE to identify intracardiac masses more effectively than TEE in all patients with definitive IE per the modified Duke criteria [9]. This difference is likely due to the ability of ICE to provide close‐up, high‐resolution imaging of crucial areas like the tricuspid valve and lead segments, whereas TEE often falls short because of acoustic shadowing and resolution limitations.

Furthermore, ICE supports continuous monitoring throughout the extraction process, enabling real‐time adjustments to manage complications such as pericardial effusion or thrombus formation, thereby enhancing safety. ICE is also less burdensome for patients, as it does not necessitate general anesthesia or endotracheal intubation, which are required for TEE. This feature is particularly advantageous in patients with a heightened risk for anesthesia‐related complications, simplifying procedural logistics and reducing overall risk. Also, ICE can more accurately identify lead binding sites and areas of fibrous adherence, which is invaluable for planning and executing complex lead extractions. As detailed in the 2017 Heart Rhythm Society's Expert Consensus Statement on Cardiovascular Implantable Electronic Device Lead Management and Extraction, the strategic integration of ICE into lead management protocols offers a significant improvement in both the efficacy and safety of these procedures [10].

It is important to note that there are several important risks that should be documented and discussed. Aside from access site complications, incomplete aspiration of the vegetation is a recognized risk, potentially resulting in residual infected material and persistent bacteremia. There is also a risk of fragmentation or embolization of the vegetation during aspiration, which may lead to septic pulmonary emboli or systemic embolic events, depending on the location and mobility of the vegetation.

The use of an ICE catheter introduces additional procedural risks. Complications from ICE catheter manipulation within the cardiac chambers include vascular injury, cardiac perforation, and pericardial effusion or tamponade. These complications, while infrequent, are well described in the literature and may require urgent intervention. The Heart Rhythm Society specifically notes pericardial effusion and tamponade as acute complications of intracardiac procedures, emphasizing the importance of careful catheter handling and real‐time imaging to minimize risk [11, 12, 13].

Overall, our patient's case corroborates the growing body of evidence supporting the use of ICE for guiding catheter‐based procedures in the management of lead‐related IE, providing a promising alternative for patients with cardiac devices who are not eligible for surgery after weighing the risks and benefits.

Conclusion

ICE‐guided catheter‐based vacuum‐assisted vegetation removal is a promising approach for removing lead‐related vegetations in patients with IE who are ineligible for surgery due to compromised heart function and comorbidities. The ICE approach allowed for precise targeting and safe extraction of a large vegetation on the RV lead and a successful lead extraction, thus lowering the risk of embolic complications in our patient. This approach is a viable alternative to surgical intervention, providing the advantages of advanced imaging and percutaneous techniques. Studies are needed to establish standardized protocols for ICE‐based vegetation extraction and lead removal in patients with device‐related IE.
