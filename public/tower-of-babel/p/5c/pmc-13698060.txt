Natural products in insomnia: Molecular mechanisms, action targets, and therapeutic potential
Authors: Haonan Gao, Zhenhua Liu, Xia Zhong, Shimeng Lv, Yitong Lu, Ruirui Shang, Guoqiang Li, Xiaowen Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Insomnia is a disorder characterized by sleep disturbances and daytime impairments. It can be alleviated with conventional clinical medications, but considerable individual variability and adverse effects (AEs) remain persistent challenges. Many natural products are known for their low toxicity and multi-target properties. These products offer distinct advantages in treating insomnia by modulating its underlying pathological mechanisms. Although previous reviews have summarized the effects of natural products on insomnia, systematic classification based on natural products and comprehensive exploration of their interactions with multidimensional pathological mechanisms remain insufficient. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the molecular mechanisms by which natural products modulate insomnia. It also offers an in-depth discussion of the current limitations and challenges. The goal is to establish a theoretical foundation and technical framework to optimize treatment strategies and advance the clinical translation of natural product research.

Introduction

Insomnia is characterized by sleep disturbances occurring at least three times per week for a duration of at least three months. These disturbances include difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, or early morning awakening. The symptoms are often accompanied by daytime impairments such as fatigue, concentration deficits, mood disturbances, or decreased performance [1]. Insomnia frequently co-occurs with neurological and psychiatric disorders. Among patients with Alzheimer's disease, epilepsy, or stroke, its prevalence ranges from 25% to 60% [2]. Prospective longitudinal studies have shown that individuals with insomnia are at an increased risk of acute myocardial infarction [3].

Hypnotic medications include three main classes: benzodiazepine (BZD) receptor agonists (BzRAs), tricyclic antidepressants, and melatonin receptor agonists. However, drug interactions and adverse effects (AEs) must be carefully considered. For instance, BzRA hypnotics can cause amnesia, falls, and rebound insomnia [4]. Therefore, the development of safer and more effective hypnotic agents has become an urgent focus of current research. Natural products, compounds or components derived from plants or other organisms, have shown remarkable progress in disease prevention and treatment. Compared with traditional synthetic small-molecule drugs, natural products offer several advantages, including abundant availability, low cost, easy accessibility, fewer adverse reactions, and notable therapeutic efficacy, making them an important source for new drug development [5].

Insomnia is closely associated with dysfunctions in five biological factors, including neuroplasticity, neural circuits, neuroendocrine signaling, neuroinflammation, and the microbiota-gut-brain (MGB) axis. However, systematic reviews examining the relationships between these biological factors and the pathophysiology of insomnia remain limited, particularly regarding the mechanisms by which natural products exert therapeutic effects. Therefore, this review systematically explored the roles of neuroplasticity, neural circuits, neuroendocrine regulation, neuroinflammation, and the MGB axis in the onset and progression of insomnia. It also summarized the pharmacological mechanisms through which natural products modulate these pathways to alleviate insomnia. The aim of this study was to provide a scientific basis for future basic research and clinical translation.

Review methodology

Following systematic review protocols and adhering to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) standards, relevant literature on the pathological mechanisms of insomnia, natural product therapies, toxicology, and side effects was collected for comprehensive analysis. All data were sourced from three major databases, Web of Science, PubMed, and ScienceDirect, with literature published up to September 2025. The search terms included “insomnia”, “neuroplasticity”, “neural circuits”, “neuroendocrine”, “neuroinflammation”, “microbiota-gut-brain axis”, “natural products”, “hypnotics”, and “toxicity”. Inclusion criteria were: i) original articles written in English and ii) studies investigating the mechanisms of natural product treatments for insomnia in insomnia models. Exclusion criteria were: i) non-English language articles; ii) grey literature, conference abstracts, or case reports; iii) duplicate entries; and iv) studies in which interventions did not involve natural materials, such as synthetic drugs or chemical compounds. All screened literature was independently reviewed by two researchers, focusing on key experimental designs, findings, and mechanistic insights.

Pathological mechanisms of insomnia

Neuroplasticity abnormalities

Monoamine neurotransmitters

Imbalances in neurotransmitters, such as 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), norepinephrine (NE), and dopamine (DA), within the central nervous system (CNS) can contribute to the development of insomnia [6]. Electrophysiological, neurochemical, genetic, and neuropharmacological studies suggest that 5-HT primarily promotes wakefulness and inhibits rapid eye movement (REM) sleep [7]. Additionally, research shows that 5-HT deficiency alters neuronal firing patterns in insomniac rats. After sleep deprivation, the amplitude of spikes in the dorsal raphe nucleus (DRN) and hippocampus decreases, repolarization slopes are reduced, and firing frequency increases [8].

DA and NE, both metabolites of tyrosine (Tyr), play critical roles in the sleep-wake cycle, as well as in learning, memory, and emotion. DA is essential for the transition from non-REM (NREM) to REM sleep. It acts through D2 DA receptors, transiently increasing activity in the basolateral amygdala to trigger the NREM-to-REM shift [9]. NE, a key neurotransmitter in arousal, is produced by brainstem noradrenergic nuclei A1 and A2, which project to hypothalamic regions involved in sleep regulation. NE also contributes to the synthesis of melatonin, thereby influencing both circadian rhythms and homeostatic sleep processes [10].

The monoamine neurotransmitters (5-HT, NE, and DA) collectively maintain the delicate equilibrium of the sleep-wake cycle through their complex and synergistic actions. Dysfunction in the levels, metabolism, or receptor activity of any one of these neurotransmitters may disrupt this dynamic balance, serving as the neurochemical basis for inducing or sustaining insomnia. The dysregulation of these wake-promoting monoamine neurotransmitters underscores the necessity for a counterbalancing inhibitory force within the CNS to initiate and maintain sleep, which is primarily mediated by the gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) system.

GABA

The GABAergic system is the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter network in the CNS. GABA acts through GABAA and GABAB receptors to balance excitatory neurotransmitters, such as glutamate (Glu), NE, 5-HT, acetylcholine, orexin, and DA, which are elevated during wakefulness. Additionally, it reduces oxidative stress by inhibiting reactive oxygen species (ROS), scavenging free radicals, and lowering malondialdehyde (MDA) levels. GABA also interacts with circadian-regulated melatonin to help improve insomnia [11].

GABAergic neurons are primarily located in the basal forebrain (BFB) and anterior hypothalamus. Neuroimaging studies indicate that GABA levels are reduced in key cortical regions of insomnia patients [12]. And benzodiazepines (BZD) and barbiturates exert their effects by acting on GABA receptors. Since the 1970s, the widespread use of BZD for insomnia has highlighted the critical role of GABAergic modulation in promoting sleep [13]. Compared to the normal control group, individuals in the insomnia disorder (ID) group exhibit significantly reduced messenger RNA (mRNA) expression of the GABAA receptor α1 and α2 subunits. Since GABAA receptor activation is essential for sleep onset, this reduction may disrupt sleep initiation and maintenance, contributing to insomnia. These findings suggest that the inhibitory effects of GABA in the peripheral system are impaired in insomnia patients, likely due to abnormal receptor subunit expression [14].

The GABAergic system, through its extensive inhibitory regulation, serves as the core neurochemical foundation for maintaining sleep homeostasis. Its functional impairment in insomnia patients, particularly reduced GABA levels and abnormal receptor subunit expression, directly leads to an imbalance between central excitation and inhibition. This further establishes that enhancing GABAergic inhibition is one of the clinically effective treatment strategies for insomnia. The balance between these inhibitory and excitatory neurotransmitters sets the foundational tone for neuronal excitability, which is directly challenged by the primary excitatory neurotransmitter, Glu.

Glu

In the CNS and several peripheral organs, Glu is the primary excitatory neurotransmitter. It mediates fast excitatory neurotransmission through ionotropic Glu receptors (iGluRs) and modulates neuromodulation via metabotropic Glu receptors (mGluRs). Glu regulates both spontaneous and rhythmic neuronal activity and plays a critical role in sleep-wake regulation. Activation of glutamatergic neurons promotes wakefulness, which can disrupt sleep continuity [15].

The N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR), a subtype of Glu receptor, plays a critical role in sleep maintenance. NMDAR activation in the lateral preoptic hypothalamus helps stabilize neuronal sleep discharges [16]. In ID patients, abnormal glutamatergic neurotransmission, including upregulation of NMDAR NR1 subunits, suggests a direct link between insomnia and altered Glu receptor expression [17]. Numerous proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H-MRS) studies show that Glu is involved in the neurobiology of primary insomnia [18]. The glutamatergic system plays a pivotal role in the sleep-wake cycle. Disruptions in Glu homeostasis, particularly altered mGlu receptor activity, contribute to various CNS disorders. The expression and function of mGlu receptors fluctuate across the circadian sleep-wake cycle [19].

High Glu levels are observed during both wakefulness and sleep deprivation [20]. Activation of glutamatergic neurons in the ventral tegmental area (VTA), which projects to the nucleus accumbens (NAc) and lateral hypothalamus, prolongs wakefulness. In contrast, inhibiting these neurons shortens wakefulness and increases NREM-sleep transitions. These findings highlight the role of overlapping neural circuits and neurotransmitter systems, with glutamatergic signaling as a central component, in governing sleep-wake transitions and associated oscillatory functions [21]. Glutamatergic neurons in the posteroventral medial amygdala (MePVGlu) mediate arousal behaviors. These neurons respond to threat signals during sleep, and excessive excitation may contribute to insomnia [22].

As an excitatory neurotransmitter, excessive activity within the glutamatergic system serves as a key molecular basis driving insomnia. Abnormal signaling within specific neural circuits directly disrupts the transition and maintenance balance between sleep and wakefulness. Therefore, regulating the hyperactivity of the glutamatergic system emerges as a critical potential target for intervening in insomnia. The dynamic interplay between Glu-mediated excitation and GABA-mediated inhibition ultimately manifests at the synaptic level, influencing the strength and adaptability of neural connections, a process central to synaptic plasticity.

Synaptic plasticity

Extensive research demonstrates that sleep is essential for cognition, learning, and memory, with each sleep stage exhibiting distinct electrophysiological and neurochemical characteristics. Neurotransmitters released from presynaptic axons bind to postsynaptic receptors, and the strength of these synapses can be modulated by various physiological and pathological factors, a phenomenon known as synaptic plasticity [23].

Molecular and structural changes associated with synaptic scaling also occur during sleep [24]. The insomnia-related gene fragile X autosomal homolog 1 (Fxr1) encodes a protein involved in synaptic scaling and synchronized electroencephalogram (EEG) responses to heightened sleep demand, thereby regulating synaptic strength [25]. And the synaptic homeostasis hypothesis (SHY) posits that wakefulness is associated with a net increase in synaptic strength across cortical circuits, and that sleep serves to renormalize this strength to a sustainable baseline through slow wave activity (SWA)-mediated synaptic downscaling [26]. Beyond SWA, episodic sleep events, such as hippocampal sharp waves and thalamocortical sleep spindles, contribute to sleep-dependent synaptic plasticity by inducing local protein synthesis bursts, consolidating synaptic modifications, and supporting memory formation [27].

Synaptic plasticity is a fundamental mechanism for brain adaptation and functional maintenance, and plays a pivotal role in the pathophysiology of insomnia. The sleep-wake cycle maintains neural network equilibrium by dynamically regulating synaptic strength. Targeting synaptic plasticity regulation may not only alleviate insomnia symptoms but also serve as a therapeutic strategy to restore neural network function. The homeostatic regulation of synaptic strength is not only a cellular process but is also profoundly influenced by the body's systemic stress and arousal signals, primarily governed by the neuroendocrine system.

Neuroendocrine disorder

Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis

The HPA axis is a key component of the neuroendocrine system, crucial for sleep regulation, homeostasis, and stress responses. Dysfunction of the HPA axis can lead to disruptions in sleep-wake mechanisms and contribute to various neurological disorders [28].

The HPA axis primarily operates through corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), and corticosterone (CORT). Activation begins with the hypothalamic release of CRH, which stimulates the pituitary to secrete ACTH, triggering the adrenal cortex to release cortisol. Overproduction of these hormones promotes wakefulness and reduces NREM sleep [29]. Cortisol follows a circadian rhythm, peaking in the morning to promote wakefulness and reaching its lowest point at night to facilitate sleep. Disruption of this rhythm decreases slow-wave sleep (SWS) and increases wakefulness [30].

Insomnia can activate the HPA axis through stress-related pathways, and hyperactivity of this system contributes to both the onset and persistence of insomnia. CRH, as an immediate effector, has been implicated in insomnia, with evidence linking HPA hyperactivity to elevated CRH secretion in these patients [31]. Cortisol, the final output of the HPA axis, is critical in stress regulation. Activation of the HPA axis triggers cortisol release, which disrupts circadian rhythms and increases blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability, allowing inflammatory mediators to enter the CNS, exacerbating neuroinflammation, and further impairing sleep regulation [32].

The suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus regulates circadian rhythms and stress responses. Several studies suggest that insomnia patients have elevated cortisol levels compared to healthy controls [33]. In a cohort of 58 healthy individuals, reduced total sleep duration was associated with lower cortisol levels upon awakening [34]. Conversely, inadequate sleep correlates with elevated morning cortisol levels, while sleep disruption may also reduce morning cortisol levels [35]. Intervention studies indicate that casein hydrolysate or casein supplementation can alleviate neuroendocrine dysfunction by modulating protein expression within the HPA axis, improving insomnia symptoms [36].

In summary, the HPA axis coordinates stress responses and sleep-wake regulation via CRH, ACTH, and CORT. Therapeutic strategies targeting the HPA axis can improve insomnia by modulating these hormones, making the HPA axis a promising intervention point. However, current research has limitations: HPA activity patterns vary across insomnia populations, and the molecular pathways underlying HPA regulation remain incompletely understood. Additionally, most natural products targeting the HPA axis lack systematic mechanistic validation. Future studies integrating multi-omics and neuroendocrine approaches are needed to elucidate the dynamic changes in HPA activity and hormonal regulation in insomnia, paving the way for targeted interventions. Beyond the systemic hormonal modulation of the HPA axis, sleep-wake transitions are also directly and rapidly governed by specific hypothalamic neuropeptides. Among these, orexin (hypocretin), a potent arousal-promoting signal, plays a pivotal role in stabilizing wakefulness and is intricately linked to the energy-sensing functions of the hypothalamus.

Orexin

Hypocretins, also known as orexins, are hypothalamic neuropeptides that regulate sleep-wake behavior through neuropeptidergic signaling. Orexin is unique in that it is synthesized by a restricted population of neurons in the posterior lateral hypothalamus. These neurons activate monoaminergic and cholinergic populations in the hypothalamus and brainstem, sustaining prolonged wakefulness. Orexin neurons respond to metabolic cues from feeding-related hypothalamic nuclei and adapt arousal levels accordingly, thereby coordinating sleep-wake cycles with energy demands [37]. Hypocretin (Hcrt) and its two peptide forms, hypocretin-1 (Hcrt-1) and hypocretin-2 (Hcrt-2), are expressed in the lateral hypothalamus. Hcrt expression follows a circadian rhythm, peaking during periods of wakefulness, and is closely associated with active wake behaviors [38].

Initially thought to primarily regulate feeding and metabolism, orexin is now recognized as a principal modulator of wakefulness and attention [39]. According to the “trigger or switch” model proposed by Saper et al. [40], loss of orexin-producing neurons in the lateral hypothalamus disrupts the balance between sleep- and arousal-promoting nuclei. This imbalance results in intrusions of active sleep into wakefulness and frequent awakenings during sleep [40].

Optogenetic activation of Hcrt neurons during sleep induces transitions to wakefulness and inhibits the rapid progression from wakefulness to REM sleep [41]. Hyperactivity of Hcrt neurons is thought to contribute to insomnia. For example, downregulation of miR-137 in Hcrt neurons increases Hcrt mRNA levels, enhancing arousal at the onset of the dark phase and prolonging wakefulness. This suggests that the interaction between miR-137 and Hcrt plays a key role in regulating sleep-wake cycles [42].

The function of orexin neurons is influenced by both sleep- and wake-promoting neurons. GABAergic neurons in the ventrolateral preoptic area (POA) provide inhibitory input, while cholinergic neurons in the BFB provide excitatory input [43,44]. These findings support the role of orexin in coordinating sleep-wake transitions. Fiber photometry studies in mice further demonstrate that orexin neuron activity directly influences susceptibility to wakefulness and modulates wake-promoting neurons in the paraventricular hypothalamus [45].

Overall, orexin integrates signals related to arousal, motivation, and energy balance through widespread projections in the brain. Activation of orexin neurons rapidly promotes transitions from sleep to wakefulness, and hyperactivity of this system is implicated as a key mechanism underlying insomnia. With its circadian-dependent expression, which peaks during active periods, orexin directly sustains wakefulness. Dysregulation or hyperfunction of the orexin system represents a promising therapeutic target for insomnia treatment. The peptides and hormones released by the neuroendocrine system, such as cortisol and orexin, serve as powerful neuromodulators that directly act upon and regulate the activity of specific neural circuits involved in sleep-wake transitions.

Neural circuit alterations

Brain function relies on the transmission and integration of information within complex neural circuits that span various regions. The governance of neural circuit assembly, regulation, and dynamic balance by synaptic molecules and neuronal activity is fundamental to brain states and behaviors [46]. Specifically, the dysfunction or destabilization of neural circuits governing sleep-wake regulation is a core pathological mechanism underlying insomnia.

The paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of the hypothalamus, located adjacent to the third ventricle, contains a variety of neuropeptide-producing neurons, including large-cell and small-cell neurosecretory neurons, as well as central projection neurons [47]. The PVN plays a key role in autonomic regulation, cardiovascular control, circadian rhythm maintenance, energy metabolism, and feeding behavior [48]. The ventromedial hypothalamus, lateral septum, POA, and paraventricular thalamic nucleus receive reciprocal axonal projections from PVN neurons, all contributing to sleep-wake regulation [49]. Through these extensive reciprocal connections with key arousal and sleep regulating nuclei, the PVN emerges as a critical hypothalamic hub capable of modulating wakefulness.

The paraventricular thalamus (PVT) is a central node within the thalamus that regulates multiple functions, including arousal, stress, emotional memory, and motivation. Numerous studies emphasize the PVT's significance in promoting arousal. Under normal conditions, activation of the PVT-NAc pathway induces arousal, whereas inhibition of the PVT-bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST) pathway attenuates hunger-induced arousal [50]. Notably, the PVT extensively innervates the amygdala, a region involved in sleep-wake transitions and stress responses [51]. Therefore, the PVT-amygdala pathway plays a critical role in adaptive behaviors linked to stress, particularly in regulating heightened arousal induced by acute stress. Consequently, the sustained or maladaptive hyperactivity of these PVT-mediated pathways, particularly under chronic stress, is hypothesized to contribute to the hyperarousal state that characterizes insomnia, thereby disrupting sleep initiation and maintenance.

The activity of PVT neurons projecting to the central amygdala (CeA) varies throughout the sleep-wake cycle, peaking during periods of alertness. Furthermore, activation of dopaminergic neurons in the VTA projecting to the CeA also promotes wakefulness, further highlighting the role of the PVT-CeA circuit in arousal regulation [52]. Thus, the PVT-CeA circuit exemplifies a key neural substrate where aberrant circadian or stress-induced activity can directly promote wakefulness and impede sleep, positioning it as a critical intervention target within the neural circuitry of insomnia.

Among the downstream targets influenced by these arousal-promoting hubs, the ventral pallidum (VP) serves as a critical integrator. The VP integrates signals related to motivation, reward, and stress, and plays a key role in regulating wakefulness [53]. The integrated arousal signals from forebrain structures like the VP and PVT ultimately converge upon and modulate the activity of fundamental subcortical arousal systems, most notably the ascending reticular activating system (ARAS). The ARAS, which includes the reticular formation and its associated connections, is a critical network for arousal and sleep-wake transitions. Neuronal discharge rates in the ARAS decrease during sleep and increase during wakefulness. Arousal pathways ascend through the parabrachial area of the midbrain, with projections branching into dorsal and ventral pathways: the dorsal pathway projects to the thalamus, while the ventral pathway innervates the hypothalamus, brainstem, and cortex [54]. Additionally, corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF) neurons in the medial thalamic nuclei regulate arousal during REM sleep by rapidly integrating sensory inputs, such as olfactory and visual stimuli [55].

In summary, neural circuits are essential for information transfer and the regulation of brain function, and disruptions in these circuits play a key role in insomnia. The PVN and PVT are central nodes in the regulation of arousal, emotion, and stress. PVT neuronal activity closely tracks wakefulness, modulating the function of multiple brain regions through various projection pathways. Dysregulation or aberrant activation of these circuits can disrupt sleep-wake transitions and contribute to insomnia. However, most mechanistic studies remain confined to animal models, limiting their relevance to human sleep disorders. The complex interactions between brain regions and the molecular mechanisms underlying these circuits are still poorly understood. Future research integrating multi-scale approaches will be essential to unravel the dynamic encoding of sleep-wake regulatory networks and their dysregulation in insomnia. Chronic overactivation of these arousal-promoting neural circuits can induce a state of cellular stress within the brain, potentially triggering and sustaining neuroinflammatory responses.

Neuroinflammation

Neuroinflammation refers to the activation of immune cells in the CNS, including microglia and astrocytes, in response to injury, infection, or chronic stress. This activation, often accompanied by the infiltration of peripheral immune cells, can lead to neuronal damage and is implicated in various neurological disorders, including insomnia, Alzheimer's disease, and Parkinson's disease [56]. Critically, the pro-inflammatory cytokines and other mediators released during this process can directly disrupt the function of sleep-wake regulating nuclei, thereby contributing to the initiation and maintenance of insomnia.

Sleep plays a crucial role in maintaining immune homeostasis by modulating inflammatory mediators such as cytokines, which in turn regulate the sleep-wake cycle [57]. Evidence suggests that insomnia is associated with chronic infections and inflammatory conditions, with alterations in sleep-wake cycles and circadian rhythms being intricately connected to neuroinflammation [58]. The transition of microglia from a resting (M0) state to either an anti-inflammatory (M2) or pro-inflammatory (M1) state plays a key role in modulating their molecular clocks and influencing the severity of inflammation [59].

Microglia are central to neuroinflammation and are regulated by circadian rhythms. In studies involving lipopolysaccharide-induced human microglial clone 3 (HMC3) and primary rat microglia cells, the use of short hairpin RNA targeting microRNA-29a (sh-miR-29a) promoted expression of period circadian regulator 2 (PER2) and CD206, while inhibiting the expression of pro-inflammatory markers such as ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (IBA-1), nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), and CD86. This effect was blocked by small interfering (si)-PER2. These findings suggest that the sh-miR-29a-NP axis promotes M2 polarization of microglia via the PER2/NF-κB pathway, thereby inhibiting neuronal apoptosis and alleviating chronic insomnia [60].

Fatty acid-binding protein 7 (Fabp7), expressed by stellate glial cells, is associated with sleep-wake regulation and behaviors linked to fear and anxiety. Fabp7 follows a circadian expression pattern, regulated by the core clock genes brain and muscle arnt-like 1 (BMAL1) and the repressor nuclear receptor subfamily 1, group D, member 1 (REV-ERBα) [61]. Studies in full-length, human, wild-type alpha-synuclein under the Thy-1 promoter (Thy1-αSyn) mice have shown early neuroinflammatory changes, microglial activation and astrocytic proliferation, in key sleep-regulating brain regions [62]. Microglial activation leads to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and neurotoxic factors, causing neuronal damage [63], whereas reactive astrocytic proliferation impairs neuronal support, Glu uptake, and BBB integrity [64], all of which contribute to sleep disturbances.

Myeloid differentiation primary response gene 88 (MyD88) plays a crucial role in Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling, activating the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and IL-1β, which influence sleep-wake timing [65]. In healthy brains, microglia lacking MyD88 may exhibit abnormal synaptic pruning, where unused synapses undergo apoptosis, potentially promoting microglial phagocytosis to maintain synaptic integrity [66]. Circadian genes such as circadian locomotor output cycles kaput (CLOCK) and BMAL1 are key regulators of pro-inflammatory activity in microglia, and their disruption can impair the circadian regulation of synaptic pruning and apoptosis, potentially contributing to insomnia [67].

Exposure to low-pressure hypoxia has also been shown to disrupt sleep homeostasis, reducing the duration of SWS in rats, with peak disruptions occurring on day 3. Biochemical analyses revealed decreased levels of antioxidant enzymes and increased proi-nflammatory cytokines. Serum biomarkers indicated hypoxia-induced oxidative stress, characterized by reduced superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione levels, alongside elevated MDA. This shift activated the HPA axis, increasing secretion of CRH, ACTH, and CORT, and elevating pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β and IL-6, ultimately exacerbating insomnia [32].

In summary, neuroinflammation is a critical CNS immune response involving the activation of microglia, astrocytes, and the infiltration of peripheral immune cells. These processes release pro-inflammatory mediators that induce neuronal damage, disrupt synaptic function, and impair BBB integrity, all of which contribute to insomnia. The mechanisms underscore the neuroimmunological basis of insomnia and provide a foundation for developing therapeutic strategies targeting glial polarization and inflammatory pathways. However, several key questions remain: the specific mechanisms underlying neuroinflammation across different insomnia subtypes are not fully understood; and the translational pathway from animal models to clinical applications remains unclear. Moreover, targeted interventions addressing neuroinflammatory pathways are still in early stages. Future research should aim to validate these mechanisms in human studies and explore the potential for precision therapies targeting the neuroinflammation-sleep axis (Fig. 1). MGB axis disorder

The MGB axis connects the enteric nervous system (ENS) and the CNS via the vagus nerve, serving as a crucial communication pathway through which gut microbiota (GM) influences brain function. Microbial metabolites affect numerous physiological processes and behaviors, contributing to neuropsychiatric disorders such as mild cognitive impairment, depression, and Alzheimer's disease [68]. The GM modulates the MGB axis through endocrine, neural, and immune signaling pathways.

The GM plays a critical role in the development of the ENS and regulation of intestinal motility. Sleep deprivation, restriction, and fragmentation have been shown to alter gut microbial composition. For example, fasting-induced microbiota depletion reduces θ wave power density during REM sleep in mice [69]. Mendelian randomization analyses have identified bacterial taxa associated with insomnia, revealing a bidirectional causal relationship between the genus Odoribacter and sleep disturbances [70].

Other GM metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and bioactive derivatives of tryptophan, play important roles in sleep regulation. Tryptophan, a precursor of L-5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP), is metabolized into 5-HT, which is then converted into melatonin, a potent sleep-promoting molecule [71]. SCFAs interact with gut-brain pathways to modulate neural function, learning, memory, and mood. In infants, higher fecal propionate levels are associated with longer uninterrupted sleep, while in older adults with insomnia, elevated levels of acetate, butyrate, and propionate correlate with poorer sleep quality [72,73].

Experimental evidence underscores the importance of the GM and its metabolite butyrate in maintaining sleep-wake balance. Depletion of GM reduces nighttime sleep duration, while transplanting GM from individuals with insomnia into germ-free (GF) mice induces sleep disturbances similar to human insomnia [74]. In fecal microbiota transplantation-insomnia models, the gut microbiome can hyperactivate orexin neurons. Recent studies suggest that the GM modulates hypothalamic gene expression, indicating a causal role in sleep disorders by disrupting hypothalamic neuronal homeostasis via altered butyrate metabolism [75]. These findings highlight the essential role of the GM in sleep regulation and its contribution to sleep disorders.

And elevated levels of IL-6 are frequently observed in patients with primary insomnia. Transplantation of microbiota from sleep-deprived humans into GF mice results in increased systemic IL-6. Dysregulation of neurotransmitters like 5-HT and DA may also influence the GM, creating a bidirectional relationship between the microbiome and sleep regulation [76]. Antibiotic-induced GM depletion in mice increases NREM sleep during the dark period and decreases it during the light period [69].

The GM establishes a bidirectional communication network with the CNS via the MGB axis, playing a pivotal role in the pathophysiology of insomnia. Both insomnia patients and sleep-deprived animal models have shown significant alterations in microbial composition and diversity. Moreover, vagus nerve-mediated gut-brain signaling, gut-derived peptides, and antibiotic-induced microbiota depletion can profoundly disrupt NREM sleep architecture. Given the multifactorial pathophysiology of insomnia, involving disrupted neuroplasticity, neuroendocrine dysregulation, altered neural circuitry, neuroinflammation, and MGB axis imbalance (Fig. 3), there is increasing interest in therapeutic strategies that can synergistically regulate multiple pathways. In this context, natural products show unique advantages due to their multi-component and multi-target characteristics. Molecular mechanisms of natural products

Terpenoids

Terpenoids, the largest class of plant-derived natural products, exhibit diverse chemical structures and extensive biological activities [77]. These compounds hold significant promise in drug discovery and are essential for plant growth, development, and adaptation to environmental stressors [78]. Loganin, a major cycloartenol glycoside isolated from Cornus officinalis Siebold & Zucc., demonstrates neuroprotective, immunomodulatory, and lipid metabolism-regulating properties [79]. Loganin increases c-Fos expression in GABAergic neurons of the rat ventrolateral POA and effectively alleviates insomnia induced by p-chlorophenylalanine (PCPA). Its hypnotic effects are enhanced when combined with 5-HTP, primarily by modulating the serotonergic and GABAergic systems [80].

Jujuboside A (JB-A), a triterpene saponin and the principal bioactive compound of Semen Ziziphus Spinosae (SZS), mediates the sedative and hypnotic effects of SZS [81]. JB-A possesses antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties. Mechanistically, JB-A alleviates insomnia by enhancing mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, regulating the mitochondrial permeability transition pore to maintain mitochondrial homeostasis, and mitigating structural neuronal damage [82]. Zerumbone (ZER), a sesquiterpene isolated from Syringa pinnatifolia Hemsl., exhibits anti-myocardial ischemia and sedative effects [83]. ZER significantly modulates sleep latency, duration, and spontaneous activity in mice, likely through the GABAergic system. It increases GABA and Glu levels in the hypothalamus, upregulates GAD67 and gephyrin expression, and enhances GABAA receptor channel opening frequency and chloride ion permeability in neuronal membranes, suggesting its sedative effects are mediated via GABAergic modulation [84].

Geraniol, an acyclic monoterpene abundant in the essential oils of lemongrass, rose, and lavender, has anti-inflammatory and anti-traumatic properties [85]. Geraniol prolongs the general anesthesia induced by propofol, pentobarbital, and isoflurane in mice by enhancing θ-wave activity, which is associated with NREM sleep. This effect is mediated specifically within the wakefulness-related PVT. Through GABAA receptors, geraniol induces membrane hyperpolarization and suppresses PVT neuron activity via prolonged inhibitory postsynaptic currents and enhanced tonic inhibition, thereby producing sedative, anticonvulsant, and hypnotic effects [86]. β-myrcene, a terpene found in Myrtus communis essential oil, demonstrates antibacterial properties both in vitro and in vivo [87]. It significantly reduces locomotor activity, shortens sleep latency, and prolongs sleep duration in conjunction with barbiturates. These behavioral improvements are associated with increased levels of GABA and 5-HT, decreased Glu in the hypothalamus, and the upregulation of serotonergic and GABAergic signaling components. Collectively, β-myrcene alleviates insomnia through modulating neurotransmitter balance and related neural pathways [88].

In summary, diverse terpenoids demonstrate significant sedative-hypnotic potential primarily through the potentiation of GABAergic signaling. Their ability to concurrently modulate complementary pathways, such as serotonergic function and mitochondrial homeostasis, underscores their value as multi-target therapeutic candidates for insomnia.

Flavonoids

Flavonoids are a diverse family of small-molecule natural compounds known for their wide range of pharmacological activities, including anticancer, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and vasoprotective effects [89]. Lonicerin (LON), a flavonoid abundant in Lonicera japonica Thunb., demonstrates multiple bioactivities, such as anticancer, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, antibacterial, anti-biofilm, anti-apoptotic, and neuroprotective effects [90]. LON exhibits a strong binding affinity to GABAA receptors through multiple interactions, exerting potent sedative effects and significantly increasing sleep duration in animal models [91].

Flavone from mulberry leaves (MLF), which includes flavonols and flavonol glycosides like quercetin, kaempferol, luteolin, and naringenin, has been shown to promote sleep [92]. MLF shortens sleep latency, prolongs sleep duration, and enhances sleep efficiency in mice by elevating 5-HT and GABA levels in serum, hypothalamus, and hippocampus. The key mechanisms likely involve the modulation of tryptophan metabolism and neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions [93]. Genistein (GE), a flavonoid from Glycine max Merr., has well-established immunomodulatory properties and protective effects against conditions such as obesity, cardiovascular disease, cancer, and liver injury. GE has been shown to reduce serum CORT levels and decrease c-Fos and CRF immunoreactivity in the PVN, thereby alleviating stress-induced sleep disturbances. Additionally, GE increases DRN 5-HT immunoreactivity and reduces wakefulness [94]. Icariin (ICA), a key flavonoid from Epimedium brevicornu Maxim., exerts neuroprotective and sedative-hypnotic effects. ICA regulates GABA and 5-HT neurotransmitter levels, upregulates GABAAα1 and GABAAγ2 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels, and has been shown to alleviate circadian rhythm disorders, shorten sleep latency, and increase sleep duration [95].

In summary, flavonoids exert notable sedative-hypnotic effects primarily through the positive modulation of GABAergic signaling and the regulation of serotonergic pathways. Their ability to concurrently engage multiple targets, including neurotransmitter receptors and metabolic pathways, underpins their potential as multi-faceted therapeutic agents for insomnia.

Saponins

Saponins are a diverse group of amphiphilic compounds found abundantly in medicinal plants. These compounds are recognized for their broad range of pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer effects [96]. Saponins from Rhodiola rosea L. have shown potential in reversing insomnia-induced alterations in neurotransmitter and cytokine levels. Specifically, they increase plasma levels of GABA, 5-HT, NE, prostaglandin D2 (PGD2), and IL-1β, while reducing elevated IL-6 levels. These changes contribute to the sedative and hypnotic effects of saponins from Rhodiola [97].

Ginsenosides, the primary active compounds in Panax ginseng C.A. Mey., play a significant role in regulating sleep and protecting the CNS. Ginsenoside Rg1 (Rg1) has been found to activate serotonergic neurons in the DRN and reduce the activity of locus coeruleus (LC) noradrenergic neurons, thus improving sleep disorders [98]. Rg1 also exerts its effects by suppressing orexin neurons in the lateral hypothalamus and CRH neurons in the paraventricular hypothalamic nucleus [99]. Furthermore, Rg5 and ginsenoside Rk1 (Rk1) increase the levels of GABA and 5-HT, working synergistically with 5-HTP to promote sleep through the modulation of GABAergic, glutamatergic, and serotonergic systems [100]. Senegenin (SEN), the primary active compound from Polygala tenuifolia Willd. rhizomes, is well known for its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, and anti-apoptotic properties [101]. SEN regulates insomnia through the GAD67-mediated Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1)/nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)/E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase parkin (Parkin)/PTEN induced kinase 1 (PINK1) pathway and affects serotonergic, GABAergic, and immune systems [102].

In summary, saponins alleviate insomnia through a coordinated modulation of neurotransmitter systems (GABA, 5-HT, and NE) and immune-inflammatory responses. This synergistic regulation across neuro-immune axes underscores their potential as multi-system interventional agents for the disorder.

Alkaloids

Alkaloids are a class of organic compounds characterized by a ring structure containing one or more basic nitrogen atoms. Due to their structural diversity, alkaloids exhibit a wide range of pharmacological properties and have significant biological activities [103]. The total alkaloid extract of Nelumbinis Plumula (NPA) is one of its primary chemical constituents. NPA has been shown to significantly prolong sleep duration and shorten sleep latency in animal models. Mechanistically, NPA increases GABA levels, decreases DA levels, and modulates the activity of GABRA1, DRD2, 5-HT1A, and acetylcholinesterase (AChE) proteins. By affecting both the dopaminergic and GABAergic systems, NPA helps regulate sleep-wake balance [104]. Nuciferine (NF) is an aromatic alkaloid derived from the leaves of Nelumbo nucifera Gaertn. It exhibits numerous pharmacological properties, including anticancer, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and vasodilatory effects [105]. NF has been shown to synergistically interact with 5-HTP, leading to elevated levels of 5-HT and 5-HT1A receptors in the hypothalamus of insomniac mice. This interaction suggests that NF regulates sleep primarily through the serotonergic system and 5-HT1A receptors [106].

In summary, alkaloids exert sedative-hypnotic effects by orchestrating a balance between key neurotransmitter systems, primarily through the potentiation of GABAergic and serotonergic signaling alongside the modulation of dopaminergic activity. This multi-system intervention underlies their therapeutic potential for insomnia.

Herbal extracts

Herbal extracts, derived from plants with a rich history of medicinal use, offer a diverse array of bioactive compounds. These compounds are often utilized in the treatment of various ailments, including insomnia, due to their natural sedative and therapeutic properties [107]. Here, we explore several prominent herbal extracts and their mechanisms in modulating sleep. Lilium davidii var. unicolour Cotton (Liliaceae) possesses multiple bioactive properties and is traditionally used to treat insomnia. Its extract can regulate hypothalamic neurotransmitters and melatonin by increasing 5-HT and GABAA expression, and maintain the steady-state balance of the HPA axis, thereby alleviating insomnia symptoms and restoring normal sleep patterns [108].

Prunella vulgaris L. (P. vulgaris), known for its immunomodulatory, antioxidant, and antibacterial properties [109], has been shown to reduce sleep latency and prolong sleep duration in rats. The hypnotic effects are attributed to its active constituents, which are absorbed into the bloodstream in their native form, directly influencing sleep regulation [110]. Rosa roxburghii Tratt. is a plant with an extensive history of use in traditional Chinese medicine. Its extract helps regulate insomnia by activating GABA receptors, enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity, and modulating inflammatory cytokines. This combination of effects contributes to its therapeutic potential in improving sleep quality [111]. Hop (Humulus lupulus L.) is known for its memory-enhancing, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties. Hongcheon-hop extract significantly increases NREM sleep by enhancing GABAergic neurotransmission. It upregulates the expression and protein levels of GABAA and 5-HT1A receptors in the brain while increasing concentrations of GABA and 5-HT, collectively improving sleep and alleviating insomnia [112].

Piper nigrum L. (PN) extract mitigates hippocampal damage caused by sleep deprivation by increasing the production of neurotransmitters like 5-HT, GABA, and DA, while reducing Glu levels [113]. These neuroprotective effects are mediated through the suppression of overactivation of the Janus kinase 1 (JAK1)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling pathway, which plays a key role in inflammatory responses [114]. Polygonatum sibiricum Redouté (P. sibiricum) exhibits a range of beneficial physiological activities, including anti-aging, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects. Its rhizome water extract promotes sleep by increasing both the protein and mRNA expression of GABAA-R2 and 5-HT1A receptors, acting through direct interaction with GABAA receptors [115]. Water extracts of Coptidis Rhizoma (WCR) bind strongly to GABAA-BZD and 5-HT2C receptors in a dose-dependent manner. These effects are mediated through the modulation of GABAergic and serotonergic systems, alongside activation of arylalkylamine N-acetyltransferase (AANAT), which regulates sleep-wake cycles [116].

Panax ginseng is widely used for treating various health conditions. The alcohol extract of ginseng (GAE) extends sleep duration, enhances cognitive performance, prevents hippocampal neuronal damage, increases Nissl body density, and improves sleep-related biomarkers. These effects are mediated through the modulation of orexin production in the hypothalamus, interaction with orexin receptors, suppression of the phosphatidylinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt)/mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase (mTOR) signaling pathway, and promotion of autophagy [117]. Ganoderma lucidum (G. lucidum) contains multiple bioactive compounds. Its spore extract suppresses the expression of NF-κB, NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain associated protein 3 (NLRP3), and caspase-1 in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), while increasing GABA levels in the parabrachial nucleus (PBN) and restoring GABAergic neuronal activity, ultimately normalizing sleep patterns [118]. SZS, traditionally used for insomnia and palpitations, modulates the expression of GABAA receptors and GAD65/67 in specific brain regions [119]. SZS aqueous extract normalizes levels of 5-HT, GABA, DA, Glu, and NE, restoring excitatory-inhibitory balance in the brain and improving sleep architecture through the GABAergic system [120]. It acts primarily through the regulation of serotonergic and GABAergic synaptic pathways, significantly improving sleep by modulating high hypothalamic expression of HTR1A, GABRA1, and GABRG2, while reducing HTR2A expression [121].

Passiflora incarnata L. (PI) has sedative, hypnotic, and anxiolytic effects. Passionflower extract promotes sleep by enhancing GABAergic neurotransmission, activating specific brain regions, and increasing endogenous melatonin secretion [122]. Valeriana officinalis L. is commonly used to alleviate anxiety and sleep disturbances. Its extract significantly upregulates the expression of GABAergic and serotonergic receptors in the brain, alleviates oxidative stress, and modulates apoptotic proteins to promote sedative effects [123]. Dendropanax morbiferus contains multiple bioactive constituents. Its leaf extract exhibits anti-inflammatory and cholesterol-lowering properties [124]. Additionally, it elevates GABA and 5-HT levels and reduces oxidative stress, all of which contribute to improved sleep quality [125]. Chrysanthemum morifolium (Chry), primarily cultivated in Asia, is widely used to treat insomnia. Its extract and active component linarin exert hypnotic effects, which may enhance NREM sleep intensity by regulating the GABAergic system and activating Cl−channels [126]. Alpinia galanga (L.) Willd. (AG) exhibits neuroprotective, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory effects [127]. Extracts from this plant modulate GABAergic and glutamatergic receptors, elevate 5-HT levels, and increase brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels to improve sleep latency and duration [128]. Sophora flavescens Aiton is a traditional medicinal herb with potential as a functional food ingredient. Its alcohol extract (SFAE) increases 5-HT and 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) levels, while decreasing NE and DA levels. SFAE improves sleep quality and duration by promoting PI3K/Akt/BDNF signaling in the brain [129].

Collectively, these herbal extracts function as multi-target therapeutics, orchestrating a restoration of neurochemical equilibrium and systemic homeostasis. However, each class exhibits unique mechanistic emphases beyond this common framework. Terpenoids prominently integrate mitochondrial protection and metabolic regulation with their sleep-modulating effects. Flavonoids often mediate effects through direct receptor interactions. Saponins function as broad-system modulators, uniquely engaging multiple monoaminergic, neuroendocrine, and orexinergic pathways simultaneously. Alkaloids tend toward precise receptor interplay, notably orchestrating dopaminergic-cholinergic balance. Challenges and limitations

Insomnia is a widespread sleep disorder, yet its underlying pathophysiological mechanisms remain incompletely understood. This knowledge gap significantly impedes the development of targeted therapeutic strategies. While first-line pharmacological interventions, such as BZD, exhibit some efficacy, their single-target modes of action and pronounced AEs (including dependence and cognitive impairment) limit their long-term application. In contrast, natural products, with their multi-component and multi-target synergistic properties, offer a unique and complementary therapeutic value for insomnia management. Conventional hypnotics provide rapid symptom relief, which is crucial for acute insomnia, but their long-term safety profile is often unfavorable. Natural products typically demonstrate a more favorable safety and tolerability profile with a lower risk of dependence, making them potentially more suitable for managing chronic insomnia. Therefore, leveraging the multi-system regulatory advantages of natural products within precision intervention frameworks may overcome the limitations of conventional single-target drugs and enhance overall therapeutic outcomes. This integrative approach has emerged as a significant direction in contemporary translational medical research.

Extensive preclinical studies have demonstrated the potential of natural products to improve sleep. However, high-quality clinical evidence remains scarce. Large-scale, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials are notably absent, presenting a major barrier to clinical application. Therefore, whether natural products can offer clinically meaningful symptom relief, sustained therapeutic benefits, and reliable long-term safety through their multi-system, multi-target effects requires systematic validation in rigorously designed clinical studies. To bridge this gap, studies should employ objective sleep monitoring tools alongside validated subjective scales to provide comprehensive efficacy data. Furthermore, clinical trial design needs to incorporate patient stratification strategies, based on insomnia phenotypes, biomarkers, or genetic profiles, to identify subgroups most likely to respond to specific natural product interventions, thereby paving the way for personalized insomnia therapy.

The clinical application of natural products faces several challenges. Their intrinsic physicochemical limitations, such as poor chemical stability, low oral bioavailability, and limited the BBB permeability, impair their druggability and CNS efficacy. Future efforts should prioritize the development of advanced drug delivery systems, such as lipid nanoparticles, polymer-based nanoformulations, or self-emulsifying drug delivery systems, to enhance solubility, stability, and targeted brain delivery. Additionally, variations in the quality and composition of raw materials, which are influenced by factors like cultivation conditions, harvesting practices, and processing methods, complicate standardization. Addressing this requires the establishment of stringent quality control protocols from farm to tablet. For complex mixtures, standardizing based on bioactive marker compounds or defined pharmacological activity, rather than a single component, may be a more pragmatic approach. These factors also hinder reproducible mechanistic research. Furthermore, potential interactions between natural products and conventional sedative-hypnotic agents require careful evaluation. A thorough understanding of in vivo metabolic interactions and the establishment of personalized administration guidelines are crucial to ensuring their safe and rational clinical use.

It is also important to acknowledge that natural products, as bioactive substances, may exert non-specific off-target effects or toxicological risks while producing therapeutic benefits. The geraniol at concentration of 800 μg/mL is associated with genotoxicity and may also cause developmental malformations and embryonic lethality [130]. β-myrcene at concentrations above 250 μg/mL demonstrates cytotoxicity toward white blood cells, inducing DNA damage and chromosomal abnormalities [131]. GE concentrations above 10 mg/mL can inhibit male fertility via suppression of the acrosome reaction [132] and may exhibit genotoxicity within certain concentration ranges [133]. Ginsenosides at a concentration of 50 μg/mL exert embryotoxic effects in rodent embryo cultures [134]. SEN may cause gastric mucosal damage at a dose of 200 mg/kg and has limitations such as low oral bioavailability and rapid clearance [135]. NF faces challenges such as limited permeability, inadequate gastrointestinal stability, and reduced oral bioavailability [136]. SFAE is associated with potential hepatotoxicity at a dose of 1.25 g/kg [137].

In multi-component systems, complex synergistic or antagonistic interactions may occur, and their impact on normal tissues remains insufficiently understood. The lack of a comprehensive and standardized safety evaluation framework is a critical bottleneck for clinical translation. To meet regulatory requirements, a stepwise safety assessment framework must be implemented. This should include comprehensive in vitro toxicology analysis, rigorous repeated-dose toxicity studies in relevant animal models, and monitoring for long-term and rare adverse events. Therefore, systematic investigations of metabolic interactions in combination therapies, the development of personalized treatment protocols, and the creation of holistic safety assessment frameworks, incorporating long-term and dynamic monitoring, are urgently needed to ensure the safe and rational clinical use of natural products.

Finally, current animal models of insomnia present inherent limitations for translational research. These models struggle to fully replicate the chronic nature and complex etiology of human insomnia. Species-specific biological differences often prevent accurate modeling of core clinical features, which limits the predictive value of preclinical studies. These shortcomings in biological validity and clinical relevance pose significant challenges in translating preclinical findings into effective therapeutics.

Conclusions and prospects

Insomnia, a prevalent sleep disorder, can profoundly affect patients' physical and emotional well-being, as well as overall quality of life. In recent years, research into natural products for the treatment of insomnia has garnered increasing attention, with ongoing investigations into their mechanisms of action and clinical applications. A key breakthrough lies in recognizing that the therapeutic effects of many natural products depend not only on their chemical composition but are significantly influenced by the synergistic interaction between administration timing and circadian rhythm biology. To translate such mechanistic insights into clinical practice, future work must prioritize specific, actionable research pathways. First, clinical trials should move beyond simple efficacy verification and adopt chronotherapeutic designs that compare morning versus evening dosing. Second, patient stratification biomarkers must be integrated into trial design. Prospective studies should incorporate polysomnography and actigraphy as objective endpoints alongside validated patient-reported outcomes, and collect biospecimens for biomarker discovery to identify predictive responders. Third, systematic exploration of combination therapies integrating natural products with conventional sedative-hypnotic agents is warranted. Adaptive clinical trial designs may be employed to rigorously evaluate the synergistic effects and clinical value of such combination strategies while ensuring continuous monitoring of safety and efficacy.

Future studies should use cutting-edge technologies, such as single-cell transcriptomics and spatial multi-omics, to comprehensively map the dynamic molecular networks that underpin the development and progression of insomnia. These approaches will enable the construction of dynamic models of its neuropathological evolution, facilitating precise identification of critical time windows for natural product interventions. Ultimately, these efforts aim to optimize and validate personalized therapeutic strategies. Additionally, addressing the persistent challenge of low CNS delivery efficiency for natural products has become a critical priority for advancing clinical translation. This necessitates the development of intelligent targeted delivery systems, including multifunctional nanocarriers based on liposomes or polymer nanoparticles, integrated with BBB-penetrating technologies like active targeting ligands or cell-penetrating peptides, to enhance brain accumulation and targeted distribution. Simultaneously, a standardized quality control system spanning the entire supply chain of herbal medicines should be established to ensure consistent product quality and support the safe clinical translation of natural products for insomnia.

A comprehensive understanding of the neurological mechanisms underlying insomnia, coupled with systematic investigation of precise intervention strategies, is essential to position natural products as innovative therapeutic agents. Future preclinical and translational clinical research must focus on constructing a robust evidence chain from mechanistic insights to practical application. Such efforts will provide a solid theoretical foundation and clear translational pathway for natural product development, accelerating their progression toward safe, effective, and personalized clinical treatments for insomnia.
