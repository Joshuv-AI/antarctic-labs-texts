LEAP! Rx: A randomized trial of a pragmatic approach to lifestyle medicine
Authors: Amber Watts, Amanda Szabo‐Reed, Jordan Baker, Jill K Morris, James Vacek, Jonathan Clutton, Jonathan Mahnken, Mickeal N Key, Eric D Vidoni, Jeffrey M Burns
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONClinicians lack the tools to incorporate physical activity into clinical care for Alzheimer's disease prevention. We tested a 52‐week exercise and health education program (Lifestyle Empowerment for Alzheimer's Prevention [LEAP! Rx]) that integrates clinician referrals and community‐based fitness resources.METHODSWe randomized 219 participants to the LEAP! Rx (ie, exercise and monthly brain health education) or a standard‐of‐care control group and tested the effects on cardiorespiratory fitness, insulin resistance, body composition, lipids, and cognitive performance.RESULTSPhysicians were able to connect their patients to a community lifestyle intervention. The intervention group increased in cardiorespiratory fitness at 12 and 52 weeks (p = 0.005). We observed no effects on secondary measures. Participants meeting 80% of weekly goals (150 min, moderate to vigorous activity) saw greater fitness improvements than those with less than 80% (p < 0.001).DISCUSSIONThese results hold promise for broad implementation of exercise interventions into larger healthcare systems and have implications for improved research recruitment strategies.TRIAL REGISTRATIONNCT No. NCT03253341.Highlights Our community‐based exercise program increased cardiorespiratory fitness.Our digital physician referral method increased the diversity of the participant sample.Our findings have implications for personalized dementia risk reduction strategies. Keywords: cardiorespiratory fitness, exercise, exercise prescription, health care delivery, healthy lifestyle, intervention, physical activity, randomized controlled trial (RCT), recruitment

BACKGROUND

According to US Census Projections, the number of adults aged 65 and older will increase from 54 million to 95 million by 2060. 1 Aging brings increased cognitive and physical decline, with dramatic rises in the risk of cardiovascular disease, stroke, diabetes, and Alzheimer's disease (AD), which will require more specialized healthcare resources. 2 There is strong evidence that these risks can be modified by lifestyle interventions. 3 , 4 The extant literature and ongoing research suggest that lifestyle modifications, specifically exercise, are among the most effective and cost‐effective tools available for chronic disease management. 5

Public health bodies typically recommend at least 150 min of moderate‐intensity aerobic exercise and two sessions of resistance exercise per week as part of a regular exercise regimen to maintain health and fitness. Though the results of trials conducted to date have been mixed, overall, the evidence suggests that aerobic exercise in healthy, older adults may benefit cognitive performance, brain plasticity, and hippocampal atrophy while improving visual attention and memory. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Resistance training is also essential to a complete exercise program for older adults. 10 , 11 It uses muscular contraction against resistance to mitigate the effects of aging on neuromuscular function and functional capacity 12 and may improve muscle strength, mass, and output, 13 and its typically recommended frequency is at least twice weekly.

Despite the promise of exercise interventions to reduce the chronic disease burden of aging, public health efforts currently fall short. There has been little systematic progress in translating lifestyle intervention research into meaningful gains in population health. While laboratory‐based trials demonstrate efficacy in highly controlled environments, they are often not effective when translated to the broader population. Many clinicians recommend increased physical activity and exercise to their patients. However, this recommendation lacks specificity, 14 and numerous barriers conspire against older adults to limit physical activity. 15 In addition, recommendations from clinicians are often singular events with little follow‐up.

The current approach of education and encouragement alone is insufficient: Up to 25% of all adults in the US are completely sedentary, and more than half are insufficiently active to achieve health benefits, 5 , 16 with physical activity levels declining through adulthood to low levels in older adults, 5 , 17 exacerbating risk of functional decline. 18 We designed the Lifestyle Empowerment for Alzheimer's Prevention (LEAP! Rx) study to help fill a critical gap between clinician recommendations and resources to support a healthier lifestyle. The intention was to provide specific and effective tools for clinicians to offer their patients to help initiate and sustain healthy lifestyle behavior changes.

Innovative studies have successfully used clinician‐prescribed exercise. 19 , 20 , 21 For example, New Zealand's experiment with the written green prescription program has become the standard of care in that country. A 2023 systematic review of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) suggest green prescriptions have demonstrated consistent benefits for physical activity, cardiometabolic health, psychological health, and inflammation. 22 To our knowledge, little research has considered long‐term reductions of AD risk, though each of the established benefits is known to contribute to healthy brain aging. The success of the green prescription as part of New Zealand's national health plan lies in its integration of the provider and the larger community support structure for increasing physical activity. 23

Our program, LEAP! Rx, was developed to flexibly integrate clinician referrals with community‐based fitness resources to help overcome barriers to healthy lifestyle adoption, such as motivation, access, and support. The primary goal of this study was to test a framework for clinicians to engage their patients in healthy behaviors and sustained lifestyle changes following public health recommendations. The framework included referral workflows, a network of community sites for delivery, a structured but flexible exercise program, and healthy lifestyle behavior education. We tested the efficacy of an exercise and healthy lifestyle program using a unique referral method embedded in the electronic health record (EHR) compared to the standard of care. We hypothesized that the program would benefit cardiorespiratory fitness and markers of chronic disease risk factors at both 12 and 52 weeks.

METHODS

Study design

LEAP! Rx was a 52‐week RCT testing the effects of exercise combined with education on the primary outcome, cardiorespiratory fitness (VO2peak). Participants were randomly assigned at a 1:1 ratio to the LEAP! Rx Program or standard‐of‐care control group. The control group received educational materials at entry, mimicking the current standard of care to encourage health behavior change and provide outcome measures parallel to the active intervention group. After completion of the outcome measures, the control group was provided access to the LEAP! Rx program to enhance recruitment efforts and reduce control group attrition, though we did not assess their outcomes in response to the program. We compared outcomes from the LEAP! Rx intervention with the standard‐of‐care control group to test the efficacy of the LEAP! Rx program to (1) increase cardiorespiratory fitness at 12 and 52 weeks after intervention (co‐primary outcome measures) and (2) improve secondary outcome measures of chronic disease risk factors: insulin resistance, body composition, and lipids. As an exploratory action, we also measured change in cognitive performance. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Kansas Medical Center and was performed in accordance with the ethical standards articulated in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Referral and recruitment methods

Recruitment began on July 1, 2018, and ended due to accrual on June 20, 2022. We employed two recruitment methods: (1) a physician referral approach through the EHR and (2) a traditional self‐referral method. The self‐referral method followed our usual channels of recruitment at the University of Kansas Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (KU‐ADRC), leveraged our research recruitment infrastructure 23 developed over a decade, and included centralized management of a database of potential research participants recruited through digital advertising, public presentations, and word of mouth. In contrast, the physician referral approach included direct referral from clinicians of The University of Kansas Hospital (TUKHS) through a system embedded in the EHR as a “prescription” to the LEAP! Rx program. Figure 1 illustrates how “best practice alerts” appeared to referring physicians in the health record with an option to refer patients to the program by clicking and entering information in a pop‐up window.

Best practice alerts example.

Our study design and execution addressed diversity, equity, and inclusion in recruitment procedures. Our center has outreach coordinators and multiple research investigators devoted to the recruitment and representation of ethnic and racial minorities in our research projects. These study team members have developed community partnerships that have allowed our center to grow in its recruitment of underrepresented participants that mirror the demographic makeup of our region. Further, our physician referral recruitment method allowed the inclusion of participants throughout the healthcare system, not only those who might respond to traditional outreach attempts. In the results section, we describe the characteristics of our sample in terms of race, ethnicity, sex, geography, and socioeconomic diversity.

The prescription embedded in the EHR was developed in collaboration with our medical informatics team to (1) develop an algorithm to identify potentially eligible participants based on their health record, (2) flag potentially eligible participants in the EHR for providers (best practice alert), and (3) communicate the prescription from TUKHS to our research team and the network of YMCAs where the participants were assigned to engage in supervised exercise with a trainer. The electronic prescription or recruitment division referrals were routed to study staff to make initial contact and complete a phone screen with potential participants to determine eligibility. Eligible patients were scheduled for baseline testing. For participants who were self‐referred, a clinician or physician clearance to exercise was required, while for those who were referred by physicians, the safety of exercise was considered as part of the recommendation for referral.

Clinician recruitment and participation

Referring physicians agreed to participate in surveys and interviews to assess the implementation and scalability of the program. A total of 54 clinicians from TUKHS clinics (Internal Medicine, Endocrinology, Family Medicine, Geriatrics, Cardiology, and Diabetes) were included. We began recruitment of participating physicians by starting with six “physician champions” who helped to spread the word to other physicians in their practices. We reached out to potential participating physicians via emails, video conference meetings, and visits to the clinics. Once physicians expressed willingness to participate, we added their names to our best practice alerts in the EHR.

We aimed to minimize the burdens of time and effort for physicians to encourage the successful adoption of the program. 24 , 25 At the initiation of the study, study team members met with physicians individually and provided them with a one‐page primer 26 that outlined strategies for (1) referring to the LEAP! Rx program and (2) offering tips on leading a brief discussion on the importance of physical activity with their patients. Physicians were asked to “prescribe” the program to potentially eligible patients via the best practice alert in the EHR, just as they would other orders. With participant consent, we provided feedback to referring physicians by email about the successful enrollment of referred participants and data on participants’ improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness and HgbA1c. These results were not included in the EHR.

Participants

We recruited 219 older adult participants according to the following intentionally broad inclusion and exclusion criteria. These criteria were selected to provide a generalizable cohort of older adults and encourage clinician referral of participants who the clinician believed would benefit from increased physical activity. Upon successful completion of baseline testing, the study coordinator randomized participants using the REDCap randomization module. The study statistician (J.M.) developed randomization tables in SAS and uploaded them to REDCap to securely allocate participants to the treatment arm.

Intervention: The LEAP! Rx program

The LEAP! Rx program included an intensive 12‐week empowerment phase of regular supervised exercise at a network of YMCAs, followed by a 40‐week lifestyle phase of intermittent supervised exercise (52 weeks total) with a dedicated LEAP! Rx coach. The overall goal of the training was to meet current recommendations of 150 min/week of aerobic exercise (over 3 to 5 days/week) and 2 days/week of resistance exercise. 5 In addition, we offered monthly Alzheimer's prevention classes (eg, diet, cognitive engagement, sleep, stress reduction). In partnership with the YMCAs, participants had a 1‐year membership to the YMCA fitness center, including group exercise classes. Participants received a physical activity monitor (Garmin Vivofit 3, Olathe, KS, USA) to encourage increases in activity, though we did not monitor heart rate or physical activity engagement.

The LEAP! Rx coaches served as the primary point of contact for study staff and participants, set individualized goals for participants, monitored physical activity levels, as measured by physical activity monitor, and reviewed progress. They led the exercise training sessions and provided supervision for aerobic and resistance exercises with a focus on safety. Coaches were required to have certification from a nationally recognized organization (eg, American College of Sports Medicine, American Council on Exercise). Coaches also underwent training specific to the LEAP! Rx study and received a manual of procedures.

The 12‐week empowerment phase consisted of supervised and unsupervised exercise training sessions. Participants were trained by coaches who provided supervision during this titration period two times per week from weeks 1 to 6 and one time per week in weeks 8, 10, and 12. By week 6, participants were able to walk, cycle, or elliptical for 150 min/week over 4 or 5 days at a level deemed “somewhat hard” or 3 to 5 (out of 10) on the modified Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) scale. The use of an exertion rating as a proxy for a target heart rate zone decreased complexity, allowed greater individualization in the context of rate‐limiting medications, and is a validated method of exercise prescription. 28 During the first several weeks of exercise, participants were taught how to rate their exertion levels based on their heart rate during supervised sessions. Coaches provided generalized guidance to participants to increase total aerobic exercise duration by approximately 15 min each week over the initial 6 weeks to a goal of 150 min/week. Coaches encouraged participants to exercise at an RPE of 3 to 4 during the initial 3 weeks, 3 to 5 in the subsequent 6 weeks, and 4 to 5 for the remainder of the study.

Participants were encouraged to perform resistance exercises twice a week. Resistance exercise targeted major muscle groups (eg, pectorals, rhomboids, latissimus dorsi, biceps, triceps, quadriceps, hamstrings, calves). During weeks 1 to 6, coaches identified exercises and machines that were comfortable for participants. Resistance was set using the RPE to a resistance that was “somewhat hard” (RPE 3 to 5) to lift 10 times. Like aerobic training, the use of the RPE is a valid method for gauging resistance training intensity. 29 Coaches counseled participants on modifying exercises and resistance as participants’ strength increased. Participants were encouraged to attend group exercise classes as a strategy to complete the 150 min/week of aerobic exercise and 2 days/week of resistance exercise.

The lifestyle phase consisted of 40 weeks of monthly exercise training sessions. Coaches encouraged adherence to the exercise program, assured progression of exercise routines, and reviewed progress toward goals. The goal of the lifestyle phase was to continue to challenge participants safely while allowing greater independence in the timing and location of exercise. Each YMCA has a variety of group exercise opportunities available to YMCA members. Participants were encouraged to attend group exercise classes to augment their individualized, supervised exercise training sessions or independent exercise. Group exercise opportunities were categorized into beginner, intermediate, and advanced classes so that participants could progress their exercise volume and intensity appropriately. Coaches assisted in identifying classes that effectively fulfilled aerobic and resistance exercise goals.

The monthly educational curriculum was delivered throughout both the empowerment and lifestyle phases of the LEAP! Rx program. It was offered in person at the KU Clinical Research Center and online by study staff. The overall goal of the curriculum was to initiate and sustain behavior change and provide a framework for lifestyle enhancement. Sessions included online videos and guided the completion of a workbook (https://www.amazon.com/LEAP‐Action‐Guide‐Exercise‐Health/dp/108226024X) with a LEAP! Rx coach that asked participants to (1) reflect on their habits indicated by monitoring and changes with age, (2) set realistic and measurable goals and describe expectations for change in behaviors and barriers to making change, (3) evaluate perceived benefits of increasing activity and reinforce strategies for rewarding behavior change, (4) determine enjoyable activities to pursue, and (5) rate confidence in the ability to make changes. The workbook also provided tips for ways to change behavior (generic and targeted to individual habits and barriers). The educational and goal‐setting were based on concepts from social cognitive theory and behavioral choice theory. 30 , 31

Control group: standard of care

The standard‐of‐care control group was designed to represent the current standard of care. Individuals randomized to this group were given educational information on the benefits of a healthy lifestyle at baseline. We did not discourage participants from exercising on their own, though we asked them not to join a YMCA or hire a personal trainer during this time. Our data 32 and others 19 , 33 suggest that education only does not result in significant and sustained physical activity changes. Participants randomized to the control group were informed they had been randomized to a 52‐week “waitlist,” during which time they completed all outcome measures parallel to the active intervention group. All participants (both active and control group participants) received the same educational materials at the enrollment visit. After completing outcome assessments at 52 weeks, control group participants were offered access to the full LEAP! Rx program at no cost, including membership and coaching fees, to enhance recruitment and minimize dropout from the control group. Outcome assessments were not repeated during their participation in the LEAP! Rx program.

Response to COVID‐19 pandemic

In March 2020, exercise facilities were closed in response to the COVID‐19 pandemic. Many facilities remained closed until late May 2020. During this time, participants were able to continue to meet with their LEAP! Rx coach virtually over Zoom to complete exercise sessions at home. In addition, in‐person education curriculum sessions were discontinued and only offered online. After May 2020, those participants who were comfortable and interested in resuming in‐person exercise did, but this was not required to remain active in the study.

Measures

All outcome assessments were completed in a single appointment lasting approximately 2 h to minimize burden. The primary outcome was improvement in cardiorespiratory fitness (VO2peak). We hypothesized that the program would benefit cardiorespiratory fitness at 12 and 52 weeks. VO2peak was measured as the highest achieved oxygen consumption 34 , 35 during a symptom‐limited graded treadmill test in a clinical research testing center. The test was led by an exercise physiologist and supervised by a clinician using a Cornell‐modified Bruce protocol. 36 Subjects were attached to a 12‐lead electrocardiograph and wore a non‐rebreathing facemask to assess heart rate, blood pressure, and expired air (oxygen, carbon dioxide) using a Parvomedics Trueone 2400 system (parvo.com). We used the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) guidelines 37 or participant request to determine test termination. Primary and secondary outcome administrators were blinded to group assignments.

Participants kept a weekly exercise log to measure their adherence to the recommended 150 min/week of moderate to vigorous exercise. We also collected attendance records of exercise training sessions, participation in group exercise classes, and online educational sessions. These were combined to produce a measure of adherence.

The secondary outcomes were metabolic measures of insulin resistance (hemoglobin A1c [HbA1c]), body composition (lean mass, fat mass), and lipid profile (total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL, and HDL). We hypothesized that the program would benefit these outcomes at 12 and 52 weeks. Participants arrived at the clinical research center following an overnight fast. Fresh whole blood was obtained using serum‐separator tubes for same‐day analysis of a standard lipid panel in serum (total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL‐C, LDL‐C, non‐LDL‐C; Quest Diagnostics). Additional fresh whole blood was obtained with EDTA as an anticoagulant for measurement of HbA1c (Quest Diagnostics). We assessed body composition using dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry (DEXA, Lunar Prodigy, version 11.2068, Madison, WI, USA) to determine fat‐free mass, fat mass, and percent body fat. We weighed participants with a digital scale accurate to ± 0.1 kg in a standard hospital gown after a voiding attempt and removal of jewelry.

As an exploratory measure, we evaluated cognitive performance using the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Toolbox. The NIH Toolbox cognition 38 core measures include Picture Vocabulary, Oral Reading Recognition Test, Flanker Inhibitory Control and Attention Test, Dimensional Change Card Sort Test, Picture Sequence Memory Test, List Sorting Working Memory Test, Pattern Comparison Processing Speed test, Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test, and the Oral Symbol Digit Test. The NIH Toolbox scoring guide provides a total cognitive function composite score, a fluid abilities composite score, and a crystallized abilities composite score.

Given the community‐based nature of our study and the population health perspective of this work, we sought to define the range of socioeconomic diversity in our sample using the Area Deprivation Index (ADI) derived from the Neighborhood Atlas. 39 ADI values range from 1 to 100, with lower values representing lower neighborhood disadvantage. Neighborhoods are defined by the US Census block group and are ranked on multiple factors, including but not limited to housing quality and employment. Since participants were recruited from two different states, the values used here are associated with national rankings. 40

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for referral status, randomization group, retention, and demographics. Statistical tests were conducted to compare differences in referral status, retention, and demographics and included chi‐squared tests for categorical variables and t tests for continuous variables. For the primary outcome, VO2peak, a linear mixed model was utilized to compare differences at 12 and 52 weeks. A contrast matrix was used to estimate the difference at 12 and 52 weeks. Cohen's d was calculated to estimate the effect size. For the linear mixed models, Cohen's d was calculated by dividing the difference at the 12‐ or 52‐week time point by the standard deviation, which was the square root of the sum of the between and within subject variances. Differences in compliance were compared using chi‐squared tests for categorical variables and t tests for continuous variables. ANOVA was used to test the difference between compliant intervention participants, non‐compliant intervention participants, and control participants. Similar to the primary outcome, the secondary outcomes of body composition, lipid profile, insulin resistance, and cognitive performance were compared at 12 and 52 weeks using linear mixed models, contrast matrices, and Cohen's d. Since a single primary endpoint was identified, no further multiplicity adjustments were made for the secondary outcomes. A sample size of 110 participants per group was estimated with an a priori power analysis (see Appendix), p < 0.05 was used to define statistical significance.

RESULTS

Recruitment, randomization, and retention characteristics

Of 54 physicians recruited, 28 referred participants to the program. We received 3463 physician referrals and 125 self‐referrals, and 6.1% of all participants referred were randomized to the intervention. Of our total 219 randomized participants, 118 (54%) were physician referred, and 101 (46%) were self‐referred. The recruitment method influenced the racial and ethnic makeup of the sample with a higher rate of Black/African American participants (n = 23, 19.5%) enrolled from the physician referral arm compared to the self‐referred group (n = 3, 3.0%; Χ 2 [df] 17.25 [3], p < 0.001). Physician‐referred participants had fewer average years of formal education compared to those who were self‐referred (mean = 15.6 vs 16.3 years; t [df] = 2.24 [209.78], p = 0.022, d = 0.270), but there were no differences in the average age of participants between the physician‐referred and self‐referred groups (M = 71.5 vs 71.8 years of age; t [df] = −0.466 [217], p = 0.641, d = 0.145). Physician‐referred participants came from more disadvantaged neighborhoods than self‐referred participants (mean ADI = 50.3 vs 38.6; t [df] = 3.81 [213.04], p < 0.001). Our attrition rates were 32.8% (n = 33 control, n = 39 intervention), higher than rates observed in our published exercise studies of similar duration (eg, 22% to 24%). 41 , 42 Individuals who dropped out of the study had fewer years of formal education (p = 0.007), were more likely to be physician‐referred (p < 0.001), and were more likely to have had the timing of their participation interrupted by the pandemic (p < 0.001) than those who completed the study. There were no differences in attrition rates between the intervention and control groups by education, biological sex, referral source (self‐ vs physician‐referred), age, race, baseline cardiorespiratory fitness levels, or stage of the pandemic when they were referred (pre‐pandemic vs post‐pandemic onset).

Primary outcome: VO2peak

Our primary aim was to examine the efficacy of our intervention at increasing and sustaining cardiorespiratory fitness as measured by peak oxygen consumption (VO2peak) at both 12 and 52 weeks. We hypothesized that our program would increase VO2peak at 12 weeks and sustain increases at 52 weeks after initiation of the intervention. Our primary analysis was intent to treat and thus included all 219 individuals randomized in the study (controls n = 109, Intervention n = 110). Overall, the intervention group had greater increases in mean VO2peak percent change over the intervention period compared to the control group (F = 5.48, p = 0.005; Table 3 and Figure 3).

The primary analysis combined both 12‐ and 52‐week outcomes. We conducted secondary analyses examining VO2peak response at the individual timepoints of 12 and 52 weeks. These contrast analyses showed that the percent change between baseline and week 12 (4.0% in the intervention group vs 1.0% in the control group) did not reach statistical significance (t = 1.64, p = 0.101, d = 0.132) but was statistically significant for the difference between baseline and week 52 (4.0% in the intervention vs −2.0% in controls; t = 3.30, p = 0.001, d = 0.285). There was a trend toward more responders (ie, an increase in cardiorespiratory fitness at both time points) in the intervention group (44%) compared to those in the control group (30%; p = 0.075; n = 139 with complete cases).

Compliance with exercise intervention

We defined intervention compliance as achieving 80% or greater of stated exercise goals of achieving 150 min/week of moderate to vigorous physical activity based on weekly exercise logs completed by participants. Of 109 participants randomized to the intervention group, 45 (41.2%) reached compliance goals. Participants who were compliant with the intervention had greater improvements in VO2peak compared to those who were not compliant (M (SD) 22.0 (5.0) compliant vs 17.9 (3.6) non‐compliant, p < 0.001, d = 0.316). For comparison, VO2peak at week 52 was highest in participants who were compliant within the intervention goals (n = 45; M (SD) 22.0 (5.0), next highest for control participants who were not exposed to the intervention (n = 109, M (SD) = 20.5 (4.3), and lowest for participants in the intervention group who were not compliant (n = 64, M (SD) = 17.9 (3.6). These groups differed significantly (F = 7.49, p < 0.001).

The COVID‐19 pandemic directly interrupted participation and compliance for 25 (23%) of our 109 participants randomized to the intervention. Compared to those who started the intervention after June 15, 2020, those disrupted by the pandemic had fewer weeks of self‐reported exercise without a trainer (40 vs 47, p = 0.007, d = 0.487) and fewer weeks during which they exercised at community‐based exercise facilities (vs remotely at home) (77% vs 83%, p < 0.001, d = 0.178). Community‐based exercise facilities were closed for an average of 17% of the weeks during the intervention for these participants. This disruption disproportionately affected physician‐referred participants. Self‐referred participants logged a higher number of weeks of compliance (48 vs 42 weeks p = 0.006, d = 0.336), more aerobic minutes per week (128 vs 119 min, p = 0.051, d = 0.171), and a higher percentage of aerobic minutes toward meeting exercise goals (68% vs. 75%, p = 0.038, d = 0.291) compared to the physician‐referred group.

Secondary outcomes: body mass, lipids, and insulin resistance

Our secondary outcomes measured the effect of our intervention on markers of chronic disease risk factors, including body composition, lipid profile, and insulin resistance. We hypothesized that our program would improve the metabolic profiles of participants in the intervention group compared to those in the control group. Overall, there were no statistically significant differences in secondary outcomes by intervention group across the intervention period for total lean mass (F = 2.23, p = 0.109), total fat mass (F = 1.00, p = 0.371), total cholesterol (F = 0.48, p = 0.619), HDL cholesterol (F = 0.23, p = 0.795), LDL cholesterol (F = 0.61, p = 0.543), triglycerides (F = 1.31, p = 0.271), or HbA1c (F = 0.02, p = 0.977).

Cognitive performance

We collected cognitive performance data to explore cognitive effects, despite being underpowered to see meaningful changes. We explored the effect of our intervention on changes in cognitive performance in two domains, fluid and crystallized. We hypothesized that our program would improve the cognitive performance of participants in the intervention group compared to those in the control group. Our secondary analysis was intent to treat and thus included all 219 individuals enrolled and randomized in the study (controls n = 109, intervention n = 110). Overall, there were statistically significant differences in cognitive performance by intervention group across the intervention period for the crystallized domain (F = 3.69, p = 0.026), but not for the fluid domain (F = 1.27, p = 0.282) (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Our study aimed to test a year‐long exercise and healthy lifestyle program digitally integrated into physician workflow during direct patient care. Consistent with our hypotheses, the intervention group showed increased cardiorespiratory fitness, though we did not observe expected improvements in other markers of chronic disease or cognitive performance. We observed that compliance with exercise goals of 150 min/week of moderate to vigorous exercise was an important mechanism of successful cardiorespiratory fitness improvement. The physician referral recruitment method based on workflows embedded in the EHR yielded a more racially and socioeconomically diverse sample compared to our traditional recruitment methods.

The study design focused on cardiorespiratory fitness as a primary intervention outcome. Our prior work suggests that exercise also provides benefits to the brain. 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 We have found that greater cardiorespiratory fitness in early AD is associated with greater whole brain volume, 43 most strongly in regions relevant to AD (the hippocampus and parietal lobe). 44 Longitudinal studies suggest a decline in cardiorespiratory fitness is associated with brain atrophy in early AD and cognitively normal individuals, 45 while maintenance of cardiorespiratory fitness mitigates brain atrophy and cognitive decline. 9 , 47 , 48 , 49 We have also found that insulin levels are associated with cognitive performance and brain volume. 52 , 53 , 54

The LEAP! Rx program produced an average increase in VO2peak of ∼1 mL/kg/min (4%) for those in the intervention group compared to a decrease of ∼0.5 mL/kg/min (2%) for those in the control group. This finding was approximately half the increase of what is observed in supervised exercise programs. 41 , 50 However, without exercise, the age‐predicted decline in cardiorespiratory fitness is 12% to 20% per decade for women and 20% to 25% per decade for men after age 70. 51 Therefore, preventing declines in cardiorespiratory fitness is essential.

Thus, we chose cardiorespiratory fitness and the gold‐standard procedure of a graded exercise test as our primary outcome. Individualized exercise prescriptions aimed at enhancing cardiorespiratory fitness have the benefit of whole‐body wellness effects, even without our demonstration of cognitive benefits. Although the intensity level of physical activity is a likely driver of cognitive benefits, our study design did not allow us to distinguish between time spent in moderate versus vigorous intensity. Further, we did not measure the degree to which the standard‐of‐care group may have engaged in moderate to vigorous exercise. As the field further pursues individualized medicine, future work may seek to identify individualized frequency, intensity, time, and type prescription of exercise in a way that fits efficient clinical workflows. 55 , 56 Personalized exercise regimens may be a key strategy for mitigating cognitive decline and enhancing overall health in older populations.

Exercise is crucial for overall health, but older adults often face specific barriers that can hinder their physical activity. Such barriers include lack of time, pain or discomfort, fear of injury, limited mobility, fixed income, and lack of social support or motivation. 57 Like previous studies, exercise compliance (ie, at least 80%) is important for achieving desired outcomes such as increases in cardiorespiratory fitness. 41 Our results supported the idea that better compliance with the recommended exercise prescription was associated with a greater increase in cardiorespiratory fitness. 58 Many older patients respect their physician's advice and have regular contact with their family doctor. Physicians can play a key role in the initiation and maintenance of exercise behavior among the older adult population. 57 Prior research suggests that physicians who have a follow‐up plan for patients when prescribing exercise see better exercise adherence. 27 This suggests that physician‐referred exercise programs like LEAP! Rx could be a means of increasing physical activity and maintaining cardiorespiratory fitness in older adult populations by providing regular follow‐ups regarding exercise for patients.

Not only is physician referral important for the initiation of exercise, but physicians can support study recruitment. Over 50% of the patients enrolled in the LEAP! Rx study were referred by physicians through the EHR. Although this approach is not novel for clinical trial referral in general, 59 to our knowledge this is the first study to use the EHR to refer older adult patients to an exercise program.

The randomization rate in the current study is in line with our prior studies (7%to 28%) 7 , 41 , 42 but is difficult to directly compare given the novel recruitment strategy of using physician referrals through the EHR (ie, low staff effort, computer algorithms to identify potentially eligible participants, physician participation in recruitment). However, the increased racial and socioeconomic diversity of the referred potential participants alone argues for increased consideration of this strategy for future research. We attribute much of this success to the use of clinically familiar tools such as EHR alerts and referrals. A previously convened panel of clinicians suggested these tools are optimal for busy clinicians because they are consistent with existing workflows in the same health system. As with the green prescription program, the use of clinical tools such as an electronic referral may be well understood and have a symbolic meaning for participants, conveying additional importance beyond a brief statement that one should “exercise more.” 19 Though best practice alerts and electronic referrals for research may not be available in all settings, we encourage future implementations of programs like LEAP! Rx to leverage the existing clinical tools available to convey physical activity recommendations as a standard part of medical care.

The LEAP! Rx program adds to the growing body of research on the possible benefits of digital tools and health information technology for broader implementation of lifestyle medicine approaches. 56 , 57 The EHR is a valuable tool for patient recruitment and integration and communication of multiple sources of health data that can improve patient care. 60 , 61 Our findings have valuable implications for the potential expansion of reach for healthy lifestyle interventions into larger healthcare systems and building physician buy‐in. Research shows that physician recommendation is a powerful motivator for participant lifestyle change in patients, but recommendations alone are not adequate to support and maintain lifestyle change. 62 , 63 Our program offers tools to healthcare providers to close the gap between recommendations and actions that improve fitness, which is associated with numerous health benefits including improved cardiovascular disease, reduced abdominal adiposity, improved cognitive function, 64 , 65 and reduced healthcare costs. 66 Results also suggest the use of physician referrals for research recruitment of populations that tend to be left out of research. 67 Trends in implementation science urge us to take our interventions out of the highly controlled research laboratory and adapt them for real‐world implementation in a wide range of participant populations. Our study is one example of successful community‐based lifestyle medicine in a “real‐world” setting.

Our ultimate goal is to create a scalable and cost‐effective program for clinicians and their patients to reduce the risk of chronic disease by inducing healthy lifestyle behavior changes through this multilevel intervention and bringing together health systems with community‐based fitness resources. While supervised exercise is more effective in maintaining healthy outcomes, it also involves financial costs. By partnering with community‐based fitness resources, we can introduce participants to settings like the YMCA that offer relatively accessible and low‐cost group fitness classes that support long‐term maintenance of fitness goals that are ultimately less costly than the treatment of chronic disease conditions. Recent advances in online fitness supervision strategies also have the potential to reduce costs. 68 , 69 With these goals of scalability and cost‐effectiveness in mind, we also offer limitations of our study that point to growth areas of future research. The COVID‐19 pandemic disrupted our exercise intervention when gyms were closed, and we learned new lessons about how to offer online alternatives to in‐person personal training sessions. The intervention did not have overall effects on secondary disease markers, including blood biomarkers, body composition, and cognitive function. Nor were we able to monitor time in different levels of exercise intensity. Although we did not observe cognitive improvements in the present study, improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness have nevertheless been repeatedly demonstrated in studies to support brain health and dementia risk reduction. 9 , 47 , 48 , 53 We will adapt future interventions to better target and measure these important health outcomes. The next steps include an analysis of relationships with our referring physicians to determine predictors of referral rates, perceived value of the program, and workflow barriers for our team of referring physician partners. We could not evaluate program delivery cost in the present study, a step that would move us toward our future goals. The LEAP! Rx exercise and health lifestyle program represent a step toward large‐scale, real‐world programs to offer clinicians and their patients implementable tools to improve healthy brain aging through behavior changes by linking them to existing community‐based resources.
