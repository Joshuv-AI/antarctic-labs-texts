Delayed diagnosis of patients with rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis and its associated factors in a high tuberculosis endemic area Guizhou, China
Authors: Wen Rao, Dafu Wang, Xiaoxue Ma, Tingting Gong, Pu Chen, Feng Hong, Yun Wang, Jinlan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (RR-TB) threatens global TB control; diagnostic delays worsen transmission and outcomes. Evidence on delays in high-burden settings is limited. This study quantified patient, health-system, and total diagnostic delays among RR-TB patients in Guizhou Province, China (2019–2023) and identified factors associated with prolonged delays to inform targeted control measures.MethodsWe included 2,677 RR-TB patients diagnosed and registered in Guizhou from 2019 to 2023. The status of three types of delays at different levels was investigated. Group comparisons used chi-square tests; temporal trends were evaluated with chi-square trend tests. Binary logistic regression models identified independent predictors of prolonged delays at multiple cutoffs.ResultsMedian delays were 32 days (IQR 3–118) for patient delay, 15 days (IQR 2–62) for health system delay, and 86 days (IQR 32–261) for total delay. From 2019 to 2023, health system and total delay rates declined significantly (P < 0.05), whereas patient delay rates showed no significant temporal change (P > 0.05). At a 14-day cutoff, patient delay was highest in Qianxinan Prefecture (75.0%, P < 0.05); health system and total delay rates were highest in Qiandongnan Prefecture (73.7% and 88.9%, respectively; P < 0.05). Multivariable analysis indicated that referral/recommend (OR = 1.44), comorbidities (OR = 1.51), initial treatment failure (OR = 1.32), and combined extrapulmonary TB (OR = 1.50) were independently associated with patient delay > 14 days; returned patients (OR = 1.84) and other registration classifications (OR = 1.39) predicted delays > 60 days. For health system delay, students (OR = 1.73) and migrant populations (OR = 3.39) had an increased risk at > 14 days, while initial treatment failure (OR = 1.45) and other registration classifications (OR = 1.98) were significant at > 60 days. For total delay, migrant populations (OR = 1.90), comorbidities (OR = 1.34), initial treatment failure (OR = 1.94), and other registration classifications (OR = 1.51) were key predictors.ConclusionAlthough progress has been made in reducing diagnostic delays for RR-TB in Guizhou Province, rising patient delay rates and significant regional disparities persist. Strengthening primary healthcare capacity, establishing active tracing and follow-up mechanisms, optimizing migrant population management, implementing revisit reminders for patients with initial treatment failure, and accelerating the deployment of molecular drug susceptibility testing are recommended to enhance early diagnosis and treatment outcomes, thereby advancing the global goal of ending tuberculosis by 2035.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13608-9.Keywords: Rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis, Delayed diagnosis, Associated factors, Western China

Introduction

Drug-resistant tuberculosis (DR-TB) represents a formidable obstacle to global TB control efforts, with rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (RR-TB)—defined as resistance to rifampicin by Mycobacterium tuberculosis [1]—posing a particularly significant threat. The management of RR-TB is notoriously complex, characterized by prolonged and costly treatment regimens that often yield suboptimal outcomes [2]. The global scale of this challenge is substantial; the World Health Organization (WHO) reported nearly 390,000 new cases of RR-TB in 2024, yet a concerning 58% of these individuals did not receive a timely diagnosis and appropriate treatment [3]. This global burden is reflected in China, one of the world’s high-burden countries for drug-resistant TB, which accounted for an estimated 28,000 new cases in 2024, representing 7.1% of the global total [3]. Within this national context, Guizhou Province in southwestern China emerges as a key region facing an escalating RR-TB epidemic. Despite initiating prevention and control programs in 2014, the province has witnessed a sharp increase in case detection, from 280 cases in 2016 to 1,038 in 2020 [4], indicating persistent and growing challenges. A critical bottleneck in the management of RR-TB is diagnostic delay, which encompasses both patient-related delays in seeking care and health system-related delays in providing a diagnosis [5].

Diagnostic delay is defined as the time interval from the initial onset of tuberculosis symptoms to the final confirmation of diagnosis [6]. While diagnostic delays are a well-documented global phenomenon in diverse settings, the duration varies significantly. For instance, the median diagnostic delay for multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) patients in Cape Town, South Africa, was 246 days in 2003 [7] and 27 days in 2017 [8]. In India, median diagnostic delays varied across regions: 11 days in Puducherry and 14 days in Chennai [9]. The median diagnostic delay for MDR-TB patients in Yangon, Myanmar, was 9 days [10]. In China, historical data from a national program (2006–2013) and a more recent multi-region study reported median diagnostic delays of 84 and 90 days, respectively, for RR/MDR-TB patients [11, 12].

Research indicates that compared to patients diagnosed early, those with delayed diagnosis often present with more severe disease, including a higher likelihood of pulmonary cavitation, high sputum bacillary load, bilateral lesions, prolonged infectiousness, and significantly increased risk of community transmission [13, 14]. Furthermore, diagnostic delay adversely affects treatment outcomes. Studies suggest that delayed diagnosis can lead to inappropriate or inadequate treatment, a key driver for the progression from rifampicin-monoresistant TB to MDR or extensively drug-resistant TB, consequently requiring longer treatment courses, more expensive medications, and resulting in lower treatment success rates [2, 15]. Risk factors for diagnostic delay in DR-TB encompass multiple dimensions. Patient factors include sex, age, education level, disease awareness, disease severity, and comorbidities such as HIV or diabetes mellitus. Healthcare system factors include geographical accessibility of services, healthcare provider expertise, and the type of DST available [14, 16–18]. These factors interact to prolong the interval from symptom onset to diagnosis. Therefore, identifying and intervening in key points contributing to diagnostic delay is crucial for improving outcomes in RR-TB patients.

To mitigate diagnostic delay, several innovative diagnostic strategies have been promoted in recent years, both domestically and internationally. Molecular drug susceptibility testing (mDST) techniques, such as Xpert MTB/RIF and line probe assays, can reduce the diagnostic time for DR-TB from weeks required by conventional culture-based DST to just hours [19]. The WHO recommends molecular testing as the initial diagnostic method for DR-TB [20]. Additionally, strengthening the capacity of primary healthcare facilities, establishing specimen referral networks, implementing active case-finding strategies, and promoting electronic DST result reporting systems can effectively reduce health system-related delays [8, 16, 21, 22]. In China, some high-burden regions have integrated DR-TB screening into basic public health services and established a tiered diagnostic model of “county-level sampling, city-level testing, and province-level confirmation“ [4, 23].

Notably, there is currently no universally accepted threshold for defining diagnostic delay specifically in RR-TB patients. For instance, common standards for measuring patient delay in pulmonary TB include an interval from symptom onset to first consultation exceeding 14 days [24], 30 days [25], or the median value [26]. However, applying these criteria to RR-TB patients remains debated, and no consensus has been reached [27]. Different delay thresholds may correspond to distinct risk factors and intervention windows. Moreover, most studies have focused on factors, phenomena, and patterns associated with diagnostic delay in pulmonary TB patients. In contrast, dedicated research on diagnostic delay specifically for RR-TB patients is scarce, preventing the formation of a comprehensive research framework and leaving numerous gaps that require urgent investigation. This underscores the critical need for more comprehensive studies on diagnostic delay in RR-TB.

The challenges associated with diagnostic delay are likely amplified in economically underdeveloped and mountainous regions like Guizhou Province, where populations of ethnic minorities, remote geography, limited transportation, and constrained healthcare resources converge. Therefore, this study aims to comprehensively analyze three potential types of delay within the RR-TB patient population in Guizhou Province. For each type of delay, three commonly used cut-off values derived from the literature will be employed to identify factors distinguishing “short delay” from “long delay.” This approach will provide a basis for developing stage-specific prevention and control strategies and offer data to support further optimization of DR-TB control measures, ultimately contributing to the goal of ending TB by 2035.

Methods

Study setting

This study was conducted in Guizhou Province, southwestern China, an economically underdeveloped, mountainous province with a multi-ethnic population and a high burden of drug-resistant tuberculosis. The RR-TB diagnostic network comprises three tiers of designated hospitals: county-level facilities screen bacteriologically positive patients for drug resistance, performing mDST where available or referring specimens to municipal-level hospitals otherwise; municipal hospitals conduct confirmatory testing, with complex cases forwarded to the provincial laboratory. mDST was gradually scaled up from 2019 and covered all municipal-level regions and selected counties by the end of the study period, with cold-chain transport required for specimens from non-covered areas. Confirmed RR-TB patients are referred to municipal MDR-TB designated hospitals for standardized treatment. Data were extracted from the China Information System for Disease Control and Prevention, which registers all RR-TB cases province-wide.

Study design

This study employed a retrospective, population-based design. All RR-TB cases reported in Guizhou Province between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2023, were extracted from the China Information System for Disease Control and Prevention. The study aimed to quantify three types of diagnostic delay—patient delay, health system delay, and total delay—and to determine their prevalence using predefined, evidence-based cut-off values. In addition, the study explored sociodemographic and clinical factors associated with prolonged diagnostic delays among RR-TB patients.

Data collection and participants

Data were obtained from the Guizhou Provincial Tuberculosis Information Management System, a provincial subsystem of the China Information System for Disease Control and Prevention. The system records all TB cases diagnosed and registered in Guizhou Province and includes standardized information on patients’ demographic characteristics, clinical details, diagnostic history, and drug resistance profiles. Of 2883 registered patients, 206 (7.15%) were excluded due to logical inconsistencies (e.g., first visit date later than diagnosis date), diagnostic reclassification (revised to non-resistant tuberculosis or non-tuberculosis upon review), or incomplete records (missing any key variable: symptom onset date, first visit date, or diagnosis date). Ultimately, 2677 patients were included in the analysis.

Definitions

The delay cut-off values used in this study were all based on commonly adopted standards reported in the literature. Specifically, patient delay and health system delay were frequently defined as > 14 days [28, 29], > 30 days [30, 31], or > 60 days [31, 32]; total delay was commonly defined as > 28 days [30], > 60 days [29], or > 90 days [33]. Accordingly, in this study, (1) Diagnostic delay: Also referred to as total delay, this is defined as the time interval from a patient’s first onset of presumptive TB symptoms to the confirmation of diagnosis [28]. The three categories of total delay were defined as follows: (1) time interval > 28 days; (2) time interval > 60 days; and (3) time interval > 90 days. Total delay comprises both patient delay and health system delay. Patient delay refers to the time interval from the first onset of symptoms to the patient’s first medical consultation [34]. Health system delay refers to the time interval from the patient’s first medical consultation to the diagnosis of RR-TB [27]. The three categories for both patient delay and health system delay were as follows: (1) time interval > 14 days; (2) time interval > 30 days; and (3) time interval > 60 days.

In this study, (1) migrant population refers to patients with pulmonary TB who left their registered residence for various reasons to reside in other provinces or cities. (2) Registration classifications included: “new patient”, defined as a patient who had never received anti-TB drug treatment or had received it for less than one month (excluding treatment for other diseases); “initial treatment failure”, defined as a patient whose sputum smear or culture was positive at the end of the fifth month of treatment or at the completion of the treatment course; “relapse”, defined as a patient with a clear history of TB who was considered cured after completing the prescribed treatment regimen but subsequently developed the disease again; “return”, defined as a patient who received treatment for ≥ 1 month, interrupted treatment for ≥ 2 months, and then resumed treatment; and “others”, defined as cases not included in the above four categories. (3) Treatment classifications were divided into: “initial treatment”, referring to patients who had never received anti-TB drugs for TB, those who were undergoing regular standardized chemotherapy without completing the full course, and those who had received irregular chemotherapy for less than one month; and “retreatment”, referring to patients who had received irregular or inappropriate anti-TB drug treatment for TB for ≥ 1 month, as well as patients with initial treatment failure and relapse. (4) Key populations: In this study, key populations were defined as individuals with the following characteristics: people living with HIV/AIDS, patients with diabetes, staff and students in schools and kindergartens, correctional facility personnel, livestock workers, individuals with dust exposure/ pneumoconiosis patients, residents of nursing homes/welfare institutions, close contacts of patients with bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB, healthcare workers, or individuals in other crowded settings. (5) Comorbidities: This term refers to patients with concurrent conditions such as diabetes, pneumoconiosis, HIV/AIDS, or mental illness. These classification criteria were based on the World Health Organization (WHO) standards [3] and the Technical Guidelines for Tuberculosis Prevention and Control in China (2021 Edition) [1]. Furthermore, in this study, “other occupations” refers to all occupations other than farmer, homemaker/unemployed, stable employment/retired, and student, including but not limited to those in the catering service industry and commodity service industry.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3). Quantitative data with non-normal distribution such as delay time should be described using median and interquartile range [M(IQR)]. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and proportions (%). The chi-square trend test was applied to assess temporal trends in rates or proportions, while the chi-square test was used to compare differences between groups. Variables with P < 0.20 in the univariate chi-square analysis were included in a binary logistic regression model to identify factors associated with diagnostic delays. A forward stepwise conditional method was used for variable selection, and odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. A sensitivity analysis was also conducted to assess the robustness of results using alternative cut-off values based on the median delay, as applied in previous studies. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided α level of 0.05.

Result

General characteristics of RR-TB patients

From 2019 to 2023, 2,677 patients were finally included in this study. The mean age of the patients was 45 ± 17 years. Most were male (68.36%), of Han ethnicity (76.95%), aged 40 ~ 64 years (48.56%), and farmers (49.16%). Key populations, migrant populations, and those with comorbidities accounted for 16.14%, 10.24% and 15.24%, respectively, and the proportion of retreatment cases exceeded that of new cases. The proportion of patients diagnosed via mDST was 89.88%. The number of detected RR-TB patients increased from 229 cases in 2019 to 961 cases in 2023 (Table 1).

Status of diagnostic delay among RR-TB patients

Status and temporal trends of diagnostic delay

The median durations of patient delay, health system delay, and total delay were 32 (3, 118) days, 15 (2, 62) days, and 86 (32, 261) days, respectively. As shown in Fig. 1, for patient delay, 63.28%, 51.44%, and 37.47% of patients sought medical care within 14, 30, and 60 days after symptom onset, respectively (A–C), with no significant temporal trend (P > 0.05). Regarding health system delay, 50.09%, 35.75%, and 25.18% of patients were diagnosed with RR-TB within 14, 30, and 60 days after their first visit, respectively, showing a significant decreasing trend (trend χ² = 2.409, 3.156, 2.719; P < 0.05) (D–F). For total delay, 76.06%, 59.47%, and 48.64% of patients were diagnosed within 28, 60, and 90 days after symptom onset, respectively, and all showed a significant downward trend (trend χ² = 3.100, 3.192, 2.719; P < 0.05) (G–I).

Factors associated with patient delay

As shown in Table 2, multivariate analysis indicated that at the > 14-day cut-off, students (OR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.43–0.96), other occupations (OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.48–0.97), and migrant populations (OR = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.11–0.21) were less likely to experience patient delay. Conversely, referral/recommend (OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.17–1.76), comorbidities (OR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.18–1.94), initial treatment failure (OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.04–1.68), and concomitant other tuberculosis (OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.17–1.95) were risk factors for patient delay. At the > 30-day and > 60-day cut-offs, homemaker//unemployment was a protective factor (OR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.67–0.96; OR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.51–0.73, respectively). At the > 60-day cut-off, return (OR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.25–2.72) and other registration classifications (OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.02–1.62) emerged as new risk factors.

Factors associated with health system delay

Multivariate analysis (Table 3) showed that at the > 14-day cut-off, Han ethnicity (OR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.55–0.81), referral/recommend (OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.43–0.63), following up (OR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.57–0.89), and use of mDST (OR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.52–0.90) were protective factors, whereas students (OR = 1.73, 95% CI: 1.16–2.59) and migrant populations (OR = 3.39, 95% CI: 2.53–4.61) were risk factors. Compared with the > 14-day cut-off, the association for students was no longer statistically significant (P > 0.05) at the > 30-day and > 60-day cut-offs. At the > 60-day cut-off, homemaker/unemployment became a protective factor (OR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.58–0.92), while initial treatment failure (OR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.11–1.89) and other registration classifications (OR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.40–2.79) emerged as risk factors.

Factors associated with total delay

In multivariate analysis (Table 4), at the 28-day cutoff, Han ethnicity (OR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.62–0.98), referral/recommend (OR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.60–0.96), following up (OR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.48–0.81), and mDST use (OR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.41–0.83) were protective factors, while migrant populations (OR = 1.90, 95% CI: 1.35–2.74), comorbidities (OR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.04–1.75), initial treatment failure (OR = 1.94, 95% CI: 1.48–2.57), and other registration classifications (OR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.05–2.19) were risk factors. At the 60-day and 90-day cutoffs, homemaker/unemployed newly appeared as a protective factor (OR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.61–0.88; OR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.59–0.86), while returned cases became an additional risk factor (OR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.20–2.74; OR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.21–2.65). At the 90-day cutoff, referral/recommend and following up were no longer significant (P > 0.05).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses using median cut-off values (> 32 days for patient delay, > 15 days for health system delay, and > 86 days for total delay) are presented in Table 5. Compared with the original cut-offs (> 14 days, > 14 days, and > 28 days, respectively), the factors associated with patient delay remained unchanged. For health system delay, the association for students was no longer statistically significant (P > 0.05). Discussion

This study systematically evaluated diagnostic delays among RR-TB patients in Guizhou Province from 2019 to 2023. By categorizing diagnostic delay into patient delay, health system delay, and total delay, and applying three cut-off values for each category, we aimed to identify distinct factors influencing short-term versus long-term delays. Sensitivity analyses demonstrated that the choice of cut-off values significantly affected the results. We revealed the current status and temporal trends of different delay types and identified key determinants of delay, providing an important evidence base for drug-resistant tuberculosis prevention and control.

Our findings revealed a steady increase in the number of RR-TB cases detected during the study period. This upward trend aligns with Guizhou’s comprehensive implementation of the 13th Five-Year Plan for Tuberculosis Control, which mandated expanded screening, the introduction of molecular diagnostic tools such as mDST, and strengthened case detection systems [4]. These measures reflect notable improvements in surveillance coverage and detection capacity for DR-TB. However, the rising case numbers also highlight the need for continuous vigilance and resource investment to address the increasing burden of RR-TB.

Despite recent advancements, the median patient delay in this study was 32 days—longer than that reported in Bangladesh (28 days) [13] —and the proportion of delayed patients showed an upward trend. Most RR-TB patients in our cohort were farmers, a population often characterized by limited health literacy and lower healthcare-seeking motivation [24]. The province’s mountainous terrain, economic underdevelopment, and uneven distribution of healthcare resources further compound these challenges. Targeted health education campaigns, improved rural healthcare infrastructure, and strengthened early symptom recognition at the primary care level are therefore crucial [35, 36].

In contrast, both health system delay and total delay demonstrated a declining trend over the study years, suggesting progress in diagnostic efficiency. This improvement can largely be attributed to Guizhou’s implementation of a comprehensive TB control framework, including: (1) the widespread adoption of rapid diagnostic technologies such as GeneXpert to improve speed and accuracy; (2) the establishment of 12 designated MDR-TB hospitals and expert working groups providing regular training to clinicians; and (3) the inclusion of RR-TB under the provincial medical insurance program for chronic and special diseases, thereby enhancing treatment accessibility [37]. Nevertheless, the median health system delay (15 days) remained higher than that reported in Bangladesh (5 days) [13], and the median total delay (86 days) exceeded those in Hunan (27 days) [6] and Yunnan (29.5 days) [38]. These findings indicate persistent diagnostic bottlenecks after patients seek care, potentially linked to the late introduction and uneven implementation of mDST. In many counties, sputum samples collected at local TB facilities must be transported to city-level or provincial-level laboratories for DST, resulting in time-consuming processes involving transport, specimen registration, and result reporting.

Significant geographical disparities were also observed across regions. In Qianxinan Prefecture, most patients sought care within 14–60 days after symptom onset. This region has long faced a high TB burden, and although patients are motivated to seek care, limited local diagnostic capacity and informal medical practices often lead to moderate delays [39, 31]. Strengthening community-based TB education, early screening, and integrated referral systems in this area could mitigate mid-term delays. Conversely, in Tongren and Anshun, a substantial proportion of patients experienced delays exceeding 60 days, likely due to low-risk perception and mild initial symptoms affecting care-seeking behavior. Enhanced risk communication and symptom-based health management programs are needed to promote early consultation. Notably, Qiandongnan Prefecture consistently exhibited the highest rates of health system and total delays under all cut-off criteria, suggesting slower diagnostic responsiveness, warranting further investigation into system-level inefficiencies.

In this study, diagnostic delays among RR-TB patients were influenced by multiple factors across different cut-off values. Our analysis revealed that certain variables were significant only at specific thresholds, demonstrating the staged nature of diagnostic delay.

For patient delay at the > 14-day cut-off, students and those in other occupations were less likely to experience delay than farmers, possibly due to higher tuberculosis knowledge among students and regular health monitoring in service industries [35, 40]. Conversely, referral/recommend, initial treatment failure, and concomitant tuberculosis were associated with significantly greater patient delay. This is unlikely attributable to mild symptoms, but rather to complex disease presentations, atypical symptoms, or multiple referrals across departments requiring repeated queuing [6, 41], thereby prolonging the time to presentation. Therefore, strengthening early symptom recognition and referral capacity at primary healthcare institutions for drug-resistant tuberculosis patients with previous treatment history or multiple comorbidities is warranted, alongside establishing tracing and follow-up reminder mechanisms for patients with comorbidities or treatment failure to reduce long-term patient delay. Additionally, homemaker/unemployment was a protective factor at the 30-day and 60-day cut-offs, possibly because most patients seek care only when their health is substantially compromised [31], and because houseworkers/unemployed individuals—who more often reside in urban communities—may experience widening gaps in social support and community medical intervention compared with farmers as symptoms persist or worsen. This indirectly underscores the urgency of improving health literacy among farmers. Notably, return after default was a risk factor only at the > 60-day long-term delay threshold, with no statistical significance at the > 14-day short-term threshold. This suggests that returned patients do not initially delay care-seeking, but rather experience prolonged diagnostic processes when re-entering the healthcare system after treatment interruption. Existing literature indicates that patients with previous treatment interruption are more likely to face pathway disruptions or process delays during diagnosis or treatment initiation even when they re-seek care [42]; re-presenting patients often require additional steps to achieve definitive diagnosis due to disease progression, prior treatment experience, and complicated care pathways [6]; moreover, lack of social support, insufficient physician-patient communication, and weak awareness of standardized treatment are common barriers contributing to longer diagnostic pathways in this population [43]. In contrast, comorbidities were significant across all patient delay cut-offs, indicating that patients with comorbidities face persistent barriers to care from symptom onset, necessitating early intervention.

In health system delay, the OR for migrants increased with higher thresholds, suggesting that migrants are more likely to experience prolonged diagnostic processes after initial presentation due to difficulties in follow-up and delayed result feedback. That is, the amplifying effect of mobility on diagnostic delay manifests primarily in long-term system delay rather than short-term identification delay. Enhanced systematic support and follow-up management for migrant patients are recommended, such as developing intelligent navigation systems that push examination reminders, result notifications, and referral pathways to reduce human-induced delays. Compared with farmers, students were a risk factor only at the > 14-day threshold, with no significance at longer thresholds, indicating that students face diagnostic barriers—such as inadequate school medical officer recognition and poor referral processes—primarily within 14 to 30 days after presentation, without elevated delay risk thereafter. This suggests that school medical officers and primary healthcare institutions should receive enhanced training in tuberculosis identification, referral, and tracing for student populations to shorten post-presentation diagnostic time. Initial treatment failure and other registration categories were risk factors only at the > 60-day threshold, possibly related to economic hardship, poor adherence, and psychological barriers stemming from previous treatment experience in these patients [44], reflecting the complexity and management difficulty of their care pathways. We recommend establishing a revisit reminder system within 30 days for patients with initial treatment failure, alongside intensified patient management and health education to improve adherence. Our study also found that ethnic minorities experienced greater delays than the Han population, possibly associated with relatively weak medical resource allocation and behavioral/lifestyle patterns in minority areas [45]. Additionally, phenotypic DST was associated with greater health system delay than mDST. mDST technology can substantially shorten detection time from the 63 days required for conventional drug susceptibility testing to same-day results, facilitating early detection and treatment of RR-TB [46]. We recommend vigorously promoting and accelerating mDST deployment in counties and districts where it is not yet available, particularly in ethnic minority concentrated areas.

Results for total delay further corroborated these staged characteristics. Return after default was a risk factor at the > 60-day and > 90-day thresholds; comorbidities were significant across all thresholds; the OR for migrants continued to rise with increasing thresholds; and housework/unemployment became a protective factor at longer thresholds. Furthermore, referral and tracing were protective factors at the > 28-day threshold but no longer significant at the > 90-day threshold, suggesting that these measures primarily reduce early total delay, with limited effect on extreme long-term delays.

This study has several limitations. First, the date of symptom onset was based on patient self-report and may be subject to recall bias. Second, some records in the Tuberculosis Information Management System contained missing or logically inconsistent data and were excluded, which may have affected the accuracy of our findings. Third, this study did not incorporate treatment delay (the interval from diagnosis to treatment initiation) into total delay, potentially underestimating the overall delay burden and its impact on prognosis. Fourth, data were derived from the Tuberculosis Information Management System, which lacks key variables such as disease severity (e.g., radiographic cavitation, bacterial load), income level, and healthcare accessibility, limiting our ability to conduct more comprehensive analyses and potentially confounding the observed associations. Fifth, despite these limitations, the large sample size and multi-threshold delay analysis provide a robust evidence base for developing targeted interventions.

Conclusion

Between 2019 and 2023, health system and total delays among RR-TB patients in Guizhou Province decreased, whereas patient delay increased with significant regional disparities. Targeted measures are warranted: intelligent follow-up systems for migrants to reduce prolonged health system delays; revisit reminders for initial treatment failure and streamlined re-enrollment for returnees after default; multimorbidity screening with expedited referral for comorbid patients; and enhanced school medical officer training for student populations. Accelerated deployment of mDST in primary care and ethnic minority areas is essential to improve early diagnosis and treatment outcomes, contributing to the goal of ending tuberculosis by 2035.
