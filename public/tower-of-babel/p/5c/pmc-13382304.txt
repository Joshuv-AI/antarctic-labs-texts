Honokiol attenuates neuroinflammation and enhances remyelination in mouse models of multiple sclerosis through PPARγ-mediated ERK/AKT signalling
Authors: Xueli Yu, Long Wei, Yanghao Zheng, Xueyu Qi, Xiaolin Mu, Qian Wang, Yuxin Hua, Qiyuan Chen, Yaman Zhang, Han Jiang, Ziyue Wang, Peiyan Ni, Wanjun Guo, Wei Deng, Tao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multiple sclerosis is characterized by inflammatory demyelination and insufficient myelin repair, which together drive progressive neurological impairment. While existing immunomodulatory treatments have demonstrated efficacy in reducing acute relapse frequency, they fail to address the critical deficits in oligodendrocyte precursor cell (OPC) differentiation and subsequent remyelination, leaving patients with irreversible neurological disability. This highlights the pressing necessity for pharmacological interventions that can simultaneously mitigate neuroinflammation and stimulate endogenous myelin regeneration. In the present work, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of honokiol, a naturally occurring bioactive polyphenol derived from Magnolia officinalis, using both experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) and cuprizone-induced demyelination paradigm. Our results demonstrate that honokiol improved myelin restoration and exerted potent anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing glial activation, modulating inflammatory cytokine profiles, and restoring lipid homeostasis. Transcriptomic analysis indicated a global downregulation of immune-related pathways, and significant upregulation of signalling cascades critically involved in myelination and oligodendrocyte maturation. Molecular dynamics simulations revealed that honokiol stably bound to cannabinoid receptors and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) receptor via energetically favourable interactions. In primary OPC cultures, honokiol directly facilitated the differentiation of OPC into mature oligodendrocytes. Pharmacological blockade of PPARγ eliminated both honokiol-induced OPC maturation and subsequent activation of the extracellular signal-regulated kinase/protein kinase B (ERK/AKT) signalling axis. Taken together, these findings indicate that honokiol attenuates neuroinflammation and may facilitate myelin repair through both immunomodulatory actions and direct effects on oligodendrocyte lineage cells, supporting its potential as a disease-modifying strategy for demyelinating disorders.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) represents a chronic immune-mediated neurological condition primarily characterized by neuroinflammation and demyelinating pathology. The advancement of this disease is associated with oligodendrocyte loss and progressive axonal injury throughout the central nervous system (CNS). Globally, MS affects over 2.8 million individuals and the disease exhibits a marked female predominance, with disease prevalence approximately twice as high in females as in males [1]. Typical clinical manifestations include sensory loss, impaired coordination, bladder dysfunction, and other neurological symptoms, all of which arising from demyelinating lesions. Current immunotherapies have shown substantial efficacy in reducing relapse frequency and mitigating acute attacks. However, these treatments do not adequately address persistent demyelination, leaving axons susceptible to progressive degenerative injury [2].

Remyelination is an endogenous repair process that generates new myelinating oligodendrocytes from oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPC) in response to demyelinating injury. Accumulating evidence has validated that efficient remyelination is essential for maintaining axonal integrity and neuronal physiological function, as confirmed in both MS animal models [3] and clinical patient cohorts [4]. However, remyelination efficiency progressively declines with ageing and prolonged disease duration [5], and currently approved therapies do not directly enhance this repair process. Therefore, therapeutic strategies capable of simultaneously suppressing autoimmune neuroinflammation and promoting endogenous remyelination remain urgently needed.

To date, no FDA-approved therapy is available that directly promotes myelin regeneration. This limitation largely reflects an incomplete understanding of molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying myelin regeneration. Recent studies have identified several key regulators of OPC differentiation and function, including Sox10 [6], mTOR [7], ERK-1/2 signalling [8], and the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) signalling axis [9]. These signalling networks play central roles in oligodendrocyte maturation, myelin sheath formation, and the repair of demyelinated lesions. Hence, pharmacological modulation of these pathways represents a compelling avenue to enhance endogenous remyelination and facilitating neurological recovery in demyelinating conditions.

Honokiol, a bioactive small molecule extracted from the bark of Magnolia officinalis, has been utilized in traditional clinical practice for centuries. Accumulating pharmacological studies have validated its diverse biofunctions, encompassing anti-tumour, anti-infection, and antioxidant activities, with favourable biological safety [10]. Its low molecular weight and strong lipophilicity enable honokiol to efficiently penetrate the blood-brain barrier. Such superior delivery property allows honokiol to exert prominent neuroprotective effects in various preclinical models of CNS disorders, such as neuropathic pain [11], depression [12], and cognitive impairment [13].

In the present work, we demonstrate that honokiol directly facilitate OPC differentiation and accelerating remyelination in two well-established mouse models of MS. Integrated transcriptomic, immunohistochemical, and PCR analyses reveal the broad immunomodulatory effects of honokiol, as evidenced by suppressed glial activation and inflammatory cytokine expression. Mechanistic studies further show that honokiol promotes OPC differentiation primarily through PPARγ activation, along with increased ERK and AKT phosphorylation. Collectively, these findings indicate that, through its dual effects on neuroinflammation and myelin repair, honokiol holds promise as a naturally derived candidate against demyelinating disorders such as MS.

Materials and methods

Animals

The EAE and cuprizone-induced demyelination models were established in 8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice. Primary OPCs were obtained from Sprague–Dawley rat embryos at gestation day 14. Animals were acquired from the Shanghai SLAC Laboratory and kept in a specific pathogen-free barrier facility with free access to standard chow and drinking water. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University (approval No. ZJU20230382) and performed in strict accordance with the National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Drug administration

For in vivo studies, honokiol (Selleck, USA, S2310) and clemastine (Selleck, S1847) were formulated in a vehicle containing Tween 80, DMSO, and normal saline at a 1:1:8 vol ratio and administered by intraperitoneal injection. The same vehicle was used for control injections. For in vitro studies, 10 mM stock solutions of honokiol, AM251 (MCE, USA, HY-15443), AM630 (Selleck, S8033), and T0070907 (MCE, HY-13202) were first prepared in DMSO and subsequently diluted with culture medium to working concentrations.

EAE induction and treatment

To induce EAE, 8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were immunised subcutaneously on the dorsal flank with 200 μg of MOG35–55 peptide emulsified in complete Freund’s adjuvant (Millipore Sigma, USA, F5881) that was enriched with 100 μg of heat-killed Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37Ra (BD Difco, 231141). The immunizing emulsion was freshly prepared in PBS. Pertussis toxin (200 ng) was given by intraperitoneal injection on days 0 and 2. Sham-immunised controls were immunised with CFA and PBS without MOG35-55 peptide. From the first day of clinical onset, each mouse was given a daily intraperitoneal dose of fingolimod (1 mg/kg), honokiol (5 or 10 mg/kg) or vehicle, continuing until day 30. Body weight was recorded daily, and neurological deficits were assessed independently by two investigators using the standard EAE scale: 0, no clinical signs; 1, mild hindlimb weakness; 2, tail atony accompanied by hind limb weakness; 3, hind limb partial paralysis; 4, hind limb complete paralysis; 5, moribund or death.

Induction of demyelination and treatment

A five-week course of 0.2% (w/w) cuprizone (S30349, Orileaf, China) mixed into powdered chow was employed to trigger demyelination in mice. After this demyelinating phase, animals were switched back to a standard diet and allowed to undergo a three-week recovery period, during which vehicle, 10 mg/kg clemastine or honokiol was delivered daily via intraperitoneal injection. To evaluate pathological changes during demyelination and subsequent remyelination, subsets of mice were euthanised at weekly intervals. Each animal was terminally anaesthetised with pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, i.p.), then subjected to transcranial perfusion with physiological saline. Whole brains were subsequently dissected and prepared for histological evaluation.

Primary OPC culture and treatment

OPCs were isolated using an adapted protocol derived from a previously published method [14]. Neural stem cells were harvested from the hippocampal tissue of embryonic day 14 rats and expanded in vitro for 7 days. The resulting neurospheres were subsequently dissociated with accutase and plated in oligodendrocyte differentiation medium for 48 h. After removal of bFGF and PDGF-AA, cultures were treated with honokiol, AM630, AM251, or T0070907 for 3 days prior to analysis of OPC differentiation. All antagonists were used at a final concentration of 1 μM, a dose validated in prior studies to achieve selective and complete target inhibition without inducing non-specific cytotoxicity [15,16].

Histopathological analysis

Thoracic and lumbar spinal cord segments were harvested, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Inflammatory infiltration was assessed on haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) -stained sections (Beyotime, Shanghai, C0105) and graded using a 4-point scale: 0, no detectable inflammatory cells; 1, scattered cellular infiltrates; 2, moderate infiltration; 3, dense inflammatory cell accumulation. Myelin integrity and the extent of demyelination were evaluated by Luxol fast blue staining (Beyotime, C0633). The extent of demyelination was semi-quantitatively assessed by calculating the ratio of demyelinated area to the total white matter area within each section.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

On day 30 post-immunisation, white matter from the thoracolumbar spinal cord was collected for ultrastructural assessment. Samples were initially fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and then embedded in epoxy resin. 70 nm sections were cut and counterstained with 3% uranyl acetate and 2.7% lead citrate. Imaging was performed on a JEM1400 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). Axon diameters and myelin sheath thickness were quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software. The g-ratio was defined as the inner axonal diameter divided by the total fibre diameter.

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue sections and cultured OPCs were first washed with PBS, then permeabilised in 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Nonspecific binding was blocked with 10% goat serum, after which samples were probed with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. On the next day, samples were washed with PBS and incubated with fluorophore-labelled secondary antibodies for 1 h at ambient temperature, then mounted onto glass slides. Fluorescence labelling was imaged on either a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 900; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) or a whole-slide scanner (SLIDEVIEW VS200, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Quantification of fluorescence intensity was carried out with Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software.

Western blotting

Primary OPCs were homogenised in ice-cold RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors, and protein concentrations were measured by a bicinchoninic acid assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels, transferred to PVDF membranes, and blocked with 5% non-fat milk. Blots were then probed with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Membranes were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Chemiluminescent signals were generated with chemiluminescence reagents and captured on a ChemoCam Imager (Amersham ImageQuant 800; Cytiva). Antibody details are listed in Supplemental Table S1.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Spinal cords of mice were dissected, and total RNA was isolated and quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, the integrity was verified on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Libraries were prepared using the Universal V10 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit and subjected to 150-bp paired-end sequencing on a DNBSEQ-T7 instrument. Library preparation and sequencing were performed by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Raw reads were processed with fastp to trim adaptors and filter out low-quality reads, yielding clean datasets for all downstream analyses. Differential gene expression was analysed with the DESeq2 package in R. Genes exhibiting |fold change| > 2 with an adjusted P value < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed for Gene Ontology (GO) categories and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways using hypergeometric testing in R (v3.2.0). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was also carried out to identify significantly enriched biological pathways.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from spinal cord tissues using TRIzol reagent, then reverse-transcribed into cDNA with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Kyoto, Japan). Quantitative PCR assays were performed using a SYBR Green-based detection system (Sparkjade, Qingdao, China, AH0104), with each sample run in triplicate. Primer sequences are listed in Supplemental Table S2. Relative transcript levels were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH included as the internal reference gene.

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations

Three-dimensional structures of PPARγ (ID: 2G0H), CB1 (ID: 5U09), and CB2 (ID: 8GUT) were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank. Prior to docking, each receptor was stripped of its bound ligands and crystallographic water molecules using PyMOL. The honokiol structure was constructed and subjected to energy minimization with the MMFF94 force field [17]. Ligand–receptor docking was performed using LeDock. For each complex, the top-scoring pose was subjected to molecular dynamics simulations with GROMACS under the CHARMM36 force field. The simulation systems were solvated in a TIP3P water box, neutralized, and adjusted to a physiological ionic strength (0.15 M). Following energy minimization, the systems were equilibrated under NVT and NPT ensembles, followed by 100 ns production simulations, and then analysed with GROMACS tools for Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), radius of gyration, and hydrogen bond occupancy. Free energy landscapes were constructed using RMSD and radius of gyration as reaction coordinates. Visualization was carried out using PyMOL and MD analysis tools [18]. Custom scripts and DulvyTools v0.6.0 were used for additional analyses and visualization.

Data analyses

Longitudinal EAE clinical scores and body weight were analysed by two-way repeated-measures ANOVA. Non-parametric data were examined using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s procedure for multiple comparisons. All other group were assessed by one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post hoc correction. Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and were analysed using GraphPad Prism 8 (San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

In parallel, undifferentiated OPC marker PDGFRα was not significantly affected by honokiol (Fig. 1E). Honokiol attenuates EAE severity and promotes remyelination

Based on in vitro findings, we evaluated honokiol’s translational potential in the EAE model. Fingolimod, an FDA-approved therapy for MS, was used as a positive comparator. Daily administration of honokiol, fingolimod, or vehicle began at the first onset of neurological signs (day 11) and continued through day 30. From day 14 onwards, both honokiol- and fingolimod-treated groups showed steady improvement in clinical deficits. At the peak of illness (day 19), 70% of vehicle-injected animals developed severe hindlimb weakness or paralysis (EAE score ≥2.5), whereas only 20% of mice receiving honokiol (5 or 10 mg/kg) exhibited severe signs (Fig. 2A). Fingolimod exerted the strongest therapeutic effect, with no mice in this group reached severe clinical scores. Consistent with the neurological findings, vehicle-treated mice displayed a significant decline in body weight from day 15 onward relative to control mice (Fig. 2B). We then assessed how honokiol affects inflammatory infiltration and myelination. H&E staining revealed extensive inflammatory cell accumulation within the white matter of vehicle-treated EAE mice. Honokiol attenuated inflammatory infiltration, while fingolimod exerted a more pronounced inhibitory effect (Fig. 2C and D). In line with these findings, LFB staining showed extensive demyelination in the vehicle-treated group. Honokiol (10 mg/kg) or fingolimod significantly attenuated this demyelination (Fig. 2E and F). TEM confirmed that vehicle-treated mice had thinner myelin sheaths and fewer myelinated axons compared with controls. Both honokiol and fingolimod significantly preserved myelin sheath thickness (Fig. 2G and H). Transcriptomic analysis reveals anti-inflammatory and pro-remyelinating signalling of honokiol in EAE mice

To elucidate the molecular basis of honokiol’s therapeutic activity in EAE, transcriptomic profiling was performed using spinal cord samples from mice treated with honokiol or vehicle. In the honokiol group, differential expression analysis identified 2412 genes using the cutoffs of P < 0.05 and |fold change| > 2, of which 2095 were downregulated and 317 upregulated. (Fig. 3A). GSEA based on the KEGG database identified 51 significantly enriched upregulated pathways and 111 downregulated pathways. Notably, several upregulated pathways associated with the positive regulation of OPC maturation were enriched, including retrograde endocannabinoid, cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), ErbB, and calcium signalling (Fig. 3B). Among the downregulated pathways, in addition to cholesterol metabolism, several were associated with immunomodulatory processes, including the T-cell receptor signalling pathway, chemokine signalling pathway, cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, NF-κB signalling, IL-17 signalling, TNF signalling, and natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity (Fig. 3C). The downregulated genes were predominantly enriched in immune response and cytokine activity-related pathways, as determined by GO analysis. (Fig. 3D). Consistently, KEGG pathway analysis further supported the enrichment of immune and inflammatory signalling pathways (Fig. 3E). Honokiol modulates neuroinflammation, lipid dysregulation, and myelin maintenance in EAE mice

To further elucidate the therapeutic mechanisms of honokiol in EAE, we performed immunostaining for microglia (Iba-1) and astrocytes (GFAP) (Fig. 4A). Quantification revealed substantially higher counts of both Iba-1 and GFAP-positive cells in EAE mice relative to controls, and it was significantly attenuated by honokiol treatment (Fig. 4B and C). We subsequently performed a systematic RT-PCR analysis of cytokine profiles, lipid metabolism-related genes, and myelin-related markers. The expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including Tnf-α, Il-6, Ifn-γ, and Il-1β were significantly upregulated in EAE mice, and these changes were reversed by honokiol treatment (Fig. 4D–G). In contrast, Il-4 expression remained unchanged across groups (Fig. 4H). Although anti-inflammatory cytokines including Il-10, Tgf-β, and Arg1 were elevated in EAE mice, their expression was significantly reduced by honokiol treatment (Fig. 4I–K). Notably, except for Cpta1 (Fig. 4L), lipid metabolism regulators Cd36, ApoE, and Abca1, along with the pro-apoptotic factor Bax, were elevated in EAE mice, and honokiol treatment markedly attenuated these changes (Fig. 4M–P). Among myelin-related genes, Mbp expression remained stable across groups (Fig. 4Q), whereas the EAE-induced downregulation of Plp was effectively restored by honokiol (Fig. 4R). Honokiol promotes remyelination in the cuprizone-induced model of demyelination

Demyelination was established by feeding mice a diet formulated with 0.2% (w/w) cuprizone for five weeks. Upon cuprizone withdrawal, animals received daily intraperitoneal injections of normal saline, honokiol, or clemastine, a known remyelination-promoting agent for 1–3 weeks (Fig. 5A). Remyelination efficacy was assessed by immunofluorescence analysis of myelin-related markers (Bcas1, SOX10, CC1, Olig2, and MBP) within corpus callosum (Fig. 5B–G). After 5 weeks of cuprizone feeding, a marked elevation was observed in the number of actively myelinating oligodendrocytes, identified as Sox10 and Bcas1 double positive cells (Fig. 5C), whereas the numbers of Olig2-positive cells (Fig. 5D), CC1-positive mature oligodendrocytes (Fig. 5E), and MBP expression were significantly reduced (Fig. 5F), confirming effective demyelination. Molecular dynamics simulation reveals differential binding stability of honokiol with CB1, CB2, and PPARγ receptors

Honokiol is known to function as an agonist at CB1, CB2, and PPARγ receptors [19,20], each of which has been implicated in the regulation of oligodendrocyte differentiation [21,22]. To investigate the conformational dynamics of honokiol within each binding pocket, 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations were carried out for the three complexes. RMSD analysis revealed markedly different stability profiles among the complexes (Fig. 6A), each of which attained equilibrium during the simulation. The CB1–honokiol complex exhibited the highest RMSD during the final 20 ns (0.53 ± 0.03 nm), indicating greater conformational flexibility. PPARγ showed intermediate RMSD values (0.40 ± 0.03 nm), while CB2 exhibited the lowest RMSD (0.34 ± 0.02 nm), consistent with a more rigid complex. Radius of gyration (Rg) analysis further corroborated these findings (Fig. 6B). CB1 maintained the largest Rg (2.96 ± 0.05 nm), consistent with its dynamic RMSD profile. In contrast, CB2 (2.11 ± 0.01 nm) and PPARγ (1.93 ± 0.01 nm) adopted more compact conformations, indicative of greater structural rigidity. Evaluation of short-range Coulombic (Coul-SR) and Lennard–Jones (LJ-SR) interaction energies revealed distinct energetic signatures among the receptor–honokiol pairs. The CB1 complex exhibited the strongest Coul-SR interactions (−36.3 ± 10.5 kJ/mol over the final 20 ns), approximately 2.4-fold greater than those observed for CB2 (−15.2 ± 7.0 kJ/mol) or PPARγ (−14.1 ± 9.1 kJ/mol). Similarly, LJ-SR interactions were more favourable for CB1 (−162.5 ± 8.8 kJ/mol) and CB2 (−158.9 ± 9.4 kJ/mol) than for PPARγ (−134.9 ± 8.8 kJ/mol). Taken together, these interaction profiles suggest that honokiol forms more stable interactions with cannabinoid receptors than with PPARγ (Fig. 6C and D).

Free energy landscape analysis revealed distinct conformational stability profiles among the receptor–honokiol complexes. The CB1 complex exhibited a broad energy basin, indicative of enhanced conformational flexibility and dynamic ligand binding (Fig. 6E), In contrast, CB2 and PPARγ displayed narrower energy basins, reflecting more rigid and stable binding modes (Fig. 6G–I). Analysis of interactions across 5000 molecular dynamics trajectory frames revealed that honokiol stabilizes its binding primarily through hydrophobic contacts, particularly those mediated by aromatic residues. Additional contributions come from hydrogen bonding—especially prominent in CB1 and PPARγ—and π–π stacking interactions (Supplementary Figs. S1–S3). Further analysis of the lowest-energy conformations further demonstrated that honokiol interacts with its binding pockets through synergistic hydrophobic, van der Waals, hydrogen-bonding, and π–stacking interactions with critical residues (Fig. 6F, H, J; Supplementary Fig. S4).

Honokiol promotes OPC differentiation primarily through PPARγ activation

To determine which receptor mediates the pro-differentiation effects of honokiol, we performed pharmacological inhibition experiments, differentiating OPCs were treated with honokiol (5 μM) alone or combined with antagonists of CB1 (AM251, 1 μM), CB2 (AM630, 1 μM), or PPARγ (T0070907, 1 μM) for 72 h. Immunocytochemical analysis showed that none of the treatments significantly altered the proportion of Olig2+ cells. However, application of the CB1, CB2, or PPARγ antagonist alone reduced the fraction of MBP+ cells. Crucially, honokiol-induced increase in MBP+ cells was significantly abolished only when the PPARγ antagonist T0070907 was co-applied (Fig. 7A–C). Conversely, co-administration of honokiol with either the AM251 or the AM630 did not significantly alter the protein levels of PDGFRα, MBP, or MAG. Honokiol induces PPARγ-dependent ERK and Akt activation in primary OPCs

The ERK1/2 and Akt/mTOR signalling cascades serve as key regulators of oligodendrocyte differentiation and myelination, and earlier work has documented the ability of honokiol to influence these pathways [23,24]. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying honokiol-induced oligodendrocyte maturation, we examined the temporal phosphorylation dynamics of these pathways in differentiating OPC cultures. Treatment with 5 μM honokiol induced distinct temporal phosphorylation patterns. ERK1/2 phosphorylation increased in a progressive manner, reaching a peak at 25 min (Fig. 8A and B). In contrast, Akt phosphorylation at Ser473 showed a rapid increase within 5 min, remained elevated until 20 min, and then declined towards baseline levels by 25 min (Fig. 8C and D). Notably, mTOR phosphorylation at Ser2448 remained unchanged throughout the 25-min treatment period (Fig. 8E and F). Discussion

Here, we demonstrate that honokiol acts as a dual-function therapeutic agent by coupling immunomodulation with remyelination. Mechanistic analyses revealed that honokiol enhances OPC differentiation primarily through PPARγ receptor activation, followed by the induction of downstream ERK/Akt signalling pathways. Taken together, these results underscore the therapeutic promise of honokiol for demyelinating neurodegenerative disorders such as MS.

Current immunotherapies for MS often fail to stimulate OPC maturation, thereby limiting remyelination and functional recovery despite the abundant presence of OPC within demyelinating lesions [25]. Our data demonstrate that honokiol directly stimulates OPC differentiation both in vitro and in vivo, as shown in the cuprizone demyelination paradigm. Honokiol treatment led to a marked expansion of actively myelinating oligodendrocytes, as evidenced by elevated numbers of Sox10+/Bcas1+ double-positive cells, followed by an increase in CC1+ mature oligodendrocytes and elevated MBP levels. Notably, compared with clemastine, a known inducer of OPC differentiation, honokiol induced more robust remyelination while preserving the OPC reservoir, as the number of Olig2+ oligodendrocyte lineage cells was not significantly altered. This feature is clinically relevant, as maintenance of an adequate OPC reservoir is essential for sustained remyelination. In contrast, prolonged clemastine administration (8 weeks) has been reported to inhibit OPC proliferation, leading to OPC pool depletion and cellular senescence [26]. Furthermore, a recent clinical trial in patients with MS reported accelerated disability progression following clemastine treatment, and cerebrospinal fluid analysis revealed enhanced purinergic signalling and pyroptosis, ultimately leading to early termination of this treatment arm [27]. In contrast, honokiol has been shown to attenuate pyroptosis through regulation of the SIRT3-mediated NLRP3 inflammasome [28], suggesting a potentially more favourable efficacy and safety profile compared with clemastine.

EAE represents a widely employed preclinical model for studying MS because it recapitulates major pathological features of the disease, including immune-driven demyelination and neuroaxonal injury [29]. In the current study, honokiol markedly attenuated clinical severity in EAE mice, reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, and alleviated demyelination. Although its effects on clinical scores and inflammatory infiltration were not superior to those of fingolimod, it uniquely increased the number of myelinated axons, which was not evident in fingolimod-treated animals. These observations suggests that honokiol may exert additional pro-regenerative actions beyond its immunomodulatory effects.

Transcriptomic profiling further supported this interpretation. Both GSEA and KEGG enrichment analyses demonstrated suppression of multiple inflammation-associated pathways following honokiol administration, particularly those related to T cell receptor signalling, chemokine signalling, chemokine activity, cytokine interactions, and natural killer cell-mediated responses. Consistent with these transcriptional signatures, honokiol-treated mice exhibited lower numbers of Iba1+ microglia and GFAP+ astrocytes within the CNS. Cytokine profiling revealed that EAE induction resulted in broad activation of immune signalling, affecting both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory mediators. Interestingly, honokiol treatment restored many of these altered cytokines towards baseline levels rather than selectively inhibiting only pro-inflammatory factors, suggesting a re-establishment of immune balance.

In addition to its immunomodulatory effects, honokiol restored disrupted lipid metabolic homeostasis in EAE mice. Treatment with honokiol reversed the abnormal expression of several lipid-associated genes, including Cd36, ApoE, and Abca1. Increased Cd36 expression has previously been linked to impaired myelin debris clearance [30], and dysregulation of ApoE and Abca1 is commonly associated with impaired reverse cholesterol transport in demyelinating conditions [31]. The transcriptomic data further supported these findings, as cholesterol metabolism-related pathways were reduced following honokiol administration. Given that Cd36, ApoE, and Abca1 are recognised downstream targets of PPARγ, these results suggest that restoration of lipid homeostasis by honokiol may involve PPARγ-mediated transcriptional regulation. Such effects may contribute to the broader therapeutic actions of honokiol beyond suppression of neuroinflammation.

GSEA also identified increased activity in signalling cascades associated with endocannabinoid, cAMP, ErbB, and calcium signalling, all of which are critical regulators of oligodendrocyte lineage progression and myelin formation. Previous studies have shown that activation of endocannabinoid signalling [32], together with cAMP/ERK1/2 signalling [33] facilitates OPC maturation and remyelination. ErbB signalling has been implicated in myelin maintenance and cognitive function through regulation of oligodendrocyte metabolism [31]. In parallel, calcium signalling plays a dynamic role throughout OPC proliferation, differentiation, and myelin sheath assembly by mature oligodendrocytes [34]. Morphological observations were consistent with these pathway-level changes, as honokiol-treated mice exhibited thicker myelin sheaths and a greater proportion of myelinated axons. Interestingly, honokiol selectively restored the reduction in Plp transcription induced by EAE, whereas Mbp levels remained unchanged. Although both PLP and MBP are major constituents of myelin, their biosynthetic processing differs substantially. MBP is translated predominantly through local ribosomal mechanisms, while PLP undergoes ER-dependent processing and trafficking, making it more vulnerable to ER stress and Bax-associated oligodendrocyte injury [35]. In this context, the simultaneous restoration of Plp expression and reduction in Bax expression following honokiol treatment may indicate an additional protective effect on ER homeostasis and oligodendrocyte survival.

Honokiol has previously been identified as a ligand for CB1, CB2 [36] and PPAR-γ receptors [37]. While a recent study reported that honokiol ameliorates white matter injury via CB1-dependent AKT/mTOR/SOX10 activation [38], our findings reveal a different mechanism. One possible explanation may relate to differences in culture conditions, particularly the inclusion of exogenous differentiation-promoting factors. In the previous study, OPC cultures were supplemented with 10 ng/mL ciliary neurotrophic factor together with 15 μM triiodothyronine (T3), both of which are well-established drivers of OPC differentiation. Importantly, even substantially lower concentrations of T3 have been reported to activate AKT/mTOR and ERK signalling pathways [39]. In contrast, our basal culture system enabled us to isolate and reveal the intrinsic pro-differentiation effects of honokiol. Under these conditions, honokiol activated ERK/AKT signalling without engaging the mTOR pathway, and neither CB1 nor CB2 receptor antagonism attenuated honokiol-induced myelin protein expression and ERK/Akt phosphorylation. Collectively, these results establish PPARγ as the primary mediator of honokiol’s promyelinating effects.

The present study has several limitations. First, only female mice were used to establish both the EAE and cuprizone-induced demyelination models, reflecting the markedly higher prevalence of MS in females, which is approximately twofold greater than in males. Nevertheless, the potential influence of sex-specific factors, including gonadal hormones, on honokiol’s therapeutic efficacy cannot be excluded and should be addressed in future studies. Second, honokiol is a pleiotropic small molecule with multiple reported molecular targets, including PPARγ, cannabinoid receptors, gamma-aminobutyric acid receptors, and glutamate receptors. Although our data identify PPARγ as a key mediator of honokiol-induced OPC differentiation, the extent to which these additional targets contribute to the observed in vivo effects remains incompletely defined and warrants further investigation using receptor-specific pharmacological tools or genetic approaches. Third, in the EAE model, the observed increase in myelinated axon numbers following honokiol treatment may reflect not only enhanced remyelination but also reduced demyelination and axonal loss secondary to its immunomodulatory and neuroprotective effects. Because inflammatory activity directly influences the extent of myelin damage in EAE, this model does not allow a definitive distinction between myelin protection and myelin repair. Future studies using lineage-tracing strategies or conditional genetic approaches will be required to further delineate these mechanisms.

Collectively, honokiol shows promising preclinical potential as a multi-target therapeutic agent for MS. Its pharmacological actions may involve several complementary mechanisms. Activation of CB1 receptors has been associated with anxiolytic and antiallodynic effects in previous studies [40,41], whereas CB2 receptor signalling appears to participate in immune regulation through correction of the Th17/Treg imbalance that contributes to MS pathogenesis [42,43]. In parallel, growing evidence suggests that metabolic disturbances, particularly mitochondrial dysfunction and abnormal lipid metabolism, play important roles in disease progression in MS [44]. PPARγ activation is therefore of particular interest because of its reported ability to suppress inflammatory responses in macrophages and microglia, regulate lipid metabolism, facilitate OPC differentiation, and protect oligodendrocytes against inflammation-associated mitochondrial damage [45]. The broad spectrum of these actions may explain the simultaneous anti-inflammatory and pro-remyelinating effects observed in the present study. Despite these promising properties, the current use of honokiol remains largely limited to cosmetic and nutraceutical applications [46].

Overall, our findings indicate that honokiol exerts substantial protective effects as a multi-target therapeutic intervention for MS. It exhibits dual efficacy by potently attenuating neuroinflammation while robustly promoting remyelination. These pro-regenerative effects suggest that honokiol may offer broad therapeutic potential for demyelinating disorders characterised by both inflammatory and regenerative deficits.
