Recurrent croup in a young child from a low- to middle-income setting
Authors: P Goussard, M Merven, A Gie
Published: 2026

A 13-month-old HIV-negative boy presented with his second episode of ‘croup’ within 6 weeks. He had previously been well, had never been intubated, and had no history of stridor. He required hospital admission and needed intubation because of progressive airway obstruction. Th s was performed in theatre using a video laryngoscope, and only a size 3.0 mm endotracheal tube could be passed. After 5 days of ventilation, no air leak was visible. A chest radiograph was normal, with no evidence of airway pathology.

Extubation was performed under bronchoscopic vision in theatre. The distal airways and subglottic area were normal; however, white wart-like tissue was visible on the vocal cords and in the anterior commissure, suggestive of human papillomavirus (HPV) papillomatosis. The papillomas were removed using rigid forceps, and an adequate airway was created. He was successfully extubated.

The mother confi med that genital viral papillomas were present at the time of delivery and reported that the child’s voice had been hoarse from 6 months of age. HPV infection was histologically confi med. Oral prednisolone was administered for 5 days, and the child also received Gardasil 9 vaccine.

Several missed opportunities are evident in this case. Primary prevention was inadequate, as the mother did not receive multivalent HPV vaccination prior to pregnancy – of note is that the bivalent vaccines (HPV 16 and 18) do not protect against the HPV strains of genital papilloma or juvenile-onset recurrent respiratory papillomatosis. During pregnancy, early diagnosis and appropriate treatment of maternal papillomas were suboptimal, and counselling regarding the risk of vertical transmission was not undertaken. Intrapartum preventive strategies were limited; although caesarean delivery is not routinely recommended, delivery planning could have been considered in the presence of extensive genital papillomas to reduce neonatal exposure. The absence of follow-up for the infant resulted in delayed diagnosis of laryngeal papillomatosis. During infancy, the presence of a persistent hoarse voice was not recognised as a reason for referral for further investigation.
