“It’s not about being a parent-therapist, but simply a parent”: the silent pillars of autism care - a qualitative study of fathers’ experiences
Authors: Jan Domaradzki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite increasing attention to autism caregiving, the emotional and relational experiences of fathers remain insufficiently understood. This study explores how caregiving shapes fathers’ emotional experiences, close relationships, and paternal identity within the context of autism.MethodsThis qualitative descriptive study draws on 14 in-depth interviews with fathers actively involved in raising autistic children. The interviews were analysed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis, following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework.ResultsFive major themes were identified: (1) Conceptions of fatherhood, (2) Personal understanding of autism, (3) Living with autism, (4) Emotional and psychological well-being, and (5) Fathers’ needs and recognition. Participants framed fatherhood as a morally anchored, emotionally engaged role that reshaped their identity and values. Despite high levels of fatigue, anticipatory anxiety, and social withdrawal, many participants emphasized emotional growth, pride in their children’s progress, and the deep satisfaction derived from close relationships. Autism was often reframed not as a deficit, but as a form of difference – a perspective that supported greater acceptance, resilience, and meaning-making. Fathers frequently reported feelings of social isolation and marginalization, including exclusion from extended family life, public spaces, and professional systems. Fathers called for greater recognition and gender-sensitive support that would reflect their lived experiences and the emotional labour of caregiving.ConclusionFathers of autistic children are key emotional and relational caregivers, yet they remain structurally marginalized. Findings underscore the need for gender-inclusive, father-sensitive support systems that validate paternal experiences and integrate fathers more fully into autism care and policy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08008-x.

Background

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD; hereafter referred to as “autism”; ICD-10: F84; ICD-11: 6A02; DSM-5: F84.9) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent difficulties in social interaction and communication, along with restricted, repetitive behaviours and interests [1, 2]. Now understood as a spectrum disorder, autism includes formerly separate diagnoses such as autism and Asperger’s syndrome, reflecting wide variability in symptom severity and functioning.

Symptoms typically emerge in early childhood, often within the first year, and persist throughout a person’s life [3]. Autistic children may exhibit communication challenges, limited social awareness, and repetitive behaviours, and, in some cases, behaviours such as self-injury or aggression, which can significantly impact family life [4]. Co-occurring conditions, e.g., intellectual disability, ADHD, epilepsy, sleep and mood disorders, gastrointestinal issues, and allergies, are common [5].

According to the World Health Organization, autism affects around 1 in 36 children [6], with higher prevalence in boys, and is the leading cause of disability among children under five [7, 8]. Early diagnosis and interdisciplinary intervention significantly improve outcomes, especially when family support and parental education are included.

Raising an autistic child involves complex challenges that impact nearly all aspects of family life, including caregivers’ health, relationships, and overall well-being [9–11]. It often reshapes parental roles, strains partnerships, and disrupts sibling dynamics. Many parents experience caregiver burden, both objective (physical demands) and subjective (emotional strain), with the latter often having a greater psychological impact [12]. As with other special needs caregiving, the associated physical, emotional, financial, and social costs can be substantial.

Polish studies indicate elevated levels of parenting stress among parents of autistic children, with mothers reporting particularly high emotional burden, but fathers also experiencing significantly increased stress compared to parents of typically developing children [13–16]. At the same time, fathers of autistic children in Poland often report lower levels of perceived family empowerment and limited access to effective institutional support, pointing to structural constraints within the service system [17]. Empirical data further show reduced quality of life across psychological, social, and material domains among Polish parents of autistic children, with financial strain and employment instability emerging as strong predictors of parental well-being [10, 15, 18]. Access to specialised services remains uneven and frequently dependent on local resources and parental initiative, placing substantial responsibility on families to navigate complex institutional pathways [14].

Caregiving responsibilities in Poland remain strongly gendered. Mothers are typically positioned as primary caregivers and emotional anchors of the family, whereas fathers are more often associated with economic provision and interaction with formal institutions [19, 20]. Most Polish studies on families of autistic children have therefore focused predominantly on mothers’ coping strategies and stress, leaving fathers’ emotional and relational experiences comparatively underexplored [13, 14, 21].

Systemic characteristics of autism support in Poland further contextualise parental experiences. Although access to diagnostic and therapeutic services has improved, families frequently navigate fragmented diagnostic pathways and uneven access to long-term support services, particularly in adulthood [16, 21]. Research on disability assessment practices highlights additional structural challenges, including limited autism-specific expertise and asymmetrical power relations between families and institutions [22]. Such institutional conditions may intensify parental stress and contribute to feelings of marginalisation, particularly among fathers who often assume responsibility for navigating formal systems.

Despite the growing number of clinical studies on autism in Poland, in-depth research on family caregivers, particularly fathers, remains limited. Most Polish studies focus on clinical aspects or the psychosocial functioning of mothers [18–20, 23, 24]. As a result, the specific experiences and needs of fathers remain largely underexplored. Meanwhile, fathers of autistic children often face a complex emotional landscape involving grief, identity shifts, and ongoing adjustment. The diagnosis is commonly experienced as a psychological shock, triggering reactions such as denial, sadness, anger, or guilt. Many fathers go through a cyclical grieving process as they adjust to the diagnosis and redefine fatherhood in light of their child’s developmental trajectory [25–27].

Studies indicate that the early stages of adaptation are often marked by confusion, helplessness, isolation, and a search for alternative explanations or treatments [26, 28]. These emotional reactions may differ from those of mothers, frequently manifesting as internalized distress or frustration, with many fathers feeling societal pressure to remain composed – the “strong and silent” figure [29, 30]. This contributes to limited emotional expression and a reduced tendency to seek support [30, 31].

Although fathers’ caregiving responsibilities have increased in recent decades, through active involvement in daily routines and advocacy within healthcare and education, their contributions are still often overlooked by professionals and institutions that continue to prioritize mothers as primary caregivers [30, 32, 33]. Cultural norms and structural barriers reinforce traditional gender roles, forcing many fathers to navigate the tension between breadwinner expectations and their desire to be more engaged in caregiving [30, 34].

Fathers also report emotional strain rooted in perceived inadequacy, social comparison, and anticipatory grief about their child’s long-term independence and well-being [27, 35]. These challenges can lead to symptoms of depression, burnout, or a sense of personal loss, particularly when social or systemic support is lacking [36]. However, studies indicate that when fathers feel competent and supported, both their own well-being and their child’s developmental outcomes tend to improve significantly [34, 35].

Importantly, fathers bring unique emotional and behavioural strengths that can support their children’s resilience, including playfulness, emotional regulation, consistent structure, and physical presence [26, 30]. To fully harness these capacities, it is essential to involve fathers as equal partners in family-centred interventions [29, 33]. This study, therefore, explores how caregiving shapes fathers’ emotional experiences, close relationships, and paternal identity within the context of autism.

Methods

Study design

As no prior Polish studies have examined the experiences of fathers of autistic children, the study employed a Qualitative Descriptive design [37], which is particularly suitable for exploring under-researched, practice-oriented topics in participants’ own language. Although this study was conducted as part of a broader project examining fathers’ experiences of caring for an autistic child, the present analysis focuses specifically on how caregiving shapes fathers’ emotional experiences, close relationships, and paternal identity. In-depth interviews were conducted with fourteen fathers actively involved in caregiving. The study was guided by three research questions concerning how fathers experience caregiving, make sense of autism, and define their paternal identity.

The study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [38], which was used to ensure transparency of reporting, reflexivity, and analysis [39].

Ethical issues

The study followed the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [40]. Ethical approval was granted by Poznan University of Medical Sciences’ (PUMS) Bioethics Committee (KB – 569/25). In accordance with the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), personal information was separated from the research data.

Participants were informed that participation was voluntary, and they could withdraw at any time or skip any questions without consequences. Confidentiality, anonymization, and data protection procedures were explained in full, and verbal consent was recorded at the beginning of each interview.

Participants and setting

Participants were selected through purposive sampling, targeting fathers directly and actively involved in the care of an autistic child. Recruitment took place via Facebook pages of autism-related foundations and support groups, and was supplemented by snowball sampling.

Inclusion criteria were: age over 18; being a father of a child with a confirmed ASD diagnosis; at least five years of parenting experience; active caregiving involvement; fluency in Polish; access to the internet and basic digital literacy; and willingness to participate in an online interview with informed consent. The requirement of a minimum of five years of parenting experience was introduced to ensure that participants had accumulated sustained caregiving experience and had moved beyond the earliest phase of adjustment. Given that the study focused on paternal identity, emotional trajectories, and relational adaptation, this criterion was intended to enable reflective accounts grounded in longer-term engagement rather than in immediate, short-term reactions. Of the sixteen eligible respondents, fourteen participated; two declined due to time constraints.

Although the researcher is the father of an autistic child, he had no personal or professional ties to the autism advocacy community. This position, an insider in terms of lived experience but an outsider in terms of community affiliation, was transparently communicated before each interview and reflected upon throughout the study as part of ongoing reflexivity.

The researcher’s insider position facilitated rapport and a shared frame of reference during interviews, which appeared to support openness and narrative depth. At the same time, this proximity required sustained reflexive awareness to reduce the risk of unexamined assumptions or over-identification with participants’ accounts. Reflexive memo-writing was conducted throughout both data collection and analysis, focusing on emotional responses, interpretive tendencies, and potential blind spots. Reflexivity was further strengthened through systematic self-interrogation during coding and theme development, alongside the maintenance of a detailed audit trail documenting analytic decisions.

Research tool

The interview guide was developed using the Qualitative Pretest Interview approach [41] and was informed by literature on caregiving experiences among fathers of autistic children. The researcher, who is himself a father of a child diagnosed with ASD, drew both on scholarly sources and experiential insight when constructing the guide. It was designed to explore how caregiving reshapes paternal identity, emotional experiences, and close relationships (Supplementary material).

While the guide broadly addressed the experiences of fathers, this study focused specifically on emotional and relational aspects of caregiving, as well as the evolving needs of male caregivers. Key thematic areas included definitions of fatherhood, shifts in identity, and the emotional challenges associated with raising an autistic child. The guide consisted of two parts: the first gathered demographic data; the second focused on participants’ views on fatherhood, the father-child relationship, the impact of autism on family life, emotions, and caregiving needs. In keeping with the Qualitative Descriptive approach, the semi-structured format enabled flexibility and allowed fathers to introduce issues they considered personally meaningful, thereby preserving openness to participants’ own language and framing.

Two pilot interviews were conducted to evaluate clarity and structure, but were excluded from the final analysis. After minor revisions, the guide was reviewed by two external experts: a father of an autistic child and a medical sociologist. Although the guide underwent external consultation, the study did not include formal Patient and Public Involvement (PPI) in the co-design of the research tool.

Data collection

Due to the geographic dispersion of caregivers of autistic children and limited availability, interviews were conducted via Microsoft Teams (Microsoft 365) between March and May 2025.

All fourteen interviews were conducted by a medical sociologist with expertise in qualitative methods and the social aspects of autism, who is also the sole author of this manuscript. Sessions averaged 68 min, producing approximately 16 h of audio, which was transcribed verbatim. All participants joined from home, supporting a sense of privacy and openness.

All interviews were video-recorded, and transcripts were initially generated using the automatic speech recognition function in MS Teams and subsequently manually reviewed and verified by the author against the original recordings to ensure accuracy. Minor corrections were introduced where necessary (e.g., misheard words, punctuation, formatting). Follow-up interviews were not required, given the depth of responses and the iterative nature of the analysis. Minor ambiguities were addressed through contextual interpretation, cross-case comparison, and memo-writing. Reflexive memo-writing was conducted throughout both data collection and analysis. Initial memos were written immediately after each interview to document contextual observations, preliminary interpretations, and emerging analytic insights. During the coding and theme development phases, memos were used to record interpretive decisions, explore alternative meanings, and revisit potentially ambiguous excerpts before finalizing codes and themes.

Data analysis

All identifying information was removed from transcripts to ensure confidentiality. In addition to verbal content, relevant non-verbal cues (e.g., tone, silences, emphasis) were retained to preserve communicative context.

The data were analysed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis (RTA), following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework – a flexible yet systematic method for identifying and interpreting patterns of meaning in qualitative data [42, 43]. The analysis was semantic and inductive: codes and themes were constructed from participants’ explicit meanings rather than imposed by pre-existing theory. The process was grounded in a constructivist-interpretivist paradigm [44, 45]. This epistemological stance was appropriate given the study’s aim to explore how fathers construct meaning around caregiving, paternal identity, and emotional experience. A constructivist-interpretivist framework assumes that reality is socially and relationally produced, and that experiences are interpreted rather than objectively measured. As the study sought to understand subjective sense-making processes rather than to test predefined hypotheses, this paradigm provided a coherent foundation for inductive, meaning-oriented analysis. Furthermore, this orientation recognizes themes not as objective entities emerging from the data, but as interpretive constructions generated through the researcher’s reflexive engagement with participants’ accounts. This position aligns with RTA, which conceptualises knowledge as situated and co-constructed rather than discovered [45].

After familiarization with the material, the researcher developed open, descriptive codes, grouped them into categories, and refined them through recursive engagement with the data. Themes were clearly defined, named, and illustrated with participant quotes.

This approach aligned with the study’s Qualitative Descriptive design, which prioritizes low-inference interpretation, close attention to participants’ language, and practice-relevant findings. The use of semantic, inductive RTA was fully compatible with this methodology. All coding and theme development were conducted manually, reflecting RTA’s emphasis on close interpretive engagement.

Analytic sufficiency was achieved to ensure adequate depth and meaningful interpretation [42]. Rigour was maintained through ongoing reflexivity, memo-writing, and critical engagement with how disciplinary background and experiential positioning shaped the analysis [46]. Prolonged engagement with the dataset, iterative refinement of codes and themes, and systematic reflexive memo-writing accompanied the entire analytic process. Continuous critical self-questioning was employed to examine emerging interpretations, and a transparent audit trail was maintained to document key analytic decisions.

All coding and theme development were carried out by a single researcher, which is consistent with the RTA approach that views the analyst’s subjectivity as a valuable resource rather than a threat to rigour [43, 45]. In line with RTA, the researcher’s subjectivity was treated as an analytic resource, while remaining subject to ongoing critical scrutiny.

Results

Study participants

The study included 14 fathers aged between 36 and 53 (M = 42.7), the majority of whom were married (n = 13) (Table 1). Most participants had completed higher education (n = 13) and were employed full-time (n = 12). They resided in towns of various sizes, with the largest group living in municipalities of 10,000–50,000 residents. In total, the participants had 31 children, including 19 diagnosed with ASD. Most fathers rated their health as good or very good, and assessed their financial situation similarly, though a few reported neutral perceptions of their health or economic status.

Themes and subthemes on the fathers’ experiences of raising an autistic child

Theme 1: Conceptions of fatherhood

Participants discussed how they understood and experienced fatherhood, both in general terms and in the specific context of raising an autistic child. Their accounts highlighted fatherhood as an evolving role shaped by personal values, emotional involvement, and everyday caregiving.

Perceptions of fatherhood

Many participants described fatherhood as an active and emotionally engaged role. While they acknowledged the traditional expectation for fathers to act as financial providers and protectors of the family, this responsibility was not viewed as sufficient on its own. Fathers saw ensuring material stability as necessary but not sufficient; they emphasized instead “being present” – engaging in caregiving tasks, supporting their partner, and offering emotional security. Fatherhood was often described as a “desire,” “dream,” or “important role,” and was framed as a relational and developmental commitment rooted in love, attentiveness, and everyday connection. Active involvement, rather than distant provision, was central to how they understood and embodied their paternal identity. These accounts suggest that participants constructed fatherhood less as a status or provider role and more as an everyday relational practice, in which emotional presence and caregiving competence served as core markers of paternal identity.

In such cases, their motivation to remain engaged stemmed from a desire to avoid repeating past patterns. References to their own fathers’ emotional absence functioned as a negative point of comparison, through which participants articulated a deliberate intergenerational shift toward involvement, closeness, and accountability.My father was rather absent and not really interested in my life, so I kind of tried to find ways to avoid the mistakes that were made with me (…) I’m starting to enjoy that it’s this process of introducing and caring for a young person, just introducing them into life. In addition to emotional presence, many participants described fatherhood as a complex and demanding responsibility. They referred to it as a core life task, or even a man’s “most important mission”, that gave purpose and direction to their adult lives. For many, fatherhood represented a lifelong commitment that required active self-development, emotional maturity, and moral integrity. Their accounts often framed fatherhood as an evolving project: one that required consistent emotional investment, protection, guidance, and the creation of a stable and supportive family environment. In this framing, paternal identity was anchored in moral responsibility and self-work, with fatherhood described as a long-term project requiring ongoing emotional regulation, reflection, and personal development.Being a father is the most important task we face as men (…), it’s a fundamental task. Several participants spoke about the internal tension between wanting to do their best and struggling with self-doubt or fear of failure. They also described their role as one of moral guidance, modelling respect, patience, and resilience, and emphasised the importance of passing on life knowledge, fostering independence, and building emotional security.I think it’s a mix: responsibility, fear, joy, pride. These narratives highlight fatherhood as emotionally ambivalent but ethically charged: pride and joy coexisted with persistent self-doubt, while “doing fatherhood well” was tied to moral modelling and the management of fear and uncertainty.

Ideals and expectations

Despite declarations of deep paternal involvement, several participants acknowledged that before becoming fathers, they had not given the role much thought or attached specific expectations to it. For some, fatherhood was described as something that “just happened,” rather than a consciously anticipated life event. Their identities as fathers evolved gradually in response to everyday caregiving experiences and their child’s emerging needs. This contrast suggests that fathers’ ideals of parenting were not always pre-formed but often crystallised through caregiving practice, indicating that paternal identity was negotiated retrospectively rather than defined in advance.No, I didn’t have any images or expectations. I just started getting into it. No, I don’t think there were any plans. (F5)I didn’t really think about it. Maybe one shouldn’t say it like that, but ‘it just happened.’ I was busy with completely different things in life. (F7)

In contrast, other participants described fatherhood as a long-held aspiration – something they had reflected on and prepared for well before becoming parents. These reflections often began in adolescence or early adulthood, when they imagined the kind of fathers they hoped to become. Their ideas were shaped by personal values or a conscious wish to build a particular type of relationship with their future children. For some, these aspirations were rooted in traditional beliefs that positioned fatherhood as a central life goal and moral responsibility. Fatherhood of an autistic child

Many participants reflected that raising an autistic child required rethinking what it means to be a father. Initially, some adopted a therapeutic stance, but over time they shifted toward a more intuitive, emotionally attuned style, focused less on structured interventions and more on connection, trust, and everyday presence. This shift stemmed from the awareness that constant therapy could overshadow emotional closeness. Fathers learned to balance structured support with simply “being there,” offering stability and shared experiences. Several described moving away from the “parent-therapist” identity to embrace a more authentic, nurturing role, seeing themselves less as “fixers” and more as companions walking alongside their child. They also reflected on the emotional significance of their presence, particularly with daughters, noting the importance of male emotional availability.At the beginning, one becomes a kind of therapist (…). Personal understanding of autism

Fathers expressed varied understandings of autism, reflecting different conceptual frameworks. Their narratives revealed three main perspectives: autism as a medical condition, as a form of diversity, or as an everyday challenge shaped by social context. These three framings functioned as meaning-making resources: they shaped how fathers interpreted their child’s needs, evaluated progress, and positioned themselves as caregivers.

Clinical framing

Some participants used clinical language, describing autism as a disorder or dysfunction. This view was grounded in biomedical frameworks that positioned autism in relation to “normal” development. Fathers adopting this perspective spoke in terms of impairment, emphasising its biological or neurological basis. For them, diagnosis offered a concrete framework for understanding their child’s behaviour and needs, even if it also implied deficit and difference.Because this illness is very, well, illness… this disorder is so complex. (F5)This is some kind of disorder on an intellectual level. (F6)

Because this illness is very, well, illness… this disorder is so complex. (F5)

This is some kind of disorder on an intellectual level. (F6)

Clinical language offered an explanatory framework that could bring coherence and predictability, yet it also risked reinforcing deficit-oriented expectations that fathers then had to emotionally negotiate.

Autism as “difference”

Other fathers described autism as a natural form of human variation, emphasising difference rather than deficit. In this view, autism was seen as part of an individual’s identity, a distinctive way of being in the world. Fathers using this perspective often normalised autism, arguing that it requires understanding rather than correction. Some associated it with heightened sensitivity or unique perceptual abilities, portraying their children’s experiences as different but meaningful aspects of human diversity.It is a neurodivergence; he is simply different, and I treat it like, well, some people are redheads, others are blond, some have blue eyes, others brown, some have autism, others don’t. By normalising autism as “difference,” fathers reduced the moral weight of blame and failure, shifting emphasis from fixing the child to understanding and adapting to the child’s way of experiencing the world.

Another group of fathers focused on the practical and emotional demands of raising an autistic child. Autism was not necessarily viewed in negative or pathological terms, but rather as a source of complexity that required ongoing adjustment. In these narratives, challenges were often described as situational and deeply linked to the child’s environment:So it is some kind of challenge (…) it is a certain challenge, a nuisance, but to a large extent dependent on, well, the environment in which we function, and that environment is often the bigger problem. Living with autism

Fathers reflected on how autism shaped daily life, including their relationship with the child, family dynamics, and experiences of isolation and marginalisation. They described both the emotional depth and practical demands of caregiving, revealing a complex picture of adaptation, connection, and resilience. Living with autism was experienced simultaneously as an intimate family process and as a socially exposed condition requiring constant adjustment.

Relationship with the autistic child

Fathers described their relationships with their autistic children as close and meaningful, though often shaped by everyday demands and limited time. Some reflected on how work and responsibilities affected their availability, noting that a lack of time sometimes constrained deeper engagement. Many spoke about teaching practical life skills, such as shopping or cooking, and guiding children through daily routines. They often saw themselves as guides, helping their children build experience and independence through shared activities. Rather than focusing on outcomes, they emphasised everyday presence and gentle encouragement to foster both practical and relational autonomy. Fathers described how raising an autistic child reshaped family relationships, affecting both their partnership and ties with extended family. Many reflected on the strain caregiving placed on their relationship with their partner, noting uneven divisions of care, emotional fatigue, and reduced time together. They observed that one parent often assumed most caregiving while the other focused on financial support, which limited closeness and deepened exhaustion on both sides. Fathers noted that time and energy constraints reduced opportunities for connection and often led to emotional exhaustion on both sides. Emotional and psychological well-being

Fathers spoke of the emotional intensity of raising an autistic child, describing constant mental and physical strain, exhaustion, and moments of helplessness. Yet many also shared experiences of joy, pride, and personal growth that emerged through their caregiving journey. Strain and meaning frequently coexisted in their narratives.

Psychophysical strain

Fathers described parenting an autistic child as both physically and emotionally exhausting. They spoke of chronic fatigue, mental overload, and feeling pushed to the limit, with little opportunity to regain balance. Emotional overwhelm was a constant part of daily life, often managed through coping strategies such as withdrawal, solitude, or herbal remedies. Many also mentioned unpredictable mood shifts, from anger and helplessness to brief moments of joy, intensified by sensory and caregiving stress. Fathers’ needs and recognition

Although fathers showed resilience and dedication, many spoke of unmet needs for support, understanding, and recognition. They stressed that effective fathering requires not only practical effort but also emotional balance, validation, and psychological well-being.

Recognition of fatherhood

Fathers stressed the need for their caregiving role to be recognised by society, professionals, and families. Despite deep involvement in daily care and emotional support, many felt their efforts remained invisible or undervalued. They noted that cultural assumptions still position caregiving as the mother’s domain, leading to marginalisation or surprise when fathers take an active role. Some were treated as exceptions rather than competent, nurturing parents – a gap felt most acutely by fathers of children with disabilities.Because people mainly talk about motherhood, motherhood here, motherhood there (…). Discussion

Based on the analysis, fathers in this study described caregiving as a transformative experience that reshaped their sense of identity, family roles, and daily functioning. Fatherhood was framed as an emotionally involved, morally grounded role, while life with autism brought both challenges and meaningful rewards. Despite exhaustion and limited support, many participants found strength, connection, and personal growth through their parenting journey. This study, therefore, highlights several important findings that help understand fathers’ experiences in autism caregiving.

Firstly, fatherhood emerged as a central dimension of identity, marked by emotional presence, active caregiving, and moral responsibility. In contrast to traditional notions of paternal detachment or breadwinning, participants described fatherhood as a “mission”, “the most important role”, and a satisfying part of everyday experience. This aligns with findings showing that fathers of autistic children develop a strong caregiving identity, reshaping gendered expectations [28, 31, 34]. Fathers emphasized involvement in daily routines, emotional support, and fostering autonomy, reflecting caregiving as rooted in love, duty, and moral purpose [47, 48].

This value-driven perspective aligns with Greenlee et al. [35], who found that engaged fathers derive deep meaning from their roles even when these challenge social norms. Burrell et al. [49] and André et al. [47] similarly noted that fatherhood becomes a key axis of adult male identity, reshaping their understanding of masculinity and maturity. Fathers contrasted their own involvement with the emotional distance of previous generations, seeing themselves as more attuned and morally accountable [25, 32]. This redefinition aligns with the concept of generative fathering, where caregiving is viewed not as a deviation from masculinity but as a moral vocation grounded in care, patience, and reflection [50]. Autism thus became not only a family circumstance but a framework for moral and emotional development, consistent with accounts describing fatherhood in autism as built through endurance and empathy [26, 28, 31].

In the Polish sociocultural context, where caregiving continues to be strongly associated with mothers and fathers are frequently positioned as economic providers rather than primary emotional caregivers [19, 20], these narratives also reflect a subtle negotiation of entrenched gender norms. While Polish studies have predominantly focused on maternal stress, coping, and emotional overload [14, 18], the present findings foreground fathers as emotionally engaged and reflexive caregivers. This suggests a shift in paternal self-understanding that both aligns with global transformations of fatherhood and responds to specific local cultural expectations.

Secondly, participants experienced life with autism as both socially isolating and deeply instructive. Fathers reported marginalization, misunderstandings from extended family, loss of friendships, and exclusion from public spaces, a pattern confirmed elsewhere [14, 27, 30]. These experiences reflect what Uribe-Morales et al. [33] term “double invisibility,” as fathers remain unseen both as caregivers and as parents of an autistic child. Participants described withdrawal from mainstream society and shrinking social circles [32, 47].

Within the Polish institutional landscape, such experiences may be intensified by structural characteristics of the support system. Previous Polish studies indicate uneven access to specialised services, fragmented diagnostic pathways, and heavy reliance on parental initiative in navigating complex institutional procedures [14, 16]. Research on disability assessment practices further highlights limited autism-specific expertise and asymmetrical power relations between families and decision-making bodies [22]. Fathers in the present study, who often assumed responsibility for institutional navigation, may therefore experience a compounded burden: emotional caregiving combined with systemic advocacy.

Alongside isolation, autism was also described as a catalyst for introspection and personal growth. Fathers framed their experience as a journey from denial to acceptance, comparable to identity reconstruction [14, 27]. Frye [26] and Samadi & McConkey [36] observed that caring for an autistic child reshaped fathers’ values, fostering empathy and patience. Other studies report that emotionally engaged fathers develop greater resilience over time [29, 34, 51]. Notably, several fathers described their children as “simply different,” reframing autism less as a deficit and more as a form of difference. This reinterpretation appeared to facilitate emotional adjustment and reduce parental guilt [31, 35]. Emphasizing acceptance and mutual learning helped fathers cultivate stronger, more empathetic relationships with their children [52, 53].

This meaning-oriented reframing is particularly noteworthy when contrasted with findings from Polish studies focused primarily on mothers, where prolonged emotional strain, chronic stress, and reduced quality of life are frequently emphasized [10, 19]. While emotional burden was also evident in the present study, fathers more often integrated narratives of growth, competence, and moral commitment. The findings, therefore, complement existing Polish quantitative research by revealing how fathers subjectively reinterpret structural and emotional strain through processes of meaning-making.

Thirdly, despite chronic emotional strain, caregiving was also a source of joy, pride, and resilience. Fathers experienced fatigue and anxiety [29], yet also expressed deep satisfaction in their children’s progress. This emotional duality mirrors findings by Gill and Renwick [54] and Natasya et al. [55], who describe caregiving fathers as oscillating between exhaustion and gratitude. Even amid adversity, caregiving fostered emotional growth and renewed purpose [28, 31, 51, 52]. Fathers described everyday moments – shared laughter, communication progress, independent tasks – as milestones of deep meaning. Dembosky and Heckert [56] likewise found paternal engagement grounded in meaning-making and moral endurance. This aligns with Uribe-Morales et al. [33], who argue that caregiving fathers navigate between burden and transformation. Participants here portrayed themselves not as victims but as agents constructing value and coherence in hardship, a theme also evident in Frye [26] and André et al. [47].

When considered against Polish data documenting reduced quality of life and heightened parenting stress among parents of autistic children [10, 17], these accounts of resilience highlight the coexistence of structural strain and subjective growth. Fathers’ narratives suggest that emotional engagement and relational closeness may function as protective mechanisms within a context of limited systemic support.

Fourthly, and perhaps most critically, the study revealed a persistent gap between fathers’ lived experiences and structural support systems. Participants felt invisible to professionals, marginalized in educational and health settings, and excluded from networks dominated by mothers. Such experiences reflect institutional gender bias, as fathers often report being treated as secondary caregivers [25, 49, 53]. Previous studies also found that fathers expressed frustration with professionals who overlooked their competence [14, 34]. Social expectations of stoicism further constrained help-seeking [27, 32], causing fathers to suppress distress to maintain control, reinforcing isolation [52, 54].

In Poland, where early screening remains inconsistently implemented and long-term adult services are unevenly distributed [16], institutional invisibility may be compounded by systemic fragmentation. Fathers’ narratives of frustration with schools, assessment bodies, and public institutions resonate with broader Polish findings concerning limited coordination of services and insufficient transparency in disability evaluation [22]. These structural conditions may reinforce traditional gender divisions, positioning fathers as negotiators of bureaucratic systems rather than emotionally supported caregivers.

These structural and cultural pressures intersect with workplace demands. Fathers struggled to balance employment and caregiving, often at the cost of emotional presence or career growth [26, 35]. Such dynamics perpetuate what Rudelli et al. [31] term the “invisible labor” of fatherhood – emotional regulation and advocacy performed beyond public view. Still, several participants called for professional and policy recognition of fathers as equal partners in caregiving. These insights suggest that current family support models should include father-specific components of psychoeducation and respite care. This echoes calls for father-inclusive frameworks integrating men into therapeutic and community contexts [29, 33, 52]. Camilleri [28] and Vatne et al. [30] likewise stress that meaningful inclusion requires not only access but also cultural change, creating spaces where fathers’ emotions and insights are valued.

Taken together, these findings reveal a nuanced portrait of fatherhood in autism – one that intertwines burden and meaning, exhaustion and growth, isolation and intimacy. As in prior research [25, 26, 31, 47], fathers emerge as emotionally reflective, morally engaged agents negotiating complex caregiving ecologies. Situating these findings within the Polish sociocultural and institutional context underscores that fathers’ experiences are shaped not only by individual coping processes but also by gendered norms, systemic fragmentation, and unequal access to services. By extending predominantly mother-focused Polish studies and integrating structural considerations into the analysis, this study contributes to a more contextually grounded and gender-inclusive understanding of autism caregiving.

By amplifying their voices, this study contributes to a broader shift from maternal-centric frameworks toward inclusive, gender-sensitive understandings of autism caregiving, recognizing fathers’ emotional labor, relational depth, and advocacy. It underscores the need for systems that support fathers materially while affirming their evolving identities as caregivers and moral agents within families raising autistic children.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size limits the transferability of findings. However, men are generally less likely to participate in research or share personal experiences. Participants were purposefully selected for their caregiving experience, which yielded rich and meaningful insights. As a qualitative study, the design prioritized interpretive depth and contextual understanding over statistical generalization, aiming for analytical rather than numerical representativeness. Although the sample encompassed variability in children’s age and developmental functioning, it was comparatively homogeneous with respect to fathers’ educational attainment, employment status, and socioeconomic circumstances. This relative homogeneity may constrain the transferability of the findings to fathers experiencing greater economic hardship or social marginalization. In addition, although the study was not designed to examine differences across diagnostic subtypes and such comparisons were beyond the scope of this qualitative design, children’s diagnostic profiles varied and may have shaped fathers’ experiences of parenthood in ways not systematically explored in the present analysis. Thus, future studies with more balanced samples could usefully explore whether and how diagnostic profiles shape fathers’ meaning-making processes. Second, voluntary participation and the online format may have biased the sample toward digitally literate and more engaged individuals. Recruitment through autism-related Facebook groups could have further contributed to selection bias, possibly favouring fathers who are more self-reflective and active in peer communities. Third, data collection and analysis were conducted by a single researcher. While this might raise concerns about interpretive bias, it aligns with the principles of Reflexive Thematic Analysis, which values the researcher’s interpretive role as a resource rather than a threat to validity. Fourth, the study did not include formal Patient and Public Involvement (PPI) in the design phase. Although the interview guide underwent external consultation, fathers were not engaged as co-designers of the research process. While the researcher’s positionality as a father of an autistic child may have enhanced experiential sensitivity during tool development, this does not substitute for structured participatory involvement. The absence of formal co-design may have reduced the study’s participatory depth and limited opportunities for shared decision-making in shaping research priorities. From an ethical and epistemic perspective, the lack of structured participatory engagement may also have constrained opportunities for fathers to influence the framing of research questions and priorities, which is increasingly recognized as an important component of inclusive health research. Future research could benefit from integrating structured participatory involvement, particularly in the development of research tools and the interpretation of findings. Fifth, the researcher’s dual position as investigator and father of an autistic child may have enhanced interpretive sensitivity, but it also carries the possibility of over-identification or unexamined assumptions, especially in areas of shared experiential meaning. Although reflexive strategies, including memo-writing and maintaining an audit trail, were applied throughout the study, the influence of prior understandings cannot be entirely excluded. Sixth, the inclusion criterion requiring at least five years of parenting experience, while intended to capture more stabilized and reflective perspectives on fatherhood and long-term adaptation, may have excluded fathers in the earliest stages of adjustment. Experiences during the initial period following diagnosis, including acute emotional responses and rapid identity disruption, might differ substantially and could offer distinct insights into early-stage adaptation processes. Moreover, although the sample included children across a broad age range, fathers of younger children and adolescents predominated; therefore, the experiences of fathers of autistic adults may be underrepresented, and their caregiving trajectories and long-term concerns may differ in important ways. Future research may usefully examine the experiences of fathers of autistic adults to better understand long-term caregiving trajectories. Seventh, because participants reconstructed their experiences retrospectively, their accounts may be affected by recall bias. Lastly, remote interviews limited observation of non-verbal cues, which may have constrained interpretive depth, although the home-based format likely enhanced comfort and openness.

Despite these limitations, the study has important strengths. It is among the first qualitative inquiries in Poland to explore fathers’ experiences of raising an autistic child, addressing a clear gap in the literature. Including participants from varied residential contexts and family situations contributes to the contextual relevance of the findings. Additionally, the sample reflected diversity primarily in caregiving contexts, including differences in children’s developmental profiles, communication abilities, and levels of functioning. In-depth interviews provided nuanced insights into caregiving, and methodological rigour, including adherence to COREQ guidelines and iterative analysis, supports credibility. The study sheds light on often overlooked emotional and practical dimensions of fatherhood in the context of autism and underlines the need for more inclusive, gender-sensitive support. Importantly, providing fathers with space to share their stories may itself have had a validating and therapeutic effect.

Conclusions

This study highlights the complex emotional, relational, and moral dimensions of fatherhood in autism caregiving. Fathers described their roles not as secondary or peripheral but as emotionally engaged, morally committed, and deeply transformative. Despite fatigue, anxiety, and systemic exclusion, they often reframed caregiving as a source of meaning, growth, and identity. Autism was perceived not merely as a clinical challenge but as a relational journey that reshaped their understanding of parenting, masculinity, and family life.

The findings reveal patterns of social marginalization, emotional isolation, and institutional invisibility, with fathers frequently reporting a lack of recognition from professionals, family, and support systems. They called for greater emotional validation, relational space, and structural assistance, reflecting their lived caregiving realities. These narratives challenge persistent gender stereotypes and maternal-centric models of care, underscoring the need to recognize fathers as active, competent, and emotionally involved caregivers. To address these gaps, several recommendations are proposed:
