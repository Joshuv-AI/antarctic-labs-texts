Early Feasibility and Efficacy of a Novel Skill-Based Training Program for Poststroke Dysphagia
Authors: Mariana M. Bahia, Julia Carpenter, Kelly Rogers, Leora R. Cherney
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop a skill-based swallowing training program for poststroke survivors with dysphagia, to examine the acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility of the program from the perspective of clinicians and researchers, and to investigate the early efficacy of the program.DesignThe Plan-Do-Study-Act framework was used to conduct a clinician survey and to measure within-subject changes pre and posttreatment.SettingInpatient rehabilitation hospital.ParticipantsFifteen swallowing clinicians and 7 researchers with a background in speech-language pathology responded to a survey about the intervention’s acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility (n = 22). Two patients (a 56y woman and a 33y man) with moderate-severe dysphagia secondary to poststroke received the swallowing intervention during inpatient rehabilitation (n = 2).InterventionsThe Effort Accuracy Timing-Skill Training Applied to Rehabilitation in Swallowing (EAT-STARS) program is a novel, systematic, and progressive skill-based training program designed to improve swallowing function in poststroke dysphagia. The program consists of up to 8 treatment sessions, spanning 2 weeks, and incorporates principles of motor learning and neuroplasticity to enhance participants’ ability to recognize correct/incorrect motor patterns, thereby promoting skill acquisition and mastery. It requires patients to generate different levels of swallowing effort (ie, regular swallow, effortful swallow) and control the timing of swallowing (ie, prompt swallow, swallow with bolus hold) using real-time surface electromyography visual biofeedback.Main Outcome MeasuresClinicians and researchers answered a survey that included the Acceptability of Intervention Measure, Intervention Appropriateness Measure, and Feasibility of Intervention Measurequestionnaires. The early efficacy of the EAT-STARS program was assessed through standardized pre- and postswallowing measurements, including the Mann Assessment of Swallowing Ability, the Modified Barium Swallowing Impairment Profile, the Eating Assessment Tool, and a visual analog scale to indicate patient-perceived swallowing difficulty.ResultsThe EAT-STARS program met preestablished cutoff scores for the Acceptability of Intervention Measure (90%), Intervention Appropriateness Measure (87.5%), and Feasibility of Intervention Measure (83.4%) measures, indicating excellent acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility, respectively. The early efficacy measures indicated that patients demonstrated improvements in both clinical swallowing ability and swallowing physiologyposttreatment. Moreover, patients reported perceived improvements in swallowing (visual analog scale and Eating Assessment Tool).ConclusionsThe novel EAT-STARS program showed excellent acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility measures. Clinical, physiologic, and patient-reported swallowing outcome measures indicated benefits of the program for poststroke patients with dysphagia. Further early-phase testing will include more patients and provide data on patients’ satisfaction.

Swallowing is a complex, skilled task that involves multiple brain regions to coordinate the precise and accurate movements of swallowing structures, enabling the passage of food from the mouth to the stomach. Thus, brain injuries can disrupt the normal execution of swallowing, leading to a swallowing disorder or dysphagia. Stroke is the most common cause of dysphagia, affecting as many as 70% of individuals.1 Dysphagia is not only associated with clinical complications such as dehydration, malnutrition, and aspiration pneumonia,2 but it is also responsible for psychosocial distress because of food restrictions, prolonged meal times, and fear of choking.3,4 Dysphagia rehabilitation aims to improve swallowing function and physiologic deficits, consequently reducing complications. Traditional dysphagia therapy includes compensatory strategies to prevent airway invasion and ensure adequate food intake, as well as exercises to improve the movement of swallowing-related structures (eg, the larynx) or strengthen muscles (eg, the tongue, pharynx). A combination of compensatory strategies and exercises is usually implemented during rehabilitation.5

Skill-based training approaches have gained attention in dysphagia rehabilitation because of the recognition that swallowing is a highly coordinated, skilled task that depends on precise timing and sequencing of movements. While traditional strengthening exercises benefit swallowing by addressing muscle weakness, these exercises do not fully target the intricate coordination demands of swallowing. In contrast, skill-based swallowing training focuses on the voluntary control of swallowing movements, including the timing and force of these movements.6 Skill-based swallowing training incorporates principles of motor learning7 and neuroplasticity,8 such as practicing swallows (specificity) while requiring the patient to reach specific targets (practice challenge), for example, swallowing at a specific effort level. Moreover, another critical component in skill-based training is feedback, which helps individuals refine their movement patterns.6 A common visual biofeedback modality used in skill-based swallowing training is surface electromyography (sEMG).9,10 The sEMG biofeedback facilitates learning and practice of swallowing tasks, as well as improves task accuracy.11

This study aimed to develop an intensive, skill-based swallowing training program for individuals with dysphagia after a stroke, examining the acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility of the program from the perspectives of clinicians and researchers, and investigating the early efficacy of the program.

Methods

Study design

The Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) quality improvement framework12 was used to develop the treatment protocol, gather stakeholder input, make modifications, and assess the program’s efficacy (fig 1). A clinician/researcher survey and a within-subject pre- and posttreatment study were conducted at the Shirley Ryan AbilityLab. The Institutional Review Board of Northwestern University approved this study (STU00219746). Informed consent was not applicable because the project was designed and implemented as a quality improvement study.Fig 1The PDSA-guided development process. Initial program development

The study team conducted a literature review on best practices in swallowing exercises, skill-based swallowing training, and principles of motor learning and neuroplasticity (cycle 1). This resulted in the development of the Effort Accuracy Timing-Skill Training Applied to Rehabilitation in Swallowing (EAT-STARS) program. The Template for Intervention Description and Replication13 was used to ensure that key treatment elements were delineated and reported. Targets, treatment ingredients, and hypothesized mechanisms of action were outlined in the treatment protocol, as per the Rehabilitation Treatment Specification System14 (RTSS) framework.

Acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility

Certified speech-language pathologists (SLPs) who work at the rehabilitation hospital, providing care to adults with dysphagia, and researchers with a background in speech-language pathology provided feedback about the developed program (cycle 2). The skill-based swallowing program was presented at a routine hospital-based committee meeting, and feedback was collected through standardized questionnaires, informal survey questions, and live/written commentary. The meeting consisted of a 1-hour introduction to the literature, the EAT-STARS program, and a question-and-answer session.

Participants completed a survey that included the Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM), Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM), and Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM) questionnaires,15 as well as questions proposed by Leis and Shojania16 and derived from the Template for Intervention Description and Replication13 (supplemental appendix S1, available online only at http://www.archives-pmr.org/). Early efficacy

The final version of the EAT-STARS program was pretested in poststroke inpatients with dysphagia for early efficacy (cycle 3). Moreover, qualitative feedback was provided by the SLP delivering the program to the patients. Additional program refinements were captured through the Framework for Reporting Adaptations and Modifications to Evidence-based Intervention Strategies (table 1).

Participants

Adults with dysphagia secondary to subacute stroke received the swallowing intervention program during their inpatient rehabilitation. Stroke was confirmed via brain computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging by a neurologist. Participants were clinically evaluated using the Mann Assessment of Swallowing Ability18 (MASA) and the Modified Barium Swallowing Study (MBSS) to confirm dysphagia. Participants were also eligible for the study if they could safely swallow 5 mL of at least one of the following liquid consistencies according to the International Dysphagia Diet Standardization Initiative19 (IDDSI) levels: thin liquid (IDDSI=0), mildly thick (IDDSI=2), or moderately thick (IDDSI=3). Participants were excluded if they had a history of prior dysphagia, active pneumonia, or were not suitable for submental sEMG use, such as those who were unable to follow verbal instructions, had significant visual impairments, had facial hair under the chin, or were unwilling to shave.

Assessment procedures

Case history was retrieved from the medical chart to obtain demographics and medical, stroke, and swallowing histories. The patients also completed the Montreal Cognitive Assessment20 (version 8.1). Early efficacy of the program was assessed through pre- and postswallowing measurements, including MASA, the Eating Assessment Tool21 (EAT-10), a visual analog scale (VAS) to characterize patient-reported swallowing difficulties, and MBSS. MBSSs were analyzed using the Modified Barium Swallowing Impairment Profile22 (MBSImP) and the Penetration-Aspiration Scale23 (PAS). Moreover, feedback from the SLP and patients addressing facilitators and barriers during therapy sessions was collected to evaluate the intervention program.

The MBSS is a videofluoroscopic examination to assess swallowing physiology and airway protection. A certified SLP and a radiologist conducted all MBSS examinations in a routine radiology suite at the rehabilitation hospital. Digital videofluoroscopic images were captured using a high-resolution imaging platform at a continuous fluoroscopy rate of 30 frames per second (TIMS 2000 SP DICOM System, Foresight Imaginga). Videofluoroscopic images were obtained with the patients seated upright in the lateral viewing plane.22 Each patient completed 8 swallows using a standardized, commercially prepared barium contrast (Varibar E-Z-EM, 40% w/v, Bracco Incb) of varying volumes and consistencies, as well as a Lorna Doone cookie. The swallows were presented in the following order: (1) 5 mL thin liquid barium with cued bolus hold via a teaspoon (<15 cups), (2) 5 mL thin liquid barium via a teaspoon, (3) cup sip thin liquid barium, (4) thin liquid barium sequential swallows via a cup (∼3 sips), (5) 5 mL nectar-thick barium via a teaspoon (150-450 cups), (6) 5 mL honey-thick barium via a spoon (800-1800 cups), (7) 5 mL pudding barium via a spoon, and (8) solid (1/2 cookie coated with 3 mL pudding barium).

Intervention protocol

The EAT-STARS is a systematic and progressive skill-based training program designed to improve swallowing function in poststroke dysphagia. The program comprises up to 8 treatment sessions, spanning 2 weeks, which aligns with the typical length of hospital stay in rehabilitation for poststroke patients. It requires patients to generate different levels of swallowing effort (ie, regular, effortful swallows) and to control the timing of swallowing (ie, prompt vs bolus hold swallows) using real-time submental sEMG visual biofeedback (Mobili-T, True Anglec) displayed on an iPad. Swallowing effort targets are calibrated at 110% of the peak submental sEMG amplitude by device default during 5 swallows, and the timing of swallows is displayed on a 10-second screen. A dashed line indicates the effort threshold between regular and effortful swallows. An arrow was applied to a 5-second timing threshold between prompt and bolus hold swallows (fig 2). Visual biofeedback enables patients to modulate their effort and timing during swallowing to achieve specific goals determined by each module of the program.Fig 2Visual biofeedback display indicating the swallowing types by (A) effort and (B) timing levels. The EAT-STARS is organized into 3 parts (effort, timing, and effort-timing), each with specific modules that represent the progression and complexity of swallowing tasks. An accuracy target of 80% for each module is required to progress to the next module. In the “Education” modules (effort and timing parts), patients receive an explanation of the swallowing process, the sEMG device, and the waveform. In the “Identification” modules (effort, timing parts), patients identify effort and timing levels using 4×4 cm cards that display 1 sEMG waveform each. In the “Learning” modules (effort and timing parts), patients practice effortful swallows, prompt swallows, and bolus hold swallows, watching their swallows on the iPad. Each type of swallow is practiced 10 times with either 5 mL of thin or mildly thick liquid (whichever is safe for each patient). In the “Training” module, patients practice intermixed swallows at different effort levels (regular and effortful swallows) while matching the clinician’s instructions. This module is also repeated using intermixed swallows at different timings (prompt, bolus hold swallows). The “Training” module intermixes 2 liquids (thin and mildly thick or mildly and moderately thick) and includes 20 swallows. The last part of the program includes the modules “Acquiring” and “Mastering,” which combine effort and timing levels (eg, effortful swallow with bolus hold). These modules include 2 blocks of 20 swallows each and intermix 2 liquid consistencies. In “Acquiring,” patients use sEMG visual feedback; however, in “Mastering,” patients are not allowed to use visual feedback.

Outcome measures and data analysis

Primary outcome measures included the AIM, IAM, and FIM questionnaires (cycle 2), the MASA score, MBSImP oral total and pharyngeal total scores, the EAT-10 score, and the VAS score (cycle 3). Although a cutoff score for interpreting the AIM, IAM, and FIM is unavailable, higher scores indicate greater acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility, respectively. This study aimed to achieve a preestablished 80% target for each measure. Secondary outcomes included qualitative feedback from participants (cycles 2 and 3) and PAS scores (cycle 2).

All MBSS swallowing tasks were analyzed by an MBSImP-certified SLP to extract MBSImP and PAS scores. The rater was blinded to both the patient and the time point. The MBSImP overall impression scores for each component (the worst score across all swallowing trials) were used to calculate the oral and pharyngeal total scores. If a bolus was withheld because of safety concerns, the highest score was assigned to each component. The oral total score was calculated by summing components 1-6, and the pharyngeal total score by summing components 7-1 and 14-16. Two independent evaluators measured each VAS, a horizontal, undifferentiated line anchored at “no difficulty” (0 mm) and “maximum difficulty” (100 mm), blinded to the patient and time point. The evaluators used a ruler to measure the distance from where patients marked their perceived swallowing difficulty in increments of 0.5 mm.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (version 2024.04.2).d Means, SDs, medians, interquartile ranges, and percentages were calculated to characterize the participants. Additionally, visual analyses were conducted through the examination of graphical information pre- and posttreatment.

Discussion

Program development

Although multiple brain regions support swallowing, sensorimotor areas play a key role in representing relevant swallowing structures (eg, the tongue, larynx) and coordinating skilled swallowing movements.24 Therefore, skill-based swallowing training may enhance sensorimotor excitability and drive plasticity. Additionally, swallowing exercises that vary in complexity, feedback, and practice demands are thought to improve skill acquisition, promote behavioral changes, and facilitate brain reorganization.6,7 The EAT-STARS program incorporates motor learning and neuroplasticity principles through progressively challenging modules that address the multifaceted nature of swallowing.

Humans use multiple learning processes, including error-based, perceptual, use-dependent, and reinforcement learning, to acquire or refine skills.25,26 These processes involve distinct but interacting neural networks during skill (re)acquisition,25 promoting cortical changes.25,26 Furthermore, cognitive processing is also required in the early stages of learning, facilitating tasks and instructions.25 The EAT-STARS “Education,” “Identification,” and “Training” modules engage cognitive processing and perceptual learning by requiring patients to detect, discriminate, and interpret performance through experience and feedback. Intrinsic feedback is supported by sEMG visual biofeedback and clinician input, enabling reinforcement learning through knowledge of results.27 Feedback is gradually reduced across modules to encourage intrinsic feedback and greater independence.7 In the “Learning,” “Training,” and “Acquisition” modules, patients practice swallowing tasks by attempting them, making errors, noticing these errors, and then adjusting their effort or timing levels to reach accurate targets in subsequent swallowing attempts (error-based learning). Finally, repetitive task-specific practice promotes use-dependent learning, which facilitates motor planning, while practice variability supports flexible and adaptable movement patterns across conditions (eg, different bolus consistencies), thereby strengthening motor planning and improving patients’ ability to adapt to challenges.

Acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility

Developing new interventions should involve researchers, clinicians, and patients to enhance their effectiveness, acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility.28,29 Stakeholders’ input, especially those delivering the intervention, helps identify content and delivery issues and suggests practical improvements.28,29 The EAT-STARS program was codeveloped with SLPs who participated in all PDSA cycles, including decision-making. This partnership was crucial for understanding the needs of the target population and clinical routines. Additional feedback from stakeholders was collected through a consultation meeting and survey. Researchers and clinicians provided suggestions to refine the program, adapt it to different levels of care, and identify training needs for sEMG and program use. A key recommendation was to include patient input. Patient feedback provides critical insight into the acceptability of the program and alignment with user values, preferences, and needs.28 Including the patient voice ensures that the program is both clinically sound and personally meaningful, supporting greater usability, engagement, and real-world effectiveness.

Early efficacy

Early efficacy testing was essential for evaluating the initial effects of the EAT-STARS program on swallowing outcomes and for collecting feedback from patients and the clinician delivering the program. This process helped to identify benefits, challenges, and areas for refinement, ensuring that the program is clinically relevant and responsive to user experiences before broader implementation. Posttreatment results were promising, with both patients showing clinical, physiological, and patient-reported improvements in swallowing.

Skill-based training targets the voluntary control of swallowing, thereby enhancing awareness and attention during the swallowing process. Increased awareness and attention may facilitate the precise execution of the swallowing motor sequence, allowing individuals to monitor and adjust their performance. sEMG visual biofeedback reinforces this process by offering immediate, real-time cues, helping patients identify errors, reinforce accurate movements, and stay engaged.11,30,31 This combination of internal focus and external feedback likely contributed to the observed swallowing improvements. Additionally, practice amount, variability, and scheduling can also optimize swallowing outcomes. Consistent and higher numbers of swallowing trials may strengthen the motor representation and provide opportunities for refining swallowing.8,32 Varying practice conditions (eg, bolus consistency, swallowing timing, and effort) challenge the system to adapt to new contexts, promoting the generalizability of swallowing and reflecting everyday eating habits. Finally, intermixing practice also supports long-term retention and transfer of knowledge.8 These elements were embedded in the EAT-STARS program and may have enhanced its impact on swallowing function.

Final feedback from the clinician and patients was essential to further refine the program. Clinicians, as primary users, may offer insights into the feasibility, clarity, and practical implementation of the program, highlighting potential barriers and necessary adaptations.28,33 Patients, on the other hand, can provide feedback on acceptability, comfort, and perceived benefits—key factors for adherence and long-term engagement.28 Incorporating end-user feedback ensures the treatment is evidence-informed, meaningful, and sustainable in routine practice. This user-centered approach aligns with implementation science principles and supports the translation into clinical care.33,34

Study limitations

This study is not without limitations. First, the early efficacy included only 2 participants to pretest the program. However, the purpose of early efficacy was not only to evaluate the effects of the program on swallowing but also to gather feedback from stakeholders. Moreover, a fourth PDSA cycle, not included in this manuscript, is currently underway to scale the pilot testing of the EAT-STARS program. This cycle will assess the efficacy of the refined program and incorporate additional patient feedback about the usability, engagement, and acceptance of EAT-STARS. Second, we did not assess posttreatment outcomes beyond the immediate end of the program. Retention of intervention outcomes can offer additional information about the consolidation of skill acquisition.

Conclusions

This study developed a skill-based training program for poststroke dysphagia, grounded in motor learning and neuroplasticity principles and guided by the RTSS framework. The EAT-STARS program demonstrated excellent acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility. Preliminary clinical, physiologic, and patient-reported improvements in swallowing suggest benefits for individuals with poststroke dysphagia.
