Coexistence of primary clear cell subtype of hepatocellular carcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma, and ordinary type hepatocellular carcinoma: A case report
Authors: Jian Xu, Xiang-yu Lu, Yu Zhang, Xiao-jiong Yu, Juan Li, Hao Zhang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and importancePrimary Clear Cell subtype of Hepatocellular Carcinoma (PCHCC) is a rare kind of Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC). The coexistence of PCHCC, Intrahepatic Cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), and ordinary-type HCC(OHCC) in different parts of the liver is seldom reported in the literature.Case presentationA 66 years old man with three masses in his liver was admitted. Introduction

Combined Hepatocellular Carcinoma and Cholangiocarcinoma (CHCC) is a rare type of primary liver cancer (PLC) and was commonly composed of Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC) and Intrahepatic Cholangiocarcinoma (ICC). It was first reported in 1949 [1] and was classified into three types: double type, combined type, and mixed type. It accounts for about 1.0–4.7 % of incidences of PLC [1,2]. Primary Clear Cell subtype of HCC(PCHCC) is a rare carcinoma with a 3–7 % frequency in all HCC types [3]. But the occurrence of CHCC mixed with primary clear cell carcinoma is extremely rare. Here, we report a case of PLC composed of ordinary-type HCC (OHCC), ICC, and PCHCC. To our knowledge, we do not discover any other cases with three 3 kinds of components of carcinoma cells in different parts of the liver.

Case presentation

The work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [4]. A 66-year-old man was diagnosed with three liver tumors during a physical examination three weeks ago and was hospitalized in our hospital. He experienced no abdominal pain, no chills or fever, no jaundice, and no weight loss. He got a history of hypertension for 10 years and which was well controlled by Calcium antagonist medicine. No history of diabetes﹑alcohol addiction and smoking. Eating raw meat and/or living in the epidemic area history was denied. His wife died of lung cancer five years ago. No other tumor family history was claimed. Physical examination was negative. The HBsAg, HBeAb, and HBcAb tests were positive. The other blood test, including HCVAg, Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), were normal. Preoperative liver function (CHILD-PUGH classified) was an A-level (AST 21 U/L, TBIL 18.0umol/L, ALB 45.4 g/L, PT 11.7 s, and no signs of ascites or hepatic encephalopathy). Gastroscopy showed erosive gastritis and no esophageal and gastric varices. Abdominal enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (E-MRI) suggested possible liver cirrhosis and three hepatic masses in the right lobe and all were likely malignant (Fig. 1, named Tumor-α, Tumor-β, and Tumor-γ). Positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) showed 2 lesions with increased 18F-FDG uptake were malignancy. The 3rd lesion found with decreased 18F-FDG uptake was benign. A whole-body scan (WHS) showed the fifth lumbar vertebra (L5) left edge slightly higher uptake and recommended a double check 3 months later.

During the operation, exploration of the peritoneal cavity and application of intraoperative liver Doppler ultrasound were routinely used and no other tumor or metastasis was discovered except for several lymph nodes with 0.9 cm diameter in the hepatoduodenal ligament. Then, S5 and S7 resection were underwent with more than 1 cm of the resection margin, and the hepatoduodenal ligament was skeletonized. A moderate differentiation PCHCC(Tumor-α, Vsegment, 1.8 cm × 2.5 cm) (Fig. 2), a well-differentiation ICC(Tumor-β, VII segment, 1.0 cm × 1.3 cm), and a well-differentiation OHCC(Tumor-γ, VII segment, 3.3 cm × 4.2 cm) were diagnosed by pathology, and the lymph nodes were all negative.

The patient was discharged on the 14th-day post-operation and he refused to take further treatments, such as targeted therapy of tyrosine kinases and so on. Also, the follow-up did not perform well because of the absent of patient. Twelve months later, he came back to our hospital because of the recurrence of liver cancer. Enhanced computed tomography (E-CT) showed a big mass with a diameter of 10.2 × 9.2 × 8.9 cm in the right lobe, suggesting the recurrence of the tumor. The WHS suggested no signs of bone metastasis this time. The patients refuse to accept the biopsy or further surgery, and then transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) was carried out with lipiodol 10 mL and 20 mg Pirarubicin embolism. One month later, 3 courses of chemical therapy with FOLFOX 4 were followed. Unfortunately, the tumor was unsuccessfully controlled, and 8 months later he succumbed to liver failure.

Discussion

According to the latest World Health Organization (WHO) ICD-11 Statistics [5], this case should be diagnosed as Combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma with two subtypes of HCC: PCHCC and OHCC, as well as one ICC. PCHCC is relatively rare, and it becomes even rarer when coexisting with OHCC and ICC simultaneously. PCHCC primarily consists of cells with clear cytoplasm due to the accumulation of glycogen. More than 80 % of the tumor exhibits clear cell morphology, and some degree of steatosis is permitted in establishing the diagnosis [6]. Furthermore, histological evaluation should be conducted to differentiate clear cell HCC from metastatic clear cell renal cell carcinoma (RCC). In our case, the immunoenzymatic labeling outcome of Tumor-α showed RCC was “±”, and Glypican-3 and Hepatocyte were positive. Based on these, the diagnosis of the clear cell subtype of primary hepatocellular carcinoma originating from the liver is definitive. The causes of the three tumorigenic components remain unclear. Most doctors [7,8] assert that stem cells are likely responsible for the occurrence of mixed types of liver cancer.

FDG-PET was often used in the preoperative diagnosis of malignant liver tumors, especially in HCC and CHCC [9]. But some researchers [10,11] insisted PET-CT with limited value in the diagnosis of CHCC and suggested a biopsy should be considered when the clinical evidence was weak. In our case, one placeholder which was a well-differentiated OHCC was misdiagnosed as benign by FDG-FET. So, we cannot rely solely on FDG-FET for diagnosis. A liver biopsy should be performed routinely when the diagnosis of CHCC is suspected.

Surgical resection is the primary treatment of CHCC [12,13], and TACE [14] and systemic chemotherapy [15] should be also considered. The role of liver transplantation in CHCC has yet to be defined [12,16]. Regarding for the high recurrence rate of CHCC, postoperative combination treatments such as TACE and systemic chemotherapy should also be considered. Previous research has proven major hepatectomy performed in CHCC with cirrhosis should be associated with poor prognosis (5-year overall survival rate: 34.5 % versus 54.1 %, P = 0.032) [17]. Considered the separated distribution of lesions and the background of HBV infection and liver cirrhosis, minor hepatectomy was performed in this case. But the patient refused to accept further treatment and regular follow up, which had delayed the early diagnosis of tumor recurrence and might have hampered his overall survival.

Conclusion

In short, we report here a rare case of CHCC (a mixed hepatocellular and bile duct carcinoma subtype) with 3 components of tumor cells coexisting in one liver, the etiology of which is still not clear. Minor hepatectomy should be performed for patients with the coexistence of PCHCC, Intrahepatic Cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), and ordinary-type HCC(OHCC), especially when combined with cirrhosis. After surgery, the regular treatment and follow-up are necessary, but the prognosis is poor.
