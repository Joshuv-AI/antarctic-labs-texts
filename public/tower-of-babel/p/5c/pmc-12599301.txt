Indigenous perspectives of a culturally tailored dietary intervention: Qualitative findings from the CHEERS study
Authors: Susanna V Lopez, Sara London, Tori Taniguchi, Kaylee R Clyma, Kristina E Gabriel, Joy Standridge, Tyra Shackleford, Valarie B B Jernigan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA nutritious diet is an important protective factor against hypertension and cardiovascular disease. However, there are substantial barriers to healthy eating within American Indian (AI) communities resulting from colonization, the effects of which are still present today in systemic inequities. To address these inequities, many AI communities work toward restoration of access to and knowledge of traditional (i.e. pre-colonial) foods. The Chickasaw Healthy Eating Environments Research Study (CHEERS) was one such approach that provided Chickasaw Nation citizens with heart-healthy foods in line with the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet, as foods in the DASH diet represent many foods found in traditional AI diets, such as beans and fresh produce. In consultation with Chickasaw Nation community partners, there was interest in exploring lived experiences with traditional food and food practices and any recognized connections with overall health. Therefore, the current study is a qualitative analysis exploring these concepts with a subset of CHEERS participants. Emerging themes suggest participants have rich lived experiences with traditional foods and food practices and recognize many connections between traditional foods and health. These results substantiate the importance of traditional diets on AI health and can be integrated to strengthen culturally tailored nutrition interventions.

Introduction

Within American Indian (AI) communities, cardiovascular disease (CVD) continues to present a significant public health concern. AIs exhibit disproportionate rates of morbidity and mortality from CVD when compared to the general population and experience an elevated prevalence of CVD-related risk factors, including hypertension (defined as systolic or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 130 or 80 mmHg, respectively; 39% versus 29%) [1–3]. AIs with hypertension are less likely to achieve optimal blood pressure (BP) control than non-Native individuals [4].

Poor diet is the primary risk factor for hypertension and CVD, yet many AI communities experience substantial barriers to healthy eating [5]. These difficulties originate from colonization that resulted in heavy losses of AI life, culture, and land [6]. Tribes were often barred from engaging in their spiritual practices, speaking their native language, and wearing their traditional clothing [7]. In addition to this cultural-wide loss and trauma, settler colonialism disrupted traditional Indigenous food systems. AI people were forcibly removed from their traditional lands to new, unfamiliar regions where their standard ecological practices could not be easily replicated in the new environments and resulted in an abrupt disconnection to traditional foods and food practices [6]. Specific to the Chickasaw Nation of Oklahoma, their original homelands took up parts of present-day Mississippi, Alabama, Tennessee, and Kentucky, before relocating to Oklahoma in the mid-1800s [8]. Without traditional knowledge of foods in these new lands, many AI communities, including the Chickasaw Nation, suffered from starvation and became overly reliant on government distribution programs such as the Food Distribution Program on Indian Reservations (FDPIR), which, until recently, rarely provided fresh vegetables and instead provided canned, packaged, and processed foods high in fat, sugar, and sodium to ˃275 tribes [9, 10]. Over time, the limited availability of traditional foods and abundance of highly processed Western foods led to high prevalence of hypertension and CVD among AIs [6].

The impacts of colonization and disrupted traditional food systems remain evident today in the Chickasaw Nation. In a prior study involving the Chickasaw Nation, findings showed that just 57% of participants found it convenient to buy fruits and vegetables locally, and only 35% perceived the available produce to be of high quality. Moreover, more than half (56%) reported traveling at least 20 miles round trip to shop for food, posing a challenge for AIs to achieve the recommended daily intake of fruits and vegetables [11]. These findings were supported by a subsequent study, which revealed that 56% of participants reported insufficient access to enough food, and 62% reported insufficient access to high quality food [12]. After adjusting for age, gender, education, and income, individuals experiencing inadequate access to high quality food had a higher prevalence of hypertension compared to individuals with adequate access to high quality food (52.5% versus 42.5%) [12].

To address diet-related health disparities such as hypertension and CVD, many AI communities aim to restore access to traditional Indigenous foods that reflect pre-colonized dietary patterns, as these foods are highly nutritious and vary greatly across communities [13, 14]. One diet recommended by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) to prevent hypertension and CVD is the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet [15]. The DASH diet features foods low in sodium and fat and encourages intake of fruits, vegetables, legumes, nuts, fish, and minimally processed meats. This diet reflects many of the foods considered to be traditional foods in some AI communities and was therefore utilized to develop and pilot the Chickasaw Healthy Eating Environments Research Study (CHEERS), an intervention co-created by university and tribal partners to improve blood pressure, body mass index, food security, and diet among Chickasaw Nation adults with uncontrolled hypertension [16]. The current paper used qualitative methods to evaluate the reach, saturation, and acceptability of the CHEERS intervention. Following a community-based participatory research approach, focus groups with a subgroup of CHEERS participants were conducted to gain understanding of community-prioritized questions, namely: 1) how Chickasaw participants define traditional foods, 2) what lived experiences Chickasaw participants have with traditional foods and food practices, and 3) what connections participants perceive between lived experiences with traditional foods, food practices, and holistic aspects of health.

Materials and methods

The aims of the larger CHEERS study were as follows: [1] to evaluate the study’s ability to improve healthy food access by collecting quantitative data on changes in perceived ability to purchase and prepare healthy food, and by using qualitative methods to evaluate the reach, saturation, and acceptability of the CHEERS intervention; and [2] to measure the intervention’s effect on change in blood pressure and secondary outcomes among adults with poorly controlled hypertension [16]. The larger study was co-created by the Chickasaw Nation, Oklahoma State University, and Washington State University. The Chickasaw Nation Institutional Review Board (IRB), Department of Health, and Nutrition Services program oversaw and approved of every component of the study, including the grant proposal submission, intervention planning stage, recruitment, data collection, data analysis and interpretation, manuscript writing, and publication. The CHEERS study was reviewed and approved by the Oklahoma State University Center for Health Sciences IRB (protocol #2021026) and the Chickasaw Nation IRB.

The CHEERS intervention consisted of monthly food box deliveries, including heart-healthy foods, educational materials, and healthy recipes. All participants also received a physical activity tracker, a culturally tailored smartphone application, and a fitness center membership. More information regarding the CHEERS intervention can be found elsewhere [16, 17]. Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, we were forced to stop recruitment early for the intervention. In lieu of continuing recruitment, and as part of Aim 1 of the study, we held focus groups to discuss perspectives of the culturally tailored CHEERS intervention. The decision to pivot to focus groups was a joint decision between university and tribal partners in the IRB, the Department of Health, and the Nutrition Services program. The study changes were approved by all IRBs. The tribal IRB, Department of Health, and Nutrition Services program continued to oversee focus group recruitment, qualitative data collection, qualitative data analysis and interpretation, manuscript writing, and publication for the qualitative component of the CHEERS study. The current manuscript describes methods and results from the focus groups. The Chickasaw Nation IRB, Department of Health, Nutrition Services program, and the Communications Department reviewed the manuscript and approved it for submission for publication.

Focus group recruitment and data collection

Data collection for focus groups occurred in the summer of 2022. Recruitment was completed by sending emails to all currently enrolled CHEERS participants. An initial email was sent to participants inviting them to participate in the focus group. If participants were interested, they were asked to reply to the email at their earliest convenience. Once we reached capacity, we closed enrollment and let participants know in a follow-up email that enrollment was closed. Informed consent was obtained online before data collection began. The data collection was estimated to last ~75–90 min for each focus group session and was facilitated by an individual from the research team with a co-facilitator who assisted with timekeeping and notes. After data collection, participants were mailed an $80 gift card for participating in the focus group.

Focus groups

Focus groups were held with AI adults with uncontrolled hypertension who were part of the CHEERS study. Focus group methodology is vital when working with AI communities as it captures cultural norms and provides a unique understanding of participants’ lived experiences using their own words [18]. We aimed to recruit 27 participants, split into three sessions (nine participants per session). Due to the ongoing pandemic, we held focus groups online via Zoom. The open-ended, semi-structured focus group questions are listed in Table 1. The purpose of the focus groups was to understand perceptions and lived experiences with traditional foods, how they relate to overall health, and how they are prioritized in Chickasaw Nation.

Data analysis

With participants’ permission, the three focus groups were video recorded using the recording function on the Zoom video platform and transcribed verbatim through the Zoom automatic transcription feature. Data organization and coding were conducted using Dedoose version 9 [19]. Two qualitative analysts reviewed the qualitative data. The head coder (SL) is a Senior Scientist Qualitative Researcher with expertise in Indigenous research methodologies and qualitative research design. As a doctoral candidate with a MEd in Leadership and Higher Education, she has several years of experience utilizing community-based participatory research with AI communities investigating topics such as historical trauma, adverse childhood experiences, and resilience. The second coder (SVL) was carefully trained by the head coder in qualitative analysis. SVL has a PhD in Clinical Psychology and has several years of experience conducting quantitative research and community-based participatory research with AI communities on topics related to mental health, such as drinking among AI college students and the relationship between food insecurity on mental health outcomes. She currently works as a psychologist with the Chickasaw Nation. The two analysts independently coded the first focus group transcript to create a codebook. The two analysts met to resolve any differences in coding and finalize the codebook, which was then used to guide coding of the final two transcripts. To describe and interpret themes relating to the role of lived experiences with traditional foods on health, themes were derived from the text following a conventional content analysis methodology [20]. General descriptive characteristics of the focus groups are shared below. Descriptive statistics will not be used to quantify the qualitative findings so as to avoid misinterpretation of qualitative findings and to protect participant anonymity [21, 22].

The results were then shared with two co-authors who work for the Chickasaw Nation (JS and TS), who assisted with data interpretation, and approved the final results. The full manuscript was then submitted to the Chickasaw Nation communications department and IRB, who approved the manuscript’s content and cultural accuracy.

Results

There were a total of three focus group sessions with 22 participants: six in session 1, eight in session 2, and eight in session 3. More than half (73%) were women. Focus groups lasted approximately 78–96 minutes long. Three themes and six subthemes emerged relating to traditional food experiences and how these experiences impact overall health and connection to land.

Traditional food experiences

Several guiding questions during the focus groups were related to knowledge of and experience with traditional foods. These questions sparked a discussion wherein participants shared stories of growing, gathering, and hunting traditional foods and eating those foods with family members. The first theme from the focus groups was therefore Traditional Food Experiences. Within this theme, three subthemes emerged: 1) Traditional Foods; 2) Traditional Food Practices; and 3) Family and Community Connection.

Traditional foods

Focus group participants were first asked to name foods they considered traditional. While participants named a wide variety of foods, the most frequent foods mentioned were beans, fry bread, wild onions, and Pashofa (a traditional Chickasaw dish oftentimes made from hominy and pork). Participants named many other fruits and vegetables, such as blackberries, grapes, wild mushrooms, wild plums, tomatoes, peppers, and meats such as bison, deer, and pork. Notably, many participants named commodity foods as traditional, as they were sometimes the only items they had access to. One participant explained,

Growing up, I lived here in Ada, and we had got what you call commodity food. So whatever the commodities they gave us, we cooked it. Like, for dessert. We didn't get to go to town and get cookies or ice cream or anything like that. So they give you peanut butter. So we would make peanut butter cookies, peanut butter pie… So my mother would cook with all the commodities that we got, we didn't buy bread. We made fry bread. If it wasn't fry bread, it would be biscuits. … Mostly we ate everything that was the commodities because that's what they issued to all the Native American Indians. I don't remember it having any, we didn't get vegetables. It was canned meat, peanut butter. … We didn't get like canned biscuits. We didn't get like loaf of bread. (4143_SPB)

This participant later shared that ‘peanut butter pie’ became a valued traditional food during Thanksgiving, and that it was important to them to pass the recipe down to their children.

Traditional food practices

Participants were asked to share stories and reflections on traditional food practices. They named many practices they participated in or witnessed from family and community. The most frequent responses were intergenerational traditional food practices, gardening, growing, gathering, cooking, and raising livestock.

Many reflections included sharing traditional food practices across generations and the shared sentiment that traditional food practices need to be passed on to future generations. Participants shared their stories of cooking, gathering, and gardening with their grandparents. They also shared their memories of witnessing their mothers and other family members preparing food in their homes or bringing home food to prepare for meals. One participant reflected on traditional foods gathered and prepared by their grandparents from their garden:

I know I can remember growing up … my mom lives on my grandpa's land and just right beside the house, my grandpa had like, a big garden, and we used to help every summer harvesting those foods. Every summer, every morning, having to go. All summer long, getting up in the morning. And I remember we never wanted for anything with corn, green beans. And in the evening time, we'd be out there cleaning green beans and strawberries and berries. We'd have all the strawberries we wanted. Yeah, and we had corn and all that stuff. And my grandma would be canning all that stuff. We'd have it down in the cellar and she'd make fried pies and pies. You don't see those things nowadays. (1007_SBP)

Another participant shared the importance of passing knowledge and ‘carrying on’ traditional food practices to future generations:

And I think they put the love into that tradition food. And so that's why you always want to carry it on or make sure that the next person knows how to make it. I mean, I hope they can remember saying old 4143_SPB told us, always make a peanut butter pie for Thanksgiving. So I want them to carry it on and on. So that was something that, you know, I want them to always remember that it's always been in our family. (4143_SBP)

Growing, gardening, and gathering was another rich response surrounding participants’ reflections on traditional food practices. Participants shared many stories about the food they grew and gathered with their parents, grandparents, and family members when they were children. Some participants had their own gardens and found it helpful for their health and wellness. The participants saw growing and gathering as a traditional food practice and shared how they believed it is healthier than modern food access. One participant shared their story of growing up with a garden and how it helped them always have food they needed:

We always grew up with a garden, and we've moved several times. Mama grew up out in New Mexico, so that's when we would go get the beans. Every time we go visit grandma, we'd go get big bags of beans and bring them back. So we always knew we'd have those beans to eat, and she would have us pick different seeds to plant ourselves. In that way, that was like our little crop that we would take care of, and then she would have all the regular stuff that we needed for the rest of the year. We always had chickens. (4134_KC)

Cooking was another common response participants shared regarding how they practice traditional food preparation. Some participants reflected on the joy and laughter they shared with their families in the kitchen cooking food and how cooking was a primary activity for events such as ceremony preparation, tribal fairs, church gatherings, and family reunions. They also shared many ways to cook traditional foods, and how they cook it can affect how healthy it is. One participant shared their story of cooking for a reunion alongside their family:

I help my aunts and whenever the Chickasaw Nation... I know every year at the Chickasaw reunion, I'm the one that's usually frying at the salt pork while my other aunts make grape dumplings, fry bread. All those traditional foods there--I would say that in preparing traditional foods, you didn't do it by yourself. You always had a family member to help you, and that's still carrying on, that we do it together and just not one person is doing it. (4121_TJ)

Participants also shared their past experiences raising and butchering livestock, commonly referring to chickens, hogs, and cows. Some participants also shared how they are currently accessing and raising livestock to return to healthier eating:

I think we're going back to more traditional foods now with everything skyrocketing. So, like, people are getting chickens, buying cows. We're going back to the olden days. I feel just because you can't afford it, it's sad that you can afford a cow more than you can afford to go get ground beef or milk or anything like that. But I saw where eggs are supposed to be $22 by the end of the year. (4081_SPB)

Family and community connection

While reflecting on healthy food practices and well-being, participants shared the importance of incorporating a family and community connection with food practices to maintain their happiness and health. They used the terms ‘sharing,’ ‘connection,’ ‘laughter,’ and ‘fun’ when sharing stories about food preparation with their loved ones and communities. One participant shared how important it was to bond with their daughters through food gathering:

Some of our older daughter's funnest memories are going out and picking sand plums with Mimi to take in to go make jelly, or to eat the plums. They'll just eat them right off the tree. So just having that relationship. I think it's a lot about community and about connecting and being there with your kids and your kids in the community. (4134_KC)

Another participant shared their memory of watching their aunts and mother connecting over cooking and how it brought them joy, connection, and used it as a tool for spreading a passion for learning:

4121_TJ: Well, I just know that whenever I'm cooking with my aunts, my mom has seven sisters, and there's at least five of them, six of them, that we all get together when we do cook like that. There's a lot of laughter, there's a lot of sharing, there's a lot of... Some gossiping also that's going on. I enjoy being with my aunts and doing that.

Interviewer: Do we consider that something that's important within our food traditions?

4121_TJ: I think it is. Now, even seeing the little ones brought around, and I think it's good for them to see too. Maybe one day they'll want to learn.

Health

Participants were asked to reflect on what traditional foods are healthy. While reflecting on this question, they discussed their overall thoughts on health-related food practices such as how cooking and preparation affect the nutritional benefits of foods, the common health issues they have due to their intake of food and lifestyle, and the effects of physical activity on their well-being. Thus, the second theme from the focus groups was Health, and the three subthemes that emerged from this theme were: 1) Cooking and Preparation; 2) Health Struggles and Illness; 3) Mental, Spiritual, and Emotional Health.

Cooking and preparation

When reflecting on healthy food practices, many participants shared their beliefs about how different styles of cooking and food preparation impact making food healthy. They described unhealthy cooking practices such as cooking with grease, frying food, and adding sugar and salt.

One participant shared how preparing food in different ways can affect their health:

I know we've talked about it before, but I don't think necessarily the kind of food you pick, but I think, like people have said before, it's mainly how you prepare the food. I've seen people lose weight on different diets baking their chicken, grilling their chicken or beef steak, whatever. I know of people who've got healthy preparing their food in different ways. (4082_KC)

Another participant reflected on how food preparation can affect the food’s level of health and how additives can affect food:

I think even though we have to basically rely on processed food that we buy at the store, that we can help ourselves by the way we cook it. I forgot who said, baking it or charring it or whatever. That instead of always frying it up or adding a lot of salt to it... Especially the vegetables, I think about squash and how good it taste whenever you just steam it. Even your okra, even though that sometimes it's fried – I always wonder how they fried their stuff in lard, pure lard and how that was more unhealthy than us using the oils. I don't know. But I think a lot goes into how we prepare it and how we eat it. How do we pick the tomatoes? As soon as we get the tomatoes, we put a bunch of salt on it or some people put sugar on it. But do we eat it plain or how much do we eat of it? (4064_TJ)

Participants also discussed healthy ways to cook food. They indicated baking and steaming vegetables is better for health than frying them. They discussed cooking in a crock pot versus frying, not using grease to cook foods, and using less salt while cooking are all ways to prepare healthier meals. One participant reflected on how they believed their traditional food preparation is not healthy and could be cooked in a better way for their health and wellness:

I think most of the time, as traditional food is normally cooked, it is not healthy per se, but it can be cooked healthily. My family, we fry the squash instead of baking it or roasting it. But I think a lot of ingredients that you find in traditional foods can be cooked in a healthy way, but most of the time in traditional cooking, they're not cooked healthily. (2008_KC)

Health struggles and illness

While reflecting on healthy food practices and lifestyles, the participants shared their health struggles and perceptions of food intake-related illnesses in the Native population with modern-day food practices and food access. One participant observed high prevalence of chronic diseases:

One thing that I have noticed in the Native American, first American communities is there is a high rate of high cholesterol, high blood pressure, diabetes. You throw a stick in a crowd of Chickasaws and you'll come out with at least three that have diabetes or high blood pressure. (2008_KC)

One mentioned how food preparation and access to traditional food has changed and how that has affected their grandparents:

I think our grandmothers and that older group right there, diabetes kind of wiped them out because they didn't grow up eating sugar, or something like that. We said they ate a lot of deer or whatever. If they lived out in the country, squirrel, rabbits, fish, things of that nature. Then once that generation got a little older and they got into sugar, that diabetes started wiping out, which we are still going through right now. (4032_TJ)

Mental, spiritual, and emotional health

Participants were asked how traditional food affects mental, spiritual, and emotional health. Participants responded that traditional food boosts these aspects of health through social connection, spiritual connection to their ancestors, and improved diet. Some have noticed that practicing a healthy lifestyle has improved their mood, while eating ‘fast food’ or ‘greasy’ food has made them feel ‘lazy and tired.’

Many stated the importance of eating together, as that is an avenue of connection and ‘developing relationships.’ One participant stated,

When you're cooking traditional foods, usually you have a large group of people there, you have your family there, you have your church community or neighborhood community. That brings in a lot of laughter … just a lot of joking. Being around that atmosphere, I think is extremely healthy for your mental and emotional wellbeing. (4044_TJ)

While many emphasized the mental health benefits of cooking in a group, some also described the spiritual benefits of preparing and eating traditional food:

With traditional food, I eat a piece of fried bread, I'm in a happy place. It reminds me of the families that came before me, my ancestors, this is what, the way they were eating, what they survived on. It brings me closer to them in a way to know that I'm still eating the same foods they're eating. That's how it connects me emotionally and spiritually. (2008_KC)

Others noted that some foods are associated with good feelings. As one participant stated,

I know that some days when I've had a very, particularly rough day at work, I come home and supper's on the table, and it's one of my favorite foods, and that instantly picks up my mood, it makes me feel better. (2008_KC)

Land connection

The last theme from the focus groups was Land Connection. Participants were asked whether there were any perceived connections between the land and what they ate. Various responses were discussed, as some emphasized a connection while others denied a connection between the land and the food they ate. Notably, some shared how ‘modern-day life’ is ‘fast-paced,’ and they do not have time to build and maintain gardens and incorporate traditional food practices into their busy schedules. One participant explained,

Today, I don't think so. I think it's more about convenience. What's fast? What can I get? Everybody's in a hurry. So I don't think, as far as looking at it that way, convenience and something fast. There's fast food restaurants, and either, where I'm from, there's a lot of those and you can get something fast from a gas station. So looking at it that way, I feel like, no. (4082_KC)

I agree with you. I think it's a lot of convenience. You can walk into the grocery store now and buy anything practically. Even right down to some cactus leaves. So I don't think it has so much to do with the land anymore, not what we eat today. (4026_KC)

Others had differing opinions, reflecting on the importance of connection to the land when hunting, growing, and gathering traditional food. Some described the food and the land as being ‘one in the same’ and ‘intertwined.’ Many noted that different regions have different traditional foods because of the climate and types of foods that thrive on the land. One participant reflected,

I've lived all over the United States, and when we lived in Maryland and Virginia and Pennsylvania, they don't grow okra up there. They don't even know what okra is. Of course, they don't know what grit is either. But Oklahoma has just so many different vegetables grown here where other states don't have. When I lived in California, we had orange trees, lemon trees in our backyard, and it was so different. (1002_KC)

Discussion

This study explored participant perceptions of 1) Traditional Food Experiences; 2) Health; and 3) Land Connection. Several subthemes emerged, including the role of traditional food experiences on family and community connection; healthy cooking and preparation practices; and mental, spiritual, and emotional health. The insights gathered in the current study represent valuable knowledge of how experiences with traditional foods and food practices impact overall health among Chickasaw citizens.

Participants named many fruits, vegetables, and meats that were considered traditional as well as commodity foods, as some stated the majority of their food came from food distribution programs. Despite this, participants described other ways to improve access to food, such as growing their own food, foraging for berries, and raising livestock. Participants recognized the importance of these traditional practices not only for their health, but also for connection to family and community. Indeed, these are longstanding practices that have documented health benefits among many other AI communities [23].

Our study further indicated participants knew how to incorporate healthy cooking and preparation practices such as grilling or baking instead of frying as well as reducing sodium intake. They reported noticeable differences when eating well (e.g. improved mood) versus other food such as fast foods (e.g. ‘lazy,’ ‘tired’). They also described strong connections between lived experiences with traditional foods and improved mental, spiritual, and emotional health. A significant driver of this relationship appears to be social connection—large groups and family members come together to cook, and eating traditional food reminds people of the generations that came before them as well as the opportunity to pass these traditions down to future generations. This social and intergenerational connection has been cited in past research as a key element of physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual health among AIs [24].

Finally, participants discussed how food is ‘one and the same’ and ‘intertwined’ with the land. They explained that definitions of traditional foods differ across communities based on the climate and landscape they were connected to. However, some participants believed that due to modern society and the focus on fast production and convenient food choices, food is no longer connected to the land. Other communities have expressed similar concerns and have emphasized revitalization of traditional food ways and reconnection to land are necessary to restore traditional food practices, thereby addressing ongoing health disparities [25].

Overall, participants provided many examples of strength and resilience in their community. While they acknowledged struggles such as the shortcomings of commodity foods and high rates of diabetes, they also discussed the ability of their community to come together and connect over food, to honor their ancestors who have survived on the very food are now eating, to pass recipes and traditional food knowledge down to new generations, and to take what resources they have been given and turn it into something valued, such as peanut butter pie. It is a testament to the endurance of AI peoples and a reminder that AI health interventions should focus on strengths (versus deficits) [26].

Colonization and systematic genocidal tactics have disrupted and transformed AI communities by seeking to eradicate and marginalize their cultural voices and traditional knowledge [27]. Our study substantiates evidence that traditional foods pre-colonization feeds not only the physical body, but also the mind and spirit [28]. Information on nutrition and healthy cooking practices alone are not enough to create long-term health improvements. By restoring relationships with traditional foods, lands, and skills, AI communities may be able to achieve the healing needed to address chronic diseases among their people.

Upon completion of this study, findings were presented more broadly to Chickasaw Nation community members as part of a tribal grant planning initiative to increase access to traditional foods and education about traditional food practices. Study findings helped guide the development of a large-scale community garden initiative at the Chickasaw Nation hospital, a project currently underway.

In Oklahoma, tribally led food interventions have already led to positive outcomes. For example, increasing the availability of fruits and vegetables in tribally owned convenience stores led to higher sales of fruits and vegetables in some Oklahoma tribal nations [29]. A farm-to-school family intervention on diet in another Oklahoma tribe led to significantly increased squash and bean intake among children [30] and significantly decreased carbohydrate, cholesterol, and caffeine intake among adult caregivers [31]. This intervention led to short-term improvements in menu and meal quality provided by schools as well [32]. Notably, in another study using mixed methods to assess what it means to eat healthy among citizens of several Oklahoma tribes, participants discussed the importance of intergenerational connections through food and learning the historical context of certain foods as part of their definition of healthy eating [33]. Taken together with the results of the current study, the knowledge gathered in this study may therefore be incorporated in future culturally grounded health programs within the Chickasaw Nation and could potentially be shared with other communities.

Limitations to the study should be considered. As all participants were members of the Chickasaw Nation, this sample is not representative of all AI communities. Additionally, qualitative methods are subject to analysis bias, reactivity of the participant to the interviewer, recall error, and self-serving responses [34]. However, qualitative data is a critical methodology that illuminates Indigenous knowledge to a depth that often cannot be captured with quantitative methods [35].

The present study indicated CHEERS participants had positive insight into and knowledge of traditional foods and healthy food practices. They further recognized many mental, spiritual, and emotional health benefits such as community and intergenerational connection by sharing food-related knowledge and recipes. Future studies should continue to evaluate health promotion programs that incorporate traditional food and food practices as a culturally grounded approach to address CVD and related health conditions among AI individuals at risk of these outcomes.
