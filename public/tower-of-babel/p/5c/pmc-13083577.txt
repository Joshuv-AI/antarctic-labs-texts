Interventions to Reduce Surgical Site Infection in Transmetatarsal Forefoot Amputations: A Feasibility Survey
Authors: Lakna Harindi Alawattegama, Nina Al‐Saadi, David Bosanquet, Ian Chetter, Ismay Fabre, Andrew Garnham, Brenig Gwilym, Louise Hitchman, Judith Long, Laura Magill, Katie Worrallo, Thomas Pinkney, Matthew Popplewell, Michael Wall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSurgical site infection (SSI) following transmetatarsal amputation (TMA) is common and associated with significant morbidity. However, there is limited evidence to guide perioperative strategies for SSI prevention in this population. A prospective, cross‐sectional survey was conducted among vascular specialists. The questionnaire assessed current practice in SSI prevention for TMA, perceptions of evidence gaps, and willingness to participate in future research. Responses were analysed descriptively. Eighty‐four valid responses were analysed, with 64.3% from consultant vascular surgeons and 84.5% from UK centres. Most respondents (84.5%) considered SSI after TMA to be a significant issue. The majority preferred primary closure in clean wounds (78.6%) and commonly used short‐course antibiotics and interrupted sutures. Over 70% agreed no clear best practice exists, and 65.5% felt high‐quality randomised trials are needed. Ninety‐three percent expressed willingness to engage in future studies. Equipoise existed regarding interventions including antibiotic duration, wound adjuncts, and closure techniques. Opinion varied regarding whether TMA and major lower limb amputation should be pooled when undertaking research into SSI prevention. Current practice in SSI prevention for TMA is highly variable. This survey demonstrates broad support for rigorous trials to establish effective strategies and highlights the feasibility of future research in this area.Keywords: amputation, infection, perioperative care, surgical site infection, transmetatarsal

Introduction

In 2022–23, 1872 transmetatarsal forefoot amputations (TMAs) were performed in England [1]. The reporting of incidence, predisposing factors and outcomes for TMA is poor compared to major lower limb amputation (MLLA). Both the Vascular Society of Great Britain and Ireland (VSGBI) [2] and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) [3] have published guidelines to improve post‐operative outcomes following MLLA. The incidence of surgical site infection (SSI) following TMA surgery has been reported as 24.0% in one systematic review (5 studies, 233 patients) [4] and 16.7% (range 3.0%–30.7%) in another systematic review [5], with re‐intervention rates as high as 46% [6]. Post‐TMA SSI may lead to revision to a more proximal MLLA, which is associated with prolonged hospital stay and increased morbidity, mortality and healthcare costs [6, 7, 8].

The evidence for SSI‐reducing interventions specifically in TMA surgery is limited. Five studies published between 2005 and 2022 evaluated interventions in TMAs, including prolonged pre‐operative and postoperative antibiotic courses, the use of intra‐operative antibiotic beads, skin preparation use and negative‐pressure wound therapy (NPWT) application to open wounds [8, 9, 10, 11, 12].

Souroullas et al. [8], who evaluated both TMA and MLLA patients, reported a significant reduction in SSI post‐TMA with 5 days of prophylactic antibiotics; however, other studies have shown that a prolonged course of post‐operative antibiotics (i.e., > 2 weeks) did not have an effect on re‐amputation rates [11] and that the duration of pre‐ or post‐operative antibiotics in TMAs were not related to SSI rates [10]. Krause et al. [12] reported the use of antibiotic‐impregnated beads in a TMA wound prior to closure increased the SSI rate compared to the control group (p < 0.005, OR: 8.89, 95% CI: 2.28–34.7), though the wound breakdown rate was lower (8.2% vs. 25.0%). The use of alcoholic chlorhexidine and alcoholic povidone iodine has also been evaluated with no significant difference in SSI rates [8]. Armstrong et al. [9] reported a statistically higher SSI rate in TMA wounds where NPWT was applied compared to the control group (17% vs. 6.0%). They also reported a reduction in wound healing time (56 days vs. 77 days, p = 0.005). The recent SWHSI‐2 trial [13] (686 patients) concluded that NPWT did not reduce time to healing or SSI rates in foot wounds compared to standard dressings.

Given the lack of evidence regarding SSI prevention in TMA surgery, we aimed to characterise current practice and assess clinician equipoise regarding potential randomised control trial (RCT) interventions by surveying vascular specialists.

Materials and Methods

The checklist for reporting of survey studies (CROSS) was used to compile this survey manuscript (see Supporting Information: Appendix 1).

Questionnaire Development, Validation and Content

The questionnaire was designed by three core investigators (two consultant vascular surgeons and a vascular trainee), who were part of a wider Surgical Site Infections in Major Lower Limb Amputation: Transmetatarsal Extension (SIMBA‐T) Group. Questions were subsequently reviewed by the wider group of 10–15 clinicians and researchers (including consultant vascular surgeons, professors of surgery, vascular surgery trainees and research fellows) for assessment of suitability, readability and feasibility. Feedback was incorporated over two iterative rounds, which led to some questions being reworded and additional intervention options being added. The survey included 14 questions related to TMA SSI, current strategies employed to reduce SSI in TMAs, participant equipoise and willingness to participate in a potential RCT investigating various combinations of interventions. Responses were either dichotomous, multiple choice via tick box or dropdown menus or scored using a Likert scale. Participants that answered ‘no’ to the use of interventions or participation in a RCT were requested to provide explanations. The final version can be found in Supporting Information: Appendix 2.

Survey Administration

A prospective, cross‐sectional survey was launched at the Vascular Societies of Great Britain & Ireland (VSGBI), Annual Scientific Meeting on 27th November 2024 (Brighton, United Kingdom) [14]. The target population was consultant vascular surgeons, vascular surgery trainees and clinical fellows of all grades, and any allied health professionals that are involved in the care of vascular or diabetic patients with surgical wounds. The meeting was attended by approximately 950 people. The VSGBI administration office provided attendance figures, roles and organisations for the meeting. Members of industry were excluded. Convenience sampling was largely used to capture data via an online Google Form questionnaire [15], supplemented by broad promotion to maximise reach. Survey responses were only accessible to the first author (L.H.A.). The questionnaire was accessed by either scanning a custom‐generated QR code at the meeting or clicking a direct link. It was also advertised via the Vascular and Endovascular Research Network (VERN: https://vascular‐research.net) and the VSGBI administration office. The questionnaire was kept open after the meeting until 31st January 2025 to maximise responses. Questionnaire participants were anonymous but provided their occupation, grade and current institution to inform the survey results. Participants also provided an email address, which was used to screen for multiple participation. Other measures taken to prevent multiple participation include an instruction within the introduction to ‘only complete the survey once’, and removing the link to ‘submit another response’ at the end of the questionnaire.

Ethical Approval

Ethical approval was not sought as the survey did not meet the requirements specified by the Health Research Authority [16], however, the principles of Good Clinical Practice (GCP) were followed [17].

Data Analysis

Survey results and data could only be accessed by the first author (L.H.A.). Data were exported from Google Forms into Microsoft Excel for cleaning and analysis which was password protected and only accessed by the first author (L.H.A.). Incomplete questionnaires were included in the analysis. Duplicate entries by the same participant were excluded. Dichotomous data were expressed as a percentage and multiple‐choice elements were expressed as parts of the whole (%). Likert scales were presented graphically.

Results

Nine hundred and sixty‐four individuals attended the Vascular Society Annual Scientific Meeting (VSASM) in 2024. Of these, 121 (12.6%) identified themselves as a consultant vascular surgeon. Eighty‐five individuals completed the survey, giving an overall response rate of 8.8%; one response was excluded due to it being the 2nd response from the same individual. The majority of respondents were consultant vascular surgeons (54/84; 64.3%) as per Figure 1. Nearly 85% (71/84) of respondents represented UK centres compared to 12 responses from outside the United Kingdom (14.3%). The distribution of UK centres geographically is presented in Table 1, with the majority of responses from the West Midlands (18/84) and Yorkshire (13/84). A large proportion of respondents felt that SSI was a problem in TMAs (71/84; 84.5%) with 57.8% of this group stating that they felt it was a ‘major problem’ (41/71).

Current Practice

The majority of people would choose to close a TMA wound primarily in patients with intact blood supply and no concerns regarding infection (66/84, 78.6%), with fewer opting for delayed primary closure and closure by secondary intention (6/84, 7.14% and 11/84, 13.1%, respectively). One person did not respond to this question. The most common strategies used to reduce infection in TMAs by survey respondents were short‐course antibiotics and wound closure using interrupted sutures (Figure 2). ‘Other’ strategies include use of steristrips, tension‐free wound closure +/− application of VAC therapy and targeted antibiotic use based on intra‐operative clean proximal bone samples.

Responses to the question ‘What strategies do you presently deploy to reduce infection in trans‐metatarsal amputations which you primarily close if vascular supply is adequate?’ in a survey of 84 participants.

Current Evidence Base

Most survey respondents agreed that there is no current best practice for reducing SSI in patients undergoing TMA (61/84, 72.6%) and that high‐quality research, such as a RCT, is required to determine which interventions are effective at reducing SSI post‐TMA (55/84, 65.5%—Figure 3).

Likert scale responses to statements regarding best practice for reducing SSI in transmetatarsal amputations and the requirement of high‐quality evidence in this field.

RCT Participation

The majority of respondents stated that they would be happy to engage in a study of interventions to potentially reduce SSI following TMA (78/84, 92.9%). Participants that were not happy to participate were asked to provide a reason in a free‐text box. Almost all of the reasons given were that they do not perform this procedure or lack of experience with TMA surgery. One respondent expressed that they had recently withdrawn input into local services to improve TMA outcomes due to lack of funding. Discussion

This survey represents the largest assessment to‐date of contemporary practice and attitudes regarding the prevention of SSI in patients undergoing TMA. The findings highlight a marked variation in perioperative strategies, as well as widespread recognition among vascular specialists that SSI is a major problem in this patient group.

Most respondents (84.5%) considered SSI following TMA to be a significant issue, with over half identifying it as a major problem. This aligns with prior reports estimating SSI rates between 16.7% and 24%, and reintervention rates as high as 46% following TMA [4, 5, 6]. Despite this burden, there remains no consensus on the optimal perioperative management strategies to reduce infection. The survey results confirm substantial heterogeneity in practice, with the most common interventions including short‐course antibiotics, interrupted suture closure, and occasional use of adjunctive measures such as NPWT and antibiotic‐impregnated materials. These findings are consistent with previous systematic reviews demonstrating inconsistent evidence for any single intervention [4, 5]. Similarly, the evidence supporting NPWT remains inconclusive. The recent large, multicentre RCT, SWHSI‐2 trial, demonstrated no reduction in healing time or SSI rates compared to standard dressings in open wounds [13]. Notably, in our survey, a proportion of respondents still indicated ongoing use of NPWT, underscoring the uncertainty and persistence of variation in practice. However, there were also concerns expressed in the survey ‘additional comments’ section regarding equipoise and including NPWT as an intervention in any future studies given the SWHSI‐2 trial results.

Encouragingly, a large majority of survey participants expressed a willingness to contribute to further research efforts, with over 90% indicating interest in participating in studies evaluating interventions for SSI prevention in TMA. Moreover, respondents demonstrated equipoise about a range of perioperative strategies, including the duration of antibiotic therapy, wound closure technique, use of antibiotic‐impregnated materials, and topical NPWT. This suggests that there is substantial support for conducting a robust RCT to generate high‐quality evidence. In this context, the feasibility of such research appears promising. Additionally, the economic implications of commonly used adjunctive interventions such as antibiotic‐impregnated beads and NPWT devices should be considered, as their higher costs may impact both adoption in routine practice and the design of adequately powered trials evaluating their cost‐effectiveness alongside clinical outcomes.

Interestingly, opinion was divided as to whether TMAs should be considered with MLLA in research and whether evidence from MLLA would be transferable to TMA care. Given that SSI rates and re‐operation rates are similar in TMA and MLLA, and that grouping the two procedures together would make future trials more feasible and deliverable, this would seem a sensible option.

This study has several strengths, including its multicentre design, broad representation of consultant vascular surgeons, and comprehensive survey instrument. However, there are important limitations. The response rate was relatively low compared to the number of conference attendees, which may introduce responder bias, as clinicians with a particular interest in SSI prevention were more likely to complete the survey. The modest response rate likely also reflects wider barriers to clinician engagement, including competing clinical demands, survey fatigue, and the perceived niche relevance of SSI following TMA compared with MLLA. Future studies may improve engagement through targeted dissemination to clinicians managing diabetic foot disease and integration with established research or audit networks. The majority of responses originated from the United Kingdom, potentially limiting the generalisability of the findings to other healthcare systems. Additionally, the self‐reported practices may not fully reflect actual clinical behaviour, and incomplete questionnaires were included in the analysis, which may have affected the consistency of responses. Finally, as with all cross‐sectional surveys, causality cannot be inferred.

Despite these limitations, this work provides an important foundation for the design of future studies. Given the lack of standardised perioperative protocols and the significant clinical and economic burden of SSI in TMA, there is a clear need for further high‐quality, prospective research. A pragmatic, multicentre trial evaluating the clinical and cost‐effectiveness of interventions such as antibiotic duration, wound adjuncts, and closure techniques could generate much‐needed evidence to inform practice and improve outcomes in this vulnerable patient group.

Translating these findings into a pragmatic RCT will require prospective ascertainment of baseline SSI rates following TMA and refinement of candidate interventions informed by the equipoise demonstrated in this survey. Additional preparatory work, such as confirmation of outcome definitions, follow‐up pathways and integration with existing research or audit infrastructure, could support the design of a deliverable multicentre trial.

Due to the population sampled, this survey can only reflect practice within vascular surgery. It is unclear if the findings here are representative of other specialties who perform TMA (orthopaedics/plastic surgery etc.).

Conclusion

In conclusion, this survey demonstrates substantial variation in SSI prevention strategies for TMA and highlights widespread recognition of the need for high‐quality evidence. The strong clinician support for further research underscores the feasibility and importance of dedicated trials to establish effective approaches to reduce SSI risk following TMA. There is broad support for a future multi‐arm multi‐stage trial investigating antibiotic course length, wound adjuncts and closure techniques.
