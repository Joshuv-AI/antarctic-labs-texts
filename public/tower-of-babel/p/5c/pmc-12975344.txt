Mid-term Outcomes Following Primary Covered Stent Implantation in Supra-Aortic Arch Vessels for Atherosclerotic Occlusive Disease
Authors: Georgios Sachsamanis, Piotr M Kasprzak, Wilma Schierling, Milena Pavlovic, Evgenia Zygouridou, Karin Pfister
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe aim of this study was to present the mid-term outcomes of patients undergoing treatment for atherosclerotic stenosis or occlusion of the supra-aortic arch vessels using covered stents.Materials and MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, single-center analysis of patients treated for supra-aortic arch vessel atherosclerotic occlusive disease (AOD) at our institution between 2015 and 2024. The study included symptomatic patients who received endovascular treatment with covered stents for lesions involving the subclavian artery, proximal common carotid artery (pCCA), or the brachiocephalic trunk (BT). All patients underwent preprocedural computed tomography angiography within 6 months and color duplex ultrasonography before and after the intervention.ResultsA total of 20 patients (8 male; mean age, 69.5±14.3 years) underwent primary covered stent implantation for supra-aortic arch vessel AOD, with 21 vessels treated (11 [52.4%], 6 [28.6%], and 4 [19.0%] in the subclavian artery, pCCA, and BT respectively). The main presenting symptoms were related to cerebrovascular insufficiency. Two patients presented with ischemic finger lesions due to subclavian artery occlusion. Technical success rate was 100% (21/21), and no intraoperative complications were recorded. Perioperative mortality was 0%. Two patients developed transient cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome after pCCA recanalization, one required temporary hemodialysis, and one underwent wound revision for access-site hematoma. Sixteen patients reported postoperative symptom resolution. Mean follow-up was 47.9±33.1 months (median, 40 months; interquartile range, 32 months). Mortality during follow-up was 30% (6/20), with no AOD-related deaths. During follow-up, no duplex ultrasound findings suggestive of >50% in-stent restenosis were detected, and no reinterventions were required.ConclusionCovered stent implantation for supra-aortic arch vessel atherosclerotic lesions appears to be associated with acceptable mid-term outcomes.Keywords: Atherosclerosis, Arterial occlusive diseases, Supra-aortic vessels, Stents, Covered stent

INTRODUCTION

Atherosclerotic lesions in the vessels of the supra-aortic arch can lead to a variety of severe symptoms affecting the upper extremities or cerebrovascular circulation. Lesions in the proximal common carotid artery (pCCA) that restrict blood flow may result in dizziness, headache, visual impairment, and stroke [1]. Similarly, stenosis of the subclavian artery can cause upper extremity claudication, muscle weakness, rest pain, or digital embolization. Additional symptoms may include vertigo and nausea, which arise from retrograde blood flow in the ipsilateral vertebral artery, potentially leading to vertebrobasilar insufficiency, a condition referred to as subclavian steal syndrome [1,2].

Several open surgical revascularization techniques are available for managing supra-aortic atherosclerotic occlusive disease (AOD), including extra-anatomic bypasses or transposition of the subclavian artery to the carotid artery [3]. In recent years, endovascular management using stent implantation has become increasingly popular due to its low mortality and morbidity rates, acceptable long-term patency, and shorter hospitalization periods [3,4]. Bare metal stents (BMS) are commonly used in these cases; however, they are associated with intimal hyperplasia and the potential for in-stent restenosis [4]. Implantation of covered stents represents a viable alternative; however, the literature comparing BMS and covered stents for management of supra-aortic arch AOD remains limited [5].

The aim of this study was to present the mid-term outcomes of patients undergoing management for atherosclerotic stenosis or occlusion for the supra-aortic arch vessels using covered stents.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1) Patient cohort

We performed a retrospective, single-center analysis of all patients who underwent treatment for supra-aortic arch vessel pathologies at our institution between 2015 and 2024. The study included patients who received endovascular treatment with covered stents for symptomatic AOD involving the subclavian artery, pCCA, or brachiocephalic trunk (BT). Recorded symptoms included arm claudication, signs of digital embolization, or signs of vertebrobasilar insufficiency (e.g., vertigo or nausea) in cases of subclavian artery stenosis, and symptoms suggestive of cerebrovascular insufficiency (e.g., dizziness, headache, or transient ischemic attack [TIA]) in cases of pCCA stenosis.

Covered stenting was the standard endovascular approach at our institution during the study period for the management of symptomatic supra-aortic AOD. This practice was guided by contemporary evidence suggesting favorable patency outcomes of covered stents compared with BMS in atherosclerotic lesions [6].

All patients underwent computed tomography angiography (CTA) within 6 months prior to the procedure. Additionally, all patients underwent color duplex ultrasonography (CDUS) examinations of the supra-aortic arch vessels before and after the intervention. Data collected for analysis included patient demographics, medical history, perioperative outcomes, and follow-up information. In cases of subclavian artery and BT disease, additional upper-extremity arterial blood pressure measurements of the target vessel were obtained before and immediately after the intervention. The Ethics Committee of University Medical Center Regensburg approved this study (Registration No. 24-3726-104). Since this was a retrospective analysis, informed consent was not obtained.

2) Endovascular procedure

Interventions were preferably performed under general anesthesia. Lesions of the subclavian artery or BT were treated via transbrachial, transaxillary, or transfemoral cut-down or percutaneous access, according to the operators’ preference. All lesions of the pCCA were treated through an ipsilateral retrograde transcarotid cut-down approach with distal surgical clamping for cerebral protection. No embolic protection device was used.

After systemic heparinization, the target lesion was crossed using a standard hydrophilic guidewire and an angiographic image was obtained. After advancement and deployment of a covered stent across the target lesion, a final angiography was performed. The access site was closed with direct reconstruction, and intraoperative CDUS was performed to assess target-vessel patency. Patients with AOD of the BT or pCCA were monitored postoperatively in the stroke unit. All patients were discharged on dual platelet therapy for 4-6 weeks.

3) Study endpoints

The primary endpoints were technical success, covered stent-related complications, and freedom from reintervention perioperatively and during follow-up. Technical success was defined as the absence of residual stenosis ≥50% within the target lesion after covered stent implantation. Freedom from reintervention was defined as the absence of clinically driven interventions related to the index procedure during follow-up, based on symptoms and imaging findings. Secondary endpoints were symptom relief and perioperative adverse events, including cardiac, pulmonary, renal, or neurological complications.

4) Follow-up and data analysis

All patients were monitored clinically and with CDUS postoperatively, at 6 and 12 months, and then annually. Peak systolic velocity (PSV) was used to assess in-stent restenosis. For subclavian artery and BT lesions, stenosis severity was assessed using PSV according to prespecified institutional criteria, with PSV >240 cm/s suggestive of >70% stenosis. For pCCA lesions, suspected hemodynamically significant restenosis on CDUS prompted additional cross-sectional imaging for confirmation. Because duplex velocity thresholds may vary by vascular territory and stent type, these criteria reflect our institutional surveillance protocol.

Vessel patency was additionally evaluated indirectly using flow characteristics. In cases demonstrating a post-stenotic waveform, additional cross-sectional imaging was planned for further evaluation of the covered stent. Reintervention was planned in cases of new-onset symptoms when their association with the index intervention was confirmed through additional cross-sectional imaging. Patients lost to follow-up were contacted by telephone during data collection.

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (ver. 27.0, IBM Co.). Continuous variables are presented as mean±standard deviation, and categorical variables as number and percentage. Statistical analysis was undertaken using crosstabs, non-parametric tests, and Kaplan–Meier analysis. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

1) Patient population

A total of 187 procedures involving the supra-aortic arch vessels were performed during the study period. Of these, 144 patients required open surgical repair, including carotid-carotid or carotid-subclavian bypass, or aortic debranching as part of the management of aortic arch pathologies. No patient underwent open surgical reconstruction of the supra-aortic arch vessels due to AOD. Two patients underwent BMS implantation in the supra-aortic arch vessels in collaboration with the Department of Neuroradiology. In 41 cases, covered stents were implanted for various pathologies, leaving 20 patients (8 male; mean age, 69.5±14.3 years) who underwent primary covered stent implantation for symptomatic supra-aortic arch vessel AOD (Fig. 1). One patient presented with significant stenosis of the BT and the left pCCA.

2) Perioperative outcomes and follow-up

A total of 21 covered stents (20 balloon-expandable and 1 self-expanding) were implanted in 21 vessels: 11 (52.4%) in the subclavian artery, 6 (28.6%) in the pCCA, and 4 in the BT. The stent grafts used were 13 BeGraft peripheral (Bentley InnoMed), 7 Advanta V12 (Atrium Medical Corp.), and 1 Viabahn (W.L. Gore & Associates). Nineteen interventions were performed under general anesthesia: in one case, local anesthesia was used via a transbrachial approach. Technical success was 100% (21/21). No residual stenosis > 50% was demonstrated on final angiography.

A transaxillary, transcarotid, transbrachial, or transfemoral access was used for covered stent implantation in 9 (42.9%), 7 (33.3%), 4 (19.0%), and 1 (4.8%) cases, respectively. Target lesion and covered stent characteristics are presented in Table 2.

No intraoperative complications were recorded. In-hospital mortality was 0%. Mean length of hospital stay for all patients was 6.0±6.2 days. Eight of 9 patients with BT or pCCA lesions were monitored for 1 day in the stroke unit. The patient with concomitant stenosis of the BT and left pCCA had prolonged intensive care unit and hospital stays of 9 and 25 days, respectively, due to postoperative complications related to the hemodialysis access. Another patient had a prolonged hospital stay of 23 days because of further management of an intracerebral aneurysm following stenting of the left pCCA.

Sixteen patients reported resolution of their symptoms postoperatively. Two patients developed transient cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome following pCCA recanalization. No cardiac, pulmonary, or other neurological adverse events were recorded. One patient with chronic kidney disease required temporary hemodialysis postoperatively. One patient underwent wound revision due to an access-site hematoma in the axillary region. All patients showed improved target-vessel blood flow on postoperative CDUS. DISCUSSION

Endovascular stenting has become an established option for AOD of the supra-aortic arch vessels. Most published series have reported favorable durability with BMS. In a study from 2011, Linni et al. [7] reported an 89.3% patency rate at 5 years following BMS implantation for lesions of the pCCA. In another report by van Hattum et al. [8], stenting of the BT demonstrated a 79% primary vessel patency at 24 months. Regarding the management of atherosclerotic lesions of the subclavian artery, current literature shows acceptable long-term patency rates [9].

Because in-stent restenosis is one of the most common complications after BMS implantation, covered stents have been introduced for supra-aortic arch vessel disease in recent years; however, the evidence base remains limited. In a recent report from 2023, Wei et al. [5] compared the outcomes of BMS and covered stent implantation in the subclavian artery in a total of 161 patients. Their study demonstrated significantly better cumulative mid-term primary patency with covered stent than with BMS (93.8% vs. 73.7%, respectively; P=0.01) over a mean follow-up of 45±18 months. Another long-term issue after stent implantation is stent fracture, typically occurring at flexion sites, such as the knee joint for popliteal artery stents and the hip joint for common femoral artery stents, and has been reported particularly with self-expanding BMS [10,11]. In our study, stent fractures at the subclavian artery, pCCA, and BT were considered less concerning because these segments are not exposed to substantial mechanical stress.

Our study demonstrates acceptable 4-year outcomes following primary covered stent implantation in the supra-aortic arch vessels for AOD, with no clinically relevant in-stent restenosis and no need for reintervention during follow-up. A key advantage of covered stents is their ability to reduce inflammatory response, as the stent coating forms a physical barrier between the vessel wall and the vessel lumen, thereby isolating the underlying atherosclerotic plaque from direct contact with the bloodstream [12]. In addition, the covering may limit neointimal hyperplasia and intimal ingrowth through the stent struts, potentially reducing the risk of in-stent restenosis. However, their use typically requires the insertion of longer and larger sheaths, which can pose procedural challenges. Covered stents have also been reported to provide superior clinical and patency outcomes compared with BMS, particularly in the treatment of long femoropopliteal lesions [13]. Finally, covered stents provide immediate hemostatic control in cases of intraprocedural vessel rupture, thereby enhancing procedural safety [14].

In cases involving subclavian artery or BT lesions, most procedures were performed using a transbrachial or transaxillary surgical cut-down approach. Only one case was treated via the common femoral artery, and one via percutaneous transbrachial access. Based on our experience, surgical cut-down access through the arm allows direct visualization of the vessel, provides excellent hemostatic control, accommodates the slightly larger sheaths required for covered stent implantation compared with BMS, and reduces guidewire manipulation within the aortic arch. For all pCCA lesions, an ipsilateral retrograde transcarotid cut-down approach with distal clamping was employed without an embolic protection device. This strategy is well suited for lesions located near the aortic arch, where transfemoral access may be challenging, and may offer additional protection against embolization [15].

A major limitation of our study is the lack of a comparison between covered stents and BMS. Unfortunately, this was not feasible because only two patients underwent BMS implantation during the study period. Additionally, CDUS may have limited reliability for assessing proximal supra-aortic segments due to anatomic depth and acoustic window constraints. Cross-sectional imaging, such as CTA or magnetic resonance angiography, may allow better evaluation of the covered stent. Other limitations of our study include the small sample size and its retrospective design. Furthermore, due to the relatively limited number of cases, we were only able to report overall outcomes of covered stent implantation in supra-aortic arch vessels without providing vessel-specific data.

CONCLUSION

Covered stent implantation for supra-aortic arch vessel AOD appears to be associated with acceptable mid-term outcomes. However, larger multicenter studies and vessel-specific analyses are needed to draw more definitive conclusions and clarify the comparative durability of covered stents versus BMS.
