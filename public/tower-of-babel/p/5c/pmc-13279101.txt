Right Ventricular‐Pulmonary Artery Coupling in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation and Changes After Catheter Ablation
Authors: Kazutoshi Hirose, Koki Nakanishi, Masao Daimon, Kentaro Iwama, Yuriko Yoshida, Yasuhiro Mukai, Yuko Yamamoto, Hikari Seki, Megumi Hirokawa, Tomoko Nakao, Tsukasa Oshima, Takumi Matsubara, Yu Shimizu, Gaku Oguri, Toshiya Kojima, Eriko Hasumi, Katsuhito Fujiu, Hiroyuki Morita, Makoto Kurano, Norihiko Takeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is a predominant risk factor for heart failure, even in patients with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction. Emerging evidence suggests the significance of right ventricular‐pulmonary artery (RV‐PA) uncoupling in heart failure occurrence. We aimed to elucidate the prevalence and associated factors of RV‐PA uncoupling and the efficacy of catheter ablation (CA) for RV‐PA adaptation in patients with AF without history of heart failure.MethodsWe studied 164 patients with AF free of heart failure who had normal left ventricular ejection fraction and underwent first CA. Echocardiography was performed before and 6 months after CA. RV‐PA coupling was calculated from the ratio of tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion to PA systolic pressure. Abnormal RV‐PA coupling was defined as tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion/PA systolic pressure <0.70 mm/mm Hg.ResultsAmong the participants, 32 (19.5%) patients were classified as having abnormal RV‐PA coupling. The abnormal RV‐PA coupling group was older, and had higher prevalence of persistent AF, higher CRP (C‐reactive protein) and B‐type natriuretic peptide levels than the normal RV‐PA coupling group. Multivariable analysis demonstrated that an elevated CRP level was significantly associated with RV‐PA uncoupling (P=0.004). At 6 months after CA, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion/PA systolic pressure was significantly improved but was still lower in the abnormal RV‐PA coupling group than in the normal RV‐PA coupling group (P=0.047). Furthermore, improvement of tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion/PA systolic pressure after CA was observed only in patients without recurrent AF.ConclusionsApproximately 20% of patients with AF with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction had abnormal RV‐PA coupling, and CA improved RV‐PA coupling status, whereas its benefits were observed in patients free from recurrent AF.Keywords: atrial fibrillation, catheter ablation, echocardiography, right ventricular‐pulmonary artery couplingSubject Categories: Atrial Fibrillation, Echocardiography

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained arrhythmia encountered in daily clinical practice, affecting 50 million individuals worldwide. 1 AF poses a 5‐fold risk of heart failure (HF), even with preserved left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (EF), 2 , 3 accompanied by significantly increased mortality, 4 highlighting the need for early identification of high‐risk patients who are susceptible to HF. Left heart remodeling, including LV systolic/diastolic dysfunction and left atrial (LA) enlargement, plays a pivotal role in HF development, 5 , 6 , 7 and emerging evidence suggests that right ventricular‐pulmonary artery (RV‐PA) uncoupling, namely RV contractile maladaptation to increased afterload, serves as a key player for the occurrence of HF. 8 , 9 , 10 Echocardiography allows noninvasive assessment of RV‐PA coupling based on the ratio of tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) to PA systolic pressure (PASP), 11 , 12 and the TAPSE/PASP ratio is related to the severity and progression of HF. 10 , 13 , 14 However, the prevalence and related factors of RV‐PA uncoupling in patients with AF are unknown. Furthermore, although recent clinical studies demonstrated a beneficial effect of catheter ablation (CA) over medical therapy on HF prevention, 15 , 16 the potential impact of CA on RV‐PA coupling has not been elucidated. Therefore, the aims of the present study were to investigate the following: (1) the prevalence and associated factors of RV‐PA uncoupling and (2) the efficacy of CA for RV‐PA adaptation in patients with AF without history of HF who had normal LVEF.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population

The present study included 164 patients with AF who underwent a first CA at the University of Tokyo Hospital between May 2019 and March 2022 and with adequate assessment of RV echocardiographic parameters. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) congenital heart disease, (2) moderate or severe valvular disease, (3) dilated or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, (4) receipt of cardiothoracic surgery within 3 months, (5) history of pacemaker implantation, (6) severe renal impairment on hemodialysis, (7) history of HF, and (8) LV systolic dysfunction (LVEF <50%). AF was defined as paroxysmal if it terminated spontaneously within 7 days of onset and as persistent if it continued for more than 7 days. 17 The research reported in this article adhered to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines. All patients provided written informed consent. The study conformed to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and the institutional ethics committee of the University of Tokyo approved the study protocol (2018120NI).

Risk Factor Assessment and Laboratory Testing

Body mass index was calculated based on height and body weight (kg/m2). Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure≥140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure≥90 mm Hg, or receiving antihypertensive agents. Diabetes was determined by fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL or current usage of insulin or hypoglycemic drugs. Dyslipidemia was identified as total cholesterol >240 mg/dL or use of lipid‐lowering drugs. Blood samples were drawn to investigate lipid and glycemic profiles, creatinine, liver enzymes, CRP (C‐reactive protein), and BNP (B‐type natriuretic peptide) levels. The estimated glomerular filtration rate was calculated with the abbreviated Modification of Diet in Renal Disease equation: estimated glomerular filtration rate (mL/min per 1.73 m2) =194 × (serum creatinine)‐1.094 × (age)‐0.287 × (0.739 for women).

Left Heart Echocardiographic Parameters

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed with a commercially available system, Vivid E95 (GE Vingmed Ultrasound, Horten, Norway) or EPIQ 7 (Koninklijke Philips N.V., Netherlands), 1 to 4 days before and 6 months after CA. All images were acquired by experienced and registered cardiologists. The dimensions of the cardiac chambers were assessed in the standard manner. 18 LV mass was calculated as follows: LV mass=0.8×1.04×[(LV end‐diastolic dimension+posterior wall thickness+interventricular septum thickness)3−(LV end‐diastolic dimension)3]+0.6. 18 LVEF and LA volume were obtained using the Biplane Simpson's rule. 18 LV mass and LA volume were indexed for body surface area. Peak early diastolic velocity was measured from transmitral inflow signals. Peak early diastolic velocity of the septal and lateral mitral annulus was derived from tissue Doppler imaging, and the ratio of early diastolic transmitral flow velocity to early diastolic mitral annular velocity was then calculated.

Right Heart Echocardiographic Parameters

RV end‐diastolic and end‐systolic area measurements were derived from an RV‐focused 4‐chamber view. RV fractional area change was calculated with the following formula: (RV end‐diastolic area – RV end‐systolic area)/RV end‐diastolic area × 100. 19 The peak systolic velocity of the tricuspid annulus was measured by tissue Doppler imaging. TAPSE was measured as the amount of longitudinal motion of the tricuspid annulus at peak systole. 19 The peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity was acquired from continuous Doppler imaging. PASP was obtained from the peak tricuspid velocity using the modified Bernoulli equation and estimated right atrial pressure based on the diameter and collapsibility of the inferior vena cava. 19 TAPSE/PASP was then calculated as a noninvasive marker for RV‐PA coupling, 11 , 12 , 20 , 21 and abnormal RV‐PA coupling was defined as TAPSE/PASP <0.70 mm/mm Hg, according to a previous investigation. 22 RV free‐wall strain was measured by 2‐dimensional speckle‐tracking echocardiography from the RV‐focused 4‐chamber view using vendor‐independent commercial software (2D Cardiac Performance Analysis; Tomtec Imaging System, Germany). 23 , 24 The RV endocardial border was semiautomatically traced and tracked throughout the cardiac cycle, and manual correction was performed in cases of inadequate endocardial detection. Right atrial volume was measured at end‐systole by the single‐plane Simpson's method and then indexed to body surface area according to the current guidelines. 19

CA Procedure and Follow‐Up

CA was performed under sedation. All patients underwent pulmonary vein isolation by point‐by‐point radiofrequency energy or the balloon technique to restore sinus rhythm, with an end point of bidirectional block between the LA and the inside of the circumferential pulmonary vein isolation area. Additional procedures, including cavotricuspid isthmus ablation, superior vena cava isolation, and roof line and mitral isthmus line ablation, were performed at the physician's discretion. After the CA procedure, patients were evaluated with a 12‐lead ECG every 1 to 2 months at the outpatient clinic. Twenty‐four‐hour Holter monitoring was also performed at 3 and 6 months after the procedure. Recurrence of arrhythmia was defined as any episode of atrial arrhythmia that lasted >30 seconds on a 12‐lead ECG or Holter monitoring after a 2‐month blanking period from the CA procedure. 25 , 26 Follow‐up echocardiography was conducted at 6 months after CA.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean±SD or median (interquartile range), and categorical data were described as number and percentage. Patients were categorized into 2 groups according to abnormal or normal RV‐PA coupling (TAPSE/PASP <0.70 mm/mm Hg or ≥0.70 mm/mm Hg, respectively). Patient demographics, laboratory parameters and echocardiographic measurements were compared using unpaired t test or Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables and χ2 test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables as appropriate. Univariable logistic regression models were constructed to investigate the associated factors for RV‐PA uncoupling (TAPSE/PASP <0.70 mm/mm Hg), and variables at P<0.05 level were introduced into a multivariable model. Due to possible collinearity between TAPSE and other RV systolic parameters, RV functional properties were not included in the logistic regression models. Pre‐ and postprocedural echocardiographic parameters were compared with the paired t test or Wilcoxon signed‐rank test as appropriate. The frequency of abnormal RV‐PA coupling before and after CA was also assessed with the McNemar test. A value of P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed with JMP Pro 17 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

The mean age was 64±11 years, and 116 (70.7%) patients were men. The median TAPSE/PASP was 0.88 (0.73–1.16) mm/mm Hg, and 32 (19.5%) patients were classified as having abnormal RV‐PA coupling (TAPSE/PASP <0.70 mm/mm Hg). Baseline characteristics according to RV‐PA coupling are displayed in Table 1. The abnormal RV‐PA coupling group was older and had a higher prevalence of persistent AF than the normal RV‐PA coupling group (both P<0.05). In contrast, the frequencies of the other AF risk factors did not differ between the 2 groups. As for the laboratory testing, patients with RV‐PA uncoupling had higher circulating plasma BNP and CRP levels than those with normal RV‐PA coupling (both P<0.001), and there was no significant difference in liver enzymes between the 2 groups.

Baseline Echocardiographic Measures

Echocardiographic parameters before CA are shown in Table 2. There were no significant differences in LV size and LVEF between patients with and without abnormal RV‐PA coupling, whereas LV mass index tended to be larger in the abnormal RV‐PA coupling group (P=0.053). Trajectory of RV‐PA Coupling After CA

At 6 months after CA, the status of RV‐PA coupling could be assessed in 144 patients. There were no statistical differences in baseline demographic and echocardiographic parameters between patients with and without assessment of postprocedural RV‐PA coupling status. In the entire study population, TAPSE and TAPSE/PASP were significantly improved after CA (TAPSE: 20.1±4.6 mm to 21.7±4.1 mm, P<0.001 and TAPSE/PASP: 0.87 [0.73–1.10] mm/mm Hg to 0.97 [0.82–1.25] mm/mm Hg, P=0.002). In line with this, the prevalence of patients with RV‐PA uncoupling was reduced from 20.1% to 9.7% after CA (P=0.009). Table 4 displays the echocardiographic parameters at baseline and 6 months after CA, stratified by preprocedural RV‐PA coupling status. LV size and LVEF did not change much after CA, and LA volume index was significantly reduced. Both groups demonstrated improvement of right atrial size and RV systolic parameters after CA. Although postprocedural TAPSE/PASP was significantly improved in the abnormal RV‐PA coupling group (0.60 [0.54–0.64] mm/mm Hg to 0.88 [0.73–1.07] mm/mm Hg, P<0.001), it was still lower than that of the normal RV‐PA coupling group (P=0.047; Figure 1). The differences in TAPSE/PASP before and after the procedure were 0.32 (0.15 to 0.54) mm/mm Hg in the abnormal RV‐PA coupling group and 0.05 (−0.19 to 0.28) mm/mm Hg in the normal RV‐PA coupling group (P<0.001). Twenty‐two (15.3%) patients experienced AF recurrence, which included 3 (10.3%) patients in the abnormal RV‐PA coupling group and 19 (16.5%) patients in the normal RV‐PA coupling group (P=0.567). When we stratified patients according to AF recurrence status, TAPSE/PASP at 6 months after CA was significantly higher in the nonrecurrent group than in the recurrent group (1.01 [0.85–1.29] mm/mm Hg versus 0.84 [0.71–0.96] mm/mm Hg, P<0.001; Figure 2), although preprocedural TAPSE/PASP was comparable between patients with and without recurrence (0.86 [0.75–1.05] mm/mm Hg versus 0.87 [072–1.17] mm/mm Hg, P=0.958). The differences in TAPSE/PASP before and after the procedure were − 0.07 (−0.26 to 0.07) mm/mm Hg in patients with AF recurrence and 0.16 (−0.09 to 0.37) mm/mm Hg in those free from AF recurrence (P=0.003). Postprocedural CRP and BNP levels were available in 141 and 142 patients, respectively. CRP level tended to decrease after CA (0.06 [0.03–0.13] mg/dL to 0.05 [0.03–0.12] mg/dL, P=0.064), and significant improvement of BNP level was observed (69 [30–200] pg/mL to 30 [13–51] pg/mL, P<0.001).

DISCUSSION

The present study demonstrated that (1) ∼20% of patients with AF who underwent first CA had abnormal RV‐PA coupling, (2) elevated CRP level was significantly associated with RV‐PA uncoupling, (3) TAPSE/PASP was improved at 6 months after CA but remained lower in patients with abnormal RV‐PA coupling at baseline, and (4) improvement of TAPSE/PASP after CA was observed only in patients free from recurrent AF.

RV‐PA Uncoupling in the Setting of AF

The RV basically adapts to changes in afterload by increasing contractility to maintain its coupling to the pulmonary circulation.22, 27 However, in conditions of excessive pressure overload, this adaptive response becomes exhausted, and RV fails to cope with afterload, leading to uncoupling between RV and PA.11, 27, 28 TAPSE/PASP is a noninvasive and easily available measure to assess RV‐PA coupling in daily clinical practice,11, 12 and impaired TAPSE/PASP provides a prognostic value over left heart parameters in various clinical situations.8–10, 14 Nakagawa et al. reported that ∼40% of patients with HF with preserved EF had RV‐PA uncoupling, which was independently associated with the composite end point including mortality and HF hospitalization. 14 Cahill et al. also showed that the prevalence of abnormal RV‐PA coupling was 39% in patients with severe aortic stenosis who underwent aortic valvular replacement, accounting for an independent predictive value of adverse cardiovascular outcomes. 10 Recent studies using cardiac magnetic resonance imaging also explored the RV‐PA coupling and found that the maladaptation of the right ventricle in response to increased afterload was correlated with extracellular volume fraction and was related to unfavorable clinical outcomes. 29 , 30 However, the frequency of RV‐PA maladaptation in patients with AF remained unknown. In the present study, we demonstrated for the first time that ∼20% of patients with AF and preserved LVEF had abnormal TAPSE/PASP.

In addition, the results of the present study showed that CRP level was significantly associated with RV‐PA uncoupling even after adjustment for AF severity and left heart mechanics. Previous studies demonstrated associations of chronic inflammation with PA and RV remodeling. 31 , 32 In the setting of pulmonary hypertension, changes in CRP level after initiation of pulmonary vasodilators were correlated with pulmonary hemodynamics. 31 An experimental study also demonstrated that enhanced inflammation could affect the RV myocardium directly, leading to maladaptive RV remodeling. 32 These results may partially explain our findings. Considering the significant association between RV‐PA uncoupling and HF with preserved EF, 8 , 9 , 14 our observations suggest that inflammation could play a pivotal role in the mechanisms linking AF and HF with preserved EF. Indeed, recent multicenter cohort studies have reported that patients with AF with elevated inflammatory markers were more prone to developing HF. 33 , 34

Impact of CA on RV‐PA Coupling

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate RV‐PA coupling status before and after CA by echocardiography, and we found that the prevalence of RV‐PA uncoupling decreased by ∼50% at 6 months after CA in patients with AF with normal LVEF. Several studies have shown RV functional improvement after restoration of sinus rhythm in patients with AF. 35 , 36 Therkelsen et al. reported that RVEF assessed by cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was improved at 180 days after cardioversion in patients with persistent AF. 35 Soulat‐Dufour et al. also found a significant increase in RVEF 12 months after cardioversion or ablation in patients with AF by 3‐dimensional echocardiography. 36 Our results are in accordance with those findings and extend to RV‐PA coupling status in patients with AF free of HF. In addition, despite a significant postprocedural increase in TAPSE/PASP in the abnormal RV‐PA coupling group, it was still lower than that of the normal RV‐PA coupling group, suggesting that more careful follow‐up may be warranted in these patients. Furthermore, patients with recurrent AF did not experience improvement in TAPSE/PASP, indicating that the maintenance of sinus rhythm could be essential for the improvement of RV‐PA coupling after CA.

Clinical Implications

In the present study, we revealed the prevalence and trajectory of RV‐PA uncoupling in patients with AF with normal LVEF and free of HF. Given the clinical significance of RV‐PA uncoupling in the setting of HF, 13 , 20 our results may shed light on the pathophysiological mechanisms linking AF and incident HF. Previous studies found systemic and local chronic inflammation to be a key player in the occurrence or perpetuation of AF. 37 , 38 On the other hand, AF promotes atrial enlargement and fibrosis, which enhances neurohormonal activation and the inflammatory process, contributing to the progression of atrial myopathy and its clinical consequences. 39 These findings suggest a complex interplay between inflammation and AF. The significant association between CRP and RV‐PA maladaptation in the present study may add valuable information regarding the pathophysiological mechanisms linking inflammation and AF, and also support previous observations showing that enhanced inflammation carries a significant risk for HF occurrence in AF. 33 Furthermore, the favorable effect of CA on RV‐PA coupling status could partially explain the efficacy of CA on reducing HF risk. 15 , 16 Indeed, recent studies demonstrated that persistent RV‐PA uncoupling after specific therapies such as cardiac resynchronization therapy and tricuspid transcatheter edge‐to‐edge repair carried a significant risk for adverse outcomes. 40 , 41 Further studies are required to investigate the impact of RV‐PA coupling status and its trajectory on the development of HF with longer follow‐up in patients with AF.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted regarding the present study. First, this was a single‐center study of patients with AF eligible for CA; thus, the results cannot be applied to other study populations (eg, patients with permanent AF). Second, we evaluated RV‐PA coupling by echocardiography, although the gold standard of RV‐PA coupling is the catheter‐based end‐systolic elastance/arterial elastance ratio. 11 , 12 Unfortunately, we did not perform right heart catheterization in this study, and future studies are warranted to validate the echocardiography‐derived TAPSE/PASP in patients with AF, although it was validated in other clinical settings. 42 Third, TAPSE and PASP are both load‐dependent markers, and the status of RV‐PA coupling could be altered by the loading conditions. 21 , 22 , 28 However, no patients received fluid boluses or selective pulmonary vasodilators at the time of echocardiography in the present study. Fourth, although there was no significant difference in liver enzymes between patients with and without abnormal RV‐PA coupling, hepatic dysfunction might be involved in the association between elevated CRP levels and impaired RV‐PA coupling. Finally, the relatively short‐term follow‐up duration of the present study may limit evaluation of the impact of RV‐PA uncoupling on HF incidence, requiring further long‐term investigation.

CONCLUSIONS

Approximately 20% of patients with AF with preserved LVEF and free of HF had RV‐PA uncoupling, and enhanced inflammation was associated with abnormal RV‐PA coupling. CA significantly improved the RV‐PA coupling status, whereas its benefit was observed only in patients without recurrent AF.
