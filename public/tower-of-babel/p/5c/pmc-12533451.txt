Multi-modal characteristics of LncRNA-derived subtypes in colorectal cancer
Authors: Minghao Xiong, Jie Li, Xue Li, Jiaojiao Zhao, Qin Liu, Mengjie Tu, Fanxin Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe growing knowledge of long non-coding RNA (LncRNA) has suggested the role and predictive potential of LncRNA in cancer, but has not been translated into effective practical tools. The morbidity of colorectal cancer (CRC) has been declining because of effective screening tools. However, the mechanism of the disease is not well understood.MethodsThe LncRNAs that extracted using univariable Cox analysis were explored for the potential functions and applied for classification of LncRNA-derived subtypes of CRC. Multi-modal data involving microscope to macroscope levels were applied to evaluate the molecular, cell, and medical image characteristics of LncRNA-derived subtypes. Radiomic signature calculated by LASSO regression analysis was utilized for distinguishing the CRC subtypes. The performance of LncRNA and radiomic signature were assessed with respect to its calibration, discrimination, and clinical usefulness.ResultsThe LncRNA signature composed of 8 LncRNAs significantly associated with prognosis, metabolism, and organismal system showed good performance in patients’ prognosis assessment (AUC, 0.717; time-dependent ROC > 0.71). Patients separated into two subtypes (HRG vs. LRG) based on the LncRNA signature displayed notable prognostic differences (Kaplan-Meier, p < 0.001). Multi-modal analysis indicated obvious heterogeneity between HRG and LRG subgroups, mainly focused on the abnormal enrichment of glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway, different cellular differentiation, and density level characteristics in the tumor region. In addition, the radiomic signature showed good accuracy (AUC, 0.919), discrimination (p < 0.05), and generalization ability (external independent test cohort, p < 0.05) for LncRNA-derived subgroups assessment.ConclusionThis study described the multi-modal characteristics landscape of LncRNA-derived subtypes in CRC and strengthened the connection from microscope to macroscope levels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12920-025-02242-0.Keywords: Colorectal cancer (CRC), Long non-coding RNA (LncRNA), Multi-modal analysis, Glycerophospholipid metabolism, Radiomics

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC), ranking as the third most prevalent malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally, poses a substantial public health burden [1–3]. With advancing research into CRC pathogenesis, accumulating evidence indicates that its development involves a complex interplay of multiple risk factors [4]. For example, mutant rat sarcoma (RAS) proteins exert prominent oncogenic effects, driving malignant transformation of tumor cells and promoting aggressive phenotypic traits [5, 6]. Consequently, patients harboring RAS mutations exhibit poorer prognoses, enhanced resistance to anti-EGFR therapy, and elevated metastatic potential [7]. The high frequency of RAS mutations leads to aberrant activation of downstream mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) signaling pathways, which critically regulate cell proliferation, survival, and migration [8, 9].

Long non-coding RNAs (LncRNAs), a class of RNA molecules exceeding 200 nucleotides in length [10], have emerged as key regulators of gene expression in CRC and are closely implicated in tumor progression as pivotal risk factors [11]. Notably, LncRNAs modulate the expression and functional activity of RAS genes and their downstream pathways, including PI3K-AKT and MAPK signaling [12]. However, current understanding of LncRNAs in CRC remains constrained by limitations such as incomplete and unreliable datasets, suboptimal specificity and sensitivity of detection methodologies, and the inherent complexity and diversity of LncRNA functions. Thus, further investigations are warranted to elucidate the roles and mechanisms of LncRNAs in CRC, particularly their associations with RAS genes.

Mounting evidence supports the characterization of CRC as a multi-modal disease [13], underscoring the value of multi-modal analysis in unraveling its pathogenic mechanisms. Therefore, this study aimed to achieve three interconnected objectives: First, we systematically identified RAS-associated LncRNA subtypes in CRC patients by leveraging multi-modal datasets, establishing a biologically meaningful subtype classification rooted in LncRNA expression patterns. Second, through integration of multi-omics and imaging data, we sought to delineate the multi-scale heterogeneity between these subtypes and uncover potential circular regulatory pathways driving disease progression. Third, building upon the LncRNA-derived subtype framework, we constructed a radiomic signature comprising 10 imaging features, validated its performance in non-invasive subtype identification and prognostic prediction, and thereby bridged the biological insights from LncRNA subtypes with clinically applicable imaging-based tools.

Materials and methods

Patients and samples

The primary cohort of this study consisted of an evaluation of the Dazhou database for electronic medical records from February 2018 to March 2019 to determine patients with pathologically confirmed CRC who underwent radical surgery. All tissue samples were acquired as liquid nitrogen-frozen samples from the Department of Pathology for subsequent sequencing. The detailed sequencing procedures and methods are attached in the Supplementary file. A total of 96 patients were identified and formed the primary cohort after excluding patients with incomplete clinical information. Patients were further stratified into five cohorts based on the single-modal data, including cohort 1 (N = 96) with transcriptomic data, cohort 2 (N = 39) with metabolomics data, cohort 3 (N = 31) with proteomics data, cohort 4 (N = 33) with hematoxylin-eosin staining results and cohort 5 (N = 89) with radiomics features of V phase (Fig. 1). From January 2018 to September 2019, an independent external test cohort of 145 CRC patients with V phase of computed tomography (CT) images was screened from 160 patients using the same criteria as those for the primary cohort (Fig. 1).

The flowchart of participants. Multi-omics data for a single patient are incomplete. Patients were further stratified into five cohorts based on single-modal data. The presence of HE staining and radiomics phase V characteristics were used as criteria for inclusion in the omics and radiomics analyses, respectively

Identification of LncRNA-derived subtypes

Firstly, the RAS-related LncRNAs were screened using Pearson`s correlation analysis (|R| > 0.5 and p < 0.05), the prognostic RAS-related LncRNAs were filtered from RAS-related LncRNAs by univariate Cox regression analysis (p < 0.05). Secondly, the prognostic LncRNAs-based classifier was constructed after calculating the LncRNAs signature score based on the formula: LncRNAs signature (LS) = ( means the hazard ratio value and means the expression of each prognostic RAS-related LncRNA). Thirdly, LncRNA-derived subtypes (HRG VS LRG) were identified based on the cutoff value. Finally, the performance of the prognostic LncRNAs-based classifier was evaluated by the area under the curve (AUC), Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis, and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis.

Development of an individualized prediction model

Univariate Cox regression was applied for risk factors screening in the following clinical candidate predictors: age, tumor site, tumor stage, gender, and LncRNAs signature. Multivariable Cox regression analysis was performed for filtering independent risk factors. To provide the clinician with a quantitative tool to predict the survival status of CRC patients, we built the nomogram based on multivariable Cox regression analysis in cohort 1. Calibration curves, decision curves, and AUC were plotted to assess the performance of the nomogram.

Diversities of single-omics data

Data preparation

To obtain different molecules for enrichment analysis, we only retained the duplicated genes and proteins with the maximum average value. The volcano plot depicted significantly different molecules with log2 (|Fold Change|) > 0 and p < 0.05, including genes, proteins, and metabolites. It is worth noting that the additional condition of significantly different metabolites was a variable importance in the projection > 1 that was calculated using orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis after performing the Pareto scaling process. In addition, the LncRNAs-related genes were filtered by Pearson’s correlation analysis with |R| > 0.5 and p < 0.05.

Enrichment analysis

For the two single-omics data (transcriptomic, proteomic) and LncRNAs-related genes, pathway enrichment analysis was performed using R software (version 4.1.2) based on The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO). Metabolic pathway enrichment was determined using MetaboAnalyst (https://new.metaboanalyst.ca/MetaboAnalyst/). The gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was utilized to evaluate the potential functions of LncRNAs.

Radiomic features extraction

All patients underwent contrast-enhanced CT reconstruction performed as part of the CT image acquisition process. The acquisition parameters were as follows: Siemens Definition AS 64-row CT, 100 KV, CAREDose4D, 0.5-s rotation time, detector collimation: 128 × 0.6 mm, field of view 380 × 380 mm, matrix: 512 × 512. After the contrast enhancement, the arterial and 60 s Post-venous scan reconstructed the CT image with a thickness of 1 mm. Subsequently, all the contrast-enhanced CT images were used to establish a three-dimensional tumor tissue by two professional data readers using 3D Slicer software (version 4.10.2) to delineate the tumor tissue under the guidance of a clinical imaging specialist. Finally, the radiomic features were extracted from three-dimensional tumor tissue using the SlicerRadiomics plug-in. Furthermore, the density of three-dimensional tumor tissue was displayed by the Rainbow module of 3D Slicer.

Construction and validation of radiomic features-based prediction model

The risk score-related radiomic features with an absolute correlation value greater than 0.1 were applied to develop a predictive model for survival status in cohort 5. The predictive model was constructed by the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) analysis with five-fold cross-validation. The mean maximum value of the area under the curve of the training and validation datasets as the screening criteria selected the best predictive model. The external validation was performed in the Nanchong dataset.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were conducted with the use of the software R (version 4.1.2) by a statistical analyst (M. X.). Demographic and clinical data were summarized by descriptive statistics. The continuous variables and ranked variables were tested by the Kruskal-Wallis test and Fisher’s exact test, respectively. The difference test between the two groups was conducted using the Wilcoxon signed rank test. The model differences were determined by the analysis of variance (ANOVA). All p < 0.05 were considered indicative of a statistically significant difference.

CRC tumors from 106 patients were profiled using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), Data-independent Acquisition (DIA), Liquid Chromatography-tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis, digital pathology, and radiomic feature filtering. Good classification performance of LncRNAs signature

Performing Pearson`s correlation analysis, we identified 252 LncRNAs associated with RAS genes. Subsequently, we filtered 8 prognostic RAS-related LncRNAs from 252 RAS-related LncRNAs (Fig. 3A, B) by univariate Cox regression analysis in the primary cohort. Based on the expression of 8 prognostic RAS-related LncRNAs, the LncRNAs-based classifier generated two groups, the high-risk group (HRG, LS score above 657.47) and the low-risk group (LRG, LS score below 657.47) (Figure S1A). No significant difference was observed in terms of age, gender distribution, tumor stage, and tumor sites between the HRG (N = 34) and LRG groups (N = 62) (Table S1). The significant differential result of the KM analysis revealed that the classifier had good predictive performance and confirmed that HRG had poor survival status (Fig. 3C). The AUC of the time-dependent ROC analysis of the classifier for the probability of survival status demonstrated good agreement between prediction and observation (Figure S1B).

Of the clinical features in the primary cohort, 8 features were reduced to 2 potential predictors (age, tumor stage) and were significantly associated with survival status in univariate and multivariate Cox analysis (p < 0.05, Figure S1C, D). Furthermore, the classifier that incorporated age, tumor stage, and LS score was developed with the highest AUC of 0.873 (Fig. 3D) and presented as a nomogram (Figure S1E, F). In addition, the survival status was significantly different between the LRG and HRG groups at different clinical status strata (Figure S2A-H). Although the decision curve analysis suggested that the combined nomogram was not significantly superior to the LS classifier (Figure S3A), a good calibration curve of the nomogram was observed for the probability of survival status in the primary cohort (Figure S3B-D). Naturally, the LS classifier was valuable for predicting LncRNA-derived subtypes in the absence of clinical information (Fig. 3D).

The potential functions of the LncRNA signature

On the basis of pathway classification, we found that the LncRNA-related genes were enriched in human diseases, metabolism, and organismal systems (Fig. 3E). For the single LncRNA functions, most were correlated with metabolism, including CASP3-3, C11orf71-2, and C12orf66-1 (Fig. 3F). Notably, C12orf66-1 and C11orf71-2 had the potential to influence the KRAS signaling pathway, which is a hallmark of many cancers. Interestingly, we found that the TME cell composition had some significant differences between LRG and HRG, including activated B cell, effector memory CD4 T cell, mast cell, and monocyte. In those cell types, the cell composition was highly expressed in LRG (Figure S4A). Simultaneously, C12orf66-1 was negatively correlated with activated B cells, effector memory CD4 T cells, and mast cells. Similarly, CASP3-3 was negatively correlated with mast cell and monocyte, SPINT2-4 was the negative factor of the cell composition of effector memory CD4 T cell, mast cell and monocyte, NAP-7/AK6-8 was only negative to the monocyte (Figure S4B). Although those LncRNAs had fewer negative effects on TME cells, all of those LncRNAs reduce the immune efficacy of CRC patients.

Different dimensions of tumor heterogeneity

Inter-subtype heterogeneity comprises image heterogeneity of the tumor region and molecular heterogeneity. In image heterogeneity, the tumor density in the 3D tumor region varies between LncRNA-derived subtypes, specifically, the HRG group generally had a lower color value (dark blue is the lowest color value). Notably, intralesional heterogeneity, as represented by cellular differentiation, is also different between LncRNA-derived subtypes (Fig. 4A).

In molecular heterogeneity, we detected the significantly different expression levels of molecules between the HRG and LRG groups using metabolomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic data. In the metabolomic dimension, the orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) indicated the heterogeneity of LncRNA-derived subtypes (Figure S5A, B). There were 23 significantly different metabolites between LncRNA-derived subtypes from 1299 metabolites (Figure S6A, Table S2, Figure S5C-D). In general, most of the significantly different metabolites were lipids or lipid-like molecules affecting lipid metabolism (Figure S6B, C). KEGG enrichment analysis demonstrated that these metabolites were significantly enriched in glycerophospholipid metabolism (Fig. 4B). In the molecular heterogeneity of the transcriptomic and proteomic dimensions, 9408 genes and 265 proteins were different between LncRNA-derived subtypes (Figure S6D-E). Most of the differential genes and proteins were involved in the biological process, such as the cell division process. Interestingly, the glycerophospholipid metabolic process was one of the biological processes that were enriched. In contrast, some pathways connected with the glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway were only enriched in the transcriptomic data, like the glycerophospholipid biosynthetic process (Fig. 4B). The functional annotation results of the multi-omics analysis indicated that the glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway was the shared pathway. Notably, in the whole exome sequencing data, the KRAS mutation frequency of HRG was 50% and higher than that of LRG (Figure S6F). Briefly, significantly different molecules, abnormally enriched pathways and image differences indicated the inter-subtype heterogeneity.

Variation of the glycerophospholipid metabolism

We depicted the variation in glycerophospholipid metabolism using transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data (Fig. 4C). The differential analysis of multi-omics identified a series of different molecules that had different expression levels between LncRNA-derived subtypes. Among them, phosphatidylcholine, LysoPC, and phosphatidylethanolamine were the key components of glycerophospholipid metabolism and highly expressed in HRG (Fig. 4D-E). From phosphatidylcholine to choline, phosphatidylcholine and LysoPC were highly expressed in HRG. The high expression level of phosphatidylcholine was probably caused by excessive output mediated by the high expression level of PLA2G (PLA2G6, PLA2G4B), CHK, and CHPT1. The high expression level of LCAT increased LysoPC expression, and the high expression of phosphatidylcholine reduced LysoPC consumption. Simultaneously, the high expression level of phosphatidylcholine and PNPLA6 probably led to phosphatidylethanolamine upregulation in HRG. Conversely, the high expression level of phosphatidylethanolamine probably decreased the accumulation level of L-threonine. In addition, RAF1 and BRAF were highly expressed in HRG, and RAF1 was significantly associated with AK6-8/NAIP-71 (correlation value, 0.44, p < 0.001). This evidence suggested that prognostic LncRNAs may mediate tumor progression of CRC through glycerophospholipid metabolism.

Feature selection and radiomics signature building

In cohort 5, 10 features were screened out of 454 radiomic features by LASSO regression analysis for the prediction model of CRC subtypes (Fig. 5A, Figure S7), and the coefficients of these features were presented in the feature-score calculation formula (Data Supplement). The feature-score radiomics signature yielded an AUC of more than 0.850 in the train and test sets (train set: 0.919, 95% CI [0.842–0.996], test set: 0.859, 95% CI [0.687–1.000.687.000], Fig. 5B). A significant association between the radiomics signature and survival status was also found when stratified analysis was performed in the primary cohort and the external independent validation cohort (KM analysis, p < 0.05, Fig. 5C, D). These results indicated that radiomic features have the potential to predict molecular-derived subtypes, such as LncRNA-derived subtypes, and underscored the critical role of radiomic features in connecting the micro and macro dimensions of the tumor.

Discussions

Previous research has highlighted the important roles of LncRNAs in CRC and has characterized the multi-omics landscape of CRC. However, these studies have not adequately addressed the clinical applicability of LncRNAs or provided comprehensive explanations for CRC occurrence and development at a multi-modal level. In our study, we leverage the readily accessible properties of radiomics information rather than relying solely on LncRNA signatures. Additionally, we establish a multimodal characterization of LncRNA-driven isoforms.

In our study, we observed that the composition of LncRNA signatures exhibits comparable predictive efficiency to those reported in other studies [14, 15]. Additionally, we expanded the functional map of LncRNAs, deepening our understanding of their roles. However, despite their essential regulatory functions in tumorigenesis and CRC development [16, 17], gaps persist in understanding the precise functions and clinical applications of LncRNAs related to the LncRNA signature. Bridging this gap remains a challenge due to the complexity and variability of LncRNA expression profiles in routine clinical settings [18, 19]. To address this, we leveraged radiomics features extracted from CT images as an alternative to the LncRNA signature. Our predictive model, based on these radiomics features, demonstrated excellent efficiency, suggesting its practical utility in clinical contexts. This approach aligns with the findings of Aerts et al. [20], who emphasized the potential of radiomics in enhancing cancer diagnosis and treatment by providing detailed phenotypic information that complements genomic data. Furthermore, recognizing the increasing interest in LncRNAs as therapeutic targets [11, 21], we explored the specific functions of individual LncRNAs. Notably, our analysis revealed an intriguing association between C11orf71-2 and C12orf66-1 with the KRAS signaling pathway—an essential player in CRC pathogenesis. This discovery sheds light on the functional roles of these LncRNAs within the broader LncRNA signature. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying their involvement in chemotherapy resistance in CRC remain enigmatic.

By integrating the differences of multi-omics, we have found that the inter-subtype heterogeneity was concentrated in the glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway. This pathway has emerged as a critical player in immunotherapy [22, 23]. Our study significantly enriches our understanding of the therapeutic potential of glycerophospholipid metabolism. Specifically, we found elevated expression levels of several metabolites within this pathway in the HRG. These metabolites include choline kinase (CHK), phosphatidylcholine (PC), lyso-phosphastidylcholine (LysoPC), and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE). The pronounced enrichment of CHK [24], PC, LysoPC, and PE indicates heightened cell proliferation [25, 26]—a finding consistent with our observation that HRG exhibits a high density of 3D tumor regions and low cellular differentiation levels. This alignment with previous studies underscores the importance of these metabolites in cell membrane synthesis and signaling, both of which are critical for rapid cell division and tumor growth [24]. Consequently, the abnormally enriched glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway is associated with poor survival in patients. Although choline tended to be positively associated with rectal cancer risk [27], the enrichment level of choline was not significantly different. However, the upstream and downstream of choline were highly enriched, including phosphatidylcholine and LysoPC. This suggests that the metabolic flux through the glycerophospholipid pathway is more critical than the absolute levels of choline. PC-related enzyme-mediated productions support colorectal cancer chemoresistance [28]. The higher enrichment levels of these metabolites are negatively associated with good survival, indicating that they may contribute to the aggressive behavior of CRC. Interestingly, PC, LysoPC, and PE play important roles in CRC, and their involvement in the disease process has been highlighted in several studies [29–31]. For instance, the development of CRC with bisphenol A exposure may be mediated by the glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway [30]. These findings support the notion that the glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway and its related molecules have significant potential as therapeutic targets for CRC. Our attempts to study LncRNA-derived subtypes of CRC using multi-modal analysis indicated that the glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway is highly likely involved in tumor progression. This analysis revealed the heterogeneity of LncRNA-derived subtypes, suggesting that different subtypes may have distinct metabolic profiles and therapeutic vulnerabilities. By understanding these differences, we can develop more targeted and effective treatments for CRC.

Although many molecular models have been shown to have good prediction efficiency for prognostic status [32, 33], their practicality for clinical use remains limited compared to more established techniques such as CT imaging [34]. With the role of radiomics evolving, radiomic features offer information and produce accurate and robust systems and models [35, 36]. The vast majority of radiomic studies assess correlations between radiomic features and other points of interest such as genes, neglecting the information that leads to a favorable benefit-risk balance for the patient provided by radiomics [37]. In this study, the radiomics signature demonstrated adequate discrimination of LncRNA-derived subtypes, which was then confirmed in the external independent cohort. Given the shortcomings of LncRNAs [38], the prediction model has greater practicality than the classifier.

Comprehensive analysis may deepen our understanding of tumors. Unfortunately, the principal challenge is the optimal collection and integration of diverse multi-modal data sources [39]. Besides that, there are several limitations. First, although we explored a potential pathogenic pathway, this conclusion requires further experimentation for verification. Simultaneously, the potential functions associated with LncRNAs are not well-defined and have not been validated or thoroughly explored. Second, we confirmed only the effectiveness of the radiomic features-based predictive model. The weak correlation between multi-omics and medical data has been verified, however, the abnormal enrichment of glycerophospholipid metabolism between groups discriminated by the predictive model is not verified in an external independent cohort. Further studies using large cohorts are warranted to verify the clinical value of this pathway. Third, the heterogeneity of multi-modal data was confirmed by medical images without quality assessment. Because the extraction of information from images obtained during standard clinical workflows facilitates extensive tumor characterization and improves the generalization ability of the model [40].

Conclusion

Our study comprehensively revealed the heterogeneous landscape of the LncRNA-derived subtypes at micro and macro levels using multi-modal approaches and pointed out that the glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway has extremely high treatment potential for CRC. Simultaneously, the predictive model derived from medical images provided a clinically useful tool to predict the survival status of CRC patients. In addition, this study provided a strong resource for future investigations of CRC.
