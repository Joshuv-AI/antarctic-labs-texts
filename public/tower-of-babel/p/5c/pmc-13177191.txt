Generative Artificial Intelligence–Driven Voice Assistance for Patient Education in Ophthalmology
Authors: Allon Jacobs, Dana Anselmo, Raya McHugh, Ignacio Fernandez-Garcia, Chris Fang, Rae Lasko, Subhabrata Mukherjee, Nancy Holekamp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This qualitative study aimed to evaluate the usability, perceived effectiveness, and acceptability of a voice-based generative artificial intelligence assistant for educating patients about intravitreal therapy for wet age-related macular degeneration and supporting treatment understanding and adherence.

Treatment nonadherence leads to poor health outcomes and elevated health care costs.1 Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and diabetic retinopathy are leading causes of blindness globally.2 Although intravitreal therapy (IVT) prevents vision loss, it requires injections for years, burdening patients and caregivers.3 IVT adherence is critical, yet first-year adherence rates are only 30% to 60%. Limited education about disease, treatment goals, and follow-up care contributes to discontinuation.4 Artificial intelligence (AI) may improve patient education, engagement, and population health. To our knowledge, most generative AI applications in health care focus on answering medical questions or summarizing electronic health records,5 with limited use as an educational tool for chronic ophthalmic disease (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). We evaluated participant responses to Polaris, a voice-based generative AI system designed for nondiagnostic patient-facing health care conversations across clinical fields, including ophthalmology.6

Methods

Polaris is designed to support clinical objectives, such as wellness check-ins, medication adherence assessments, appointment confirmation, procedural logistics review, and dietary assessments, but is not designed to provide diagnoses or prescribe medications. Unlike prior rule-based systems, Polaris conducts real-time, natural, multiturn voice conversations that emulate clinician-patient interactions.6

A qualitative focus group study evaluated the AI assistant’s effectiveness in educating patients about IVT for wet AMD. Patients were assigned a persona to support realistic, fictional, and unscripted engagement with the AI assistant (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). Participants role-played as newly diagnosed patients and engaged in open conversations with the AI (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). Pre- and postinteraction surveys assessed understanding, usability, and confidence in AI-assisted care. Ophthalmology health care professionals (HCPs) and practice managers (PMs) reviewed audio recordings and provided feedback through structured surveys and discussions (eTable 2 in Supplement 1). Institutional review board approval was not required as this study did not involve human subject research. Participants enrolled under written contracts including informed consent and compensation.

Results

Twenty-six patients (6 men, 20 women; aged 24-83 years) participated. Two reported AMD and 4 had diabetic macular edema; others had varied disease backgrounds. Before interaction, 19 (73%) believed AI would positively impact health care. Afterward, all (100%) found the session helpful, 25 (96%) rated the AI as organized and understandable, and 22 (85%) considered it useful for newly diagnosed AMD patients. Twenty-one (81%) reported increased confidence in AI’s role in health care. One participant with a speech impediment experienced initial communication difficulty (Figure, A).

Nineteen retinal specialists and 5 PMs participated. Before exposure, 16 HCPs (85%) and 4 PMs (80%) believed AI would positively impact health care. After reviewing patient interactions, all agreed the AI was accurate, helpful, and appropriate for patient education. Twelve HCPs (63%) and 4 PMs (80%) reported increased confidence in AI’s potential. HCPs valued its empathetic tone and conversational realism, noting potential value for pre- and postdiagnosis education, adherence monitoring, and patient triage (Figure, B and C). PMs emphasized administrative potential (eg, billing support). Concerns included liability, misinformation, and transparency in AI validation.

Discussion

This qualitative study demonstrates a fully generative, voice-based large language model for patient education in ophthalmology. Unlike text- or rule-based systems, Polaris provides dynamic, empathetic, and evidence-informed dialogue.6 Participants valued its accessibility and human-likeness, while HCPs noted potential to enhance efficiency and continuity of care. Clinician oversight, rigorous validation, and transparent design are essential to mitigate misinformation risk. Limitations include the small sample size and a hypothetical scenario, highlighting the need for broader assessment across disease areas and real-world settings.

Conclusions

A fully generative, voice-based AI assistant was well received by participants. This approach may support patient education but requires evaluation in real-world settings.
