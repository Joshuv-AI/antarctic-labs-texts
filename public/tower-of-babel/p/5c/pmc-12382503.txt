Enhanced detection of patients with previous COVID-19: superiority of the double diffusion technique
Authors: Gerald Stanley Zavorsky, Giovanni Barisione, Thomas Gille, Roberto W Dal Negro, Marta Núñez-Fernández, Leigh Seccombe, Gianluca Imeri, Fabiano Di Marco, Jann Mortensen, Elisabetta Salvioni, Piergiuseppe Agostoni, Vito Brusasco
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPersistent pulmonary dysfunction is common after COVID-19, yet traditional assessments using carbon monoxide diffusing capacity (DLCO) alone may miss alveolar-capillary impairment.ObjectiveTo determine whether combining nitric oxide (DLNO5s) and carbon monoxide (DLCO5s) diffusing capacities enhances detection of post-COVID-19 lung impairment and whether summed z-scores outperform individual measures in classifying affected individuals.Design and methodsWe conducted an individual participant data meta-analysis using hierarchical mixed-effects modelling. The dataset included 572 COVID-19 survivors and 72 matched controls from six European centres. Lung function metrics—including spirometry, total lung capacity, DLNO5s and DLCO5s—were standardised into z-scores. Logistic models were compared using Bayesian Information Criterion and Leave-One-Out Information Criterion. Classification accuracy was assessed with Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) and net reclassification improvement (NRI). Principal Component Analysis examined score structures, and dyspnoea severity was correlated with z-scores. Assessments were conducted 32–575 days post-infection (median=130 days).ResultsThe number of days between SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis and testing did not affect any of the measured z‐scores. Summed DLNO5s + DLCO5s z-scores consistently outperformed individual metrics. The combined model improved MCC by 0.06 (95% CI 0.01 to 0.11) and NRI by 37% (95% CI 13 to 62%) over DLCO5s alone. The top model (summed DLNO5s + DLCO5s z-scores model) explained 10% of fixed and 59% of random variance. DLCO5s alone failed to identify reduced membrane diffusion in approximately 16% of cases. Dyspnoea severity was significantly associated with all diffusion indices (p<0.001), though combined scores showed no stronger correlation than single predictors.ConclusionSummed DLNO5s + DLCO5s z-scores enhance classification of post-COVID-19 pulmonary impairment beyond DLCO5s alone. The NO-CO double diffusion approach offers improved diagnostic discrimination between previously infected individuals and controls and aligns with symptom severity. These findings support broader clinical integration of combined diffusion metrics in post-COVID assessment.Keywords: COVID-19, Respiratory Measurement, Respiratory Function Test

Introduction

Individuals recovering from COVID-19, caused by SARS-CoV-2, often experience lingering health issues that persist beyond 3 months post-infection.1 One major concern is persistent lung function impairment. Meta-analyses report that 30%–40% of COVID-19 survivors show a lung diffusing capacity below 80% of predicted values approximately 30 days post-infection, with lung restriction and obstruction observed in 12%–15% and 8% of patients, respectively.2 3 These findings highlight the substantial prevalence of respiratory complications among COVID-19 survivors, but traditional DLCO-only testing may underestimate dysfunction. The 2022 European Respiratory Society (ERS) Statement on COVID-19 follow-up emphasised the need for more detailed data on lung diffusing capacity using the nitric oxide (NO)–carbon monoxide (CO) double diffusion technique in this population.4 This review integrates diverse study data to assess whether the NO-CO double diffusion approach provides superior diagnostic accuracy.

Combining measurements of lung diffusing capacity for nitric oxide (DLNO) and carbon monoxide (DLCO) provides a deeper understanding of the underlying pathophysiological changes. The Roughton and Forster equation—published in 19575—explained that the total resistance to CO uptake in the lung was comprised of two resistances in series; DLCO primarily reflects diffusion limitations associated with vascular and blood volume (comprising ~80% of the red cell barrier); DLNO is more sensitive to the alveolar-capillary membrane’s function (~60% of the interaction)6,8 (figure 1). The concurrent use of these measurements yields a comprehensive understanding of different diffusion pathways. As mentioned in Figure 1, there are alternative views on NO and CO uptake in the lungs, other than the Roughton-Forster morphological explanation of gas transfer through two different resistances in series. This new thought, called the time-based understanding of NO and CO absorption, states that DLNO is said to indicate ‘surface absorption of NO’, or NO absorption is determined largely by plasma boundary space separating the alveolar-capillary membrane from the RBC, while DLCO represents ‘volume absorption’ or ‘bulk absorption’ that provides indication of the haematocrit. In this view, NO and CO capture is based on a physical-chemical process (i.e. diffusion and reaction) and not based on morphology, which breaks lung diffusion into parts,9 10 with DLNO providing insights into areas where DLCO may fall short.11 Since 1957, alternative views of CO and NO uptake have emerged12,14 (figure 1). Despite its utility, the NO-CO double diffusion technique has yet to be widely implemented. Although devices for this measurement exist,15 their application is limited due to a lack of awareness among clinicians and the absence of FDA approval for any NO-CO measuring device. Consequently, this technique has primarily been confined to research settings by a select group of scientists. As there are several technical advantages of measuring DLNO,16 we encourage manufacturers to pursue the necessary regulatory approvals, making DLNO as commonplace as DLCO in standard pulmonary function tests (PFTs).

This illustration details the pathway of inhaled nitric oxide (NO) and carbon monoxide (CO) as they move from the alveoli in the lungs to bind with haemoglobin (Hb) in red blood cells (RBCs). The Roughton-Forster equation offers a method to understand this process by dividing the total resistance to NO or CO absorption into two main components: membrane resistance (1 /DM), which describes the resistance encountered as gases transfer from the alveolar-capillary membrane to the RBC membrane and red cell resistance (1/(θ·Vc)), which accounts for resistance due to diffusion and the chemical reaction within the RBC.5 There are notable differences in how each gas experiences resistance. For CO, around 80% of the resistance occurs within the RBC, with the remaining 20% at the alveolar-capillary membrane. In contrast, for NO, approximately 40% of the resistance is within the blood, while 60% takes place at the alveolar-capillary membrane.7 Note: A February 2024 update by Borland and Patel14—using chemical kinetic modelling—proposes that NO uptake is strongly influenced by a plasma boundary layer and a pseudo-first-order chemical reaction at the RBC’s outer membrane, rather than within the red cell interior as previously thought.8 This new insight differs from earlier perspectives, which held that the RBC’s interior played a crucial role in NO’s membrane resistance in vitro but was less significant in vivo.8 Additionally, Kang and Sapoval have introduced an alternative approach that goes beyond the Roughton-Forster equation by suggesting a time-based understanding of NO and CO absorption in the lungs. In this model, DLNO represents surface absorption related to the plasma boundary layer’s morphology, while DLCO corresponds to volume or bulk absorption, indicating haematocrit levels.12 This approach provides a unique perspective on DLNO and DLCO without relying on the component-specific details offered by the Roughton-Forster equation. Hence, DLNO and DLCO are considered two distinct, standalone measures. According to the time-dependent diffusion reaction process concerning DLNO and DLCO, the DM and ϴ·Vc components from the Roughton-Forster equation are one and the same, so no specific information is obtained from each component.13

This study aims to: (1) evaluate whether combining a 5-s breath-hold manoeuvre for DLNO (DLNO5s) and DLCO (DLCO5s) improves the identification of COVID-19-related symptomatology compared with using DLCO5s, spirometry, or total lung capacity (TLC) alone and (2) assess whether the summed combination of DLNO5s+ DLCO5s z-scores enhances net reclassification improvement (NRI) and improves Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) compared with DLCO5s z-scores alone. Prior research suggests that combining DLNO5s and DLCO5s z-scores improves diagnostic performance,11 and we hypothesised that summed z-scores of DLNO5s and DLCO5s would outperform individual metrics, spirometry or TLC z-scores in identifying post-COVID-19 symptomatology.

Methods

Study design, search strategy and data collection

Individual participant data (IPD) meta-analysis with hierarchical (mixed-effects) modelling was used to harmonise raw, participant-level data across studies. This approach involves obtaining the original datasets from each study and analysing them together in a consistent framework. It is considered one of the most rigorous methods of data synthesis because it enables harmonisation of variables and analytical methods across studies.

On 30 June 2025, GSZ performed a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)-IPD–guided systematic search of the Web of Science platform (182 million+ records) using the terms DLNO/TLNO (“double-diffusion”, “NO-CO transfer”, “nitric oxide diffusing capacity”) AND COVID-19/SARS-CoV-2/post-COVID/long COVID AND human/patients/survivors/adults, yielding 19 hits. After excluding studies without DLNO data and duplicate cohorts, GSZ requested IPD from 14 sources via email17,30 and obtained data from 11 published studies,18,2125 plus 10 unpublished controls (n=715 post-COVID; n=126 controls) (online supplemental figure S1). GSZ then excluded 69 cases measured with the Hyp’Air Compact PFT device to avoid inter-device incompatibility concerns,31 32 removed implausible values and non-White participants, and finalised a cohort of 572 COVID-recovered and 72 never-infected individuals from nine published studies17,2125 26 28 29 plus the 10 unpublished controls. See Supplement for more details on the search strategy and quality control data filtering.

Ethical approval for anonymised data usage was obtained for all original studies,17,2125 26 28 29 but this secondary analysis of de-identified data did not require IRB approval. The pooled dataset, non-filtered and filtered, is publicly available on Mendeley Data.33

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Lung function testing and data standardisation

Participants underwent lung function tests using the single-breath NO-CO double diffusion technique,6 alongside spirometry and TLC measurements. The NO-CO method measures DLNO and DLCO diffusing capacities with median breath-hold time of 5.6 s, enabling detailed characterisation of pulmonary diffusion.

To account for equipment-related differences in DLNO and alveolar volume (VA),31 32 we used reference equations that also control for specific testing equipment.32 Furthermore, reference equations previously developed using the Jaeger MasterScreen Pro34 PFT device—or a combination of two device brands6—were used for the development of fitted z-scores for DLNO5s, DLCO5s or VA5s (online supplemental table S1). For spirometry and TLC, fitted z-scores were derived from Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI) reference equations.35 36 Furthermore, the GLI equations for DLCO were also used to generate z-scores.37 The finalised pooled dataset includes the generated z-scores from these reference equations. This dataset is freely accessible on the online cloud-based repository Mendeley Data.33

Imputation of missing total lung capacity (TLC) values

Missing TLC values were imputed via five multiple imputations with chained equations and predictive-mean-matching in R (mice v3.16.0, R v4.3.2), using age, height, weight, FVC, COVID status and study site as predictors under a missing-at-random assumption. See the online supplement for details.

Statistical approach

We employed both frequentist and Bayesian frameworks to analyse the lung function data, with a focus on differentiating individuals with post-COVID-19 lung symptomatology from controls (online supplemental table S2).

Frequentist framework

Mixed-effects logistic regression (glmer in R) with study site as a random intercept. Fixed effects included fitted z-scores of DLNO₅ₛ, DLCO₅ₛ, VA₅ₛ, FEV₁, FVC, FEV₁/FVC and TLC, including summed DLNO5s+DLCO5s z-scores.

Bayesian framework

Bayesian analysis was performed using the ‘brms’ package in R, which implemented Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling to generate posterior distributions for model parameters. Leave-One-Out Information Criterion (LOOIC) was used for model selection, as Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and Akaike Information Criterion are not directly applicable.38

Data conversion and variable selection

All lung function measures were converted to z-scores per ERS/ATS 2022 standards.39 Independent variables comprised individual fitted z-scores (DLNO₅ₛ, DLCO₅ₛ, VA₅ₛ), combined DLNO₅ₛ + DLCO₅ₛ z-scores, spirometry metrics and TLC, impairment thresholds followed Zavorsky and Cao.32 Several normative reference equations were used for calculating fitted z-scores.3235,38

Nitric oxide (DLNO)/carbon monoxide (DLCO) ratio and time since diagnosis effects on lung function z-scores

We assessed the effect of prior COVID-19 on DLNO/DLCO ratio by fitting a generalised linear mixed model (GLMM) with a Gamma family and log link (glmer, lme4, R packages), specifying the DLNO/DLCO ratio as the outcome, COVID‐19 history as a fixed effect and a random intercept for study site; parameters were estimated by maximum likelihood (Laplace) and expressed as log‐ratios (back‐transformed to multiplicative effects with 95% CIs). Time from SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis to testing (median 130 days, range 32–575) was then examined in the group with previous COVID-19, fitting separate linear mixed‐effects models (lmer, lmerTest, R packages) for each of 18 lung‐function z-scores, with the number of days between diagnosis and testing as a fixed effect and study site as a random intercept; fixed‐effect estimates, standard errors, Satterthwaite’s df, and p values quantify the per-day change in each metric.

Model selection and classification metrics for predicting those with previous COVID-19

Models were evaluated based on BIC (frequentist)37 40 and LOOIC (Bayesian)38 differences, categorised to assess model superiority (online supplemental table S2). Classification performance was assessed by (1) area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC), with 95% CIs via DeLong’s method,41 42 and false discovery rate among AUROCs controlled at 0.05 by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure,43 and (2) MCC, whose 95% CI was estimated by 10-fold cross-validation repeated 10 times44 to account for class imbalance.45,47 Optimal diagnostic thresholds were set by maximising Youden’s J statistic (sensitivity+specificity –1).48

Performance evaluation of carbon monoxide (DLCO)-only and combined models

We fitted two mixed-effects logistic regression models (random intercept for study site): one using DLCO₅ₛ alone and one using the summed DLNO₅ₛ + DLCO₅ₛ z-scores. Predicted probabilities were dichotomised at the Youden-optimal cut-off to calculate sensitivity, specificity, misclassification rate and MCC. To quantify improvement in discrimination beyond the base model, we computed bootstrap-based NRI and integrated discrimination index (IDI) with 10 000 replicates each.49

Agreement analysis

Agreement between classifications of DLNO₅ₛ < LLN5 and DLCO₅ₛ < LLN5 (where the LLN5 is 5th percentile) was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa, with 95% CIs obtained via bootstrap resampling and interpreted according to standard guidelines.50

Association with dyspnoea severity

In cohorts reporting mMRC dyspnoea scores,1719 20 25,27 29 we explored relationships between lung function z-scores and exertional breathlessness using polyserial correlations and proportional odds models. A Bayesian mixed-effects cumulative link model (random intercept by study) estimated the probability of increasing dyspnoea categories based on z-scores. Pairwise comparisons of estimated marginal means (via the emmeans package) yielded posterior median differences and 95% credible intervals. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were derived from posterior variance components to quantify between-study heterogeneity.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R v4.3.2 and RStudio 2025.05.0 (build 496), using lme4 (mixed-effects models), brms (Bayesian analysis), pROC (AUROC), mice (multiple imputation) and emmeans (marginal means). Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05.

Nitric oxide (DLNO)/carbon monoxide (DLCO) ratio and time since diagnosis effects on lung function z-scores

Median DLNO/DLCO ratio was 4.26 in controls and 4.39 in those with previous COVID-19. Using a Gamma‐family GLMM with a log link and a random intercept for study site, the DLNO/DLCO ratio was not affected by having previous COVID-19 (estimate of log‐ratio = –0.0047, SE=0.0129, z = –0.36, p=0.72), corresponding to a multiplicative change of 0.995 (95% CI 0.97 to 1.02) DLNO/DLCO ratio. In those with previous COVID-19—after adjusting for between‐location clustering—the time since diagnosis had essentially zero impact on any lung‐function z-scores: per-day estimates ranged narrowly between –0.00054 and +0.00040, and the smallest p value was 0.286 (all others ≥0.414), so there is no evidence that the number of days between infection and pulmonary function testing influences any of the measured severity metrics.

Pulmonary function outcomes

The median NO–CO double-diffusion breath-hold was 5.6 s (range 4.2–11.5 s) and mean inhaled NO concentration was 53±11 ppm (35–85 ppm). Multiple imputation for TLC explained 88% of variance in combined observed/imputed values (86% in observed only; online supplemental tables S3,S4). Among 72 controls, 59 (67%) had pre-COVID-19 PFTs, and 13 (18%) were vaccinated (online supplemental tables S3,S4); only each subject’s first test was analysed. Summaries of anthropometric and pulmonary function characteristics from individual studies are presented in online supplemental tables S5,S6.

At a median of 130 days post-infection, 53% of post-COVID participants had ≥1 lung parameter below the LLN5 (z < –1.645, versus 18% of controls (p<0.0001, table 1). In controls, 4% showed mild DLCO impairment; in post-COVID patients, 38% mild, 4% moderate and 0.2% severe DLCO5s impairment (1% had elevated DLCO); for DLNO, 3% mild in controls versus 35% mild, 4% moderate and 0.2% severe in patients (1% elevated). DLCO5s and DLNO5s z-scores shared 73% of their variance (online supplemental figure S3).

Model performance

Combined DLNO5s + DLCO5s z-score models led by BIC and LOOIC, capturing ~68% of model weight (figure 2, online supplemental tables S7,S8). In the segmented-linear regression (SLR) DLNO5s-only z-scores model, each 1-unit increase in DLNO5s z-score reduced the odds of prior COVID-19 by 63% (OR ≈ 0.37; online supplemental table S9, figure S4). Fixed effects explained ≤11% of variance (marginal R²), while study-site random effects contributed 49%–64%, underscoring their necessity.

Principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical partitioning

Principal component analysis (PCA) identified PC3—a DLNO-dominant component—as most predictive (SLR OR=3.91; GAMLSS OR=2.42; online supplemental tables S10,S11) but lacked the fit of mixed-effects models. Hierarchical partitioning attributed 47% (SLR)/43% (GAMLSS) of unique variance to DLCO5s, 31%/35% to DLNO5s and 22% to VA5s, highlighting the value of joint diffusion measures.

Agreement analysis

Cohen’s κ showed moderate agreement (0.58–0.71) between DLNO5s and DLCO5s LLN5 classifications, with GAMLSS-based DLNO5s missing fewer cases (online supplemental table S12).

Classification metrics

Figure 3 displays the performance of different predictive models based on the AUROC and MCC scores. Both the AUROC and threshold-based evaluation metrics again favoured summed DLNO5s + DLCO5s (GAMLSS) models over all single predictor (figure 3) and some multipredictor models (Youden’s J=0.714; Youden z=1.911; online supplemental table S13). Sensitivity analyses (online supplemental tables S14-S16) confirmed superior sensitivity, specificity and accuracy. Summing DLNO5s z-scores with DLCO5s z-scores improved MCC by 0.06 (95% CI 0.01 to 0.11), NRI+ 0.37 (0.13 to 0.62) and the IDI ≈ 0.012 (–0.004 to 0.027), demonstrating better separation of post-COVID and control probability distributions.

Dyspnoea severity

In 348 patients, summed (combined) DLNO5s + DLCO5s z-scores correlated with mMRC dyspnoea (r = –0.33; p<0.001). Every 1-unit decrease in combined z-scores increased the odds of a higher dyspnoea grade by 12% (95% CI 6% to 17%); for DLCO5s-only, it was 25% (14%–35%) and for DLNO5s-only 19% (9%–29%) (online supplemental table S17). Summed DLNO5s + DLCO5s z-scores were similarly associated to dyspnoea ratings compared to DLNO5s-only and DLCO5s-only single model predictors. Between-study heterogeneity accounted for 50%–58% of dyspnoea variability (ICC 0.50–0.58). Diffusion measures, not spirometry, were the primary predictors of breathlessness (figure 4).

Discussion

In a one-step hierarchical (mixed-effects) IPD meta-analysis, combining DLNO₅ₛ and DLCO₅ₛ z-scores outperformed DLCO₅ₛ alone in distinguishing individuals with a prior history of COVID-19 from matched controls. The number of days between SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis and testing did not affect any of the measured z‐score severity metrics. The summed DLNO₅ₛ + DLCO₅ₛ z-score model (via GAMLSS) yielded a 37% NRI, while the integrated discrimination improvement was modest (IDI ≈ 0.012). At the optimal Youden threshold, incorporating DLNO₅ₛ z-scores reduced overall misclassification by approximately 3%.

The PCA identified PC3—reflecting DLNO-specific variance—as the strongest predictor of previous infection, underscoring the role of alveolar-capillary membrane dysfunction in post-COVID-19 pathophysiology. However, PCA-only classifiers underperformed when study-level heterogeneity was ignored, highlighting the necessity of mixed-effects modelling. Hierarchical partitioning attributed 43%–47% of between-subject variance to DLCO₅ₛ, 31%–35% to DLNO₅ₛ and approximately 22% to alveolar volume (VA₅ₛ), reinforcing diffusion impairment as the primary marker of prior SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Overall, the combined z-score model explained nearly 60% of the random-effects variance and improved the detection of diffusion defects. DLCO₅ₛ alone missed 15% of cases with the reduced gas transfer, whereas DLNO₅ₛ more sensitively captured membrane-driven impairments. Furthermore, dyspnoea severity (as measured by the mMRC scale) correlated with diffusion metrics. Each one-unit decrease in the combined z-score increased the odds of clinically significant breathlessness by 12%, compared with 25% for DLCO₅ₛ alone and 19% for DLNO₅ₛ alone (see online supplemental table S9).

Strengths and implications

This IPD meta-analysis leverages standardised protocols across multiple European centres, enhancing external validity and statistical power. Our findings support integrating DLNO₅ₛ into routine pulmonary function testing to improve diagnostic accuracy for post-COVID-19 sequelae—and potentially other alveolar-capillary disorders.11 They echo the 2022 ERS recommendation for combined DLNO + DLCO trials in large, multicentre cohorts.4

Limitations

Our study has several important limitations. First, the exclusion of non-White participants restricts the applicability of our findings to racially and ethnically diverse populations. Second, the lack of pre-infection lung function data necessitated reliance on reference equations to infer impairments rather than direct within-subject comparisons. Third, while the combined DLNO₅ₛ + DLCO₅ₛ z-score model—per Zavorsky and Cao32—performed best overall, it may not be optimal for conditions such as anaemia,52 polycythaemia or carbon monoxide poisoning53 that selectively affect DLCO₅ₛ; in those cases, summed DLNO₅ₛ + DLCO₅ₛ z-scores may not outperform DLCO₅ₛ only z-score models. Finally, nearly 18% of participants labelled as ‘never-COVID’ controls displayed previously undetected lung function abnormalities, which may have diluted the classification performance differences.

Future directions

Future research should prioritise the inclusion of racially and geographically diverse cohorts to assess the generalisability of NO–CO diffusion findings. Parallel efforts are needed to establish paediatric reference standards and standardised testing protocols for combined DLNO and DLCO measurements. Longitudinal studies tracking lung diffusion metrics after SARS-CoV-2 infection will also be essential to delineate trajectories of recovery or progression over time.

Conclusion

The dual-gas diffusion technique (DLNO₅ₛ + DLCO₅ₛ) enhances the detection of post-COVID-19 pulmonary impairments compared with DLCO₅ₛ alone, with significant net reclassification gains. While the absolute improvement in classification is moderate, its clinical impact—particularly for membrane-dominated diffusion defects—warrants consideration. Integrating DLNO₅ₛ into routine practice could refine the identification of diffusion abnormalities in post-COVID-19 and other alveolar-capillary disorders, supporting broader clinical adoption.
