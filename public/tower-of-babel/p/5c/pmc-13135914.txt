Criterion and convergent validity of plasma biomarkers in early‐onset Alzheimer's disease: Initial findings from LEADS

ABSTRACT
Abstract INTRODUCTION: Despite expanding use of plasma biomarkers for Alzheimer's disease (AD), minimal examination has been undertaken in early‐onset AD (EOAD). Prior analyses assessed criterion and convergent validity of common plasma biomarkers in a well‐characterized sample with sporadic EOAD. METHODS: Plasma amyloid beta (Aβ) 42/40, tau phosphorylated at threonine 231 (p‐tau231), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and neurofilament light change (NfL) levels were obtained for 189 EOAD, 52 early‐onset non‐AD (EOnonAD), and 83 cognitively normal (CN) participants. Diagnostic group differences, convergence with imaging biomarkers, and the capacity to predict specific cognitive domains were investigated. RESULTS: After controlling for cognitive status, EOAD participants exhibited more pathologic levels of Aβ42/40, p‐tau231, and GFAP relative to EOnonAD participants. However, NfL displayed the greatest sensitivity to non‐amnestic cognitive impairment across the sample. DISCUSSION: These results establish criterion and convergent validity of plasma biomarkers in sporadic EOAD and highlight complementary roles that AD‐specific and non‐specific plasma markers may play across the course of AD care.

INTRODUCTION

Revisions to the criteria for the diagnosis and staging of Alzheimer's disease (AD) 1 emphasize neuropathological detection using AD biomarkers. Beyond positron emission tomography (PET) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) markers, interest in plasma‐based biomarkers has intensified given their non‐invasiveness, relatively lower cost, and increased accessibility. 2 For each aspect of the “amyloid/tau/neurodegeneration framework” of AD, plasma‐based biomarkers have been developed and empirically validated for use. 1 Although a plethora of plasma biomarkers exist, detection of amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau proteinopathy for AD diagnosis appears to have coalesced around markers of Aβ protein ratios (e.g., Aβ 42/40) and phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) variants, 3 respectively. In particular, p‐tau variants can be differentiated by phosphorylation site (e.g., at threonine 231, threonine 217, threonine 181), with suggestions that tau accumulation may be characterized earliest in the progression of AD by p‐tau231. 4 Other biomarkers have also been proposed to possess important roles in staging AD. 1 Neurofilament light chain (NfL) serves as a marker of neuronal injury or neurodegeneration, and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) is believed to signal astrocytic activation and subsequent inflammation associated with amyloid plaque and tau neurofibrillary tangle accumulation. 5 A further requirement of successful AD plasma biomarkers will be the ability to predict and track biological treatment effects during pharmaceutical intervention trials. 1 It is therefore important to understand how well each of these common markers relates to performance across a range of cognitive domains, which has been understudied thus far.

Extensive research has validated plasma‐based biomarkers in late‐onset Alzheimer's disease (LOAD; see 1 , 2 , 3 , 5 , 6 for review); however, relatively little work has been conducted in sporadic early‐onset Alzheimer's disease (EOAD). EOAD begins prior to the age of 65 and represents a minority of all presentations of AD (5% to 10%). 7 It was recently shown that NfL, GFAP, and p‐tau181 levels were elevated in patients with sporadic EOAD relative to those with non‐AD neurodegeneration or primary psychiatric disorders. 8 Additionally, higher levels of NfL and GFAP have been shown for amyloid‐positive sporadic EOAD participants relative to participants with amyloid‐negative early‐onset non‐AD dementia (EOnonAD). 9

These studies provide preliminary support for the use of plasma biomarkers in EOAD and a foundation for validation using large, multisite cohorts. The amyloid‐positive EOAD cohort from the Longitudinal Early‐Onset Alzheimer's Disease Study (LEADS; National Institute on Aging [NIA] R56057195, NIA U016057195) 10 is well poised to expand this earlier work toward validation of plasma biomarkers in sporadic EOAD. As LEADS is the largest study of sporadic EOAD in North America, 10 it provides an opportunity to validate previous smaller‐sample studies in understanding EOAD disease presentation and revealing new insights. Findings from LEADS have consistently displayed unique phenotypic manifestations in sporadic EOAD compared to LOAD (e.g., higher rates of primary progressive aphasia, posterior cortical atrophy, and frontal variant AD), 7 despite common amyloid and tau pathology. Additionally, an “EOAD atrophy signature” identified disproportionate atrophy of parietal, posterior cingulate, and temporal cortices 11 and greater impairments in executive functioning, visuospatial, and processing speed skills in EOAD relative to LOAD. 12

We sought to establish criterion and convergent validity of common plasma biomarkers in sporadic EOAD, leveraging the large, geographically representative sample available via LEADS. We hypothesized that participants classified as EOAD would have abnormal plasma biomarkers of amyloid and tau proteinopathy, neurodegeneration, and inflammation at baseline relative to EOnonAD and cognitively normal (CN) participants, after accounting for disease severity. Convergent validity is additionally expected between AD biomarkers across plasma‐ and imaging‐based methods, including the newly developed EOAD atrophy signature. We further hypothesized that, given the variety of mechanisms of action among plasma biomarkers, 3 , 4 , 5 they would not predict baseline cognitive performance uniformly; instead, specific plasma‐based tools would be uniquely sensitive to particular cognitive domains. For example, given the particular vulnerability of executive functioning networks in sporadic EOAD, 12 it was anticipated that changes in plasma biomarkers (in particular, p‐tau231) would predict worsening of executive skills the most. This work will provide valuable information regarding the validity of currently available plasma biomarkers for clinical diagnosis and staging of sporadic EOAD 1 and could have important bearings on predicting indications of biological treatment effects for pharmaceutical trials. Overlapping findings with LOAD would further increase confidence in the applicability of AD plasma markers across the age spectrum.

METHOD

Participants

Data from 324 LEADS participants were used in the current study, including 189 participants with amyloid‐positive EOAD, 52 with amyloid‐negative EOnonAD, and 83 CN participants (regardless of amyloid results). All participants had baseline cognitive and plasma biomarker data that passed quality‐control checks as of March 2024. Please see the Supplementary Materials for a complete list of inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as diagnostic classifications for EOAD, EOnonAD, and CN. Indiana University School of Medicine managed single‐Institutional Review Board approval for LEADS, and informed consent/assent was obtained in writing from study participants or their legally authorized representatives.

Procedure

Details about LEADS protocols have been published elsewhere. 10 Participants underwent standardized cognitive, imaging, and plasma biomarker assessment at baseline, which will be described briefly in what follows. A more detailed description of the cognitive assessments, cognitive composites, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), PET imaging, plasma biomarker assays, and analyses can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

The cognitive measures in LEADS included the Uniform Data Set (UDS) 3.0 and Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration Module from the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC). 13 Cognitive performances were combined into the following domain composites: episodic memory, language, speed/attention, visuospatial, and executive functioning (see Table S1 for a breakdown of cognitive variables in each domain). The calculation of domain‐specific Z‐scores was undertaken in a consistent fashion with previous LEADS studies. 14 Robust Z‐scores were calculated by first subtracting the median absolute difference (MAD) from each variable and then standardized using a robust scale estimate suitable for non‐normal data, Z‐score = (X − Median)/(1.486 × MAD). The robust Z‐scores for the cognitive variables were subsequently averaged by cognitive domains to generate the composite value.

The MRI protocol in LEADS was previously documented in Touroutoglou et al. 11 For the current project, age‐ and sex‐adjusted w‐scores were used for baseline bilateral hippocampal volume (additionally corrected for intracranial volume) and the LEADS‐specific EOAD atrophy signature. 11 , 15

For amyloid and tau PET imaging, procedures were consistent with those observed in Cho and colleagues. 16 Amyloid burden was operationally defined as global composite Centiloid value corrected for whole cerebellum uptake, and tau burden was measured as a meta‐region of interest standardized uptake value ratio corrected for inferior cerebellar gray matter.

Plasma samples were analyzed at the Clinical Neurochemistry Laboratory, University of Gothenburg, Sweden. Biomarker measurements were performed following standardized protocols. P‐tau231, NfL, and GFAP were natural log (LN) transformed due to violations of normality, whereas raw values for Aβ 42/40 were used without transformation; all values were standardized to the mean and SD for the CN group.

Analyses

Selection of covariates

For group‐related covariates, independent‐sample t‐tests and two‐way chi‐squared analyses were used for the comparison of demographic, cognitive, and imaging variables between CN, EOAD, and EOnonAD groups. Additionally, bivariate correlations were examined between plasma biomarkers and demographic variables to determine covariates in subsequent partial correlation and hierarchical linear regression analyses.

Criterion and convergent validity analyses

Multiple analysis of covariance (MANCOVA), accounting for significant demographic variables (age, education, sex, ethnicity/race, MMSE), examined differences in plasma biomarker values across diagnostic groups. ANCOVA and pairwise contrasts were conducted to determine group‐specific differences (e.g., EOAD vs EOnonAD). For the examination of convergent validity, partial correlations were examined between plasma and imaging biomarkers after collapsing across groups and accounting for age, sex, ethnicity/race, MMSE, apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 status, and diagnosis.

Further, a series of hierarchical linear regressions examined the capacity of individual plasma biomarkers to predict cognitive composite performance. Plasma biomarkers, demographic variables (age, sex, ethnicity/race, APOE ε4 status, and MMSE), and diagnostic status were regressed hierarchically on each of the five cognitive composite scores. Note that while education significantly differed between groups, the calculation of cognitive Z‐scores previously accounted for education, and further covariation was not necessary. Specifically, Step 1 included demographics and diagnosis, and Step 2 added LN NfL, LN GFAP, Aβ 42/40, and LN p‐tau231. For validation purposes and to ensure proper interpretation of the hierarchical linear regression models, variable selection analyses were conducted using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression to evaluate agreement between the two modeling approaches. Finally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis where we repeated the hierarchical regression models in the EOAD group alone.

Measures of effect size were expressed throughout as absolute values for η 2 (ANCOVA/MANCOVA) and r2 (correlations and regressions). To protect against elevated Type I error as a result of multiple comparisons, all primary analyses incorporated the use of multivariate procedures and application of the Holm's sequential Bonferroni method of family‐wise adjustment 17 of the two‐tailed alpha level.

Criterion and convergent validity analyses

MANCOVA revealed significant differences between diagnostic groups among plasma biomarkers after accounting for covariates (Wilk's lambda = 0.657, p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.190; Figure 1). Post hoc ANCOVA indicated that group differences existed for all four plasma biomarkers, including Aβ 42/40, p‐tau231, GFAP, and NfL (ps = 0.001 to 0.04, η 2s = 0.020 to 0.184). Pairwise contrasts between groups revealed that the EOAD group displayed significantly higher values than the CN group for p‐tau231, GFAP, and NfL (ps = 0.001 to 0.003, η 2s = 0.033 to 0.182) and a lower value for Aβ 42/40 (p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.169). Additionally, p‐tau231 and GFAP were higher for EOAD than EOnonAD (ps < 0.001, η 2s = 0.123 to 0.170), and Aβ 42/40 was lower for EOAD (p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.123). There was also a non‐significant trend in the expected direction for NfL values between impairment groups (p = 0.057, η 2 = 0.016). No significant differences were observed between CN and EOnonAD groups across any plasma biomarker (ps = 0.23 to 0.77, η 2s = 0.001 to 0.012). As a result of these findings, diagnostic status (CN, EOAD, or EOnonAD) was additionally included as a covariate in subsequent analyses.

Further, the ability of individual plasma biomarkers to predict domain‐specific cognitive composites was examined. When collapsing across groups, a series of hierarchical linear regression models including demographic variables (age, sex, racial/ethnic diversity, APOE ε4 status, and MMSE), diagnosis, NfL, GFAP, Aβ 42/40, and p‐tau231 significantly predicted performance on all five cognitive domains (F[10,299] = 45.29, p < 0.001, r 2 = 0.60 for episodic memory; F[10,301] = 3.13, p < 0.001, r 2 = 0.09 for language; F[10,297] = 41.39, p < 0.001, r 2 = 0.58 for processing speed/attention; F[10,252] = 15.01, p < 0.001, r 2 = 0.37 for visuospatial skills; and F[10,302] = 50.97, p < 0.001, r 2 = 0.63 for executive functioning). Beta weights revealed that lower levels of NfL, GFAP, and p‐tau231 were associated with higher cognitive performances, whereas lower levels of Aβ 42/40 were associated with lower cognitive performances. Examination of significance values in Table 3 reveals that each plasma biomarker predicted a unique profile of cognitive domains, after accounting for demographics and diagnosis. Specifically, plasma NfL significantly predicted cognitive performance for processing speed/attention, visuospatial skills, and executive functioning (ps = 0.003 to 0.04). P‐tau231 predicted cognitive performance for episodic memory and visuospatial skills (ps = 0.001 to 0.04). Plasma GFAP predicted cognitive performance for episodic memory (p < 0.001) and executive functioning (p = 0.059; showing potential for association and requiring need for further investigation). Conversely, Aβ 42/40 only predicted the episodic memory composite (p < 0.001). When LASSO analyses were run on a grid of alpha values (ranging from 0.01 to 0.61) with a hold‐out proportion of 30% set to perform five‐fold cross‐validation for selecting the tuning parameter, results were comparable to what was observed with the hierarchical regression findings with a small penalty parameter. Finally, sensitivity analyses isolating the EOAD sample revealed that plasma NfL continued to predict performance on processing speed/attention (p = 0.001), executive functioning (p = 0.002), and visuospatial skills (p = 0.007) domains, whereas limited other significance was observed (Aβ 42/40 predicted episodic memory, p = 0.03; GFAP predicted visuospatial skills, p = 0.03).

DISCUSSION

This study sought to establish criterion and convergent validity of plasma‐based biomarkers in sporadic EOAD using a large, geographically diverse, US sample. Specifically, primary analyses supported hypotheses that Aβ 42/40, p‐tau231, and GFAP would reflect greater pathology (higher levels for p‐tau231 and GFAP; lower levels for Aβ 42/40) in EOAD than in EOnonAD or CN participants (ps < 0.001; Figure 1). NfL was higher in EOAD than CN (p = 0.003), with non‐significant trends observed in the expected direction between clinically impaired groups (p = 0.057). These results are consistent with findings from other studies examining plasma biomarkers in EOAD. Bhalala and colleagues observed increases of 2.0‐fold, 3.5‐fold, and 1.4‐fold in p‐tau181, GFAP, and NfL, respectively, for EOAD patients versus those with non‐AD neurodegenerative disease (e.g., frontotemporal dementia, Parkinson's disease). 8 Asken and colleagues similarly found that EOAD participants displayed 4.2‐fold, 2.2‐fold, and 2.0‐fold increases in p‐tau217, GFAP, and NfL relative to EOnonAD participants, 9 respectively. A closer look at Figure 1 reveals that EOAD participants from the current study had increases of 5.5‐fold, 3.7‐fold, and 2.8‐fold on p‐tau231, GFAP, and NfL over EOnonAD participants, respectively, and had a 4.3‐fold decrease on Aβ 42/40. These differences reflect large magnitudes of effect between clinical groups (η 2s = 0.123 to 0.170, excluding the non‐significant NfL result), even after accounting for overall cognitive severity (i.e., MMSE). This increased effect of 30% to 70% over the previous literature may be explained by the thorough characterization of the participants in LEADS, as well as greater overall severity in the current clinical samples.

This study's primary proteinopathy findings can be explained by the mechanisms of Aβ and p‐tau change in plasma. As Aβ 1‐42 forms toxic oligomers that accumulate into cerebral amyloid plaques, plasma levels are reduced relative to Aβ 1‐40, leading to the lower Aβ 42/40. 18 Although careful study comparing EOAD and LOAD has not yet been conducted, these standalone results in sporadic EOAD coincide with findings repeatedly observed in LOAD, which has established low Aβ 42/40 as a marker of amyloid proteinopathy and cognitive decline. 19 , 20 , 21 While we did not have plasma p‐tau217 data available for this analysis, our elevated plasma p‐tau231 levels in EOAD samples are also supported by the literature using a variety of plasma tau biomarkers (231, 217, 181). 4 , 22 Future work will evaluate p‐tau217 as well as other tau forms via analyses that are currently planned. Neocortical tau accumulation predominantly occurs in conjunction with elevated cerebral amyloid, 23 with its spread being triggered by amyloid‐mediated hyperconnectivity between tau‐specific epicenters. 24 Greater plasma‐tau levels would thus be anticipated in participants with higher cerebral amyloid deposition, as is observed within the current EOAD sample.

Although GFAP and NfL are not specific to AD, our results highlight the role of inflammation via reactive astrocytes and neurodegeneration in signaling the downstream consequences of amyloidosis in sporadic EOAD. In response to amyloid aggregation, restructuring of the astrocytic environment occurs around plaques. This leads to increased glial cell counts and changes in glial morphology for the protection/repair of affected neurons 25 and corresponds to elevated GFAP concentrations (see Bellaver et al. 26 for meta‐analysis in AD). Additionally, the toxicity of Aβ oligomers and loss of integrity of tau microtubules leads to neuronal damage that can be detected by NfL. Specifically, plasma NfL measures cytoskeletal neurofilament proteins in the blood released from injured neurons and can therefore be observed after a host of neuronal insults. Our results showing comparable elevated NfL levels in EOAD and EOnonAD are consistent with prior work 9 , 27 and support both the non‐specificity of this biomarker and that neuronal injury is abundant across early‐onset neurodegenerative conditions. Findings also support a role for the use of NfL as both a staging marker of disease (i.e., level of neurodegeneration) and an output in future clinical trials in sporadic EOAD to document neuronal response following disease‐modifying treatment.

Consistent with our hypotheses, convergent validity with PET and MRI biomarkers of AD was demonstrated. Unsurprisingly, plasma and PET markers of amyloid and tau proteinopathy were most strongly associated with each other, accounting for 12% of the variance for amyloid and 22% for tau (Table 2). Correlations with the EOAD atrophy signature were more modest (r 2s = 0.04 to 0.10), and associations with hippocampal volume were statistically significant, but small (r 2s = 0.01 to 0.05). Our strong results with PET are consistent with measuring the same‐trait (amyloid or tau proteinopathy) using different methods, whereas the subtle relationships with hippocampal volumes likely reflect the reduced reliance on hippocampal atrophy in sporadic EOAD. 11 , 28 Relatedly, the convergence between plasma markers and the EOAD atrophy signature adds additional validation 15 of this recently developed marker of EOAD. Further, the magnitude of our plasma marker and imaging correlations is generally consistent with the LOAD literature. For example, current rs = 0.47 to 0.49 between meta‐temporal tau PET and both p‐tau231 and GFAP correspond to rates observed for p‐tau181 and GFAP in LOAD (r = 0.53 and 0.50, respectively). 29 Our 12% variance accounted for between amyloid PET and Aβ 42/40 was also similar to literature earlier in the AD spectrum (19% in subjective cognitive decline). 30 Similarly, several studies have shown high discriminative capacity of plasma markers (p‐tau217, p‐tau181, NfL, Aβ 42/40) for PET results in LOAD samples. 31 , 32 Of note, the strong but less than perfect associations between PET and plasma markers in our study likely reflect differences behind the biological substrates of these markers. It has been suggested that tau PET signals neurofibrillary tangle pathology later in the condition (e.g., Braak stage 4+), 33 while plasma p‐tau appears to be a more dynamic indicator of multiple interdependent abnormalities related to pathological tau, including increased phosphorylation and loss of soluble tau from compromised neurons. 34 As such, it has been proposed that tau PET measures insoluble aggregated tau deposited over time, whereas plasma p‐tau is a snapshot of dynamic factors (production versus clearance of tau) at the time of measurement. 35

Regarding cognitive profiles, the results of hierarchical linear regressions supported our hypothesis that plasma biomarkers would not uniformly predict cognition, though our anticipation that executive functioning would be the most sensitive domain was only partly supported. Models were all successful at predicting cognitive performance (Table 3), with most biomarkers appearing to uniquely contribute to the prediction of performance on a memory composite (ps = 0.001 to 0.015 for Aβ 42/40, p‐tau231, and GFAP). However, the non‐specific NfL predicted cognitive performance across the most domains (i.e., processing speed/attention, executive functioning, and visuospatial skills; ps = 0.003 to 0.04), which remained present when isolating the effect in the EOAD sample alone. Variable selection analyses using LASSO regression supported these findings. Although this appears to be the first investigation into cognitive domain selectivity for plasma biomarkers in sporadic EOAD, our results are generally consistent with similar investigations in LOAD. Work from the Ontario Neurodegenerative Disease Research Initiative revealed that plasma NfL, GFAP, and p‐tau181 were inversely related to performance across cognitive domains of attention, memory, executive functioning, language, and visuospatial processing; this occurred in both mixed dementia and AD cohorts, with NfL possessing the greatest magnitude of effect. 36 This may be explained by differences in the time course of each of the underlying pathologies in AD. As described earlier, NfL is a measurement of soluble neurofilaments released by damaged neurons, so it is anticipated to reflect more in‐the‐moment changes in neurons and subsequently display the greatest sensitivity to cognitive change. Conversely, Aβ 42/40 and p‐tau231 reflect changes earlier in the disease course of AD, associated with accumulation of pathology beginning 10 to 20 years prior to the manifestation of cognitive impairment. 1 Regarding the cognitive domains themselves, while not anticipated, it is not necessarily surprising that memory performances were predicted by most plasma biomarkers, given the prevalence and severity of memory dysfunction across the cohort.

This study is subject to limitations. First, the observed results are unique to the cognitive and plasma biomarker measures administered in LEADS. As such, more recent advances in plasma markers (e.g., p‐tau217, MTBR243 tau, brain‐derived tau forms) may lead to some variability in results. Second, as the LEADS cognitive battery was selected to be consistent with the NACC UDS, some cognitive domains were more thoroughly examined (e.g., memory) than others (e.g., visuospatial skills). Third, as alluded to earlier, despite deliberate efforts to recruit increasingly diverse demographic samples into LEADS (e.g., Alzheimer's Association Diversity Supplement; LDRFP‐21‐818464), the clinical groups were predominantly White and highly educated. This may have impacted the influence of our ethnic/racial covariation on the analyses; these results should be replicated in more demographically heterogenous samples. Fourth, although LASSO regression was used in lieu of stepwise linear regression to avoid concerns about the statistical validity of estimated coefficients and associations, 37 LASSO is still susceptible to limitations when predictors are highly correlated. As such, future investigation is encouraged to examine the optimal combination of plasma biomarkers to predict cognitive domain performance as outcomes in clinical trials.

CONCLUSIONS

These results of criterion and convergent validity support the use of plasma biomarkers in sporadic EOAD and highlight the complementary role that the various plasma markers may play across the course of care for the condition. Specifically, measures of proteinopathy appear to distinguish EOAD from EOnonAD (and normal cognition) best, whereas non‐specific markers of GFAP and NfL may uniquely track individual changes in cognitive performance, which is ideal for monitoring the response to interventions. Future investigation is needed to compare these markers in EOAD and LOAD directly, as well as within clinical phenotypes of EOAD.
