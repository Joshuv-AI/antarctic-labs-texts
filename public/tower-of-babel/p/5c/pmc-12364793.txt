Assessment of behavioral emotion regulation and relationship with mental health in adults: BERQ Japanese version
Authors: Yoshiko Kato, Takahiro Yamane, Yasunori Murata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to examine the psychometric properties of the Japanese version of the Behavioral Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (BERQ) and explore the relation between the BERQ and mental health (depression, anxiety, and well-being).MethodsFirst, with a sample of 489 adult participants (246 males and 243 females; mean age 45.8 ± 13.5 years), the psychometric properties of the BERQ and its correlation with mental health were examined. Second, with a two-wave, three-week panel design among 375 Japanese participants (189 males and 186 females; mean age 46.9 ± 13.2 years), structural equation modeling was employed to examine the cross-lagged associations between the subscales of BERQ score and mental health.ResultsConfirmatory factor analysis supported the allocation of the items to the subscales, with high Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for all the scales. Greater use of various factors, such as Seeking Distraction, Actively Approaching, and Seeking Social Support, was correlated with greater well-being. Conversely, greater use of Withdrawal was related to higher depression and anxiety levels but lower well-being. However, interestingly, the results of latent cross-lagged panel analysis indicated Withdrawal had a positive effect on depression, a negative effect on well-being, however, no significant effect on anxiety. Whereas anxiety had a positive effect on Withdrawal.ConclusionThe results of this study highlight BERQ Japanese version is useful tool to assess the behavioral emotion regulation strategies. Moreover, an aspect of psychological process has been suggested, indicating that anxiety may play a key role in amplifying withdrawal, and may lead depression.

Introduction

Emotional regulation plays an important role in maintaining and promoting mental health. Emotional regulation has also been associated with overall mood and well-being [1], psychosomatic disorders [2], obesity [3], and inflammatory responses [4], all of which are related to both physical and mental health. Additionally, emotional regulation and related coping strategies in stressful situations have become the subjects of focus in recent research [5, 6]. Consequently, numerous emotion regulation scales have been reported in Japan. Among these scales, Emotion Regulation Questionnaire, developed by Gross and John [1], is widely used internationally to measure the impact of emotion regulation on mental health. This scale assesses cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression based on Gross’s process model of emotion regulation [7, 8]. Meanwhile, in recent years, emotion regulation strategies that consider both cognition and behavior have garnered attention.

On the basis of these studies, psychoeducation [9] and psychotherapy [10] have also been applied to regulate emotions through cognitive and behavioral interventions. In Japan, as Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) is increasingly adopted in medical and educational settings, cognitive emotion regulation strategy scales and behavioral emotion regulation strategy scales are expected to play a useful role as assessment tools. Therefore, reporting on behavioral emotion regulation strategy scales is essential, alongside the cognitive emotion regulation strategies that have already been developed.

In light of these developments, the Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (CERQ) [11] and the Behavioral Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (BERQ) [12] have been created and applied internationally. Specifically, the CERQ measures cognitive coping strategies, whereas the BERQ measures behavioral coping strategies. In this case, cognition and behavior complement one another when coping with stressful situations. Thus, the CERQ and BERQ are related, despite possessing different attributes.

For the CERQ, French [13], Chinese [14], Turkish [15], Persian [16], Spanish [17], and Arabic [18] versions have been created, in addition to a Japanese version [19]. With respect to the BERQ, versions in Chinese [20], Turkish [21], Hindi [22], Portuguese [23], Persian [24], and Romanian [25] have been developed, with no Japanese version. As mentioned earlier, there are two types of emotional regulation strategies, i.e., those that involve cognition and those that involve behavior. Therefore, this study develops a Japanese version of the BERQ and examines its psychometric properties.

Furthermore, a longitudinal study was conducted to deepen the examination of the relationship between the subfactors of the developed BERQ (Seeking Distraction, Withdrawal, Ignoring, Actively Approaching, and Seeking Social Support) and mental health (anxiety, depression, and well-being).

Behavioral emotion regulation and mental health

It has been suggested that both the BERQ and the CERQ include adaptive and maladaptive coping strategies, with the former negatively associated with depression and anxiety, whereas the latter is positively associated with these conditions. Among the subscales of BERQ, Withdrawal is consistently and strongly correlated with depression and anxiety across different cultures. Conversely, Actively Approaching is consistently and negatively associated with depression and anxiety across cultures [12, 20–22, 24]. Moreover, Withdrawal is frequently discussed as a maladaptive coping strategy, whereas Actively Approaching is considered a relatively adaptive coping strategy across various cultures. However, these results are based on cross-sectional studies. Thus, to determine whether the coping strategies of the BERQ subscales are adaptive or maladaptive, it is important to extend previous research by conducting a longitudinal study. Moreover, other strategies have shown different trends across cultures, especially in their associations with depression and anxiety. From this perspective, further research should be conducted on different cultures and groups to obtain appropriate findings and suitable coping strategies.

In general, examining mental health and recovery has been considered mainly from a negative perspective, such as depression and anxiety. However, recently, there have been positive approaches to promoting a healthy state of mind by valuing well-being.

It’s internationally recognized that well-being encompasses both hedonic well-being and eudaimonic well-being. Imai, through the development of the Ikigai-9 scale, found that Ikigai is composed of three factors: optimistic and positive emotions toward life (e.g., “I often feel that I am happy.“), positive attitudes towards one’s future (e.g., “I would like to learn something new or start something.“), and recognition of the meaning of one’s existence (e.g., “I feel that I am contributing to someone or society.“) [26]. It means the content of these three factors is represented by aspects such as life satisfaction, challenge/personal growth, and recognition of social role. From this perspective, Ikigai is a concept that includes both types of hedonic and eudaimonic well-being, suggesting that the Ikigai-9 comprehensively captures well-being.

In recent years, given the reported association between Ikigai and physical health, Ikigai has garnered attention as a factor expected to offer prospective protective effects not only for mental health but also for physical health [27]. On this context, international research on Ikigai is progressing. The English version of Ikigai-9 did not support the three-dimensional structure initially proposed by Imai et al. [26]; instead, a single-factor solution for Ikigai was supported due to high internal consistency [28]. This indicate that optimistic and positive emotions toward life, positive attitudes toward one’s future, and recognition of the meaning of one’s existence may overlap. Furthermore, the English and German versions of Ikigai-9 have been reported to show a positive association with psychological well-being and a negative association with depression. However, consistent results regarding its association with anxiety have not been obtained; while a negative association was reported in the German version, no association was found in the English version [28, 29]. These findings suggest that although Ikigai-9 exhibits different characteristics across cultures, evaluating Ikigai with Ikigai-9 holds promise for understanding the potential protective effects of Ikigai, not limited to the Japanese population.

Considering the expected implementation of CBT in healthcare in Japan, it’s meaningful to investigate the relationship between behavioral emotion regulation and Ikigai for obtaining effective information regarding interventions for stress coping strategies.

Therefore, in this study, we examine the relation between Ikigai well-being (a concept of well-being originating in Japan) and behavioral emotion regulation. The initial results suggest that adaptive coping strategies are positively related to Ikigai well-being, whereas maladaptive coping strategies are negatively related to this concept.

Overall, this study created a Japanese version of the BERQ, examined its psychometric properties, and reported its correlations with depression, anxiety, and well-being. Noteworthy is that, taking previous investigation a step further, we use longitudinal data to examine the relations between the BERQ score and depression, anxiety, and well-being based on cross-lagged model.

Method

Participants and procedure

The initial survey included 500 participants (252 males and 248 females) aged 20–69 years (mean age: 45.8 ± 13.6 years). However, 489 participants (246 males and 243 females; mean age 45.8 ± 13.5 years) were included in the final sample after those with incomplete answers (Time 1) were excluded. The follow-up survey initially included 388 participants (194 males and 194 females; mean age 47.0 ± 13.3 years). However, after those with incomplete answers were excluded, the final sample included 375 participants (189 males and 186 females; mean age 46.9 ± 13.2 years) (Time 2).

In this study, an online survey was conducted by Rakuten Research, Inc., in Japan. The participants were randomly selected from among those registered with the research group on the basis of equal distribution according to age, gender, and province of residence. The research requirements were thoroughly explained to the participants prior to the commencement of the study, and informed consent was obtained from all participants (Time 1). A follow-up survey was conducted three weeks after the initial survey (Time 2). Stress coping strategies are expected to have different effects in the short, medium, and long term. However, this study aimed to examine the relationship between stress coping strategies and mental health as comprehensively as possible. Considering that the diagnosis of acute stress reaction is made based on stress response symptoms within one month, investigating stress responses three weeks later allows for an analysis that not only considers short-term stress reactions but also takes into account the potential for mid-to-long-term stress responses. The sample size for this study was determined using G*Power [30], with calculations set to an effect size of 0.15, a significance level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.95. Additionally, considering a 60% response rate for two surveys conducted at three-week intervals, the required sample size for initial survey was established as 430 participants (215 males and 215 females). Consequently, the sample size used in this study exceeded the final threshold. This study was conducted in accordance with the procedures approved by the ethics committee of the authors’ university.

Materials

Behavioral Emotion Regulation: The Japanese version of the BERQ was created through back translation on the basis of the original version by Kraaij and Garnefski [12]. This version consists of five subscales: Seeking Distraction, Withdrawal, Ignoring, Actively Approaching, and Seeking Social Support. Each subscale included four items, for a total of 20 items. The responses were based on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). In this case, a higher score on a subscale indicated more frequent use of this behavioral strategy. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of the subscales ranged from 0.79 to 0.88.

Cognitive Emotion Regulation: The short version of the CERQ is an 18-item scale comprising two items for each of the strategies [31]. In this study, we used items from the Japanese version of the CERQ [19] that corresponded to those in Garnefski and Kraaij [31]. This version consists of nine subscales: Self-blame, Other-blame, Rumination, Catastrophizing, Positive Refocusing, Refocus on Planning, Positive Reappraisal, Putting into Perspective, and Acceptance. The responses were based on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). In this case, a higher score on the subscale indicated more frequent use of this specific cognitive strategy. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of the subscales ranged from 0.77 to 0.90.

Depression: The Japanese version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) was used to measure depression [32]. Same to the original version developed by Radloff [33], the participants were asked to answer 20 questions about their physical and mental states related to depressive symptoms on a 4-point scale (0 to 3). In this case, the higher the score is, the greater the level of depression. Overall, the scale showed positive internal consistency (α = 0.92).

Anxiety: The Japanese version of the State Trait Anxiety Inventory was used to measure anxiety. The original version was developed by Spielberger et al. [34], and the Japanese version was translated by Shimizu and Imae [35]. With respect to the 20 items related to their state of mind, the participants were asked to answer how they felt in general on a 4-point scale (1 to 4). Overall, the scale showed positive internal consistency (α = 0.91).

Well-being: A Japanese well-being scale, the Ikigai-9, was used to measure well-being [26]. Ikigai is a comprehensive concept defined as a subjective evaluation of well-being among Japanese people; it is related to positive emotions, i.e., happiness and life satisfaction, as well as the cognitive evaluation of the meaning of one’s life, self-esteem, and self-efficacy [36]. This scale comprises nine questions: three for life satisfaction (e.g., “I frequently feel I am happy”), three for challenge/personal growth (e.g., “I would like to learn new things or start something new”), and three for recognition of social roles (e.g., “I think I am valuable for other people and society”). This scale also included hedonic and eudaemonic well-being. The participants responded via a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (almost never true) to 5 (almost always true). Overall, the scale showed positive internal consistency (α = 0.88).

Data analysis

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was conducted to examine the factor structure of the BERQ, and measurement invariance testing was performed in four steps. First, single-group CFA was conducted for the full sample and according to gender. Second, configural invariance with multi-group CFA was examined without equality constraints. Third, the model was tested for weak (metric) measurement invariance with multi-group CFA by restricting all factor loadings to be equal across both samples. Finally, the model was tested for strong (scalar) measurement invariance by restricting all factor loadings and means to be equal across both samples. The goodness of fit of the model was assessed by inspecting the comparative fit index (CFI), chi-square statistic (χ2), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) [37]. CFI and TLI values > 0.90 and RMSEA and SRMR values ≤ 0.08 are considered good model fits [38]. The recommendation of Chen [39] was followed when sample size is adequate (total N > 300) and sample sizes are equal across the groups, and comparing the nested model fit in the analysis invariance, such that ΔCFI ≥ − 0.010, ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015, ΔSRMR ≤ 0.010 would indicate invariance. The Akaike information criterion (AIC) [40] and Bayesian information criterion (BIC) [41] were used to compare and select the model. A lower AIC or BIC value indicates a better fit. The model with the lowest value was preferred for both indices, particularly for BIC.

The means and standard deviations of the BERQ were also obtained, whereas Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated to determine the reliability of the BERQ. Additionally, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to assess test–retest reliability, whereas Pearson’s correlation was used to examine the relations of the BERQ with the CERQ and mental health (i.e., depression, anxiety, and well-being). To control for possible intercorrelations in the BERQ, partial correlations between the BERQ subscales and depression, anxiety, and well-being were calculated. Finally, latent cross-lagged panel analysis was performed to examine the reciprocal longitudinal relations among the BERQ score, depression, anxiety, and well-being, controlling for the effects of gender and age (see Fig. 1). In this study, statistical analysis was performed by using IBM SPSS Statistics 29, Amos 26, and M-plus version 8.8 software, and the full information maximum likelihood estimation method was used to estimate the missing values in structural equation modeling.

Results

Confirmatory factor analysis

In this study, CFA was conducted via the five-factor model, as in the original version. All of the factor loadings exceeded 0.62, indicating a good model fit (CFI = 0.92, TLI = 0.91, RMSEA = 0.056, SRMR = 0.057) and confirming the five-factor structure (see Tables 1 and 2). To determine whether the factor structure of the BERQ was invariant between men and women, which to examine the measurement invariance of the BERQ across gender, multiple group structural equation modeling was conducted (see Table 2). Compared with the other models, the scalar invariance model showed only a slight decrease in each goodness-of-fit index. Although the Akaike information criterion was relatively high, the Bayesian information criterion was the lowest, according to the sample size. These results supported the scalar invariance model.

Correlations between the BERQ subscales and mental health

This study also calculated Pearson’s correlation coefficients and partial correlations between the BERQ subscales and depression, anxiety, and well-being (Table 6). First, Withdrawal had a significant positive relation with depression, whereas Ignoring had a small positive relation. When adjusting for the other scales, no significant relation was found between Ignoring and depression. Second, Withdrawal had a significant positive relation with anxiety, whereas Actively Approaching had a small negative relation with anxiety. Third, Seeking Distraction, Seeking Social Support, and Ignoring did not correlate with anxiety. However, when adjusting for the other scales, a small significant negative relation was found only between Ignoring and anxiety. Fourth, Withdrawal had a negative significant relation with well-being, and Seeking Distraction, Actively Approaching, and Seeking Social Support had a small to moderate significant positive relation with well-being. Finally, Ignoring had no significant relation with well-being.

Cross-lagged relations between behavioral emotion regulation and mental health

Finally, we investigated the cross-lagged associations between the BERQ and mental health (Figs. 1, 2 , 3, 4, 5), the findings of which showed a moderate model fit (CFI = 0.90 – 0.92, TLI = 0.89 – 0.93, RMSEA = 0.041 – 0.077, SRMR = 0.061 – 0.096). Accordingly, Withdrawal at T1 had a positive cross-lagged effect on depression at T2 (β = 0.15, p < 0.001), whereas this behavioral regulation was not related to anxiety. However, anxiety at T1 was related to Withdrawal at T2 (β = 0.17, p = 0.011). Additionally, Withdrawal at T1 had a negative effect on well-being at T2 (β = −0.11, p = 0.002), whereas well-being at T1 had a positive cross-lagged effect on Actively Approaching at T2 (β = 0.12, p = 0.023).

Discussion and conclusion

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the psychometric properties of the Japanese version of the BERQ among 489 adult participants, along with its associations with mental health (depression, anxiety, and well-being). A longitudinal study was also conducted with 375 participants to examine the relation between the BERQ score and mental health. On the basis of these findings, the factor structure was supported, and factorial validity was confirmed, which is in line with previous studies [20, 21, 23, 24]. In addition, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were within an acceptable range, while small to moderate correlations between the subscales were observed, except for two correlations, i.e., between Actively Approaching and Seeking Social Support, and between Seeking Distraction and Ignoring. However, as discussed in previous research [12], the shared variances of these scales were not strong enough (25% and 32%) to suggest that they were the same constructs. In fact, as shown in the CFA, the contents of these items differed. Specifically, both Actively Approaching, and Seeking Social Support are coping strategies that the participants use to confront stressful events proactively and work toward resolving them. On the other hand, Seeking Distraction and Ignoring are coping strategies that aim to distance oneself from such situations and protect oneself form stressful evets. In this context, it is reasonable to consider that these strategies, although different, are correlated with one another.

Moreover, the multiple group structural equation modeling supported the configural invariance, metric invariance, and scalar invariance of the Japanese version of the BERQ across gender. The support for these invariances suggested that the Japanese version of BERQ measured the same constructs among men and women, measured behavioral emotion regulation in the same manner in both genders, and indicated that the intercepts of the BERQ items were equal between both groups. These results indicated that comparing the scores of Japanese of BERQ is meaningful across genders in this Japanese sample.

Interestingly, when reviewing other studies in different cultures, the correlation coefficients among the subscales of BERQ have been inconsistent. For example, in the Netherlands, a strong correlation (r = 0.57) between Actively Approaching and Seeking Social Support has been reported [12], similar to Japan. However, studies conducted in Portugal, India, China, and Turkey have only reported weak correlations (r = 0.20 − 0.28) [15, 20, 22, 23]. Additionally, regarding the relationship between Seeking Distraction and Ignoring, a strong correlation has been observed in Turkey (r = 0.57), similar to Japan [15]. Meanwhile, moderate correlations have been reported in Portugal (r = 0.37) [23] and India (r = 0.32) [22], whereas no correlations have been reported in China (r = 0.03) [20] and Netherlands (r = 0.18) [12]. In this context, an individual most likely uses several strategies and adjusts them in light of the stressful event and his/her respective culture.

Moreover, the test–retest reliability of the scales is described as moderate, meaning that the results are somewhat stable but not consistent across three weeks after testing. However, since the period between the first and second surveys was three weeks, it is conceivable that the stressful events during this period might have had a certain influence, even though the behavioral coping strategies were relatively stable.

As in previous studies [12, 20, 24], the BERQ and CERQ subscales showed small to moderate correlations with one another, supporting our hypothesis that the BERQ and CERQ complement one another. Furthermore, a significant strong correlation between Seeking Distraction (e.g., “I engage in other, unrelated activities.”) and Positive Refocusing (e.g., “I think of nicer things than what I have experienced.”) was found. For example, it is natural for cognitive coping strategies, such as diverting one’s thoughts from the cause of stress, to ultimately lead to behavioral coping strategies, such as engaging in unrelated activities. Additionally given the significant relation between Withdrawal and Rumination, considering that Rumination is associated with anxiety [19] and that anxiety is related to Withdrawal, it is also reasonable to predict that the strong correlation between Rumination and Withdrawal is mediated by anxiety. Actively Approaching is related strongly to both Refocus on Planning and Positive Reappraisal. In these strategies, individuals attempt to adopt a positive attitude (both cognitively and behaviorally) toward the stressful event. Therefore, there is a strong correlation between these subscales.

With respect to the relation between the BERQ score and mental health, Seeking Distraction and Actively Approaching may be adaptive coping strategies, with a small negative correlation with depression and/or anxiety. Moreover, Withdrawal may be a maladaptive coping strategy consistently across cultures, with a significant moderate positive correlation with depression and anxiety. Similar results were reported in other cultures [12, 20–22, 24]. In relation to well-being, Seeking Distraction, Actively Approaching, and Seeking Social Support were positively related to well-being and could be regarded as positive coping strategies. Conversely, Withdrawal was negatively associated with well-being and could be considered a negative coping strategy. In Romanian [25], the relations of Withdrawal of positive affect were reported to be similar to its relations with well-being in Japanese.

Compared with previous studies, Seeking Social Support and Ignoring also had different associations with mental health. Seeking Social Support has different results across cultures. For example, in Japan, it was not associated with either depression or anxiety, which is similar to the results of a Turkish [21] and Romanian [25] study. On the other hand, a moderate positive correlation was found with well-being, indicating that this strategy has a positive dimension in Japan. In Persia [24], a positive association with anxiety and depression was found, suggesting that it may be a negative coping strategy, whereas in the Netherlands [12] and China [20], it was negatively associated with anxiety and/or depression, indicating that it might be a positive coping strategy in these cultures. Moreover, despite depression and anxiety being strongly associated with one another [42], Ignoring showed a weak positive association with depression, whereas it showed a weak negative association with anxiety. These contradictory results suggest that, as fundamentally different aspects [42], the relation between depression and anxiety is not always the same since it depends on the type of coping strategy.

Previous studies have shown that Seeking Distraction, Actively Approaching, and Seeking Social Support, as more adaptive scales, are used more often than less adaptive scales are [12]. As for Seeking Distraction was used the most, whereas Seeking Social Support was used the least, although these were positively associated with well-being. In related research, the Japanese sample presented lower levels of social support, compared to the Austrian sample [43]. This difference may be explained by the fact that BERQ depends on an individual’s appraisal of external/internal resources and values within the context of culture. This may also be due to the difference in Japanese response styles, which typically include a mid-point response instead of an acquiescent response [44].

Finally, we further examined the relation between the BERQ score and mental health through a longitudinal study based on cross-logged model. This analysis allowed us to examine not only the unidirectional relationship from the subscales of the BERQ to mental health but also the unidirectional relationship from mental health to the subscale of BERQ. The results revealed that only Withdrawal was significantly correlated with all three mental health indicators (anxiety, depression, and well-being). Specifically, Withdrawal was positively related to depression and negatively related to well-being after three weeks. This result is in line with the perspective that Withdrawal might be a maladaptive and negative coping strategy. However, while no association from Withdrawal to anxiety was found, an association from anxiety to Withdrawal was identified, suggesting that Anxiety, as a stress response triggered by perceiving an event as a threatening, may act as a temporary signal that leads to withdrawal for self-protection. From these results, the following perspective can be predicted: Anxiety is often a critical emotion for self-protection from stressful event, however, when anxiety becomes excessive, it can result in withdrawal, which may and contribute to depression and a decline in well-being. This supports the following two points discussed thus far: First, from the relationship between various coping strategies and depression and anxiety, it suggests that while both represent negative psychological responses, they fundamentally possess different aspects, and their roles may vary depending on the type of coping strategy. Second, the relationship between Rumination and Withdrawal also suggests the hypothesis that an increase in anxiety caused by Rumination may lead to Withdrawal, which could potentially lead to an increase in depression and a reduction in well-being. Essentially, in this three-week longitudinal study conducted on Japanese participants, it can be suggested that Withdrawal may be a negative coping strategy. Thus, reducing excessive anxiety could mitigate the negative impact on mental health indicators like depression and well-being. However, this study has not sufficiently examined causal relationships. Therefore, further investigation through intervention studies, such as cognitive-behavioral therapies targeting anxiety reduction, is needed to clarify this point.

The other subscale with significant associations was Actively Approaching, indicating that greater well-being is related to greater use of this subscale. Moreover, other subscales with weaker associations with mental health were not significant in this longitudinal study. However, since this study was conducted over three weeks, it is possible that a shorter period could have produced different results.

There are several limitations in this study that should be noted. First, since the relation between the BERQ score and mental health was examined in a cross-sectional, longitudinal study, future research should determine the causal relation between these aspects. Second, since our sample consisted of the general population, future studies should focus on the psychometric properties of the BERQ in a clinical population. Third, as correlations were observed among the BERQ subscale, it is evident that people generally employ multiple coping strategies in their everyday lives. However, this study did not examine coping styles. Thus, future studies should investigate coping styles and the function to obtain more practical and useful information for psychological education and related interventions.

Despite these limitations, the present study is the first to adapt the Japanese version of the BERQ. One of the strengths of this study is that we were able to extract data from throughout Japan, with equal distributions according to age, gender, and region. Furthermore, on the basis of our cultural comparison, since Withdrawal might be a maladaptive and negative strategy, examining the relations between Withdrawal and anxiety, depression, and well-being in more detail would be meaningful. Moreover, although the BERQ can be a useful and reliable tool for assessing coping strategies, further research is needed to confirm the potential of this scale.
