Impact of per diem payment on medical expenditure, service efficiency, and quality of care for patients with mental disorders: an interrupted time series study
Authors: Yuanping Pan, Zhiyong Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe high cost of hospitalization for people with mental disorders can push them into poverty, thereby exacerbating health inequities. While per diem payment (PDP) reforms aim to address this issue, there is limited evidence on the effects of PDP reform in China. This study evaluates the effects of PDP implementation on hospitalization burden, service efficiency, and care quality for patients with mental disorders.MethodsInterrupted time series analysis (ITSA) was used to evaluate the impact of PDP reform on hospitalization burden, service efficiency, and care quality of hospitalization in primary and secondary hospitals. Policy implementation in January 2022 served as the intervention time point. All data were obtained from the City W Medical Insurance Bureau, China.ResultsAfter the PDP reform, the average cost per hospitalization (primary hospitals: β3 = -0.044, P < 0.001; secondary hospitals: β3 = -0.021, P < 0.021), the average out-of-pocket (OOP) per hospitalization (primary hospitals: β3 = -0.034, P < 0.001; secondary hospitals: β3 = -0.042, P < 0.001) and the average basic medical insurance (BMI) reimbursement per hospitalization (primary hospitals: β3 = -0.045, P < 0.001; secondary hospitals: β3 = -0.020, P = 0.037) of people with mental health conditions in primary and secondary hospitals all showed a downward trend. Before the PDP reform, the average length of stay (ALOS) in secondary hospitals showed an upward trend (β1 = 1.384, P = 0.002). The 30-day all-cause admission rate of patients with mental disorders in primary and secondary hospitals showed a downward trend after the reform (primary hospitals: β3 = -0.029, P = 0.009; secondary hospitals: β3 = -0.018, P = 0.008).ConclusionThe PDP reform effectively reduced the financial burdens and improved equitable healthcare access for patients with mental disorders. While enhancing service efficiency and resource allocation, policymakers should monitor potential unintended consequences including insurance fund underutilization and ensure sustained care quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13419-9.

Background

Mental disorders pose a major global public health challenge, contributing to substantial disability, premature mortality, and significant socioeconomic burdens [1, 2]. In Many OECD countries, inpatient care accounts for 28–69% of total mental health expenditures [3]. One study has found that rates of mental disorders are on the rise in countries around the world and are expected to cost the global economy $16 trillion by 2030 [4]. For example, in the United States, the hospitalization rate for primary mental disorders among people aged 18 to 64 is 175 per 100,000 people [5]. China also faces a substantial mental health burden, with a lifetime prevalence of 16.57% and a 12-month prevalence of 9.32%, excluding Alzheimer’s disease [6]. The cumulative three-year cost per psychiatric patient exceeded CNY 70,000, with long-term hospitalizations surpassing CNY 550,000 in China [7].

Mental disorders are characterized by high recurrence rates and treatment difficulties [8]. Thus, patients with mental disorders usually have longer average length of stay (ALOS) and higher hospitalization costs [9]. In China, one study Reported that the ALOS in psychiatric hospitals ranged from 50 to 60 days [10]. Prolonged hospital stay is significantly associated with high hospital costs [11]. How to control hospitalization costs and improve service efficiency and quality is the focus of global mental health services [12]. Medical insurance payment reform is considered as one of the important means to solve these problems [13, 14]. Notable examples include the Inpatient Psychiatric Facility Prospective Payment System (IPFPPS) in the United States and the NHS Payment Scheme (NHSPS) in the United Kingdom [15].

To alleviate the financial burden on patients with mental disorders, China has established a basic medical insurance (BMI) system. This system is primarily based on fee-for-service (FFS) payments to promote equitable access to healthcare. In China, doctors’ performance is closely related to the number of services provided [16]. Thus, the FFS model may incentivize overprovision of services, contributing to escalating healthcare costs and inefficient resource utilization [17]. Faced with the increasing hospitalization costs of inpatients with mental disorders, there is an urgent need to reform FFS payments. Similar to other countries in the world, China has begun to reform the BMI payment system based on FFS payment. Per diem payment (PDP) has emerged as a preferred approach for patients requiring long-term hospitalization, providing a fixed daily reimbursement based on hospital level and length of stay [18].

Previous research has primarily examined the effects of diagnosis-related group (DRG) and diagnosis-intervention packet (DIP) reforms on hospitalization costs and the quality of medical services [19–21]. In contrast, limited attention has been given to the impact of PDP reform on hospitalization costs and care quality for patients with mental disorders. The payment method design of DRG/DIP has different design logic from that of PDP. Whether PDP can reduce patients’ financial burden and promote a more equitable allocation of healthcare resources remains uncertain [22, 23]. There are inconsistent conclusions on the effects of PDP reform on the treatment costs, service efficiency, and quality of mental health patients. Some suggest that PDP may prolong the ALOS and increase hospitalization costs for psychiatric patients [24]. While others report that it can reduce both ALOS and medical expenses [25]. In addition, another study found no significant effect on ALOS but reported increased medical costs following PDP implementation [26]. PDP as an innovative BMI payment method for psychiatric inpatient care deserves further study. Clarifying the impact of PDP on hospitalization costs, service efficiency, and quality of care is critical to optimizing its application and guiding future policy development.

This study employed interrupted time series analysis (ITSA) to evaluate the impact of the PDP reform. The analysis focused on hospitalization costs, care quality, and service efficiency for inpatients with mental disorders. The study was conducted in primary and secondary hospitals in City W. Our findings further provide important insights for optimizing PDP reform implementation. These insights are especially relevant for regions facing similar challenges.

Materials and methods

Study setting and data sources

City W is located in an economically underdeveloped region in central China. By the end of December 2023, the coverage rate of BMI had reached 99.56%. Chinese hospitals are divided into tertiary, secondary and primary levels based on factors such as hospital size, staffing and equipment [27]. Tertiary hospitals manage complex and severe diseases, secondary hospitals treat common diseases, and primary hospitals provide basic medical and public health services to community residents [28]. In 2023, City W had a total of 15 mental health hospitals: including one tertiary hospital, three secondary hospitals, and eleven primary hospitals. Until January 2022, all hospitalization expenses for patients with mental disorders in City W were reimbursed by the BMI fund under a FFS model. Although the FFS model is relatively easy to administer, it often leads to excessive use of medical resources [29]. In order to control the rapidly rising medical expenses of hospitalized psychiatric patients and avoid wasting medical resources, City W decided to replace FFS with PDP. Because the Majority of psychiatric patients in City W receive care at secondary and primary hospitals, the city implemented a PDP reform in these institutions starting in January 2022.

PDP is a prospective payment method that sets daily reimbursement rates based on the actual average length of stay (ALOS) and the hospital’s level [30]. Separate PDP standards were developed for primary and secondary hospitals, based on the average actual hospitalization costs for patients with mental disorders over the preceding three years. The Medical Insurance Bureau of City W adjusts these payment standards annually in accordance with the city’s social and economic development. For example, the daily reimbursement rates for primary hospitals are as follows: CNY 130 per day for hospital stays of 1 to 30 days; CNY 115 per day for stays of 31 to 60 days; CNY 100 per day for stays exceeding 60 days.

The study analyzed de-identified claims data from the City W Medical Insurance Bureau, encompassing 25,327 psychiatric hospitalization episodes between January 2020 and June 2023. The dataset includes sociodemographic characteristics, hospitalization costs, and insurance reimbursements for patients with mental disorders. To ensure the accuracy of the results, we removed records that were unreasonable, missing, and unclear for key variables. A total of 25,047 records were included in the final analysis, including 21,440 records in primary hospital and 3,607 records in secondary hospital (Fig. 1).

Outcome measures

Based on previous studies, we used the following indicators to assess the financial burden of psychiatric hospitalization: average cost per hospitalization, average out-of-pocket (OOP) per hospitalization, and average BMI reimbursement per hospitalization [31–33]. To address the skewness of the medical expense data and improve model stability, the medical expense data were log-transformed before inclusion in the model [34].

Statistical model and analysis

We used the ITSA to estimate the impact of PDP reform on financial burden, service efficiency, and care quality for patients with mental disorders. In the ITSA, we used a segmented ordinary least squares (OLS) regression model to assess whether the PDP reform reduced the financial burden of hospitalization and improved the efficiency and quality of care, respectively. In this study, Yt represents the value of the outcome variable at time point t. β0 denotes the baseline level of the outcome indicator at t = 0. β1 estimates the time trend of the outcome variable prior to the PDP reform. β2 represents the immediate change in the outcome variable at intervention point. β3 captures the change in the slope after the PDP reform. The variable T corresponds to the time-series value t over the study period, ranging from 1 ~ 42, and is measured once a month. The value of Xt is 0 prior to the implementation of the PDP reform. It takes the value of 1 starting in January 2022, the month the reform was introduced. TXt denotes the interaction term, which is 0 before the reform and T after the reform.εt denotes the error term.

Durbin-Watson test was used for autocorrelation analysis. If there was autocorrelation, the Newey-West method was used to deal with it [35]. This study also conducted a seasonal verification and used seasonal dummy variables to handle the situation if seasonality existed [36]. Additionally, the consumer price index (CPI) for 2020 was used as the base year, and all healthcare cost data in the study were CPI discounted. Statistical analysis was performed using STATA 16.0 software. Differences were considered statistically significant at a two-sided P < 0.05.

Results

Descriptive analysis

Table 1 presents a comparison of economic burden, service efficiency, and quality of care for inpatients with mental disorders before and after the implementation of the PDP reform. After the reform, primary hospitals exhibited significant reductions in average cost per hospitalization (from CNY 8,266.78 to CNY 4,675.07; P < 0.001), average OOP per hospitalization (from CNY 614.00 to CNY 301.87; P < 0.001), average BMI reimbursement per hospitalization (from CNY 6,700.92 to CNY 3,789.54; P < 0.001), and ALOS (from 67.01 to 43.99 days; P < 0.01).

Secondary hospitals also showed decreases in average cost per hospitalization (from CNY 7,631.20 to CNY 6,063.24; P = 0.003), average OOP per hospitalization (from CNY 925.19 to CNY 639.06; P < 0.001), average BMI reimbursement per hospitalization (from CNY 5,218.68 to CNY 4,146.42; P = 0.008 ), and ALOS (from 70.03 to 51.96 days; P = 0.028). In addition, secondary hospitals experienced a significant decrease in the 30-day all-cause readmission rate (29.19–21.16%; P = 0.002).

ITSA outcomes of PDP reform in primary hospitals

Table 2; Fig. 2 present the ITSA results evaluating the impact of the PDP reform on inpatient outcomes for patients with mental disorders in primary hospitals. Compared with the pre-reform period, the average cost per hospitalization decreased significantly by about 4.4% per month after the reform (β3 = −0.044, P < 0.001). The average OOP per hospitalization decreased immediately by 33.5% in the month of the reform (β2 = −0.335, P < 0.001), and the monthly trend decreased significantly by 3.4% (β3 = −0.034, P < 0.001) after the reform. Compared with before the reform, the monthly trend of average BMI reimbursement per hospitalization decreased by 4.5% (β3 = −0.045, P < 0.001) after the reform. The 30-day all-cause readmission rate showed an upward trend with slope of 0.017% before the reform (β1 = 0.017, P = 0.044), but the monthly trend after the reform decreased by 0.029% (β3 = −0.029, P = 0.009). No statistically significant changes in average length of stay (ALOS) were observed before or after the reform.

ITSA outcomes of PDP reform in secondary hospitals

Table 3; Fig. 3 present the ITSA results evaluating the impact of the PDP reform on inpatient outcomes for patients with mental disorders in secondary hospitals. Prior to the reform, the average cost per hospitalization showed an upward monthly trend of 1.5% (β1 = 0.015, P = 0.003). In the month the reform was implemented, the average cost dropped sharply by 36.1% (β₂ = −0.361, P < 0.001), and the monthly trend decreased significantly by 2.1% after the reform (β3 = −0.021, P < 0.021). For average OOP per hospitalization, the monthly trend decreased significantly by 4.2% after the reform (β3 = −0.042, P < 0.001). The average BMI reimbursement per hospitalization showed an increasing trend before the reform, with an upward slope of 1.7% per month (β1 = 0.017, P = 0.002), and fell by 41.2% in the month of the reform (β2 = −0.412, P < 0.001), and the monthly trend decreased by 2% (β3 = −0.020, P = 0.037). The ALOS showed an upward trend before the reform, rising by 1.384 days per month (β1 = 1.384, P = 0.002). Regarding quality outcomes, the 30-day all-cause readmission rate increased with a monthly slope of 0.018% prior to the reform (β1 = 0.018, P < 0.001), but the monthly trend decreased significantly by 0.018% (β₃ = −0.018, P < 0.05) after the reform.

Sensitivity checks

To improve the robustness of our findings, we conducted placebo tests using incorrect intervention timings. Drawing on previous studies, we selected the third month (March 2022) and the sixth month (June 2022) after the actual implementation of the PDP reform as placebo points to test for the robustness [23, 31].

As shown in Tables S1–S2 of the Additional file, in primary hospitals, the trend changes in most outcome variables after PDP implementation were smaller than those observed after the actual policy implementation. In secondary hospitals, the PDP reform was not significantly associated with most outcome variables when using the placebo intervention dates. These findings suggest that the actual implementation time of the PDP policy (Jan 2022) has a more significant impact on the results. This result also verifies the stability of the results of this study.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the effects of the PDP reform on the economic burden of hospitalization, service efficiency, and care quality among patients with mental disorders. Our findings demonstrate a sustained downward in economic burdens related to hospitalizations and BMI fund expenditures in both primary and secondary hospitals after PDP implementation. In addition, significant reductions in 30-day all-cause readmission rates were observed across both hospital tiers after the reform.

Prior to the PDP reform, the average cost per hospitalization exhibited an upward trend in both primary and secondary hospitals. Post-reform, these costs began to decrease, aligning with findings from previous studies [37]. The PDP reform aimed to control overall treatment costs by setting a fixed daily reimbursement rate for psychiatric inpatients. This change responded to concerns that the former FFS model incentivized overutilization and escalating medical costs [38]. Previous research has indicated that secondary hospitals typically provide more advanced and diverse treatments compared to primary hospitals [39, 40]. This may help explain the continued rise in hospitalization costs in secondary hospitals prior to the PDP reform.

The mechanisms underlying cost reductions after PDP reform appear to be multifaceted. On one hand, hospitals may have improved efficiency by streamlining diagnostic procedures, optimizing medication regimens, and enhancing discharge planning. On the other hand, there is a risk that providers curtailed necessary services or reduced treatment intensity to a fixed daily reimbursement limit. Studies have shown that hospitals using prospective payment systems often adjust clinical behavior to the detriment of medical quality [41]. Therefore, while the PDP may have reduced overtreatment and resource waste, it also carries potential risks [42]. It is essential to monitor for unintended consequences such as under-treatment, delayed interventions, or premature discharges. Moreover, secondary hospitals would receive a higher BMI reimbursement amount under the PDP reform than primary hospitals. Patients in secondary hospitals use more health care resources than those in primary hospitals. Therefore, policymakers should consider that PDP reform may lead to unfairness in the utilization of medical services among patients visiting secondary and primary hospitals.

After the PDP reform, the average OOP costs per hospitalization for patients with mental disorders in both primary and secondary hospitals showed a downward trend. This reduction in economic burden significantly alleviates economic pressure on patients, thereby supporting equitable access to medical resources and their continued treatment. Previous studies have identified economic burden as a key risk factor for treatment discontinuation among patients with mental disorders [43, 44]. Thus, PDP reform may help patients with mental disorders improve treatment adherence in this population [45]. However, existing studies have also found that the benefits of PDP reforms for patients are limited [46]. Policymakers should take a more cautious approach to this.

In China, the BMI system plays a critical role in promoting equitable access to healthcare, particularly for low-income populations. In our study, we observed an upward trend in average BMI reimbursement in secondary hospitals prior to the reform, which may be attributed to the FFS model’s tendency to overuse of services [47]. After the PDP reform, the average BMI reimbursement per hospitalization showed a downward trend, suggesting reduced waste in the use of medical resources. By setting fixed daily reimbursement rates, the PDP model aimed to curb such irrational utilization [48]. Nonetheless, the continued decline in BMI reimbursements post-reform raises concerns about potential under-treatment of patients with mental disorders. This highlights the need for ongoing monitoring and evaluation. Future research should examine clinical outcomes and patient-reported experiences to assess the real-world impact of these cost reductions on care quality and patient well-being.

Our study found an increasing trend in the ALOS for patients with mental disorders in secondary hospitals prior to the PDP reform, followed by a decreasing trend after the reform, although the post-reform decrease was not statistically significant. The increase in ALOS before PDP implementation likely reflects the incentives inherent in the FFS model, which encouraged prolonged hospitalizations to maximize revenue [49]. The insignificant decline in ALOS after the introduction of PDP may indicate that the cost control mechanisms in the reform are insufficient to completely offset the previous incentives. Since PDP reimbursement is based on historical average costs, hospitals may still retain some flexibility to extend hospital stays without financial penalty. Liu et al. found that PDP reform did not significantly affect ALOS in primary hospitals [16]. However, Jian et al. found that PDP may prolong the length of stay in a tertiary psychiatric hospital [26]. This underscores the importance of analyses tailored to different levels of hospitals and different types of psychosis.

The 30-day all-cause readmission rate is a widely used indicator for assessing the quality of inpatient care [50]. In our study, both primary and secondary hospitals exhibited an increasing trend in 30-day readmission rates prior to the PDP reform. Following the implementation of the reform, this trend reversed. This improvement may be partly attributed to the design of the PDP system, which incorporates explicit disincentives for frequent hospital readmissions. For instance, if a patient is admitted to the same facility three or more times within a calendar year, the reimbursement rate is reduced. These policy measures discourage unnecessary readmissions and promote continuity of care. However, while lower readmission rates may reflect improved care coordination, they may also signal increased barriers to necessary re-hospitalization due to cost containment pressures. Thus, policymakers should remain cautious and ensure that reductions in readmissions are not achieved at the expense of appropriate care.

The study also has the following limitations. First, the analysis was based on aggregated monthly data, which limited our ability to assess the differential impact of PDP reform on the financial burden and treatment outcomes across specific types of mental disorders. Second, the data were drawn from a single city, which limits the generalizability of our findings. Third, although Many studies have used ALOS and 30-day all-cause readmission rate as alternative indicators of medical service efficiency and quality. But it may be necessary to find more appropriate alternative indicators, such as bed occupancy rate and patient satisfaction. Finally, due to data limitations, some potentially important covariates were not included in the analysis. Future research should aim to incorporate a broader range of variables to strengthen and deepen the understanding of PDP reform impacts.

Conclusion

The implementation of the PDP reform for hospitalized patients with mental disorders in both primary and secondary hospitals has significantly reduced the financial burden on patients and improved the efficiency and quality of care. However, the problem of under-treatment due to over-restrictions on the daily cost of treatment for people with mental disorders still needs to be addressed.
