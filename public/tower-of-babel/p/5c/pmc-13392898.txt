Hydrates-Based Separation of CH4/N2 Gas Using Copper Foam and Chemical Promoters
Authors: Lanyun Wang, Zecheng Lv, Shifang Mu, Yongliang Xu, Xinwei Mi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydrate-based gas separation (HBGS) offers a safe and promising route for capturing low-concentration coalbed methane (CH4). Herein, the formation behavior of CH4 hydrates promoted by 1,3-dioxolane (1,3-DIOX) and porous copper foam (CF) was systematically investigated. Experimental results demonstrated that the combined use of CF (60 PPI) and 5.56 mol % 1,3-DIOX under a coupled spraying-stirring mode markedly accelerates hydrate formation, yielding a maximum gas storage capacity of 0.1733 mol CH4 per mole H2O at 3.0 MPa, which is 10.1 times higher than that of the static pure water system. At 4.0 MPa, the maximum capacity is 0.1244 mol CH4/mol H2O, which is 3.7 times higher than that of the static pure water system. Furthermore, due to the higher thermodynamic driving force, the induction time for the 10% CH4 system is shorter than that of the 5% system. Specifically, the induction time with 0.3 wt % l-leucine was 21.31%–40.38% shorter than that of the combined CF and 1,3-DIOX system. After single-stage separation, the CH4 content was effectively enriched from 5% to 8.0%–10.25% and from 10% to 17.9%–21.7%. Compared to l-leucine, the system containing sodium lignosulfonate demonstrated a higher CH4 concentration in the hydrate phase, indicating superior selectivity. The CH4 recovery rates for the feeding gas systems with 10% and 5% CH4 were approximately 40% and 50%, respectively. Comparing to the reported results in smaller reactors of 58–1000 mL, the comparable CH4 recovery here is realized in a much larger scale reactor of 3927 mL, which is really a great improvement. These findings confirm that the proposed CF/1,3-DIOX system is an effective strategy for the enrichment of low-concentration CBM, providing a viable technical route for multistage industrial separation processes.

Introduction

Coalbed methane (CBM) is an unconventional natural gas that is primarily composed of methane (CH4). It is stored within coal seams predominantly by adsorbing on the surfaces of coal matrix particles, while a lower fraction exists as free gases in coal pores or is dissolved in the water of the coal seam. , The direct emission of coalbed gas with low-concentration CH4 mines significantly exacerbates the greenhouse effect. In China, CH4 recovery and utilization mainly target high-concentration coalbed gas with CH4 concentrations over 30%. In fact, coalbed gas is a kind of supplement of natural gas due to the CH4 with higher calorific value generating greater energy. However, the amount of nitrogen in the coalbed gas hinders the secondary utilization of CH4. Conventional purification and separation methods such as extraction, adsorption, and cryogenic distillation suffer from high energy consumption, equipment corrosion, consumable demand, and stringent low-temperature and high-pressure. , In contrast, the hydrate-based gas separation (HBGS) technology has been proposed as a promising approach to overcome these challenges due to its low operating cost, process simplicity, and high safety level under mild temperature and pressure conditions.

Previous studies demonstrate that the unique cage-like lattice structure of clathrate hydrates enables selective encapsulation of small gas molecules-such as CH4, CO2, and N2-based on their size and affinity. , This molecular selectivity underpins the significant potential of hydrate-based technologies in gas separation applications. Yan et al. reported that a CH4/N2 feeding gas containing 30 mol % CH4 could be enriched to 57 mol % after a single-stage separation and further increased to 80 mol % after a two-stage process. Zhao et al. provided experimental evidence confirming that adding 5.6 mol % tetrahydrofuran (THF) to the hydrate separation system can reduce the formation equilibrium pressure by approximately 40%, and simultaneously enriching CH4 in the hydrate phase to 55–65%. Various chemical promoters, including THF, cyclopentane (CP), and tetrabutylammonium bromide ([N4444]­Br), have been applied to reduce equilibrium pressure and improve separation performance. The structure and concentration of promoters, as well as operating pressure, have been identified as the key factors influencing the efficiency. For example, Li et al. reported that under a combined additive system of THF and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), with an optimal ratio of 1.0 mol % THF and 500 ppm of SDS, the formation pressure of CH4 hydrates could be significantly reduced. For two sets of simulated coalbed gas samples (CH4/N2 mixtures containing 20% and 50% CH4), the CH4 concentration by the hydrate-based separation approximately is increased by 20%. Similarly, using [N4444]Br in a semibatch process achieved a CH4 recovery of 25% and a separation factor of 9.9 under 3.9 MPa and 277.15 K14.

Amino acids have also been applied as environmentally friendly promoters. Zhang et al. demonstrated a significant synergistic enhancement effect when employing 5.56 mol % THF with 5000 ppm of tryptophan, effectively improving hydrate formation and CH4 enrichment performance in the CH4/N2 system. Experimental results indicate that when the feed gas contained 30.0 mol % CH4, the CH4 concentration within the hydrate phase could be enriched to 40.7–56.8 mol %, achieving a maximum CH4 recovery rate of 71.4%. It is also observed that reducing the gas-to-liquid ratio enhances CH4 enrichment, recovery, and the system separation coefficient, although this concurrently diminishes hydrate formation rates. In addition, other combinations, e.g., THF/SDS and graphite nanoparticles, or bioadditives such as tea polyphenols and catechin, further improved the CH4/N2 separation. Despite these advantages, the potential toxicity of current chemical promoters remains a critical challenge for the large-scale industrial application of HBGS, highlighting the need for sustainable and environmentally benign alternatives.

Despite the chemical promoters, various insoluble porous materials have been applied as promoters to enhance the hydrate formation kinetics, including activated carbon, , nanoparticles, − nanotubes, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), , and ceramic foam. These materials were reported to be able to promote the nucleation and growth by enlarging the gas–liquid interfacial area. For example, Li et al. reported that the combination of 500 ppm graphene oxide nanosheets (GONs) with CP enriched the CH4 concentration from 30% to 76% through a two-stage hydrate separation process. Metal foams have also shown a potential to enhance gas consumption during hydrate formation due to their high thermal conductivity and large surface area. Combining the 40 PPI aluminum foam with 0.03 wt % SDS at 273.15 K and 8.3 MPa accelerated the CH4 hydrate formation rate by nearly 9-fold compared to the system without aluminum foam. Similarly, in the CF (15 PPI) + 0.05 wt % SDS system, the CH4 consuming rates reached about 25.81 (cm3·cm–3·min–1), with a maximum storage capacity of 0.1395 mol/mol29. The hydrate conversion rates increased by 8.8%–35.6%, and maximum storage rates rose by 4.7%–40.4%, compared to pure SDS solutions at 6–8 MPa. Wang et al. further reported that CF outperformed aluminum foam and iron foams, with CH4 storage capacity reaching 0.15 mol/mol H2O combining with 0.3 wt % l-leucine, approximately 3–4 times greater than that in pure water. Smaller CF pore size is favorable for improving the heat transfer and thus promotes the CH4 hydrate formation. Nevertheless, research studies on the application of metal foams in gas separation remain limited.

Studies have confirmed that the introduction of thermodynamic promoters under relatively mild conditions can effectively improve CH4 selectivity and recovery efficiency. In particular, recent research has shown that advanced promoters can significantly enhance hydrate formation performance in low-concentration coalbed CH4 systems. Meanwhile, comprehensive reviews have highlighted the rapid development of hydrate-based technologies for coalbed CH4 recovery, emphasizing that the integration of thermodynamic promoters, kinetic additives, and optimized reactor configurations is essential to overcome limitations in formation kinetics and separation efficiency. Despite these advances, achieving rapid hydrate formation and efficient CH4/N2 separation under practical conditions remains a challenge.

Most studies primarily focus on single-factor enhancement strategies, such as thermodynamic promoters or structural materials, while the synergistic effects among multiple-promotion mechanisms are still not fully understood. This limitation is particularly critical for low-concentration coalbed CH4, where slow nucleation kinetics and low separation efficiency significantly hinder practical applications. Therefore, developing a synergistic strategy that simultaneously enhances thermodynamics, kinetics, and mass transfer is of great importance.

In this study, a spray-agitation coupling mode combined with CF and 1,3-dioxolane (1,3-DIOX) is proposed to promote hydrate formation and improve CH4/N2 separation performance. l-Leucine and sodium lignosulfonate are further introduced as kinetic additives to regulate interfacial behavior and enhance gas uptake, aiming to elucidate the synergistic effects among thermodynamic, structural, and interfacial promotion mechanisms. Notably, 1,3-DIOX has been recognized as a promising alternative to tetrahydrofuran (THF), exhibiting superior promotion performance under similar conditions, while its synergistic interaction with porous media remains insufficiently explored. On the basis of this concept, this work systematically investigates the combined effects of CF, 1,3-DIOX, and kinetic additives on CH4 hydrate formation and hydrate-based separation of low-concentration CH4/N2 mixtures. The influences of the porous structure and mechanical enhancement modes on hydrate formation kinetics and gas separation performance are comprehensively analyzed, providing new insights into the design of efficient hydrate-based gas separation processes.

Experimental Section

Materials

Two binary gas mixtures (CH4/N2) were supplied by Henan Yuanzheng Special Gas Co., Ltd., with CH4 (mixed with N2) volume fractions of 10 vol % and 5 vol %, respectively. The CF samples used in the experiments were supplied by JiangSu Yiminglong Metal Materials Co., Ltd. The specific macroscopic structures and pore densities are displayed in Figure . 1,3-DIOX with a purity of ≥99.5% was obtained from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.

Structure diagram of natural gas hydrate reactor (a) not filled with copper foam (CF), reactor filled with copper foam (b), and reactor (c) with copper foam of different pore densities.

Considering the harsher thermodynamic formation conditions required for hydrate formation of the coalbed gas compared to pure CH4 system, 5.56 mol % 1,3-DIOX was preferred as a thermodynamic promoter based on the previous research studies , to moderate the phase equilibrium condition and accelerate the hydrate formation. All solutions were prepared by mixing laboratory-grade deionized water with an electrical conductivity of ≤5 μS/cm.

For the hydrate-based gas separation, l-leucine , (purity ≥99%) and sodium lignosulfonate (purity ≥96%) were introduced as kinetic additives to enhance the gas uptake rate. Aqueous solutions were prepared by dissolving the respective additives in the deionized water at mass fractions of 0.3, 0.5, and 0.7 wt %.

The selection of pore densities of 15, 60, and 130 PPI represents low, medium, and high structural configurations of porous materials, considering the fact that pore density influences the specific surface area and pore structure, thereby affecting hydrate formation behavior. ,−

Apparatus

Figure illustrates the self-developed spraying apparatus for gas hydrate formation. On the basis of the cycling spraying, the gas–liquid mass transfer and gas hydrate generation can be promoted efficiently. The apparatus consists of a reactor with atomized spraying, a circulation-based cooling system, a gas reservoir, a gas compensation system, and a data monitoring system.

Schematic diagram and photograph of the experimental apparatus used for gas hydrate formation and gas separation.

The pressure in the reactor was precisely regulated via a pneumatic valve directly linked to an air compressor. When the gas consumption during gas hydrate formation causes a drop in pressure, the system automatically supplies fresh gas from the storage tank to maintain a constant experimental pressure.

A flow rate of 0–200 mL/min can be adjusted for the atomized spraying system, with the spray angle adjustable between 45° and 165°. The spraying system continuously circulates the liquid from the bottom of the reactor and reinjecting it through the top nozzle, rather than continuously adding new liquid. To further improve gas–liquid mixing, a dual-blade mechanical stirrer is employed with a variable rotation speed ranging from 0 to 1000 rpm. The temperature in the reactor is controlled using a circulating ethanol-based cooling system, ranging from 258.15 to 333.15 K. Before introducing the gas into the reactor, the gas storage tank is precooled in a cooling bath to ensure stable reaction conditions.

Experimental parameters, e.g. pressure, temperature, and time, were recorded by a real-time monitoring system. The integrated data acquisition system automatically recorded values at 2s intervals. The measurement uncertainties of temperature and pressure sensors are ±0.1 K and ±0.01 MPa, respectively, providing a high temporal resolution and reliable accuracy.

Procedures

Kinetics of CH4 Hydrate Formation

First, the reactor was rinsed with the deionized water. After being dried, a CF plate was circled along the inner wall of it, and 400 mL of 5.56 mol % 1,3-DIOX solution was then filled. To remove residual air, the reactor and pipelines were purged three times with specific experimental gas. Afterward, the temperature was gradually reduced from the room temperature to the set initial temperature 285.15 K at a rate of 0.2 K/min by the cooling system. The reactor pressure was then adjusted to a set initial pressure. Simultaneously, stirring at 400 rpm and liquid spraying at 200 mL/min were initiated. The temperature and pressure values were recorded with the real-time monitoring system. The process was regarded as being finished when the pressure kept constant for at least 30 min. To ensure reproducibility of the data, each experimental condition was repeated three times, and the optimal values were reported.

Hydrate-Based Gas Separation

In subsequent mixed-gas experiments, leucine or sodium lignosulfonate was employed as an additive, with the mixed gas injected into the reactor. Following hydrate formation, the unreacted gas was purged from the reactor. The system was then gradually heated to 293.15 K at a rate of 0.05 K/min to decompose the hydrate. The released gas was collected in gas sampling bags and analyzed by gas chromatography (GC) to determine the composition of the released gases. On the basis of this analysis, the concentrations of CH4 and N2, the separation factor, and the CH4 recovery rate were calculated.

Determination of the Kinetics of CH4 Hydrate Formation

Figure illustrates a typical temporal evolution of the pressure of the gas storage tank, pressure, and temperature of the reactor throughout the CH4 hydrate formation under a constant pressure, which is realized by a gas compensation system. During the initial stage, a marginal decline in the gas storage tank pressure is observed due to gas dissolution, indicating the induction period preceding the crystal nucleation. The induction time is defined as the time from the beginning of the experiment to the time when the pressure drop rate of the gas tank increases, the aeration rate of the reactor accelerates, and a large number of hydrates begins to form. This is followed by a precipitous drop in the storage tank pressure, accompanied by a simultaneous exothermic temperature spike, signifying the onset of the rapid growth stage. This phase is characterized by a significantly accelerated gas consumption rate (which is denoted by the frequent supplementation of gas from the storage tank) because of the rapid clathration of guest gas molecules. As gas hydrate growth proceeds and mass transfer resistance increases, the gas uptake gradually slows. Finally, when the pressure keeps unchanged for 30 min, the gas storage tank is no longer restarted to replenish gas into the hydrate reactor, implying that gas is not consumed any more and the formation of CH4 hydrates is finished.

Gas Consumption and Separation Parameters

Gas Consumption

The gas consumption (gas uptake) is determined by the temperature and pressure from t = 0 to any time t. Based on the actual gas equation of state, the gas consumption equation is calculated by eq . 1 ΔnH=ng,0−ng,t=VR[(PZT)0−(PZT)t] where n g, 0 and n g, t are the molar amount of gas (mole) in the gas storage tank at the moments 0 and t, respectively. V is the volume of gas in the gas storage tank. P and T are the pressure and temperature recorded by the pressure transmitter and temperature sensor, respectively. R is the gas constant, 8.3145 J/(mol·K). Z is the compression factor determined by the universalized second virial equation, and the value is determined by the combination of Amagat’s law and the universal compression factor, which is calculated by eq . 2 Z=1+B0PrTr+ωB1PrTr where ω is the eccentricity factor, 0.012. T r and P r are the specific temperature and pressure of the gas, respectively. B 0 and B 1 are expressed in terms of the Abbott equations, which are described using eq . 3 B0=0.083−0.422Tr1.6B1=0.139−0.172Tr4.2Pr=PPcTr=TTc where T c and P c represent the critical temperature and pressure of CH4, T c = 190.564 K and P c = 4.5992 MPa, respectively, and T and P represent the gas storage tank temperature and pressure, respectively.

Gas consumption (gas uptake) is normalized considering the amount of water used in the experiment and is calculated by eq . 4 Δn=ΔngasΔnH2O where Δn gas is the total gas consumption, representing the amount of gas that enters into the hydrate phase at the end of the experiment, mole. Δn H2O is the amount of water used in the reactor, mole.

CH4 Recovery

The CH4 recovery ratio was employed as a quantitative indicator to evaluate the efficiency of gas separation and purification, as defined in eq . A higher recovery ratio indicates an improved separation and purification performance. The ratio of CH4 in the hydrate phase to that in the feed gas is defined as the CH4 recovery ratio. 5 RCH4=nCH4HnCH4f=nHyCH4HnfyCH4f where the initial CH4 moles in the hydrate and feed gas are denoted by n H CH4 and n f CH4, respectively, whereas y H CH4 and y f CH4 refer to the final CH4 concentrations in these two phases.

Separation Factor

During the hydrate-based gas separation, the separation factor is a key parameter for evaluating the purification performance and is determined by eq . 6 S.F.=yCH4HyN2H×yN2lyCH4l=yCH4HyN2H×nf·yN20−nH·yHN2nf·yCH40−nH·yHCH4 where y H CH4 and y H N2 denote the final concentrations of CH4 and N2 in the hydrate phase, while y 1 CH4 and y 1 N2 represent the residual concentrations of CH4 and N2 in the gas phase, respectively.

Unexpectedly, increasing the pressure does not show enhancement for the gas-consuming rate, possibly due to the high pressure aggravating the blockage of the initially formed gas hydrate especially in the small pores. Therefore, the influence of three CFs on CH4 consumption does not show a certain linear relationship with the pore density.

According to Figure , the 60 PPI CF + 1,3-DIOX system has the best promotion effect on CH4 hydrate. In contrast, the gas consumption of CH4 hydrate in the 130 PPI CF + 1,3-DIOX system is relatively lower. This may be because 130 PPI CF increases the specific surface area but also provides smaller pore spaces. When some CH4 hydrate nuclei form, they will adhere to the surface of CF, blocking the CF pore channels and hindering the mass transfer of gas–liquid and the continuous formation of hydrate, which leads to less CH4 consumption.

According to the reported gas consumption of CH4 hydrate formed in the presence of the other metal foam system, e.g., AF + 0.03% SDS with 0.136 mol/mol­(8.3 MPa), 15PPICF + 0.05 wt % SDS with 0.139 mol/mol­(8.0 MPa), this work obtains the maximum CH4 consumption in the presence of CF + 1,3-DIOX of 0.1733 mol/mol, increased by 9.68%–36.46%. Meanwhile, the experiments here were conducted under lower pressures, not only reduced energy consumption but also weakened the physical explosion of high-pressure reactor, and thus ensured the safety of CH4 hydrate storage and transportation.

Figure a presents the induction times for 5% CH4 + 95% N2 hydrate formation in the presence of CF, 1,3-DIOX, leucine, and sodium lignosulfonate under 4.0 MPa. In the CF + 1,3-DIOX system, the induction time was 423.53 min. When leucine was introduced (i.e., CF + 1,3-DIOX + leucine), the induction time ranged from 437.43 to 464.17 min, representing an increase of 3.28%–9.60% compared with the system without leucine. This indicates that leucine does not reduce the nucleation time for the 5% CH4 + 95% N2 hydrate. The addition of sodium lignosulfonate further prolonged the induction period. This delay in nucleation in the low-concentration CH4 system may be attributed to the “salting-out” effect or steric hindrance caused by the large additive molecules at the gas–liquid interface, especially with a lower CH4 partial pressure.

Induction times of 5%CH4 + 95% N2 and 10%CH4 + 90% N2 hydrate formation in different promotion systems. (a) 5% CH4 + 95% N2 system. (b) 10% CH4 + 90% N2 system.

Figure b shows the induction times for 10% CH4 + 90% N2 hydrate formation in the CF + 1,3-DIOX + leucine or sodium lignosulfonate systems under 4.0 MPa. Unlike the 5% system, upon increasing the CH4 concentration in the feeding gas to 10 vol %, systems containing leucine exhibit shorter induction times than those without leucine. The shortest induction time of 141.67 min occurs in the CF + 1,3-DIOX +0.3 wt % leucine system, corresponding to a reduction of 21.31%–40.38% compared with the system without leucine. In contrast, higher concentrations of sodium lignosulfonate delay nucleation. However, although sodium lignosulfonate still prolongs the induction period, the induction times are considerably shorter than those observed for the 5% CH4 + 95% N2 hydrate. This may be explained by the fact that CH4 is more favorable for creating gas hydrate than N2. With a higher partial pressure of CH4 in the 10% mixture, the stronger thermodynamic driving force leads to preferential formation under milder conditions.

Figure presents the time-dependent gas consumption of 5% CH4 + 95% N2 hydrates formed in the CF-assisted systems with various chemical promoters under 4.0 MPa. A typical gas consumption curve demonstrates four stages, i.e., a slow increase due to gas dissolution, a stagnation period associated with hydrate nucleation, a sharp rise during hydrate growth, and a final stabilization indicating the completion of hydrate formation. The slope of the curve reflects the rate of gas consumption.

Real-time CH4 gas consumption curves for 5% CH4 + 95% N2 hydrate formation under different promotion systems.

The gas consumption rate (inferred from the slopes during the growth stage) for the 5% CH4 + 95% N2 mixture is only slightly affected by leucine, which is particularly evident at higher concentrations. However, the fastest rate is observed in the CF + 1,3-DIOX system without additional kinetic promoters. In contrast, sodium lignosulfonate-containing systems exhibit similar slopes, indicating a negligible effect on the gas consumption rate. Overall, based on the slope analysis of real-time gas consumption, both leucine and sodium lignosulfonate make a limited impact on accelerating gas consumption for the 5% CH4 + 95% N2 feeding gas. This indicates that under conditions of low CH4 concentration, the rate at which CH4 dissolves from the gas phase into the liquid phase and diffuses to the growth interface becomes the rate-limiting step. Consequently, additives that merely improve nucleation at the interface or crystal surface kinetics are unlikely to enhance the overall gas consumption rate. ,

Figure displays the real-time gas consumption during the formation of hydrates from a 10% CH4 + 90% N2 gas mixture at 4.0 MPa in systems comprising CF and different chemical promoters (1,3-DIOX, leucine, or sodium lignosulfonate). A comparison with Figure reveals that increasing the CH4 concentration in the feeding gas from 5% to 10% significantly changes the gas consumption performance. In CF + 1,3-DIOX + leucine systems, a significant enhancement in the consumption rate is achieved with an increasing leucine concentration relative to the system without leucine. Compared to leucine, 0.5 and 0.7 wt % sodium lignosulfonate systems show a second rise in the gas consumption curves, which may be due to its large molecular structure hindering gas transfer and thus extending the consumption time.

In general, on the basis of CF and 1,3-DIOX, adding leucine and sodium lignosulfonate can further accelerate the hydrate formation rate, particularly at higher concentrations. Compared with the 5% CH4 + 95% N2 systems, the 10% CH4 + 90% N2 systems exhibit a markedly shorter induction period and faster gas consumption, indicating an earlier transition into the rapid growth stage. This phenomenon is likely attributable to the higher partial pressure of CH4, which increases its solubility concentration in the aqueous phase and elevates the chemical potential associated with occupying sites within the hydrate phase. This enhances the tendency for CH4 to enter cage sites, thereby facilitating nucleation and early stage growth. ,

Furthermore, the experiments were conducted in a relatively larger reactor, 3927 mL, compared to typical laboratory-scale systems­(58–1000 mL). It is known that larger reactor may have weaker heat and mass transfer, leading to reduced formation and separation efficiency. Nevertheless, with the favors of stirring and spraying, the recovery and separation performance is close to most of those in much smaller reactors. It is a great improvement in the hydrate-based gas separation, especially considering the future process designing. This provides more realistic insights into hydrate formation behavior under conditions closer to industrial applications. Therefore, the proposed system demonstrates a satisfactory performance in terms of CH4 recovery, operating conditions, and scalability, highlighting its potential for practical CH4 recovery from low-concentration gas mixtures, and bridging the gap between laboratory research and industrial implementation.

The separation factor ranges for 10% CH4 + 90% N2 gas and 5% CH4 + 95% N2 gas are 2.04–2.58 and 1.68–2.20, respectively. The higher CH4 concentration in the hydrate phase usually results in higher separation factors.

From the separation performance of hydrate-based experiments, it is reasonable to conclude that this technology is greatly feasible for concentrating CH4 from coal-bed gas with a very low CH4 composition, promisingly increasing from 5% to 20% by two-cycle separation by continuous or intermittent operation.

The separation mechanism illustrated in Figure can be interpreted based on the synergistic effects of thermodynamic, structural, and interfacial enhancement mechanisms contributed by different additives. In this system, 1,3-DIOX acts as a thermodynamic promoter by directly participating in hydrate cage formation, thereby shifting the hydrate equilibrium to milder conditions and significantly shortening the induction time. In addition, its cyclic ether structure stabilizes the hydrate lattice, facilitating rapid nucleation and sustained crystal growth. ,,, In contrast, CF primarily promotes hydrate formation through physical effects. Its three-dimensional interconnected porous structure provides abundant heterogeneous nucleation sites and significantly enhances heat transfer and gas–liquid contact efficiency. These features effectively accelerate hydrate growth kinetics and increase gas consumption. ,,, Beyond these two primary components, l-leucine and sodium lignosulfonate further regulate hydrate formation through interfacial mechanisms. l-leucine, as an amino acid promoter, enhances hydrate nucleation by inducing hydrophobic interactions at the gas–liquid interface. Its hydrophobic side chains promote local gas enrichment near the interface and facilitate the formation of a semiordered interfacial structure, thereby reducing the nucleation energy barrier. , Sodium lignosulfonate, acting as a surfactant-like compound, reduces gas–liquid interfacial tension and improves dispersion, which increases the effective contact area between gas and liquid phases. Moreover, it can form micelle-like aggregates that serve as additional heterogeneous nucleation centers, further accelerating hydrate formation. ,,,, Overall, while 1,3-DIOX reduces the thermodynamic barrier for hydrate formation, CF enhances mass and heat transfer as well as nucleation kinetics. Meanwhile, l-leucine and sodium lignosulfonate regulate interfacial behavior and promote gas enrichment and dispersion. The coupling of these effects results in a pronounced synergistic enhancement that is significantly greater than that achieved by any single promoter alone.

Although this study achieved encouraging results, several limitations should be acknowledged. The experiments were conducted at the laboratory scale, and the performance under large-scale or continuous operation conditions requires further investigation. In addition, the long-term stability and recyclability of the additives and porous materials have not yet been evaluated. Future research should focus on the scale-up of the system, optimization of the combination of thermodynamic and kinetic promoters, and exploration of their performance under practical operating conditions. Moreover, integrating hydrate-based separation technology with other gas separation techniques may provide a more efficient strategy for the utilization of low-concentration coalbed CH4.

In addition, although the synergistic application of porous media, stirring, spraying, and composite promoters significantly enhances hydrate formation kinetics and CH4 recovery efficiency, this combined system simultaneously increases the equipment complexity and potential operating costs. Therefore, future research should focus on further optimizing process parameters, including the regulation of promoter component flow rates, pressure, temperature, and water input, as well as quantifying the energy consumption across each pipeline unit in order to reduce the cost of industrial implementation of this method.

Conclusion

Compared with previous studies that mainly adopted single or dual promotion strategies, this study proposes a synergistic system integrating thermodynamic, structural, and kinetic enhancement measures for hydrate-based CH4/N2 separations. Specifically, 1,3-DIOX is introduced as a thermodynamic promoter, while CF provides a porous structure to enhance the heat and mass transfer. Moreover, l-leucine and sodium lignosulfonate are usually used as kinetic additives to regulate the gas–liquid interface and promote hydrate nucleation. The combination of these components enables simultaneous improvements in the nucleation and mass transfer efficiency. Such multimechanism synergistic strategies have rarely been systematically investigated for the more challenging yet practically valuable gas low-concentration of CH4.

The results show that higher concentration of CH4 in the feeding gas results in shorter induction times, faster gas consuming rates, and higher final gas consumption. Adding higher concentrations of leucine or sodium lignosulfonate leads to longer induction period but larger CH4 + N2 gas capacity. However, the better separation performance presents in the systems with a low concentration of leucine or sodium lignosulfonate. In the presence of 60PPI CF, 1,3-DIOX, and low concentration (0.3 wt %) of leucine or sodium lignosulfonate, the concentration of CH4 in the hydrate almost doubled, approaching 21.71% and 10.25%, respectively, for the separation of feeding gas of 10% CH4 + 90% N2 and 5% CH4 + 95% N2. The recovery of CH4 for the 10% CH4 + 90% N2 gas and 5% CH4 + 95% N2 mixed gas ranges 34.26%–45.41% and 31.76–50.40%, respectively. Comparing to the reported results in smaller reactors of 58–1000 mL, the comparable CH4 recovery here is realized in a much larger scale reactor of 3927 mL, which is really a great improvement.

Accordingly, it is believable that this technology is greatly feasible to concentrate CH4 from coalbed gas with a very low CH4 composition, prospectively increasing from 5% to nearly 20% by two-stage separations based on gas hydrate.
