“Cup-in-Cup Technique” in the Management of Acetabular Defects in Hip Arthroplasty: Early to Midterm Results
Authors: Santhosh Kumar, Vikas Kulshrestha, Prashant Padhi, Barun Datta, Biraj Gogoi, Munish Sood
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroudComplex acetabular defects (Paprosky II and III) present a challenge in total hip arthroplasty (THA). We used a “cup-in-cup” (CiC) construct wherein a trabecular metal cup (TMC) was press-fit and a dual mobility cup (DMC) was cemented within it. We added a porous acetabular augment (cup-in-cup with augment; CiCA) or a cage (cup-cage-cup; CCC) when required.MethodsWe reviewed patients who underwent complex acetabular reconstruction at our center between January 2012 to December 2021, with 2 to 11 years of follow-up. We assessed radiological restoration of the center of rotation using digital templating software (TraumaCad), functional outcome using the Harris Hip Score (HHS) and Forgotten Joint Score, and evaluated survival of the construct over 2–11 years by examining reoperation rates for any reason.ResultsAmong 1,532 THAs performed in 1,448 patients (578 revisions), a TMC was used for acetabular reconstruction in 82 patients: 55 CiC, 15 CiCA, and 12 CCC were done. Eleven hips had Paprosky type IIC, 52 had IIIA, and 19 had IIIB acetabular defects. The mean cup size used was 64.10 ± 4.68 mm, the mean change in vertical and medial offset from the opposite side was 5.27 ± 4.68 mm and –1.21 ± 5.33 mm, respectively. The HHS improved from 49.01 ± 7.10 to 82.45 ± 6.66 at a mean follow-up of 54.7 months. Three-year survival was 96%, and 86% of the constructs survived at the latest follow-up.ConclusionsThe TMC construct is a good option for Paprosky type II and III acetabular defects. Cementing a DMC restored optimal hip biomechanics. Our study provides robust data supporting its selection as the standard of care for addressing complex acetabular defects of all causes.Keywords: Acetabular defects, Trabecular metal cup, Cemented dual mobility cup, Mid-term outcome

The last 3 decades have seen increasing use of total hip arthroplasty (THA) in young patients. This has increased the need for complex acetabular reconstructions as a part of revision THA (rTHA).1) The burden of rTHA is likely to rise to 42% by 2040 and 101% by 2060.2) On the acetabular side, large expansile bony defects resulting from septic or aseptic osteolysis pose significant difficulties in restoring biomechanics and native anatomy.2,3,4) It requires surgeons to innovate, improvise, and use various implants and constructs to achieve optimum patient outcomes. The challenge remains to achieve primary implant stability while allowing osteointegration.

Prevalent strategies to tackle Paprosky type II and III defects are the use of acetabular porous augments, structural bone grafting, impaction bone grafting, and cage constructs.3,4) Each technique has its limitations, trabecular metal cups (TMCs) were introduced to address these complex defects 2 decades ago.5) We perform complex acetabular reconstructions with large TMC oriented to maximize host bone contact, along with screws placed in a multidirectional manner to cover all sectors. Inside the TMC, we cement a dual mobility cup (DMC) to achieve the desired orientation and stability (cup-in-cup; CiC construct). In some cases, with eccentric large defects, we add an augment (cup-in-cup with augment; CiCA construct), and in cases with a non-supportive acetabular floor, we add a cage inside the TMC before cementing a DMC inside it (cup-cage-cup; CCC construct). This also allows lateralization and distalization of the migrated hip center. Failure to restore the center of rotation (COR) of the hip and offset is implicated in instability, impingement, leg length inequality, abductor weakness, and dislocation.4) Our retrospective review describes our results of TMC constructs over 2–11 years of follow-up.

METHODS

After obtaining approval from the Institutional Review Board of Army Hospital Research and Referral Hospital (IRB No. AHRR/JRC23/2023), we retrospectively reviewed all THA cases performed at our center from January 2012 to December 2021, where a TMC construct (CiC/CiCA/CCC) was used. Informed consent was obtained from all patients. Follow-up ranged from 2 to 11 years. We documented patient demographics and disease profiles of the patients including the cause of hip arthritis and the severity and type of acetabular defect as described by Paprosky. Details of the method of the acetabular reconstruction used were recorded, along with postoperative restoration of the hip center using digital templating software (TraumaCad, Brainlab Inc.) (Fig. 1). Patient satisfaction was assessed using the Harris Hip Score (HHS) and Forgotten Joint Score at 2 years of follow-up. Survivability of the construct at early to midterm follow-up (2–11 years) was recorded. Among a total of 1,532 THAs performed in 1,448 patients during the study period, we identified 82 patients receiving TMC constructs as suitable candidates for our study (Fig. 2). Patients were excluded if they were unwilling to participate, had Paprosky acetabular defects lesser than type IIB, or received only the TMC with compatible polyethylene liners, other types of revision cups, constrained liners, or cages with DMCs in isolation. Eighty-two patients received a TMC as part of a CiC, CiCA, or CCC construct (Fig. 3). There were 53 men and 29 women, with a mean age of 60.02 ± 11.3 years and 62.68 ± 10.6 years, respectively (Table 1).

During the preoperative workup, along with plain radiographs in 2 planes, a computed tomography (CT) scan with 3-dimensional reconstruction of the pelvis was performed to define the acetabular defects and plan the preoperative acetabular construct. The type of acetabular defects, tentative size of the acetabulum, number and positioning of screws, presence of pelvic dehiscence, and requirement for an additional acetabular cage were determined.

Surgical Technique

All the surgeries were performed using a posterolateral approach. After raising the skin and subcutaneous tissue flaps, the abductors were carefully protected, and the prosthetic joint was exposed, cleared of fibrous tissues, and dislocated carefully. In cases with a previous first-stage revision, the cemented spacer was extracted. In those undergoing 1-stage rTHA, the acetabulum was exposed 360°, and the acetabular cup was carefully extracted with minimal bone loss using explant instruments.

The type of acetabular defect was redefined and ascertained before the acetabular bed was prepared for implantation of the TMC, by carefully and sequentially graded reaming until optimal fit was achieved. In type IIC and type IIIA defects, the primary bone loss was usually in the anteroinferior and posterosuperior sectors, with adequate bone stock in the anterosuperior and posteroinferior quadrants serving as the sourcil for receiving the TM substrate using a graded reaming technique. To maximize the host bone contact, the TMC had to be inserted in a more vertical and less anteverted version than optimal cup positioning as defined by the safe zone. The aim was to achieve an optimally fitting TMC with a minimum host bone contact of 50% or more of the surface area required for osteointegration, which is desirable in revision scenarios. The rim of the TMC was removed and placed in a desired position, and additional holes were drilled into it as per the requirement of screw position in the best available bone within the acetabulum, using carbide drill bits. After impaction of the TMC in the best possible fit, it was further secured using multiple acetabular bone screws, preferably in all 4 quadrants, aiming for ischial, pubic, and iliac bone. An attempt was also made to use the kick-strand screw configuration wherever possible, where 1 long screw was inserted into the ischium and 1 in the pubis.

In Paprosky type IIIB defects where the superior defect exceeded 2 cm, the bone stock of the acetabulum (anterior and posterior pillars) did not allow over-reaming to achieve a concentric fit. We were required to use a superior porous acetabular augment to achieve a stable TMC, which was fixed with screws in all quadrants, locking the augment also in place with a thin layer of cement between the TMC and augment to prevent metallosis. In a few cases of type III B defects and those with non-supporting acetabular bone stock or chronic pelvic discontinuity, we used the distraction technique to insert a large TMC with screws in all quadrants and, in addition, protected it with a cage to ensure primary stability and allow integration of the TMC (Fig. 4). To restore COR and obtain the desired femoral head coverage and ensure optimal biomechanics and stability, the DMC was face-changed and cemented inside the TMC or cup and cage using pigmented high-viscosity bone cement with additional antibiotic (vancomycin 1 gm).

All cases were closed in layers over the suction drain, which was kept postoperatively until the drain volume decreased below 50 mL, usually by 48 to 96 hours. All aseptic revisions received broad-spectrum parenteral antibiotics for 5 days, and septic revisions received extended antibiotic therapy with empirical or strain-specific antibiotics based on culture results for 3 weeks, followed by long-term oral therapy for 3 to 6 months. Postoperative multimodal, both parenteral and oral, analgesia was instituted to ensure comfortable physiotherapy and rehabilitation. All patients received deep vein thrombosis (DVT) prophylaxis with enoxaparin injection subcutaneously for 35 days. Touch-down weight-bearing ambulation with isometric hip physiotherapy was allowed for an initial 6 weeks, and subsequent graded weight-bearing ambulation with hip range of motion exercises was encouraged over the next 6 weeks to 3 months. Sutures were removed on the 21st postoperative day. Patients were followed up at 3, 6, and 12 months for procedure-specific complications and progress of rehabilitation. We retrieved the follow-up data for all patients from 2 to 11 years.

RESULTS

A total of 1,532 hip arthroplasties were performed in 1,448 patients during the study period; 82 patients received a TMC as a part of a CiC, CiCA, or CCC construct (Fig. 3). Of 82 patients, 47 (56.6%) were revised for aseptic loosening, 23 (27.7%) had septic failures, 6 (7.2%) had to be revised for recurrent dislocations, 4 (5%) were operated for pelvic–acetabular fractures with secondary arthritis either following failed osteosynthesis (n = 2) or conservatively managed fracture (n = 2), and 2 had ankylosing spondylitis with secondary osteoarthritis of the hip. Nearly two-thirds (n = 53) underwent 1-stage rTHA and the remaining one-third (n = 25) had to be revised in 2 stages, whereas 5 underwent complex primary THA. Overall hospital stay for all cases was 10.93 ± 6.35 days, compared to 20.25 ± 4.54 days for septic revisions in prosthetic joint infection (PJI) cases and 7.34 ± 1.21 days for all other causes of revision. The mean follow-up till failure was 54.64 ± 25.36 months. Eleven hips had Paprosky type IIC defects, 52 were IIIA, and 19 were IIIB (Table 1).

The mean diameter of the acetabular cup used in the surgeries was 64.10 ± 4.68 mm. Both vertical and medial offsets were successfully maintained, demonstrating effective restoration of hip biomechanics. The average change in the medial offset was –1.21 ± 5.33 mm, while the vertical offset increased by 5.27 ± 4.68 mm (Table 2).

At the final follow-up, patient outcomes showed significant improvement in hip function and quality of life. The mean HHS improved to 82.45 ± 6.66, suggesting a marked enhancement in joint function. Additionally, the mean EuroQol 5-Dimensions (EQ-5D) score, which reflects the patient’s general health status and quality of life, reached 0.81 ± 0.18, further confirming the positive impact of the surgery on patients’ daily lives. However, complications were noted in 11 cases, accounting for 13.4% of the total cases. PJI was the most frequently encountered complication, affecting 7.3% of patients (n = 6). In 2 patients, Girdlestone arthroplasty was performed, while in another 2 cases, wound debridement and suppressive antibiotics were given. Two patients required revision of the implant. Prosthetic dislocations occurred in 4.9% of the cases (n = 4); in 2 cases, this was managed with an exchange of the DMC to a cemented constrained liner within the existing TMC (Fig. 5). The midterm survivability of the TMC was promising, with 96% of implants remaining intact at 3 years after surgery, decreasing slightly to 86% for those followed between 3 and 11 years. DISCUSSION

Introduced in the middle of the twentieth century, THA is one of the most successful orthopedic procedures.6) With the increasing demands of an ageing population and expanding indications for its use, the last 2 decades have seen an increasing need for rTHA.7,8) In rTHA, there is bony deficiency, and the success of any surgery depends on acetabular and proximal femur restoration. We need to evaluate the bone loss with modern imaging and preplan the strategies. The aim is to obtain a stable acetabular and femoral construct at the optimum position and to ensure biological integration into available host bone. Until the advent of TM, the most popular option for restoring bone on the acetabular side was to use structural or morselized allograft with or without autograft, depending on defect size and location. The graft was protected by a metallic cage fixed to host bone using screws, and a polyethylene cup was cemented in the desired orientation into the cage 4) Since it was not a biological reconstruction allowing osteointegration, failure due to graft resorption and cage loosening and breakage at midterm was high.9,10) With the advent of the TMC and its use in CiC constructs as described in our study, the use of dead allogenic bone is not required. The TMC fills the bony defect and acts as a good biological substitute for allogenic bone graft. With multiple sectoral screws, it provides primary stability, which allows early osteointegration. The DMC loads the TMC, which in turn loads the host bone, thus avoiding a weak link of the non-biological cage. The cage used in the CCC construct protects the TMC when the host bone is extremely deficient and requires a period of load protection for integration. We also used acetabular TM augments (CiCA) in cases where the superior defect was large (> 2 cm), thus precluding concentric expansion restricted by the anteroposterior pillars of the acetabulum.

Survival data of our study show close to 96% survival at 3 years, decreasing to 86% between 3 and 11 years. The survival was lower for rTHA after septic loosening than for aseptic failures. It compares well with a larger review of TMCs in rTHA from the Finnish registry; Skytta. et al. described a 3-year overall survivorship of 92% and less than 2% re-revisions for aseptic loosening.11) Mohaddes et al.12) reviewed 805 patients from Swedish registry data who underwent first revision with TMC and supported the its use in rTHA with respect to survivorship and rerevisions. We had excellent survival of TMC acetabular reconstructions at midterm follow-up. Ours is the first midterm outcome data being published for CiC, CiCA, and CCC constructs from a single center. In aseptic cases, the construct survived in 85% of patients at midterm follow-up (2–11 years). In the septic group, the majority of the failures occurred in less than 5 years; however, all constructs surviving at 5 years had 100% survival at more than 8 years. The failure rates were comparable in both aseptic and septic groups after 8 years. Therefore, once integrated, the CiC and CCC constructs demonstrated 100% survival.

In our study, we had 11 Paprosky type IIC, 52 type IIIA, and 19 type IIIB defects. In contrast, Ji et al.13) reported 9 type IIC (43%) and 12 type IIIB (57%) defects, while Zhang et al.14) studied the use of double trabecular cups in 11 IIIA (61.1%) and 7 IIIB (38.9%) defects. A comparative study by Valenzuela et al.15) on CCC included 1 IIC (4%), 9 IIIA (36.0%), and 8 IIIB (32.0%) defects in TM + constrained liner group and 3 IIC (12%), 6 IIIA (24%), and 6 IIIC (24%) defects in TM + DM groups.

Ji et al.,13) in their multi-cup reconstruction technique study, noted a postoperative change in mean COR from 35 mm to 17 mm cranialization and 11 mm to 25 mm of lateralization, and they were able to correct preoperative high hip centers in 12 patients postoperatively.13) In a retrospective review of 131 CCC, Chaudhary F and colleagues recognized statistically significant changes in limb length discrepancy (LLD), vertical offsets, and horizontal offsets from a mean of 12.2 mm to 3.9 mm, 9.2 mm to 8 mm, and 10.2 mm to 10.9 mm, respectively.16) Similarly, Zhang et al.,14) with their double TMC technique, were able to achieve COR and offsets comparable with the contralateral hips in 18 patients they followed up for 91 months or more. Compared to these studies, in our study we were able to more accurately restore the hip center as compared to the native hip center. Our mean change in the medial offset was –1.21 ± 5.33 mm, while the vertical offset showed an increase of 5.27 ± 4.68 mm (Table 2).

The double socket method in revision arthroplasties, where a DMC is cemented within a well-fixed acetabular shell, was first described by Wegrzyn et al.17) In their retrospective review of 28 peri-octogenarian patients undergoing the said technique for wear-related dislocations, they found it to be an encouraging alternative as it is less invasive, bone loss sparing, blood loss minimizing, and an invigorating construct in these frail patients with limited life expectancy. Similar observations were made by Bellova et al.18) Nineteen of 33 patients available at the final follow-up of 28.5 ± 17.3 months (range, 3–64 months) had improved HHS and Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) scores, while the survival rate free of loosening was 90.9% after a mean follow-up of 22.3 ± 18.5 months (range, 1.5–64.7 months). Chiarlone et al.,19) in their limited series of 9 cases with a mean followup of 35.3 ± 10.8 months, demonstrated implantation of 2 hemispherical cups in a cup-on-cup fashion to address complex acetabular defects. Their technique was effective in addressing bone defects and created mechanically stable constructs, which also restored the COR in complex acetabular reconstructions. A double TMC construct’s retrospective review by Loppini et al.,20) in 16 patients with a mean follow-up of 34 months, found improved HHS and WOMAC scores, reduction in LLD, and superior migration with survivorship of 100% for aseptic loosening and 88.9% for any cause of revision. Cup-on-cup construct with TMCs in a retrospective series of 20 patients studied by Webb et al.21) had dislocations in 30% of patients (n = 6) and 4 infections within the first year of surgery. Blumenfeld et al.22) implanted a porous-coated cup in a cup akin to our constructs in 8 hips that yielded good abductor strength in 50% of constructs, improved horizontal and vertical offsets by 10.5 mm (range, 8–16 mm), and an average of 18.4 mm (range, 10–29 mm), respectively. They did not find any loosening or migration of the cup in their series at a minimum of 12 months (average, 28 months; range, 12–50 months) of follow-up. The mean HHS, which evaluates pain, function, and range of motion, improved to 82.45 ± 6.66, suggesting a marked enhancement in joint function after the procedure. Additionally, the mean EQ-5D score, which reflects the patient’s general health status and quality of life, reached 0.81 ± 0.18, further confirming the positive impact of the surgery on patients’ daily lives.

We had 11 complications in our series, including 6 PJIs, 4 dislocations, and 1 DVT. Two patients underwent revision for septic revision failure because of persistent PJI in the third and fifth postoperative years, and another 2 for aseptic loosening, who developed refractory PJI and underwent a Girdlestone procedure. In our study, 2 patients with recurrent PJI responded to debridement and implant retention procedures and were put on long-term suppressive antibiotics. Two cases of dislocations occurred in the second and third postoperative weeks, respectively, and were managed with closed reduction under general anesthesia. In 2 cases of recurrent prosthetic dislocation with severe abductor insufficiency, the DMC was revised to a constrained liner along with Gluteus maximus muscle transfer to the greater trochanter. One patient developed DVT in the operated limb postoperatively and was managed conservatively (Table 4).

Our retrospective case series of 82 rTHA undergoing TMC with DMC reconstruction with or without augments and cages, appears to be the first large series from a single center describing survival and functional scores at early to midterm follow-ups to date. Secondly, this is the first large series wherein the DMC was used in TMCs as a face-changing option to obtain the desired version and inclination for near anatomical restoration of the COR. Thirdly, our study has the advantage of surgical equipoise of 4 senior arthroplasty surgeons exercising uniform surgical steps and protocols. We also had no loss to follow-up. Our study had some limitations; being a retrospective study of a heterogeneous group of patients with widely differing indications for the use of a CC construct limits its ability to prove the suitability of the technique for particular indications. With an average follow-up of 54 months, it still needs a longer follow-up to prove its efficacy in the long term.

Our study highlights the excellent midterm survivability of a novel technique of CiC reconstruction of acetabular defects while performing rTHA with or without augment and CCC using a modern trabecular metal cup as a bone graft substitute. The robust biomechanics of the construct is proven by the survivability in the midterm.15,23,24,25) With the data this study presents, it has clear superiority over graft-cage and cemented cup constructs, which have literature showing poor survivability at midterm. While we continue to follow up on these cases over the long term, it could be beneficial to follow up with a comparable other cohort who undergo alternative reconstruction strategies to prove its superiority over prevailing techniques.
