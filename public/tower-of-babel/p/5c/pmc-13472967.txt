FcRH5×CD3 bispecific antibody cevostamab in relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma: a phase 1 trial
Authors: Adam D. Cohen, Joshua Richter, Suzanne Trudel, Alexander Lesokhin, Jacob P. Laubach, Sheeba K. Thomas, Simon J. Harrison, Luciano J. Costa, Andrew Spencer, Rafael Fonseca, Peter A. Forsberg, Jesus G. Berdeja, Rayan Kaedbey, Nizar J. Bahlis, Paula Rodriguez-Otero, Maria-Victoria Mateos, Tulika Tyagi, Divya Samineni, Wenyu Liu, Rin Nakamura, Voleak Choeurng, Chihunt Wong, James Cooper, Amrita Krishnan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fc receptor-homolog 5 (FcRH5) is a membrane protein that is ubiquitously expressed on myeloma cells. Cevostamab is a first-in-class, FcRH5×CD3 bispecific antibody. GO39775 is a phase 1 dose-escalation and dose-expansion study evaluating fixed-duration cevostamab in relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma. Cevostamab was initiated with step-up dosing and continued at the target dose (TD) once every 3 weeks for 17 cycles (~12 months) unless disease progression or unacceptable toxicity occurred. Primary objectives were to evaluate safety, including determination of the maximum tolerated dose (MTD), and to identify a recommended phase 2 dose and schedule (RP2D) for cevostamab monotherapy. Secondary objectives included determining the rate of response and the duration of response (DOR). As of 24 February 2025, 324 patients had been enrolled; most were heavily pretreated (median prior lines, 6; triple-class refractory, 89.5% (290/324); prior B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA)-targeted therapy, 47.5% (154/324)). The MTD was not reached. Across all TD levels (0.15–252 mg), grade 3 or 4 adverse events (AEs) and serious AEs occurred in 59.6% (193/324) and 60.2% (195/324), respectively. Grade 5 AEs excluding disease progression occurred in 4.6% (15/324); 0.9% (3/324) were considered treatment related (hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, n = 2; disseminated intravascular coagulation in the context of pseudomonal sepsis, n = 1). Objective response and very good partial response or better rates were 42.1% (136/323) and 25.1% (81/323), respectively. Median DOR was 11.2 months (95% confidence interval, 8.3, 15.6). A total of 167 patients received treatment at the 160 mg TD level (RP2D). Objective response and very good partial response or better rates were 44.3% (74/167) and 25.7% (43/167) in all patients and 60.6% (43/71) and 39.4% (28/71) in the BCMA naive, respectively. Median DOR was 10.4 months in all patients and 19.7 months in the BCMA naive, with durable responses maintained after the completion of treatment. Cytokine release syndrome was grade 1 or 2 in the 0.3/1.2/3.6/160 mg triple step-up cohort (RP2D). Cevostamab had manageable safety and induced durable remissions in late-line relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma. ClinicalTrials.gov registration: NCT03275103.

Main

Almost all patients with multiple myeloma eventually become triple-class refractory to proteasome inhibitors, immunomodulatory drugs and anti-CD38 antibodies1,2. Recently, chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells and bispecific antibodies targeting B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) or G protein-coupled receptor class C group 5 member D (GPRC5D) have shown activity in triple-class exposed and/or refractory patients3–7. However, some patients do not respond and almost all responders eventually progress3–7. In addition, the management of infection risk remains a key consideration for BCMA-targeted T cell-redirecting agents3,5–7, while GPRC5D-targeted agents may be compromised by on-target, off-tumor toxicities4. Continuous treatment with these agents may further exacerbate these toxicities and also lead to treatment resistance through target loss or mutation and/or acquired T cell dysfunction8–10. New treatments, especially those that target alternative antigens and can be given for a fixed duration, are needed.

Fc receptor-homolog 5 (FcRH5) is a membrane protein that is expressed exclusively in the B cell lineage and ubiquitously on myeloma cells, independent of BCMA expression, making it an attractive therapeutic target11–16. Cevostamab is a first-in-class, humanized, immunoglobulin G1-based FcRH5×CD3 bispecific antibody that induces FcRH5-targeted, T cell-mediated killing of myeloma cells15.

We present the results of a phase 1 study (GO39775; NCT03275103) evaluating the safety, pharmacokinetics, immunogenicity and anti-tumor activity of cevostamab in late-line relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM) when administered for a fixed duration of treatment (~12 months).

Results

GO39775 is an open-label, multicenter, phase 1 dose-escalation and dose-expansion study in patients with late-line RRMM for whom no established therapy was available or considered appropriate or likely to be tolerable by the investigator. Prior T cell-redirecting therapy was allowed. Cevostamab (intravenous infusion) was initiated with step-up dosing for the mitigation of cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and continued at the target dose once every 3 weeks for 17 cycles (~12 months) unless disease progression or unacceptable toxicity occurred. In the dose-escalation stage, escalating step-up and target doses, and alternative step-up schedules, were evaluated in a standard 3 + 3 design. In the dose-expansion stage, dosing regimens showing promising safety and activity were selected for further evaluation. Primary objectives were to evaluate safety, including determination of the maximum tolerated dose (MTD), and to identify a recommended phase 2 dose and schedule (RP2D) for cevostamab monotherapy. Secondary objectives were to evaluate pharmacokinetics, immunogenicity and activity. Activity endpoints were the rate of response and duration of response (DOR) as determined by investigators according to International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) criteria17. Progression-free survival (PFS) was assessed in a post hoc analysis. The safety and efficacy of tocilizumab as premedication for the mitigation of CRS after cevostamab dosing was evaluated in an additional expansion cohort, but these data were not included in the current analysis and will be reported elsewhere. Data on cevostamab retreatment were also not included in the current analysis and will be reported elsewhere.

Patients

Between 19 September 2017 and 18 July 2023, 324 patients were enrolled into the monotherapy cohorts at 17 centers in the United States, Canada, Spain and Australia (Supplementary Table 1). As of 24 February 2025, all patients had received at least one dose of cevostamab. Of these, 48 patients completed 17 cycles of treatment and 276 discontinued (Table 1). The most common reason for discontinuation was disease progression (64.8%). Twenty-two dose-escalation cohorts were opened (Extended Data Fig. 1). Outcomes in all patients

Dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) occurred in one patient in the 3.6/90 mg single step-up cohort (grade 3 pneumonia) and one in the 3.6/198 mg cohort (grade 1 CRS, which was initially graded as grade 3 using criteria from a previously published grading system18; Extended Data Fig. 1); grade 3 pneumonia occurred after the initial target-dose administration on cycle 1 day 8 (C1D8; onset of event: C1D19), and grade 1 CRS occurred after the initial target-dose administration on C1D9 (onset: C1D9). Both events resolved. The MTD was not reached per the protocol-defined criteria for MTD determination.

In all patients with ≥1 post-baseline result, anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) were detected in 63 of 296 (21.3%) and neutralizing antibodies in 23 of 290 (7.9%).

Among all efficacy-evaluable patients (n = 323), the objective response rate was 42.1% and the very good partial response (VGPR) or better rate was 25.1% (Extended Data Table 1). Median time to first response was 1.4 months (minimum to maximum: 0.5–5.9) and to best response was 2.5 months (minimum to maximum: 0.5–27.3). The median DOR was 11.2 months (95% confidence interval (CI), 8.3, 15.6) among patients in partial response or better (n = 136) and 25.1 months (95% CI, 19.4, 36.4) among those in VGPR or better (n = 81) (Extended Data Table 1).

On the basis of a comprehensive assessment of cumulative efficacy and safety and a comprehensive quantitative clinical pharmacology evaluation that will be reported elsewhere, once every 3 weeks (Q3W) dosing at the 160 mg target-dose level and triple step-up dosing at the 0.3/1.2/3.6/160 mg dose level were found to have the optimal benefit–risk profile (RP2D for cevostamab monotherapy). Notably, across the range of Q3W target doses tested (0.15–252 mg), the clinical data, along with exposure–response analyses, showed that increasing cevostamab exposure was associated with a higher objective response and VGPR or better rate (Extended Data Table 2), with the attainment of a plateau at the 160 mg target-dose level, implying diminishing gains at target doses beyond 160 mg. While the majority of AEs of interest (including grade ≥1 and ≥2 CRS, grade ≥3 cytopenias and grade ≥3 AEs) did not show a strong correlation with the target dose or exposure, a significant exposure–response safety relationship was observed for SAEs and grade ≥3 infections, suggesting that, at target doses beyond 160 mg, there was an increased risk of these events compared with the 160 mg dose. In addition, triple step-up dosing at the 0.3/1.2/3.6/160 mg dose level showed the most favorable CRS profile among all of the tested priming regimens. Cumulative data for the 160 mg target-dose level and 0.3/1.2/3.6/160 mg triple step-up priming regimen are summarized below and in the tables.

Outcomes in patients who received the recommended phase 2 dose and schedule

A total of 167 patients were enrolled at the 160 mg target-dose level. Of these, 10 patients initiated treatment with single step-up dosing, 97 with double step-up dosing and 60 with triple step-up dosing (Extended Data Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics were generally comparable with those of all patients (Table 2). Median follow-up was 20.0 months (range: 0.5–54.9).

Safety

The most common (≥20%) AEs of any grade at the 160 mg target-dose level were CRS (74.3%), neutropenia (31.7%), cough (30.5%), nausea (28.1%), diarrhea (24.0%), anemia (24.0%) and fatigue (21.6%) (Table 3). Grade 3 or 4 AEs and SAEs of any grade occurred in 96 patients each (57.5%). Grade 5 (fatal) events excluding disease progression occurred in 10 patients (6.0%); events in 3 patients (1.8%) were considered treatment related (hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) in 2 patients, disseminated intravascular coagulation in the context of pseudomonal sepsis in 1 patient) (Extended Data Table 3). AEs leading to cevostamab discontinuation excluding myeloma progression occurred in 29 patients (17.4%); events in 13 patients (7.8%) were considered treatment related (Extended Data Table 4).

Grade 3 or 4 neutropenia occurred in 48 patients (28.7%) and grade 3 or 4 febrile neutropenia in 8 (4.8%) at the 160 mg target-dose level. Thirty-seven (22.2%) patients received colony stimulating factors, and 19 (11.4%) received dose modifications or interruptions for any-grade neutropenia. Grade 3 or 4 thrombocytopenia occurred in 18 patients (10.8%). No neutropenia or thrombocytopenia leading to cevostamab discontinuation was reported.

Infections occurred in 91 patients (54.5%) at the 160 mg target-dose level (Table 3 and Extended Data Table 5). Infections in ≥5% were pneumonia (9.6%), upper respiratory tract infection (8.4%), urinary tract infection (7.2%) and rhinovirus infection (5.4%). Grade 3 or 4 infections occurred in 26 patients (15.6%) and grade 5 infections in 6 patients (3.6%). Infections leading to treatment discontinuation were uncommon (9 patients, 5.4%). Anti-infective prophylaxis for viral, fungal, bacterial or pneumocystis infections was not mandated but was recommended per institutional practice; one patient developed pneumocystis infection despite prophylaxis with sulfamethoxazole–trimethoprim. Forty-nine patients (29.3%) had a reported medical history of any hypogammaglobulinemia, while 13 (7.8%) had ≥1 treatment-emergent AE of hypogammaglobulinemia. Of the 79 patients who had an IgG level <400 mg dl−1 at any time between cycle 2 and the first post-treatment visit, 40 had an IgG level ≥400 mg dl−1 at baseline, with median time to first result below 400 mg dl−1 of 80 days. Fifty-five patients (32.9%) received intravenous immunoglobulin during treatment. Three of the 32 patients who had a grade 3+ infection had received intravenous immunoglobulin within the preceding 30 days.

Possible immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (see Table 3 for the definition) occurred in 23 patients (13.8%; 11 grade 1, 6.6%; 9 grade 2, 5.4%; 3 grade 3, 1.8%) at the 160 mg target-dose level (Table 3); events in ≥3 patients were confusional state (4.8%; 3 grade 1, 1.8%; 3 grade 2, 1.8%; 2 grade 3, 1.2%) and dysphonia (1.8%; 1 grade 1, 0.6%; 2 grade 2, 1.2%). Infusion-related reactions occurred in 12 patients (7.2%; all grade 1 or 2). No cases of tumor lysis syndrome were reported. Grade 3 HLH occurred in one additional patient who discontinued treatment.

CRS occurred in 19 of 30 patients (63.3%) who received the 0.3/1.2/3.6/160 mg triple step-up priming regimen (Table 4). CRS was grade 1 in 14 (46.7%) and grade 2 in 5 (16.7%). No grade 3 or 4 events or events leading to treatment discontinuation occurred. CRS occurred in 18 patients in cycle 1 and in 4 patients in cycle 2+ (Extended Data Fig. 2). Ten patients had ≥1 CRS event, including all 4 of the patients who had CRS in cycle 2+. All 4 of the patients who had CRS in cycle 2+ had previously experienced a CRS event. The median time to CRS onset from the last end of infusion was 13.1 h (minimum to maximum: 0–57.1) and median time to resolution was 1 day (minimum to maximum: 0–5). CRS was managed with tocilizumab in 9 patients (30.0%), steroids in 4 (13.3%) and both agents in 2 (6.7%). Pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity

Cevostamab pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity were assessed with a data cutoff of August 2023. Systemic exposure increased in a dose-proportional manner over the tested dose range. Serum concentrations peaked at the end of infusion and declined in a multi-phasic fashion. At the 160 mg target-dose level, the estimated median half-life was ~13 days. In patients with ≥1 post-baseline result at the 160 mg target-dose level, ADAs were detected in 29 of 148 (19.6%) and neutralizing antibodies in 5 of 142 (3.5%). As the sample size for neutralizing antibodies was limited (n = 5), no conclusive characterization could be carried out to assess their impact on pharmacokinetics, efficacy or safety.

Anti-tumor activity

At the 160 mg target-dose level, the objective response rate was 44.3% and the VGPR or better rate was 25.7% (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Table 1). Median time to first response was 1.4 months (minimum to maximum: 0.5–4.6) and to best response was 2.6 months (minimum to maximum: 0.5–13.4). Discussion

We evaluated the safety, pharmacokinetics, immunogenicity and activity of cevostamab, a first-in-class, FcRH5-targeted bispecific antibody, in late-line multiple myeloma. Dosing regimens included those assessing single, double and triple step-up dosing and 0.15–252 mg target doses. The MTD was not reached per the protocol-defined criteria. Q3W dosing at the 160 mg target-dose level and triple step-up dosing at the 0.3/1.2/3.6/160 mg dose level were found to have the optimal benefit–risk profile (RP2D for cevostamab monotherapy).

Notably, the population in this phase 1 study was heavily pretreated. At the 160 mg target-dose level, patients had received a median of 6 prior lines of therapy; 95.8% were triple-class refractory, and 73.7% were penta-drug refractory. In addition, 57.5% had received prior BCMA-targeted therapy, with the availability of these and other targeted agents evolving considerably during the enrollment period.

The most frequent grade 3 or 4 events at the 160 mg target-dose level were reversible neutropenia (28.7%), anemia (18.0%) and thrombocytopenia (10.8%). Grade 3 or 4 neutropenia (32–64%), anemia (23–37%) and thrombocytopenia (11–24%) were also reported in the pivotal studies of the GPRC5D-targeted bispecific antibody, talquetamab, and the BCMA-targeted bispecific antibodies, teclistamab and elranatamab4–6.

Grade 3 or 4 infections occurred in 15.6% of patients at the 160 mg target-dose level, which appears to differ from the rates reported in the pivotal studies of teclistamab (45%), elranatamab (40%) and talquetamab (7%), although cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted with caution4–6. Rates of grade 3 or 4 febrile neutropenia (4.8%) were also low in the current study. Grade 5 infections occurred with a low frequency in all studies4–6. Of note, anti-infective prophylaxis, including intravenous immunoglobulin replacement therapy, was not mandated in the current study and guidelines for patients receiving bispecific antibodies were evolving during the enrollment period, which may have influenced the infection rate. Cevostamab did not cause substantial dysgeusia, weight loss, nail changes, desquamation or ataxia, toxicities associated with GPRC5D-targeted therapies that can significantly impact quality of life4,19.

In the current study, extensive efforts were made to mitigate CRS. At the 160 mg target-dose level, treatment initiation with a 0.3/1.2/3.6 mg triple step-up priming regimen had a favorable CRS profile. Among 30 patients who received this schedule, CRS primarily occurred in cycle 1, and was all grade 1 or 2. No events leading to treatment discontinuation were reported, and all events resolved.

At the 160 mg target-dose level, cevostamab was associated with an objective response rate of 44.3% and a VGPR or better rate of 25.7%. Responses were durable, especially in patients in VGPR or better. These data are compelling, given that FcRH5 is a new therapeutic target for myeloma, the population was highly refractory and >50% had received prior BCMA-targeted therapy. Notably, the objective response rate in patients without prior BCMA-targeted therapy (60.6%) was higher than in those with prior exposure (17.2–43.8%), and appeared to be generally comparable with those reported in the pivotal studies of teclistamab (63%), elranatamab (61%) and talquetamab (64–70%) in BCMA therapy-naive populations, while again acknowledging the limitations of cross-trial comparison4–6. Interestingly, the VGPR or better rate in patients without prior BCMA-targeted therapy (39.4%) appeared to be lower than in the abovementioned pivotal studies (58.8%, 56.1% and 52–57%, respectively), potentially reflecting the higher rate of penta-drug-refractory disease in this study4–6. Response rates in patients with prior BCMA-targeted CAR T cell (36.2%) and ADC (43.8%) exposure were also clinically meaningful, supporting exploration of cevostamab as a non-BCMA-targeted option for patients relapsing after these therapies.

The short median PFS observed at the 160 mg target-dose level was largely driven by the high proportion of BCMA therapy-exposed, penta-drug-refractory patients in this cohort, and was relatively similar to that reported with other T cell-redirecting therapies when used after prior BCMA-targeted therapies5,20–22. The relatively short washout period in this trial (≥12 weeks for CAR T cells, ≥4 weeks for bispecific antibodies) may also have had a role, as recent reports suggest that a shorter interval between T cell-redirecting therapies may be associated with poorer T cell function and worse outcomes20,23,24. Further studies exploring the optimal timing and sequencing of T cell-redirecting therapies are needed. Notwithstanding this observation, it is notable that durable responses were observed in this cohort of patients, especially among those who achieved a VGPR or better.

Most targeted therapies for RRMM, including approved bispecific antibodies, are given until progression. However, indefinite T cell engagement can produce unintended negative consequences, including persistent hypogammaglobulinemia, progressive T cell dysfunction and high rates of tumor antigen loss or mutation8–10,25. This can not only lead to high infection rates and loss of efficacy, but may also compromise future therapies against the same target. In this study, a fixed duration of treatment of ~12 months was evaluated. At the 160 mg target-dose level, 26 patients completed treatment in partial response or better, with 17 having ongoing responses off treatment at data cutoff. Most durable responders were in complete response or better at end of treatment, suggesting that, in patients who achieve a complete response to cevostamab, it may be possible to achieve an extended treatment-free interval that could help to alleviate cumulative toxicities and improve quality of life.

Limitations of this study include small patient numbers in the step-up dosing cohorts; non-uniform follow-up, with relatively short follow-up in the latest cohorts; heterogeneity in the step-up dosing schedules used in the 160 mg target-dose level group; and the evolution in CRS management and availability of targeted myeloma therapies during the enrollment period. A standardized approach for anti-infective prophylaxis was also lacking, with guidance for patients receiving bispecific antibodies evolving over the duration of study. Risk status according to the most recent IMWG criteria could not be determined post hoc because key genetic alterations were not evaluated.

Collectively, these data validate FcRH5 as a therapeutic target for myeloma and show that fixed-duration cevostamab has manageable safety and induces durable remissions in patients with late-line disease. These data support the continued development of cevostamab alone or in combination with standard of care or investigational agents26,27. Given its distinct target, lack of GPRC5D-related skin or mucosal toxicities, and manageable infection risk profile, cevostamab may offer a complementary therapeutic option within the expanding landscape of T cell-redirecting therapies for multiple myeloma.

Methods

The GO39775 study was conducted in accordance with the International Conference on Harmonization guidelines for Good Clinical Practice. The protocol was approved by the institutional review board and ethics committee at each participating center (Supplementary Data Table 1). Patients provided written informed consent and were not compensated.

Patients

In summary, eligible patients were those with measurable disease (defined by at least one of serum monoclonal protein ≥0.5 g dl−1 (≥5 g l−1), urine monoclonal protein ≥200 mg per 24 h or involved serum free light chains (SFLCs) ≥10 mg dl−1 (≥100 mg l−1) and an abnormal SFLC ratio (<0.26 or >1.65)) for whom no established therapy for RRMM was available or considered appropriate or likely to be tolerable by the investigator. Prior CAR T cells ≥12 weeks and ADCs or bispecific antibodies ≥4 weeks before cevostamab initiation were allowed.

Treatment

Cevostamab (intravenous infusion) was initiated with step-up dosing for CRS mitigation and continued at the target dose Q3W for 17 cycles unless disease progression or unacceptable toxicity occurred (Extended Data Fig. 1). In the dose-escalation stage, escalating step-up and target doses, and alternative step-up schedules, were evaluated in a standard 3 + 3 design (see the protocol for full details). In the dose-expansion stage, dosing regimens showing promising safety and activity were selected for further evaluation. Corticosteroid premedication (20 mg dexamethasone intravenous or 80 mg methylprednisolone intravenous) was initially mandated before each cevostamab administration for the mitigation of CRS. This requirement was subsequently updated to corticosteroid premedication before each administration in cycle 1 and cycle 2, with the option to discontinue corticosteroid premedication from cycle 3 onwards in patients who do not experience CRS with their previous administration. Premedication with acetaminophen or paracetamol (500–1,000 mg, oral) and diphenhydramine (25–50 mg, oral) was also required before each administration. Patients were hospitalized for ≥48 h after each cevostamab infusion in cycle 1. Anti-infective prophylaxis, including intravenous immunoglobulin, was per institutional practice and was not protocol mandated.

Objectives and endpoints

Primary objectives were to evaluate safety, including characterization of DLTs (see below for information regarding the DLT assessment windows and DLT definition) and determination of the MTD (see the protocol for the dose-escalation rules, including the criteria for MTD determination), and to identify an RP2D for cevostamab monotherapy. Secondary objectives were to evaluate pharmacokinetics, immunogenicity and activity.

Safety was assessed by evaluating the incidence and severity of AEs, graded by National Cancer Institute criteria29. CRS was initially graded by Lee criteria18 and was subsequently graded by American Society of Transplantation and Cellular Therapy criteria30, and is reported according to the latter. Re-grading was carried out algorithmically using statistical programming based on data on signs and symptoms and interventions collected in the electronic case report form.

Pharmacokinetics were assessed by measuring the concentration of cevostamab in serum samples. Immunogenicity was assessed by detecting ADAs in serum samples using validated homogeneous bridging enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays following a tiered approach (screening, confirmatory, titer). Confirmed positive samples were also analyzed in a cell-based neutralizing antibody assay using bead extraction with acid dissociation pretreatment. Immunogenicity samples were obtained at baseline; before dosing in cycles 2, 3 and 4; and subsequently at every even-numbered cycle (cycles 6–16). Additional samples were collected at treatment discontinuation or early termination, as well as during follow-up visits.

Activity endpoints were the rate of response and DOR, defined as time from first partial response or better to disease progression or death from any cause (whichever occurs first), as determined by investigators according to IMWG criteria30.

Minimal residual disease negativity was an exploratory endpoint that was evaluated at the <10−5 level by next-generation sequencing in study samples collected at the time of suspected complete response in patients who achieved a VGPR or better. Incomplete data are not reported. FcRH5 expression level on myeloma cells collected at baseline by bone marrow aspiration was an exploratory endpoint that was assessed by flow cytometry. The relationship between FcRH5 expression and 1q abnormal status, and FcRH5 expression and response, was assessed in an analysis that was not prespecified.

PFS, defined as time from first exposure to treatment to disease progression or death, was assessed in a post hoc analysis.

Treatment assignment and statistical methods

Patients were identified for potential recruitment by the investigators using prescreening enrollment logs and institutional databases. Patients were assigned to open study cohorts in the order they were enrolled. Planned enrollment was ~180 patients in the dose-escalation stage and ≥30 patients per dosing regimen in the dose-expansion stage (see the protocol for full details). Notably, 30 patients is a reasonable size to obtain an estimate of the response rate; with a sample size of 30 and an observed response rate of 20%, the 90% CI for the true response rate is (8%, 32%) and the expected number of responses is six. Thirty patients is also a reasonable size to obtain an estimate of the rate of grade ≥2 CRS events; with a sample size of 30 and an observed grade ≥2 CRS rate of 27%, the 90% CI for the true grade ≥2 CRS rate is (13%, 40%) and the expected number of grade ≥2 CRS events is eight.

The Medidata RAVE electronic data capture system was used to capture, manage, clean and report the trial data. Descriptive statistics were used to describe and summarize the data in patients who received at least one dose of cevostamab. Tables were generated using SAS version 9.4. Figures were generated using R version 4.4.3. Patients with missing or no response assessment were classified as nonresponders. No formal hypothesis testing was planned. Sex was based on self-reported demographics obtained at screening.

An internal safety committee was used during the study to make recommendations regarding study conduct on the basis of trial safety data to ensure enhanced patient safety monitoring while receiving study treatment. The internal safety committee consisted of the study medical monitor, safety scientist, biomarker scientist and biostatistician, and a medical monitor who was not associated with the study.

Protocol deviations

Investigators were asked to document and promptly report to the sponsor, institutional review board and ethics committee any protocol deviations that might have had an impact on patient safety and/or data integrity. Protocol deviations were reviewed by the sponsor to assess whether any represented a serious breach of good clinical practice guidelines and required reporting to health authorities. Prospective requests to deviate from the protocol were not allowed. Corrective measures are identified in the summary of protocol amendments, which has been included in Supplementary Information.
