Small proline-rich repeat protein 1A-mediated ephrin-A5 downregulation drives neuropathic pain via brain-derived neurotrophic factor/Tropomyosin receptor kinase B signaling
Authors: Meiling Deng, Ting Zhang, Jie Yang, Qiqi Yan, Jun Yang, Wangyuan Zou, Jun Cao, Li Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuropathic pain is a common chronic pain condition that severely impacts patients’ quality of life. While Small proline-rich protein 1A (SPRR1A) is a well-known marker for axonal regeneration, its specific role and the underlying molecular mechanisms in neuropathic pain remain elusive. The present study demonstrates that SPRR1A is significantly increased in the spinal dorsal horn of spared nerve injury (SNI) mice, accompanied by ephrin-A5 downregulation, activation of the brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)/tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB) pathway, and enhanced neuronal excitability. SPRR1A overexpression induces allodynia in naïve mice, whereas knockdown of SPRR1A alleviates hyperalgesia and reduces neuronal hyperactivity. Silencing SPRR1A restores ephrin-A5 expression and suppresses BDNF/TrkB activation. Co-immunoprecipitation confirms that SPRR1A interacts with ephrin-A5, while ephrin-A5 competitively binds TrkB to block BDNF-mediated signaling. Conversely, SPRR1A overexpression decreases ephrin-A5 level and facilitates BDNF/TrkB activation. Ephrin-A5 siRNA reverses the inhibitory effects of SPRR1A knockdown on TrkB signaling. Furthermore, intrathecal administration of recombinant ephrin-A5 significantly attenuates hyperalgesia in SNI mice. Together, these findings identify SPRR1A as a key mediator of neuropathic pain that promotes central sensitization by suppressing ephrin-A5, thereby enhancing BDNF/TrkB signaling in the spinal dorsal horn. Targeting the SPRR1A-ephrin-A5-BDNF/TrkB axis may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for neuropathic pain management.Keywords: Neuropathic pain, Small proline-rich repeat protein 1A, Ephrin-A5, Brain-derived neurotrophic factor, Tropomyosin receptor kinase B

Introduction

Chronic pain is a debilitating disorder that burdens millions of people worldwide [1]. Its subtypes include neuropathic pain, which arises from lesions or dysfunctions of the somatosensory system [2,3]. Neuropathic pain is characterized by the presence of abnormal sensory symptoms such as allodynia and hyperalgesia [4,5]. Epidemiological studies estimate its prevalence to range from 6.9% to 10% globally [6,7], with a relatively high incidence observed in women and older adults [8,9]. Despite the existence of several therapies, the available treatments provide only partial and temporary relief [10,11]. Therefore, identifying novel molecular targets involved in the pathogenesis of neuropathic pain is essential for developing more efficacious therapeutic strategies.

Small proline-rich repeat protein 1A (SPRR1A) is a member of the SPRR gene family involved in cytoskeletal remodeling and epithelial differentiation [12]. In addition to its well-characterized functions, recent studies have shown that SPRR1A is highly upregulated in injured neurons and regenerating axons [13]. Single-cell and transcriptomic analyses have consistently revealed elevated Sprr1a expression in the dorsal root ganglia (DRG) and spinal dorsal horns of animal models of neuropathic pain [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. These results prompted us to hypothesize that SPRR1A might be involved in the establishment of neuropathic pain. However, the link between SPRR1A and the molecular pathways involved in neuropathic pain is still unknown.

Central sensitization is a hallmark pathogenic mechanism of neuropathic pain [18]. It is characterized by enhanced neuronal excitability within nociceptive spinal and supraspinal circuits that amplify nociceptive input and maintain chronic pain long after the resolution of peripheral injury [19,20]. Maladaptive plasticity normally renders innocuous stimuli painful and leads to chronic pain [21]. Mechanistically, central sensitization is induced by persistent neuroplastic changes in neuronal circuits arising from alterations in glutamatergic transmission, neurotrophin signaling, and neuromodulatory network remodeling in the dorsal horn [22,23]. Among these pathways, the activation of the BDNF/TrkB signaling pathway has been identified as a critical mediator of spinal hyperexcitability [24,25]. Inhibition of the BDNF/TrkB pathway efficiently attenuated neuronal sensitization and relieved pain behaviors in different neuropathic models [26,27]. While inhibiting this pathway has shown promise in preclinical models, the endogenous regulatory mechanisms that modulate TrkB activation during nerve injury remain poorly understood.

Ephrin-A5 is a member of the ephrin family of receptor tyrosine kinases that is highly expressed in the brain, spinal cord, DRG and peripheral tissues such as skin and adipose tissue [28,29]. It regulates key physiological processes, including neurogenesis and angiogenesis, where it modulates the frequency of spontaneous miniature postsynaptic currents induced by BDNF and activates downstream signaling pathways [30,31]. More interestingly, ephrin-A5 can compete with BDNF for binding to the TrkB receptor and further suppress BDNF-mediated signaling [32]. Despite these findings, the role of ephrin-A5 in controlling BDNF/TrkB signaling in the context of neuropathic pain remains largely unexplored.

In this study, we demonstrate that SPRR1A directly interacts with and suppresses ephrin-A5, thereby releasing the “molecular brake” on TrkB signaling and promoting neuronal hyperexcitability. Our findings reveal the SPRR1A–ephrin-A5–TrkB pathway as a promising pharmacological target for the management of neuropathic pain.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Adult male C57BL/6 mice (8–12 weeks) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Chongqing Medical University (Chongqing, China). The animals were housed under controlled temperature (22 °C) and lighting conditions (12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (approval number: IACUC-CQMU-2023-0255) and conducted in accordance with national and international guidelines for animal care.

Spared nerve injury (SNI) model

Animals were randomly assigned to groups using a computer-based random number generator. SNI models were established as described previously [33]. The mice were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium, and an incision was made in the left thigh to expose the sciatic nerve branches. The common peroneal and tibial nerves were tightly ligated with silk, and the distal portions were excised (∼2 mm sections removed), while the sural nerve was preserved. The overlying muscle and skin were then sutured after surgery. Sham-operated mice underwent identical procedures without nerve ligation. The mice were allowed to recover individually in a warm environment.

Behavioral assessments

Behavioral testing were performed by observers blinded to the treatment groups. The paw withdrawal threshold (PWT) was evaluated via von Frey filaments (0.04–2.0 g; Stoelting, USA) via the up-and-down method [34]. PWT was measured three times per paw at 10-min intervals, and the average was calculated. The paw withdrawal latency (PWL) was assessed via a radiant heat stimulator (Ugo Basile, Italy) [35]. Following a 30-min acclimation, the plantar surface was exposed to radiant heat, with a 15 s cut-off to minimize tissue damage. The measurements were repeated three times per paw, and the mean value was calculated.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

L4–L6 spinal dorsal horn segments and HT22 cells were collected, and total RNA was extracted via TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. RNA integrity was verified by denaturing agarose gel electrophoresis, and the RNA concentration was measured with a NanoDrop 2000. Reverse transcription was performed via the All-in-One™ qPCR Mix Kit (GeneCopoeia, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR) was conducted via Hieff® SYBR Green Master Mix (Yesen, China) with the primer sequences listed in Table 1.

RNA sequencing and library preparation

RNA-seq libraries were prepared from 3 μg of total RNA via the KC-Digital™ Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Seqhealth, China), which incorporates unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) to reduce PCR bias. Libraries (200–500 bp) were sequenced on a DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI Technologies, China, PE150). Low-quality reads and adapters were removed with Trimmomatic (v0.36). Reads were deduplicated via UMI clustering, aligned to the reference genome with STAR (v2.5.3a), and quantified with featureCounts. Differential expression was analyzed with edgeR (v3.12.1), and |logFC| >1 and P < 0.05 were used as the thresholds for significance.

Cell culture

HT22 hippocampal cells (RRID: CVCL_0321) derived from the hippocampus tissue of mouse (NCBI Taxonomy: 10090) were purchased from Fuheng Biology (catalog number FH1027, Shanghai, China) on August 15, 2025. The HT22 cells remained completely uncontaminated from the time of acquisition through the entire duration of the cytological experiments and were maintained in high-glucose DMEM (HyClone, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated horse serum, 50 U/mL penicillin, and 50 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The medium was replaced every other day, and the cells were passaged every three days.

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) and intrathecal injection

Chemically modified siRNAs targeting SPRR1A and ephrin-A5, as well as a scrambled negative control, were obtained from RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). Modifications included methylation for increased stability and cholesterol conjugation for efficient delivery. The sequences were as follows: SPRR1A, GCTGTGACCTTCAATCTTA; Ephrin-A5, GCTATTGCACGACATCAAT. SiRNAs were delivered via intrathecal injection into the L4–L6 spinal cord. A 30-gauge needle was inserted at the midline of the iliac crest between the L5–L6 intervertebral space, and successful dural puncture was confirmed by a tail flick. Each mouse received 5 μL of siRNA solution (1 nmol/day) over 30 s for five consecutive days. The injection cannula was left in place for an additional 30 s to ensure proper delivery. Motor function was assessed before behavioral testing by placing, stepping, and righting reflexes; mice exhibiting motor deficits were excluded.

Lentiviral vector

Lentiviral vectors expressing SPRR1A (LV-SPRR1A) or control vectors (LV-NC) were obtained from BrainVTA (Wuhan, China). The viral titer of LV-SPRR1A was 2 × 10ˆ8 TU/mL. HT22 cells were transduced at an MOI of 100 for 24 h. After transduction, the cells were maintained in complete medium containing 5 μg/mL puromycin. GFP fluorescence was used to confirm successful transfection, and stable overexpression was validated via qRT‒PCR and Western blotting.

Self-complementary adeno-associated Virus (scAAV) vectors

In vivo SPRR1A overexpression was achieved via the use of scAAV vectors (BrainVTA, Wuhan, China). The mice were intrathecally injected with scAAV-hSyn-SPRR1A-mCherry-WPREs (5 × 10ˆ12 TU/mL, 5 μL), while scAAV-hSyn-mCherry-WPREs served as controls. The use of the hSyn promoter ensured neuron-specific expression of SPRR1A, as confirmed by its colocalization with NeuN in immunofluorescence analysis. The injection procedures were as described for siRNA delivery.

Drug administration

Ephrin-A5 Fc and the TrkB inhibitor ANA-12 were purchased from MCE. Ephrin-A5 Fc was dissolved in saline, and ANA-12 was dissolved in 1% DMSO. Dosing regimens were determined on the basis of preliminary experiments and are detailed in the Results section and Figure legends.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Frozen coronal spinal cord sections and HT22 cells on coverslips were processed for IF. After fixation and dehydration, the tissues were sectioned using a Leica CM1950 cryostat. The samples were treated with blocking solution at room temperature for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: SPRR1A (Signalway Antibody; 30069; 1:200), NeuN (Proteintech; 26975; 1:200), GFAP (CST; 80788; 1:200), Iba1 (Novus; NB100-1028; 1:200), and ephrin-A5 (Affinity; AF0560; 1:200). After washing, the sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488/555-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Images were captured via a Nikon fluorescence microscope.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) combined with IF

To identify the neuronal subtypes expressing SPRR1A in the spinal dorsal horn, FISH was performed using probes against Vglut2 and GAD67 using a Fluorescence in situ hybridization Kit (PinpoRNA, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Vglut2 and GAD67 probes were designed and synthesized by PinpoRNA (China). Following completion of the hybridization procedure, tissue sections were subjected to immunofluorescence staining for SPRR1A. Sections were incubated with anti-SPRR1A primary antibody (Signalway Antibody; 30069; 1:200) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the appropriate fluorescent secondary antibody. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence signals were visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Colocalization analysis was performed in lamina II of the spinal dorsal horn. The proportions of SPRR1A-positive neurons expressing Vglut2 or GAD67 were quantified from multiple sections obtained from independent animals. For each animal, three to five non-overlapping sections containing the ipsilateral dorsal horn were analysed, and the percentage of Vglut2-or GAD67-positive cells among total SPRR1A-positive neurons was calculated.

Western blotting

Proteins extracted from L4–L6 spinal dorsal horn tissue or HT22 cells were separated via SDS–PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: SPRR1A (Signalway Antibody; 30069; 1:1000), ephrin-A5 (Affinity; AF0560; 1:1000), BDNF (Zenbio; 381133; 1:1000), TrkB (Abcam; ab187041; 1:5000) and β-Tubulin (Bioss; bsm-33034 M; 1:5000). After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, the bands were detected via Super ECL reagents (Millipore) and quantified via Image Lab 4.1 (Bio-Rad).

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Spinal dorsal horn tissue and HT22 cells were lysed in lysis buffer (Servicebio, G2038). A Pierce Spin Column was inserted into a microcentrifuge tube, and AminoLink Plus Coupling Resin together with an affinity-purified antibody against ephrin-A5 (Santa Cruz; sc-81945; 5 μg), BDNF (CST; 16696; 5 μg) or control IgG was added. The mixture was rotated at room temperature for 120 min to immobilize the antibodies. The lysates were applied to the resin columns and incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle rocking. After centrifugation, the spin columns were transferred to new collection tubes, elution buffer was added, and the flow-through was recovered by centrifugation. The immune complexes in the eluate were analyzed via Western blotting using ephrin-A5 (Santa Cruz; sc-81945; 1:1000), BDNF (CST; 16696; 1:1000), TrkB (ABclonal; A2099; 1:1000) and β-Tubulin (Bioss; bsm-33034 M; 1:5000) antibodies.

Whole-cell patch-clamp recording in spinal cord slices

The mice were deeply anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium and transcardially perfused with preoxygenated ice-cold sucrose-based ACSF (80 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 25 mM NaHCO3, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 0.5 mM CaCl2, 3.5 mM MgCl2, 75 mM d-sucrose, 1.3 mM sodium ascorbate, and 3 mM sodium pyruvate). The L4–L6 spinal segments were embedded in agarose and sectioned (300 μm) on a vibratome (Leica). Slices were incubated in a recording solution (120 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 26 mM NaHCO3, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 2 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2 and 25 mM d-glucose) at 32 °C for 30 min and then maintained at room temperature for at least 30 min before recording.

Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were performed on lamina II neurons of the spinal dorsal horn at 37 °C. Lamina II was visually identified under a differential interference contrast (DIC) microscope as a distinctive translucent band situated between the more opaque Lamina I and Lamina III. To ensure consistency, cells were selected from the inner or outer part of Lamina II where the dense population of interneurons is most responsive to peripheral nerve injury. The recording pipettes had a resistance of 5–8 MΩ when filled with an internal solution containing 120 mM K-gluconate, 2 mM MgCl2, 20 mM KCl, 2 mM Na2-ATP, 0.5 mM Na2-GTP, 20 mM HEPES, and 0.5 mM EGTA (pH 7.3, 290–310 mOsm). Membrane currents and action potential (AP) firing were acquired via a MultiClamp 700B amplifier (Axon Instruments), filtered at 2 kHz, and digitized at 10 kHz via a Digidata 1440A digitizer (Axon Instruments). Only recordings with a seal resistance >2 GΩ and access resistance <35 MΩ were included. In the presence of 100 μM picrotoxin (Tocris) and 1 μM strychnine (Sigma-Aldrich), spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs) were recorded to block GABA and glycine-mediated inhibitory currents. Neuronal excitability was assessed via a step protocol of depolarizing currents (40 pA steps from 40 pA to 120 pA for 500 ms) to evaluate the AP firing frequency. The relationship between the frequency and injection current was analyzed via Clampfit 10.7.

Statistical analysis

The data are expressed as the mean ± SD. Sample sizes were based on prior behavioral and molecular studies to minimize animal use. For the behavioral and time-course experiments, two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post-hoc correction was used. For other data, we employed one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post-hoc correction to determine the differences among groups, and a two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test was used for two-group comparisons. All analyses were conducted via GraphPad Prism 10.0, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Nerve injury induces SPRR1A upregulation in the spinal dorsal horn

Transcriptomic profiling was carried out on lumbar spinal dorsal horn (L4–L6) tissues from the SNI and sham groups to investigate gene expression variations induced by nerve injury. We analyzed different expression profiles between the SNI and sham groups and found that 41 mRNAs were significantly upregulated in the SNI group, with SPRR1A showing the greatest upregulation (Fig. 1A). To confirm the successful establishment of the SNI model, behavioral assessments were conducted at baseline and on each postoperative day. In agreement with previous reports, a mouse model of SNI exhibited a significant and sustained decrease in the paw withdrawal threshold and paw withdrawal latency, suggesting strong mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia (Fig. 1B and C). After behavioral testing, qRT‒PCR analysis further confirmed that the mRNA level of SPRR1A was upregulated nearly 40-fold in the spinal dorsal horn of SNI mice compared with that in the sham group (Fig. 1D), suggesting that SPRR1A may represent a key transcriptional response to peripheral nerve injury. To define the temporal dynamics of SPRR1A expression, qRT‒PCR and Western blot analyses were conducted across multiple postoperative time points. SPRR1A mRNA and protein levels were significantly increased on postoperative day 3 and increased until day 21, which was strongly correlated with the time course of pain hypersensitivity (Fig. 1E–G). This persistent upregulation suggested that the upregulation of SPRR1A might be a sustained molecular event during the process of neuropathic pain development.

Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that the expression of SPRR1A was significantly greater in the ipsilateral dorsal horns of SNI mice than in the contralateral horns (Fig. S1A). Quantitative fluorescence analysis revealed that the SPRR1A intensity progressively increased from postoperative days 3–21 and that the increase in SPRR1A intensity strongly correlated with the behavioral pain trajectory (Fig. S1B and C). Double immunolabelling with NeuN, GFAP and Iba1 revealed that SPRR1A was expressed mainly in NeuN-positive neurons and was scarcely colocalized in astrocytes or microglia (Fig. 1H and I). To further characterize the neuronal subtype expressing SPRR1A in lamina II of the spinal dorsal horn, we performed RNAscope FISH for Vglut2 and GAD67 combined with SPRR1A immunofluorescence. Confocal imaging revealed that SPRR1A-positive neurons largely colocalized with Vglut2 (excitatory marker, 85.6 ± 4.1%), while only a small fraction colocalized with GAD67 (inhibitory marker, 5.6 ± 2.5%), indicating that SPRR1A is predominantly expressed in excitatory glutamatergic neurons (Fig. 1J and K). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that nerve injury induces sustained and neuron-specific upregulation of SPRR1A in the spinal dorsal horn.

SPRR1A amplifies dorsal horn neuronal excitability and pain-like hypersensitivity

To investigate whether increased SPRR1A expression is sufficient to induce neuropathic pain–like phenotypes, we overexpressed SPRR1A via the intrathecal delivery of scAAV-hSyn-SPRR1A-mCherry into the spinal cord of naïve mice, with scAAV-hSyn-mCherry serving as a control (Fig. 2A). Compared with control mice, SPRR1A-overexpressing mice presented a sustained and significant decrease in paw withdrawal threshold and paw withdrawal latency starting at 10 days post-infusion, suggesting the development of mechanical allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia (Fig. 2B and C). These behavioral changes persisted for at least 21 days, suggesting that SPRR1A overexpression alone is sufficient to induce persistent pain hypersensitivity.

To examine the underlying neuronal mechanisms, we performed whole-cell patch-clamp recordings on Lamina II neurons, a primary integration site for nociceptive inputs. Compared with control neurons, SPRR1A-overexpressing neurons presented a marked increase in the action potential (AP) firing number in response to depolarizing current steps (Fig. 2D and E), as well as a significant increase in both the frequency and amplitude of spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents (sEPSCs) (Fig. 2F–H). These electrophysiological properties indicate that the intrinsic excitability of SPRR1A-overexpressing neurons is significantly increased. Expression of Cherry fluorescent protein in the spinal dorsal horn was detected 10 days after intrathecal scAAV injection (Fig. S2A). Western blot analyze demonstrated that SPRR1A protein levels were still significantly increased from day 10 to day 21 postinjection (Fig. S2B and D), immunofluorescence analysis also demonstrated significant upregulation of SPRR1A in the dorsal horns of SPRR1A-overexpressing mice (Fig. 2I and J), while the expression of SPRR1A protein in the control groups did not show any significant change (Fig. S2C). Double immunolabelling with NeuN, GFAP and Iba1 revealed that SPRR1A was increased in NeuN-positive neurons and was scarcely colocalized in astrocytes or microglia (Fig. S2E). Together, these results demonstrate that neuronal SPRR1A overexpression drives neuronal excitability, resulting in pain-like hypersensitivity even in the absence of peripheral nerve injury.

SPRR1A drives dorsal horn neuronal excitability and neuropathic pain

To determine whether elevated SPRR1A expression is necessary for neuropathic pain and spinal dorsal horn neuronal excitability, we evaluated the effects of SPRR1A knockdown in the SNI model. The mice received intrathecal injections of SPRR1A siRNA (1 nmol in 10 μl, once daily for five consecutive days) either one or seven days after SNI surgery. Behavioral testing revealed that SPRR1A knockdown strongly ameliorated SNI-induced mechanical and thermal hypersensitivity. Specifically, compared with the scramble siRNA, the SPRR1A siRNA significantly ameliorated the SNI-induced decreases in paw withdrawal threshold and paw withdrawal latency from day 3 to day 9 after SNI (Fig. 3A and B). On day 7 after SNI, SPRR1A siRNA promoted recovery of the paw withdrawal threshold and paw withdrawal latency beginning on day 9, with effects persisting through day 13 (Fig. 3C and D). These findings demonstrate that the inhibition of SPRR1A effectively alleviates both the early and sustained phases of neuropathic pain.

Electrophysiological recordings from lamina II neurons further revealed that SNI markedly increased the number of AP firings and the frequency and amplitude of sEPSCs compared with those in the sham group. Notably, these SNI-induced enhancements in excitatory synaptic activity were significantly reduced by SPRR1A siRNA treatment (Fig. 3E–I). Western blot analysis confirmed a sustained decrease in the SPRR1A protein level after siRNA treatment (Fig. 3J). In summary, our results demonstrate that SPRR1A is required for the maintenance of neuropathic pain, suggesting that SPRR1A may be a potential target for modulating chronic pain.

SPRR1A-mediated ephrin-A5 downregulation contributes to neuropathic pain

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which SPRR1A contributes to neuropathic pain, we conducted integrated bioinformatics and experimental analyses. STRING-based protein–protein interaction (PPI) mapping identified ephrin-A5 as a putative SPRR1A-interacting partner (Fig. S3A). Co-expression analysis of the RDDC database also revealed that the expression of SPRR1A and ephrin-A5 was highly correlated (Fig. S3B). Western blot analysis revealed that ephrin-A5 protein levels markedly decreased from day 3 after SNI and remained persistently low through day 21(Fig. 4A and B), suggesting that ephrin-A5 was constantly suppressed during pain maintenance. To determine the functional role of ephrin-A5 in neuropathic pain, we intrathecally injected different doses of recombinant ephrin-A5 Fc protein into SNI model mice on day 7 after SNI surgery. Unlike the vehicle group, the ephrin-A5 Fc protein induced rapid and dose-dependent analgesic effects, as shown by the paw withdrawal threshold and paw withdrawal latency, which significantly increased within 1 h after injection. The degree and duration of the analgesic effect of ephrin-A5 Fc were positively correlated with the dose, and 2.0 μg of ephrin-A5 Fc had a significantly greater effect and longer duration (Fig. 4C and D). Western blot analysis revealed that ephrin-A5 protein levels were markedly increased after the ephrin-A5 Fc injection (Fig. 4E and F).

Interestingly, we found that intrathecally administered SPRR1A siRNA strongly rescued the expression of ephrin-A5 (Fig. 4G and H), indicating that the downregulation of ephrin-A5 was dependent on SPRR1A. Co-IP assays also demonstrated that SPRR1A interacted with ephrin-A5 (Fig. 4I). Double immunofluorescence staining revealed that SPRR1A and ephrin-A5 were colocalized in the spinal dorsal horn (Fig. 4J). Taken together, these results suggest that SPRR1A directly regulates the expression of ephrin-A5 signaling in the spinal dorsal horn after nerve injury.

To further prove that SPRR1A-induced hyperalgesia is mediated by ephrin-A5 suppression, we conducted two complementary experiments. In naïve mice overexpressing SPRR1A, the intrathecal administration of ephrin-A5 Fc (2 μg, three times daily from day 10 post-injection) significantly reversed the SPRR1A-induced decreases in paw withdrawal threshold and paw withdrawal latency beginning on day 11 (Fig. 4K–M). Conversely, in SNI mice treated with SPRR1A siRNA, the administration of ephrin-A5 siRNA (from day 3 post-surgery) significantly diminished the analgesic effects of SPRR1A knockdown from day 5 to day 9 (Fig. 4N–P). Collectively, our results demonstrated that ephrin-A5 is an important downstream effector of SPRR1A involved in neuropathic pain, and the restoration of ephrin-A5 function could effectively alleviate hyperalgesia. Therefore, the SPRR1A–ephrin-A5 axis is an important molecular mechanism regulating maladaptive plasticity in neuropathic pain and represents a potential target for the treatment of this disease.

Ephrin-A5 attenuates SPRR1A-driven neuropathic pain via competitive inhibition of BDNF/TrkB signaling

To further delineate the downstream mechanism by which ephrin-A5 mediates the analgesic effect of SPRR1A knockdown, we investigated the involvement of the BDNF/TrkB signaling pathway, a well-established contributor to central sensitization in neuropathic pain. Western blot analysis revealed that the expression of BDNF in the spinal dorsal horn was significantly increased on day 3 after SNI and remained elevated through day 21 (Fig. 5A and B), indicating sustained activation of the BDNF pathway during the progression of neuropathic pain. However, the total protein level of TrkB did not change (Fig. 5A and C), suggesting that nociceptive sensitization may be induced by changes in ligand–receptor interactions instead of changes in the expression level of TrkB. Notably, although SPRR1A siRNA significantly rescued the expression of ephrin-A5, the total protein levels of BDNF and TrkB did not change (Fig. 5D–F), prompting us to evaluate whether SPRR1A knockdown affects protein–protein interactions involving TrkB. In previous reports, ephrin-A5 was reported to act as an endogenous competitive inhibitor of the TrkB ligand. In the present study, we found that ephrin-A5 directly interacted with TrkB in the spinal dorsal horn (Fig. 5G). Notably, SPRR1A knockdown significantly enhanced the interaction between ephrin-A5 and TrkB (Fig. 5H), while reducing the binding of BDNF to TrkB (Fig. 5I and J). These results indicate that increased ephrin-A5 expression competitively inhibits BDNF–TrkB interactions, thereby attenuating pathological activation of the BDNF/TrkB signaling pathway.

To validate the mechanism in vivo, we performed a pharmacological rescue experiment (Fig. 5K). As shown above, the analgesic effect of SPRR1A siRNA was partially reversed by ephrin-A5 siRNA, suggesting that ephrin-A5 was required for the analgesic effect of SPRR1A siRNA. We further explored whether SPRR1A knockdown affects TrkB signaling in neuropathic pain. Compared with vehicle control injections, intraperitoneal injection of the TrkB inhibitor, ANA-12 (0.5 mg/kg, once daily for five consecutive days) beginning 5 days after SNI surgery significantly restored both the paw withdrawal threshold and paw withdrawal latency from day 7 to day 9 (Fig. 5L and M), supporting the conclusion that suppression of TrkB signaling is sufficient to rescue neuropathic hypersensitivity. Together, these findings demonstrate that SPRR1A promotes central sensitization by downregulating ephrin-A5, thereby facilitating pathological activation of the BDNF/TrkB pathway. Conversely, upregulating the expression of ephrin-A5 competitively inhibited the binding of BDNF to TrkB, further inhibiting TrkB signaling and alleviating neuropathic pain.

SPRR1A downregulates ephrin-A5 to activate BDNF/TrkB signaling in vitro

To validate the underlying mechanism in vitro, we transduced the HT22 mouse neuronal cell line with a lentivirus designed to overexpress SPRR1A and subsequently assessed alterations in the ephrin-A5 and BDNF/TrkB signaling pathways. The efficiency of SPRR1A overexpression mediated by LV-SPRR1A was confirmed in HT22 cells. Green fluorescent protein (GFP) expression was observed 72 h post-transduction in both the LV-NC and LV-SPRR1A groups (Fig. 6A), indicating successful viral transduction. Double immunofluorescence staining revealed that SPRR1A and ephrin-A5 were colocalized in HT22 cells; however, following SPRR1A overexpression, the colocalization of SPRR1A with ephrin-A5 was reduced (Fig. 6B). Quantitative analyses demonstrated a significant increase in both mRNA and protein levels of SPRR1A, accompanied by a marked decrease in the mRNA and protein expression of ephrin-A5 (Fig. 6C–G). Notably, SPRR1A overexpression significantly attenuated the interaction between ephrin-A5 and TrkB (Fig. 6H and I), while enhancing the binding affinity of BDNF to TrkB (Fig. 6J and K). These findings further support the role of SPRR1A as a negative regulator of ephrin-A5 expression and highlight its indirect facilitatory effect on BDNF/TrkB signaling through suppression of ephrin-A5.

Discussion

In this study, we identified unexpected upregulation of a previously unidentified molecular pathway involving SPRR1A, ephrin-A5, and BDNF/TrkB signaling in the spinal dorsal horn, shedding light on the regulation of neuropathic pain. We demonstrated that SPRR1A upregulation resulted in the suppression of ephrin-A5, which otherwise negatively regulates BDNF/TrkB signaling. By downregulating ephrin-A5, SPRR1A promoted the activation of BDNF/TrkB signaling, which led to increased neuronal excitability and pain hypersensitivity (Fig. 7). We identified SPRR1A as a key regulator of pain pathways, with potential therapeutic implications for targeting the SPRR1A–ephrin-A5–BDNF/TrkB axis in the treatment of neuropathic pain.

Schematic diagram of the proposed mechanism of neuropathic pain regulation by SPRR1A.Following peripheral nerve injury in mice, upregulated SPRR1A in the spinal dorsal horn interacts with ephrin-A5, leading to downregulation of ephrin-A5 expression. This reduction decreases ephrin-A5 binding to the TrkB receptor while enhancing BDNF binding to TrkB, thereby activating the BDNF/TrkB signaling pathway. The subsequent activation of this pathway contributes to the development of neuropathic pain. SPRR1A, small proline-rich repeat protein 1A; BDNF, brain-derived neurotrophic factor; TrkB, tropomyosin receptor kinase B.

Our study extends previous observations that neuronal injury and axonal regeneration induce SPRR1A upregulation in the DRG and spinal cord to show that SPRR1A upregulation occurs in the spinal dorsal horn in the context of neuropathic pain. Previous studies have shown that SPRR1A is upregulated in the DRGs and spinal cords of animal models of neuropathic pain [15,17]; however, it remains unknown whether SPRR1A upregulation confers any phenotypes related to central sensitization and neuropathic pain. Here, we observed that SPRR1A expression was robustly upregulated in the spinal dorsal horn after SNI and that this upregulation correlated with behavioral pain hypersensitivity and synaptic changes in spinal neurons. Our choice to record from lamina II neurons was based on the well-established role of this region as a critical site for the integration and modulation of nociceptive transmission [36]. We acknowledge that lamina II contains a heterogeneous population of both excitatory and inhibitory interneurons with distinct intrinsic firing properties and functional roles in sensory processing [37]. Therefore, the electrophysiological alterations observed in randomly recorded lamina II neurons should not be interpreted as reflecting a uniform neuronal population. To further clarify the cellular identity of SPRR1A-expressing neurons, we performed additional RNAscope FISH combined with immunofluorescence analysis. These experiments showed that SPRR1A was predominantly colocalized with the excitatory neuronal marker Vglut2, whereas little colocalization was detected with the inhibitory marker GAD67, indicating preferential expression of SPRR1A in excitatory glutamatergic neurons. This molecular evidence supports the interpretation that the enhanced firing frequency and increased excitatory synaptic transmission observed following SPRR1A upregulation are primarily attributable to altered excitatory dorsal horn circuitry. Nevertheless, given the cellular complexity of lamina II, future studies using genetically targeted recordings to characterize the distinct electrophysiological firing patterns of SPRR1A-positive neuronal subpopulations will be essential to further refine our understanding of their precise roles in spinal pain circuits.

Another major finding of this study is the identification of ephrin-A5 as an analgesic molecule and a critical regulator of BDNF/TrkB signaling in the context of neuropathic pain. Previous studies have demonstrated that ephrin-A5 is involved in many physiological processes [[38], [39], [40], [41]]; however, its expression in the spinal dorsal horn and its role in pain signaling are not well understood. We found that ephrin-A5 was significantly downregulated in the spinal dorsal horns of SNI mice. Intrathecal administration of recombinant ephrin-A5 markedly alleviated hyperalgesia, indicating a correlation between ephrin-A5 downregulation and the severity of neuropathic pain. Our findings suggest that ephrin-A5 may represent a novel analgesic agent not previously reported in the literature. BDNF is a key neural regulatory factor involved in pain signal transmission [42]. On the one hand, it contributes to the activation of peripheral nociceptors and the relay of pain signals to the somatosensory cortex; on the other hand, it promotes the initiation and maintenance of pain by modulating pain perception processing and synaptic plasticity in spinal dorsal horn neurons [[43], [44], [45]]. In the present study, we observed that the BDNF/TrkB signaling pathway was activated in the spinal dorsal horn of SNI mice. Importantly, we found that ephrin-A5 competes with BDNF for binding to the TrkB receptor and thereby negatively regulates the BDNF/TrkB signaling pathway, which is pivotal for central sensitization and pain processing. We further showed that the SPRR1A-mediated downregulation of ephrin-A5 enhanced BDNF/TrkB signaling, thereby amplifying nociceptive signaling in the spinal dorsal horn. This finding highlights that ephrin-A5 may be an important molecular player in the modulation of pain pathways and suggests that restoring ephrin-A5 function may be a promising approach for treating neuropathic pain.

Our results provide novel insights into the regulatory relationship between SPRR1A and ephrin-A5. In the spinal dorsal horn of SNI mice, SPRR1A was upregulated, whereas ephrin-A5 was downregulated. SPRR1A upregulation led to ephrin-A5 downregulation, and knockdown of SPRR1A rescued ephrin-A5 expression in the spinal dorsal horn. Co-IP and immunofluorescence results demonstrated that SPRR1A directly interacts with ephrin-A5, a finding confirmed by both in vivo and in vitro experiments showing that SPRR1A negatively regulates ephrin-A5 expression. Furthermore, pharmacological rescue experiments using ephrin-A5 Fc and siRNA revealed that SPRR1A exerts its regulatory effect on neuropathic pain by downregulating ephrin-A5. This SPRR1A–ephrin-A5 axis represents a previously unidentified mechanism linking neuronal injury responses to pain.

Furthermore, the implications of the SPRR1A–ephrin-A5–BDNF/TrkB axis likely extend beyond intrinsic neuronal excitability to the broader neuroinflammatory microenvironment in the spinal dorsal horn. Central sensitization is not only a neuronal phenomenon but is also driven by reciprocal communication between neurons and non-neuronal cells [46]. BDNF, a key downstream effector in our identified pathway, is well-documented as a critical mediator of neuron-glia crosstalk [47]. Upon its enhanced release and signaling facilitated by SPRR1A upregulation, BDNF can act as a potent paracrine signal that triggers the activation of neighboring microglia and astrocytes. Activated microglia and astrocytes, in turn, release a cascade of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, which further sensitize nociceptive neurons and sustain a state of chronic neuroinflammation [48]. By demonstrating that SPRR1A serves as a molecular gatekeeper for BDNF/TrkB activation, our findings suggest that neuronal SPRR1A may act as an upstream “master switch” that initiates or amplifies the neuroinflammatory response following peripheral nerve injury. Thus, targeting the SPRR1A-mediated axis may offer a dual therapeutic benefit: direct attenuation of neuronal hyperexcitability and indirect suppression of the persistent glia-mediated inflammatory cascade in the spinal dorsal horn.

Although we gained valuable insights from our study, there are several limitations that should be considered in future studies. First, we provide strong evidence that SPRR1A and ephrin-A5 are involved in neuropathic pain; however, the specific molecular mechanisms by which SPRR1A downregulates ephrin-A5 remain unknown. It will be interesting to investigate whether SPRR1A regulates ephrin-A5 expression at the transcriptional level or whether it downregulates ephrin-A5 expression at the posttranslational level. Identifying the molecular mechanisms by which SPRR1A downregulates ephrin-A5 expression could reveal other potential targets for the treatment of pain. Second, we used pharmacological modulation and siRNA-mediated knockdown to investigate the involvement of SPRR1A in neuropathic pain. While these approaches are very effective strategies in the experimental design of this research, they do not specifically manipulate neuronal-expressed SPRR1A, which would help define its role more precisely in spinal neurons. Future studies using neuron-specific knockout models will help determine the specific role of neuronal-expressed SPRR1A in central sensitization and neuropathic pain. Finally, this study was performed exclusively in male mice, and this should be considered when interpreting the present findings. Previous studies have demonstrated sex-dependent differences in spinal pain processing [49,50], particularly in microglia-dependent BDNF/TrkB signaling mechanisms within the dorsal horn [51]. For example, microglial BDNF signaling has been shown to play a more prominent role in neuropathic pain hypersensitivity in male rodents, whereas alternative immune-related mechanisms may contribute in females [52]. Given that BDNF/TrkB signaling represents a key downstream component of the SPRR1A–ephrin-A5 pathway identified in this study, it remains unclear whether the same regulatory mechanism operates similarly in females. Future studies including female animals will therefore be important to determine whether SPRR1A-dependent modulation of ephrin-A5/TrkB signaling exhibits sex-specific characteristics and to further evaluate the generalizability of these findings.

In summary, our study reveals a novel molecular pathway by which SPRR1A modulates neuropathic pain by downregulating ephrin-A5 and activating the BDNF/TrkB signaling axis. By connecting these molecular mechanisms, our findings provide new insights into the pathophysiology of neuropathic pain and suggest that targeting the SPRR1A-ephrin-A5-BDNF/TrkB axis may provide an effective therapeutic strategy for the management of chronic pain.
