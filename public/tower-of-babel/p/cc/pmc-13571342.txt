Case Summary

A 96-year-old woman with recent minor ischemic strokes was transferred after recurrent syncope, abdominal pain, hypoxia, and shock requiring vasopressor support. On arrival, blood pressure was 76/50 mm Hg and oxygen saturation was 87% on room air. High-sensitivity troponin I was 1,191 ng/L and B-type natriuretic peptide was 1,410 pg/mL, consistent with right ventricular strain.Take-Home Messages•Right atrial CIT may contribute to hemodynamic compromise in acute pulmonary embolism through dynamic tricuspid inflow obstruction in addition to pulmonary embolism–related right ventricular pressure overload.•A staged, physiology-guided approach prioritizing selective right atrial thrombectomy may provide rapid hemodynamic stabilization while reserving pulmonary artery thrombectomy for persistent or recurrent instability.•This case demonstrates that carefully selected catheter-directed therapy may be feasible even in very old patients when individualized to clinical status, procedural risk, and treatment response.

Abdominal/pelvic computed tomography obtained during evaluation of abdominal pain incidentally demonstrated pulmonary embolism, right-sided chamber enlargement, hepatic venous contrast reflux, and a right atrial filling defect concerning for clot-in-transit (CIT) (Figures 1A and 1B). Because the pulmonary arteries were only partially visualized, the overall embolic burden could not be fully characterized. Dedicated chest computed tomographic angiography was deferred because of chronic kidney disease and acute kidney injury.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated right ventricular dilation and dysfunction with McConnell's sign and an estimated right ventricular systolic pressure of 30 mm Hg. A large, mobile right atrial thrombus intermittently prolapsed across the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle, suggesting dynamic impairment of right ventricular inflow in addition to pulmonary embolism–related right ventricular pressure overload (Figures 1C and 1D).1

Initially, the procedural strategy was to prioritize removal of the proximal right atrial CIT because of its mobility, embolic risk, and potential contribution to hemodynamic compromise. A staged approach was planned, with reassessment of hemodynamic status after clot removal and escalation to pulmonary artery thrombectomy only if persistent hypotension, ongoing shock, or clinical instability remained.

Through right femoral venous access, a 24-F large-bore aspiration thrombectomy system was advanced into the right atrium. A single aspiration pass successfully extracted a 15-cm thrombus, with complete removal confirmed by intraprocedural echocardiography (Figure 1E). Following thrombectomy, the patient's blood pressure improved immediately, and vasopressor requirements rapidly resolved (Figure 1F).2

Given the prompt hemodynamic recovery after right atrial clot aspiration, pulmonary artery thrombectomy was deferred in favor of therapeutic anticoagulation and close intensive care unit monitoring. Pulmonary artery intervention remained available as an escalation strategy should recurrent instability or progressive right ventricular failure occur.3

The patient remained clinically stable, vasopressors were discontinued, and she was discharged on apixaban.
