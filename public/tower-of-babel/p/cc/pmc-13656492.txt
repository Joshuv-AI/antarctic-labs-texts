Kinesiophobia, proprioception, and functional status after total knee arthroplasty: the mediating role of deep flexion joint position error
Authors: Simay Aksakallı, Burak Ünal, Ahmet Alperen Öztürk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveKinesiophobia is known to adversely affect functional recovery following total knee arthroplasty (TKA); however, its relationship with the proprioceptive system has not yet been sufficiently elucidated. The aim of this study was to examine the associations between kinesiophobia and pain, functional performance, lower limb functional performance, and proprioception following TKA, and to evaluate the potential mediating role of proprioception in the effect of kinesiophobia on functional outcomes.MethodsThis cross-sectional observational study included a total of 40 patients (37 female, 3 male; mean age: 66.0 ± 7.48 years) who had undergone TKA for knee osteoarthritis and were at least 3 months post-surgery. Kinesiophobia was assessed using the Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia (TSK). Pain was evaluated with the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), functional status with the WOMAC Osteoarthritis Index, and lower limb functional performance with the Five Times Sit-to-Stand Test (FTSST). Proprioception was analyzed through joint position error (JPE) measured at 30° and 60° of knee flexion using a digital goniometer. Statistical analyses included correlation analysis, Steiger’s t-test, hierarchical regression, and mediation analysis.ResultsSignificant positive correlations were found between TSK scores and WOMAC (ρ = 0.559, p < 0.001), FTSST (ρ = 0.491, p = 0.001), and JPE at 60 degrees (ρ = 0.482, p = 0.002). No significant association was observed with JPE at 30 degrees (ρ = 0.215, p = 0.183), though the correlation at 60 degrees was significantly stronger (p = 0.046). Hierarchical regression identified FTSST, movement pain, and JPE at 60 degrees as the primary predictors of functional status. Mediation analysis demonstrated that JPE at 60 degrees exerted a significant indirect effect on the relationship between kinesiophobia and functional status (B = 0.453, 95% CI [0.130, 0.994]).ConclusionIn patients post-TKA, kinesiophobia is associated with pain, lower limb functional performance, and increased joint position error, particularly at 60° of flexion. Mediation analysis indicated that joint position error at 60° statistically mediated the association between kinesiophobia and functional limitation, suggesting fear of movement relates to functional outcomes partly through proprioceptive accuracy. Integrating proprioceptive training targeting deep flexion angles and cognitive-behavioral strategies into rehabilitation programs may optimize functional recovery, though prospective studies are needed to confirm these exploratory findings.Keywords: Kinesiophobia, Total knee arthroplasty, Proprioception, Functional status, Joint position error

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most prevalent joint disease worldwide, affecting over 240 million individuals and representing a leading cause of functional disability among adults [1]. Knee osteoarthritis (KOA), one of the most common forms of OA, is characterized by pain, restricted range of motion, diminished proprioception, and joint deformity, all of which may substantially impair quality of life [2, 3].

In patients with advanced-stage KOA who are unresponsive to conservative treatment, total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is regarded as the gold-standard surgical intervention. Although the majority of patients experience reductions in pain severity, improvements in knee joint range of motion, and enhanced quality of life following TKA, persistent pain and functional limitations may continue in a subset of patients [4, 5].

During the early postoperative period after TKA, patients may exhibit movement avoidance behaviors due to heightened pain levels, diminished muscle strength, and functional limitations [6]. In TKA patients, kinesiophobia is considered a maladaptive response that triggers avoidance of physical activity driven by pain-related fear. Although this response may represent a natural physiological reaction during the early postoperative phase, it has also been associated with the chronification of acute pain and a decline in health-related quality of life [7, 8]. Advanced age, lower educational attainment, and maladaptive coping strategies have been identified as factors associated with the development of postoperative kinesiophobia [9].

During the acute postoperative phase following TKA, kinesiophobia is highly prevalent and may compromise early recovery. High levels of kinesiophobia have been reported in 55.8% of patients at hospital discharge, correlating with reduced functional capacity and an increased risk of falls [10]. Several clinical investigations have underscored the substantial impact of kinesiophobia on TKA outcomes across different recovery phases. Systematically, kinesiophobia has been shown to negatively influence postoperative functional status [7], with its adverse effects on acute pain perception and the recovery trajectory persisting from long-term follow-up [11], back to the initial hospitalization period [12].

Furthermore, even in the late postoperative phase, high levels of kinesiophobia remain closely associated with diminished functional performance, pain severity, and functional parameters such as the sit-to-stand test [13].

A review of the literature suggests that although the associations between kinesiophobia, pain, and functional parameters following TKA have been investigated, their potential relationship with proprioception remains to be fully elucidated. In light of this gap, the primary objective of the present study was to investigate the association between kinesiophobia and functional status, and to explore the potential mediating role of proprioception in this relationship. As secondary objectives, we aimed to examine the associations between kinesiophobia, pain, lower limb functional performance, and joint position error.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was designed as a cross-sectional observational investigation. The research was conducted between December 1, 2025, and March 1, 2026, among patients who had previously undergone TKA and presented to the Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation and Orthopedics and Traumatology outpatient clinics of Ağrı Training and Research Hospital. The study was reviewed and approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Ağrı İbrahim Çeçen University (Decision No. 501, dated November 27, 2025). The research was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients who met the inclusion criteria were provided with verbal and written information regarding the purpose of the study and the assessment procedures to be performed, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Clinical and demographic data were recorded using standardized patient assessment forms.

Sample size

The sample size was determined through a priori power analysis using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7), based on the association between kinesiophobia and pain severity reported by Bakırhan et al. [13]. The calculation was based on the reported association between kinesiophobia and pain severity. Assuming a correlation coefficient of r = 0.489, a significance level of α = 0.05, and a statistical power of 1 − β = 0.80, a minimum of 28 patients was calculated as necessary for inclusion. To account for potential data loss, a total of 40 patients were enrolled in the study.

Recording of demographic and clinical data

Demographic and clinical data of the enrolled patients were recorded using standardized patient assessment forms. Documented demographic variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), time elapsed since surgery, and the side of the operated knee.

Kinesiophobia assessment

Kinesiophobia levels were assessed using the Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia (TSK). The TSK is a 17-item self-report questionnaire developed to measure fear of movement and re-injury. The scale comprises items evaluating fear of injury or re-injury and fear-avoidance behaviors. Higher scores indicate greater levels of kinesiophobia [14].

Pain assessment

Pain levels were measured using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). Pain intensity was evaluated separately at rest and during movement. The VAS is a widely used 10-cm scale for assessing pain severity, in which a score of 0 indicates no pain and a score of 10 represents the most severe pain [15].

Functional assessment

Functional status was assessed using the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC). The WOMAC is a widely used instrument in clinical studies, developed to evaluate symptoms associated with osteoarthritis. The index comprises 24 items across three subscales: pain, stiffness, and physical function. Higher WOMAC scores indicate increased pain and stiffness and decreased physical function [16].

Proprioception assessment

Proprioception was evaluated via knee joint position sense (JPS) at target angles of 30° and 60° flexion using a digital goniometer (1° precision). These specific target angles were selected based on the biomechanical properties of the knee joint and established protocols in osteoarthritis and arthroplasty research. The 30° angle represents early flexion, where joint stability is primarily provided by passive constraints such as capsular tension and prosthetic geometry. Conversely, the 60° angle evaluates proprioceptive function during deeper flexion, a phase that demands greater active neuromuscular control due to increased ligamentous laxity following anterior cruciate ligament resection [17, 18]. Patients were assessed in the supine position with their eyes closed. The goniometer’s center of rotation was aligned laterally with the knee joint axis, and its arms were taped parallel to the femoral and tibial shafts.

To minimize measurement error, the procedure was demonstrated twice at each angle before testing. Starting from full extension (goniometer zeroed), the target angle was verbally communicated, and the patient slowly flexed the knee until the target was reached. The patient maintained this position for 5 s to consolidate awareness. The average of the absolute deviation scores from three trials at each angle was recorded as the final JPS error [19–21].

While joint position sense assessment protocols vary, a systematic review in the literature examining the measurement properties of goniometric tracking methods supports their utility for evaluating knee joint position sense in clinical research [22].

Lower limb functional performance assessment

Lower limb functional performance was assessed using the Five Times Sit-to-Stand Test (FTSST). In this test, participants performed five consecutive sit-to-stand repetitions from a standard chair, and the time to completion was recorded using a stopwatch. Longer completion times indicate poorer functional performance [23].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Jamovi (v2.7.18), an R-based statistical software platform. The distribution of continuous variables was examined using the Shapiro–Wilk test, histograms, and Q–Q plots. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed data are reported as median (interquartile range [IQR]). Given that the primary variable, TSK, did not follow a normal distribution, the relationships between TSK and demographic and clinical parameters were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis, and effect sizes were interpreted according to Cohen’s classification (ρ = 0.10–0.30, small; 0.30–0.50, moderate; ≥0.50, large) [24].

TSK scores were compared according to the operated knee side using the Mann–Whitney U test. Differences between joint position error (JPE) values at 30° and 60° were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Additionally, the statistical significance of the difference between the correlation coefficients between kinesiophobia and JPE values at these two angles was evaluated using Steiger’s t-test.

Hierarchical regression analysis was conducted to identify factors predicting the WOMAC total score. To justify the use of this complex modeling in a relatively small cohort (n = 40), the model was constructed sequentially across four controlled steps to preserve degrees of freedom and carefully monitor changes in variance. Continuous independent variables were mean-centered, and variance inflation factor (VIF) values were verified. The model was constructed in four steps: In Step 1, age, BMI, and time elapsed since surgery were entered as control variables. In Step 2, VAS movement pain score and FTSST time were added. In Step 3, JPE at 60° flexion was included, and in Step 4, the TSK score was entered to complete the model. At each step, the change in total explained variance (ΔR²) and the statistical significance of this change (ΔF) were reported.

The mediating role of 60° joint position error (JPE 60°) in the relationship between TSK and WOMAC was tested using mediation analysis. To enhance the reliability of coefficient estimates and partially mitigate the limitations associated with the small sample size, in light of the limited sample size (n = 40), 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the Bias-Corrected and Accelerated (BCa) Bootstrap method with 5000 resamples in both the hierarchical regression and mediation analyses. While bootstrapping serves as a robust computational strategy to handle non-normality and control Type I error rates in small cohorts, it was treated as an exploratory/mitigating technique rather than a definitive resolution to sample size constraints. Effects were considered statistically significant when the confidence interval did not contain zero.

The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Analytical approach

The analyses followed a sequential, hypothesis-driven structure informed by the primary aim of the study. Spearman correlation analyses were conducted first to identify variables associated with kinesiophobia. Hierarchical regression analysis subsequently examined independent predictors of functional status, for which the WOMAC Osteoarthritis Index was selected as the primary outcome variable given its comprehensive assessment of pain, stiffness, and physical function in TKA populations. Finally, mediation analysis tested whether joint position error at 60° statistically mediated the relationship between kinesiophobia and functional status.

Discussion

In the present study, significant associations were identified between kinesiophobia and pain severity, functional status, lower limb functional performance, and joint position error at 60° of knee flexion in patients who had undergone TKA. The finding that the association between kinesiophobia and joint position error reached statistical significance specifically at the 60° flexion angle — but not at 30° — suggests that the relationship between kinesiophobia and proprioceptive accuracy, as measured by joint position error, may vary depending on joint angle. Although kinesiophobia did not emerge as a statistically significant independent predictor in the hierarchical regression analysis, mediation analysis revealed that joint position error at 60° of flexion exerted a statistically significant indirect effect within the relationship between kinesiophobia and functional status. These findings suggest that functional limitations following TKA may not be solely associated with pain severity, and that kinesiophobia may also be related to joint position accuracy.

The restrictive role of kinesiophobia on functional recovery following TKA has been emphasized in both comprehensive systematic reviews [7] and long-term prospective studies [11]. The strong association between kinesiophobia and functional status observed in our study is consistent with the current literature and aligns with the Fear-Avoidance Model. According to this model, the perception of pain as a threat triggers kinesiophobia, and the resultant fear amplifies avoidance behaviors, ultimately leading to functional impairment [25]. A key contribution of the present study to the existing literature lies in the observation that kinesiophobia was significantly associated not only with functional outcomes but also with joint position error — a specific component of proprioceptive function — suggesting that the relationship between kinesiophobia and functional limitation may extend beyond behavioral avoidance alone. It is well established that pain — or the anticipation of pain — induces reorganization of muscle activity, giving rise to sensorimotor adaptations aimed at protecting the affected tissue [26]. Our findings raise the possibility that kinesiophobia may be associated with reduced proprioceptive accuracy through such adaptive mechanisms, although the cross-sectional design of this study does not allow for the establishment of directionality or causality.

Another noteworthy finding of our study is that the association between kinesiophobia and joint position error varied as a function of joint flexion angle. Although no statistically significant difference was found between proprioceptive error magnitudes at 30° and 60°, the emergence of a significant correlation between kinesiophobia and joint position error exclusively at 60° of flexion — confirmed by Steiger’s t-test — suggests that the relationship between kinesiophobia and proprioceptive accuracy may become more pronounced at deeper flexion angles. The literature demonstrates that mechanoreceptors in muscles, tendons, and the joint capsule convert mechanical stimuli into afferent signals that contribute to proprioception, and that these inputs are processed by the central nervous system to shape sensorimotor responses [27]. When primary stabilization provided by the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is insufficient, the joint capsule and muscle spindles activate a secondary reflex arc that engages the hamstrings as an on-demand torque regulator [17]. The resection of the ACL during TKA suggests that secondary neuromuscular mechanisms may assume greater relative importance in maintaining dynamic stability. In this context, the observed angle-dependent dissociation between 30° and 60° may potentially be explained by the angle-specific biomechanical characteristics of the knee joint and the changes in stability that occur during the mid-flexion phase. Biomechanical studies have demonstrated that millimetric deviations in the anatomical positioning of the femoral component following TKA result in marked ligamentous laxity, particularly within the mid-flexion range. Accordingly, the passive mechanical stability provided by prosthetic component geometry and capsular tension at early flexion angles [18] may have masked the association between kinesiophobia and joint position error. In contrast, beyond the 40° flexion threshold — widely recognized in the literature as the boundary of maximum instability — the association between kinesiophobia and joint position error appeared to become more pronounced during the mid-flexion phase [17, 18], where the knee relies most heavily on active neuromuscular regulation rather than passive structures. These observations raise the hypothesis that kinesiophobia may be more closely related to proprioceptive accuracy at joint angles where passive stabilization is reduced and greater active neuromuscular control is required.

Despite the observed associations described above, kinesiophobia did not reach statistical significance as an independent predictor in the hierarchical regression model once other clinical variables were included (p = 0.071). This finding warrants caution in interpreting the independent contribution of kinesiophobia to functional outcomes. The dominant variables in our analysis — pain severity, lower limb functional performance, and joint position error at 60° — may have accounted for a large proportion of the variance, thereby attenuating the independent predictive contribution of kinesiophobia. Consistent with this, studies in the kinesiophobia literature have similarly reported that although kinesiophobia demonstrates robust associations with numerous factors in univariate analyses, statistical significance may be lost in multivariable regression models due to shared variance among correlated variables [28]. Furthermore, a close relationship between kinesiophobia and proprioceptive sensitivity of the knee joint has been reported in the literature [29], and the effect of kinesiophobia on proprioception and postural control has been described as extending beyond direct pathways, exhibiting partial mediation through variables such as increased pain severity and reduced functional mobility [30].

Consistent with this multidimensional interaction model, the mediation analysis conducted in the present study suggests that the association between kinesiophobia and functional outcomes may operate partly through an indirect statistical pathway involving joint position error, rather than through a direct association alone. Although the independent association between 60° joint position error and functional status did not reach the threshold of statistical significance in our mediation model (p = 0.062), contemporary mediation literature establishes that a significant indirect effect can exist—and is evaluated primarily based on bootstrap confidence intervals—even when individual path coefficients are not independently significant [31].

It should be noted, however, that mediation analysis in a cross-sectional design provides evidence for statistical mediation — that is, the pattern of associations observed is consistent with a mediational model — but does not establish causal mediation or temporal ordering.

The findings of our study suggest that rehabilitation following TKA may benefit from addressing not only mechanical parameters but also fear-related psychological factors. The observed association between kinesiophobia and increased joint position error at 60° of knee flexion — a deeper flexion angle where neuromuscular control demands are relatively higher — supports the potential importance of integrating cognitive-behavioral approaches targeting fear of movement into rehabilitation programs. To optimize the recovery process following TKA, early assessment of kinesiophobia may be warranted. In patients demonstrating both elevated kinesiophobia and increased joint position error, balance-focused training and proprioceptive exercises targeting deeper flexion angles may be considered as part of a comprehensive rehabilitation approach. Future intervention studies are needed to determine whether such targeted strategies improve functional outcomes in this population.

Strengths of the study

The present study holds methodological advantages in that it addresses the associations among kinesiophobia, proprioception, and functional status following TKA from a multidimensional perspective. Rather than merely identifying the association between kinesiophobia and functional outcomes, this study examined how this relationship may be statistically mediated through joint position error at 60° (JPE 60°), thereby providing exploratory evidence that contributes to the existing literature. Specifically, Steiger’s t-test was used to demonstrate that the association between kinesiophobia and joint position error becomes more pronounced at deeper flexion angles, while hierarchical regression analysis allowed for the control of potential confounders — including age, body mass index, and time since surgery — thereby enabling a clearer evaluation of the independent associations among the primary variables. Finally, the sample size was determined a priori through power analysis based on effect sizes reported in comparable studies in the literature (n = 28), and 40 patients were enrolled to exceed this minimum requirement. The use of a BCa Bootstrap procedure with 5000 resamples to support the reliability of coefficient estimates further enhances the statistical robustness of the findings.

Limitations of the study

The present study has certain limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the relatively modest sample size (N = 40) may restrict the generalizability of the multivariable regression models. Although the sample size was appropriately powered for testing a standard bivariate correlation, it was not calculated using more conservative dual-parameter correlation guidelines [32]. Furthermore, it was not specifically configured for multivariable modeling. Consequently, the direct effect of JPE 60° on functional status remaining at the threshold of significance in the mediation analysis (p = 0.062) indicates a statistical power constraint attributable to sample size. Although variables were mean-centered to minimize the risk of overfitting, multicollinearity assumptions were rigorously verified, and a 5000-iteration Bootstrap (BCa) procedure was applied, this limitation regarding multivariable modeling should be noted. Nevertheless, the fact that the confidence interval for the indirect effect did not include zero supports the statistical significance of the indirect pathway. Second, the cross-sectional design of the study limits the determination of temporal ordering and potential causal relationships among kinesiophobia, proprioception, and functional status, as all variables were assessed at a single time point. The mediation analysis findings should therefore be interpreted as evidence of statistical mediation rather than as evidence of causal pathways. Third, the exclusive use of joint position error as the measure of proprioception represents a notable measurement limitation. Joint position error reflects only one component of proprioceptive function (joint position sense), while other components — such as kinesthesia and force sense — were not assessed. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as relating specifically to joint position accuracy rather than to broader sensorimotor function. Fourth, the underrepresentation of male patients in the sample (n = 3, 7.5%) limits the generalizability of the findings with respect to sex-based differences. Although this distribution is consistent with the higher prevalence of knee osteoarthritis and TKA utilization among women [33, 34], studies with more balanced sex distributions are needed to validate the observed associations in male patients. Future controlled and prospective studies that longitudinally examine the associations between kinesiophobia and joint position accuracy across different postoperative phases will be of critical importance for establishing temporal relationships and informing the individualization of rehabilitation protocols following TKA.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that kinesiophobia is significantly associated with pain, functional status, and lower limb functional performance in patients who had undergone TKA, and is also moderately correlated with increased joint position error at 60° of knee flexion. The finding that this association was statistically significant only at 60° — and not at 30° — of flexion suggests that the relationship between kinesiophobia and joint position accuracy may be more pronounced at angles where active neuromuscular control demands are greater following ACL resection during TKA. Mediation analysis indicated that part of the association between kinesiophobia and functional outcomes was statistically mediated through joint position error at 60°, suggesting that fear of movement may be related to functional limitation partly through its association with proprioceptive accuracy. However, given the cross-sectional and observational design of this study, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory statistical associations rather than as evidence of causal or mechanistic pathways. In light of these findings, early assessment of kinesiophobia in TKA rehabilitation may be warranted, and the potential benefits of integrating proprioceptive training targeting deeper flexion angles alongside cognitive-behavioral approaches should be investigated in future prospective intervention studies.
