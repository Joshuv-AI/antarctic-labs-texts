Strengthening Australia's research integrity system
Authors: Warwick Anderson, Kerry Breen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMisconduct in research is a serious issue and is probably increasing. To grapple fully with this, to protect the reputation of honest researchers and to improve public accountability, the Australian research integrity system needs strengthening. The Australian Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research must be revised to mandate how research institutions investigate allegations of research misconduct. The Australian Research Integrity Committee must be empowered by government to scrutinise any institution's investigation, demand a re‐investigation when needed at the institution's expense and to report publicly on the incidence of allegations of misconduct and the outcomes of all investigations.

We present a proposal to strengthen Australia's research integrity system at a time when research misconduct internationally appears to be increasing, and artificial intelligence (AI) is probably accelerating it. 1 , 2

Misconduct and fraud in medical research corrupt the reliable information needed for clinical and public health practices and for the development of public and health systems policy advice. The consequences can be huge; for example, Andrew Wakefield's fraudulent research three decades ago has undermined confidence in the MMR (measles, mumps and rubella) vaccine, and widespread measles outbreaks in the UK and USA are the result. Apart from the negative effects on health policy and practice, fraudulent research also misleads private‐sector investors and money is wasted on unsuccessful attempts to develop new therapies. Fraudulent research may have impeded research on Alzheimer's disease for many years and exposed trial participants to ineffective and even harmful treatments as a result. 3

The problem was brought to public attention recently in Australia, where high‐profile researchers have been reported to have published fraudulent research. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8

Medical researchers themselves are harmed when they base their work on fraudulent publications and fraudsters can unfairly gain funding and recognition advantages at the expense of the vast majority of researchers who work honestly.

Calls for an Australian national integrity body have grown, including from the Australian Academy of Science. 9 We agree that Australia needs to do better in preventing and investigating research misconduct. We propose here ways to strengthen our systems, building upon the system put in place by the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) and the Australian Research Council (ARC) almost a decade and a half ago.

What is the current system? It comprises a code for the conduct of research (the Australian Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research) and an appeals body (the NHMRC/ARC Australian Research Integrity Committee (ARIC)). Both the code and ARIC warrant revision and strengthening.

Australian code for the responsible conduct of research

The Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research (‘Australian Code’) a was first issued in 2007 10 (replacing the Joint NHMRC/AVCC Statement and Guidelines on Research Practice 1997) and then revised in 2018. 11 Institutions and individuals wishing to receive ARC or NHMRC funding must sign a statement affirming that they will abide by the provisions of the code.

The code sets out eight general principles of responsible research conduct and outlines the responsibilities of institutions and researchers in order to adhere to these. The principles include honesty, rigour, transparency, fairness, respect, recognition (of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islanders' interests), accountability and promotion of responsible practices.

It takes a realistic approach to failure to uphold these principles because it properly recognises that deviations from these principles, what it calls breaches of the code, occur along a continuum from minor and unintentional through to serious and intentional. The latter are defined as ‘research misconduct’.

Thus, under the Australian Code, breaches of the code that may be research misconduct can be more than just fabrication, falsification and plagiarism. They can be serious and intentional departures from any of the Australian Code principles, such as unfair authorship, non‐compliance with legislation (e.g. on occupational health and safety, use of public funds) and undisclosed conflicts of interest for self‐gain. Most attention nevertheless is focussed on fabrication and falsification.

Reforming investigations of breaches of the Code and allegations of research misconduct

How should breaches and misconduct be investigated? The first step in Australia and almost everywhere internationally is an investigation conducted by the employing research institution. It is here that the current Australian Code has a serious weakness.

Previously, how to investigate allegations of research misconduct was part of the code itself, with clear obligations for institutions. The current code, in contrast, does not contain such contents, but instead ‘is supported by a number of guides’ including a guide to ‘Managing and investigating potential breaches of the Code’. But it is only a guide. In our view, being a ‘guide’ gives institutions too much ‘wriggle room’ to minimise, deflect or hide allegations directed at their staff in order to protect the institution's reputation.

In the next revision, the code must mandate the framework and investigatory process, to make it clear to both accusers and the accused how an investigation will be conducted. This is essential if the national integrity body (currently ARIC but see below) is to be able to assess properly the adequacy of institutions' investigations.

That the initial investigation for research misconduct is performed by the institution itself is justified for practical reasons. With the increased scope of what may constitute breaches of the principles of the code, there may be many initial complaints that require investigation, including those that arise through external platforms such as PubPeer (https://pubpeer.com/). Not all of these complaints turn out to be well founded. In addition, institutions hold the required documents, can put employees' obligations into contracts and can implement disciplinary actions.

However, institutions have a conflict of interest. Proven research misconduct can sully the institution's reputation, and there is little doubt that institutions have sought to push cases ‘under the rug’ and keep everything in house. Though the current guide acknowledges that for inquiries ‘There will be occasions where some or all members should be external to the institution’, no further guidance is given on how to decide when this is necessary.

So, while an institutional investigation is the necessary first step, there need to be much stronger ways of scrutinising how they are conducted. We suggest that this can be best achieved by strengthening the role of the ARIC, as described below.

Public accountability is needed too. At present, there is no requirement for an institution to report the incidences and outcomes of investigations. This leaves a serious gap in our knowledge of the frequency of allegations and findings of research misconduct and of the actions taken by the institutions. This may suit some senior research administrators, but the Australian community (which pays for the research) cannot develop an understanding of the extent and seriousness of the problem or how well it is being addressed.

The need to strengthen the role of the ARIC b

The ARIC was established in 2011 by the NHMRC and ARC with the aim ‘to ensure that in investigating potential breaches of the Code, institutions observe proper processes’… and ‘to review the processes by which an institution that is eligible to receive funding from the ARC and/or NHMRC has managed and/or investigated a potential breach of the Code’. Researchers, the accused or the accuser, can complain to ARIC that an institution has not conducted an investigation in accord with the requirements of the code (2007–2018) or the guide (from 2018) to the current code. However, as currently established, ARIC cannot itself initiate an examination of an institution's investigation. Nor is it able to collect any information from institutions about the occurrence and outcome of allegations of research misconduct with the organisation. ARIC reports that it examined 13 complaints in 2023–2024. 12

ARIC's current power comes from its reporting obligations: it must provide its findings and recommendations to the CEO of ARC and/or the CEO of NHMRC. ARC and NHMRC can then decide to suspend or cancel funding.

However, a recent review 13 found that awareness of ARIC was low; ‘Research institutions, ARIC applicants, and peak bodies possessed limited knowledge of ARIC's existence and its role’, suggesting that many eligible persons may not have taken the opportunity to ask ARIC to review decisions. In reality, institutions have no incentive to refer cases to ARIC, nor to encourage their staff to do so. It is not known whether any of the recent high‐profile Australian cases were referred to ARIC.

What should be done to strengthen the system?

Two main changes are needed: ARIC's role needs to be strengthened and it must be given greater powers, and how investigations are conducted must be mandated in the Australian Code.

The Australian code needs to be revised to re‐include mandated conditions for the investigations conducted by institutions. Freckelton, a distinguished Australian barrister and academic, has set out the aspects of a good system in his comprehensive and thoughtful book ‘Scholarly Misconduct’. 14 He writes ‘The processes for dealing with scholarly misconduct need to be clear, rigorous, fair, prompt, and transparent’… ‘This requires the provision of clear statements of values, expectations, and obligations by institutions such as universities, research bodies, and journals so that there is as little ambiguity as possible in terms of what is required of employers and employee, of staff and students, of persons accused and whistleblowers, of institutions and individuals’.

Discussion

Medical researchers are human beings. When the incentives in research foster hyper‐competitiveness, it is understandable (though not forgivable) that some cut corners and breach the code's principles and that a few resort to outright research misconduct.

As researchers, it can be jarring to admit that some of our colleagues stray from the values of science and principles of the Australian Code. Byrne has written ‘Fraud departs from community norms, so scientists do not want to think about it, let alone talk about it … This becomes a vicious cycle because fraud is not discussed, people don't learn about it, so they don't consider it, or they think it's so rare that it's unlikely to affect them, and so papers are less likely to come under scrutiny. Thinking and talking about systematic fraud is essential to solving this problem’. 15 It is in the interest of all honest researchers, who represent the vast majority, to have an effective, appropriate to science means of investigating misconduct, learning the extent of it through transparent reporting and holding all institutions that receive taxpayer funding to account.

This is why we are suggesting these changes be built upon the Australian Code but with the scrutiny and oversight to be improved through strengthening the ARIC (perhaps re‐named the Australian Research Integrity Office). A strengthened ARIC with powers to examine institutional investigations and to demand better institutional investigations is essential for the detection, investigation and judgement of Australian researchers who stray from the principles of science and to provide accountability to the public.
