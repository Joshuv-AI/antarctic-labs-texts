Single Photocatalytic Hydrosilylation of Alkenes for the Synthesis of Bis(silyl) and Silaboryl Alkanes
Authors: Yuki Nagashima, Mone Suzuki, Asuha Shimose, Ryo Arai, Ken Tanaka, Masanobu Uchiyama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bis­(silyl) and silaboryl alkanes are of interest as bioactive compounds and highly functionalized synthetic building blocks, but conventional hydrosilylation reactions lack generality and/or selectivity for synthesizing multielement-containing alkanes. Here, we present a versatile photoactivated hydrosilylation reaction of silicon- or boron-containing alkenes using silylborane as a silyl radical source to construct bis­(silyl) or silaboryl alkanes. This method employs a single phenothiazine-based photocatalyst and does not require conventional transition-metal or hydrogen-atom-transfer (HAT) catalysts or cocatalysts. Consequently, it has high generality, including alkenes bearing acid/base-sensitive groups, and provides high chemo-/regio-selectivity, overcoming the limitations of existing methods. This methodology also enables cyclizative functionalization of dienes and arylsilylation of boryl alkenes, and we show that the obtained multielement-containing alkanes are useful synthetic building blocks. Mechanistic studies uncovered a novel photoactivation mechanism, in which the phenothiazine catalyst directly facilitates Si–B bond cleavage in the triplet state, in contrast to conventional redox or HAT mechanisms, enabling radical hydrosilylation with a broad scope of alkenes.

Introduction

The introduction of silicon (Si) atoms enables modulation of the electronic states, molecular orbitals, and three-dimensional structures of aliphatic compounds, and consequently aliphatic organosilicon compounds have attracted increasing interest as functional materials , and pharmaceuticals. − Recently, organosilicons containing multiple main-group elements, such as bis­(silyl) and silaboryl alkanes, have been synthesized for potential applications as bioactive compounds − and highly functionalized building blocks (Figure A). − Hydrosilylation to form C­(sp3)–Si bonds is one of the most straightforward methods for synthesizing these compounds. − For example, transition-metal-catalyzed hydrosilylations − can construct multielement-containing organosilicons, including bis­(silyl) − and silaboryl alkanes, − by using Si- or boron (B)-containing alkenes as starting materials or intermediates (Figure B). Transition-metal catalysis usually yields 1,2-bis­(silyl) alkanes through the double hydrosilylation of terminal alkynes. − In addition, 1,1-bis­(silyl) alkanes have been synthesized through the double hydrosilylation of alkynes using sophisticated Co-, Cu-, and Fe-catalysts, and 1-boryl-1-silyl alkanes have been prepared via the Pt-catalyzed hydrosilylation of boryl alkenes. However, these reactions often require case-by-case optimization of metal centers, ligands, reagents, and temperature, limiting their generality, regioselectivity, and chemoselectivity between alkenes and alkynes, so that the desired bis­(silyl) and silaboryl alkanes are sometimes inaccessible.

Recently, the visible-light-induced photocatalytic generation of silyl radicals − has emerged as an alternative method for the hydrosilylation of alkenes with silanes (X3SiH), − silylboranes (X3SiBpin), − disilanes (X3SiSiX3), − and silacarboxylic acids (X3SiCO2H) under mild conditions, driven by light-emitting diodes (LEDs). In silane-mediated hydrosilylation, a cocatalyst for hydrogen-atom transfer (HAT) with a photoredox catalyst is necessary for the efficient hydrosilylation of unactivated (electron-rich) alkene, while silylborane-mediated hydrosilylation requires a base catalyst for borate complex formation instead of a HAT cocatalyst. Alternatively, electrochemical approaches can also enable some hydrosilylations even in the absence of any catalyst under mild conditions. − However, although photocatalytic or electrochemical methods can provide high chemoselectivity for alkenes and good compatibility with functional groups, their application to the synthesis of multielement-containing organosilicons remains limited to the hydrosilylation of B-containing alkenes, yielding silaboryl alkanes. , This limitation arises because the HAT or base cocatalyst may react with the silane groups.

Here we present a hydrosilylation reaction employing a single photocatalyst that can construct a diverse range of 1,1- or 1,2-bis­(silyl) and 1,1-, 1,2-, 1,3-, or 1,4-silaboryl aliphatic compounds from both Si- and B-containing alkenes with high generality and chemo-/regioselectivity (Figure C). The key to success lies in the use of benzo­[b]­phenothiazine − as a single photocatalyst and silylborane as a silyl radical source, eliminating the need for a HAT or base cocatalyst, which is typically required in conventional silane- or silylborane-mediated photocatalytic hydrosilylations of unactivated alkenes. Experimental and computational analyses reveal that the phenothiazine directly activates the Si–B bond in the triplet state, in contrast to conventional HAT or redox mechanisms, facilitating highly chemoselective silyl-radical-mediated hydrosilylation and expanding the chemical diversity of accessible bis­(silyl) and silaboryl alkanes.

Results and Discussion

Optimization of Reaction Conditions

In 2022, our group reported dual catalysis using a photocatalyst and base (alkoxide) cocatalyst to generate silyl radicals from silylboranes (Si–B), achieving the hydrosilylation of alkenes. However, simply applying this protocol to Si- and B-containing alkenes proved unsuccessful. In 2023, we also demonstrated that quinoline derivatives could directly activate silylboranes to generate silyl radicals in the excited state without a catalyst, enabling the dearomative silaboration of quinolines. This motivated us to explore more effective photoabsorbing heteroaromatics for the activation of silylboranes with a single catalyst, eliminating the need for the HAT process or basic conditions. Therefore, we commenced our study on single-photocatalytic hydrosilylation using PhMe2Si–B­(pin) (2) and (E)-(3-phenoxyprop-1-en-1-yl)­(phenyl)­silane (1a) as model Si-containing alkenes, which are not suitable substrates for conventional silylborane-mediated photocatalytic hydrosilylation with silylborane (Table , entry 1).

In the absence of photocatalysts, no product was detected under 370 nm LED irradiation (entry 2). Screening of various organic photosensitizers revealed that Eosin Y and triphenylpyrylium did not promote hydrosilylation at all, but pleasingly, 4CzIPN and phenothiazine (PTZ-A) gave the desired 1,1-bis­(silyl) alkanes 3a in 12% and 46% yields, respectively (entries 3–6). As a result of our investigation on phenothiazine derivatives, benzo­[b]­phenothiazine PTZ-D, which absorbs visible light more efficiently than PTZ-A, afforded 3a in the highest yield of 76% (entries 7–9). In contrast, Michler’s ketone, with a redox potential similar to that of PTZ-D, did not catalyze the reaction (entry 10). Various Ir catalysts, with favorable redox potentials for the reduction/oxidation of diverse reagents, did not facilitate the silylation (entries 11–14). The lack of correlation between the yields and redox potentials of the photocatalysts suggested that the activation mode of 2 was not a redox process. Solvent screening indicated that MeCN and THF produced complex mixtures (entry 15). This hydrosilylation reaction was completely shut down in the dark (entry 16). We also found that EtOH is a better solvent than MeOH (entry 17), confirming that the conditions for entry 9 were optimal. Notably, this represented the first report of the single-photocatalytic hydrosilylation of unactivated (electron-rich) alkenes.

Reaction Scopes and Synthetic Applications

Figure A shows the scope of the photoinduced hydrosilylation of various silane-containing alkenes 1. Vinylsilane derivatives 1a–1k, featuring both polar functional [e.g., phenoxy (1a), chloro (1b and 1h), ester (1i), and cyano (1j)] and nonpolar groups [e.g., cycloalkyl (1c and 1d), linear alkyl (1g), and aryl (1e and 1f)], underwent hydrosilylation to afford the corresponding 1,1-bis­(silyl) alkanes 3a–3k in good to high yields (up to 76%) with high or complete regioselectivity. Alkenes bearing dihydrosilanes (1a–1e, 1i, and 1l) and monohydrosilanes (1f, 1g, 1h, 1j, 1k, 1m, and 1n) were compatible with this reaction. The positions of the silyl groups on the alkenes had minimal influence on the present reaction, as demonstrated by the synthesis of 1-phenyl-1,2-bis­(silyl)-alkane 3l from 1-phenyl-1-silyl-alkene 1l as a single regioisomer and bis­(silyl)-substituted compounds 3m and 3n from the corresponding alkenes 1m and 1n.

As illustrated in Figure B, B-containing alkenes 4 also undergo photoinduced hydrosilylation under the optimal conditions when the irradiation wavelength is extended to 450 nm (blue LEDs), yielding boryl- and silyl-substituted alkanes 5. 1-Boryl-2-alkyl-alkenes 4a–4e were selectively converted into 1,1-silaboryl alkanes 5a–5e in good to excellent yields (up to 93%) with high or complete regioselectivity. 1-Boryl-2-cyclopropyl-alkene 4f underwent ring-opening hydrosilylation, affording 1,1-silaboryl allyl compound 5f. 1-Boryl-1-alkyl alkenes 4g–4j selectively provided 1,2-silaboryl alkanes, and 1,3- and 1,4-silaboryl compounds 5k and 5l were also obtained from the corresponding alkenes. Furthermore, this hydrosilylation was successfully performed on a preparative scale (1–2 mmol), affording approximately 100–300 mg of the multielement-containing organosilicon compounds 3b, 3m, and 5k.

Phenothiazine-based photocatalysis was confirmed to be more effective than conventional photocatalysis for the hydrosilylation of element-containing alkenes. For example, the hydrosilylation of Si-containing alkenes 1a and 1m using conventional dual catalysis , with HAT or a base cocatalyst resulted in complex mixtures (Figure A). Similarly, in the hydrosilylation of B-containing alkene 4a, the regioselectivity of 5a decreased to 54:46 when a basic cocatalyst was used (Figure B). In addition, since phenothiazine-based photocatalysis does not require additional reagents, it is applicable to a broad range of alkenes 6 bearing acid/base-sensitive groups, including phosphate esters (6a), carboxylic acids (6b), amine derivatives (6d and 6g), and anilines (6i) (Figure C). Notably, a turnover number (TON) of 2000 was achieved with 6d in the presence of just 0.01 mol % of PTZ-D. Other silylboranes, BnMe2Si–B­(pin) (2-Bn) and Et3Si–B­(pin) (2-Et), were also available in the present hydrosilylation, affording 7a-Bn and 7a-Et, respectively. Overall, this strategy exhibited high generality and functional group tolerance, enabling the synthesis of a wide variety of highly multielement-containing alkanes.

We further examined the synthetic utility and applications of this photocatalytic system. First, it could be integrated with radical-mediated three-component coupling reactions. For example, the addition of diboron reagents as coupling partners resulted in the first example of the cyclizative silaboration of dienes 8, affording three-dimensional aliphatic rings 9 bearing both boryl and silyl groups (Figure A). In addition, incorporating aryl bromides as coupling partners and Ni catalysts facilitated the first example of the arylsilylation − of vinyl boronate 4g, where 4CzIPN proved superior to PTZ-D as the photocatalyst (Figure B). This arylsilylation can employ not only electron-rich/deficient phenyl bromides, but also heteroaryl bromide as a coupling partner, affording diverse 1-aryl-1-boryl-2-silyl alkanes 10a–10f.

Second, we confirmed the high selectivity and functional group tolerance of this photocatalytic system without conventional transition-metal catalysts, HAT catalysts, or acidic/basic conditions (Figure C). We found that the chemoselective hydrosilylation of alkenes proceeded in mixtures of Si- or B-containing alkenes 1m/4a with various alkynes 11a–11c, leaving the alkynes intact in high yields (up to >99%). The reaction also proceeded under basic (NaOEt and Et3N), acidic (TFA and TfOH), triplet-quenching (2,5-dimethylhexa-2,4-diene), and radical-inhibiting [(2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidin-1-yl)­oxy] conditions, demonstrating greater robustness than previously reported photoinduced hydrosilylation reactions.

Third, as shown in Figure D, the HAT-mediated hydrosilylation conditions reported by Wu’s group can be applied to the remaining Si–H bonds of the obtained bis­(silyl) alkanes 3. For example, 3b, containing two Si–H bonds, reacted with an alkene to yield 12a, which subsequently reacted with another alkene to form 12b, featuring a highly functionalized quaternary Si center. Similarly, 3c and 3m were converted into quaternary organosilicons 12c and 12d. In addition, 1,3-silaboryl alkanes underwent Suzuki coupling or C–N bond formation selectively on the B-containing moiety, generating organosilicon compounds 12e and 12f.

Mechanistic Considerations

Next, we elucidated the role of PTZ-D in the hydrosilylation of Si-/B-containing alkenes through experimental and spectroscopic studies. The ON/OFF experiment confirmed that continuous irradiation with light is required for the reaction (Figure A), and the quantum yield (Φ) was very low (0.0055 for 5a, Figure B), ruling out radical chain propagation as the main mechanism. In addition, after photoirradiation, compound 2 was fully converted into alkoxy or hydride silyl/boryl byproducts (Figure C). The addition of starting alkenes 1a or 4a selectively suppressed the formation of PhMe2Si–H/OEt rather than pinB–H/OEt, suggesting that silyl radicals (or equivalents) could be generated and react with alkenes, while boryl radicals (or equivalents) may be quenched by the solvent. Deuterium experiments using EtOD or d 6-EtOH revealed no effect on the reaction kinetics, indicating that the ethanol solvent is not involved in the rate-determining step or photoinduced step (Figure D).

The UV/vis absorption, fluorescence, and phosphorescence spectra showed no detectable quenching of the phosphorescence of PTZ-D in the presence of 2 at any concentration, and the UV/vis absorption of PTZ-D remained unchanged upon addition of 2 (Figure E). These results indicate that the activation process of 2 with PTZ-D would be kinetically accessible at room temperature, but thermodynamically unfavored (ΔG > 0 and ΔG ‡ < 25 kcal mol–1). Given the lack of correlation between the yields and redox potentials (Table ), both single-electron transfer (redox processes) and complex formation between PTZ-D and 2 can be ruled out.

Thus, we propose the following reaction mechanism (Figure F). Upon visible-light irradiation, PTZ-D is excited to 3 PTZ-D, which usually reverts to 1 PTZ-D via phosphorescence at approximately 520 nm. Silylborane 2 interacts with 3 PTZ-D to form I; however, this interaction is kinetically accessible, but thermodynamically unfavorable. The cleavage of the Si–B bond in I releases borylated PTZ (II) and a silyl radical (III), which reacts with alkenes 1 in the presence of ethanol to afford the hydrosilylated product 3 via the carbon-centered radical intermediate IV. The resulting ethanol radical (V) reacts with II to regenerate PTZ-D, producing boryl byproducts (pinB–H/OEt). Indeed, we detected dearomatized (borylated) benzo­[b]­phenothiazine 13-B and 13-H under the photoreaction conditions by APCI-MS, suggesting that PTZ-D photocatalyst directly activates silylboranes in the excited state to form the intermediate II (Figure G). Our proposed mechanism is orthogonal to conventional silylborane-mediated photocatalytic hydrosilylation, where single-electron transfer from silylborane to 3 PTZ-D affords the silyl radical and 2 PTZ-D radical anion, as shown in Figure F.

Next, we performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations, combined with the global reaction route mapping (GRRM) program, to systematically investigate the possible reaction pathways for the phenothiazine-based photocatalytic hydrosilylation and to clarify the structural features of the putative intermediates. Based on the experimental background, we selected the most efficient and structurally simple combination (PTZ-D, PhMe2Si–B­(eg) 2x, and (E)-but-1-en-1-yl­(phenyl)­silane 1x as a model alkene) for our calculations at the UωB97XD/def2-TZVPP/SMD­(EtOH)//UM06/6-31+G* level of theory. First, we calculated the Gibbs free energy changes (ΔG) for PTZ-D and silylborane 2x in conventional mechanisms including redox or energy transfer (Figure A). Regardless of the coordination of ethanol to 2x, the Gibbs free energy changes are so high (>20 kcal mol–1) in both mechanisms that neither redox nor energy transfer events would proceed thermodynamically in the present system. These results are consistent with the experimental findings. Then, we investigated possible pathways for the generation of silyl radicals from silylborane using PTZ-D (Figure B). Although the coordination of 2x to 3 PTZ-D was thermodynamically unfavorable, 3 PTZ-D could still attack the B atom in 2x, facilitating (i) cleavage of the Si–B bond, (ii) formation of the C–B bond, and (iii) elimination of the silyl radical through a single transition state. Six transition states (TS1_2-A, TS1_2-B, TS1_2-C, TS1_2-D, TS1_2-E, and TS1_2-F) were identified, indicating that the formation of the C–B bond proceeded smoothly at the C11 position, which has the highest spin density (0.565) in 3 PTZ-D. This process exhibited the lowest activation energy of 16.5 kcal mol–1 via TS1_2-A. The other transition states required higher activation energies (17.2, 19.3, 19.1, 24.9, and 30.8 kcal mol–1) for C–B bond formation, probably due to the lower spin densities at the C6, C10, C11a, C8, and N positions compared with that at the C11 position.

We determined the overall pathway of the photocatalytic reaction in EtOH (Figure C). The triplet energy of PTZ-D was calculated to be 57.8 kcal mol–1, which aligns well with a previous experimental value. The pathway of silyl radical generation at the C11 position of 3 PTZ-D was recalculated in the presence of an EtOH molecule, revealing that the process from IM1 to IM2 is still kinetically favored (ΔG ‡ = 11.1 kcal mol–1). IM2 was identified as thermodynamically unfavored, with a small energy loss of 2.8 kcal mol–1, which well explains the lack of change in the UV/vis absorption and phosphorescence spectra. This energy loss was compensated by a significant energy release upon the addition of the silyl radical to the Si-containing alkene 1x (from IM3 to IM4; ΔG < −20 kcal mol–1).

This radical addition occurred at the carbon attached to the silyl group via TS3_4 (ΔG ‡ = 3.8 kcal mol–1), and was faster than the reaction at the other carbon via TS3_4’ (ΔG ‡ = 5.6 kcal mol–1), resulting in the more stable bis­(silyl) intermediate IM4. This difference accounts for the complete regioselectivity observed in the hydrosilylation of Si-containing alkenes such as 1a–1k. The stability of the intermediate and the transition state would be derived from hyperconjugation between the radical-occupied carbon 2p orbital and forming C–Si σ bond orbital. In contrast, in the case of vinyl borane substrates, the corresponding intermediate and the transition state would also be stabilized by conjugation between the radical-occupied carbon 2p orbital and boron 2p orbital, resulting in low or opposite regioselectivity when using 4a, 4c, 4g, and 4h.

IM4 could be hydrogenated using ethanol to produce the desired bis­(silyl) alkane IM6, with the release of the ethoxy radical. Finally, the resulting ethoxy radical could capture PTZ-X (boryl radical equivalent) to form stable EtO–B­(eg) with an activation barrier of 14.1 kcal mol–1 (from IM7 to IM8). The large exothermicity (−35.1 kcal mol–1) of this step provides the driving force for the next catalytic cycle. The activation barriers are all sufficiently low (<25 kcal mol–1) for the reactions to proceed smoothly at room temperature. In contrast, when IM2 does not react with alkene 1x, it would decompose with the ethanol solvent via intermediate IM9 (where H–Bpin is formed) to afford the stable species IM10, which includes H–Bpin and EtO–SiMe2Ph, with a large exothermicity of −71.6 kcal mol–1. These theoretical results account well for the experimentally observed byproducts.

Conclusions

We have developed a visible-light-induced, phenothiazine-catalyzed activation of silylborane, enabling the hydrosilylation of Si- or B-containing alkenes with high chemo-/regioselectivity. This approach represents a significant departure from conventional metal-, redox-, or HAT-catalysis, which often suffer from limited generality, chemoselectivity, Si/B tolerance, and alkene scope. Although the range of available silyl groups remains to be established, the use of a phenothiazine derivative as single photocatalyst overcomes the above limitations. Mechanistic studies revealed that the phenothiazine catalyst facilitates direct Si–B bond cleavage in the triplet state, and does not involve conventional redox or HAT mechanisms. This work provides a new avenue for the synthesis of sp3-rich organosilicon compounds, such as bis­(silyl)- and silaboryl-substituted alkanes, without the need for case-by-case optimization of the catalytic system. We anticipate that this reaction will enable the rapid synthesis of new candidate biologically active and functional organosilicons.
