Midterm results of iCover stent grafts as bridging stent in fenestrated endovascular aortic repair
Authors: Wouter Van den Eynde, Nick Smet, Fleur Claes, Fien Gryffroy, Jürgen Verbist
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveComplex endovascular repair with fenestrated stent grafts is a well-established approach for the treatment of complex aortic aneurysms. Bridging stent grafts (BSGs) are essential to connect the aortic endoprosthesis to the target vessels. This single-center study aimed to evaluate the midterm outcomes of iCover stent grafts implanted as a bridging stent during fenestrated endovascular aortic repair.MethodsData from all patients treated with at least one iCover stent in a fenestration were prospectively collected between December 2022 and June 2024 and retrospectively analyzed. The database included patient demographics, BSG diameter and length, technical success rates, reinterventions, occlusions, and postoperative events. A computed tomography angiography was performed either before discharge or within 1 month postoperatively. All patients underwent follow-up at 1 year with computed tomography angiography or duplex ultrasound examination and abdominal radiography in cases of renal insufficiency (estimated glomerular filtration rate of <30 mL/min).ResultsA total of 41 patients (83% male) were included with 141 fenestrations treated using an iCover as BSG. The technical success rate was 97.6% with one failure owing to a type IC endoleak. Thirty-day mortality was 2.4% with one in-hospital death in a patient treated for a ruptured aneurysm. Primary patency during the study was 100%. During a median follow-up of 21 months (interquartile range, 18-24 months), freedom from target vessel instability was 98.5%. No aneurysm-related mortality was observed.ConclusionsEarly and midterm outcomes of the iCover stent used as a BSG in fenestrated endovascular aneurysm repair demonstrate favorable patency rates and freedom from target vessel instability.Keywords: Aneurysm, Bridging stent, Fenestrated endovascular aortic repair, iCover, Target vessel instability

Fenestrated endovascular aortic repair (FEVAR) is a minimally invasive endovascular procedure for the treatment of complex aortic aneurysms and is increasingly considered the standard of care, particularly in high-risk patients.1, 2, 3 Compared with open surgical repair, FEVAR offers several advantages, including shorter procedural times, shorter hospital stays, and lower perioperative morbidity and mortality.4 Nevertheless, the bridging stent grafts (BSGs) remain the Achilles’ heel of this technique, contributing to higher reintervention rates owing to stent fractures, fabric, tears and occlusions during follow-up.5, 6, 7, 8 An ideal BSG offers excellent visibility and flexibility combined with high radial force and resistance to stent fractures and fabric tears.9 Several balloon-expandable covered stents have been used for this purpose. The iCover stent graft (iVascular) was introduced to the market in 2021, is composed of a cobalt-chromium L605 stent encapsulated in expanded polytetrafluoroethylene, and features three radiopaque markers at each end to enhance visibility. The objective of this study was to report the midterm results of 141 iCover stent grafts used as BSGs in 41 patients who underwent FEVAR for the treatment of complex aortic aneurysms.

Methods

Study population

All patients treated in our center between December 2022 and June 2024 with at least one iCover stent during FEVAR were included in this study. Preoperative demographics, comorbidities, and clinical data were prospectively collected. Perioperative and postoperative data were recorded in accordance with the reporting standards for endovascular aortic repair of aneurysms involving the renal-mesenteric arteries.10 Written informed consent was obtained from all patients and the study was approved by the local ethical committee.

Procedural details

In all elective cases, a custom-made fenestrated endograft (Cook Medical) was implanted. In one urgent repair, an in situ laser FEVAR was performed with a thoracic endograft (Cook Medical). Preoperative planning was carried out on a dedicated 3D workstation (OsiriX Viewer, Pixmeo SARL). The diameter of the target vessels and distance from the ostium to the first branch were measured using a center lumen line. All procedures were performed in a hybrid operating room (Azurion, Philips) under general anesthesia using fusion imaging guidance and conducted by a single surgeon. After vascular access was obtained, an initial dose of 5000 IU heparin was administered, followed by an additional 2500 IU after 30 minutes. The activated clotting time was monitored every 30 minutes, targeting an activated clotting time of >250 seconds. After deployment of the fenestrated endograft, all fenestrations and target vessels were sequentially catheterized and secured with a Flexor ANL sheath (Cook Medical). Each sheath was preloaded with a covered stent. After complete release of the endograft, the BSGs were implanted with adequate overlap inside the endograft. BSGs in 6 × 6 mm fenestrations were flared using an 8-mm balloon; BSGs in 8-mm fenestrations were flared with a 10-mm balloon. Procedural data, including total procedure time, radiation dose, fluoroscopy time, contrast volume, additional procedures, and stent graft characteristics, were prospectively collected. A cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scan was performed at the end of each procedure to assess technical success. To prevent spinal cord ischemia, a standardized protocol, as described by Maurel et al11 was followed.

Postoperative treatment and follow-up

All patients received acetylsalicylic acid preoperatively. Clopidogrel was added postoperatively for ≥6 months unless full anticoagulation was necessary. Clinical and radiographic follow-up was performed at one and three months postoperatively and annually thereafter. CT angiography (CTA) was performed either before discharge or within 30 days postoperatively. Annual CTAs were performed for all patients unless the estimated glomerular filtration rate was <30 mL/min. In such cases, follow-up consisted of duplex ultrasound examination and plain abdominal radiographs in multiple projections. During follow-up, data on major adverse events, renal insufficiency, target vessel instability (TVI), and mortality were collected in accordance with the reporting standards.10 Follow-up was defined as early (within the first 30 postoperative days) and late (beyond 30 days).

End points and statistical evaluation

The primary safety end point was defined as the absence of mortality, major or minor adverse events, and reinterventions related to the BSG. Technical success was defined as successful access to the arterial system via remote arterial exposure, successful device introduction and deployment without surgical conversion or mortality, successful side branch catheterization and BSG placement with preservation of flow in all intended target vessels, absence of type I or III endoleak (EL) persisting beyond 30 days and patency of all aortic modular stent graft components and intended side branch components.10 TVI was defined as a composite end point to define any death or rupture related to side branch complication (eg, EL, rupture) or any secondary intervention indicated to treat a branch-related complication including EL, disconnection, kink, stenosis, occlusion, or rupture.10 Secondary end points consisted of clinical in-hospital and early and midterm outcomes. These included major adverse events, non-BSG reinterventions, and secondary procedures. Other complications, not related to the BSG, were described but excluded from the evaluation and described as secondary end points. Statistical analysis was performed using RStudio version 2025.05.1. Categorical variables are reported as absolute frequencies and corresponding percentages. Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Overall survival and freedom from TVI and reintervention were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method.

Results

Preoperative demographics

A total of 58 patients with complex aneurysms involving the visceral vessels were treated endovascularly with fenestrated and/or branched endografts during the study period. In 41 patients (34 men), ≥1 iCover stent was implanted as a BSG in a fenestration, accounting for 141 fenestrations in total. When the appropriate iCover stent graft size (length or diameter) was not available off the shelf, a BeGraft peripheral stent graft (Bentley InnoMed) was used as an alternative. Forty patients underwent elective treatment and one patient underwent urgent in situ laser FEVAR for a rupture of a type I A EL after a previous standard EVAR. The median patient age was 77 years (IQR, 72.0-79.0 years) and the median preoperative aneurysm diameter was 62 mm (IQR, 55.0-69.0 mm). Preoperative demographics are summarized in Table I. The majority of aneurysms were classified as juxtarenal or pararenal (n = 31 [75.6%]). Six patients (14.6%) were treated for a type IA EL after previous EVAR. One patient had a postdissection aneurysm after a type B aortic dissection. Nine patients (22%) had previously undergone a thoracic EVAR, of whom two also had an ascending repair with a frozen elephant trunk. Seven patients (17%) were treated in advance with intravascular lithotripsy and/or an endoconduit in case of anticipated difficult access.

Procedure details

Of 158 fenestrations, 141 (89.2%) were bridged with iCover stent grafts. Among the 141 iCovers implanted, 76 were placed in the renal arteries (53.9%), 35 in the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) (24.8%), 27 in the celiac trunk (19.1%), 1 in the common hepatic artery (0.7%), 1 in the splenic artery (0.7%), and 1 in the inferior mesenteric artery (0.7%). Technical success was achieved in 97.6%. All target vessels were successfully catheterized and BSGs were deployed in the intended position. However, one patient demonstrated an indeterminate EL on completion angiography, which was classified as a type IC on the first postoperative CTA. This was treated 6 weeks postoperatively with an additional iCover. No type III ELs were observed. Thirty-six patients (87.8%) were treated percutaneously. The majority of the endografts had four fenestrations (33/41 [80.5%]). Four patients (9.8%) were treated with an endograft with three fenestrations, two patients (4.9%) with an endograft with five fenestrations, and two patients (4.9%) with an endograft with two fenestrations and two branches. One patient was simultaneously treated with bilateral iliac branched devices. A concomitant hepatic artery aneurysm was treated with an iCover in one patient. No intraoperative adverse events occurred. Nine fenestrations needed a second balloon-expandable covered stent because of insufficient stent length. In eight of these cases, an iCover was used. Two patients were treated with an additional dilatation of the aortic portion of the iCover stent graft in the SMA and left renal artery (LRA) because of a stenosis detected on the final CBCT scan, probably caused by the introduction of the bifurcated endograft. Four additional nitinol stents were placed distally to the iCover to improve alignment in the presence of acute angulation. In two fenestrations, an extra balloon-expandable bare stent was required to correct a residual stenosis in a heavily calcified target vessel origin. All perioperative data are shown in Table II.

Early follow-up

The median length of stay in the intensive care unit was 1 day (IQR, 1-2 days) and in the hospital was 3 days (IQR, 2-7 days). Major complications occurred in three patients (7.3%). The first patient developed septic complications after an iatrogenic bowel perforation during exploratory laparoscopy, performed to exclude bowel ischemia. This patient had been treated for a ruptured aneurysm with in situ laser FEVAR and died 9 days after the procedure, resulting in a 30-day mortality rates of 2.4%. CTA confirmed complete exclusion of the aneurysm without BSG-related complications. The second patient, who had been treated with a four-vessel fenestrated device starting 4 cm above the celiac trunk, developed transient grade 3B spinal cord ischemia 24 hours postoperatively, classified according to the reporting standards published by Oderich et al.10 This resolved completely after vasopressor-induced hypertension. A second episode occurred >48 hours later, requiring a rescue spinal drain with complete recovery of motor and sensory function. The third patient developed grade 3C spinal cord ischemia after treatment of a type 1 thoracoabdominal aneurysm and also fully recovered without sequelae under hypertensive therapy. Two patients with preexisting renal insufficiency experienced a reduction of the renal function of >30% but <50%. None of these patients required dialysis. One pseudoaneurysm of the right common femoral artery was treated surgically 22 days after the procedure.

Late follow-up

The median follow-up was 21 months (IQR, 18-24 days). During follow-up, five late fatalities (12.2%) were recorded. Two patients passed away after cardiac failure, 14 and 16 months postoperatively. One patient died of respiratory complications associated with preexisting pulmonary fibrosis after 8 months and another of a gastrointestinal bleeding from the pancreaticoduodenal anastomosis after a Whipple procedure at 5 months postoperatively. The last death was owing to complications of newly diagnosed liver cirrhosis 15 months after FEVAR. The estimated overall survival was 93% and 84% at 1 and 2 years, respectively (Fig 1). No patients were lost to follow-up. Eight additional unplanned reinterventions were required, six of which were for ELs. Two type IC ELs were treated, one 6 weeks postoperatively, as noted, and another 7 months after the index procedure with an extra iCover in the SMA, resulting in a freedom of TVI of 98.5% at 1 and 2 years (Fig 2). One undefined EL and two type II ELs were treated with embolization at 3, 17, and 19 months after the index procedure. One dislocation of an iliac limb from a bifurcation with a short inverted limb configuration was corrected with an iliac extension 16 months after the index procedure. An in-stent stenosis was detected in a nitinol stent, placed in the SMA to align the BSG (nonstudy stent) in an outer branch. This was treated with a covered stent. A pseudoaneurysm occurred in the left brachial artery 3 months postoperatively and was successfully managed with thrombin injection. The estimated freedom from unplanned reintervention was 85% at 1 year and 73% at 2 years (Fig 3). One planned reintervention involved the embolization of an intended EL through a distal entry tear of a type B aortic dissection. Aneurysm diameter remained stable or decreased in 12 (29.3%) and 25 (61%) patients, respectively. Three cases of sac enlargement were attributed to two type II ELs and one type III A EL, all of which were treated successfully. No BSG occlusions or in-stent stenosis were observed, nor any fabric tears or stent fractures of the iCover stent grafts. No aneurysm-related mortality was noted. Discussion

BSG-related complications remain a major concern in FEVAR, with reported reintervention rates ranging from 7.6% to 26.2%.12 Various balloon-expandable covered stents have been used as BSGs. This study presents the midterm results of the iCover as BSG in FEVAR. Technical success was achieved in 97.6% of patients. No BSG-related complications were observed during the procedure. Two additional dilatations were necessary after the detection of stenoses in the SMA and the LRA in two different patients, identified on the final CBCT scan. These stenoses were likely caused by compression during the introduction of the bifurcated graft. Tenorio et al13 demonstrated the value of a final CBCT scan, which revealed positive findings requiring immediate revision in nearly 20% of patients. Similar LRA damage was also reported by Karelis et al,14 likely owing to forced deviation of the bifurcation to the left during right femoral access, combined with the LRA often being the lowest fenestration. Compression of the SMA may be attributed to the strong anteroposterior angulation of the visceral aorta. Ostial reinforcement with a bare metal balloon-expandable stent was necessary in two heavily calcified and stenosed visceral vessels. During a median follow-up of 21 months, two type IC ELs were observed and successfully treated with BSG extension. Primary patency of the target vessels treated with iCover BSGs during the study was 100% and freedom from TVI was 98.5%. These outcomes are comparable with those reported for other BSGs. Becker et al15 published midterm results with the BeGraft peripheral stent graft (Bentley Innomed) as BSG showing a freedom from TVI of 99.3%, 98.8%, and 98.8% at 1, 2, and 3 years, respectively. Katsargyris et al16 reported an estimated freedom from TVI of 93.2 ± 0.9% and an estimated patency at eight years of 98.1 ± 0.5% for fenestrations treated with the Advanta V12 (Getinge). Di Domenico et al17 documented a 36-month freedom from TVI and target vessel patency of 92.7% using the VBX (W. L. Gore & Associates). Published data on the use of iCover stents as BSG in FEVAR are limited. Hauck et al18 published short-term outcomes from a multicenter Austrian retrospective study of 58 patients who received 212 iCover stent grafts in 207 renovisceral arteries. After a median follow-up of 4 months, target vessel patency was 100%. In 5.2% of cases, a type III EL was detected during follow-up, and no BSG-related mortality was noted. Comparable results were published by Menges et al,9 in which 87 target vessels were treated with iCover stents in FEVAR. Freedom from iCover-related TVI reached 94% and both primary and secondary patency were 98% after a median follow-up of 10 months.

The iCover was first released in 2021 and is a relatively new covered stent on the market. It is a balloon-expandable covered stent based on a CoCr L605 platform with full expanded polytetrafluoroethylene encapsulation, mounted on an 0.035-inch Oceanus balloon. The inner covering of the stent surface facilitates resheathing after deployment. Its open cell design enhances flexibility, and the three tantalum markers at each end improve visibility and enable precise positioning. All iCover stents are 6F compatible up to a size of 8 × 17 mm; longer stents or larger diameters require a 7F sheath.

In an in vitro study, Amil et al19 compared the biomechanical properties of the iCover stent graft with those of the VBX balloon-expandable stent graft. Tests included an evaluation of flexibility and pull-out and shear stress forces. In a three-point bending test, the crimped iCover demonstrated greater flexibility than the VBX. However, once expanded, the VBX proved to be more flexible, likely owing to wider spacing between the crowns. In addition, the alternating links connecting the crowns of the iCover stents provide greater resistance to bending. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two stents in terms of pull-out and shear stress forces.

Limitations

Limitations of this study are its retrospective design with a small sample size and only relatively short follow-up period, which may not fully capture BSG-related complications that tend to manifest over a longer duration. Additionally, all patients were treated at a single center. Larger studies with long-term follow-up are warranted to confirm these findings.

Conclusions

The iCover demonstrates promising early and midterm outcomes as a BSG in FEVAR with high patency rates and low TVI. These results appear at least comparable with other available BSG. However, long-term results are essential to validate these findings and enable robust comparisons with competing BSGs. Strict and continuous surveillance remains crucial to ensure early detection of potential complications.
