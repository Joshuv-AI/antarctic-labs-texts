Comparative effectiveness of Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy versus tomotherapy in locally advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma: a propensity score-matched retrospective cohort study
Authors: Zihan Chen, Zongwei Huang, Sunqin Cai, Ying Li, Yuye Lin, Hanyu Zhang, Xinyi Hong, Jue Wang, Hengyu Guo, Youliang Weng, Sufang Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) remains a major healthcare burden in endemic regions, and locally advanced disease demands optimal radiotherapy for curative intent. Although both Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy (VMAT) and tomotherapy (TOMO) are both advanced conformal radiotherapy techniques, their comparative efficacy in locally advanced NPC has not been fully clarified by large-scale studies that adequately control for selection bias. This study compared the therapeutic efficacy and safety of VMAT versus TOMO in patients with locally advanced NPC.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study of 1,974 patients with histologically confirmed locally advanced NPC treated at our institution between January 2016 and December 2019. Patients received either VMAT (n = 1,720) or TOMO (n = 254) combined with systemic therapy per institutional protocols. To reduce selection bias, propensity score matching (PSM) was conducted at a 1:1 ratio using nearest-neighbor matching without replacement, with a 0.2 standard deviation caliper to balance baseline characteristics between groups. Primary endpoints included overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), locoregional recurrence-free survival (LRFS), and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS). The secondary endpoints were treatment response rates and treatment-related adverse events.ResultsAfter PSM, 979 patients were included in the final analysis (728 VMAT and 251 TOMO). Standardized mean differences (SMDs) for all baseline variables were < 0.1, confirming adequate balance between groups. With a median follow-up of 42 months, no significant differences in the 3-year survival rates were observed between the VMAT and TOMO groups: OS (85.2% vs. 87.1%, P = 0.645), PFS (78.9% vs. 81.2%,P = 0.391), LRFS (91.3% vs. 93.1%, P = 0.505), and DMFS (85.7% vs. 87.4%, P = 0.383). Both techniques achieved high overall response rates (ORR) (96.7% vs. 97.6%, p = 0.418). For safety, xerostomia occurred significantly less frequently in the TOMO group (33.86% vs. 46.84%, p < 0.001), consistent with its superior ability to spare parotid glands, while other adverse events showed no significant intergroup differences.ConclusionsVMAT and TOMO yield comparable therapeutic efficacies in patients with locally advanced NPC. Both advanced radiotherapy techniques are effective options in multimodality care. The choice between them should be based on institutional capacity and patient-specific factors, including quality-of-life needs and target volume anatomy, rather than anticipated survival benefits.Keywords: Nasopharyngeal carcinoma, Intensity-modulated radiation therapy, Tomotherapy, Propensity score matching, Survival analysis

Background

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a unique malignancy with distinct geographic and ethnic distribution patterns, with the highest incidence rates observed in Southern China, Southeast Asia, and North Africa [1]. Unlike most head and neck cancers, NPC is strongly associated with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection and genetic predisposition, particularly among individuals of Chinese descent [2–4]. The anatomical location of the nasopharynx poses notable challenges to both diagnosis and treatment: its relative inaccessibility complicates clinical examination, and the tumor tends to present as locally advanced disease due to early local extension and cervical lymph node metastasis [5–7].

Radiation therapy remains the cornerstone treatment for NPC given its high radiosensitivity and anatomical constraints that often preclude surgical resection [8, 9]. The evolution of radiation delivery techniques has significantly improved treatment outcomes while reducing treatment-related morbidities. Traditional two-dimensional and three-dimensional conformal radiation therapy have been largely superseded by Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy (VMAT), which allows for precise dose escalation to target volumes while sparing adjacent critical structures such as the spinal cord, brainstem, temporal lobes, and salivary glands [10–12].​.

More recently, helical tomotherapy (TOMO) has emerged as an advanced form of VMAT that combines intensity modulation with helical delivery, potentially offering superior dose conformity and homogeneity [13–15].This helical delivery pattern confers theoretical advantages in treating complex, concave target volumes— a common scenario in NPC, where tumors often wrap around vital anatomical structures [14, 16].

Despite the theoretical advantages of TOMO, clinical evidence comparing its effectiveness with that of conventional VMAT for NPC remains limited. Previous studies have been constrained by small sample sizes, heterogeneous patient populations, or lack of appropriate statistical methodology to control for selection bias. Notably, inadequate bias control has led to inconsistent conclusions in the literature: some small-scale studies suggest marginal disease control advantages of TOMO, while others find no significant differences between the two techniques. This inconsistency underscores the need for large-scale studies with robust statistical designs, like PSM, to balance baseline characteristics and eliminate selection bias.

The present study addressed this knowledge gap by conducting a large-scale propensity score-matched retrospective analysis comparing the therapeutic outcomes of VMAT and TOMO in patients with locally advanced NPC. Our primary objective was to evaluate whether TOMO offers superior survival outcomes compared with VMAT. Secondary objectives included the assessment of treatment response rates, toxicity profiles, and identification of potential predictive factors for treatment selection.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study included patients with histologically confirmed NPC in Fujian Cancer Hospital between January 2016 and December 2019. Inclusion criteria were: (1) disease stage (AJCC 8th edition stage I-IVA); (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) Karnofsky Performance Status ≥ 70; (4) no prior malignancy or head/neck radiotherapy; (5) complete treatment and follow-up data. Exclusion criteria included distant metastasis at presentation, pregnancy, and severe comorbidities precluding definitive treatment. The institutional review board approved the study, and all patients provided written informed consent.

Treatment techniques​

All patients received definitive radiotherapy with either VMAT or TOMO. Target volume delineation followed international consensus guidelines [9]. The gross tumor volume (GTV) included the primary tumor and involved lymph nodes. The clinical target volume (CTV) encompassed the GTV plus elective nodal regions and potential subclinical extension. Planning target volumes (PTVs) were created by adding 3–5 mm margins to CTVs. Dose prescription was 66–70 Gy in 30–33 fractions to the GTV, 60–66 Gy to high-risk CTV, and 50–54 Gy to low-risk CTV. Systemic therapy included induction chemotherapy, concurrent chemotherapy, and adjuvant chemotherapy: all patients received platinum-based induction chemotherapy, among which the most commonly used regimens were TP (paclitaxel 135 mg/m² or docetaxel 60 mg/m² on day 1 plus cisplatin 80–100 mg/m² on day 1) and GP (gemcitabine 1000 mg/m² on days 1 and 8 plus cisplatin 80–100 mg/m² on day 1), with some patients receiving the TPF regimen; concurrent chemotherapy was administered as single-agent cisplatin (80–100 mg/m²) on day 1 and day 22 of radiotherapy; and adjuvant chemotherapy was determined based on the post-treatment response [10]. To ensure treatment safety and comparability, strict dose constraints for organs at risk (OARs) were implemented: parotid glands (mean dose ≤ 26 Gy), spinal cord (maximal dose ≤ 45 Gy), and brainstem (maximal dose ≤ 54 Gy).

Propensity score matching​

The nearest neighbor matching (1:1 ratio) was conducted with a caliper width set at 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score, a standard recognized for achieving an optimal balance between reducing bias and maintaining sample size.In survival analysis, the Cox proportional hazards model utilized the ‘Enter’ method for variable inclusion to assess the independent prognostic impact of treatment modality while adjusting for known clinical factors.

Outcomes and follow-up​

The primary endpoints were overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), locoregional recurrence-free survival (LRFS), and DMFS, defined from treatment completion to event or last follow-up. Secondary endpoints included objective response rate (ORR) per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 and adverse events graded by Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 4.0 [12].Patients were followed every 3 months for 2 years, then every 6 months for 3–5 years, with clinical examination, nasopharyngoscopy, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the head/neck, chest computed tomography (CT), and EBV DNA monitoring.​.

Statistical analysis​

Categorical variables were compared using χ² or Fisher’s exact tests and continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with log-rank tests. Cox proportional hazards models were used to calculated hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All analyses used SPSS version 26.0 (IBM) and R version 4.0.0, with p < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

We identified 1,974 patients with locally advanced NPC who met our inclusion criteria, with 1,720 (87.1%) treated with VMAT and 254 (12.9%) treated with TOMO. The median age across the entire cohort was 48 years (range: 18–78 years), and the male-to-female ratio was 2.8:1. The baseline characteristics before PSM revealed several notable differences between the two treatment groups (Table 1). Approximately 57.7% of patients (n = 1,138) were ≤ 50 years old, and 64.6% (n = 1,276) had T3–4 primary tumors, indicating a predominance of locally advanced disease. Nodal involvement was equally distributed, with 48.2% (n = 951) having N0–1 disease and 51.8% (n = 1,023) having N2–3 disease. Most patients (85.6%, n = 1,689) received induction chemotherapy, and 58.0% (n = 1,144) had negative pretreatment EBV-DNA. Statistically significant imbalances were observed in clinical stage distribution (p = 0.004), induction chemotherapy utilization (p < 0.001), concurrent chemotherapy cycles (p < 0.001), and pretreatment EBV-DNA status (p = 0.044) between the VMAT and TOMO groups, justifying the use of PSM to minimize selection bias.​

After PSM, 979 patients were included in the final analysis (728 in the VMAT group and 251 in the TOMO group). The matching process effectively balanced all baseline characteristics, as evidenced by SMDs < 0.1 for all variables (Table 2). In the matched cohort, 62.3% of patients (n = 610) were ≤ 50 years old, and 62.3% (n = 610) had T3–4 tumors—consistent with the original cohort’s disease severity. Nodal involvement remained balanced, with 53.0% (n = 519) having N0–1 disease and 47.0% (n = 460) having N2–3 disease. All between-group comparisons for baseline variables yielded p-values > 0.05, confirming successful elimination of selection bias.​

The median follow-up period for the matched cohort was 42 months (range: 6–96 months). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed no statistically significant differences between the VMAT and TOMO groups across all primary endpoints, both before and after PSM (Figs. 1 and 2). Before PSM, survival curves trended toward better outcomes with TOMO, but these differences were not statistically significant: LRFS (p = 0.091), DMFS (p = 0.090), OS (p = 0.233), and PFS (p = 0.051). After PSM, these trends attenuated further, suggesting that initial differences were likely due to baseline imbalances rather than treatment technique superiority. In the matched cohort, 3-year survival rates were comparable between groups (Table 3): OS was 85.2% (95% CI: 82.1–88.3) in the VMAT group vs. 87.1% (95% CI: 82.4–91.8) in the TOMO group (HR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.62–1.34, p = 0.645); PFS was 78.9% (95% CI: 75.4–82.4) vs. 81.2% (95% CI: 75.8–86.6) (HR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.63–1.21, p = 0.391); LRFS was 91.3% (95% CI: 88.8–93.8) vs. 93.1% (95% CI: 89.4–96.8) (HR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.61–1.27, p = 0.505); and DMFS was 85.7% (95% CI: 82.6–88.8) vs. 87.4% (95% CI: 82.7–92.1) (HR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.57–1.24, p = 0.383). Notably, hazard ratios consistently favored TOMO but did not reach statistical significance, indicating that both techniques provide similar long-term disease control.​

Treatment response was evaluated 3 months after completing radiotherapy (Table 4). Both modalities achieved high overall response rates (ORR), with 96.7% in the VMAT group and 97.6% in the TOMO group (p = 0.418). In the pre-matched cohort, response patterns were similar between groups: complete response (CR) was 16.7% (n = 287) in VMAT vs. 13.0% (n = 33) in TOMO (p = 0.098); partial response (PR) was 80.0% (n = 1,376) vs. 84.6% (n = 215) (p = 0.067); stable disease (SD) was 1.3% (n = 23) vs. 1.6% (n = 4) (p = 0.751); and progressive disease (PD) was 2.0% (n = 34) vs. 0.8% (n = 2) (p = 0.194). These findings confirm that both VMAT and TOMO effectively reduce tumor burden in locally advanced NPC.​

Toxicity analysis in the matched cohort focused on radiation-related complications common in NPC (Table 5). The most striking difference was in xerostomia, with significantly lower incidence in the TOMO group (33.9% vs. 46.8% in VMAT, p < 0.001)—a finding consistent with TOMO’s theoretical advantage in parotid gland sparing. Other adverse events showed no significant between-group differences: radiation-induced encephalopathy was 7.97% (TOMO) vs. 10.44% (VMAT, p = 0.256); hearing loss was 25.50% (TOMO) vs. 22.12% (VMAT, p = 0.272); neurological complications were 5.58% (TOMO) vs. 3.85% (VMAT, p = 0.243); and mucous membrane reaction was 4.78% (TOMO) vs. 5.63% (VMAT, p = 0.607). The similar rates of serious complications (e.g., encephalopathy) suggest that both techniques provide comparable protection of critical organs, with TOMO offering a specific benefit in reducing xerostomia.

Cox proportional hazards regression identified independent prognostic factors for survival, adjusting for all baseline characteristics. Treatment modality (VMAT vs. TOMO) was not associated with any primary endpoint (all p > 0.05). Significant predictors of worse outcomes included advanced T stage (HR = 1.62,95%CI: 1.14–2.30), advanced N stage (HR = 1.58,95%CI:1.12–2.23), and positive pretreatment EBV-DNA (HR = 1.45, 95%CI:1.03–2.04). The lack of association between treatment technique and survival further supports the equivalence of VMAT and TOMO in this patient population.

Discussion​

This large-scale propensity score-matched analysis represents one of the most comprehensive comparative studies of VMAT versus TOMO in locally advanced NPC to date. Our findings demonstrate comparable therapeutic efficacy between these two advanced radiation therapy techniques, with no significant differences in survival outcomes, treatment response rates, or most treatment-related adverse events.​.

The absence of significant survival differences between VMAT and TOMO groups suggests that both techniques represent effective treatment options for locally advanced NPC. The 3-year OS rates of 85.2% for VMAT and 87.1% for TOMO are consistent with contemporary reports from high-volume centers [13, 14] and reflect the excellent outcomes achievable with modern radiation therapy techniques combined with appropriate systemic therapy.​.

These findings contrast with theoretical expectations that TOMO might provide superior outcomes due to its enhanced dose conformity and daily image guidance capabilities. However, the lack of survival advantage may be explained by several factors. First, both VMAT and TOMO represent highly conformal techniques that achieve excellent target coverage while respecting dose constraints for OARs [17–19]. Second, the high radiosensitivity of NPC may make the differences in dose distribution between techniques less clinically relevant. Third, the relatively short follow-up period (median 42 months) may not be sufficient to detect small differences in long-term outcomes, though this timeframe aligns with typical endpoints for NPC efficacy studies [3].The significantly reduced incidence of xerostomia in the TOMO group (33.9% vs. 46.8%) is a critical finding. This dosimetric advantage is likely due to the helical delivery’s ability to achieve steeper dose gradients between the target and the parotid glands [20, 21]. From a clinical perspective, this suggests that for younger patients or those with high quality-of-life expectations, TOMO may be the preferred modality. Conversely, in regions where TOMO availability is limited, our findings reassure clinicians that VMAT remains a standard-of-care providing identical survival outcomes.

The superior parotid gland sparing achieved with TOMO likely reflects the advantages of helical delivery in treating complex target volumes while avoiding adjacent critical structures [5, 6]. The ability to deliver radiation from all angles during helical delivery may provide better dose conformity around irregularly shaped targets, such as those commonly encountered in NPC where the CTV often wraps around critical structures such as the brainstem, spinal cord, and major salivary glands [22–24].

Our results suggest that the choice between VMAT and TOMO for locally advanced NPC should be based on factors other than expected survival advantages, including institutional capabilities, resource availability, patient-specific anatomical considerations, and potential quality-of-life benefits. The superior xerostomia rates associated with TOMO may make it a preferred option for younger patients or those with high quality-of-life expectations, provided that institutional expertise and resources are available.​.

The comparable efficacy between techniques also suggests that centers with limited access to TOMO should not be discouraged, as excellent outcomes can be achieved with well-executed VMAT. This finding has important implications for resource allocation and treatment accessibility, particularly in regions where NPC is endemic but advanced radiation therapy equipment may be limited.​.

The strengths of this study include the large sample size (n = 1,974 before PSM), comprehensive PSM methodology to address selection bias, systematic collection of survival and toxicity data, and adequate follow-up period for assessment of primary endpoints. The use of PSM effectively balanced key prognostic factors such as EBV-DNA status and clinical stage, creating well-matched cohorts for comparison.We acknowledge several limitations. First, the retrospective design, despite PSM, cannot eliminate all potential unmeasured confounders. Second, the assessment of xerostomia was based on CTCAE toxicity grades rather than validated patient-reported Quality of Life (QoL) instruments, which may lack the sensitivity to capture subtle functional impairments. Lastly, while our follow-up is sufficient for survival, longer observation is required to fully evaluate late-onset toxicities like temporal lobe necrosis.

Future research should focus on prospective randomized controlled trials comparing VMAT and TOMO, ideally with quality-of-life assessments using validated instruments such as the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) QLQ-C30 and HN35 questionnaires. Long-term follow-up studies (≥ 5 years) are needed to evaluate late toxicities and their functional impact, as some radiation-induced effects (e.g., temporal lobe necrosis) may manifest beyond our current follow-up window.​.

Cost-effectiveness analyses would provide valuable information for healthcare decision-making, particularly in resource-constrained settings where TOMO’s higher capital costs may be prohibitive. The integration of artificial intelligence and adaptive radiation therapy techniques may further optimize treatment delivery for both platforms, potentially reducing toxicity while maintaining efficacy.​.

Finally, the investigation of biomarkers for radiation sensitivity and toxicity prediction—such as genetic polymorphisms in DNA repair pathways or salivary gland function markers—could enable personalized treatment selection and dose optimization, moving toward precision radiation oncology for NPC.

Conclusions

In this propensity score-matched analysis, VMAT and TOMO showed comparable efficacy in locally advanced NPC, with similar 3-year survival rates and treatment response rates.​Notably, tomotherapy significantly reduced xerostomia, offering a meaningful quality-of-life benefit without compromising tumor control.​The choice between these techniques should prioritize institutional resources, patient factors, and quality-of-life needs rather than survival expectations. Both remain valuable standard options, supporting informed clinical decision-making in NPC management.
