Online versus offline Fugl-Meyer lower extremity training for physical therapy students: a randomized study
Authors: Haesu Lee, Young Keun Woo, Kyue-nam Park, Sujin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStandardized training is essential for improving the accuracy and reliability of the Fugl-Meyer Assessment of the lower extremities (FMA-LE), yet evidence comparing online and offline education for this purpose is limited.ObjectiveTo compare online versus offline education for improving FMA-LE scoring accuracy among novice physical therapy students and to examine changes in accuracy across repeated practice sessions.MethodsForty-four students were assigned to offline (n = 24) or online (n = 20) education groups and received identical FMA-LE content delivered either in person or online. Participants completed four practice scoring sessions and one final test using video-recorded stroke cases. Scoring accuracy, defined as the percentage of exact agreement with expert ratings across 17 items, was analyzed using linear mixed-effects models, and item-level differences were explored using Fisher’s exact tests.ResultsBoth groups demonstrated significant improvements in overall scoring accuracy from baseline to the final test (F(1, 42) = 9.85, p = .003, partial η² = 0.16), with no significant difference between the online and offline education groups (F(1, 42) = 1.23, p = .273, partial η² = 0.03). Scoring accuracy improved progressively across the five sessions, with significant gains appearing at the third practice session (p = .009) and persisting through the final test (p = .007) relative to baseline. Additionally, certain items, such as synergistic ankle dorsiflexion and plantar flexion, remained more difficult, with no significant between-group differences.ConclusionsNo significant difference was observed between online and offline education in improving FMA-LE scoring accuracy, suggesting that online training may be a viable and effective alternative for standardized assessment training.

Introduction

Proper patient assessment is fundamental to effective motor rehabilitation after stroke [1, 2]. Standardized instruments rely on rater judgment, so consistent administration and scoring across evaluators are essential for reliability in clinical and research settings [3–6]. Accordingly, structured training is required during professional education and continuing development.

Educational approaches for therapists and students have shifted from face-to-face instruction to online learning formats [7–9]. While in-person training remains vital for hands-on clinical skills [10], it is constrained by geographic, scheduling, and public health-related barriers, driving interest in scalable online alternatives [11, 12]. Within this evolving landscape, it is important to determine whether complex clinical assessments, such as the Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA), one of the most widely used quantitative measures in stroke rehabilitation [13], can be effectively taught through online formats.

A previous study showed that online self-training reduced scoring errors and variability in the FMA-Upper Extremity among physical therapy students [5]. However, direct comparisons between online and offline FMA training are limited [14, 15], and no prior work has examined how repeated practice affects evaluators’ scoring performance. To address these gaps, physical therapy students in this study received FMA-Lower Extremity (FMA-LE) training either online or offline and participated in repeated practice sessions. Within competency-based health professions education, accurate scoring can be conceptualized as an observable assessment competency [16], and repeated practice aligns with deliberate practice principles that support skill refinement over time [17].

Therefore, this study aimed to compare the effectiveness of online and offline training approaches for FMA education and to provide evidence to guide best practices in standardized evaluator training. We hypothesized that both groups would improve with repeated practice and that online training would produce gains in scoring accuracy comparable to those of offline training under the study conditions.

Method

Study design and setting

This study was a two-arm parallel randomized educational trial comparing online versus offline education for scoring the FMA-LE, with repeated measures of scoring accuracy across four practice sessions and one final test over three weeks (Fig. 1B). The educational lecture was delivered either online or offline (face-to-face), whereas all scoring sessions were conducted online using video-recorded clinical cases. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Jeonju University (jjIRB-220728-HR-2022-0712), and written informed consent was obtained from all student participants.

Participants and materials

Student participants

Forty-four undergraduate students majoring in physical therapy (male = 18, female = 26) were recruited from the third year of the physical therapy program. Inclusion criteria were: (1) ability to learn and practice basic range of motion testing, manual muscle testing, and reflex testing; (2) currently receiving education related to clinical assessment tools; and (3) no prior experience scoring the FMA-LE [18]. Students who had prior knowledge or experience with the FMA (e.g., prior formal training or scoring practice) or who were unable to participate in the online education format were excluded.

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power (v3.1.9.7). Because the primary analysis used a linear mixed-effects model with repeated measurements, sample size estimation was based on a two-way repeated measures ANOVA (within–between interaction). Assuming a moderate effect size (f = 0.25), α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and a conservative within-subject correlation of 0.5, the minimum required sample size was 22 participants (11 per group). To allow for variability and potential dropouts and to support robust mixed-effects modeling, we targeted 40 participants (20 per group).

Assessment instrument: FMA-LE (training content)

The FMA-LE is designed to evaluate motor function in patients with stroke. The FMA consists of five domains: motor, sensory, balance, range of motion, and joint pain. The motor domain is further divided into upper and lower extremities. In this study, only the motor domain was included due to its high sensitivity [19].

The motor domain of the FMA-LE consists of 17 items, each scored on a three-point scale (0–2) by the evaluator. When administered by physical therapy professionals, the FMA-LE demonstrated excellent intra-rater reliability with an ICC (3,1) of 0.99 for the lower extremity motor domain. Inter-rater reliability between an FMA expert and therapist raters, who had completed a 2-day in-person training program, was also high, with an ICC (2,1) of 0.91 for the lower extremity motor scores [6]. The protocol used for the FMA-LE assessment is available at www.neurophys.gu.se/rehabmed.

Video-recorded stroke cases (training materials)

Six video-recorded clinical cases of stroke survivors with hemiparesis were used to develop FMA-LE scoring materials for student training. Their characteristics are presented in Table 1. Stroke cases 1–5 were used for practice videos, whereas case 6 was used for the test video. All cases involved individuals with chronic stroke, with FMA-LE scores ranging from 10 to 30 (mild to moderate impairment).

Intervention and procedure

Randomization and group allocation

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups: the online education or offline education group. Randomization was performed using a Latin square design [20]. The allocation sequence was generated by an investigator not involved in enrollment, and assignments were revealed after enrollment.

Educational intervention (online vs. offline)

Both groups received the same lecture content on the FMA-LE. The online education group attended the lecture via Google Meet and accessed instructional materials through the Korean Rehabilitation Outcome Measurement (KoROM) web platform (https://korom.co.kr/). The offline education group attended an in-person lecture covering identical content.

KoROM is a web-based platform that allows therapists to enter clinical test scores via a computer or tablet PC, replacing traditional paper-based assessments. Additionally, KoROM provides educational materials, including detailed instructional videos on how to administer the FMA-LE and explanations of its clinical importance (Fig. 1A). More information about KoROM can be found in a previous study [21]. Both groups received the same lecture content, covering the following topics: (1) Overview of the FMA, (2) How to assess and score the FMA, (3) Reliability of the FMA, (4) Predictive value of recovery, (5) Minimal detectable change, and (6) Minimal clinically important differences. For the online education group, lectures via the Google Meet lecture was recorded and uploaded to the KoROM platform, allowing participants to re-watch them it as needed [22]. However, the offline education group could not re-watch the lectures, as traditional classroom teaching sessions were not recorded. This discrepancy was intentionally designed to reflect real-world educational settings, in which the typical offline lecture was not reproducible.

Experimental procedure

The experiment lasted three weeks and consisted of one educational lecture followed by five FMA-LE scoring sessions (Fig. 1B). The first four scoring sessions served as practice, while the final session was administered as a final test. During all scoring sessions, participants scored the FMA-LE by watching pre-recorded videos of physical therapists assessing stroke patients. A total of six videos, each featuring a different stroke patient, were used. These videos featured patients with chronic stroke, with FMA-LE scores ranging from 10 to 30 (mild to moderate impairment). One video was carefully selected for the final test because the patient had ambiguous scores either between 0 and 1 or between 1 and 2, making the evaluation more challenging. The remaining videos were used for the scoring practice sessions. After each practice session, participants received the expert-rated answers and feedback for comparison after submitting their scores.

Data processing

The primary outcome was the overall accuracy of FMA-LE scoring, which was evaluated for each of the four practice sessions and the final test. The secondary outcomes included: (1) the number of participants who achieved accuracy above predefined cut-off levels (90%, 80%, and 70%) in the final test, and (2) the average proportion of correct responses for each FMA-LE item. Achieving 90% accuracy corresponded to ≥ 16 correct responses out of 17 items, whereas 80% and 70% corresponded to ≥ 14 and ≥ 12 correct responses, respectively.

For item-level analysis, the number of correct responses for each FMA-LE item was summed across all participants and divided by the total number of respondents, yielding the proportion of correct responses for each item, which reflects item difficulty. Additionally, item-level correct response proportions were compared between groups to examine potential differences in item performance. All data were verified for completeness, and no missing values were observed.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Differences in overall accuracy between the online and offline education groups over time were examined using a linear mixed-effects model. Sessions (first practice vs. final test) and group were included as fixed effects, and participants were modeled as random intercepts. Four nested models were constructed: Model 1 included only the session factor, Model 2 included only the group factor, Model 3 included both session and group, and Model 4 added the session × group interaction. Model comparisons were performed using the anova() function in R. The same procedure was repeated using all four practice sessions to explore the learning curve over sessions.

For secondary outcomes, Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the number of participants exceeding the cut-off scores between groups in the final test. For item-level analysis within each group, a binomial test was used to assess whether the proportion of participants who scored each FMA-LE item correctly exceeded 0.50 (50%), indicating that more than half of the participants identified the item correctly. Additionally, Fisher’s exact test was performed for each item to compare the proportion of correct responses between the online and offline education groups. Statistical significance was set at p = .05.

Results

Overall accuracy for group comparison before and after online and offline education

At the final test, overall scoring accuracy was 71.47% ± 15.3% (mean ± SD) in the online education group (n = 20) and 64.45% ± 20.1% in the offline education group (n = 24) (Fig. 2A). A linear mixed-effects model revealed a significant main effect of time, with both groups showing significant improvements in overall accuracy between the first practice session and the test session (F(1, 42) = 9.85, p = .003, partial η2 = 0.16). However, the main effect of group was not significant (F(1,42) = 1.23, p = .273, partial η2 = 0.03), and no significant group x time interaction was observed (p > .05), indicating that the change in accuracy over time did not differ significantly between the two education modalities (Fig. 2A).

Changes in accuracy depending on practice session and education type

Across the five scoring sessions, both groups demonstrated a significant main effect of time on overall accuracy (p < .01, partial η2 = 0.08). There was no significant interaction between session and education type ((F(4, 172) = 0.45, p = .772, partial η2 = 0.05) (Fig. 2B). Post-hoc analysis revealed that accuracy reached its peak at the third session (67.5% ± 2.55%, p = .009) and remained significantly higher at the final test compared to the first session (p = .007). No further significant improvements were noted between the third and subsequent sessions.

Comparison of pass/fail classification based on varying cut-off scores

The distribution of participants meeting different proficiency levels is presented in Table 2, based on accuracy thresholds of ≥ 70%, ≥ 80%, and ≥ 90%. At the ≥ 70% threshold, 15 participants in the online education group and 13 in the offline education group met the criterion. When the threshold was raised to ≥ 80%, the number of passing participants decreased to 5 and 7 in the online and offline education groups, respectively. At the ≥ 90% threshold, only one participant in the online group met the criterion, whereas none met the criterion in the offline education group (Table 2).

FMA-LE item comparisons within and between items

While both groups provided correct answers for most FMA-LE items, a significantly higher proportion of incorrect responses was observed for item 5 (synergistic ankle dorsiflexion) and item 9 (synergistic ankle plantar flexion) (Fig. 3). Based on a 0.50 (50%) benchmark, binomial tests showed that the proportion of correct responses for these two items was significantly below 0.50 in both groups (adjusted p < .001). After Fisher’s exact tests with multiple-comparison adjustment, no significant between-group differences were observed for any individual item (Table 3).

Discussion

This study examined the effectiveness of both online and offline educational approaches for training physical therapy students in the FMA-LE. The findings indicate that both methods significantly improved overall scoring accuracy, with no significant difference in learning outcomes observed between the two groups. Moreover, at least three practice sessions were necessary to achieve reliable and accurate scoring. These results suggest that the online training format may provide a comparable level of educational effectiveness to traditional offline training for teaching FMA-LE scoring. However, the scope of these findings is limited to training and performance on FMA-LE scoring using video-recorded cases. Therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating these results to broader clinical assessment competence, hands-on examination skills, or clinical management decisions.

Certain items within the FMA-LE (e.g., item 5 (synergistic ankle dorsiflexion) and item 9 (synergistic ankle plantar flexion) were identified as particularly challenging. This highlights specific areas that require enhanced instructional focus in future training. These findings provide practical implications for educators and healthcare professionals designing standardized training programs for FMA-LE assessment.

Overall accuracy for group comparison before and after online and offline education

The overall FMA-LE scoring accuracy improved from the first practice session to the final test session in both the online and offline education groups. This pattern suggests that, under the conditions tested, the online format appears to offer educational opportunities similar to the offline format in terms of improving FMA-LE scoring accuracy. A previous study by See et al. (2013) used online supplementary materials to train Doctor of Physical Therapy students in the FMA-upper extremity procedure and reported significant improvements in scoring accuracy [5]. Our findings align with those results, demonstrating that structured training with repeated practice can improve scoring accuracy in the lower extremity portion of the FMA.

However, these results should be interpreted with caution. When applying accuracy cut-off scores of 90% or 80%, fewer participants in both groups achieved correct answers on the final test. While the previous study reported minimal deviation between student scores and the answer key (0.9 ± 4.9 points), our participants produced more scoring errors. This discrepancy may be related to inherent differences between assessing the upper and lower extremities, particularly the difficulty of accurately evaluating subtle ankle movements through video (see FMA-LE item comparisons within and between groups section). Additionally, because our participants were undergraduate students, they may have had less baseline knowledge or motivation to achieve high accuracy in scoring the FMA-LE compared to the graduate-level students in the previous study.

No difference was found between the online and offline education groups in FMA-LE scoring accuracy. Because FMA-LE scoring depends primarily on clinical judgment and interpretation rather than hands-on skill performance, this result is broadly consistent with previous studies showing that the relative effectiveness of online and offline learning varies according to the type of task. Online learning has been associated with higher post-test knowledge and test scores [7], whereas lower satisfaction [23] and lower effectiveness for newly acquired manual skills have also been reported [8]. The comparable outcomes between modalities, together with the improvement observed across repeated sessions, support the interpretation proposed in the Introduction. However, because this study was not designed as an equivalence or non-inferiority trial with a pre-specified margin, the findings should be interpreted as showing no evidence of a difference between modalities within this sample rather than definitive equivalence.

Changes in accuracy depending on practice time

Scoring accuracy for both groups significantly improved by the third and final sessions compared to the first practice session. These results suggest that one or two practice sessions may be insufficient for novices to achieve proficiency; instead, a minimum of three sessions appears necessary to enhance assessment accuracy. While previous studies have reported high FMA-LE reliability [19, 24–27], those cohorts typically comprised experienced clinicians. In contrast, our participants were undergraduate students with no formal clinical experience. Our findings underscore the necessity of a structured learning process—incorporating repeated practice and error correction—consistent with the principles of deliberate practice, where iterative refinement through feedback facilitates effective learning for novice evaluators [17].

Item-level comparisons within and between groups

Among the 17 FMA-LE items, reflex-related items showed the highest accuracy (87.5%–98.5%), while synergistic ankle dorsiflexion (item 5) and plantarflexion (item 9) yielded the lowest accuracy (30.8%–36.0%). These results align with previous reports of high agreement for reflexes and lower consistency for distal ankle movements [27, 28]. The lower accuracy of the ankle items may reflect the combined demands of judging multi-joint coordination, detecting subtle compensations, and interpreting small distal excursions from limited video viewpoints [29]. These demands may be particularly challenging for novice raters, who may rely on less efficient observational and clinical reasoning strategies and experience greater cognitive load during real-time scoring [30].

Limitations and clinical application

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, participant engagement in the online group could not be verified because camera use was not required, which may have affected educational fidelity. Second, all scoring was based on pre-recorded videos rather than direct patient assessment, limiting generalizability to real-world clinical practice. Third, unequal access to lecture review, available only to the online group, may have confounded the results. Finally, although KoROM offers a more standardized training format than other online resources, these findings are limited to FMA-LE scoring accuracy and may not generalize to other instruments or full clinical administration.

Future studies are needed to examine the effectiveness of online training across a broader range of essential rehabilitation assessment tools, such as the Berg Balance Scale [31], Functional Gait Assessment [32], and Activities-Specific Balance Confidence Scale [33] (ANPT Stroke Recommendations). Despite these limitations, our results underscore that repeated practice is essential for achieving scoring proficiency.

Conclusion

This randomized study compared online and offline education for FMA-LE scoring among novice physical therapy students. Both modalities yielded similar improvements in scoring accuracy, with a significant “learning threshold” observed by the third practice session. These findings suggest that multiple practice opportunities are essential for developing proficiency, regardless of the delivery format. However, the results are specific to video-based scoring and may not generalize to in-person administration or other instruments. Future research should focus on optimizing online feedback and evaluating these strategies across broader clinical assessment contexts.
