A molecular switch from tumor suppressor to oncogene in ER+ve breast cancer: Role of androgen receptor, JAK-STAT, and lineage plasticity
Authors: Sarah Asemota, Wendy Effah, Jeremiah Holt, Daniel Johnson, Linnea Cripe, Suriyan Ponnusamy, Thirumagal Thiyagarajan, Yekta Khosrosereshki, Dong-Jin Hwang, Yali He, Brandy Grimes, Martin D. Fleming, Frances E. Pritchard, Ashley Hendrix, Meiyun Fan, Abhinav Jain, Hyo Young Choi, Liza Makowski, D. Neil Hayes, Duane D. Miller, Lawrence M. Pfeffer, Balaji Santhanam, Ramesh Narayanan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThis work evaluated the hypothesis that maintaining proteins in natural agonistic or holo/apo conformation in cancer will provide sustained tumor growth inhibition. Contrary to this hypothesis, sustained activation of tumor suppressor AR in ER-positive breast cancer results in aggressive resistant cancer due to lineage plasticity, transformation of tumor suppressor into oncogene, and activation of oncogene. The molecular portrait of the aggressive cancer corresponds to shorter patient survival. This result suggests that even maintaining some proteins in natural agonistic conformation in cancer is not sufficient to prevent resistance. The work also identifies transcription factor PROP paired-like homeobox 1 (PROP-1) signature as a potential facilitator of resistance and provides a roadmap for follow-on treatments using combinatorial approaches to manage resistance to AR agonists.

Targeted therapies for cancer rely on antagonists or inhibitors of oncogenes. Proteins in nature exist in an apo/holo or an activated conformation. Hence, the antagonist conformation of proteins is unnatural and likely unstable. This instability results in the oncogene reverting to its agonistic conformation by either mutating its binding residues, alternatively splicing to produce aggressively tumor-promoting variants, or utilizing a closely related protein to overcome the inhibition (1–3). Alternate strategies to treat cancer with an agonist of tumor suppressor will be ideal to maintain the protein in its natural agonistic conformation. Tumor suppressors currently are not pharmacological targets, making cancer treatment reliant on the strategy of deactivating oncogenes. Breast cancer (BC) is a classic example that has relied on antagonists or inhibitors of various proteins, including estrogen receptor (ER), aromatase (4–6), mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) (7, 8), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (EGFR/HER2) tyrosine kinases (9, 10), cyclin dependent kinase (CDK4/6) (11, 12), and Poly (ADP ribose) polymerase (PARP) (4, 13–15). The exception to this is the androgen receptor (AR), which is a ligand-activated tumor suppressor in ER-positive BC (16, 17), providing an opportunity to explore the hypothesis that prolonged activation of a tumor suppressor like AR will consistently uphold the protein in its natural active state. Consequently, this sustained activation is expected to prevent protein instability, thereby avoiding the development of resistance. While AR is a ligand-activated tumor suppressor in ER-positive BC, other proteins also play a context-dependent tumor suppressor role, though cannot be generalized, broadening the scope of this strategy to achieve growth inhibition (18, 19).

About 90% of ER-positive BCs are AR positive (20–23). Using BC cell lines and patient-derived models, we and others have shown that AR is a tumor suppressor in ER-positive BC (16, 17). AR agonists inhibited BC tumor growth by significantly shifting the AR, ER, and pioneer transcription factor (PTF) Forkhead box A1 (FOXA1) cistromes, resulting in an inhibition of ER function (16, 17). This mechanism of action could occur in patients depending on the intratumoral androgen levels (16, 17).

Androgens were used in the past to treat BC, but their use was discontinued due to the virilizing side effects and discovery of more effective therapies (24, 25). The discovery of tissue-selective nonsteroidal selective AR modulators (SARM) renewed interest in AR-targeted treatment (26–28). SARMs tissue selectively activate the AR with anabolic effects on muscle and bone mass, and growth inhibitory effect on BC, without virilizing side effects (26, 29–31). Currently, enobosarm is in phase III clinical trial (NCT04869943) for ER-positive BC (16, 17).

Despite initial success, cancers develop therapeutic resistance limiting the sustainability and effectiveness of treatment (13). Though some therapy resistance-promoting genetic mutations and epigenetic changes in molecular pathways are understood, combined therapies are sometimes needed to block several pathways to achieve efficacy (5, 13). Inhibiting a target over a period of time results in resistance due to several mechanisms, including overexpression (trastuzumab) (32), mutations in the binding domain (tamoxifen) (33), or upregulation of a parallel oncogenic pathway (lapatinib) (13, 34, 35).

Here, we show that maintaining agonist conformation of the context-dependent tumor suppressor AR for a sustained period will result in resistance development. AR switches from being a tumor suppressor to an oncogene with a complete shift in AR, ER, and FOXA1 cistromes, an increased Janus kinase/signal transducer (JAK/STAT) pathway activation, and plasticity toward a less differentiated and more aggressive subtype, which are all classic hallmarks of evolution. This metamorphic tumor phenotype responds to individual treatment options antagonizing or inhibiting AR and JAK individually or by combinatorial approaches.

Results

We extensively characterized AR’s role in ER-positive BC using a multiomics approach in various translational models (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). SI Appendix, Fig. S1B describes the cellular state of ER-positive BC and two terms repeatedly used in the manuscript. Elastic describes a reversible cellular state, and plastic, which describes the permanent shift from one cellular type to another.

AR-High BC Cells Have Lower Expression of Oncogenic Pathways.

The cancer genome atlas (TCGA) analysis indicated that higher AR expression in ER-positive BC contributes to better survival, while in ER-negative BC to lower survival (16, 36). This suggests a complex function for AR in BC, which seems to depend on the expression of driver oncogene ER. This was supported by our previous finding that AR agonists reduced ER-positive BC xenograft growth by inhibiting ER function and reprograming ER and FOXA1 cistromes such that FOXA1 shifts from binding to FOX response elements (FXREs) adjacent to estrogen response elements (EREs) to FXREs adjacent to androgen response elements (AREs) (16). Although AR has been a therapeutic target for over five decades in BC, it is unclear how AR-expressing ER-positive BC cells contribute to the tumor microenvironment and to tumor growth, which we elucidated with spatial genomics. Six formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded patient specimens (Nanostring, GeoMx; SI Appendix, Fig. S1C) were stained for AR and panCK (cancer epithelial marker) (37, 38). Cancer cells expressing high and low AR were captured (region of interest shown in SI Appendix, Figs. S1D and S2A) and sequenced (500 cells/cell type/patient specimen; SI Appendix, Table ST1). AR expression was nuclear in cells, suggesting that the AR is either constitutively nuclear or has translocated to the nucleus due to ligand binding (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A, Right; magnified images from two specimens) and hence likely to be functional. Paired t test analysis followed by Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) showed that pathways associated with worse prognosis and tumor progression, including HALLMARK_EPITHELIAL_MESENCHYMAL_TRANSITION, HALLMARK_PI3K_AKT_MTOR_SIGNALING, HALLMARK_MTORC1_SIGNALING, were represented by the genes down-regulated in AR-high cells or genes higher in AR-low cells (SI Appendix, Figs. S1E, red boxes). It is also important to note that the down-regulated genes in AR-high cells represent two distinct tamoxifen resistance pathways. This result is in contrast with the spatial result obtained in triple negative breast cancer (TNBC), confirming the TCGA analysis results that AR functions context dependently in BC (36). The genes that were different between AR-high and -low cells were evaluated for survival using the TCGA database. Genes enriched in AR-high cells contribute to better overall survival of BC patients (SI Appendix, Figs. S1F). All ER-target genes were expressed in AR-high and -low cell types, and no significant differences, other than progesterone receptor (down-regulated in AR-high cells), were observed between the two cell types (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). High expression of luminal keratins (KRT8, KRT18, and KRT19) was observed, while low to no expression of basal keratins was observed (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). No significant differences in keratin expression between AR-high and -low cells were observed (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). These results suggest that AR expression protects ER-positive BC cells from becoming aggressive and resistant. In addition, AR expression is an excellent prognostic marker in ER-positive BC, and the expression of AR possibly protects the tumor microenvironment. The entire list of genes from spatial is provided in supplement file ST1.

ER-Positive BC Cells Are Lineage Elastic.

Next, we investigated the effect of AR agonists on alterations in the tumors at the single-cell level. Enobosarm, an AR agonist that selectively binds to and activates AR at less than 10 nM (28, 39, 40), was used in our studies. Results were validated with dihydrotestosterone (DHT) and R1881 (41). As naturally occurring androgens such as DHT and testosterone are susceptible to metabolism, we used a synthetic nonsteroidal SARM resistant to androgen-inactivating metabolism (28, 42, 43). Xenograft experiments were performed with ER-positive BC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model WHIM23 (Fig. 1A) and the results were confirmed with T47D cell line xenograft (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Consistent with the results shown in HCI-7 and HCI-13 PDXs (16), AR agonists inhibited the growth of these tumor xenografts. While AR agonists completely inhibited the growth of T47D tumors, they inhibited WHIM23 PDX by ~50%. Mice in vehicle-treated WHIM23 PDX groups attained euthanasia criteria (3,000 mm3) around 55 d after tumor uptake. At the same time, most of the animals bearing WHIM23 tumors treated with DHT or enobosarm did not reach euthanasia criteria until the end of the study (Fig. 1B). The animals were euthanized according to the protocol approved by the University of Tennessee Health Science Center, Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC)-approved protocol (protocol number 20-0167; 23-0432). Comparable results were observed in T47D tumor-bearing mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). Tumors were collected at the end of treatment, homogenized, and bulk RNA-seq and single-nucleus RNA-seq (snRNA-seq) were performed. GSEA of genes that were enriched in DHT and enobosarm-treated WHIM23 tumors showed downregulation of several oncogenic pathways (Fig. 1C). These results were reproduced in T47D tumors showing comparable inhibition of pathways (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). TCGA analysis of the genes up-regulated in the AR agonist–treated WHIM23 tumors corresponded to better overall survival of BC patients (Fig. 1D).

Analysis of the WHIM23 and T47D tumors by snRNA-seq showed that the cells clustered distinctly based on the treatments [Fig. 1E (WHIM23 vehicle vs. enobosarm), SI Appendix, Fig. S3D (T47D vehicle vs. enobosarm), and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–D (WHIM23 and T47D vehicle vs. DHT)]. WHIM23 and T47D tumors from animals treated with AR agonists had lower basal and higher luminal signatures, which suggests that AR agonists enrich for cells with a more luminal differentiated state and diminish the number of residual undifferentiated basal cells. This was confirmed by determining the expression of keratin 18 (KRT18), a luminal keratin that was significantly increased by enobosarm (Fig. 1E). The results were further confirmed by immunofluorescence for luminal KRT18 and basal keratin 5 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E). Tumor cells had high expression of KRT18, which was further increased by enobosarm in both models. Keratin 5 was expressed at low to undetectable levels and no further change was observed in its expression in the treatment group. This points to ER-positive BC cells being lineage elastic. These cells also had a higher AR signature and lower ER signature, an expected response to AR agonist treatment (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Figs. S3D and S4 A–D) (44). Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) and early region 2 binding factor (E2F) signaling pathways associated with tumor progression and metastasis (45–47) were also significantly decreased in AR agonist–treated tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 B and D). These data further prove that ER-positive BC treated with AR agonists exhibit markers of better prognosis at the cellular and molecular level and show mechanistically at the cellular level how a tumor suppressor agonist inhibits tumor growth. Taken together, in ER-positive BCs, AR expression and hence activity protects against aggressive tumor evolution, and in this context, AR activity could be positively modulated for better prognosis.

ER- and AR-Positive BC Xenografts Develop Resistance to Persistent AR Agonist Treatment.

Considering that we propose to treat ER-positive BC with tumor suppressor agonists, we expected the AR to remain in a natural agonistic conformation so that tumors will not develop resistance. To determine whether ER/AR-positive BC could have a sustained response to AR agonist treatment, three BC models were chosen (WHIM23, T47D, and ZR-75-1), which all express AR and ER. Western blot, gene expression, and proliferation assays were performed to evaluate ER and AR expression and function (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–G). ZR-75-1 and T47D cells and WHIM-23 express functional AR (48). Treatment of ZR-75-1 and T47D cells with synthetic androgen, R1881, inhibited ER-target gene expression and proliferation, collectively suggesting that the AR in these models is functional and blocks ER function.

WHIM23 tumor fragments and T47D cells were implanted under the mammary fat pad in female NSG mice, and the mice were treated with AR agonists, DHT and enobosarm [Fig. 2A (WHIM23) and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A (T47D)]. One group of mice from the treatment group with tumors that were still sensitive to AR agonist treatment was killed after four weeks of treatment and was labeled “sensitive” (ARaS—AR agonist Sensitive). The remaining mice continued treatment until the tumors attained the volume of vehicle control mice. These tumors were called “resistant” (ARaR—AR agonist Resistant). The resistance development was observed in WHIM23 tumors treated with another clinically developed SARM, RAD140 (49) (Fig. 2B), suggesting that resistance is inevitable irrespective of the AR agonist. This resistance development was confirmed in a cell line model, ZR-75-1. ZR-75-1 cells were treated with 1 µM enobosarm for ~5 mo with medium change every third day and passaged when required. Resistance was evaluated using BrdU and gene expression assays (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 B–D). As anticipated, ARaR cells no longer responded to AR agonists (R1881, enobosarm, and RAD140), while ARaS cells responded to AR agonist’s inhibition of proliferation and AR target gene activation (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 B and C). Interestingly, ARaR cells also failed to respond to estradiol (E2) stimulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D), suggesting that when cells are resistant to AR agonist, possibly ER function is affected as well.

Since tumors resistant to one oncogenic inhibitor can be still sensitive to a structurally distinct inhibitor, i.e., tumors resistant to tamoxifen can still be sensitive to fulvestrant (50), we expected the ARaR tumors to be responsive to another AR agonist. To test this hypothesis, ARaR WHIM23 tumors were also treated with RAD140 (49), and vice versa (Fig. 2 C and D). WHIM23 tumor-bearing mice were treated with enobosarm or RAD140 and treatments were switched when the tumors developed resistance. The ARaR tumors failed to respond to an alternate SARM. These data collectively show that ER/AR-positive BC do become resistant when persistently treated with an AR agonist and interestingly, this resistance cannot be reversed/overcome with a structurally distinct ligand. In addition, Sanger sequence data also point out the absence of mutation in the ligand-binding pocket as the driver of resistance.

ARaR Tumors Have Altered Oncogenic Signaling, Leading to a Switch from Lineage Elasticity to Plasticity.

As the results disprove our hypothesis that activating tumor suppressor AR will produce sustained tumor growth inhibition, we conducted studies to explore the mechanism of resistance development. We performed bulk RNA-seq in the ARaS and ARaR WHIM23 and T47D tumors and compared the transcriptomic changes. GSEA was performed to understand the pathways altered under various conditions [Fig. 3A (WHIM23); SI Appendix, Fig. S7A (T47D)]. Several pathways including hallmark EMT, KRAS, interferon α, and inflammation were increased in both models, while other pathways such as PI3K-AKT, JAK/STAT, and androgen response were increased in one of the two models. ARaR WHIM23 tumors showed enrichment of HALLMARK_MYC_V2 and HALLMARK_MITOTIC_SPINDLE pathways compared to ARaS tumors. Both pathways have been correlated to poor prognosis and poor treatment response respectively (51, 52). Surprisingly, ARaR T47D tumors were enriched for HALLMARK_ANDROGEN_RESPONSE, suggesting that AR signaling is increased in ARaR tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). Hallmark pathways associated with more aggressive tumor type such as KRAS signaling (HALLMARK_KRAS_UP and HALLMARK_KRAS_DN) and EMT (HALMMARK_EPITHELIAL_MESENCHYMAL_TRANSITION) were enriched in both WHIM23 and T47D ARaR tumors (Fig. 3 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B) (45, 53). In addition, multiple hallmark JAK/STAT signaling pathways that have been implicated in the lineage plasticity of breast and prostate cancers (PCa) (54–56) were also enriched in the ARaR tumors (Fig. 3A WHIM23 and SI Appendix, Fig. S7A T47D tumors; shown in green boxes; minimum enrichment observed in WHIM23 and robust enrichment observed in T47D tumors), and interferon and inflammation signaling in both models. Representative enrichment plots are shown for EMT, KRAS, and interferon signaling (Fig. 3B WHIM23 and SI Appendix, Fig. S7B T47D tumors). Representative genes from EMT, JAK/STAT, AR, and stem signatures were selected from WHIM23 and T47D tumors to be evaluated in the RNA-seq data. Relative gene expression between sensitive and resistant WHIM23 tumors is shown in the heatmap (Fig. 3C: T47D shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S7C). ARaR tumors exhibit an increase in EMT, JAK/STAT, AR, and stem signature genes. Similarly, ARaR WHIM23 and T47D tumors have reduced expression of luminal keratins 8 and 18 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7D). This suggests that ARaR tumors are gaining a more aggressive and basal-like phenotype and also shows a potential lineage plasticity in resistant tumors compared to sensitive tumors.

Since the molecular portrait of AR agonist–treated tumors corresponded to better overall survival, we evaluated the overall survival of patients expressing the molecular profile of WHIM23 ARaR tumors using the TCGA database (Fig. 3D). The genes down-regulated in ARaR tumors corresponded to poor patient outcome. Similar results were observed with T47D ARaR down-regulated genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S7E).

AR, ER, and FOXA1 Cistromes Are Reprogrammed in ARaR Tumors.

We next sought to identify whether AR, ER, and FOXA1 cistromes are reprogrammed in ARaR tumors. Previous Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-seq studies in PDX HCI-13 showed that in response to enobosarm AR and ER cistromes reprogram at AREs with FOXA1 binding to FXREs adjacent to AREs (16). This clearly shows that ER is inactivated while AR signaling is activated. This finding is again reproduced in DHT-treated WHIM23 tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S8) with a gain in ARE binding and a loss of ERE binding for AR and ER chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq). In addition, AR ChIP-seq also shows that the binding to transcription factor PROP paired-like homeobox 1 (PROP1) motif is lost in DHT-treated tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). ChIP-seq was performed using the WHIM23 ARaR tumors and the results were compared to ARaS tumors (tumors from Fig. 2A). AR, ER, and FOXA1 were immunoprecipitated and the bound DNA regions were sequenced (Fig. 4 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S9C). The significant differentially down motifs in the AR ChIP were GRE, ARE, PR, and FOXA1, suggesting that irrespective of an enrichment of AR signaling in ARaR tumors, AR’s binding to its response elements is compromised. In addition to the differentially down-regulated binding of AR to AREs, AR binding to ARE, ERE, GRE, and FOXA1RE was lost (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A and B). In addition to disrupted AR binding, ER binding is also further lost (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S10A) at ERE, ARE, GRE, and FOXA1RE in ARaR tumors. Next, we examined the binding of AR and ER at loci located near AR and ER-target genes. ARaR tumors had reduced AR and ER binding at AR/ER target gene loci (FKBP5, GREB1, CTSD, etc.) (Fig. 4C). While AR and ER binding to their binding sites are lost, AR and ER binding was gained at transcription factor binding site, PROP1. Though PROP1 expression or function has not been evaluated in BC, PROP1 mutations have been shown to be the most common cause of hypopituitarism (57), and reportedly essential for stimulating stem cells to undergo an EMT-like process necessary for cell migration and differentiation (58). Genomic profiling revealed that PROP1 binds to EMT transducer genes such as Zeb2, Notch2, and Gli2 (58). These data combined with JAK/STAT pathway enrichment in RNA-seq could be contributing factors for lineage plasticity such as EMT.

Since minimum information is available on PROP1’s expression in most cancers, we evaluated PROP1 expression in Broad Institute’s cancer cell line encyclopedia (CCLE) (59, 60). The CCLE database of all BC cell lines characterized by RNA seq, copy number ratio, and somatic mutations indicated that cell lines that are characterized as invasive breast carcinoma had statistically higher copy number and expression of PROP1, compared to other subtypes. Interestingly, all BC lines had higher copy number ratio of PROP1 compared to noncancerous breast cell line and low-grade ER-positive ductal carcinoma in situ (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A).

Since PROP1 copy number and expression are higher in invasive breast carcinoma cell lines, it is critical to understand its expression in various cancers and correlation to aggressiveness. To determine whether the expression of PROP1 corresponds to any cancer type and stage, we evaluated the gene expression profile of PROP1 across multiple cancer types in the TCGA database (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). While PROP1 expression was extremely low in the majority of the cancers, its expression was higher in metastatic breast and PCa, and castration-resistant PCa (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B red box), which are hormonal cancers and have a relationship to AR. This correlation suggests that PROP1 plays a potential role in aggressive hormonal cancers and could be a driver oncogene in aggressive cancers.

FOXA1 is a PTF that facilitates chromatin binding and function of transcription factors (61). Previous studies have shown that FOXA1 facilitates ER binding to EREs in ER-positive BC and AR to AREs in PCa and BCs (16, 17, 61, 62). In ARaR tumors compared to ARaS tumors, FOXA1 binding shifts between multiple FOX family response elements (SI Appendix, Figs. S9C and S10). Again, these data suggest that binding of not only AR and ER but also PTF, FOXA1, is dysfunctional in ARaR tumors. Collectively, the ChIP-seq data show that the transcription factor binding is completely rewired and the downstream consequence of this rewiring needs to be evaluated.

We evaluated whether the genes in GSEA pathways that were enriched in RNA-seq were also altered in ChIP-seq. Using genes from the different hallmark pathways and the AR ChIP-seq differentially up annotated binding sites, we compared the RNA-seq data from WHIM23 ARaR tumors to the ChIP-seq data (SI Appendix, Fig. S9D). Each one of the hallmark pathways (EMT, KRAS, Wnt, Inflammatory, and NOTCH), which are associated with a more aggressive tumor phenotype, overlapped with the AR ChIP-seq differentially up loci and had increased gene expression in ARaR tumors when compared to sensitive tumors. These data collectively suggest that AR binding to DNA is compromised (AR no longer has the ability to bind AREs), ER DNA binding is further disrupted in ARaR tumors, and AR and ER bind to unrelated transcription factor binding sites. All these data collectively contribute to a resistant basal-like aggressive phenotype.

ARaR Tumor Cells Have an Aggressive Phenotypic Signature.

Considering the significant shift in DNA binding combined with a dramatic shift in gene signature, we speculated that the tumor microenvironment is altered at the cellular and molecular level leading to an aggressive state. To evaluate the heterogeneous nature of the tumor microenvironment, snRNA-seq was performed on the ARaR tumors from the xenograft experiments, and the ARaR tumors were compared to the ARaS tumors [Fig. 5 (WHIM23 tumors) and SI Appendix, Fig. S11 (T47D tumors)]. ARaS and ARaR tumor nuclei separated into defined clusters. ARaR cells in both models showed a higher basal signature and a lower luminal signature when compared to sensitive cells. Individual luminal KRT18 was also significantly reduced in ARaR WHIM23 tumors (Fig. 5A). This suggests that ARaR tumors have a more aggressive basal phenotype than ARaS cells. KRAS signature, which was an enriched pathway in the bulk RNA-seq, is higher in the ARaR tumor cells (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S11A). Wnt and E2F signaling are also associated with more aggressive types of cancer (46, 47, 63). These signatures were increased in ARaR tumor cells when compared to ARaS tumor cells (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S11B). The expression is quantified for each signature and represented as violin plots. The violin plots for the signatures are all statistically significant with P-values reaching theoretical 0.

One of the interesting observations made in ChIP-seq studies is that motif for PROP1 is the top down-regulated motif in AR agonist-sensitive WHIM23 tumors (compared to vehicle treatment), while PROP1 is the top enriched motif in enobosarm-resistant WHIM23 tumors. Considering that PROP1 motifs are enriched in resistant tumors and that PROP1 expression is high in invasive BCs, we evaluated PROP1 expression in bulk and snRNA-seq. Unfortunately, cells did not express PROP1, leaving us with the possibility that AR is attaining promiscuity and binding to PROP1 motifs. To confirm this hypothesis, we obtained a list of PROP1 target genes (PROP1 signature: ADAM15, MMP2, MMP16, ZEB2, and CLDN23) from a PROP1 knockout model (64). Interestingly, PROP1 signature was significantly down-regulated in enobosarm-treated WHIM-23 tumors compared to vehicle-treated tumors (Fig. 5B). However, the signature was significantly increased in enobosarm-resistant WHIM23 tumors, suggesting that the AR binding to PROP1 response elements is driving the expression of PROP1 target genes and not PROP1 expression itself. This needs to be validated in future studies.

Cell cycle analysis was performed. ARaR tumors had more cells in the S phase when compared to cells in ARaS tumors [Fig. 5C (WHIM23) and SI Appendix, Fig. S11C (T47D)]. This suggests that ARaR tumors are proliferating more rapidly than the ARaS tumors. These data suggest that ARaR tumor cells have become more aggressive and have attained plasticity.

AR Switches from Being a Tumor Suppressor to Being an Oncogene That Leads to a TNBC-Like State.

Considering that the AR signaling was increased in bulk RNA seq and that the AR cistrome is completely altered, AR signature in snRNA-seq was evaluated in ARaR tumors and compared to ARaS tumors. We speculated that the AR might have had a gain of oncogenic function. Western blot on ARaS and ARaR tumors showed that AR protein expression was increased in WHIM23 ARaR tumors and enobosarm-resistant ZR-75-1 cells (Fig. 6 A and B). snRNA-seq data demonstrated an increase in AR signature in WHIM23 ARaR tumors (Fig. 6C). These results were reproduced in T47D tumors with ARaR tumors showing higher AR expression and AR signature than ARaS tumors (SI Appendix, Figs. S12 A and B). Given that AR protein expression and AR signaling are increased in ARaR tumors and cells, we hypothesized that these tumors and cells may have switched AR function from being a tumor suppressor to being an oncogene and that these cells have become more plastic in nature. ER signature was also significantly reduced in T47D ARaR tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S12B) and minimally in WHIM23 ARaR tumors (Fig. 6C), validating the hypothesis.

TNBC is subclassified into six subtypes. One is luminal AR TNBC (LAR TNBC), where AR is an oncogene and the tumors have enriched AR signaling and androgen-synthesizing enzymes (65, 66). We speculated that ARaR ER-positive BC has been transformed into TNBC-like state. Using the previously reported gene signatures, various TNBC subtypes were evaluated in the snRNA-seq (66). Basal-like 1 (BL1), basal-like 2 (BL2), and LAR signatures were all significantly enriched in ARaR tumors, validating our hypothesis that the ARaR tumors have molecularly switched from ER-positive to TNBC phenotype [Fig. 6D (WHIM23 tumors) and SI Appendix, Fig. S12C (T47D tumors)]. To determine whether the increase in AR expression is due to an increase in the intensity of AR or increase in the number of cells staining for AR, we performed immunofluorescence for AR in WHIM23 and T47D tumors that are sensitive and resistant to enobosarm (SI Appendix, Fig. S12D). Although AR is ubiquitously expressed in cells, the immunofluorescence staining indicates that AR staining intensity increased in enobosarm resistance compared to sensitive tumors, suggesting that the expression of AR in a cell is increasing than the number of cells expressing AR. AR and ER immunofluorescence staining was performed to determine the colocalization of the two proteins between sensitive and resistant tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S12E). Both proteins were ubiquitously expressed and no difference in localization between the sensitive and resistant tumors could be visualized.

Since AR signaling is still robust and the gene signature matched with the signature of TNBC, we hypothesized that treating ARaR tumors with an AR antagonist may inhibit ARaR cell and tumor growth. Enzalutamide, a ligand-binding domain-binding AR antagonist, and UT-105, an N-terminal domain-binding AR degrader (36), were used to evaluate this hypothesis (Fig. 6E) (67–71). An sulforhodamine B assay was performed in ARaS and ARaR ZR-75-1 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S12F). ARaR cells grew 2.5-fold faster than the ARaS cells with growth inhibited by enzalutamide. ARaS ZR-75-1 cell proliferation was unaffected by AR antagonist enzalutamide. To validate these findings in vivo, ARaR WHIM23 tumors were treated with enzalutamide or UT-105 (Fig. 6F). UT-105 significantly inhibited ARaR tumor growth compared to vehicle- and enzalutamide-treated tumors, indicating that only powerful AR antagonists such as degraders can induce a significant response in ARaR tumors, rather than a competitive binding antagonist. AR western blot in tumors showed AR downregulation in UT-105-treated tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S12G) as was observed in LAR TNBC (36). Since AR signaling is enriched and AR expression is increased, competitive antagonists may not have the ability to overcome the enriched AR signaling caused by persistent treatment with AR agonists such as enobosarm or DHT. These data suggest that ARaR ER-positive BC cells are in a lineage plastic state and that these cells are transitioning into a more TNBC-like state. Since UT-105, but not enza, inhibited the tumor growth, we expected the expression of AR splice variants in WHIM23 and T47D ARaR tumors, as was observed in LAR TNBC (36). Entire gel blots showed no evidence for AR splice variants (SI Appendix, Fig. S12H). Tumor cell proliferation in vehicle and UT-105-treated tumors was determined by immunohistochemistry for ki67 (SI Appendix, Fig. S12I). Tumors in the UT-105-treated group had very low proliferation as demonstrated by low ki67 staining.

JAK/STAT Inhibition Resensitizes ARaR Cells to AR Agonists.

JAK/STAT signaling has been shown to cause lineage plasticity in BC and PCa (54–56, 72). In PCa, JAK/STAT activation has also been shown to induce lineage plasticity in enzalutamide-resistant PCa cells and tumors (54, 55). Inhibition of JAK/STAT signaling with ruxolitinib, a JAK1/2 inhibitor, reversed enzalutamide-resistant PCa cells to an AR antagonist-sensitive state (54, 55). Bulk RNA-seq showed an enrichment of interferon, inflammation, and JAK/STAT pathways in resistant tumors (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). To assess the role of JAK/STAT signaling in -ARaR tumor cells, the expression of JAK/STAT signature genes was evaluated in the snRNA-seq dataset. The JAK/STAT signature was increased in ARaR cells in both tumor models [Fig. 7A (WHIM23 tumors) and SI Appendix, Fig. S13A (T47D tumors)]. Various members of the JAK tyrosine kinase family are activated by interferons (73, 74). Once activated they phosphorylate STAT1 and STAT3, which then form either homo- or heterodimers and bind to interferon-stimulated response elements on the DNA, resulting in the induction of interferon response genes (73, 74). Past studies have established the relationship between STAT3 and cancer stem cells and found that the IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 pathway is preferentially activated in CD44+CD24− stem-enriched BC cells, where it is required for their growth (56, 72). Western blot for phospho-STAT3 demonstrated that STAT3 is activated in ARaR tumors [Fig. 7B (WHIM23), SI Appendix, Fig. S13B (T47D)]. These data suggest that the JAK/STAT pathway is activated in ARaR tumors and potentially this pathway combined with oncogenic AR is causing the lineage plasticity toward LAR TNBC.

To determine the consequence of JAK/STAT pathway activation to patient survival, we stratified ER-positive BC patients in the TCGA database into low and high expression and performed survival analysis (Fig. 7C). Patients with high JAK/STAT expression have lower survival compared to patients with lower JAK/STAT signaling.

To determine the effects of the JAK/STAT pathway in ARaS and ARaR ER-positive BC, we determined the effect of inhibiting JAK/STAT signaling to the sensitivity of ZR-75-1 cells to enobosarm treatment. In the ARaS cells, ruxolitinib, a JAK 1/2 inhibitor, did not significantly inhibit ZR-75-1 cell growth alone, and there was no synergy with enobosarm treatment (Fig. 7D). In contrast, ARaR cell growth was significantly inhibited by ruxolitinib treatment alone (Fig. 7D). Interestingly, ARaR cell growth was further inhibited with combination of ruxolitinib and enobosarm treatment (Fig. 7D). This suggests that inhibiting the JAK/STAT signaling pathway with ruxolitinib can enable cells to revert back to an enobosarm-sensitive state.

In order to characterize the mechanism of lineage plasticity, we performed pseudotime reconstructing trajectory analysis [Fig. 7 E and F (WHIM23 tumors) and SI Appendix, Fig. S13 C and D (T47D tumors)]. We started with the transcriptional landscape of the ARaS and ARaR tumors and tracked the changes of cell lineage states. The increased pseudotime correlated with cell fitness (75). It is evident that cell fitness increases as cells become more resistant to AR agonist treatment. The arrows show the hypothesized direction of cell movement. This shows a clear path from ARaS cells to ARaR cells. ARaR tumors had a significant increase in cell fitness and decrease in pseudotime of the luminal cells, while ARaS tumors had a decrease in cell fitness and increase in psuedotime of luminal cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 A and B). Notably, an increase in cell fitness also conferred with an increase in the psuedotime of the BL1, BL2, and LAR signatures [Fig. 7F (WHIM23 tumors) and SI Appendix, S13C (T47D tumors)]. The psuedotime of the JAK/STAT and AR signatures also increased cell fitness (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 A and B). This further established the need to either inhibit the JAK/STAT pathway using a JAK1/2 inhibitor or inhibit both the JAK/STAT pathway and AR using an AR degrader. Altogether, these data demonstrate that these tumors exist in a multilineage state and that as the cells become more resistant to AR agonist treatment, their cell fitness increases, and the psuedotime of aggressive phenotypic signatures increases. Collectively, the results show the trajectory by which differentiated luminal cells become less differentiated basal cells when resistance to AR agonists develops.

To understand the mechanism by which AR agonist–resistant cells are sensitized to AR agonist by ruxolitinib, ZR-75-1-resistant cells were treated for 7 d with enobosarm alone or a combination of enobosarm and ruxolitinib, and RNA sequencing was performed. The results were compared to RNA sequence results from ZR-75-1 parental cells treated with enobosarm for 7 d (SI Appendix, Fig. S15A). Principal component analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S15B) showed a completely distinct gene expression between the sensitive and resistant cells treated with enobosarm and between sensitive cells treated with enobosarm and resistant cells treated with enobosarm and ruxolitinib. However, there were minimum differences between resistant cells treated with enobosarm and those treated with enobosarm and ruxolitinib, suggesting that this minimum difference is potentially the driver of resensitization. GSEA clearly showed the enrichment of the JAK/STAT pathway in resistant cells that was reversed partially by ruxolitinib. Several hallmark pathways (Myc, androgen response, PI3K_AKT_mTOR, and E2F) that were enriched in resistant cells were completely reversed by ruxolitinib (SI Appendix, Fig. S15D). Several inflammation and JAK/STAT signaling genes that were increased in resistant cells were completely inhibited by ruxolitinib (SI Appendix, Fig. S15D). Although further characterization of the mechanism of resensitization is needed, the data narrow the possible pathways contributing to resensitization to JAK/STAT, PI3K-AKT-mTOR, Myc, and E2F. These will have to be validated in future studies using various chemical inhibitors or genetic models.

Discussion

The data presented in this manuscript show that activation of a context-dependent tumor suppressor such as AR does not provide fail-safe sustained tumor growth inhibition. Rather the resistance occurs by a mechanism that is unique and distinct from resistance due to oncogenic inhibition. Emerging evidence shows that lineage plasticity represents an important mechanism for various cancers to gain resistance to targeted therapy (76). Though the role of lineage plasticity in BC has not been fully studied, here we demonstrate that BC cells are not only capable of being plastic, but they are also elastic. We also demonstrate that once cells become resistant to AR agonist, they gain a more aggressive phenotype and become an intermediate ER/AR-positive basal subtype.

Although AR agonists were tested in various xenograft models, they were not evaluated in BC transgenic mouse models to demonstrate the distinction in efficacy between cellular environment in localized xenografts vs. spontaneous models. Evidence for efficacy in whole organisms can be drawn from clinical trials with enobosarm where 25 to 35% of the patients demonstrated clinical benefit rate (77). Future clinical trials will provide evidence for the sustainability of this efficacy.

First, using spatial transcriptomics, we show that AR positively protects cells from attaining an aggressive phenotype underscoring its role as strong tumor suppressor. However, prolonged treatment with AR agonist can cause AR to switch from being a tumor suppressor to being oncogenic, leading to resistance development. Typically, tumor suppressors become inactivated by various mechanisms under resistant conditions and lose their ability to limit tumor growth. However, the data shown in this manuscript clearly demonstrate that AR not only loses its tumor suppressor role but becomes oncogenic. This evidence indicates a tumor suppressor switching to an oncogene under the pressure of resistant environment. Despite this switch from tumor suppressor to an oncogene, AR is still a relevant therapeutic target in ARaR BC, where AR degraders under development for advanced PCa can be repurposed.

We sought to identify downstream pathway(s) that underlie the resistance mechanism that led to the deciphering. ER-positive BC lineage plasticity is promoted by the activation of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway. Recent studies have shown that enzalutamide resistance in castration-resistant prostate cancer leads to JAK/STAT pathway activation, which causes lineage plasticity (54, 55). The JAK/STAT pathway has been implicated as tumorigenic as well as antitumorigenic, and more studies are needed to understand the roles it plays in cancer progression (73). It has also been correlated with lineage plasticity in multiple other cancers and with normal plasticity in embryonic development (56, 78, 79). Bulk RNA-seq and snRNA-seq also showed that JAK/STAT signaling was enriched in ARaR tumors. We also demonstrate that by inhibiting the JAK/STAT signaling pathway with the JAK1/2 inhibitor, ruxolitinib, resistant cells can revert back to an ARaS state. These tumors become more luminal-like in phenotype. Sustained AR agonist treatment causes an activation of the JAK/STAT pathway and causes AR to become an oncogene. These tumors then enter into a TNBC-like state where AR and ER chromatin binding are dysfunctional and the PROP1 binding site is gained. These tumors can be treated with an AR degrader or they can be treated in combination with a JAK1/2 inhibitor and AR agonists to repress tumor growth (Fig. 8). Taken together, simultaneous calibrated inhibition of the JAK/STAT pathway with their cognate antagonists in synergy with agonist treatment of AR in ER-positive BC could be a plausible therapeutic strategy to avoid the lineage plasticity due to oncogenic transformation of AR. Going forward, this could provide a framework on how to avoid oncogenic transformation of tumor suppressors that could be targeted persistently by agonists in cancers, through combinatorial drug treatments with key component downstream pathways that mediate resistance in a context-specific manner.

A schematic of the proposed mechanism of resistance and lineage plasticity upon sustained activation of a tumor suppressor (biorender.com). AR is a tumor suppressor in ER-positive breast cancer. ER/AR-positive breast cancer exists in a multilineage state (mixture of luminal and basal). ER is the driver of tumor growth and the FOXA1 facilitates this growth. When tumors are treated with an activator of the tumor suppressor AR, AR binds to ARE, and FOXA1 binds to its response elements (FXRE) near AREs. This sequestration of FOXA1 inhibits ER function and tumor growth, driving tumors to a more differentiated luminal phenotype. Sustained activation of tumor suppressor AR causes an activation of the JAK/STAT pathway and switches AR to an oncogene. Tumors then enter into an aggressive less differentiated basal TNBC-like state where AR and ER chromatin binding are dysfunctional and the master transcription factor PROP1 binding site is gained. This lineage plasticity and aggressive growth can be addressed with AR degraders or antagonists or a combination of JAK1/2 inhibitor and AR agonists. The metamorphosis to aggressive less differentiated basal cells could also be prevented by combining AR agonist and JAK inhibitor from the beginning of treatment initiation with AR agonist.

Resistance mechanisms are effected by transcriptional reprogramming (17, 80, 81) and are mediated by certain master regulator transcription factors. It is well known that master regulators both in terms of normal physiological functions and disease context emerge from major transcription factor superfamilies such as homeodomain superfamily, bHLH, and C2H2 zinc fingers. One of the least studied transcription factors that needs to be evaluated in BC is PROP1 or PROP1 signature that could potentially function as a master regulator in this context. Sequence searches on the UniProt database yielded double homeobox subfamily members such as Dux1, Dux3-5, and DuxA be the closest homologs (best e-values). Few of these Dux homeobox transcription factors are well-known master regulators (82). Therefore, PROP1 signature being a master regulator in this context is a possibility. How PROP1 signature facilitated AR and how is activated by the JAK/STAT pathway remains to be explored along with its role as a master regulator.

Here, we also show that ER/AR-positive BC has elastic properties where when cells are treated with AR agonists they return to a more luminal state (highly differentiated). Furthermore, ER/AR-positive BC cells can become resistant to AR agonist treatment suggesting that these cells exist in a multilineage state. Once BC becomes resistant to AR agonist treatment, AR switches from being a tumor suppressor to being an oncogene. This lineage plasticity is facilitated by the activation of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway. The inhibition of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway can resensitize cells to enobosarm treatment. These data need to be validated in the clinic to determine whether there are any differences between responders and nonresponders to AR-targeted treatment. Moreover, this concept of tumor suppressor activation resistance needs to be validated with other ligand-activated tumor suppressors, if they exist, and the concept cannot be generalized without additional data. The data in the manuscript provide a roadmap to achieving sustainable treatment utilizing AR as a therapeutic target through combinatorial therapeutics.
