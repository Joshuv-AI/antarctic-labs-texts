Homology-independent targeted insertion-mediated derivation of M1-biased macrophages harbouring Megf10 and CD3ζ from human pluripotent stem cells
Authors: Xing Zhen, Jieun Kim, Jong Soon Kang, Byeong Jo Choi, Ki Hwan Park, Dong-Seok Lee, Seok-Ho Hong, Jong-Hee Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundMacrophages engineered with chimeric antigen receptors (CAR) are suitable for immunotherapy based on their immunomodulatory activity and ability to infiltrate solid tumours. However, the production and application of genetically edited, highly effective, and mass-produced CAR-modified macrophages (CAR-Ms) are challenging.MethodsHere, we used homology-independent targeted insertion (HITI) for site-directed CAR integration into the safe-harbour region of human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs). This approach, together with a simple differentiation protocol, produced stable and highly effective CAR-Ms without heterogeneity.FindingsThese engineered cells phagocytosed cancer cells, leading to significant inhibition of cancer-cell proliferation in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, the engineered CARs, which incorporated a combination of CD3ζ and Megf10 (referred to as FRP5Mζ), markedly enhanced the antitumour effect of CAR-Ms by promoting M1, but not M2, polarisation. FRP5Mζ promoted M1 polarisation via nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), ERK, and STAT1 signalling, and concurrently inhibited STAT3 signalling even under M2 conditions. Research in context

Evidence before this study

Human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) hold the potential to provide a homogeneous population of myeloid cells for immunotherapy. The versatility in editing approaches, combined with the ability to produce a homogeneous functional hPSC-derived engineered chimeric antigen receptor-modified macrophages (CAR-Ms), is a compelling solution for overcoming the limitations associated with current macrophage sources. CAR-Ms provide new opportunities for immunotherapy with their immunomodulatory and infiltration ability in solid tumours. However, the derivation and application of genetically edited, highly effective, and mass-produced CAR-Ms still remain significant challenges.

Added value of this study

Here, we employed the Homology-Independent Targeted Insertion (HITI) system to achieve site-directed CAR integration into the safe harbour region of hPSCs. This approach, with a simple differentiation protocol, produced stable and highly effective CAR-Ms without risking heterogeneity. These engineered cells exhibited a remarkable cancer cell phagocytic ability, leading to significant inhibition of cancer cell proliferation in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, our engineered CAR, incorporating a combination of CD3ζ and Megf10 (referred to as FRP5Mζ), markedly enhanced CAR-Ms’ antitumour effects by promoting M1 polarisation while maintaining M2 resistance. FRP5Mζ promotes M1 polarisation through nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), ERK, and STAT1 signalling, concurrently inhibiting STAT3 signalling even under M2 conditions. These multifaceted features modulated the tumour microenvironment by activating inflammatory signalling, inducing M1 polarisation in bystander non-CAR macrophages, and enhancing the infiltration of T cell in cancer spheroids.

Implications of all the available evidence

This study provides a promising direction for the immunotherapeutic application of CAR-Ms. By employing guided CAR insertion, specifically incorporating CD3ζ and Megf10 domains, we demonstrated an effective strategy in the context of solid tumour immunotherapy.

Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy is a pivotal strategy for immune surveillance, identification, and targeted attack of cancer cells, and by focusing on the activation or modification of immune cells, particularly T cells, the health of normal tissues and organs can be preserved.1 Recent advancements in adoptive cellular therapy using dendritic cells (DCs), T cells, NK cells, and macrophages have led to marked progress in the treatment of malignancies.2, 3, 4, 5 Chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) engineered with extracellular antigen-binding, transmembrane, and intracellular activation domains enable selective targeting of molecules on cancer cell surfaces.6 CAR-modified T cells (CAR-T cells) are a pioneering synthetic biology approach and have contributed to the advancement of immunotherapy. Despite the impressive therapeutic effects of FDA-approved CAR-T therapies in haematological malignancies,7, 8, 9 their efficacy against solid tumours is hampered by restricted tumour infiltration, antigen escape, modest antitumour activity, and severe toxicity.10 In contrast, CAR natural killer cells (CAR-NK cells) are allogeneic effectors that target tumours through CAR-dependent and independent pathways, requiring no human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-matched donor and exhibiting cytotoxicity.11,12

The immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment (TME), which includes stromal cells and extracellular matrix (ECM) components, significantly influences the heterogeneity and immunotherapy resistance of cancer cells during tumour development, invasion, and metastasis.13 The TME, with its diverse cell types, ECM elements, anti-inflammatory environment, high pressure, and immune checkpoints, creates a barrier that impedes the infiltration of CAR-T and CAR-NK cells, limiting the effectiveness of immune cell therapies for solid tumours.14 Macrophages, as effectors bridging the adaptive and innate immune systems, are important for eliminating cancer because of their antigen presentation, cytokine secretion, phagocytosis, inhibition of tumour collagen deposition, and TME infiltration.15 Consequently, they serve as targeted antibody-based effectors, with their antigen presentation triggering a secondary adaptive antitumour response that boosts anticancer therapy efficacy. However, immortalised macrophage lines like THP1 have limitations for clinical use. Unlike other immune cells, primary macrophages derived from peripheral blood or bone marrow have restricted expansion capabilities and are highly refractory to genetic engineering for CAR integration, resulting in a low yield of CAR-modified macrophages (CAR-Ms).16,17 In addition, the inherent heterogeneity of human macrophages and the incomplete understanding of their features are challenges. Therefore, the acquisition of consistently stable and functional macrophages is important for effective cancer immunotherapy.

Human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) could provide a homogeneous population of myeloid cells for immunotherapy, which can be standardised and scaled up for clinical use.18 Moreover, hPSCs enable genetic engineering, allowing the introduction of diverse genetic traits into macrophages. This versatility in editing approaches, combined with the ability to produce a homogeneous functional cell population via single-colony selection and controlled differentiation, may enable hPSC-derived CAR-Ms to overcome the limitations of current sources of macrophages. Despite the predominant use of viral vectors to deliver CAR transgenes,19,20 this approach is hampered by random gene insertion mutations, the risk of oncogenesis, and uncontrolled CAR expression, rendering it unsuitable for clinical application.21 A CRISPR-Cas9-mediated transgene integration method, based on the nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ) DNA repair pathway and known as homology-independent targeted insertion (HITI), has improved site-specific integration frequency, enabling the generation of stably expressing cell lines.22 The human Hipp11 (hH11) locus, located on chromosome 22, lacks an endogenous promoter and is widely conserved in mammals and vertebrates. This locus functions as a safe harbour site for gene editing and the generation of stable cell lines.23,24 Therefore, HITI-mediated site-direct integration at the H11 locus could expand the CRISPR knock-in toolbox for hPSCs, thereby enabling the manufacture of engineered CAR-Ms.

Tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs) recruited to tumour tissues undergo reprogramming towards an M2 phenotype, suppressing the immune response and contributing to tumourigenesis. Given the high plasticity of macrophages in response to environmental factors, a primary strategy for antitumour activity is the polarisation of macrophages to the proinflammatory M1 phenotype, or converting M2 into M1 macrophages. It is now possible to produce macrophage-specific CARs, which could endow CAR-Ms with increased phagocytosis, a proinflammatory M1 phenotype, and the ability to modify the TME to enhance responsiveness to immune interventions against solid tumours. CAR-Ms incorporating CD147 transmembrane and intracellular regions activate matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), including MMP10 and MMP12, leading to increased T-cell infiltration and inhibition of tumour growth by degrading ECM.15 In addition, CAR-Ms with chemokine (C–C motif) ligand 19 (CCL19), a ligand of CCR7, and an intracellular Mer receptor tyrosine kinase (MerTK) domain, exhibit superior efficacy compared to those with the 4–1BB-CD3ζ domain in targeting CCR7-positive immunosuppressive cells.25 Engineered CARs containing cytosolic immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs) (such as multiple EGF-like domains 10 [Megf10] or FcRɣ) activate phagocytosis, resulting in CD19+ cells with marked phagocytotic activity as a result of activation of Src-family kinases and a reduction in the number of cancer cells.26 However, testing has focused on lymphoma, and efficacy against solid tumours is unknown. Further research is needed to identify the optimum combination of costimulatory domains for CAR-Ms.

We aimed to establish a simple platform to produce CAR-Ms using HITI to direct the integration of a macrophage-specific CAR transgene into the hH11 locus by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated editing of the genome of hPSCs. The integration of intracellular Megf10-CD3ζ domains in tandem to construct antigen-targeting CAR-Ms resulted in improved efficacy in solid tumour models. This was achieved by biasing towards an M1 proinflammatory phenotype, even under M2 conditions. Importantly, CAR-Ms with Megf10-CD3ζ shifted bystander non-CAR macrophages to the M1 phenotype and enhanced the infiltration of T cells into cancer spheroids. Overall, we report mass production of highly effective and homogeneous functional CAR-Ms by genome editing in hPSCs. The findings provide valuable insight into macrophage-based therapies for solid tumours.

Methods

Cell culture

This study focused on commonly occurring solid tumours, such as ovarian and breast cancer, which predominantly affect females. As a result, female-derived target cells were used. Although effector CAR-Ms can be derived from hPSCs of any sex, patient-matched studies for these cancers should use female hPSCs from the same patients. WA09 (H9) hPSCs were obtained from WiCell (https://www.wicell.org/home/stem-cells/catalog-of-stem-cell-lines/wa09.cmsx) and maintained in mTeSR™1 (Stemcell Technologies) culture medium in a six-well plate coated with Matrigel (Corning; diluted 1:30 in KO-DMEM) in a humidified 37 °C incubator with a 5% CO2 atmosphere. ReLeSR (Stemcell Technologies) was used to dissociate the cells, which were passaged and cultured in mTeSR™1 supplemented with 5 μM ROCK inhibitor Y-27632 (MedChemExpress) for 24 h; next, the medium was exchanged for normal medium. SK-OV-3, AU565, MDA-MB-231 (Fig. S3a), and Jurkat clone E6–1 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. SK-OV-3, AU565, and Jurkat clone E6–1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Welgene) and MDA-MB-321 cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S, Gibco), and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere (Table S6).

Construction of CARs and production of CAR-expressing hPSCs

The FRP5-CAR donor plasmids were composed of two hH11 insertion sites (targeted at chr22:31434450), an AmpR gene with its promoter, the EF-1a promoter, the CAR extracellular domain (comprised of the CD8a signal peptide, Myc-Tag sequence, FRP5 sequence, and Hinge sequence), the CAR transmembrane domain, the CAR intracellular domain (consisting of the DAP10, Megf10, and CD3ζ domains), and the IRES-PuroR sequence. Each domain was synthesised separately, and the CAR constructs were generated using In-Fusion® Snap Assembly Master Mix (TaKaRa). The CAR plasmid (1.5 μg) and hH11 sgRNA-Cas9 plasmid (1.5 μg, based on pCAG-SpCas9-GFP-U6-gRNA; Addgene: #79144) (sgRNA was selected using Benchling software27 as shown in Table S4) were transfected into hPSCs (1 × 106 cells) in 100 μL of buffer using a P3 Primary Cell 4D-Nucleofector X Kit (Lonza) and 4D-Nucleofector™ System (Lonza). After selection with puromycin (1 μg/mL), CAR-expressing cells were obtained.

Macrophage differentiation from CAR-expressing hPSCs

CAR-expressing hPSCs were dissociated with Accutase (Sigma) for 5 min and a 70 μm strainer (VWR) was used for single-cell filtration. On day −1, hPSCs were seeded at 2.5 × 104 cells per well in ultra-low attachment six-well plates (Corning) and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min for embryoid body (EB) formation in mTeSR™1 medium supplemented with 5 μM Y-27632 and 1% P/S. On day 0, EBs were generated in the presence of step 1 medium consisting of APEL II medium (Stemcell Technologies) supplemented with 1% P/S, BMP4 (40 ng/mL, R&D Systems), VEGF (50 ng/mL, R&D Systems), SCF (40 ng/mL, R&D Systems), and bFGF (5 ng/mL, R&D Systems). On day 5, EBs (8/well) were transferred to 12-well plates coated with Matrigel and containing step 2 medium consisting of APEL II medium supplemented with 1% P/S, VEGF (50 ng/mL), SCF (40 ng/mL), bFGF (5 ng/mL), TPO (30 ng/mL, R&D Systems), and Flt3L (50 ng/mL, R&D Systems). On day 10, the medium was exchanged for step 3 medium consisting of APEL II medium supplemented with 1% P/S, VEGF (50 ng/mL), SCF (40 ng/mL), bFGF (5 ng/mL), TPO (30 ng/mL), IL-3 (30 ng/mL, R&D Systems), IL-6 (30 ng/mL, R&D Systems), GM-CSF (100 ng/mL, R&D Systems), and M-CSF (100 ng/mL, R&D Systems). On day 15, the medium was exchanged for step 4 medium consisting of APEL II medium supplemented with 1% P/S, 10% FBS, IL-3 (25 ng/mL), and M-CSF (100 ng/mL). On day 20, the medium was exchanged for step 5 medium consisting of APEL II medium supplemented with 1% P/S, 10% FBS, and M-CSF (100 ng/mL). From days 25–50, cells were cultured in 6-well plates containing step 5 medium. Differentiated macrophages were used for in vitro and in vivo assays within 7 days after transfer from EB plates. M1 or M2 macrophage polarisation was induced in M1 medium consisting of APEL II medium with 1% P/S, 10% FBS, M-CSF (100 ng/mL), LPS (100 ng/mL, 4 h), and IFN-γ (20 ng/mL, R&D Systems) for 24 h (Fig. S2a) or M2 medium consisting of APEL II medium with 1% P/S, 10% FBS, M-CSF (100 ng/mL), IL-4 (20 ng/mL, R&D Systems), and IL-13 (20 ng/mL, R&D Systems) for 48 h.

Genotype analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from cells using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. PCR was performed using specific primers (Table S2), and products were visualised by electrophoresis in 1% agarose gels.

Immunofluorescence assay

An immunofluorescence assay was performed as described previously.28 Cells were fixed, permeabilised using Fixation and Permeabilisation Solution (BD Biosciences) and blocked with 1 × Wash/Perm buffer (BD Biosciences) at room temperature for 20 min. Next, the cells were reacted with primary antibodies (Table S5) overnight at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h in darkness. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst and the cells were visualised by fluorescence microscopy (Zeiss).

Karyotype analysis

For G-banding karyotype analysis, colcemid (10 μg/mL, Gibco) was used to arrest cells in the metaphase. The cells were washed with KCl solution (0.075 M) supplemented with 1% sodium citrate, fixed with 3:1 methanol:acetic acid and spread on slides as a monolayer. Slides with 20 metaphase spreads were assayed after GTG-banding and Giemsa staining at Korea Research of Animal Chromosomes.

Three-germ-layer differentiation assay

hPSCs were subjected to three-germ-layer differentiation in vitro using the STEMdiff™ Trilineage Differentiation Kit (Stemcell Technologies) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Differentiated cells were assayed by immunofluorescence for endodermal, mesodermal, and ectodermal markers.

Flow cytometry

Cells were harvested using enzyme-free cell dissociation buffer (Gibco), resuspended in DPBS (Welgene) supplemented with 3% FBS, and reacted with the indicated antibodies (Table S5) at 4 °C for 30 min. To detect transcription factors, cells were incubated in Fixation and Permeabilisation Solution (BD Biosciences) and 1 × Perm/Wash Buffer (BD Biosciences) at room temperature for 30 min and washed. The stained cells were assayed using a BD FACSAria III (BD Biosciences) flow cytometer and analysed using FlowJo software (TreeStar, Inc.).

Wright-Giemsa staining assay

Macrophage morphology was examined using the Wright-Giemsa Stain Kit (Abcam). Cells were seeded into a 24-well plate at 1 × 105 per well. After inducing polarisation, cells were fixed in absolute methanol for 5 min, stained with working Wright-Giemsa solution for 5 min, and washed with DDW in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. After air drying, labelled cells were visualised using a light microscope.

Western blotting

Samples were extracted from cells using protein lysis buffer with protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma) on ice. The protein concentrations of samples were measured using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo), and the samples were resolved by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Bio-Rad). Nonspecific binding was blocked using EveryBlot Blocking Buffer (Bio-Rad) for 30 min at room temperature, and the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies (Table S5). After washing in 1 × TBST buffer (LPS), the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1: 5000) for 2 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive signals were visualised using a developer and fixer film system.

Analysis of gene expression

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen). cDNA was synthesised using the ReverTra Ace-α-® High-Efficient Reverse Transcription Kit (Toyobo). Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using TB Green™ Premix (TaKaRa) and the StepOne™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with specific primers (Table S1). Reverse transcription PCR was performed using specific primers (Table S3).

Bead phagocytosis assay

Cells were seeded into a four-well cell culture chamber slide (SPL) at 1 × 105 per well. Macrophage phagocytosis activity was examined using a pHrodo™ Green Zymosan BioParticles™ Conjugate Kit (Invitrogen) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. Particles (100 μL; 100 μg/mL) were added to a cell culture slide for 4 h. After washing with Live Cell Imaging Solution (Invitrogen), samples were visualised via fluorescence microscopy.

Measurement of cytokine concentrations in culture supernatant

The human TNF-α and IFN-γ concentrations were determined in culture supernatants of macrophages after M1 polarisation. Macrophages were seeded at 1 × 106 per well in a six-well plate. After M1 induction, 2 mL fresh medium was exchanged, and at 48 h the culture supernatant was collected and stored at −80 °C. Cytokine concentrations were measured using the Human TNF-α or IFN-γ Quantikine ELISA Kit (R&D Systems) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Indirect culture using the transwell system

Indirect culture of macrophages was performed using a 24-Well Cell Culture Insert (3 μm pore, CellQART). WT macrophage suspension (200 μL, 2 × 105 cells) was added to the upper chamber; a 800 μL suspension of macrophages (2 × 105 cells) and a mixture of cancer cells (2 × 104 cells) were added to the bottom chamber. After 48 h, the cells in the upper chamber were collected for analysis.

Culture in cancer conditional medium (CM)

SK-OV-3 cells were seeded at 1 × 106 per well (2 mL medium) in a six-well plate. After 48 h, CM was collected by centrifugation and stored at −80 °C. Macrophages (1 × 106) were cultured in 2 mL CM for 48 h and analysed.

Three-dimensional culture of multicellular spheroids

After M1 polarisation, 1 × 103 macrophages and 5 × 103 SK-OV-3 cells were added to a 96-well round-bottom ultra-low-attachment microplate (Corning) in DMEM/F12 (Gibco) medium supplemented with 10 μM Y-27632, 1% P/S, 1 × B27 supplement (Gibco), 10 ng/mL EGF (R&D Systems), and 20 ng/mL bFGF for 48 h at 37 °C. Apoptosis was detected using an in situ Cell Death Detection Kit (Roche) under a fluorescence microscope. To evaluate T-lymphocyte penetration, Jurkat T cells were stained with Calcein AM (5 μM) for 30 min at 37 °C. Next, 5 × 104 labelled Jurkat T cells were incubated with spheroids for 24 h. After washing and fixing in fixation solution, spheroids were imaged via fluorescence microscopy.

In vitro phagocytosis assay

Target cells of MDA-MB-231, SK-OV-3, or AU565 were labelled using CellTracker™ Deep Red Dye (1 μM, Invitrogen) for 20 min at 37 °C. Effector cells (1.5 × 105) were cocultured with target cells at a ratio of 1: 1 protected from light in a 24-well plate for 24 h. The cells were treated with TrypLE (Gibco), reacted with an antibody, and analysed via flow cytometry (Fig. S3e-g). For immunofluorescence assays, Calcein AM (5 μM, Invitrogen)-labelled target cells and effector cells were seeded onto a four-well cell-culture chamber slide for 24 h and subsequently visualised via fluorescence microscopy.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

Effector cells (5 × 103) were seeded into a black 96-well plate (SPL) for 24 h, and effector cells with or without M1 polarisation were cocultured with EGFP (Fig. S3b)–or luciferase (Fig. S3c) -positive target cells at the indicated ratios protected from light for 24 h. Cytotoxicity was assayed using a SpectraMax iD3 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices) with the Pierce® Firefly Luciferase Glow Assay Kit (Thermo). Specific lysis = (sample signal – background) ÷ (cancer-alone signal – background).

In vivo activity against cancer cells

To investigate the inhibitory effect of CAR-Ms on the growth of female-specific tumours, such as ovarian cancer, in vivo, female NOG mice were used. Female six-week-old NOG mice, obtained from Koatech, were housed in individually ventilated cages with access to food and water for one week to acclimate to the environment. Environmental conditions were strictly controlled, including a 12-h light/dark cycle, with the temperature maintained between 22 and 24 °C. The mice had free access to water and standard rodent chow. SK-OV-3 cells (ATCC, cat #HTB-77) were suspended in serum-free medium and suspensions (5 × 106 cells/mouse) were injected subcutaneously into female NOG mice. When the average tumour volume reached 42.6 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 5). The mice received two times of intravenous administration of Tumour control, WT-Ms, and CAR-Ms (FRP5Mζ). Body weights were monitored three times per week. Tumour volumes were measured three times per week using Vernier callipers and calculated by the formula: length (mm) × width (mm) × height (mm) × 0.5. There was no registration of the protocol in any registry prior to the study.

Reagent validation

The RRIDs for all cell lines have been added to Table S6, and all have undergone STR profiling, with the relevant reports included in the ‘Reagent Validation file’. All cell lines have been tested and shown to be negative for mycoplasma contamination. The antibodies used are commonly employed antibodies, and their datasheets and validation reports have been included in the ‘Reagent Validation file’. The RRID tags for the antibodies used in this study have been added to Table S5.

Statistics

Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the two-tailed t test. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with multiple comparisons, repeated measures one-way ANOVA, and non-parametric ANOVA where appropriate. In all analyses, p < 0.05 was taken to indicate statistical significance. Each experiment was performed in triplicate unless otherwise specified in the figure legends. For animal experiments, randomisation and blinding were employed for group assignments and outcome assessments. All experimental units and data points were included in the analysis for each group, with no exclusions made during the process.

Results

Generation and characterisation of CAR-hPSC stable cell lines

CD3ζ, a stimulator of the T-cell receptor (TCR), can activate downstream targets such as Zap70 and is implicated in phagocytosis by macrophages, but may not induce the intracellular cascades required for their M1 polarisation.18,26,29,30 Use of the CD3ζ domain alone showed suboptimal persistence and antitumour activity31 but adding a costimulatory endodomain such as CD28 and 41BB enhanced the activation and functionality of CAR-T cells.32, 33, 34 Therefore, we hypothesised that the integration of CD3ζ and a macrophage-specific domain would improve CAR-Ms and possibly induce their M1 polarisation in the TME. Megf10, a receptor for C1q on apoptotic cells, regulates engulfment signals, resulting in phagocytosis.35 The recruitment of Syk into ITAMs mediates phagocytotic activity36; therefore, we designed a CAR incorporating CD3ζ and the intracellular domain of Megf10 to investigate the effect of its activation on CAR-Ms. We used DAP10, a costimulatory factor that triggers antitumour activity in NK cells via the Syk-dependent signalling pathway, which is essential for the cytolytic activity and survival of NK cells, to investigate whether CD3ζ differentially regulates macrophage functions based on the incorporated partner. CARs with FRP5Dζ (DAP10-CD3ζ), FRP5Mζ (Megf10-CD3ζ), and FRP5M (Megf10) were constructed. All of the CAR-plasmids had a CD8a signalling peptide for cell membrane translocation, a Myc-Tag domain for expression and localisation, a FRP5 domain (HER2 scFv) for antigen-specific binding, and a transmembrane domain (Fig. 1a). To ensure the functionalities of the CARs without altering the host genome, the CRISPR-Cas9 system with an sgRNA specific for HITI was used to achieve precise, safe harbour-targeted knock-in into the hH11 locus, thereby establishing stable CAR-expressing hPSCs using WA09(H9), a hPSC line derived from blastocysts established by James Thomson37 (Fig. 1a and Fig. S1a).

Next, we assessed CAR insertion and stable expression, and its effect on pluripotency and differentiation. PCR of genomic DNA confirmed correct CAR integration into the hH11 locus without targeting nonspecific genes (Fig. 1b, Fig. S1b and c). Expression of CARs was confirmed by reverse transcription PCR (Fig. 1c) and their localisation to the cell membrane was confirmed by detection of the Myc-tag (Fig. 1d). The frequencies of different edited populations were as follows: FRP5Dζ, 99.4%; FRP5Mζ, 99.5%; FRP5D, 99.3% (Fig. 1e). Immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometry indicated high-level expression of the pluripotency-maintenance proteins Nanog, Oct4, Sox2, TRA-1–60, SSEA3, and SSEA4, with no significant differences from the WT (Fig. 1f and g). G-banding chromosome analysis confirmed a stable human euploid karyotype, suggesting that HITI-mediated genetic editing did not cause chromosomal abnormalities (Fig. 1h). Analyses of mesoderm (TBXT and α-SMA), ectoderm (Nestin and PAX-6), and endoderm (FOXA2 and SOX17) markers suggested functionality comparable to WT macrophages (Fig. 1i). In summary, we generated three stable CAR-hPSC cell lines, and the CARs did not affect the pluripotency or differentiation of the hPSCs.

Differentiation and functional evaluation of CAR-hPSC–derived CAR-Ms

The enhanced differentiation of hPSCs enables research on tumour immunology and can overcome the limitations of immortalised cells and the scarcity of macrophages in the bloodstream. We produced EBs to generate uniform CAR-Ms for mesodermal specification, haemogenic differentiation, and maturation (Fig. 2a). Wright-Giemsa staining showed that M0 (untreated) CAR-Ms had a typical macrophage morphology. M0 CAR-Ms were polarised to the M1 phenotype by LPS and IFN-γ, and to M2 by IL-4 and IL-13, as were WT cells (Fig. 2b). During differentiation from stem cells to macrophages, the expression levels of the stem cell markers Oct4 and Nanog (Fig. 2c, one-way ANOVA) showed decreasing trends. By contrast, the macrophage markers TREM2 and SPI1 exhibited increasing trends, and their stable expression peaked on day 25 (Fig. 2d, one-way ANOVA). The three types of CAR-Ms showed consistent expression levels of TREM2 and SPI1 with WT cells (Fig. 2e, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons). The CAR-Ms were positive for CD45 and the macrophage markers CD68, CD14, and CD11b, as were WT macrophages (Fig. 2f, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons). A bead-ingestion assay confirmed that they were capable of phagocytosis (Fig. 2g, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons). Therefore, the CAR-Ms differentiated using our protocol had the characteristics and functions of mature macrophages.

Enhanced phagocytosis and cytotoxicity of CAR-Ms against cancer cells, particularly in FRP5Mζ

The targeted phagocytosis of tumour cells by macrophages is a critical aspect of the antitumour immune response, because it not only triggers apoptosis and the clearance of cancer cells but also the release of antitumour cytokines and antigen presentation.38 This results in the recruitment of immune cells, including T cells and NK cells, which inhibit and eliminate tumour cells.5 We investigated the phagocytotic and antitumour activities of CAR-Ms in vitro and in vivo. Upon coculture with SK-OV-3 cells stained with Deep Red dye, FRP5Mζ and FRP5M showed enhanced engulfment of dye-labelled tumour cell components even under M0 conditions, and the phagocytotic activity of FRP5Mζ increased considerably upon M1 polarisation. However, FRP5Dζ, which carries CD3ζ and DAP10, did not show significantly enhanced phagocytotic activity (Fig. 3a, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons). Fluorescence imaging confirmed the increased uptake of Calcein-labelled SK-OV-3 cells by FRP5Mζ and FRP5M (Fig. 3b, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons). To validate the anticancer activity of CAR-Ms, we established SK-OV-3 cell lines with stable expression of EGFP and firefly luciferase as target cells (Fig. S3b and c, two-tailed t-test). We assessed whether FRP5Mζ and FRP5M could induce death of cancer cells by applying CAR-Ms at the indicated effector:target (E:T) ratios to SK-OV-3-EGFP and SK-OV-3-luciferase cells. Compared to the WT, FRP5Mζ and FRP5M with the M1 phenotype not only attenuated GFP signals from tumour cells but also had a progressive antitumour effect even under M0 conditions (Fig. 3c and Fig. S3d, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons). Although FRP5Mζ showed only a slight difference compared to FRP5M in inhibition of SK-OV-3-EGFP cells, FRP5Mζ demonstrated significant anti-tumour activity in inhibiting SK-OV-3-luciferase cells at a 10:1 ratio (Fig. 3c and Fig. S3d, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons). Fluorescence imaging confirmed cytotoxicity against cancer cells, and FRP5Mζ showed superior phagocytosis of cancer cells even under M0 conditions. Moreover, M1 polarisation amplified the activation of FRP5Mζ, resulting in adhesion to, and enhanced clearance of, cancer cells. Therefore, the CAR with CD3ζ and Megf10 enhanced cancer targeting and anticancer activity, irrespective of polarisation (Fig. 3d, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons). The elevated secretion of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, such as TNF-α, IFN-γ, and CD107a, by FRP5Mζ and FRP5M upon activation by SK-OV-3 cells suggests enhancement of antitumour immunity (Fig. S4a, b and c, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons). To assess the in vivo antitumour activity of FRP5Mζ, we established a xenograft mouse model by subcutaneously implanting SK-OV-3 cells. Upon tumour establishment, HER2-targeting FRP5Mζ cells were intravenously injected into tumour-bearing mice, and tumour growth was monitored weekly (Fig. 3e). FRP5 CAR-Ms carrying CD3ζ and Megf10 exhibited significant tumour suppression after day 15 and a marked difference compared to the WT on day 22 (Fig. 3f and Fig. S3j, repeated one-way ANOVA), without significantly affecting body weight (Fig. S3h, repeated one-way ANOVA). Overall, these findings suggest that although Megf10 alone can function as a macrophage-specific CAR, its combination with CD3ζ enhances cancer-cell phagocytosis and cytotoxicity in vitro and in vivo.

Antigen-specific antitumour activity of FRP5Mζ

In CAR-based immunotherapy for cancer, accurate antigen recognition is essential to prevent nonspecific binding, which can compromise CAR-immune cell activity, leading to exhaustion and potential toxicity in normal tissues or organs.14 To assess the efficacy of FRP5Mζ and FRP5M against the target antigen HER2 on cancer-cell membranes, we conducted phagocytosis assays using HER2-negative MDA-MB-231 cells and HER2-positive AU565 and SK-OV-3 cells (Fig. S3a). Although phagocytosis was not significantly activated in FRP5Mζ, FRP5M, and WT with HER2-negative MDA-MB-231 cells, FRP5Mζ exhibited increased engulfment of labelled components in AU565 and SK-OV-3 cells, indicating improved phagocytosis of target antigen-positive cells (Fig. 4a and b, one-way ANOVA with non-parametric and multiple comparisons). We investigated the expression levels of the genes encoding TNF-α, IFN-γ, and CD107a in response to HER2-presenting cells. FRP5Mζ and FRP5M exhibited increased expression levels of these genes compared to their counterparts lacking HER2, and the pronounced enhancement of their expression in FRP5Mζ underscores the augmented antigen-specific activation inherent in CAR-expressing macrophages, particularly for CD3ζ plus Megf10 (Fig. 4c and Fig. S5a and b, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons). To determine whether specific antigen binding alone activates CAR-Ms, we incubated CAR-Ms with Alexa 488-conjugated HER2 for 24 h. Calculation of the Mean Fluorescence Intensity (MFI) showed that the binding of HER2 was increased in FRP5Mζ and FRP5M and that both phagocytosed HER2 (Fig. 4d and Fig. S5c, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons), indicating that activation of the antigen-binding region of CAR by a specific antigen is sufficient for phagocytosis. While high-purity HER2 protein obscured some effects of CAR-modified macrophages, in coculture with HER2-positive cancer cell lines, FRP5Mζ still exhibited significant cancer cell phagocytosis. These results demonstrate that FRP5-CAR-Ms can target HER2 on cancer cell membranes and activate and trigger the release of anti-tumour cytokines by macrophages, particularly FRP5Mζ.

Enhanced M1 polarisation by the combination of Megf10 and CD3ζ

Given the tumour cytotoxicity and CAR-dependent enhanced phagocytosis of FRP5Mζ, we evaluated the molecular mechanisms underlying macrophage polarisation. Under M0 conditions, FRP5Mζ showed significantly increased levels of the M1 markers CD80 and CD86 (Fig. 5a, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons). Moreover, upon M1 polarisation by LPS and IFN-γ, FRP5Mζ showed increased and decreased expression levels of genes associated with M1 and M2 polarisation, respectively (Fig. 5a, b, and Fig. S2b, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons and two-tailed t-test). Under M0 and M1 conditions, proinflammatory signals were markedly activated in the order WT, FRP5M, and FRP5Mζ. This upregulated NF-κB and ERK signalling and the levels of the M1 markers STAT1 and CD86. Activation of the ERK pathway in macrophages promotes M1 polarisation, enhancing the secretion of IL-1B, TNF-α, and IL-6.39 The NF-κB pathway promotes and suppresses M1 and M2 polarisation, respectively, via the Toll/interleukin (IL)-1 receptor (TIR) domain-mediated signalling pathway.40 The phosphorylation and total level of p65 were highly upregulated in FRP5Mζ polarised to M1 by LPS and IFN-γ, and the sustained activation of p65 and STAT1 under M0 conditions, confirms that FRP5Mζ drive M1 polarisation. The marked inhibition of M2-associated STAT3 signalling, and the reduced expression of the M2 markers, CD163 and CD206, in FRP5Mζ suggests CAR-mediated bias towards M1 polarisation (Fig. 5c). Fluorescence immunostaining revealed significant accumulation of p65, a transcription factor essential for inflammatory signalling, in the nuclei of M1 FRP5Mζ and FRP5M, confirming the involvement of NF-κB signalling in the CAR-mediated activation of macrophages (Fig. 5d). In addition, TNF-α and IFN-γ, important downstream cytokines of p65 implicated in cancer-cell inhibition, were present at high concentrations in the culture medium of FRP5Mζ and FRP5M, particularly in FRP5Mζ (Fig. 5e, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons). Therefore, the intracellular activation domain with Megf10 and CD3ζ promotes M1 polarisation by activating NF-κB, ERK, and STAT1 signalling and concurrently inhibiting M2-specific STAT3 signalling.

Modulation of the TME by FRP5Mζ enhanced T-cell infiltration and reshaped bystander macrophage polarisation

TAMs, which exhibit characteristics akin to alternatively activated macrophages, are abundant in the TME. These macrophages tend to adopt an M2 phenotype, promoting tumour progression via predominantly anti-inflammatory responses and contributing to chemotherapy resistance.41 Given their enhancement of M1 polarisation and activation of proinflammatory signals, we assessed the ability of FRP5Mζ to counteract M2 polarisation and promote a proinflammatory milieu. Under M0 and M2 conditions, FRP5Mζ and FRP5M showed reduced expression of the M2 markers CD163 and CD206 compared to the WT, indicating suppression of M2 polarisation (Fig. 6a and b, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons). We cultured macrophages in CM of SK-OV-3 cells and assessed their polarisation under M2 conditions. M2 polarisation was significantly inhibited by FRP5Mζ and FRP5M in response to SK-OV-3 CM. FRP5Mζ exhibited superior inhibition of M2 polarisation (Fig. 6c and d, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons). Overall, these findings highlight the potential of CAR-Ms with Megf10 and CD3ζ to directly counteract the M2 polarisation induced by IL-4 and IL-13 and to withstand cancer cell-mediated immunosuppression.

In the TME, macrophages promote the expression of anti-inflammatory factors and eliminate the collagen-based ECM, which functions as a physical barrier, thereby enhancing the responses of T and NK cells.15 To confirm that FRP5Mζ and FRP5M modulate the TME towards an antitumour phenotype, we used three-dimensional multicellular (SK-OV-3 cells and macrophages) spheres. In the spheres, FRP5Mζ and FRP5M induced antitumour apoptosis, with the effect of FRP5Mζ being the more potent (Fig. 7a and Fig. S3k, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons). Although T cells directly target and eliminate haematological malignancies, their efficacy against solid tumours is typically impeded by the immunosuppressive TME.42 To investigate the effect of CAR-Ms on T-cell responses in three-dimensional solid tumours, we cocultured them with T cells. The FRP5Mζ groups exhibited significant infiltration of T cells into the spheres (Fig. 7b). The direct engulfment of cancer cells by macrophages can lead to an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype, hampering the antitumour response.43 However, after prolonged coculture with SK-OV-3 cells, FRP5Mζ maintained high CD86 expression and low CD206 expression, indicating sustained M1 polarisation (Fig. 7c, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons). Next, we investigated the effects of CAR-Ms and SK-OV-3 cells on bystander non-CAR macrophages. In Transwell experiments, FRP5Mζ and FRP5M indirectly promoted M1 polarisation of regular macrophages, which are typically repolarised to M2 in tumours (Fig. 7d, one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons). In addition, FRP5Mζ directly regulated the M1 polarisation of non-CAR macrophages derived from mCherry-positive hPSCs and cocultured with cancer cells (Fig. S3i and Fig. 7e). These findings suggest that CAR-Ms with Megf10 and CD3ζ kill cancer cells in a three-dimensional structure, facilitating T-cell infiltration, inhibiting the M2 polarisation induced by phagocytosis of tumour cells, and directly or indirectly promoting the polarisation of bystander macrophages by reshaping the TME into an antitumour environment.

Discussion

We developed a method to produce CAR-Ms using CRISPR/Cas9 for CAR knock-in into hPSCs, followed by their differentiation into functional macrophages. The results suggest the feasibility of HITI-mediated CAR delivery for targeted transgene insertion into safe-harbour regions of hPSCs. Using HITI with hPSCs yielded functional CAR-Ms and is an efficient approach for large-scale manufacture. Compared to primary immune cells from peripheral blood, generating immune cells from hPSCs shows much promise for immunotherapy11 Modifying hPSCs by deleting inhibitory receptors or enhancing cytokine expression could further improve their antitumour activity.44 However, large-scale generation of CAR-immune cells from hPSCs using lentiviral vectors is challenging due to time-consuming production, low transfection efficiency, and the risk of disrupting normal gene expression.21,45 While CRISPR/Cas9-mediated HDR offers targeted integration, its low transfection efficiency is problematic. The HITI method, based on NHEJ and independent of the cell cycle, overcomes HDR inefficiencies, enabling precise insertion of large gene fragments like CAR at the target site.46 We established a stable CAR-hPSC line with an insertion at the hH11 safe-harbour locus using the CRISPR-Cas9 knock-in system and HITI. The resulting hPSCs showed stable CAR expression with stemness and pluripotency without off-target effects (Fig. 1). Subsequently, the CAR-Ms differentiated from CAR-hPSCs showed mature surface markers, consistent morphology, and phagocytosis.

Macrophages outperform T and NK cells in tackling solid tumours by enhancing immune-cell trafficking, infiltrating tumour tissue, and interacting with nearly all components of the TME.47 At tumour sites, VEGF, CCL2, and CXCL12 recruit macrophages in a hypoxia-dependent manner. Additionally, macrophages can sense hypoxia, its metabolites, and low pH directly.14,48 Despite their plasticity, macrophages in the TME often polarise to M2 due to IL-4, IL-10, CCL2, TGFβ, and tumour-derived exosomes, leading to immune suppression. To improve macrophage-based cancer immunotherapy, maintaining their proinflammatory status in anti-inflammatory environments is crucial. CAR modification could enhance this, potentially boosting the efficacy of such therapies. The reported CAR-Ms, in which intracellular signalling was mediated by a CD3ζ domain or a substitute effector domain from macrophages, could not induce M1 polarisation of macrophages.26,30 To generate M1 CAR-Ms, we fused the macrophage-specific domain, Megf10, with CD3ζ, thereby enhancing antitumour activity. As part of the TCR complex, CD3ζ transduces signals through its ITAM. Lck activation phosphorylates CD3ζ, which then binds to Zap-70's SH2 domains, initiating downstream signalling cascades, including ERK activation.49 Although the antitumour activity of the CD3ζ domain alone is suboptimal, its combination with other costimulatory domains, such as CD28, 4–1BB, and OX40, enhances the properties of CAR-T cells.32,33,50 In CAR-NK cells, linking a signalling domain (e.g., CD28, 4–1BB, or DAP10) to CD3ζ enhances cytotoxicity in several tumour subtypes. Lei et al. combined CD3ζ with a TIR domain from Toll-like receptor-4, creating a CD3ζ-TIR CAR that sustained CAR-M polarisation and improved tumour eradication.18,40

Megf10, upon recognising its ligand C1q, undergoes phosphorylation of tyrosine residues within its ITAM domain by Src family kinases. This creates binding sites for Syk, leading to the activation of phagocytic effector functions in macrophages and triggering macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses.51 Activation of Syk triggers downstream signalling molecules like AKT, IKK, and NF-κB, which induce the expression of proinflammatory genes such as TNF-α and iNOS, and the production of inflammatory mediators.52 CAR-Ms with the intracellular domain of Megf10 or FcRɣ promoted target antigens phagocytosis. However, their ability to polarise macrophages into the M1 phenotype is unclear.26 iPSC-derived macrophages (CAR-iMacs) have an M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype. Antigen-specific binding enhances phagocytosis and M1 polarisation, but M1 polarisation induced by IFN-γ and LPS is brief, complicating in vivo manipulation.41,53 LPS activates ERK signalling through MyD88-mediated TLR2 and TLR4 pathways, inducing proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α) and enhancing the inflammatory response by promoting MAPK phosphorylation.54 Activation of ERK and NF-κB is indispensable for macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses and stimulates the secretion of TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-1.55,56 In this study, the designed CARs incorporating Megf10 and CD3ζ endowed differentiated macrophages with enhanced target engulfment capacity and M1 polarisation without the need for external treatment, while conferring resistance to M2 polarisation even within TMEs. This design also conferred the ability to modulate the TME effectively. The effects of FRP5Mζ were evidenced by the upregulation of M1 markers, activation of ERK and STAT1 signalling, NF-κB/p65 nuclear translocation, increased secretion of TNF-α and IFN-γ, and downregulation of M2 markers, including suppression of the STAT3 pathway. In addition, FRP5Mζ induced apoptosis in tumour spheroids and facilitated the infiltration of T cells into these spheroids, potentially enhancing the anti-tumour immune response. Macrophages, as an important source of MMPs,57 are crucial for degrading the ECM and promoting the entry of immune cells, such as T cells, into the TME, thereby inhibiting tumour growth.15 While M2-like TAMs typically promote tumour progression,58 the conversion of TAMs from the M2 to M1 phenotype or introduction of M1-like macrophages are considered effective anti-tumour strategies.40 The value of this therapy lies not only in maintaining an M1 phenotype but also in the ability to polarise macrophages to M1 within the TME and to mediate tumour regression through direct anti-tumour activity. FRP5Mζ demonstrated the ability to sustain M1 polarisation in edited macrophages, promote the secretion of related cytokines, and enhance the inhibition of tumour cells, indicating its potential as a tumour immunotherapy agent. In addition, FRP5Mζ-edited macrophages exhibited a regulatory effect on the M1 polarisation of non-CAR macrophages, highlighting their potential in remodelling the TME, especially in transforming critical TAMs. As one class of primary antigen-presenting cells (APCs), macrophages edited with FRP5Mζ have the potential to show significantly enhanced antigen presentation, leading to improved activation and infiltration of T cells, thereby bolstering the overall anti-tumour immune response (Fig. 7f). Despite the significant anti-tumour efficacy of FRP5Mζ, several limitations must be addressed in future research. First, the potential off-target effects of the HITI technique used in generating CAR-Ms pose a significant safety concern. To ensure the safe clinical translation of this therapy, comprehensive analysis using advanced sequencing technologies, such as rhAMPseq and targeted locus amplification (TLA), will be required to accurately assess gene editing efficacy and minimise unintended genetic alterations.46 Second, while our study demonstrated the feasibility of HITI-mediated CAR delivery for targeted transgene insertion into hPSCs and subsequent differentiation into functional macrophages, the long-term effects of these CAR-Ms in vivo remain unclear. The short lifespan and limited proliferation of M1 macrophages in the TME pose significant challenges to sustaining their therapeutic effects. Exploring strategies to enhance the longevity and persistence of CAR-Ms, such as optimising injection timing and developing new mechanisms for sustained long-term effectiveness, will be crucial. Combining CAR-M therapy with other immune cells, such as NK and T cells, could potentially extend their anti-tumour activity through the activation of adaptive immune responses.59,60 Third, further optimisation of CAR-Ms through advanced gene editing strategies will be necessary to enhance their durability and effectiveness against tumours. This will include improving their resistance to M2 polarisation and ensuring sustained M1 polarisation to maintain their proinflammatory and anti-tumour activities within the TME.61,62 In addition, although macrophages are key APCs, the efficiency and impact of CAR-Ms in presenting antigens and activating T cells in vivo were not investigated in detail in this study. Understanding how CAR-Ms interact with T cells within the TME will be critical for optimisation of their therapeutic efficacy and designing combination therapies to enhance T cell-mediated anti-tumour responses.30,63,64 Finally, we predominantly utilised mouse models with established cancer cell lines, which do not fully replicate the complexity of human immune responses. Incorporating primary tumour cells into future research will better reflect the complexity and heterogeneity of tumours in patients, and thus allow a more accurate evaluation of the efficacy of CAR-M therapy and optimisation of treatment strategies across various tumour types.65 To translate CAR-Ms into a clinically applicable and FDA-compliant therapy, it will be necessary to eliminate the antibiotic resistance gene used for selecting stable CAR-hPSC clones. For compliant therapy, the CAR construct may be further optimised by incorporating a Cre-LoxP system, allowing the temporary inclusion of the antibiotic resistance gene during clone selection, followed by its excision after generation of stable cell lines.66,67 In addition, production must be conducted under Current Good Manufacturing Practice (cGMP) conditions to ensure product quality and safety.68 Addressing these challenges will allow realisation of the therapeutic potential of CAR-Ms and advance their application in cancer immunotherapy. These efforts will be crucial to overcome current research challenges and establish a robust foundation for the clinical implementation of CAR-Ms in cancer treatment.

In conclusion, we established a stable CAR-expressing hPSC cell line using the CRISPR/Cas9 system based on HITI. FRP5Mζ, which harboured Megf10 and CD3ζ domains, showed M1 but not M2 polarisation, leading to the activation of inflammatory signalling. In addition, FRP5Mζ had direct and indirect effects on bystander macrophages and facilitated the infiltration of T cells into cancer spheroids by modulating the TME. These findings suggest that the efficacy of CAR-Ms in cancer immunotherapy, particularly for solid tumours, can be enhanced by integrating the macrophage activation domain, Megf10, into the CD3ζ domain.
