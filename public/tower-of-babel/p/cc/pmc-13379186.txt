Reply to Comment on Ultrasonographic Measurements of the Liver, Gallbladder, and Hepatic Vessels among University-aged Youth in Baghdad, Iraq
Authors: Abdul Sattar Arif Khammas
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We are grateful for the accurate comments from Prof. Mahmood Dhahir Al-Mendalawi regarding our article entitled “Ultrasonographic Measurements of the Liver, Gallbladder, and Hepatic Vessels among University-aged Youth in Baghdad, Iraq.”[1] Hence, we would like to respond to the methodological concerns raised and clarify our methodology.

RESPONSE TO METHODOLOGICAL CONCERNS REGARDING CENTRAL OBESITY CLASSIFICATION

Prof. Al-Mendalawi addresses an important point about the classification approach which is used to define central obesity (CO) in our study. We agree that determining proper anthropometric cut-off values is crucial for accurately diagnosing CO and interpreting research outcomes.

RATIONALE FOR USING WORLD HEALTH ORGANIZATION/INTERNATIONAL ASSOCIATION FOR THE STUDY OF OBESITY/INTERNATIONAL OBESITY TASK FORCE ASIA-PACIFIC CLASSIFICATION

The World Health Organization (WHO)/International Association for the Study of Obesity/International Obesity Task Force Asia-Pacific classification was applied depending on several considerations.[2] Although Iraq is geographically situated in Western Asia, increasing evidence suggests that Middle Eastern populations, including Iraqis, might have similar metabolic and anthropometric attributes with Asian populations. Studies have shown that Arab populations are more susceptible to metabolic problems at lower waist circumference (WC) thresholds in comparison with European populations.[34] The Asia-Pacific criteria (waist-to-hip ratio >0.90 for males and >0.85 for females) establish more conservative thresholds that may be more effective for identifying CO-related health risks in our population. Similarly, the updated WHO classification[5] stated that waist-to-hip ratio ≥0.90 for males and ≥0.85 for females is closely associated with increasing risk of metabolic complications.

CONSIDERATION OF ALTERNATIVE CLASSIFICATIONS

We appreciate Prof. Al-Mendalawi’s regarding his reference to the updated WHO classification,[5] which recommends WC thresholds of >102 cm for men and >88 cm for women. However, these thresholds indicated to increase risk of metabolic complications and not for defining a CO. Moreover, the same report explicitly states that waist-to-hip ratio remains a valid and widely used indicator of central fat distribution. Recent research has highlighted the need for population-specific cut-off values for anthropometric measures. For instance, the International Diabetes Federation recommends ethnic-specific WC thresholds, suggesting ≥94 cm for men and ≥80 cm for women in Middle Eastern populations.[6] This further supports the appropriateness of using more conservative thresholds for Iraqi populations.

RESPONSE TO RECOMMENDATIONS FOR POPULATION-SPECIFIC NORMATIVE DATA

Regarding the second point, we completely agree on the importance of establishing local Iraqi-specific normative data for sonographic measurements of abdominal structures. This is indeed one of the primary motivations behind our research.

Population-specific normative ultrasonographic values are not determined among Iraqi population, and this indicates to a significant gap in clinical practice. Currently, clinicians depend on reference values derived from Western or other Asian populations, which may not accurately reflect the anatomical and physiological characteristics of Iraqi individuals. The variability in CO classification systems highlights the urgent need for large epidemiological studies to establish population-specific anthropometric reference values for Iraqi adults. Such studies would enable more accurate assessment of metabolic risk and improve the validity of future research in this area. Our study represents an initial step toward addressing this gap by providing baseline data from a healthy university-aged cohort.

Creating validated, easy-to-use equations needs an exclusive methodological design – a large, stratified, population-based study particularly powered for that goal. Our comparative analysis presents the initial evidence to justify and guide the design of such a study, identifying key variables (e.g., liver length, portal vein diameter) that should be considered in the development of future standard models for Iraqi individuals.

We assume that this study will serve as a basis for future, more comprehensive studies that involve wider age ranges, larger sample sizes, and more diverse geographical regions within Iraq. The development of age- and gender-specific normative data would greatly enhance diagnostic accuracy and enable clinicians to distinguish physiological variations from pathological conditions more reliably.

Although some limitations in our study,[1] we believe that our findings regarding the positive correlations between liver size, portal vein diameter, and anthropometric measurements remain valid and contribute valuable insights to the literature. The physiological relationships we observed are consistent with the established understanding of metabolic processes, regardless of the specific CO classification used. Furthermore, we hope that our findings will encourage collaborative efforts among Iraqi medical institutions to establish standardized protocols for ultrasonographic measurements and to develop validated reference equations specific to the Iraqi population.

CONCLUSION

We thank Prof. Al-Mendalawi for his valuable critique, which has provided us an opportunity to clarify our methodological approach and acknowledge important limitations. While we maintain that the Asia-Pacific classification was a reasonable choice given the available evidence, we recognize the need for future research to validate anthropometric cut-off values specifically for Iraqi populations. We hope that our work, despite its limitations, contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting the need for population-specific normative data in clinical practice.
