Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension With a Secundum Atrial Septal Defect
Authors: Hanna Haile, Kabir Malkani, Mohan Satish, Su Yuan, Luke Kim, Dmitriy Feldman, Geoffrey Bergman, Evelyn Horn, Maria Karas, Harsimran Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) and congenital heart disease (CHD), particularly in pretricuspid shunts or Eisenmenger syndrome, are associated with significant mortality and limited therapeutic options.Case SummaryA 35-year-old woman presented with hypoxemia and right heart strain at 12 weeks’ gestation. Echocardiography and right heart catheterization revealed severe PAH and a large secundum atrial septal defect (ASD). Following pregnancy termination and gradual initiation of triple pulmonary vasodilator therapy, her PAH improved significantly over 8 years. She underwent fenestrated ASD closure with significant improvement in functional status.DiscussionThis case illustrates a “treat-and-repair” strategy in PAH in CHD (PAH-CHD) and highlights how medical therapy can convert a previously inoperable patient into a procedural candidate.Take-Home MessagesCombined pulmonary vasodilator therapy can improve PAH and make ASD closure in PAH-CHD feasible. A “treat-and-repair” strategy requires multidisciplinary care.Key words: atrial septal defect, congenital heart disease, pulmonary hypertension, right ventricle

History of Presentation

A 35-year-old G4P1021 woman at 12 weeks' gestation presented with progressive exercise intolerance over several weeks. In room air, she was hypoxemic with oxygen saturation in the 80s. Physical examination revealed jugular venous distention to the mandible, a 3/6 systolic ejection murmur, a loud P2, a right ventricular (RV) heave, clear lungs, and no peripheral edema.

Past Medical History

The patient had 3 prior pregnancies: One miscarriage at 5.5 months, 1 full-term cesarean delivery for arrest of descent, and a second miscarriage at 3.5 months She worked as a social worker and had no personal or family history of rheumatologic or cardiopulmonary disease. She did not report alcohol, tobacco, or illicit drug use.

Differential Diagnosis

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) was suspected based on her physical examination, specifically pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). Other causes of PH such as left heart disease, chronic lung disease, and chronic thromboembolic PH were deemed less likely in the absence of pulmonary congestion or rales, clinical signs of advanced lung disease, or history of venous thromboembolism. Pulmonary function testing outside of the admission demonstrated moderate expiratory airflow obstruction with hyperinflation suggestive of asthma or emphysema (forced expiratory volume in 1 second/forced vital capacity = 0.66) and a low-normal diffusing capacity, which was unlikely to explain in isolation the severity of her presumed pulmonary vascular disease. Additional etiologies that were less likely included amniotic fluid embolism (exceedingly rare at 12 weeks), infectious or inflammatory pulmonary disease, and pregnancy-unmasked asthma.

Investigations

Initial laboratory investigations were normal (complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel, thyroid function, iron studies, infectious and rheumatologic testing, and hypercoagulable workup). D-dimer was low (230 ng/mL). Electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with RV hypertrophy with strain (right axis deviation, dominant R in V1, and right precordial T-wave inversions). Computed tomography pulmonary angiogram showed no pulmonary embolism or parenchymal disease but demonstrated an enlarged pulmonary artery (PA) suggestive of chronic PH. Transthoracic echocardiography showed a severely dilated RV with systolic septal flattening, preserved RV systolic function, and elevated estimated PA systolic pressure of 75 to 80 mm Hg, consistent with PH. Agitated saline study demonstrated early appearance of bubbles in the left atrium, consistent with an intracardiac shunt.

Management

A multidisciplinary team consisting of heart failure–pulmonary hypertension (HF-PH), adult congenital heart disease (ACHD), maternal-fetal medicine, and critical care specialists all recommended pregnancy termination because of the high maternal mortality associated with PH during pregnancy.1 The patient underwent dilation and curettage and copper intrauterine device placement. ACHD and HF-PH specialists guided further diagnostics and management during hospitalization. Right heart catheterization (RHC) showed suprasystemic PA pressures and severe PAH:

A step-up in oxygen saturation from the superior vena cava to right atrium (55% to 66%) supported the presence of a cardiac shunt. Transesophageal echocardiography confirmed a 1.2 × 1.5-cm secundum ASD with bidirectional but predominantly left-to-right flow (Video 1). Sildenafil was initiated and titrated to 20 mg 3 times a day, along with home supplemental oxygen.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient established ambulatory care with both HF-PH and ACHD specialists, with additional titration of her PAH therapy. Ambrisentan was added 1 month after discharge. Selexipag was initiated but discontinued because of side effects. Inhaled treprostinil was added at year 3. By year 4, she achieved maximum tolerated doses of triple therapy: ambrisentan 10 mg daily, sildenafil 40 mg 3 times daily, and inhaled treprostinil 64 μg 4 times daily.

Separately, cardiopulmonary exercise testing at 5 years showed improvements in peak volume of oxygen, O2 pulse, and minute ventilation to carbon dioxide production. Given improved hemodynamics (PVR <3 WU), and progressive RV dilation from increased left-to-right shunting, the patient was assessed for fenestrated ASD closure to allow residual pop-off shunt per current guidelines (Figure 1). The fenestration was created manually in an 18-mm Amplatzer Septal Occluder device before device implantation, then reinforced with a 4.5 × 18-mm drug-eluting stent across the fenestration (Video 2).

The patient recovered well and remained on sildenafil, ambrisentan, and inhaled treprostinil. At follow-up, she was able to walk without limitation and regained her ability to sing. Echocardiography 5 months post-ASD closure demonstrated preserved RV systolic function with decreased RV size from severely to moderately dilated.

Discussion

This case highlights 3 key principles in PAH due to congenital heart disease (PAH-CHD): 1) PAH in the setting of a pretricuspid shunt differs significantly from post-tricuspid shunts; 2) stepwise medical optimization using pulmonary vasodilator therapy can improve PVR to allow shunt closure in patients previously deemed inoperable; and 3) use of a fenestrated ASD closure device in patients with severe PAH may balance the benefits of reducing shunt severity while avoiding the risks of adverse RV response to complete closure.

Pretricuspid shunts (ASDs) cause low-pressure, left-to-right volume overload and may be tolerated into adulthood, whereas post-tricuspid shunts (ventricular septal defects, patent ductus arteriosus) produce earlier symptoms from combined pressure and volume overload of the pulmonary circulation with left-sided chamber dilation.2 Progression to severe PAH is less common in ASDs, with pulmonary vascular disease developing in 6% to 17%, compared with up to 50% of patients with large, unrepaired post-tricuspid defects, including those who may progress to Eisenmenger syndrome, a severe form of PAH characterized by high PVR, shunt reversal, and cyanosis.3,4 It is possible that patients with ASDs require additional "hits" such as genetic predisposition, environmental or physiologic triggers, metabolic dysfunction, or inflammation that contributes to their trajectory toward developing severe PAH. Relatedly, PAH associated with pretricuspid shunts also represents a heterogeneous population that includes patients with coincident idiopathic PAH and ASD, and those with bidirectional shunting without Eisenmenger physiology. These patients' different clinical trajectories and survival may be partly or largely explained by their different RV adaptation: that PAH-CHD patients have better preserved RV function, which may explain their improved survival compared with patients with idiopathic PAH.5,6 This patient's ASD was deemed to be causal rather than incidental based on her hemodynamics profile at RHC, and the lack of significant contribution from alternative etiologies such as heritable PAH, pregnancy-related hemodynamic stress, or substance use. Therefore, treatment was targeted to reducing pulmonary vascular disease with evaluation for possible eventual ASD closure.

Current guidelines for targeted PH therapy in CHD recommend the use of phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors, endothelin receptor antagonists, and prostacyclins. These have been shown to improve both hemodynamics and exercise capacity in patients with PAH-CHD, including in Eisenmenger syndrome.5 Closure of pretricuspid defects in patients with PAH-CHD requires caution due to risk of RV failure after loss of “pop off” for RV pressure overload, particularly in severe PAH with fixed pulmonary vascular disease, or Eisenmenger syndrome where it is contraindicated. This patient had severe PAH with RV dilation, preserved RV function, and persistent left-to-right shunting. With PAH therapy, her PVR reduced to nearly normal, unmasking her true shunt severity (Qp/Qs = 1.7), which supported the benefit and safety of eventual ASD closure. Management decisions regarding shunt closure require careful interdisciplinary discussion. For this particular patient, there was longitudinal shared care and decision-making between ACHD and HF-PH teams during follow-up.

Emerging evidence supports selective use of “treat-and-repair” strategy in ASD-associated PAH. The North American ASD-PAH Registry reported successful late closure in 28% of initially ineligible patients, with improved functional capacity, RV function, and survival.7 In addition, a single-center study from China demonstrated similar success using combination therapy followed by fenestrated ASD closure, with favorable hemodynamic and remodeling outcomes.8 Although not approved by the Food and Drug Administration in the United States, fenestrated devices and individualized procedural planning expand therapeutic options for this complex population, as seen in Europe and Asia. Techniques such as stent-embedded fenestrated occluders allow future modification of shunt size based on hemodynamic needs.9

Conclusions

This case highlights the importance of multidisciplinary care, longitudinal hemodynamic surveillance, and patient-tailored PAH-CHD therapy. Sequential triple vasodilator therapy reduced PVR and permitted safe closure of a previously contraindicated ASD. This aligns with emerging evidence for a “treat-and-repair” strategy in select high-risk PAH-CHD patients.
