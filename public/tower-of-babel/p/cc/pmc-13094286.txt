Merging rigor and relevance in grazingland research: a comprehensive social-ecological monitoring approach

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite decades of study, current research on grazing management’s impacts on ecosystem health and its socioeconomic drivers remains too limited in scope and scale to enable adaptive, evidence-based decision making by producers. There is a pressing need for interdisciplinary research that collects ecosystem data at broader spatial and temporal scales while incorporating working farms and ranches. Such efforts are critical for informing grazing decisions and understanding grazinglands’ potential to deliver ecosystem services, including climate mitigation, water cycling, resilience, and rural livelihoods.ResultsThe Metrics, Management, and Monitoring (3M) project addresses this need through a novel social-ecological framework that integrates biophysical, socioeconomic, and management data across U.S. grazinglands. The project combines controlled experiments at four intensively monitored “hubs” with data from 59 producer-managed farms and ranches. Its core objectives are to: (1) assess the social-ecological health of grazinglands across diverse ecoregions, (2) refine monitoring approaches to improve scalability and accuracy, and (3) integrate producer-led data to balance experimental rigor with real-world relevance. Over 50 scientists collaborate on 3M to evaluate how grazing strategies affect soil carbon, water dynamics, CO₂ fluxes, plant communities, productivity, social wellbeing, and producer economics.ConclusionsThese insights support the development of ecosystem models and decision-support tools to help producers make evidence-based choices. Beyond data generation, 3M offers a scalable research model that bridges ecological and social sciences to support adaptive, informed grazing management. This integrated framework provides a transferable template for studying any working landscape where human and ecological systems are deeply interconnected.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13021-026-00431-7.Keywords: Social-ecological systems, Ecosystem function, Monitoring, Soil carbon, Modeling

Background

Grazinglands play a critical role in addressing global challenges, including soil organic carbon (SOC) accrual for climate change mitigation, biodiversity conservation, rural livelihoods, and food production [31]. These complex social-ecological systems (SES) span diverse climates and ecosystems, from intensively managed temperate pastures to arid rangelands, delivering key ecosystem services in addition to their role in food production from livestock grazing [85]. However, despite their importance, we still lack a robust understanding of how to optimize grazing management for ecosystem services across the diversity of grazinglands – from small-scale intensively managed temperate pastures to expansive, publicly owned, arid rangelands with little management – largely due to research approaches that have been constrained by disciplinary silos, short-term experimental designs, and inadequate monitoring methods [30, 114]. These limitations hinder our ability to generate actionable insights that balance ecological and socioeconomic outcomes. To overcome these barriers, an interdisciplinary approach that integrates biophysical, management, and socioeconomic dimensions is urgently needed [114]. This study presents a framework for interdisciplinary grazingland research that addresses these challenges and provides a scalable approach to studying ecosystem dynamics and their responses to diverse grazing management strategies across multiple ecoregions. By bridging gaps between ecological and social sciences, this work aims to advance the development of evidence-based grazing management strategies that enhance ecosystem function while supporting producer livelihoods.

Grazinglands provide a range of essential ecosystem services (e.g., SOC storage, water and nutrient cycling, food provisioning, etc.), but these benefits face mounting threats from climate change, economic pressures, human development, and suboptimal grazing management [24, 73, 114, 114]. Overgrazing can degrade landscape function, triggering SOC losses [114], biodiversity decline, and diminished productivity [55]. While some advocate for the removal of grazing animals as a primary restoration strategy [114, 114], exclosure studies yield mixed results, suggesting that eliminating livestock may not always be the most effective approach for ecosystem restoration [47, 85, 114].

An alternative strategy is improved grazing management, leveraging grazing livestock as a tool for ecosystem restoration. This approach utilizes existing producer-managed livestock, reducing the need for costly interventions such as compost or fertilizers. It is scalable across grazinglands worldwide and can be adapted to local conditions by adjusting grazing timing, intensity, frequency, and duration to optimize SOC accrual, plant productivity, and biodiversity [114]. These ecological outcomes, in turn, influence socioeconomic factors such as profitability through plant productivity, animal weight gains, price premiums or reduced input costs, as well as producer wellbeing [85, 114, 114, 114].

Despite the potential of improved grazing management, several barriers limit research and application. Grazinglands exhibit significant inter- and intra-landscape variation [85, 114, 114]), requiring site-specific research [29, 114]. As a result, responses to grazing differ across ecosystem types, necessitating studies that span diverse climates and management contexts [47, 64, 85, 114, 114]. Traditional monitoring methods, developed for croplands or small-scale research, struggle to capture ecosystem responses on comparatively large and heterogenous grazinglands [28], and therefore comparing and refining novel monitoring approaches is crucial for accurate, large-scale assessment. Additionally, traditional grazing experiments are often short-term and conducted on small plots with simplistic grazing scenarios to ensure control and replication (examples include: [85, 114, 114]). While this approach is conventionally the scientific gold standard, these studies do not necessarily translate well to real-world management and their findings do not always align with long-term, landscape-scale outcomes observed on working ranches [30, 114]. Predictive models of ecosystem functioning in grazinglands are likewise limited in their applicability due to this data paucity and would benefit greatly from larger-scale research integrating producer-led data [85, 114].

Ideally, addressing this trade-off would involve pairing mechanistic experiments with “natural experiments,” in which producers implement their own grazing management across diverse ecoregions, while ecological outcomes are measured to evaluate responses along the full grazing management continuum. The latter has been traditionally challenging due to variability in producer record-keeping systems and the level of detail captured, which can limit quantitative analysis. In addition, interpreting grazing management outcomes requires social science approaches that can capture the motivations, constraints, and decision-making contexts underlying producer management choices [85]. More research is needed to identify the management factors driving ecosystem outcomes [114, 114], but understanding producer’s implementation of grazing management and replicating these in experimental scenarios poses its own challenges, as producer decision-making is shaped by tradition, climate, ownership, and socioeconomic factors [114, 114, 114]. Therefore, while advancements in biophysical research are crucial, studying the human dimensions of grazing—including social, cultural, and economic drivers—is equally vital for a holistic understanding of the benefits of, and barriers to, adoption of improved management practices [56, 62, 77, 114, 114, 114, 114]. Despite progress in social science research on farming and ranching systems, collaboration between ecological and social scientists remains limited (see Augustine et al., [9] for a notable exception), impeding the translation of research into actionable management strategies.

Addressing these challenges requires a new generation of interdisciplinary science that integrates socioeconomic drivers with ecological outcomes. This manuscript presents the framework of a novel interdisciplinary project designed to fill these gaps, balancing scientific rigor with practical applicability for the first comprehensive social-ecological study of grazinglands. This manuscript outlines the project’s goals, research design, measurement and modeling methods, and collaborative approach, offering a template for future integrated studies on land management and ecosystem function. While the 3M project was developed for grazinglands, its research framework is equally well-suited for application to other managed ecosystems.

Novelty of the 3M project

The Metrics, Management, and Monitoring (3M) project is a multi- and interdisciplinary effort designed to better understand socioeconomic drivers of grazing management and how these management approaches influence multi-indicator grazingland ecosystem health across diverse ecoregions in the U.S. To this end, the project is structured around three overarching goals:

use interdisciplinary approaches to measure comprehensive social-ecological health of grazingland ecosystems across multiple ecoregions;

use interdisciplinary approaches to measure comprehensive social-ecological health of grazingland ecosystems across multiple ecoregions;

evaluate measurement methods to develop scalable, efficient, and accurate grazingland monitoring indicators to support advancement of robust ecosystem models and producer-friendly decision-support tools;

evaluate measurement methods to develop scalable, efficient, and accurate grazingland monitoring indicators to support advancement of robust ecosystem models and producer-friendly decision-support tools;

balance experimental design with practical application by measuring across both intensive experimental “hubs” and producer farms and ranches.

balance experimental design with practical application by measuring across both intensive experimental “hubs” and producer farms and ranches.

To meet these goals, we coalesced over 50 scientists from 10 universities and institutions (Table S1), organized into 12 thematic, team-based modules spanning socioeconomics, soil, water, and plant dynamics, to carry out an integrated, multi- and interdisciplinary measurement-modeling study. In addition to module scientists, the project includes individuals essential to leadership, administration, coordination, and producer engagement. With the understanding that grazinglands are complex social-ecological systems in which outcomes are best understood holistically, we identified three contrasting ecoregions across the U.S., located in Oklahoma (OK), Michigan (MI), and Wyoming (WY) and established a nested field design, in which each ecoregion contains one to two experimental hubs serving as the scientific anchor to ~ 20 producer sites per ecoregion (59 total). We established these experimental hubs to serve as high-resolution monitoring sites to enable intensive biophysical data collection and provide a controlled yet operationally relevant setting to implement, validate, and investigate the utility of advanced measurement techniques (e.g., eddy covariance towers) prior to broader application. At each hub, we established replicated blocks with contrasting grazing management treatments to measure a suite of response metrics, including soil carbon (C) dynamics, water flux dynamics, ecosystem CO₂ fluxes, plant community composition, and both measured and remotely-sensed plant productivity. These intensive hub measurements are complemented by a broader network of 59 working producer farms and ranches across the hub ecoregions, where the same core biophysical data are measured, but at lower intensity, alongside additional metrics focused on social and economic outcomes (e.g., indicators of social wellbeing, resilience, and producer economics). Together, this dual structure is designed to balance internal and external validity: the experimental hubs provide controlled, replicated settings needed to isolate mechanisms and strengthen inference, while the network of working producer farms and ranches captures real-world management diversity, enhancing the generalizability and scalability of resulting predictions. All data generated through these measurement and monitoring efforts are organized within an innovative and comprehensive data scaffolding framework that supports the development of next-generation ecosystem models capable of mechanistically simulating grazing management and ecosystem responses, as well as integrated decision-support tools.

Here, we describe the methods employed across the entirety of the project as an example of scaled, rigorous, multi- and interdisciplinary research on working grazinglands across the U.S. Accordingly, this manuscript focuses on documenting the design, rationale, and implementation of the 3M framework at project inception; therefore, rather than presenting interim findings here, results can be found in dedicated, outcome-focused publications as the dataset develops.

Intensive experimental hubs

Hub experimental design and grazing treatments

We selected four experimental hubs for our intensive biophysical data collection to capture a broad gradient of aridity, grazingland type (e.g., rangeland vs. pasture; see SI Sect. 2. Grazingland definitions), and other ecoregional variation (e.g., dominant plant communities) across key grazing landscapes: two sites in OK operated by the Noble Research Institute and one each in MI and WY operated by Michigan State University and the University of Wyoming, respectively. In OK, one hub serves as a transitional zone with humid subtropical climate and native prairie rangeland with a mixed plant community. In contrast, the other OK hub, located in the same humid subtropical climate, was formerly cropland and has since been planted with a managed forage mix and is considered a pasture system. The MI hub is characterized by a temperate, humid managed pasture system, and the WY hub represents semi-arid, high-elevation native steppe rangeland (Table 1). This design facilitates our ability to examine ecosystem function, soil C dynamics, and other ecological outcomes from grazing management under distinct climatic and management conditions in each ecoregion. Incorporating data from these diverse landscapes strengthens the calibration and validation of the process-based models and decision-support tools developed in the project, improving their robustness, scalability, and ability to represent grazingland C and water cycling.

Following baseline measurements at the intensive hubs in Year 1 (Y1 see Fig. S1 for project timeline), we implemented two experimental grazing treatments – an adaptive (AD) approach and a prescriptive (PR) approach – designed to evaluate the ecological outcomes of flexible versus fixed rotational management (Fig. 1). The AD framework is designed to support dynamic grazing decisions grounded in soil health principles and responsive to real-time environmental conditions. In practice, these decisions are informed by multiple interacting considerations, including forage utilization, recovery following grazing, seasonal and weather-driven variation in forage production, and the condition of the entire plant community and key species (Fig. 1 )[85].

Conceptual comparison of adaptive (AD) and prescriptive (PR) grazing management treatments, illustrating differences in decision-making cycles (top) and their resulting on-the-ground implementation using example pasture rotations (bottom). In the AD treatment, livestock movements are informed by multiple dynamic indicators, including forage utilization, seasonality and weather within each hub’s climatic context, plant species indicators, and forage recovery following grazing. These indicators are evaluated iteratively within a cyclical process of planning, adjustment, execution, and evaluation. In contrast, livestock movements in the PR treatment are determined primarily by a fixed number of calendar rest days. At the hub scale, AD pasture rotations are nonlinear and context-dependent, reflecting the combined influence of ecological indicators and logistical constraints, whereas PR rotations follow a more fixed and predetermined sequence.

Across hubs, forage utilization functions as an integrative decision variable that reflects the combined effects of forage availability, stocking density, and grazing duration. Utilization targets are therefore achieved not through a single fixed parameter, but by adaptively adjusting paddock size, herd density, and the length of grazing events in response to current forage conditions. These utilization decisions operate in concert with assessments of plant and pasture recovery following prior grazing, as well as broader seasonal and weather contexts that influence forage growth and timing. Managers also incorporate species indicators – such as floral stem density, presence of seedlings, plant vigor, and proportion of green leaves in key species – as qualitative signals of plant community condition and readiness for re-grazing.

Because AD grazing decisions are responsive to these interacting indicators, implementation varies across hubs and can shift among years within a hub as growing conditions change. Common adaptive elements include adjustments to stocking density, grazing duration, rotation frequency, and rest periods, all structured around the dual goals of meeting forage utilization targets and ensuring adequate recovery. In contrast, the PR treatments adhere to a fixed rest–recovery schedule based on calendar intervals that remain consistent from year to year, but differ among hubs to reflect regional practice. Both treatments represent realistic, regionally grounded grazing scenarios (Fig. 1; Table 2). By implementing the contrasting approaches across a gradient of precipitation, vegetation, and grazingland types, this design explicitly accommodates the possibility that local ecophysiological contexts may affect the performance of AD relative to PR grazing strategies. To that end, our study seeks to evaluate and understand the contextual limits on any potential adaptive advantages that grazing management alone can achieve.

Experimental hubs were constructed using a standard randomized complete block design (RCBD). Each hub has three replicate blocks, with the exception of WY, which has five due to its relatively large size. Each block is a split-plot design where two plots within each block were randomly assigned either an AD or PR grazing management treatment. We considered several criteria to stratify blocks within each hub to ensure plots within each block were similar at baseline, including ecological site descriptions (ESDs; developed by the United States Department of Agriculture Natural Resources Conservation Service; [114]), soil texture, and land-use history (Table 1).

Within each treatment plot, we collect data on plot subdivisions into temporary paddocks (both number and size, where applicable), livestock classes and weights, herd sizes, and in-out dates associated with each pasture/paddock. With these data, we calculate grazing management metrics such as stocking density, stock days hectare− 1 (ha), grazing duration, and length of rest periods. We calculate these metrics both for each individual grazing event as well as summary statistics for the grazing season (i.e., average stocking density). An annual average stocking density for each treatment is calculated by determining the average liveweight of cow-calf pairs (average of initial and weaning weight) in the herd for that year, and the average area of the paddocks utilized in each grazing event that year. A ‘grazing event’ is defined as any new introduction of cattle to a grazing area that has had some period of rest since the last time it was grazed. Stocking density is reported in kg liveweight ha− 1 and animal unit (AU) ha− 1 with a 454 kg per AU conversion [114]. Because AD treatments are meant to “adapt” to prevailing forage and weather conditions, we report only the “target” stock density for all AD treatments and actual stock density for all PR treatments (Table 2).

In 2024, ungrazed control sites were established at three of the four hubs, with the exception of WY due to cost constraints. These sites serve as counterfactuals to our grazing treatments, capturing ecological dynamics in adjacent areas that share all other relevant stratification criteria but exclude grazing. Baseline sampling and monitoring were conducted in 2024, and these sites will remain ungrazed and unmanaged for the project duration.

Hub management and coordination

Each experimental hub has a “hub coordinator” who serves multiple critical roles. Prior to baseline sampling and monitoring at each hub in 2022, hub coordinators compiled information relevant to each module, aggregated protocols, and coordinated communication amongst and between hubs and modules to determine season and order of data collection. All biophysical science modules collected baseline data from the experimental hubs in tandem, facilitated by hub coordinators. Hub coordinators continue to serve important roles at each hub, including managing data and coordinating communication efforts. They also serve as point-of-contact for all producers associated with each region, which we detail further in Sect. "Producer survey and recruitment".

Oklahoma rangeland hub: Noble Research Institute’s Coffey Ranch

Coffey Ranch, the native tallgrass prairie rangeland experimental hub, is located in south-central OK within a humid subtropical climate (Cfa, [17]) in the Crosstimbers and Southern Tallgrass Prairie (CSTP) Ecoregion ([45]; Table 1). This region experiences hot summers, cold winters, and the highest annual precipitation among study hubs (945 ± 305 mm yr⁻¹), with a mosaic of forests, woodlands, savannas, and grasslands. The 971-ha ranch is nearly half wooded, with the remainder comprising warm-season, perennial native rangeland available for grazing. Key warm-season species at Coffey Ranch include big bluestem (Andropogon gerardii), little bluestem (Schizachyrium scoparium), eastern gamagrass (Tripsacum dactyloides), and indiangrass (Sorghastrum nutans) (Table 2).

From 1988 to 2005, the ranch was managed under Holistic Resource Management [114], utilizing high stock densities in fast rotations with extended pasture rest. Cattle were removed in 2006, and the ranch remained ungrazed until 2018. From 2018 to 2022, grazing was reintroduced under extensive, adaptive management, with grazing periods adjusted based on pasture size and seasonal conditions (Table 1).

Six permanent pastures at the ranch, averaging 10–12 ha each, were chosen as treatment plots for the 3M project. Pastures at this site have variable geographies and plant communities, containing different proportions of graminoids, forbs, legumes, and woody species (Table 1). Therefore, pastures were stratified and grouped into three blocks based on ESDs, with special considerations given to plant community composition, slope, aspect, and soil type. Soils at this site belong to the Mollisol order, which are typical of grasslands and known for their fertility and well-developed horizons ([114]; Table 1). Pastures were grouped into three blocks based on similarities within each of these attributes and randomly assigned an AD/PR treatment (Blocks #1–3; Table 2).

Grazing management treatments began in June after baseline sampling was completed in April 2022. Both treatments are assigned herds of 50 cow-calf pairs each, but management and stocking density varies according to treatment. Plots assigned PR grazing are stocked with low stock density (average 2,214 kg animal liveweight ha− 1). Grazing days per grazing event varies based on forage availability and season, though target forage utilization is ~ 50% and rest periods between grazing events for this treatment follow a planned calendar rotation schedule (target 75 days). Depending on the year (rainfall, forage availability, etc.), PR plots at this site can be grazed year-round. However, if the forage availability is less than the target (1680 kg dry matter ha− 1), then grazing PR plots is deferred beyond the usual rest period (usually during winter season), until they can be grazed again in spring of the following year (Table 2).

Plots assigned AD grazing are subdivided into smaller paddocks to allow for the use of greater stock densities, with the goal of achieving ~ 10× greater density than PR. Cattle are moved daily to multiple times per day when feasible, and paddock subdivisions and sizes are adjusted dynamically to achieve ~ 50% target forage utilization and ≥ 1,100 kg dry matter ha⁻¹ of residual biomass (Fig. 1; Fig. S2). Unlike PR, re-grazing in AD plots is not scheduled according to fixed rest intervals; instead, the timing of re-entry into AD pastures is determined adaptively based on plant height and recovery indicators (e.g., proportion of green leaves, overall plant vigor), attainment of ≥ 1,680 kg dry matter ha⁻¹ of standing biomass, and whole-ranch rotation logistics (Fig. 1; Fig. S2). In practice, AD plots are generally re-grazed only after completion of a full rotation through all AD plots.

Oklahoma pasture hub: Noble Research Institute’s Red River Grazing Facility

Red River Grazing Facility is the managed pasture experimental hub located in south central OK. This hub is located in close proximity to Coffey Ranch and shares the same ecoregion. Red River Grazing Facility is a small experimental pasture site comprised of 58.2 ha, nearly all of which are grazed. From 1980 to 2017, this site was intensively cropped as peanuts and cereal rye, including conventional use of tillage, fertilization, pesticide application, and irrigation. In 2017, the site was planted with bermudagrass for hay production and continuously fertilized and pesticide treated until 2020. From late 2020 and until the 3M project establishment in April 2022, this site was transitioned from hay production to being directly grazed. During this time, additional plants were introduced, pastures were rotationally grazed, and fertilizer and pesticide use was reduced but not discontinued (Table 1).

Six permanent pastures, averaging 9.7-ha each, were chosen as treatment plots for the 3M project and paired in three blocks (Blocks #4–6). Plant communities, ESDs, land use history and soil type are very homogeneous at this site, negating the need for stratification when assigning AD/PR treatment pastures and grouping into blocks. Soils at this site belong to the mollisol and alfisol USDA soil orders [114] and are the most coarsely textured soils of all hubs in the project (Table 1).

Grazing management treatments began after baseline sampling in April 2022. Management of the PR and AD grazing treatments are handled the same as Coffey Ranch with a few notable differences. Both treatments at this hub are assigned herds of 40 cow-calf pairs each. Plots assigned PR grazing are stocked with low stock density (average 2,313 kg animal liveweight ha− 1), and rest periods between grazing events for this treatment follow a planned calendar rotation schedule (target 60 days). Plots assigned AD grazing are subject to the same thresholds and decision making described above (Fig. 1, Fig. S2).

Michigan hub: Michigan State University’s Lake City Research Center

Lake City Research Center is the managed pasture experimental hub located in northern MI, which is a humid continental climate (Dfb; [17]) in the Northern Great Lakes Forests (NGLF) Ecoregion [45]. This region experiences warm summers and cold winters, approximately 900 ± 100 mm yr-1 of annual rainfall, and was historically dominated by diverse hardwood and coniferous forests and significant wetland habitats. Lake City Research Center is comprised of 326 ha, of which 192 ha are used for grazing. From the 1980 s until 2010, the farm was an input-intensive beef operation characterized by continuous grazing during the growing season (May-November), extensive haying, and regular fertilizer applied to seeded cool-season grasses and legumes (Table 1).

In 2010–2011, the farm underwent a transition in both input and grazing management strategies. Pastures were seeded to include a more diverse mixture of grasses, legumes, and forbs (see Table 1 for dominant species), and both haying and fertilizing ceased. Grazing transitioned from continuous grazing to adaptive multi paddock (AMP) grazing, emphasizing short grazing periods with fast rotations and long, adaptive recovery periods to prioritize forage recovery and prevent overgrazing [114]. Livestock herds were maintained at high stock densities to improve utilization, with a target of maximum 50% forage utilization.

Six permanent pastures, averaging 4 ha in size, were chosen as treatment plots for the 3M project and paired within three blocks (Blocks #7–9). Because plant communities, ESDs, and land use history at this hub are relatively homogeneous, pastures were stratified and grouped into three blocks based on soil type, each containing two randomly assigned AD/PR treatment plots. Soils at this site belong to the inceptisol and aflisol USDA soil orders [114], have minimal horizon development, and are coarsely textured (Table 1).

Grazing management treatments began after baseline sampling in June 2022. Plots assigned PR grazing were initially stocked with 30 cow-calf pairs at a low density (average 3,922 kg of animal liveweight ha− 1), with recovery periods between grazing events following a planned calendar rotation target of 30 days and target forage utilization of 70%. Plots assigned AD grazing were initially stocked with ~ 115 cow–calf pairs and subdivided into smaller temporary paddocks to facilitate substantially greater stocking densities (target ~ 10x or greater than PR). Cattle are moved daily to multiple times per day when feasible, and paddock boundaries are adjusted dynamically based on plant height and available biomass to achieve ~ 50% target forage utilization and ≥ 1,100 kg dry matter ha⁻¹ residual biomass (Fig. 1; Fig. S2). Unlike PR, re-grazing in AD plots is not scheduled according to fixed rest intervals; instead, the timing of re-entry into AD paddocks emerges from a combination of plant recovery indicators and whole-farm rotation logistics. In practice, paddocks are typically re-grazed only after completion of a full rotation through all AD paddocks, and because stocking rates at this hub are conservative relative to peak forage production, AD paddocks are often re-entered in an ‘over-rested’ condition (frequently > 60 days of recovery).

Wyoming hub: University of Wyoming’s McGuire Ranch

McGuire Ranch is the native rangeland experimental hub located in southeastern WY, which is a semi-arid cold steppe climate (BSk; [17]) in the Wyoming Basin ecoregion ([45]; Table 1). This is the highest altitude hub at 2190 m above sea level, which contributes to its unique climate (Table 1). This region is characterized by cold winters and warm summers, receives the least precipitation amongst hubs in this study (averaging 353 ± 14 mm yr− 1), and has been historically grazed and maintained as native steppe rangeland (Table 1).

McGuire Ranch spans 2246 ha, all of which have been periodically grazed historically. One previous landowner attempted cultivation from 1978 to 1979 on ~ 1000 acres by plowing, seeding regar brome (Bromus biebersteinii), alfalfa (Medicago sativa), and malting barley (Hordeum vulgare), and irrigating. Cultivation agriculture was unsuccessful at this site and was halted in 1979, after which it was extensively grazed prior to foreclosure in 1984. The ranch was then left ungrazed under state ownership until its purchase by the University of Wyoming in 1992. From then until the establishment of the 3M project in 2022, McGuire Ranch was managed in a 3–4 pasture extensive rotational summer seasonal grazing system (June – October) using cow-calf pairs (Table 1).

This site is native rangeland with high species diversity and is co-dominated by native cool season grasses and shrubs (predominantly sagebrush (Artemisia spp.) and rabbitbrush (Ericameria and Chrysothamnus spp.). However, two of the five blocks established for the 3M project contain areas that were included in the ranch’s two-year cultivation history. As a result, they contain several non-native cool-season grasses, including crested wheatgrass (Agropyron cristatum), meadow brome (Bromus biebersteinii), and Russian wildrye (Psathyrostachys juncea) (Table 1).

Ten pastures were established as plots for the 3M project, paired in five blocks (Blocks #10–14). These newly established pastures represent 42% of the total ranch property and range in size from 39 to 149 ha each. At the ranch scale, plant communities, soil types, and slope are relatively consistent at this site. However, pastures were stratified based on ESDs, soil types, and land-use histories, and grouped into blocks based on pasture-scale variation along each of these gradients. Soils at this site are aridisols, which are minimally weathered, calcareous, rocky, and are generally low fertility ([114]; Table 1).

Grazing treatments at McGuire Ranch started with the 2023 growing season, following baseline sampling in late August 2022. The five PR grazing plots each have their own cow-calf herd, which ranged from 10 to 31 cow-calf pairs depending on pasture size to maintain a consistent stocking density of 115 kg of animal liveweight ha− 1 average across all PR plots. The PR treatment at this site reflects the region’s typical conventional summer stocking strategy, characterized by a fixed grazing period in each pasture – two to three months in this case (Fig. 1; Table 2).

Pastures assigned to the AD treatment are managed as a single common cow–calf herd (~ 100 pairs) that rotates annually through all five AD plots, a strategy that reflects the shorter growing season and lower forage availability characteristic of this region (Fig. S2). Stock densities in AD plots are substantially higher than in PR plots (typically ~ 3-10x), due to the fixed herd size and variable pasture area (Table 2). Cattle are moved from one AD plot to the next when plots reach ~ 50% forage utilization, which is evaluated using the USDA Landscape Appearance Method, a visual classification approach that assesses utilization based on plant appearance and biomass removed. Unlike PR, re-grazing and rotation order in the AD system are not fixed; the starting plot and sequence of rotation are intentionally varied among years so that, over a five year period, each AD plot experiences grazing at different positions in the rotation sequence (Fig. 1; Fig. S2).

Producer sites

Premise of including producer sites

To overcome limitations of small-scale grazing experiments in understanding ecological outcomes of grazing management, the project management team recruited 59 producers to participate in the 3M project. Achieving diversity in climate, grazingland type, and land and grazing management strategies was a critical goal driving producer enrollment and selection in this project. Accordingly, recruitment efforts expanded into adjacent states relevant to each hub to include Colorado (CO) and Texas (TX). Ultimately, 59 producers across these three regions: 20 in OK/TX, 19 in MI, and 20 in CO/WY, joined the project. We refer to producer sites as farms and ranches to reflect regional conventions, as grazing operations in the western U.S. are commonly defined as ranches, while those in the east are typically defined as farms. Producer farms and ranches are monitored for the same ecological outcomes as the intensive hubs and serve as the basis for socioeconomic and wellbeing data collection. Combining these data is intended to allow for a more holistic assessment of feedback among social, psychological, and economic wellbeing, grazing management strategy, and resulting outcomes on SOC and other ecological indicators [59]. These data are also utilized to enhance model development, both for research and for creating producer feedback tools that support decision-making toward various financial and ecological goals. Although producers are asked to organize their information regarding finances and management decisions, all formal data collection is conducted by research team members.

Producer survey and recruitment

We developed an online survey using Qualtrics software to initiate the process of producer recruitment (Qualtrics, Provo, UT). We crafted survey questions intended to gather information in five categories: (1) operational characteristics (e.g., enterprises, land use history, property size), (2) grazing management (e.g., grazing strategy, decision-making, stocking rate), (3) monitoring (e.g., grazing and financial records, soil testing), (4) networks (e.g., cattlemen’s and grazing associations, social media), and (5) demographics (e.g., age, gender, race). The survey was publicized in mid-January of Y2 (Fig. S1), through partnering networks, including conservation districts, state cattlemen’s and farmer’s organizations, the Noble Research Institute, and university extension offices. After two months, we received 319 complete survey responses (and 418 total) from producers in the target states: 201 from OK/TX, 59 from Michigan, and 59 from CO/WY. Hub coordinators first reviewed survey responses to identify eligible producers given the project’s primary inclusion criteria (Fig. 2). Producers who did not meet one or more of these primary criteria and/or did not complete the survey in its entirety were deemed ineligible for participation. Roughly 100 additional survey responses fell into these categories or were later rescinded.

Decision making criteria for choosing producer collaborators in each ecoregion of the 3M project. Once surveys were completed, the final 20 producer collaborators in each hub ecoregion were chosen based on several basic primary study criteria (1), in addition to module specific criteria (2a), maximization of geographic distribution in each ecoregion (2b), and maximization of management along the grazing continuum considering their calculated “adaptiveness” scores (2c). For reference, we calculated the adaptiveness score (see Sect. 3.2) for both the prescriptive and adaptive grazing treatments at each experimental hub to illustrate how they reflect regional differences.

We assigned each eligible producer an ‘adaptiveness score’ to assess the range of grazing management strategies following Sherren et al., [114], Vivas and Hodbod [114], and others (e.g., Bork et al., [25], Fenster, [61], Teague, [114], Teague, [114]. Adaptiveness scores were generated by summing their responses to three questions related to their grazing management (see SI Sect. 4.1), with scores ultimately ranging from 0 (continuous grazing) to 18 (most adaptive). Importantly, adaptiveness scores were designed as a coarse grazing metric to identify candidate producers representing a range of management strategies.

Following a purposive sampling strategy, hub coordinators alongside members of key biophysical science modules interviewed eligible producers to assess their suitability for the project, gather missing data from the survey responses, and expand on their current and historical grazing and overall farm/ranch management. In spring of Y2, we invited producers who met all criteria, were aligned with project goals, and were committed to the project, to participate. Hub coordinators distributed and collected signed producer agreements from all final producer participants, including Michigan State University IRB Board (STUDY00006683). Shortly after, the social science module began interviews related to social wellbeing and resilience, and hub coordinators began working with producers and biophysical science modules to gather information to inform monitoring locations.

Producer grazing management continuum

Historically, studies linking grazing management practices to socioeconomic and ecological outcomes have lacked the resolution needed to establish causality, identify driving mechanisms, and determine which aspects of grazing management are most influential in specific climatic contexts. In reality, producers who graze livestock both globally and domestically fall along a “continuum,” rather than discrete, coarsely defined management categories (e.g., “rotational”). Management approaches along this continuum are often bounded by regional climatic conditions, land management scales, and tradition, among other drivers. We’ve developed a grazing management continuum module for the 3M project to overcome these issues, guided by two main goals: (1) define where producers in different US ecoregions fall on a grazing management continuum, and how their management varies within and across regions, by drawing on a recent framework that defines grazing management in terms of key levers (timing, intensity, frequency, and duration; [114]), to ultimately, (2) use this continuum to understand management decisions along each lever and their impacts on measured ecological outcomes.

To meet these goals, we’ve asked all 59 producers enrolled in the 3M project to share both ranch-scale and granular paddock-level data that mirrors the management data collected at the intensive hubs. These data include farm/ranch size, pasture numbers and sizes, information associated with any pasture subdivisions into temporary paddocks (both number and size), livestock classes and weights, herd sizes, and in-out dates associated with each rotation when applicable, etc. With these data, we calculate important metrics such as stocking rate, stocking density, stock days ha− 1, duration of grazing events, and length of rest periods. While we used coarsely calculated “adaptiveness scores” from producers’ initial survey responses as a secondary criterion during producer enrollment (see SI Sect. 4.1), ultimately our grazing continuum developed from significantly more granular management data will facilitate scalable and robust interrogation on causal grazing management-ecosystem feedback for the first time, to our knowledge.

We collect these data from producers monthly, which allows us to longitudinally assess how these management features shift both intra- (e.g., between growing and dormant seasons) and inter- (e.g., across years with variable precipitation) annually. For more details on our approach to collecting producer grazing management data, see SI Sect. 4.2.

Metrics of grazingland social-ecological health

Background on metrics & site selection

To achieve a comprehensive and integrated understanding of grazingland social-ecological health and its key indicators, our team of 12 distinct research modules initiated a large-scale measurement and monitoring plan. Figure 3 illustrates the plot-level sampling layout and the strategic co-location of measurements across hubs and producer sites. This includes measuring the entire plant-soil-water-atmosphere continuum, with a range of biophysical measurement approaches including state-of-the-art quantitative methods, remote sensing techniques, and qualitative methods accessible to producers. By project conclusion, these biophysical and micrometeorological measurements will have involved installation of 61 eddy covariance flux towers (Sect. "Ecosystem carbon dioxide (CO₂) fluxes") and 522 soil water content sensors; collection of over 3000 infiltration rate tests (Sect. "Water flux dynamics"), 1400 soil cores (Sect. "Soil carbon (C) dynamics") and 6400 biomass samples (Sect. "Remotely sensed vegetative indices") – an unprecedented number for a single, coordinated research effort. In addition to these measurements, over 250 visits will have been made across all hubs and producer sites to conduct annual visual indicator monitoring (Sect. "Plant communities and visual indicators"). To complement these ecological measurements, our socioeconomic modules will have completed over 760 h of interviews with producers (Sect. "Socioeconomic metrics").

Sample site locations for biophysical measurements were selected through an iterative process that involved frequent discussions between hub coordinators, biophysical science modules, and land managers (at experimental hub sites) or producers (at producer sites). A key part of this process was acquiring and digitizing farm and ranch maps to support site selection, as detailed in SI Sect. 4.3. Project leadership worked closely with the land manager to identify an area of 4 + ha of deeded land that met several other primary criteria, including being used to graze cow-calf pairs for at least five years, and adequate vehicle accessibility. Parcel size was determined by the flux tower design, which measures data from a fetch of approximately 4 ha, and by the need for sufficient statistical power to detect changes in SOC. To meet both requirements, we concentrated the majority of biophysical monitoring within the flux tower’s fetch area. The soil and flux modules jointly reviewed the short list of candidate parcels to identify those with site-specific physical characteristics suitable for both sets of methods. Parcels with both relatively homogeneous soil types and that met several land use criteria (e.g., native plant communities where possible, no other inputs such as compost or fertilizer, no tillage in recent history, etc.), and contained minor topography and tree cover from remotely-sensed vegetation height [74] (flux priority) were given the highest ranking. This compromise often resulted in functional selections that worked for all modules. However, in some cases, competing priorities amongst modules resulted in parcel selections that were less than ideal for any single module. A final site selection was made from the top three ranked choices based on feedback from the land manager and considerations for convenience and other project objectives (i.e., maximizing diversity across geography, soil types, and adaptiveness scores at producer sites). A flux tower was placed within each treatment plot at the experimental hubs, and, due to resource constraints, on 10 producer sites per hub region. Producer sites were ultimately selected for a tower if their location helped maximize the diversity and range of adaptiveness scores for the grazing continuum (see Sect. "Producer survey and recruitment"), soil types, and geographical locations with continuous C and water flux monitoring. For consistency, the same site selection criteria were used to identify the sampling plots (4 ha) for biophysical measurements at the remaining producer sites.

We coordinated baseline biophysical data collection to occur within a one- to two-week sampling campaign per ecoregion—ideally on a single site per day—to minimize disruption to each producer’s operation. This approach also brought numerous secondary benefits for project team members, including improved efficiency and collaboration. Exposure to each team’s methods along with opportunities to provide physical and emotional support in the field has created both an appreciation for, and understanding of, the underlying fundamentals of the data being collected. Combined, this experience in the field over the first three years of the project has built trust and respect, and supported deep knowledge sharing amongst team members, which in turn has driven and enhanced subsequent collaborative activities.

In the following sections, we briefly describe each of the biophysical and socioeconomic metrics and how they are measured and integrated. Further details of each method are provided in SI Sect. 4. Extended Methods. A primary outcome of the 3M data collection effort will be to compare the accuracy, efficiency, and scalability of each approach so that recommendations for the most appropriate indicators of grazingland social-ecological health can be made with a full understanding of the inherent tradeoffs. In parallel, we will examine how producer wellbeing, management priorities, and economic outcomes align with or diverge from these ecological indicators, offering a more comprehensive understanding of grazingland health.

Biophysical metrics

Ecosystem carbon dioxide (CO₂) fluxes

Carbon, water, and energy are continuously exchanged between the land surface and the atmosphere through natural biophysical and biochemical processes such as photosynthesis, respiration, and evapotranspiration (ET; see SI Sect. 4.4; [40]). Atmospheric turbulent flux methodologies (i.e., eddy covariance) provide a continuous measure of CO2 and water, i.e. flux data, from a representative area of several hectares surrounding each flux tower. Because of their temporal continuity and granularity, flux data offer powerful insights into ecosystem response to environmental conditions and management practices [12]. The 3M project employs flux towers to: (1) quantify the long-term net ecosystem CO2 exchange of grazinglands, (2) assess the effects of management and environment on grazingland CO2 and water exchanges, at short to long time scales, and (3) generate high-resolution datasets that support the development, calibration, and validation of biogeochemical, process-based, and AI/statistical models for CO2 and ET in grazing systems including biophysical models used in 3M (Sect. "Biophysical ecosystem models") and remote sensing products (Sect. "Remotely sensed vegetative indices").

Flux data products include 30-minute averages of key fluxes—net ecosystem exchange (NEE), ET, latent heat (ET in mass units), sensible heat, gross primary productivity (GPP), ecosystem respiration—as well as incoming and outgoing shortwave and longwave radiation, and precipitation. Ambient atmospheric conditions measured at tower height include wind speed and direction, air temperature, and relative humidity. Soil measurements include temperature, heat flux, and volumetric water content.

The 3M project has a total of 61 flux towers across the combined experimental hub and producer sites: 30 towers on producer sites, 28 towers across all experimental hub treatment plots, and an additional 3 towers at grazing exclosure control sites in OK and MI. Under current project funding, hubs will operate for five years and producer sites for three years—with the intent to extend operations beyond these initial timeframes (see Fig. S1 for complete timeline details). This temporally aligned, replicated, and large-scale deployment of atmospheric flux monitoring is unprecedented across any land use type—and especially in grazinglands—and represents a major advancement in our ability to quantify landscape-scale variability in eddy fluxes and assess the ecological impacts of management practices.

Each flux tower generates nearly a million flux measurements per day, with each measurement corresponding to a probabilistic upwind land-surface source area. We average these high-frequency measurements to reduce uncertainty, producing long-term flux estimates that typically represent a 4–8 ha area surrounding each tower—commonly referred to as the flux footprint. Because of this spatial averaging, towers must be positioned to ensure measurements accurately reflect the pasture of interest and are minimally influenced by non-target surrounding areas [43]. To guide tower placement, we used a flux tower footprint model [85] driven by ERA5 weather reanalysis data [82]. For more details, see SI Sect. 4.4.

Soil carbon (C) dynamics

The goals of the soils work within the 3M project are twofold: (1) to better characterize soil C dynamics in grazinglands, and; (2) quantify the impact and potential of grazing management on soil C dynamics, both organic (SOC) and inorganic (IC) over time to assess climate change mitigation potential and how it varies across ecoregions and the grazing management continuum. We are measuring a variety of both site-level soil characteristics and biogeochemical response variables to grazing management. Site characteristic measurements include soil pH and texture, which are important soil features that govern biogeochemical dynamics linked to our response variables. Our response variables include bulk density (BD), biogeochemical properties (SOC, IC, and nitrogen (N) stocks, stable isotopes δ¹³C and δ¹⁵N) in both bulk soils and soil organic matter (SOM) fractions (see SI Sect. 4.5), such as particulate organic matter (POM), coarse-heavy mineral associated organic matter (CHAOM), and fine mineral associated organic matter (MAOM), and water stable aggregates (WSA). While we are not measuring all aspects of soil health, soil C dynamics and the biogeochemical variables we do capture are central to the core functions that define it—such as nutrient cycling, water retention, and productivity. As such, our soil data—when integrated with data from other modules—serve as meaningful indicators of broader soil health and ecosystem function.

These response variables are complimentary and are designed to allow robust quantification and understanding of soil C and N dynamics in grazinglands, including how they respond to grazing management. Partitioning and quantifying SOC and N into functionally distinct fractions allows further exploration into SOC and N formation pathways, functionalities, stability, and turnover [48]. Additionally, measuring SOM fractions enhances our understanding of how grazing management affects SOC [11], offering several important practical benefits for grazingland managers and producers. These include the potential to develop regionally contextualized grazing recommendations that optimize POM and MAOM accrual in alignment with producer goals [114].

Our IC, δ¹³C, δ¹⁵N, and WSA measurements are strategically included to fill several important gaps in our current understanding of grazingland soils. First, while OC is typically considered to be the most responsive to management and important to climate change mitigation, grazingland soils in arid and semi-arid regions also contain considerable amounts of IC [15, 114]. While often overlooked and removed, IC interacts with SOC and contributes to overall soil C storage and is now recommended for inclusion in effective soil C stewardship strategies [49]. Measuring δ¹³C in soils helps identify the relative contributions of warm-season C4 versus cool-season C3 grasses to SOC [114], while δ¹⁵N provides insight into nitrogen dynamics, serving as an indicator of whether nitrogen is being efficiently recycled within the system or lost through processes like leaching or gas emissions [84]. Lastly, soil aggregates are important functional units in soils which influence soil health features such as water holding capacity and biogeochemical processes such as POM stabilization [23, 114]. Data from our WSA measurements will allow deeper exploration of potential benefits arising from management practices that bolster soil aggregation. Finally, we are collecting and measuring many of these metrics at-depth (down to 1 m), which will be a substantial contribution to our understanding of soil C and N dynamics, which has been primarily informed by samples historically collected to only 30 cm [114].

Detecting and quantifying changes in SOC on grazinglands is challenging due to high spatial heterogeneity and small short-term changes expected from improved grazing [46, 114]. After finalizing flux tower placements at each experimental hub, we conducted a power analysis to determine the number of soil samples required to observe change in SOC over time with 80% statistical power. To parameterize the analysis, we compiled SOC variance estimates from comparable grazingland studies and evaluated a range of plausible effect sizes and spatial variability scenarios. Across these scenarios, the required sample size ranged from 2 to 620 samples, reflecting extremes in effect size and variability. Under a medium-effect, medium-variability scenario—consistent with SOC responses reported for managed grazing systems and with spatial variability intentionally constrained through the sampling design (described below)—the analysis indicated that 10 paired sampling units collected at both baseline and resample would be sufficient. To further enhance detection power under higher-than-expected spatial variability, we increased sampling to 12 units per experimental plot (hub) and producer site. Using ArcGIS, we randomly allocated 20 potential sampling locations per site, incorporating buffer zones around roads, fences, and flux towers. We used Gaia GPS (https://www.gaiagps.com/) and a Trimble unit (Trimble Inc., Westminster, CO) for in-field navigation to sampling points, prioritizing locations 1–12 and substituting from location 13 onward as needed (e.g., if a location was inaccessible, visibly disturbed, etc.).

To capture plot-scale variability while minimizing sampling error, we adopted a geographically paired sampling approach inspired by Spencer et al., [114], in which three soil cores are collected at the vertices of a fixed, equilateral 1 m triangular sampling template and composited by depth, with each triangle treated as a single sampling unit. At baseline sampling, we buried a marker ball at the northern vertex of each triangle to enable precise relocation. After five years, triangles are resampled by relocating the northern vertex and rotating the original triangular template by 90°, thereby constraining spatial heterogeneity to within 0.5 m of the original samples and reducing the number of samples required to detect SOC change. Across baseline sampling at producer sites and both baseline and resampling at hub sites, this design will have yielded 4,140 individual soil cores, composited into approximately 1,400 triangle-level sampling units.

We collect all soil cores using either a truck-mounted hydraulic (in OK/TX and CO/WY; Giddings Machine Company, Windsor, CO) or percussion (in MI; AMS, Inc., American Falls, ID) probe (see Fig. S3). Soil cores are split in-field into four depth increments (0–15, 15–30, 30–50, and 50–100 cm). If the full target depth is not reached for any core, we record the actual depth achieved to ensure accurate BD calculations. We take special care to minimize potential sources of BD measurement error—such as compaction, volume loss, and interference from coarse fragments—as detailed in SI Sect. 4.5. Samples are then composited by triangle and depth into pre-labeled, barcoded storage bags.

We transport all soil samples in coolers to temporary cold storage before shipment to the Soil Innovation Laboratory at Colorado State University. Samples are processed in-house using gold standard methods described in Even et al., ([60]; referred to therein as the “CSU protocol”). After sieving, drying, and grinding, we scan all soil samples (across all triangles and depths) using Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy to predict a suite of soil properties in both bulk soils (e.g., texture, biogeochemical concentrations; [114]) and SOM fractions [114]. On the soil samples collected from a subset of the soil triangles selected for coordinated water measurements (hereafter referred to as “water triangles”; see Sect. 4.2.3), we measure the full suite of site characteristic and response variables, including all biogeochemical properties on both bulk soil and SOM fractions, texture, pH, and WSA. For these water triangle samples, analyses are conducted on all depth increments to 1 m, except WSA, which is measured only in topsoils (0–10 and 10–30 cm). We analyze biogeochemical properties on all baseline samples from experimental hubs (n = 1340), but on producer sites, we limit this analysis to a smaller sample subset to reduce analytical costs. This subset includes soils from the water triangles at each site, along with additional samples selected based on FTIR spectral characteristics using a representativeness approach adapted from Ramírez et al. [125]. For detailed measurement methods, see SI Sect. 4.5.

Using the same design, we resample all experimental hubs and producer sites five years after baseline to determine changes in our response variables, namely temporal SOC stock change, as a result of grazing management. We compare SOC stocks on the basis of equivalent soil mass (ESM; [114]) using baseline samples for reference mass. Rather than comparing plot or site level SOC means over time, we assess SOC stock changes over time individually by triangle and average the differences to take full advantage of our experimental design. These methods provide a gold standard account of SOC stock change at the plot level, which is key for validation of less direct techniques deployed in the project, including the flux tower estimates of NEE and modeled fluxes. Other triangle-level soil data such as texture, WSA, and BD are also invaluable for parameterizing hydrological models (Sect. "Hydrological modeling").

Water flux dynamics

Water held in the porous soil medium (hereafter referred to as volumetric water content or VWC) is the primary variable constraining forage growth and nutrient cycling in grazingland ecosystems because it mediates plant photosynthesis and microbial processes [85]. Annual average VWC is primarily dictated by soil type, infiltration, and the timing and volume of annual precipitation, and therefore it varies greatly across grazinglands in the U.S. and within the three ecoregions of the M project. Monitoring daily changes in VWC in the root zone of the soil profile allows us to quantify the water balance, e.g. how much soil water storage changes due to precipitation, evapotranspiration, and deep percolation below the root zone, as well as assess important agronomic soil variables such as plant water use efficiency, plant available water, permanent wilting point, and field capacity [37]. These variables can be compared to other soil health indicators and biophysical variables to develop linkages between management and ecosystem outcomes such as hydraulic function [10]. Further, we can quantify the limits that the intrinsic soil properties place on plant available water, a key variable for forage development and drought resilience.

Given its importance for contextualizing spatial heterogeneities in measured SOC and biomass [114], and its relevance for teasing apart the influence that grazing management may have on this key variable, we place significant effort on continuous monitoring of VWC across the hubs and (half) the producer sites. Within the 4 ha flux tower footprint where the 12 randomly placed soil triangles are located, a subset of three triangles were selected for intensive water monitoring, due to time and equipment constraints. This subset of triangles was selected based on in-field, visual assessments of the topographic positioning and vegetation conditions to capture either diverse topographic positions (e.g., local depressions, highs, and slopes), or diverse groundcover (e.g., bare ground, grass, shrub), if that is the dominant physical characteristic of a low-relief site.

VWC is measured hourly at three depths (15, 40 and 60 cm at all MI, OK/TX sites, and at 10, 20 and 30 cm depths at sites in WY/CO) at each of the three locations, beginning in Y1 at the hub sites, and Y2 at the producer sites. At the MI hub, the glacial geomorphology of the region and the low topographic relief of the region combined with the temperate climate produces highly heterogenous patches of perched groundwater within the root zone. To accurately assess the spatial variability of the water balance at the field scale it is important to understand how this ‘groundwater subsidy’ influences other measurements and plant productivity. Therefore, at the MI hub we also monitor hourly shallow groundwater level fluctuations at the same triangle locations as VWC. This high-resolution continuous time-series data is also used to calibrate hydrological models which in turn provide data products that are not measured directly and provide validation for upscaled modeling efforts (Sect. "Hydrological modeling" ).

In addition to VWC monitoring, discrete manual measurements of saturated hydraulic conductivity are also collected at all hubs and producer sites. Saturated hydraulic conductivity is a proxy for infiltration rate, an important metric of ecological health that reflects the combined effect of soil texture, compaction, organic matter accumulation, plant and root health, and macropore development [20, 65]. Low infiltration is associated with increased risk of soil erosion, and water and nutrient losses via surface runoff [136], that can lead to desertification [38]. Controlled studies have previously explored the influence of stocking density and grazing duration on infiltration and runoff responses with mixed results [114, 114], highlighting the need for further research.

Infiltration rates are measured at the same three triangles with VWC monitoring using Saturo Infiltrometers (METER Group, Pullman, WA), with a 10 cm insertion ring following the suggested cycle duration and pressure head for a given soil type and saturation level. Infiltration rates are measured twice annually (spring and fall) at the hub sites in Y1-5, and once at each producer site at baseline sampling. During each measurement event, three infiltration experiments are conducted simultaneously adjacent to the soil sampling triangle. Concurrent with, and adjacent to, all infiltration experiments, we collect soil samples using a 5 cm hand auger and separate into 0–15 and 15–30 cm depth intervals to coincide with soil module depths. We analyze these samples for total C and N concentrations and nitrate and ammonium. These biannual measurements at the hubs provide data to fill the gap between baseline and 5-year resampling by the soil module, and validation data for estimates of nutrient cycling by the modeling teams (Sect. "Biophysical ecosystem models"). To avoid taking measurements or samples from locations previously disturbed by sampling, during subsequent visits we rotate the sample infrastructure around the triangle in a pre-determined direction. For detailed methods, see SI Sect. 4.6.

Plant communities and visual indicators

The biophysical indicators detailed above all rely on robust state-of-the-art measurement methods that are both costly and often inaccessible to land managers, in addition to being prohibitively slow and expensive for short-term decision making. We developed this module to quantitatively monitor key indicators of plant communities as a biophysical metric, as well as qualitatively monitor other visual indicators that have a history of use in producer decision making and can be easily interpreted and implemented by producers (Woods et al., 2015 [114]).

To assess plant communities, we collect intensive data on species richness, species diversity, plant community composition, and canopy cover at baseline sampling, and again after five years. Each of these are important as standalone metrics and to overall grazingland health, as they are key drivers of outcomes such as resilience and productivity [19, 85]. Additionally, these vegetative components are known to directly mediate the impacts of grazing management on other indicators measured within the project such as SOC, and water and CO2 fluxes [114, 114, 114].

To complement the intensive plant community data, we annually monitor 15 other indicators using less scientifically robust methods, instead relying on visual, tactile, and olfactory observations. These indicators include live canopy cover, plant community composition by functional group, microfauna, litter and dung decomposition, erosion, and soil capping, among others (see Table S2). These low to no-cost methods are designed to encourage land managers to observe and record how their unique ecosystem responds to management decisions with an emphasis on the six principles of soil health ([57]; USDA, 2024 [114]). The goal of these visual indicators is two-fold. First, collecting these data – using accessible and consistent methods of monitoring and record keeping – gives producers long-term evidence of how management practices influence the health and functioning of their grazinglands. In addition to collecting these data, we also provide training and education to willing producer participants in the project to help them operationalize these methods on their farms and ranches (see Sect. "Producer education and engagement"). Second, these data will allow us to examine when and if these subjective visual indicators of ecological health and ecosystem functioning are a useful proxy for costly and less accessible biophysical analyses through comparison with the data from the other biophysical modules. This will inform our development of tools which balance accuracy and reliability with accessibility in ways that are useful to producer decision making on shorter, management-relevant timescales.

To monitor both plant communities and visual indicators, we implemented the Ecological Outcomes Verification (EOV) 3.0 protocol, originally developed for the Savory Institute by an academic and producer collaboration effort (Savory Institute, [114]). We chose this protocol because it is widely accepted by producers and is currently in practice for monitoring thousands of ranches globally. This protocol outlines a method for both robust plant community monitoring as well as visual methods for monitoring other indicators, grouped into four categories: water cycling, mineral cycling, energy cycling, and plant biodiversity. These data are then used to calculate an Ecological Health Index (EHI) score for each site relative to the “maximum potential for the ecoregion” [114]. By scaling certain indicators relative to the potential of each ecoregion, the final EHI scores allow for meaningful comparisons within ecoregions, while still enabling broader comparisons across regions to assess how grazinglands respond to different management approaches.

We collect intensive plant community data across all hubs and producer sites beginning at baseline and again after five years (Fig. S1). Briefly, we establish two georeferenced, 25 m transects within each flux tower footprint, along which we collect 100 line-point intercept measurements, to the species level where possible [50, 114]. To capture additional species not intercepted by the transects, we complement line-point intercept with belt and flex area sampling, monitoring the 25 × 13 m space between the two transects (Fig. S4; [80, 85]). Combined, these data are used to calculate species richness, Shannon Diversity Index, plant community composition, bare ground, and litter cover (see SI Sect. 4.7 for more details). Following the EOV protocol, we also collect several soil samples for commercial soil health measurements, water infiltration measurements, and photographs near the transects. Although these soil and water data are not used by the corresponding standalone modules, they are included in comparative analyses to evaluate how well the EOV protocol reflects site conditions relative to more robust methods.

In contrast, all 15 qualitative visual indicators are monitored annually (beginning at baseline) for five years at each of the 12 soil triangles (see Sect. "Soil carbon (C) dynamics" ) within each plot (at experimental hubs) and producer site. These indicators (see Table S2 for full list) are estimated ocularly and used to calculate annual EHI scores. This annual monitoring is intended to improve understanding of how these qualitative metrics respond in the short term and whether they may serve as leading indicators of longer-term ecological change (e.g., SOC). For detailed methods on the implementation of the EOV protocol, see SI Sect. 4.7.

Remotely sensed vegetative indices

While field-based measurements are invaluable for quantifying grazingland response to management and climate [8, 51], the information they provide can be limited in terms of both spatial (discrete measurement sites) and temporal (intermittent sampling, 5-yr timeframe) scope. To support improved grazing management at-scale, we need means to extrapolate the information collected from the 3M project field-based measurements to all representative grazinglands in the U.S. and into the future [52].

Satellite remote sensing, in conjunction with modeling, is one way to accomplish this spatiotemporal extrapolation [66, 81, 85]. Satellite earth observation data are collected over a broad range of spectral bands, providing spatially continuous information (after cloud gap-filling) about vegetation type and amount (optical bands), vegetation health/stress and water consumption (thermal-infrared bands), soil moisture (microwave bands), and vegetation canopy structure (lidar, synthetic aperture radar or SAR). We are interested in determining the ability of using multiple remote sensing data products combined with on-ground, field collected data at the hubs to generate relationships that can be used to extrapolate for the producer sites and then to larger landscapes.

Remotely sensed data are used to estimate key variables relevant to understanding feedback between grazing management and ecosystem response, including aboveground standing biomass (e.g., [85]), leaf area index (LAI; e.g., Gao et al., [67], ET (e.g., Anderson et al., [7], grass phenology, C uptake, and vegetation stress. To create these products, we primarily leverage the 30-m multi-satellite Harmonized Landsat and Sentinel-2 (HLS) dataset, with a combined revisit of 2–4 days and archival record covering 2015-present [68]. The following sections describe specific methods for each key variable in more detail.

Biomass. Standing herbaceous biomass refers to the mass of aboveground herbaceous vegetation present at a given time, including both live vegetation (i.e., actively growing) and standing dead vegetation (i.e., senesced, produced in the previous growing season). Knowing the current standing biomass is critical for determining seasonal stocking rates and adjusting grazing rotation frequency [85]. Satellite-based, near-real-time estimates of standing biomass can help land managers quickly adapt to changing conditions, ensuring livestock productivity and rangeland health [85, 85]. Other well-known satellite models estimate biomass production (i.e., plant growth) across large areas [85], but there are limited tools for estimating standing biomass for near-real time uses in adaptive grazing management decision-making. Previous models developed for semiarid rangelands with low biomass (< 1300 kg ha− 1) and short-structured plant communities explained 50–80% of the variation in standing biomass [85]. The goal for the 3M project is to test whether these models can be developed for the diverse rangelands in this study, as well as to assess whether models can be transferred across regions, with particular focus on the more humid regions with higher biomass (> 2500 kg ha− 1) and taller structured plant communities.

To evaluate the transferability of the Kearney et al., [85] model from the semiarid shortgrass steppe to the hubs and producer sites, we are using ground-collected data of standing aboveground herbaceous biomass at two frequencies: (1) every 28 days at the hubs (during the growing season) and (2) annually at the 59 producer sites. Annual producer site biomass sampling occurs throughout the growing season, spanning June-Aug in OK/TX, May/June in MI, and Sept in WY/CO. The ground sampling methods (see SI Sect. 4.8 and Fig. S5 for details) were designed to match the 30 m pixel resolution of available satellite data to improve the relationship between ground and satellite estimates.

Leaf Area Index (LAI) and Phenology. LAI is defined as the ratio of the one-sided leaf areas to the ground surface area (measured in m²/m²). LAI is an important indicator of vegetation density and productivity and is a critical input to land-surface energy, water, and C budget models including the biophysical models used in 3M (Sect. "Biophysical ecosystem models"). Ground-based measurements of LAI are often labor-intensive and have a limited scope due to the finite sampling areas. In recent decades, remote sensing data have been extensively used for LAI estimation [85, 114].

We employ 30 m HLS data alongside the 500-m MODIS LAI products to retrieve LAI for both hubs and producer sites using a reference-based approach [67]. This method allows for consistent LAI estimation across different spatial resolutions over CONUS [85]. Given that this approach has been validated and successfully applied in various projects [7], we adopted it to estimate 30 m resolution LAI and subsequently validated these estimates using field measurements (see SI Sect. 4.8) collected from hubs in MI and OK since 2022 and producer sites since 2023. Ground-based LAI observations are not collected at the WY hub due to low cover conditions.

The HLS-derived LAI products include 30 m LAI on satellite overpass dates, smoothed and gap-filled LAI, and monthly average LAI from 2013 to 2023. Additionally, remote sensing phenological metrics are derived, providing valuable information on vegetation dynamics [69]. These phenological metrics include date-related indicators such as green-up and senescence dates, as well as the minimum, maximum, and time-integrated vegetation index values, providing a metric of biomass accumulation.

Evapotranspiration (ET), Carbon (C) Assimilation, and Vegetation Stress. Remotely sensed ET can provide a wealth of information about the health of an ecosystem – the soil water availability, the amount of transpiring leaves in the plant canopy, and the stress levels of those leaves. Because plant transpiration losses and C uptake are jointly regulated by canopy stomatal conductance, getting a better handle on ET via remote sensing provides a valuable constraint for modeling C fluxes as well [3].

We use a multi-scale ET and C assimilation remote sensing framework based on the Two-Source Energy Balance (TSEB) model, which estimates total ET as well as E and T separately [7]. Key remote sensing inputs are the LAI discussed above, and the land surface temperature retrieved from satellites with thermal infrared imaging sensors, such as Landsat. The model exploits the physical relationship that exists between the evaporative cooling rate and the temperature of the evaporating surface to estimate the stress-reduction of transpiration and C assimilation fluxes from potential (unstressed) rates. We generate maps of daily ET and C flux at 30 m resolution for each hub from 2018 to 2023, validated with the flux data, which we dynamically extend to investigate the response of grazingland water use to climate and management over time. An associated Evaporative Stress Index (ESI), representing seasonal anomalies in ET relative to long-term climatological means, provides an indicator of vegetation stress and has been demonstrated to be well-correlated with agricultural drought impacts and crop productivity in cultivated lands [4–6]. In this experiment, ESI timeseries at 30 m resolution is used to visually and quantitatively assess the evolution of pasture health in response to management and climate drivers.

Socioeconomic metrics

Producer social wellbeing and resilience

There are four main elements under study in this module. Firstly, as we know that for some, agricultural livelihoods can be stressful, while for others, being on the land is what makes them happiest, or some combination of both [85, 114, 114], we aim to identify grazing management strategies that support holistic and synergistic sustainability – i.e., strategies that simultaneously build soil health and support farmer and rancher wellbeing. To this end, we explore how social wellbeing varies across the grazing management continuum. Secondly, because resilient systems are thought to maintain wellbeing during times of change, we expect measures of resiliency to vary alongside wellbeing across the grazing management continuum. By applying resilience thinking, we assess whether these systems support wellbeing and use this insight to guide improvements in agricultural sustainability.

Values held by producers also influence their behavior, such as how they approach grazing management and their perception and approach to resilience, though these linkages are not well studied. Current evidence suggests that producers that embrace “regenerative” principles often exhibit distinct values and perspectives towards agriculture [75, 114]. Therefore, our third goal is to examine values and how living within these values influences producers’ wellbeing [114]. Lastly, we examine how producers’ motivations, behaviors, and decision making towards their grazing management strategies and monitoring relates to the elements of wellbeing, values, and resilience. For example, we assess the roles of principles and philosophies, systems thinking, and social networks play in supporting or blocking producers’ use of various grazing strategies [114]. This final element is intended to facilitate triangulation across our qualitative wellbeing, values, and resilience metrics and quantitative metrics of grazing management and ecological health.

This research was approved by the Michigan State University IRB Board (STUDY00006683), and all participants provided informed consent before participating. We used a mixed-methods approach to study these multi-dimensional elements. Each year from April – June, our team conducts primarily in-person (some virtual), semi structured, conversational interviews [114] with all 59 producers. Most interviews are with one participant, but some also include producers’ spouses, family members, or employees. All interviews are conducted in a place of the interviewee’s choosing (e.g., their homes, elsewhere on their ranch, in their offices, local coffee shop), to ensure they are comfortable with the setting of their interviews [85]. Interviews typically last between one and two hours using an internally developed interview guide to ensure comparability and consistency [114]. The interview focus changes over the course of the experiment: In Y2 (the first year with producers), focus is on operation characteristics, management goals, grazing management practices, monitoring, and producer wellbeing; In Y3, on 3M reflections, monitoring, producer wellbeing, and relational values; in Y4-Y5 the focus expands to resilience at the farm/ranch scale. Interviews are audio recorded, professionally transcribed, coded in MAXQDA 24, and analyzed using an iterative process that links concepts to data through reading and rereading of transcripts, interpretations, and social theory [85, 114]. We take a scientifically descriptive approach to analysis using a coding frame based on the codebook to organize data into predetermined domain summaries and then move into a more interpretive phase of data analysis, deepening our engagement with these data [27, 63].

Based on Vivas and Hodbod [114], every summer we also ask producers to complete an online survey, developed in Qualtrics, covering six main areas: (1) the five elements of wellbeing, assessed using three validated scales: life satisfaction (Satisfaction with Life Scale; [54]), hedonic, eudaimonic, and relational wellbeing, (assessed using the PERMAProfiler; [33]) and physical wellbeing (assessed with the PERMAProfiler in combination with mental health questions and a subjective evaluation of financial conditions; [114]); (2) values, assessed using the Short Schwartz Value Survey (SSVS); (3) systems thinking [114]; (4) sources of learning; (5) financial wellbeing; and, (6) demographics. The survey consists of closed-ended questions providing respondents with pre-determined answer options. We disseminate this survey annually via email to the main (or joint) decision-makers for all 59 producers and keep it open for approximately 8 weeks. After we clean and analyze the data from survey responses, each producer is sent a summary of their results each fall.

We use a participatory action approach [16] for our resilience assessment (Resilience Alliance [83, 114]), integrating group activities at annual producer workshops to collect qualitative and visual data. Regional meetings are held annually and include a two-hour resilience assessment session. In Y2, inspired by Hodbod et al. [83], we used a timeline activity to document historical shocks in grazing systems, followed by a World Café dialogue to envision a resilient and desirable future. Timelining provides a structured, visual tool to help participants interpret and derive insights about system change [39], while World Café fosters resilience through social learning and knowledge exchange [85, 114]. These activities help us explore ‘resilience of what, to what’ within regions [35]. Data include facilitator notes, audio recordings, participant Post-its (timeline), and tablecloth notes, which are transcribed and analyzed using inductive and deductive thematic content analysis [85]. The Y3 regional meeting includes a workshop to focus on perceptions of resilience principles [22], which we expand upon at regional and cross-scale levels in Y4 and Y5 workshops.

Producer profitability and financial wellbeing

To examine the producers’ financial wellbeing, we measure and compare several aspects of their businesses across the grazing management continuum. We collect data from two audiences: the 59 3M producers, and a larger sample of beef producers through a survey instrument designed to broaden the sample size and improve representation of financial wellbeing across the 3M ecoregions. From all 3M producers, we collect information at each scale of the operation including the cow-calf enterprise, whole-farm, and household levels. Financial data collected encompass all sources of income (e.g., marketed livestock, feed sales, and off-farm income) and business expenses (both fixed overhead and variable costs), as well as the business’s assets, liabilities, and owner net worth based on standard financial statements and other supplementary information provided by producers. The comprehensive nature of the financial data allows us to analyze how different grazing management strategies relate to farm financial outcomes at different levels of the business. While these data can reveal useful associations between grazing management and financial indicators, they may not identify the incentives required to change grazing management decisions. To understand this better, we developed a survey instrument aimed at the larger cow-calf producer population to elicit producers’ willingness-to-accept changes to grazing management in response to various incentives and costs.

We use the financial data from 3M producers to construct several financial outputs. First, enterprise budgets of cow-calf costs and returns are calculated from income statement data for each producer. Enterprise budgets report both net returns over variable costs (net operating income) and returns over total costs. Net income measures are expressed on a per-cow basis to allow for comparison across breeding herd scales. Standard ratios can be calculated for each farm and ranch based on data including measures of liquidity, solvency, profitability, debt repayment capacity, and efficiency, providing participants with a holistic picture of their farm or ranch’s financial wellbeing. Existing literature has explored both barriers and motivations to adopting improved grazing management strategies [32, 42, 76] as well as financial outcomes associated with these strategies and how they vary with farm or ranch type ([70, 85]. We compare similar performance outcomes across 3M producers based on observed and self-reported grazing management information.

To supplement these analyses, we use our larger sample survey to conduct a choice experiment. We present producers with hypothetical choices of different grazing management scenarios designed to reflect the continuum within each ecoregion within the 3M project, each with varying costs, benefits, and requirements in the form of a conjoint-based choice experiment [78, 85, 85]. This enables us to estimate the revenue increase or cost reduction required by producers to change their grazing management along relevant axes. This assessment of producer profitability and financial wellbeing is compared directly to metrics of social wellbeing (Sect. "Producer social wellbeing and resilience" ) and ecological health (Sect. "Biophysical metrics") and producers’ position along the grazing management continuum. Results from the choice experiment will inform the policy model (see Sect. "Policy model").

Data scaffolding

To facilitate cross-module collaboration, enhance data exploration efficiency, enable producers to visualize data collected on their operation and ensure the longevity of project findings, we have developed a robust and intentional data scaffolding framework for all biophysical and socioeconomic data collected from the 3M project. Serving as the backbone of the project-wide data repository, this framework addresses the unique challenges of managing voluminous, multimodal geospatial data while ensuring scalability, data discovery, data privacy, and accessibility.

Big data needs. The project generates voluminous data – along with significant diversity in their acquisition rates, formats, geospatial extents, and data preprocessing requirements. Observations are associated with various types of geospatial location identifiers, such as points (specified by latitude and longitude coordinates), geospatial shapefiles (polygons defined by a set of geospatial coordinates), image tiles (specified by a bounding box over a rasterized image, e.g., a tile of satellite images), and political boundaries (e.g., state boundaries). The integrated data collection across multi-modal datasets from participating sampling sites expedites the modeling process through visual data exploration and selective retrieval during the planning and validation stages.

Temporally, observations are linked to unique acquisition intervals, which vary widely – from the 15 min time frequency at which ecosystem flux data are measured and stored to the quinquennial time frequency of measurements from the soil C module. Data from each of the 12 modules require different strategies for aggregating observations to identify patterns aligned with specific sites and grazing practices (e.g., farm/ranch level, triangle level).

Given the characteristics of terabyte-scale data volumes, high velocity, and extreme data variety, our data scaffolding approach naturally aligns with existing big data research. Our approach encompasses three key components, (1) polyglot persistence across distributed data storage systems, (2) spatiotemporal data indexing for universal data querying, and (3) a fault-tolerant and scalable storage design. In this project, we integrate multiple distributed data storage solutions to maximize efficiency by mapping each dataset to the most suitable storage technology based on its characteristics. To facilitate this integration, we developed a middleware that orchestrates queries across various backend storage systems including MySQL [58], Redis [34], MongoDB [41], and HDFS [26]. To keep the dataset manageable, we employ a novel approach that breaks it into smaller, more efficient pieces called shards, each defined by a carefully chosen shard key that optimizes both temporal and spatial queries. Each shard holds data points that share the same key, keeping all related attributes together in one place. Our partitioning strategy is designed to group records that are close in time, making it easy to retrieve observations spanning adjacent days or weeks, which are the kinds of queries researchers often need to run. Also, our system precomputes popular aggregated views, including daily averages and monthly statistical summaries, to improve query performance. To ensure fault tolerance, the data framework performs daily backups and leverages MongoDB’s point-in-time recovery feature to restore data to a specific state in case of corruption or accidental deletion.

Query system for cross-module data storage and use. We provide a unified query evaluation scheme that allows users to issue data queries across project datasets, regardless of the underlying data storage system managing data from a particular module. Users can query data based on specific locations, timestamps, or granularities of aggregation (e.g., farm/ranch-level, state-level, or aggregated trends; see Fig. S6). For spatial indexing, we use R-tree [18] and geohashing [13], both of which provide a hierarchical, nested spatial extent across geospatially labeled data.

Visualization. The system provides interactive visualization tools that allow users to explore and perform visual analytics on data. These tools are designed to handle the complexity of multimodal geospatial data while offering intuitive and customizable interfaces. The visualization features are accessible through a web browser-based user interface, which is integrated with the backend data servers. Our visual analytic engine includes features such as spatial mapping, temporal trend analysis, and comparison across multiple datasets, thereby enabling researchers to analyze relationships across scales. Whether examining a single location or multiple locations in parallel, users can explore these attributes in a structured and intuitive manner.

Differential data access for participants. To ensure data privacy for our producers, the system provides differential data access based on user roles. Our role-based access control ensures that participants can access only their own datasets along with aggregated data points at the project hub level. Aggregated datasets are crucial for participants to compare their local observations within the context of other farms and ranches operating in similar regions and under similar management practices. This access control is enforced using encryption and secure authentication protocols [85]. Full access to the dataset is granted only to approved research personnel. For greater detail on the data scaffolding methodologies see SI Sect. 4.9.

Modeling and broader impacts

In addition to analyzing direct relationships between socioeconomic and ecological metrics and management practices through traditional statistical approaches, 3M aims to expand our scope and applicability through a multipronged modeling approach. The modeling methods described below use the data in various capacities to enhance existing functionality, and calibrate and validate both the process-based ecosystem biogeochemical model MEMS 3, [114, 114, 114] and the water cycling models (HYDRUS-1D, [114]; and SWAT+, see [21]) to represent ecological responses to grazing management strategies, thereby enabling upscaling and scenario forecasting. All measured and modeled products are framed in a social-ecological system (SES) context [85] to build model products that integrate all modules within 3M (Fig. 4, Sect. "Integrated modeling"). Our ultimate goal is to make our discoveries accessible to stakeholders beyond the participants and academics involved directly in the 3M project by developing decision-making support tools targeted towards producers and land managers, grazingland scientists, and policy makers in each region [59].

Biophysical ecosystem models

Ecosystem models provide a powerful tool for understanding and predicting the complex interactions between climate, soil, vegetation, and management practices in grazing systems. While field experiments offer valuable empirical data, they are often limited in spatial and temporal scope, making it challenging to assess long-term and large-scale impacts. By integrating field measurements with ecosystem models, we can extend our findings beyond the experimental sites and evaluate alternative grazing strategies under varying environmental conditions [59].

Soil C modeling

The MEMS model [114] is a process-based ecosystem model that represents ecosystem C and N cycling. It features simulating measurable SOM pools as well as plant growth, soil temperature, and soil water movement, and represents C and N fluxes among the atmosphere, plants, and a multi-layered soil structure. In its 2.34 version, the MEMS model also simulates perennial plant growth, animal consumption of plant biomass and supplemental feed, along with the return of feces and urine to the soil [114].

To mechanistically represent different grazing management strategies, 3M data and knowledge were leveraged to further develop MEMS into a two-dimensional version (MEMS 3.0; [114]) that divides the landscape into spatial cells. At each daily time step, animals are distributed across these cells based on the specified management approach. This framework allows the model to simulate both conventional grazing systems, such as continuous grazing and low-frequency rotational grazing, as well as more adaptive practices that employ rotations across multiple paddocks.

To the best of our knowledge, existing process-based ecosystem models assume a uniform spatial distribution of animal activity, regardless of important influencers such as pasture size or stocking density. However, in reality—particularly under continuous grazing and low-frequency rotational grazing—animals tend to distribute themselves unevenly. This leads to certain areas being heavily grazed while others remain largely untouched or lightly grazed [14, 114]. Such uneven distribution affects forage utilization, influences plant growth dynamics, and ultimately impacts SOM accumulation and nutrient cycling [114]. By explicitly representing animal movement and its effects on vegetation and soil processes, our model provides a more realistic representation of grazing systems, enabling better assessments of their long-term ecological and productive outcomes.

Hydrological modeling

Hydrological models provide a numerical solution of the water balance at discrete temporal and spatial scales. Incoming (precipitation) and outgoing (potential ET) water from the system is specified using meteorological data, and users can specify other physical characteristics that determine the storage and movement of water through the soil profile and across the land surface (e.g., texture, BD, soil hydraulic conductivity, rooting depth, and topography). Hydrological models are often used in ecosystem studies to assess the effect of hypothetical changes from active (i.e., management) and passive (i.e., climate shifts) sources. Several related examples include: HYDRUS-1D to test the impact of woody plant encroachment on water cycling in grasslands [1, 71]; and SWAT to assess the impact of grazing management on nutrient runoff and water quality [85]. In 3M we represent every water monitoring triangle with a hydrological model to quantify fluxes that are not measured directly (surface runoff and recharge), and to predict how water cycling may respond to management-induced changes to the soil and forage. We also use these models to quantify the influence of heterogenous site characteristics that may mediate grazing impacts on soil moisture such as soil texture, vegetation, and topography, and at the MI hub, perched groundwater. These models were selected for their complementary strengths. HYDRUS-1D provides detailed simulations of vertical water movement and nutrient cycling through the soil profile, while SWAT + can additionally estimate surface runoff, soil erosion, and nutrient losses across the entire pasture. While previous versions of the SWAT model functioned at watershed scales, SWAT+ allows for a spatially explicit representation of water movement at field scales [36].

Both the 1 and 2-D hydrological fluxes in these models are driven by plot-level climate observations of daily precipitation, temperature, humidity and solar radiation from the flux module. Soil hydraulic properties are initialized using co-located depth-varying soil texture, BD, and SOM data from the soil module and the ROSETTA database [114], while vegetation parameters (LAI, rooting depth) that control the transpiration fraction are estimated from observations from the remote sensing and soil modules, respectively. Daily observations of VWC and ET, from the water and flux modules are used to validate the modeled water fluxes. Using the Phydrus package [44] and a global optimization algorithm in the SPOTpy package [85], the soil hydraulic properties describing the soil water retention function [114] are iteratively updated to minimize the error between modeled and estimated daily VWC in Python (version 3.11). These calibrated soil hydraulic parameters are then used to parameterize field-scale SWAT+ models of each hub treatment plot and producer site. These models quantify how potential changes to forage (i.e., species, root depth, LAI) and soil properties (i.e., BD, infiltration rate, SOM) through management decisions could alter water cycling. Additionally, through iterative removal of observation data in exchange for coarser public domain data, notably SSURGO [114] in place of soil team observations, and uncalibrated vs. calibrated soil hydraulic parameters, we assess the most important metrics for accurate predictions of water fluxes at scale in each region. This assessment is also relevant for calculating confidence intervals of models extending onto grazinglands beyond the study sites for the producer decision-support tool (Sect. "Producer decision-support tool").

Integrated modeling

Integrated models use insights from multiple disciplines to capture the often-complex interactions between human choices and natural systems [85]. Grazing systems have previously been represented with integrated models to examine relationships between livestock productivity and ecosystem services such as water provisioning [79] and SOC accrual [85]. Integrating models of soil, water, and ecosystems with economic behavior allows us to characterize field level heterogeneity in the cost of building SOC that would be missed when using average conditions at higher spatial aggregations [114]. To elucidate those trade-offs, integrated modeling work in 3M includes development of an exploratory policy model (Sect. "Policy model") that parameterizes management inputs to optimize profits and ecosystem services and assesses how policy can balance these priorities. Following a similar workflow, we also have created a producer feedback tool (Sect. "Producer decision-support tool") that allows users to compare the impacts of management choices in their region on short and long-term revenues, and ecosystem services. For a detailed schematic of the inputs, processes, and outputs of this integrated model see Fig. S7.

Policy model

The 3M policy model combines a machine learning surrogate trained on MEMS 3.0 (Sect. "Soil C modeling") simulations with economic functions for net revenue to explore the impacts of policy, market, and climate scenarios on optimal grazing management strategies. Operating over a 30-year horizon, the model identifies management strategies that maximize the net present value of private profits and/or environmental benefits by adjusting five key decision variables: stocking rate, number of paddocks, livestock movement rules (i.e., forage utilization targets), and grazing season start and end dates. The model consists of four components: (1) an economic model of the 30-year net revenue function that accounts for beef sales revenue and policy or program payments, minus operating costs; (2) an ML surrogate that rapidly predicts biogeochemical dynamics; (3) an animal growth function that translates forage consumption and quality into calf weight gains; and (4) an optimization routine that maximizes present value profits.

Optimization requires evaluating millions of management scenarios across different market, climate, and soil conditions—computationally infeasible with direct MEMS simulations. To address this challenge, we train a machine learning surrogate model on MEMS outputs using a sequential state transition approach. At each daily time step, the surrogate predicts how key state variables—forage mass, crude protein content, and SOC—transition from day t to day t + 1 given current state, management actions (grazing occurrence and intensity), and environmental conditions (day of year and weather). This single-paddock surrogate enables the economic model to rapidly simulate multi-paddock rotational grazing scenarios while preserving the biogeochemical dynamics represented in MEMS. Surrogate outputs are passed to the animal growth model which is based on National Research Council (NRC) nutritional models and parameterized with data from the 3M hub sites and the larger sample beef producer survey (see Sect. 4.3.2). The net revenue function accounts for calf sales, ecosystem service payments, and costs including supplemental feed, fencing infrastructure, and labor. The optimization is conducted separately for each combination of weather, climate, soil type, market, and policy scenario to characterize how optimal management varies across conditions.

This recursive linkage between biogeochemical dynamics and economic outcomes captures the feedback between producer choices and changes in the physical environment, parameterized to the context-specific variables (weather, climate, soil type, markets, policy) of each region. With this design, we quantify both the costs (such as added fencing, labor, and supplemental feed) and benefits (such as improved forage quality, increased SOC stocks, and greater flexibility from extended grazing seasons) of alternative management strategies. The model can compare the effectiveness of alternative policy designs (e.g., price markups vs. payment for practices vs. payment for outcomes) and demonstrates both the private and external benefits of improved grazingland health. For details on the full model structure and component functions, see SI Sect. 4.10.

Producer decision-support tool

Decision support systems (DSS) or tools, are computer-based systems designed to turn complex datasets and scenarios into a sequence of plausible actions with estimated costs/benefits and uncertainty [114]. The 3M producer feedback tool is a web-based platform available for any grazingland stakeholder to engage with the modeling and data products developed by the project. This tool enables users to compare how different grazing management scenarios in their ecoregion can propagate social, economic, and ecological changes over time, and ensures that the results of the project are directly available and applicable to the stakeholders. The tool is an educational interface, whose primary purpose is to operationalize the scientific findings from a diversity of grazing operations within their ecoregion to enable them to make more informed grazing decisions for their desired socioeconomic and ecosystem outcomes. The tool is neither a prescription for how producers should manage their operations, nor is it a promise of what will happen should they choose to alter their management.

The suite of outputs provided by the tool are informed by producer priorities, gathered during the Y3 producer meeting in a process of participatory DSS development designed to maximize its utility and adoption by the grazing land management community [85]. Through an iterative process spanning Y3-5 of the project, producers are asked to participate in a review and revision process to hone the feedback tool interface and outputs. The feedback tool is built with a look-up table approach for speed, based on exhaustive simulations encompassing a range of the studied practices along the grazing management continuum, and the diverse soil and climate regimes spanning the hubs and producer sites (Fig. 2). Modeled projections of SOC, forage availability, and water cycling (i.e., ET, runoff, recharge) are based on the mechanistic understanding of ecosystem response to grazing management developed in the biophysical ecosystem model advancements (Sect. "Biophysical ecosystem models"). Users may compare their current management scenario side by side to hypothetical management scenarios effected through levers on the timing, intensity, frequency, and duration [114] of grazing to assess how those changes may result in ecosystem benefits (or losses) over time. They also have the option to select priorities (e.g., SOC accrual, forage utilization, revenue) and view what management approach would theoretically maximize the desired outcome. These projected outcomes may either be quantitative including confidence intervals, or qualitative (e.g., a case study summary).

Producer education and engagement

Producers acquire knowledge and make decisions based on a variety of sources, including experiential learning, peer networks, and trusted organizations. Research underscores the critical role of farmer-to-farmer knowledge exchange in shaping producer decision-making, particularly when adopting new or unfamiliar practices [2, 53, 72, 114]. Recognizing these dynamics, the 3M project has prioritized producer-centered educational approaches to enhance engagement and ensure that research outcomes are both relevant and actionable for those managing working lands. These efforts complement our broader commitment to producer involvement, including data collection on working farms and ranches, the development of practical outputs beyond biophysical metrics, and the creation of modeling and decision-making tools tailored to their needs.

First, we coordinate annual, regional producer meetings to provide an avenue for knowledge transfer, training, peer-to-peer learning and networking, as well as to meet project goals, such as to distribute relevant project data to producers about their farm/ranch. Each year, the hub coordinators work with the Texas A&M Center for Grazinglands and Ranch Management staff to coordinate these regional producer meetings. The meetings are often held in locations near the regional hubs. In this way, the hubs not only provide sites for intensive measurements, but they also provide connections among the producers in each of the three ecoregions. The social wellbeing module uses these meetings as a data gathering opportunity (Sect. "Producer social wellbeing and resilience" ), but members of all modules are encouraged to join, providing rich opportunities for producers, scientists, coordinators, and others to engage within and across groups. In Y5 of the project, all producers and modules gather in one setting in lieu of the separate regional meetings. This enables us to assess the challenges and successes of the project as one. Producers are given stipends to help offset travel expenses for meeting attendance.

Second, the Plant Communities and Visual Indicators module provides both voluntary, free education for producers interested in self-monitoring their properties and individualized reports with their EHI (Ecological Health Index) scores. The education component consists of asynchronous, self-paced online trainings covering the importance of landscape monitoring, explanations of each visual indicator, and guidance on implementing the protocol to assess all 15 indicators (see Table S2). A key factor in our decision to use the EOV protocol for tracking plant communities and visual indicators is its ability to empower producers to independently monitor their land, particularly focusing on fast-changing and easily observed environmental indicators. These trainings, launched in Y2 of the project, remain continuously available so producers can revisit topics as needed. Additionally, annual producer reports provide participants with their EHI scores, a breakdown of how these scores are calculated and assessed, comparisons to the regional potential, and a guide for interpreting their results.

Lastly, with project support, several team members provide ad-hoc producer engagement and education opportunities. Leaders of several biophysical modules, in collaboration with hub coordinators, offer an annual virtual “office hours” to facilitate direct engagement between producers and researchers. Inspired by North Dakota State University Cooperative Extension’s Café Talks [114], these sessions offer informal, two-hour windows where a representative from each module is available online to answer project-related questions and discuss relevant topics with producers. According to producer feedback surveys, these office hours are not only well-attended but highly valued by participants. Additionally, many producers have expressed interest in emerging C markets and the potential use of 3M data for generating C credits. In response, our team has been able to leverage our expertise to provide extensive cost-free consultation to interested ranchers.

These engagements are intentionally structured as reciprocal learning spaces. Producer knowledge shared through field visits, meetings, and office hours has informed site interpretation and project decisions, while feedback gathered through surveys and discussion has guided refinements to protocols and communication products.

Conclusions

The 3M project represents a groundbreaking integration of interdisciplinary methods to monitor and model grazingland systems at an unprecedented scale, generating high-resolution, longitudinal data on the complex feedback between grazing management, ecosystem outcomes such as CO2 flux, productivity, and SOC and water dynamics, and socioeconomic outcomes such as producer wellbeing and profitability. By integrating detailed biophysical, economic, and social data collected from both experimental hubs and 59 producer sites, the 3M project fills critical gaps in our understanding of how grazing decisions shape—and are shaped by—ecological and socioeconomic systems. These coordinated efforts not only support the development of robust models and scalable indicators but also enable the creation of practical decision-support tools that directly inform producers while enhancing scientific understanding of grazingland resilience. The project’s modeling approach reflects this complexity, emphasizing feedback loops that connect ecological indicators with producer profitability, resilience, and decision-making. With these research outputs, we aim to help land managers identify grazing strategies that optimize SOC accrual, forage productivity, water use efficiency, and economic sustainability—goals that are often interconnected but have historically been studied in isolation. By capturing real-world management decisions alongside controlled experimental comparisons, the project offers a rare balance between scientific rigor and practical relevance. Together, these innovations offer a new standard for how to conduct rigorous, actionable research on working lands. While no single study can encompass the full diversity of global grazinglands, this work provides a flexible and transferable framework that can be adopted and extended to additional grazingland types and ecoregions (e.g., hotter, arid grazinglands), enabling broader coordination and comparability in future grazingland research.

However, this approach is not without challenges (SI Sect. 5). Deploying and coordinating a multi-institutional, interdisciplinary team across multiple regions with varied goals and management styles requires ongoing negotiation of priorities and methods. Additionally, the project confronts persistent analytical and operational challenges, such as integrating diverse datasets, managing field logistics at scale, data co-production with producers, and ensuring timely progress on research deliverables. Still, these obstacles underscore the necessity of large, collaborative efforts to produce actionable insights for inherently complex working landscapes.

We propose that the key to mitigating these challenges lies in building a team with a unified vision, with motives and values that are aligned towards a common goal. In 3M, this focus and team-thinking aided the intentional experimental design that enables us to develop novel results stemming from cross-module synergies. Dedicated and consistent leadership from all team members spanning administrative and research technician roles provides continuity in producer-partner relations and field research that keep the project on target. This consistency, careful planning, and dedicated organizational infrastructure is critical for projects at any scale, but particularly for those like 3M with dozens of team members and multiple nested objectives. Ultimately, the 3M project offers a new framework for scalable, interdisciplinary research in both grazinglands and other managed ecosystems—one that centers producers, advances monitoring methods, and creates pathways for sustainable land stewardship.
