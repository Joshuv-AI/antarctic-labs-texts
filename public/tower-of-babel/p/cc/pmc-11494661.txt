Polarity-Driven Thiyl Radical-Catalyzed Aerobic Debenzylation of Ethers and Amines
Authors: Samuel
W. Lardy, Victoria L. Lerda, Valerie A. Schmidt
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We report the use of a strongly electrophilic thiyl radical derived from commercially available pentafluorothiophenol as a demonstration of highly chemoselective H atom abstraction from electron-rich and relatively weak benzylic C–H bonds adjacent to the O and N atoms. This approach enables the selective oxidative removal of benzyl and p-methoxybenzyl groups from amines and ethers under ambient aerobic conditions.

Introduction

Many free radical species undergo nonselective H atom abstractions and consequently are implicated in cellular and DNA damage pathways.1 However, an increased number of reports that demonstrate the ability of tailored heteroatom-centered radicals to perform selective atom abstractions highlight the potential of this activation strategy for chemical synthesis.2 Thiols and their corresponding thiyl radicals are ubiquitous throughout radical chemistry,3 serving as H atom donors due to their relatively weak S–H bonds (80–90 kcal mol–1)4 and rapid rates of H atom transfer (HAT ∼ 105–108 M–1 s–1),3 making them excellent candidates for the development of selective H atom abstraction agents.

The electrophilic character of thiyl radicals favors abstraction of H atoms from silanes (Si–H),5 boranes (B–H),6 aldehydes (OC–H),7 and C–H bonds adjacent to heteroatoms.8 Well-documented polarity effects in radical-mediated atom abstractions are most evident in the relative rates of H atom transfer to thiyl radicals from different H atom donors.9 Pryor and co-workers observed that the cyclohexanethiyl radical abstracted the more electron-rich secondary benzylic H atoms from benzyl methyl ether 20 times faster than from ethylbenzene (Scheme 1a).10 MacMillan and co-workers used the ethyl-2-mercatopriopionate thiyl radical to abstract an electron-rich primary methyl H atom from methanol overcoming an ∼9 kcal mol–1 uphill barrier to achieve arene alkylations (Scheme 1b).11 Dilman and co-workers recently reported a radical-mediated thiolation of electronically unactivated alkanes.12 Generated by purple-light-mediated homolytic disulfide bond cleavage, the tetrafluoropyridinyl thiyl radical performed an estimated 13 kcal mol–1 uphill H atom abstraction from alkane substrates, after which the C-centered radical was rapidly trapped by a second equivalent of the disulfide reagent.

Polarity Effects of Thiyl Radical HAT

The strategic use of protecting groups is a common, and sometimes unavoidable, process in the construction of complex molecules,13 with benzyl (Bn) and p-methoxybenzyl (PMB) being favorable for hydroxyl and amino groups due to the relative ease of installation and removal. However, common methods for the removal of Bn and PMB groups using heterogeneous palladium hydrogenolysis14 and stoichiometric oxidative cleavage,15 respectively, offer sustainable chemistry development opportunities that would obviate the use of precious transition metals, hazardous hydrogen gas, or stoichiometric waste production.16 HAT-mediated radical debenzylation strategies offer some sustainability benefits and potential functional group compatibility advantages but often require superstoichiometric HAT reagent sources such as N-bromosuccinimide (NBS)17 or an Oxone/KBr couple,18 are limited to electron-deficient amino functionalities,17,18 and, in the case of O-benzyl ethers, may result in oxidation to the ketone following debenzylation.18 Herein, we demonstrate the impact of thiyl radical HAT polarity effects through the lens of chemoselective amine and ether debenzylation using atmospheric O2 as the terminal oxidant.19

Results and Discussion

Bertrand and co-workers previously observed the formation of an E/Z mixture of 1-phenyl-N-(1-phenylethyl)ethan-1-imine from treatment of 1-phenylethan-1-amine with aryl thiols20 proposed to have occurred via benzylic H atom abstraction followed by oxidation and primary amine condensation. We observed substantial production of p-anisaldehyde and N-methylaniline when N-(p-methoxybenzyl)-N-methylaniline was treated with 10 mol % tert-butyl hyponitrite and 10 mol % methyl thioglycolate in a 3:1 mixture of tert-butyl acetate and water at 60 °C. A subsequent experiment using N-PMB aniline (1a), 5 mol % methyl thioglycolate, and 5 mol % 2,2′-azobis(2-methylpropionitrile) (AIBN) heated to 80 °C in chlorobenzene for 1.5 h resulted in only 29% consumption of 1a and a 11% combined yield of aniline 1b and imine 1c (Table 1, entry 1). While the PMB group remained attached in 1c, we hypothesized that its formation was the result of either (a) direct formation under the reaction conditions or (b) in situ condensation of liberated aniline and p-anisaldehyde.

The use of thiophenol in place of methyl thioglycolate failed to improve the conversion of 1a (entry 2). These low conversions were initially attributed to aerobic decomposition of thiophenol at higher temperatures;21,22 however, the use of pentafluorothiophenol (F5C6SH) achieved 68% conversion and a total yield of 41% (entry 3).23 Extending the reaction time to 3 h and using 10 mol % F5C6SH resulted in 94% conversion and a combined yield of 91% of 1b and 1c (17:74). Including an acidic workup effectively funneled all material to aniline 1b HCl salt in 95% yield (entry 4).24 Acetonitrile may be used as a more favorable solvent, although despite the full conversion of 1a observed, the overall decreased yield of 1b following an acidic workup is largely attributed to solubility and miscibility challenges of MeCN (entry 5). The individual exclusion of AIBN (entry 6), thiol (entry 7), or O2 (entry 8) resulted in only a trace of debenzylation. Combined, these results support debenzylation via a radical-mediated rather than acid-mediated pathway25 and demonstrate the necessity of thiol and air.

We turned to density functional theory to investigate the observed impact of the identity of the thiol on the debenzylation efficiency. We considered the S–H bond dissociation enthalpy (BDE)26 and the electron affinity (EA)27 of the corresponding thiyl radicals of the three thiols investigated in Table 1 (Scheme 2a). These calculations indicated that F5C6SH had both the lowest S–H BDE (79.4 kcal mol–1) and the greatest, most exothermic electron affinity (EA = 68.5 kcal mol–1). While the inclusion of electron-withdrawing groups often increases the BDE, the presence of fluorine at the ortho and para positions of aryl thiols was demonstrated to decrease the S–H BDE, accounting for the ∼4 kcal mol–1 difference between thiophenol and F5C6SH.28 The electronegative nature of fluorine is likely responsible for the increased electron affinity of the pentafluorophenylthiyl radical relative to either methyl thioglycolate or thiophenol.29 To further illustrate the importance of substrate electronics on the observed reactivity, we carried out side-by-side separate flask experiments using the benzyl and p-trifluoromethylbenzyl congeners of 1a and observed significantly decreased conversion of these two substrates compared to that of the more electron-rich 1a (see the Supporting Information for details).

This collective data led to the proposed mechanism for thiol-catalyzed aerobic debenzylation shown in Scheme 2b. Radical initiation generates an electrophilic, thiyl radical from F5C6SH, which then preferentially abstracts a relatively weak (∼85 kcal mol–1),30 and electron-rich, benzylic H atom from the substrate.31 Notably, the most efficient thiyl radical precursor investigated contains a weak S–H bond, making the proposed benzylic H atom abstraction endothermic (ΔH ∼ 4 kcal mol–1). Molecular oxygen may then either perform a single-electron oxidation32 or intercept the benzylic α-amino C-centered radical33 to generate a benzylic carbocation or peroxyl radical intermediate, respectively. Both pathways are kinetically and thermodynamically feasible and lead to a common benzylic peroxy radical intermediate.34 Thiyl radical regenerating HAT produces a benzylic hydroperoxide from which elimination of the hydrogen peroxide anion, followed by hydrolysis, furnishes the product.

To demonstrate the selectivity and limitations of this thiyl radical HAT, we surveyed a variety of aryl amines (Scheme 3). Primarily, p-methoxybenzyl anilines were easily deprotected and generally found to be the most efficient substrates (2b and 3b) without the disruption of potentially sensitive functionality such as ketones (4b), bis-aryl ethers (5b), nitriles (6b), and carboxylic acids (16b), primary silyl ethers (7b), acetals (8b), and an acid labile Boc group (12b35). Substrates containing multiple electron-rich C–H bonds showed excellent selectivity for the abstraction of the weakest, electron-rich C–H group as evidenced by subsequent oxidative removal of the PMB group (7b–10b, 15b, and 17b). Debenzylation was successful to reveal sulfadiazine (13b) and sulfamethoxazole (14b), two potent antibacterial agents, as well as the cholic acid derivative (19b), an essential component of bile acid synthesis. While increasing the substitution at nitrogen decreased the reaction efficiency (15b–17b) and n-butylamine (18b) was obtained in lower yield, debenzylation of N atoms also bearing electron-withdrawing groups such as amides, carbamates, and sulfonamides was unsuccessful, resulting in only recovery of starting materials. The α-amino C–H bonds present in benzyl amides have a significantly higher BDE36 and decreased nucleophilicity compared to those of their amine analogues, allowing for thiyl radical abstraction to discriminate between multiple, otherwise similar benzylic C–H bonds.

Alcohols were similarly obtained from their PMB-derived ethers [20b–25b (Scheme 4)]. Notably, excellent selectivity was observed for the oxidative cleavage of these benzyl groups, even in the presence of additional α-amino and/or α-oxy C–H bonds. Functionalized compounds, including 2-(5-methyl-2-phenyloxazol-4-yl)ethanol (21b), an N-benzoyl methyl ester serine derivative (22b), stigmasterol (23b), and diosgenin (24b), were all isolated from their O-PMB derivatives in good yields despite the known propensity for thiyl radicals to participate in alkene additions.37 Compound 25b was isolated from its O-PMB congener, leaving the N-PMB amide intact, highlighting the chemoselectivity of this aerobic, thiol-catalyzed approach for the abstraction of the most electron-rich and weakest C–H bond present.38

Benzylated phenols and esters were not effectively cleaved. The electron-withdrawing properties of the ester acyl group are likely responsible for the disfavored H atom abstraction. During our attempts to remove a PMB group to form 6-hydroxycoumarin, only a trace of p-anisaldehyde was produced along with the expected phenolic product, suggesting that this small amount of phenol formed was sufficient to shunt reactivity and prevent additional conversion of the starting material.39

Further investigations revealed that these aerobic conditions allowed for the quantitative oxidation of p-methoxy benzyl alcohol to p-anisaldehyde and compound 26a to aromatic amine 26b. Prior reports of this latter transformation required increased temperatures (140 °C)40 or strong oxidants (KMnO4) to proceed.41

Reported herein is a demonstration of the polarity effects of thiyl radicals toward the chemoselective H atom abstraction of benzylic H atoms adjacent to N and O groups. This allowed for mild, radical-mediated removal of benzyl and p-methoxybenzyl groups from amines and ethers using commercially available pentafluorothiophenol as a catalytic HAT reagent. Combined experimental and computational mechanistic studies support a selective benzylic H atom abstraction pathway. This process uses ambient air as the terminal oxidant and the only stoichiometric reagent used. Whereas thiols are most typically viewed as H atom donors in radical processes, this approach hinges on the ability of a highly electrophilic thiyl radical to abstract electron-rich, benzylic H atoms based on a combination of electronic and thermodynamic factors.42
