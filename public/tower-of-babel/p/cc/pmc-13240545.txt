Long QT Syndrome Type 5 With Coexisting KCNE1 and RYR2 Variants: A Diagnostic Ambiguity
Authors: Mohammad Hossein Nikoo, Hossein Fatemian, Amir Hossein Hassani, Mehrdad Rabiee Rad, Sara Mostafavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLong QT syndrome (LQTS) predisposes to syncope and sudden cardiac death. Type 5 LQTS, linked to KCNE1 variants, is rare. A teenage female presented with recurrent syncope. ECG showed QTc 485 ms. Genetic testing identified KCNE1 and RYR2 variants. Beta‐blockers and ICD prevented events. The case highlights diagnostic and genotype–phenotype challenges.Keywords: KCNE1, long QT syndrome, RYR2, syncope

Background

Long QT syndrome (LQTS) is a group of cardiac electrophysiological disorders characterized by a prolonged QT interval and abnormal T‐wave morphology on the electrocardiogram (ECG), resulting from mutations in ion channel genes that impair ventricular repolarization. Congenital LQTS is estimated to occur in approximately 1 in 2000 to 1 in 2500 individuals within the general population [1, 2]. The condition predisposes affected individuals to syncope and sudden cardiac death, primarily due to torsades de pointes [3, 4].

LQTS demonstrates substantial genetic heterogeneity, with pathogenic variants identified in at least 17 distinct genes [5]. Approximately 80%–90% of genotype‐positive LQTS cases belong to types 1–3, including LQTS type 1 caused by KCNQ1 loss‐of‐function, LQTS type 2 due to KCNH2 loss‐of‐function, and LQTS type 3 associated with SCN5A gain‐of‐function mutations [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Long QT syndrome type 5 (LQT5) constitutes a rare form of QT prolongation, accounting for only 2% of all LQTS cases [10].

The rarity of LQT5 has constrained knowledge regarding its clinical manifestations, genetic diversity, and optimal therapeutic strategies, posing challenges for accurate diagnosis and effective management [11]. In this context, we describe a case of LQT5 involving Potassium Voltage‐Gated Channel Subfamily E Regulatory Subunit 1 (KCNE1) and Ryanodine Receptor 2 (RYR2) variants, presenting with recurrent syncope initially misattributed to epilepsy. This rarity complicates recognition, especially when clinical findings mimic neurological disorders, as in the present case.

Case Presentation

A female in her late teens with no previous medical history was referred to the cardiology clinic due to recurrent syncope. The first syncopal episode occurred one year prior while the patient was resting and studying, without exposure to physical exertion, emotional stress, or sudden auditory stimuli. Recovery was spontaneous within minutes. She experienced no preceding symptoms such as palpitations, visual disturbances, nausea, diaphoresis, or lightheadedness. There were no post‐event features suggestive of seizure, including confusion, prolonged fatigue, or headache. Witnesses reported no abnormal movements, tongue biting, or urinary incontinence. A second similar episode occurred two months later while she was watching television, again at rest and without identifiable triggers. Each episode lasted approximately two minutes, followed by rapid and complete recovery. The family history was negative for syncope, sudden cardiac death, or any congenital or acquired cardiac disease.

Physical Examination

The patient appeared well and in no acute distress. Vital signs were stable: heart rate, 88 beats per minute (bpm); blood pressure, 115/78 mm‐Hg; respiratory rate, 18 breaths per minute, with no abnormal orthostatic changes. The cardiovascular examination revealed normal heart sounds, without murmurs, gallops, or rubs. Pulmonary examination was unremarkable, and no cyanosis, clubbing, or edema was present.

Electrocardiography and Cardiac Evaluation

The resting 12‐lead ECG showed sinus tachycardia at 109 bpm with wide‐based T waves. The QT interval was measured in lead V5 from the onset of the QRS complex to the end of the T wave, defined as the point where the T wave returned to the isoelectric baseline. Initially, QTc was calculated manually using Bazett's formula (QTc = QT ÷ √RR), yielding 485 milliseconds (ms), consistent with a prolonged QT interval (Figure 1). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed normal biventricular size, normal wall motion, and preserved ejection fraction. The only abnormality was a mildly elevated pulmonary artery pressure of 25 mmHg. Cardiac Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) demonstrated no evidence of regional wall motion abnormalities, myocardial fibrosis, inflammation, arrhythmogenic right ventricular dysplasia, or any structural cardiomyopathy.

Neurological Evaluation

Electroencephalogram (EEG) revealed generalized spike‐and‐wave discharges, suggesting idiopathic generalized epilepsy with photosensitivity. Tilt‐table testing was negative. Although EEG findings were suggestive of idiopathic generalized epilepsy, a definitive diagnosis was not established, and antiseizure therapy was not initiated, given the stronger clinical suspicion for a cardiogenic cause of syncope.

Exercise and Ambulatory Monitoring

Treadmill stress testing revealed no ischemic changes. Because Bazett's formula can overcorrect QTc at elevated heart rates, QTc during exercise was calculated using the Framingham formula. QTc = QT + 0.154 × (1 – RR). QTc at the maximum heart rate (184 bpm, 91% of predicted maximum) was 564 ms. Seventy‐two‐hour ambulatory Holter monitoring demonstrated predominantly sinus rhythm, with no significant pauses, no premature ventricular contractions (PVCs), and only rare premature atrial contractions (PACs). No sustained or nonsustained tachyarrhythmias were detected. QTc, calculated automatically and reviewed manually, varied with heart rate: 468 ms at the maximum heart rate (137 bpm), 436 ms at the average heart rate, and 388 ms at the minimum heart rate (54 bpm) (Figure 2). No malignant arrhythmias or significant conduction abnormalities were observed. For clinical interpretation and risk stratification, QTc values derived using Bazett's formula at rest were primarily considered, while Framingham correction was applied during exercise testing to avoid overestimation at higher heart rates. QT prolongation remained evident across different correction methods.

Genetic Analysis and Family Evaluation

Whole‐exome sequencing identified a heterozygous KCNE1 variant (c.163G>A), classified as of uncertain significance and not previously reported in LQT5, along with a RYR2 variant (c.4378G>C). Family testing via Sanger sequencing showed the father and brother were heterozygous for KCNE1, while the mother carried the RYR2 variant. ECG tracing demonstrated QT prolongation in the symptomatic brother. The patient's genotype, QTc intervals, and clinical features, as well as those of their immediate family members, are summarized in Table 1. Based on clinical, ECG, and genetic findings, the patient was considered to have a suspected LQTS phenotype with a possible association with a KCNE1 variant of uncertain significance. This variant has not been previously reported in association with LQT5, and no functional studies are currently available to support its pathogenicity. Although segregation with QT prolongation was observed in the patient's brother, the presence of the same variant in the father without a clear QT phenotype limits the strength of this association. According to ACMG/AMP variant interpretation principles, the current evidence is insufficient to establish a causal relationship between this variant and the observed phenotype.

She was managed with beta‐blocker therapy and remained free of cardiovascular events during a one‐year follow‐up. During the three‐year follow‐up, both she and her brother underwent implantation of an Implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator (ICD). The decision to proceed with ICD implantation during follow‐up was individualized and based on a combination of factors, including recurrent syncope, persistent QT prolongation, genetic findings of uncertain significance, and shared decision‐making with the patient and her family. Since then, they have remained free of life‐threatening arrhythmic events, and no inappropriate shocks or device‐related complications have been reported.

Discussion and Conclusion

This case illustrates the diagnostic complexity in patients with recurrent syncope, particularly when neurological and cardiac findings overlap. Cardiac etiology was supported by the patient's history, characteristic ECG changes, and the presence of KCNE1 and RYR2 mutations, despite EEG findings suggesting idiopathic generalized epilepsy. Such overlapping phenotypes underscore the importance of integrating clinical, electrophysiological, and genetic data to guide accurate diagnosis and management.

In our patient, a heterozygous KCNE1 variant (c.163G>A) was identified, which has not been previously reported as pathogenic in LQT5. LQTS has both congenital and acquired forms [12]. Congenital LQTS is a cardiac channelopathy caused by delayed ventricular repolarization, resulting from either prolonged inward sodium or calcium currents or diminished outward potassium currents (I_Ks, I_Kr, I_K1), which prolong the action potential in cardiac myocytes [13, 14]. LQT5 is caused by loss‐of‐function mutations in the KCNE1 gene, which encodes the minK β‐subunit of voltage‐gated potassium channels. MinK primarily interacts with the Kv7.1 α‐subunit to regulate I_Ks, but it may also modulate I_Kr via association with the Kv11.1 α‐subunit [11, 15, 16]. Among KCNE1 variants, p.Asp76Asn has been most extensively characterized [15]. In the present case, her brother's variant caused prolonged QT, but the patient's father's ECG did not show a significant change that explained the prolonged QT syndrome. Therefore, while this variant may be associated with the observed phenotype, current evidence is insufficient to establish it as causative, and it should be interpreted cautiously as a variant of uncertain significance. The concurrent RYR2 variant (c.4378G>C), inherited maternally, adds another layer of complexity. RYR2 encodes the cardiac ryanodine receptor 2, a critical intracellular calcium release channel, and plays a key role in catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT) and, less commonly, arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy [17, 18]. Although our patient showed no structural abnormalities or typical CPVT features on exercise testing, the coexistence of RYR2 and KCNE1 variants may have an additive or modifying effect on the arrhythmic phenotype. In addition, this finding can be considered incidental or hypothesis‐generating at present, and its contribution to the clinical phenotype remains unclear.

While many individuals with congenital LQTS remain asymptomatic, syncope is the most frequent clinical manifestation in others, occurring in approximately 60% of untreated cases [19]. Syncope is a brief, self‐limited loss of consciousness caused by transient cerebral hypoperfusion, followed by complete recovery. It may result from cardiac causes such as arrhythmias or structural heart disease, or cerebrovascular factors such as vertebrobasilar insufficiency. Additionally, it can be caused by disturbances in vascular tone and blood flow, including vasovagal episodes, orthostatic hypotension, situational triggers, and carotid sinus hypersensitivity [20]. Our patient experienced two syncopal episodes, and her clinical presentation, critical for syncope diagnosis, was suggestive of cardiac syncope. The mildly elevated pulmonary artery pressure (25 mmHg) was hemodynamically insignificant and unlikely to contribute to syncope. QT prolongation in the ECG (485 ms) and a typical history convinced us to conduct further investigations to find more clues, including a genetic study. Whole‐exome sequencing identified heterozygous variants in KCNE1 (c.163G>A) and RYR2 (c.4378G>C). Interestingly, the EEG revealed generalized spike‐and‐wave discharges compatible with idiopathic generalized epilepsy with photosensitivity. Although limited reports have suggested a possible association between RYR2 variants and neurological manifestations, the evidence remains inconclusive. In this case, the absence of a typical CPVT phenotype and the uncertain classification of the variant preclude definitive conclusions, and the EEG findings should be interpreted with caution. Mei‐Gang Ma et al. suggested that RYR2 variants can predispose to epilepsy, and Yap et al. reported a case of adult‐onset genetic generalized epilepsy in a patient with a RYR2 mutation and a family history of CPVT, which may account for the observed EEG pattern [21]. This finding complicates the clinical picture, as convulsive syncope secondary to LQTS can mimic epileptic seizures, and conversely, seizures may cause transient ECG changes. In this case, the coexistence of genetic variants in cardiac ion channel–related genes (KCNE1 and RYR2) further blurs the distinction between neurogenic and cardiogenic mechanisms.

In our patient with LQT5, β‐blocker therapy was initiated as first‐line treatment, consistent with guideline recommendations. In LQTS, β‐blockers are the preferred initial treatment for most patients, unless the patient has a significantly low heart rate [22]. Non‐selective β‐blockers, such as nadolol and propranolol, effectively reduce the arrhythmic risk in LQTS. At slow heart rates, enhanced INa‐L prolongs QTc, and mexiletine is the only alternative therapy for markedly prolonged QTc [23]. ICDs are recommended only for symptomatic LQTS patients despite β‐blockers or genotype‐specific therapy, though shocks can trigger sympathetic activation and electrical storms. Left cardiac sympathetic denervation (LCSD) may be used in patients who cannot receive ICDs, but its efficacy is supported only by small studies, and it is not a substitute for ICDs in high‐risk patients. Overall, guidelines prioritize drug therapy first, followed by ICD implantation, with LCSD reserved as adjunctive treatment [24]. ICDs were ultimately implanted for both the patient and her symptomatic brother during the three‐year follow‐up. Therefore, long‐term follow‐up and family screening are essential for rare LQTS subtypes, as the risk may evolve and management strategies may need to be adjusted.

This case highlights the diagnostic complexity of suspected LQTS, particularly when cardiac and neurological features coexist. The presence of a KCNE1 variant of uncertain significance, along with an additional RYR2 variant, created a diagnostically ambiguous scenario that initially obscured the underlying cardiac etiology. While segregation data in the sibling suggest a possible association, a causal relationship cannot be definitively established. The RYR2 finding is best considered incidental at present. Management with β‐blockers and subsequent ICD implantation resulted in stable outcomes, although treatment decisions were individualized. This case underscores the importance of cautious genetic interpretation, comprehensive clinical evaluation, and long‐term follow‐up in patients with atypical or overlapping phenotypes.
