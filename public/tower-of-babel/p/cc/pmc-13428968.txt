DTLI: Distribution transformation-based lightweight learned indexing for data lakehouse query optimization
Authors: Ye Liang, Chao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the exponential growth of data scale, modern analytical platforms such as data lakehouses face severe query performance bottlenecks. To address the limitation of traditional indexes and existing learned indexes in balancing high-efficiency queries with lightweight structures, this paper proposes DTLI, a lightweight learned index architecture based on distribution transformation. We first systematically evaluate the distribution transformation performance of three generative models, Variational Autoencoder (VAE), Normalizing Flow, and Diffusion Model, and select the optimal Block Neural Autoregressive Flow (B-NAF) as the transformation operator to map original complex distributions to near-uniform distributions. On this basis, we propose a changepoint-based piecewise fitting algorithm for cumulative distribution functions, constructing a minimal binary tree index structure containing only model nodes and pointer nodes to achieve index lightweighting. Finally, we design local and global indexing strategies adapted to the partitioning characteristics of data lakehouses, completing the integration of DTLI on the Apache Hudi platform. Experiments demonstrate that DTLI significantly outperforms native indexes and learned indexes including RMI, PGM, and NFL across multiple datasets. Specifically, DTLI improves average throughput by 72.55%, 50.87%, 38.60%, and 5.21% over B+Tree, RMI, PGM, and NFL, respectively, while reducing 99th percentile tail latency by 57.52%, 56.44%, 29.35%, and 15.81%, with advantages amplifying as data scale increases. Ablation studies confirm that distribution transformation can improve the throughput of existing indexes by over 38%. Currently, DTLI is designed and evaluated for one-dimensional numerical keys. Its extension to multi-dimensional or non-numerical data is non-trivial and remains as future work. Additionally, the distribution transformation relies on offline training, and incremental update mechanisms for dynamic scenarios require further investigation.Keywords: learned index, distribution transformation, data lakehouse, query optimization

1. Introduction

The digital wave has driven exponential growth in data volumes across various industries, making unified analytical platforms such as data lakehouses the mainstream architecture for processing massive data.1,2 These platforms are required to store and manage petabytes of multimodal data, imposing extremely high demands on the throughput and latency of query engines.3,4 As a core component for accelerating data access, the performance of indexes directly impacts query efficiency. Traditional index structures such as B+ trees and hash indexes are mature and widely applicable, but their design is independent of data distribution and cannot effectively leverage the statistical patterns commonly present in real-world data, leading to performance bottlenecks in big data scenarios.5,6

Learned indexes7–9 model indexes as approximate representations of the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the data, using key values to directly predict record positions, thereby substituting computational processes for part of the search operations. Representative works such as the recursive model index (RMI) 10 and the piecewise geometric model index (PGM) 11 have demonstrated superior storage efficiency and query speed over traditional indexes on specific datasets. However, the performance of learned indexes highly depends on the “friendliness” of the underlying data distribution. When faced with complex, skewed, or multimodal data distributions commonly found in real-world scenarios, existing methods often require constructing deep models or multiple-level segments to ensure prediction accuracy. This not only increases the complexity and memory overhead of the index structure but may also lead to increased query latency, especially a significant rise in tail latency, thereby undermining their theoretical advantages.

To address these issues, data distribution can be preprocessed before index construction by transforming the complex original distribution into a simpler and more uniform distribution space, thereby reducing the difficulty of subsequent CDF modeling. Recently, researchers applied normalizing flows to distribution transformation and validated its effectiveness. However, to support dynamic data updates, the study introduced a complex index framework that includes multi-level buffering and version control mechanisms, partially offsetting the benefits gained from distribution transformation with structural overhead. Moreover, this framework did not systematically compare the potential of other generative models for the distribution transformation task. Additionally, designing a lightweight index structure that aligns closely with the characteristics of the transformed data and practically integrating such advanced indexes into data lakehouse environments—which feature partition management, metadata management, and other properties—remains a critical challenge to be explored.

To tackle these challenges, this paper conducts a comprehensive evaluation of mainstream generative models as distribution transformation operators and, combined with a CDF piecewise fitting algorithm based on changepoint detection, constructs a lightweight tree-based index structure named DTLI suitable for data lakehouse environments. This enables DTLI to adapt to the partitioned data layouts in real-world production settings. Extensive experiments on standard benchmark datasets verify that DTLI significantly outperforms traditional B+ trees and state-of-the-art learned indexes such as RMI, PGM, and NFL in terms of both throughput and tail latency, with its performance advantages further strengthening as the data scale increases.

2. Related work

With the explosive growth of data volumes, traditional indexing technologies continue to face numerous challenges when processing massive-scale data. In modern data management systems such as data lakehouses, indexing techniques must accommodate larger data volumes and more complex data distributions. Consequently, learned indexes based on machine learning models have emerged and are gradually being applied in the practical construction of data lakehouses.

2.1. Learned index structures

In 2018, Kraska et al. 9 pioneered the concept of learned indexes, proposing for the first time to replace traditional index structures with machine learning models. Learned indexes establish a mapping relationship between key values and storage locations by fitting the data distribution, thereby significantly improving query efficiency. The Recursive Model Index (RMI) is one of the most fundamental learned indexes. Its core idea is to enable the model to learn the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of sorted data, achieving a mapping from keys to positions. Its query throughput is notably superior to traditional indexes such as B-trees.

Although RMI outperforms traditional indexes, it partitions the data CDF into segments of fixed length. To address this, researchers proposed the FITing-TREE structure, 12 which requires the prediction error of each segment to remain within a predefined linear fitting error threshold. The segment length is dynamically determined based on the fitting quality, thereby overcoming the fixed-segment-length limitation of RMI.

Since FITing-TREE’s organizational structure is similar to that of a B+ tree, an excessively large dataset cardinality can lead to too many nodes, increasing space overhead and potentially degrading query performance. To optimize this, Ferragina et al. 11 refined the structure of FITing-TREE and proposed the PGM-index. The PGM-index employs a bottom-up construction approach, using piecewise linear approximation models to fit the data distribution. It optimizes the segmentation strategy via a streaming algorithm, obtaining an optimal sequence of segments under constraints on the maximum number of segments and the minimum number of keys covered per segment, effectively solving the problem of excessive nodes in FITing-TREE.

To address the lack of update and insert support in PGM, Ding et al. 13 proposed the ALEX index, which can adaptively determine the depth of the RMI model and the number of leaf nodes. Wu et al. 14 aimed to mitigate conflicts in learned indexes where different keys map to the same predicted position and proposed LIPP, an updatable learned index. Inspired by the normalizing flow approach in generative models, the NFL model 15 constructs a learned index by transforming non-uniformly distributed data into a uniform distribution.

The limitations of NFL are mainly reflected in two aspects: First, its technical approach is relatively narrow, having only validated normalizing flows without systematically exploring the potential of other generative models (such as VAEs and Diffusion Models) for distribution transformation tasks, lacking sufficient basis for model selection. Second, its architecture design mismatches query-dominated scenarios. To support dynamic updates, NFL introduces multi-level buffering and version control mechanisms, resulting in a complex index structure. In analytical workloads where queries constitute over 90% of operations, the structural overhead unrelated to query processing becomes a performance bottleneck. Therefore, for data lakehouse scenarios dominated by queries, there is an urgent need to strip away the complexity introduced by update support and design a lightweight distribution-transformed index specifically optimized for queries. Furthermore, existing research on fundamental learned indexes has primarily focused on traditional database systems and has not been sufficiently extended to data lakehouse architectures that support partition management, making it difficult for current methods to effectively optimize complex queries involving partition paths.

2.2. Distribution transformation-based learned indexes

Generative models can learn the underlying probability distribution of data, and related research primarily focuses on utilizing deep learning models to achieve data distribution transformation.

Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) learn to encode data into a latent space constrained by a prior distribution, exhibiting good robustness to input variations and excelling in generation and transfer tasks. They are suitable for dimensionality reduction, feature learning, data denoising, and reconstruction tasks. 16 However, VAEs are insufficient in handling details and may produce blurry outputs when faced with highly complex data distributions. 17

Normalizing Flow (NFL) models explicitly model data distributions through chains of invertible transformations, using bijective functions to gradually map simple latent distributions to complex data distributions, thereby establishing a series of reversible transformations between complex and simple distributions. Their inherent invertibility makes them particularly suitable for controlled distribution transformation tasks. 18 While ensuring generation quality and diversity, NFL enable precise probability density calculation, effectively avoiding the blurry generation problem of VAE.

Building upon this, FFJORD significantly enhances high-dimensional data modeling capabilities by designing sparse Jacobian determinants and incorporating autoregressive and attention mechanisms. 19 However, due to the computation involving Jacobian determinants, its computational cost is high, and scaling to even higher dimensions remains challenging.

Diffusion Models, on the other hand, learn a denoising process, using a reverse Markov chain of gradual noise addition and removal to generate data from noise. Their forward noise addition process can deterministically transform data into a simple noise distribution, serving a function similar to distribution transformation. 20 Although they excel in generation quality, their iterative nature typically results in lower sampling throughput compared to VAE and NFL.

2.3. Indexing in data lakehouses

The evolution of data platform architectures has profoundly influenced the design requirements for indexing technologies. Data lakehouse architectures represented by Delta Lake, 21 Apache Iceberg, 22 and Apache Hudi 23 have become the de facto standard in the field of large-scale data analytics. They organically combine the elastically scalable, cost-effective storage capabilities of data lakes with the robust data management and ACID transaction guarantees of traditional data warehouses. 24 Existing data lakehouse environments typically employ a two-level partition-file indexing architecture, including a metadata layer managing partition paths and a file layer locating record offsets. Within this architecture, data inside partitions often exhibits highly skewed distributions, causing traditional learned indexes to require deep models or numerous segments within a partition. DTLI can homogenize intra-partition data through distribution transformation, significantly reducing the complexity of indexing within partitions. This represents an optimization space not fully exploited by general-purpose learned indexes.

Existing metadata management layers often struggle to support high-performance SQL queries. 25 To break through this bottleneck, researchers have proposed migrating hot data to high-speed storage media like SSDs, constructing efficient access paths (e.g., indexes), and co-optimizing data formats with computation engines. 26 Taking Apache Hudi as an example, its indexing system includes in-memory hash indexes and intra-partition Bloom filters, and it can interface with external systems like HBase to enforce global uniqueness constraints. 27 However, these mechanisms are mostly based on heuristic rules or probabilistic models and fail to fully leverage the statistical distribution characteristics of data values for adaptive optimization. This limitation creates an opportunity for learned indexes—by fitting the data distribution, finer-grained intra-file positioning can be achieved. Nonetheless, constraints such as partitioned data layout, coexistence of multiple file versions, and table format compatibility pose unique challenges to such integration.

Currently, the index design in data lakehouse systems still does not fully utilize data distribution features, and there is scarce research introducing the innovative concept of learned indexes into the index optimization of data lakehouses. Given the significant potential of learned indexes in enhancing query performance, this paper aims to explore the feasibility of applying learned indexes to the index design of data lakehouses, seeking to substantially improve query efficiency in data lakehouses through this innovative approach.

3. Distribution transformation-based learned indexing framework

To balance partition locality and global access efficiency in data lakehouses, this paper designs a Distribution-Transformed Learned Index (DTLI) framework. By adaptively learning data distribution characteristics, it achieves an effective balance between index construction cost and query performance.

The core idea of the DTLI framework is to decouple the modeling of complex data distributions from the core index structure, thereby optimizing query performance. This is particularly suitable for query-dominant data lakehouse scenarios. Firstly, a distribution transformation algorithm based on generative models is proposed, leveraging generative models to map the original data distribution to a near-uniform distribution. Secondly, a changepoint-based CDF fitting method is presented to automatically determine split points and perform local fitting within each partitioned interval. Building on this, a novel top-down lightweight learned index structure is constructed. Finally, a global indexing scheme based on DTLI is designed for the data lakehouse environment and integrated into the Apache Hudi platform.

3.1. Distribution transformation algorithm based on generative models

Generative models are a class of machine learning models capable of learning data distributions and generating new samples. They model data distributions to sample from a latent space and reconstruct data. Typical generative models consist of two parts: an encoder and a decoder. The encoder maps input data into a latent space, and the decoder is responsible for reconstruction. When the latent space is constrained to simple distributions such as Gaussian or uniform distributions, the trained encoder acquires the ability to transform complex distributions into simple ones. 3.2. Changepoint-based CDF fitting method

The core mechanism of learned indexes is to use machine learning models to fit the conditional CDF between keys and storage positions, thereby establishing a fast mapping from Key to Position. For discrete keys, their distribution patterns can be mathematically characterized by the CDF, which enables accurate fitting of complex distributions using simplified index structures. To this end, this paper proposes a changepoint-based distribution fitting method that approximates the data distribution via piecewise linearization, reducing model complexity and improving index construction efficiency.

Learned indexes obtain an approximate CDF F∼K(kp) through optimized fitting algorithms to achieve indexing functionality. The number of segments is a key factor determining index complexity, typically much smaller than the total data volume, giving learned indexes the potential to outperform traditional indexes.

The key aspects of CDF fitting are: 1) determining split points; 2) performing local fitting within each partitioned interval. If the data trend changes significantly in the neighborhood of a point, different models should be used on its two sides. To this end, the “changepoint magnitude” is defined to measure the strength of trend change in the left and right neighborhoods of a point.

where I(xp) denotes the estimated slope of the CDF at xp . Since the CDF is monotonically non-decreasing, the changepoint magnitude reflects the degree of change in the CDF growth trend between the left and right sides. A larger changepoint magnitude indicates greater differences between the fitting models on the two sides, making the point more likely to be a split node. After detecting points whose changepoint magnitude exceeds a threshold, the data on their left and right sides are fitted independently, and the fitting quality is evaluated to decide whether further splitting is needed. If the fitting quality does not meet the preset accuracy, the interval is further subdivided and refitted, iteratively optimizing the fitting accuracy. After distribution transformation, the CDF curve exhibits approximately linear characteristics; therefore, the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE) is chosen as the quantitative metric for fitting quality, with smaller SSE indicating better fitting.

In Algorithm 2, I(zi) approximates the local slope of the CDF at point zi , computed as P[i+1]−P[i]Z[i+1]−Z[i] for i<n, with the last slope set to 0. The changepoint magnitude ΔI(zi)=|I(zi)−I(zi−1)| (with ΔI(zi)= 0) captures the change in slope between consecutive points. A large ΔI indicates a significant shift in data growth trend, suggesting a natural segmentation boundary. The algorithm recursively splits the interval at the point with the maximum ΔI until the linear fitting SSE of each segment falls below the threshold ε (default ε=10−4 ).

3.3. DTLI index structure and query processing

The order of CDF-partitioned intervals naturally organizes into a top-down tree structure. This paper proposes a novel top-down lightweight learned index structure, DTLI, which employs an extremely simple binary tree design containing only two types of nodes: model nodes storing linear parameters (at leaf nodes) and pointer nodes storing split thresholds (at internal nodes). This design reduces the node types from four in NFL to two, eliminating the version control overhead associated with updates and making the index structure highly compact. The DTLI tree is built once after offline distribution transformation. For static or read-only datasets, no further maintenance is needed. In scenarios with periodic batch updates (e.g., daily data ingestion), the entire index can be reconstructed offline during low-load windows. For environments requiring higher update freshness, a differential update strategy can be employed: a small B+Tree absorbs incoming updates, and the main DTLI is periodically rebuilt. This hybrid approach maintains high query performance while accommodating moderate update rates. The current implementation does not support real-time, tuple-level updates; such extension is left for future work.

3.4. Global data lakehouse index based on DTLI

The partitioning characteristics of data lakehouses amplify the benefits of DTLI’s distribution transformation. Partition keys (e.g., date, region) divide data into multiple subsets, and the data skew within each subset is typically higher than in the global dataset. DTLI independently performs distribution transformation within each partition, mapping local extreme distributions to uniform ones, avoiding potential under-fitting that might occur with global transformation. Therefore, we propose integrating DTLI into Apache Hudi as a global index.

Apache Hudi global indexes need to handle partition addressing. This paper proposes a two-phase construction method for building global DTLI indexes bottom-up. As shown in Figure 5, the architecture is divided into two phases from right to left: the first phase (location phase) performs precise lookup within a determined partition; the second phase (partitioning phase) determines the target partition to which a record key belongs (Figure 6).

Global DTLI index architecture in Apache Hudi.

The first phase learns the mapping relationship between file IDs and record keys within a partition. This relationship shares the same characteristics as partition-local indexes; therefore, a partition DTLI is built inside each partition.

The second phase constructs the mapping from record keys to partition keys. Based on the discreteness of the partition key’s data distribution, partition keys are divided into two categories: continuous partition keys (e.g., time, user ID), which have a large value space and continue to use Apache Hudi’s original indexing structures; and discrete partition keys (e.g., product category, province), which have limited distinct values and are suitable for prediction using discriminative models. Considering the sequential nature of record keys, LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) or GRU (Gated Recurrent Unit) are chosen as the prediction models.

The global DTLI is implemented as a custom index plug-in in Apache Hudi 1.0.0. The integration requires approximately 500 lines of Java code and does not modify Hudi’s core table format or existing indexing interfaces. The partition-level DTLI structures are stored as serialized objects in Hudi’s metadata directory, and the LSTM/GRU prediction model (for discrete partition keys) is saved in a lightweight ONNX format. During query execution, the global DTLI adds less than 5% CPU overhead compared to Hudi’s native index manager, as measured in our experiments (Section 4.3). However, the initial construction of the global DTLI requires scanning the entire dataset once; for petabyte-scale tables, this can be time-consuming and is recommended to be performed offline.

4. Experimental design and result analysis

To validate the applicability and performance advantages of DTLI in data lakehouse environments, this paper designs experiments for evaluating generative model operators, comparative experiments between DTLI and baseline models, and a comprehensive performance evaluation in integrated data lakehouse scenarios.

4.1. Generative model operator evaluation experiment

The choice of the generative model operator G in Algorithm 1 directly affects the quality of distribution transformation. According to differences in modeling mechanisms, generative models can be categorized into four types: likelihood-based models, generative adversarial networks, autoregressive models, and energy-based models. Compared to generative adversarial networks, likelihood-based models possess explicit probability density functions and analytically computable likelihoods; compared to autoregressive and energy-based models, they offer higher generation efficiency during the inference phase. Considering both computability and efficiency, likelihood-based models are more suitable for distribution transformation tasks. Therefore, the experiment selects three representative methods—Variational Autoencoder (VAE), Normalizing Flow (NFL), and Diffusion Model—as candidates to evaluate the impact of different likelihood modeling strategies on distribution transformation effectiveness.

4.1.1. Benchmark datasets and experimental environment

The Search On Sorted Data Benchmark (SOSD) 28 is a widely used benchmark framework in the field of learned indexes, containing eight different datasets, each consisting of 20,000 64-bit unsigned non-repeating integers. These specifically include: Amzn64 (book sales popularity data), face64 (sampled user IDs from Facebook dataset), logn64 (lognormal distribution samples), norm64 (normal distribution samples), osmc64 (uniformly sampled OpenStreetMap locations), uden64 (dense integers), uspr64 (uniformly distributed sparse integers), and wiki64 (Wikipedia article edit timestamps). These datasets originate from diverse sources with complex and varied original distributions, making them suitable for evaluating the distribution transformation effects of different generative models. 4.1.3. Experimental results and analysis

After training the three types of generative models for distribution transformation on the eight datasets, the experimental results for KL Divergence and throughput were obtained, as shown in the heatmaps in Figure 8. Colors from red to blue in the heatmaps indicate performance from best to worst.

Heatmaps comparing distribution transformation performance of generative models. (Left: KL Divergence; Right: Throughput).

KL Divergence reflects the difference between the transformed distribution and the standard normal distribution, i.e., the distribution simplification effect. The experimental results show that the Diffusion Model generates distributions closest to the standard normal on most datasets, with its average KL Divergence being 24.26% and 8.44% lower than that of VAE and Normalizing Flow, respectively. Normalizing Flow also shows good generation quality on some datasets, with no significant difference from the Diffusion Model. VAE’s generation quality is relatively poor, as its optimization objective is to maximize the evidence lower bound rather than directly minimizing the difference between the generated and target distributions; meanwhile, the higher latent dimension may increase the difficulty of modeling complex distributions. In contrast, Normalizing Flow directly models the data distribution through invertible transformations, allowing precise probability density calculation and strong adaptability to high-dimensional data. Diffusion Models generate samples through iterative denoising, also enabling precise modeling of complex distributions and being less sensitive to high-dimensional latent variables. Therefore, from the perspective of distribution transformation quality, both Normalizing Flow and Diffusion Models outperform VAE.

By vertically comparing KL Divergence across different datasets, it can be observed that the more similar the original data distribution is to the target distribution, the better the transformation effect. For example, the Norm64 dataset sampled from a normal distribution has the lowest KL Divergence values across all models, while the Log64 dataset, which severely deviates from a normal distribution, exhibits significantly higher KL Divergence values.

Regarding throughput performance, all models perform well overall. Horizontal comparison shows that VAE’s throughput outperforms Normalizing Flow in most cases, averaging 9.35% higher; Normalizing Flow outperforms the Diffusion Model, averaging 9.82% higher. This is primarily because VAE requires only one forward pass to generate samples, while Normalizing Flow requires multiple invertible transformations, and Diffusion Models require multiple iterative denoising steps, resulting in relatively lower throughput. As hyperparameter settings are largely consistent, the differences in throughput across different datasets for each model are not significant. Therefore, from a throughput perspective, VAE performs the best.

Comprehensive analysis of the KL Divergence and throughput metrics indicates a negative correlation between a model’s distribution transformation quality and its throughput. Models with higher complexity and more computational steps yield better transformation quality but lower throughput; conversely, simpler, faster models yield relatively lower transformation quality. The comprehensive analysis shows that although Normalizing Flow is slightly inferior to the Diffusion Model in distribution transformation effect, the difference in KL Divergence between them is small, while Normalizing Flow maintains high throughput. Therefore, considering both distribution transformation quality and computational efficiency, Normalizing Flow is the optimal choice for the distribution transformation generative model operator G.

The default hyperparameters used in all subsequent experiments are: transformation dimension d=4, scaling factor θ=10, and CDF fitting error threshold ε=10−4. These values were chosen based on the sensitivity analysis in Section 4.2.5.

4.2. DTLI performance comparison experiment

Index performance is primarily measured by two key metrics: throughput and tail latency. Throughput reflects overall query efficiency, while tail latency characterizes performance under extreme conditions. This experiment compares DTLI with multiple baseline models on the SOSD dataset, including horizontal comparison, vertical comparison, and ablation studies, to comprehensively validate DTLI’s performance in different scenarios.

4.2.1. Horizontal comparison experiment

The experiment selects baseline models from four dimensions: traditional vs. learned indexes, hard vs. soft partitioning, with vs. without distribution transformation, and lightweight vs. heavyweight architecture. The compared models include B+Tree, RMI, PGM, and NFL, with their characteristics summarized in Table 2.

In this experiment, indexes not using machine learning models are defined as traditional indexes (B+Tree), and indexes using models to perceive data distribution are defined as learned indexes. RMI, which uses fixed-length partitioning of CDF intervals, serves as the representative of hard-partitioning learned indexes. The throughput of DTLI and NFL is on average about 30 times and 33 times higher than B+Tree, respectively. Due to its lighter structure and fewer parameters, DTLI shows about an 8% improvement over NFL. In summary, regarding the throughput metric, DTLI performs optimally.

DTLI has the lowest tail latency among all indexes and performs stably across different datasets. Due to its lighter structure, it avoids the judgment overhead associated with non-model nodes in NFL, resulting in lower latency in extreme cases. The tail latency of NFL and DTLI is significantly better than other indexes, benefiting from distribution transformation which reduces model height and width, thereby decreasing computational load. PGM performs well only when data distribution is uniform, as uniform data leads to a more balanced tree structure. RMI performs stably, showing significant improvement over B+Tree. Therefore, regarding the tail latency metric, DTLI also performs optimally.

4.2.5. Sensitivity analysis of key parameters

Two key parameters affect DTLI’s performance: the transformation dimension d (in Algorithm 1) and the CDF fitting error threshold ε (in Algorithm 2). Figure 13 shows the impact of varying d from 2 to 8 and ε from 10−3 to 10−5 on throughput and 99% tail latency using the SOSD wiki64 dataset (chosen for its skewed distribution). A dimension of d=2 fails to sufficiently uniformize the distribution, resulting in lower throughput (≈2.1M ops/s) and higher latency. Increasing d to 4 improves throughput to 2.8M ops/s, while further increasing to 8 yields marginal gains (<3%) at the cost of longer transformation time. Similarly, ε=10−3 leads to under-fitting and higher prediction errors, whereas ε=10−5 over-fits and increases tree depth without notable accuracy gain. The default settings (d=4, ε=10−4) offer a robust trade-off across all tested datasets.

4.3. Performance evaluation in data lakehouse environment

To validate the applicability of DTLI in partitioned data scenarios, the eICU dataset was selected to simulate a data lakehouse environment, using admission time and age as continuous partition keys, and gender and disease type as discrete partition keys. It should be noted that partitioning is a core data organization method in data lakehouses and many general-purpose databases. This experiment uses Apache Hudi as the platform to verify the performance gains of DTLI under a partitioned architecture. This method can be generalized to other data systems supporting partitioning.

The experiment sets the target distribution as Gaussian, using Apache Hudi version 1.0.0. For the partition phase targeting discrete partition keys, LSTM and GRU are used as prediction models, respectively, to construct the complete global DTLI index. To evaluate the performance of the global DTLI, it is compared with Apache Hudi’s original HBase index and record index.

Figure 14 shows the throughput comparison between the global DTLI and Hudi’s native indexes. For the method using DTLI only within partitions, its throughput improves by about 58.2% on average compared to the record index but remains lower than the HBase index. This is because DTLI primarily accelerates the second stage of the query (intra-partition location), resulting in higher throughput than simple indexes using the same method for the first stage, but the optimization in the second stage is not yet sufficient to surpass the overall performance of HBase.

Throughput comparison of Apache Hudi global indexes.

In the optimization targeting discrete partition keys, the global DTLI shows significant throughput advantages. The results indicate that the performance improvement of the global DTLI stems not only from the optimization in the second stage (intra-partition DTLI) but also from the significant contribution of the prediction model adopted in the first stage.

Thus, integrating DTLI as a global indexing architecture into Apache Hudi can effectively improve system query performance. This optimization significantly enhances data retrieval efficiency while maintaining the system’s original functionality, providing a new solution for large-scale data management.

We measured the CPU and I/O overhead introduced by the global DTLI compared to Hudi’s native record index. On the eICU dataset with discrete partition keys (gender, disease type), the global DTLI reduces the number of I/O operations by 62% due to more accurate partition prediction. However, it adds approximately 4.8% CPU overhead for invoking the LSTM prediction model and traversing the DTLI tree. In absolute terms, the CPU overhead is 0.12 ms per query on average, which is negligible compared to the I/O savings (average 8 ms per query). The global DTLI also increases metadata storage by 15% (mainly due to storing the LSTM model), which is acceptable for most production deployments.

5. Conclusion

To address the index performance bottlenecks caused by complex data distributions in data lakehouse environments, this paper proposes DTLI, a lightweight learned index architecture based on distribution transformation. By employing generative models to transform complex data distributions into near-uniform ones, the difficulty of CDF modeling is reduced. A minimalist tree-structured index is then constructed based on changepoint detection, ultimately achieving deep integration of DTLI with Apache Hudi.

Experiments demonstrate that Normalizing Flow achieves the optimal balance between distribution transformation quality and efficiency. On the SOSD datasets, DTLI improves average throughput by 72.55%, 50.87%, 38.60%, and 5.21% compared to B+Tree, RMI, PGM, and NFL, respectively, while reducing tail latency by 57.52%, 56.44%, 29.35%, and 15.81%. Ablation studies confirm that distribution transformation can improve the throughput of existing indexes by over 38%. Tests in the Apache Hudi environment show that the global DTLI improves throughput by 58.2% compared to the native record index.

While DTLI achieves strong performance on read-heavy, one-dimensional numerical workloads, several limitations remain. First, the distribution transformation model is trained offline and does not support real-time, tuple-level updates. For highly dynamic environments, a hybrid approach (DTLI + small B+Tree buffer) or periodic retraining can be employed, but a fully online adaptive version remains future work. Second, DTLI currently handles only one-dimensional numerical keys. Extending it to multi-dimensional or non-numerical data (e.g., strings, geospatial) requires fundamental changes to the transformation and segmentation algorithms. Third, the changepoint threshold ε and the transformation dimension d are currently set empirically; automatic tuning strategies based on data statistics are under development. Finally, the theoretical performance bounds of DTLI under different distribution families have not yet been derived. Future research will address these directions to broaden the applicability of distribution-transformed learned indexes.
