The intertrochanteric line approach for irreducible femoral neck fractures: a novel open reduction technique with favorable clinical outcomes
Authors: Linglong Deng, Wang Zhang, Xue Fang, Changhuan Liu, Pengcheng Li, Zhe Xie, Zhenyu Pan, Xin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeDespite the high incidence of femoral neck fractures, the optimal surgical strategy (open reduction vs. closed reduction) for patients with an irreducible femoral neck fracture (IFNF) remains a controversial topic. Furthermore, there is a lack of consensus regarding the surgical approach to open reduction. The purpose of this study was to introduce a novel open approach for the treatment of IFNF.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 52 patients defined as IFNF who achieved anatomic reduction and definitive fixation through the intertrochanteric line approach. During surgical management, all patients underwent "sandwich mode" bone grafting. Post-operative Garden index and Hip Harris score (HHS) were recorded to assess the quality of femoral neck reduction and hip function. Patients were followed up for fracture healing time, avascular necrosis (AVN), and other complications.ResultsAll patients were followed up with an average of 32.37 months (range, 24–56 months). The fracture union was achieved in all patients with a mean duration of 4.35 ± 0.85 months. According to the Garden index, 37/52 cases achieved grade I reduction, and 15/52 cases achieved grade II reduction, respectively. Functional outcomes of 52 hips were evaluated by HHS, among which 39 cases were excellent, 7 cases were good, 3 cases were fair, and 3 cases who developed AVN were poor. None of them experienced postoperative Trendelenburg gait, infections, thrombosis, or paresthesia.ConclusionsThe intertrochanteric line approach is an effective and safe method for IFNF, providing excellent anatomic reduction, capsular decompression, which yields favorable outcomes in combination with the "sandwich mode" bone graft. More importantly, it has a short learning curve that is readily generalizable, while simultaneously minimizing complications, especially AVN and nonunion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03365-x.Keywords: Irreducible femoral neck fractures, Approach, AVN, Nonunion

Introduction

Femoral neck fracture is a common fracture in clinical practice, accounting for about 50% of hip fractures [1]. However, the management of femoral neck fracture continues to be a source of anxiety for orthopedic surgeons, primarily due to the potentially high complication rate of avascular necrosis (AVN) of the femoral head and nonunion, which statistically occurs in up to 20% of patients [2, 3]. Previous evidence has indicated that protecting the blood supply of the femoral head from being infringed, such as close reduction as possible, can largely minimize the complications [4]. In recent years, numerous studies have demonstrated promising outcomes of femoral neck fracture linked to the quality of reduction [5–7].

For complex displaced femoral neck fractures (DFNF), such as Garden III or IV fractures, anatomic reduction is difficult to achieve after multiple closed attempts, which is referred to as IFNF [8]. Numerous clinical studies on closed reduction have been investigated for DFNF; however, the results often fall short of optimism, frequently beset by AVN or nonunion [4, 9]. Some scholars hold that repeated rotation or pulling of the affected limb during closed reduction will inevitably aggravate vasospasm and distortion of the arteries in the round ligaments of the femur, may lead to vascular embolism, and then destroy the blood supply to the femoral head [7]. Hence, an increasing number of surgeons have proposed an open surgical approach to achieve anatomic reduction once closed reduction has failed [10].

Multiple surgical approaches, such as Smith-Peterson (S-P), Direct Anterior approach (DAA), Watson-Jones (W-J), and other modified approaches, have been well documented [11–13]. However, their limitations and apparent comorbidities are frequently reported. The S-P approach presents a long incision and may necessitate the amputation of the rectus femoris, resulting in iatrogenic injury [14]. DAA has focused on the technical difficulties and the higher risk of intraoperative neurovascular injury [15]. Following the reduction of the fracture via the DAA and S-P approaches, an additional incision is required to implant internal fixation devices. The W-J approach provides weak surgical visualization and involves the injury of the superior gluteal nerve and vessels, causing the gluteus medius muscle atrophy associated with Trendelenburg gait [16]. As discussed, even experts cannot agree on the best approach to IFNF.

Besides, a quantitative study has revealed that bone defects can occur in any type of femoral neck fracture [17]. Moreover, almost all of the DFNF have some degree of bone defect [18], which undoubtedly increases the rate of AVN and nonunion. The adjunctive use of bone grafts or bone substitutes, therefore, was needed to minimize associated complications.

In this article, we exclusively describe a novel intertrochanteric line approach, a true neurovascular-sparing approach to avoid neurovascular injury, to treat IFNF. Additionally, we designed a new "sandwich mode" bone grafting with sequential implantation of allograft bone—BMP2—allograft bone at fracture sites. The aim of this study was to introduce this new approach as well as “sandwich mode” bone grafting and to evaluate the clinical and radiographic outcomes. Our results overall favor the use of the intertrochanteric line approach with "sandwich mode" bone grafting to treat IFNF while minimizing complications.

Methods

Institutional Review Board approval of the study was obtained. From March 2017 to November 2022, 52 patients (29 male and 23 female), with a mean age of 48 years (range, 19–64 years), sustained unilateral DFNF and underwent a surgical procedure at the Orthopaedic Center, Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University. Those with other site fractures or who encountered previous surgery in the affected hip were excluded. All of them included Garden grades III (n = 20) and IV (n = 32) fractures. Basic patient demographic characteristics, including age, sex, and concomitant injuries, were abstracted and recorded (Table 1). The patients signed informed consent forms. All operations were masterfully executed by two senior orthopedic surgeons, each with over two decades of dedicated practice in the field.

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics (n = 52)

The procedure was carried out under spinal or general anesthesia. Patients were supine on the traction table with both limbs placed in traction position. Prior to the surgical approach, closed reduction was preferentially attempted through the classic Mc-Elvenny method [19]. Namely, the affected limb was first rotated outside with increased traction, and then the assistant and the surgeon hold the foot and knee respectively to rotate the affected limb and adduct the hip joint. If closed manipulation fails to achieve an anatomical reduction after 3 attempts, this type of femoral neck fracture is defined as IFNF, at which time open reduction of the intertrochanteric line approach is decisively performed. First, the greater and lesser trochanters of the femur were marked on the body surface under C-arm fluoroscopy (Fig. 1). A longitudinal incision (approximately 6–8 cm in length) began at a point two finger-breadth anterolateral to the tip of the greater trochanter and parallelly extended to about 1 cm below the lesser trochanter along the longitudinal axis of the affected limb, was made on the anterolateral thigh (Fig. 1). Following dissection of the fascia, the tensor fasciae latae is identified and obtusely split along the proximal axis of the femur. Next, part of the tensor fascia lata and gluteus medius were pulled to the posterolateral with anteromedial placement of the remaining tensor fascia lata and the rectus femoris. With Hohmann or Cobra retractors placed on the medial side, the joint capsule and intertrochanteric line were then exposed (Supplementary Fig. 1). Subsequently, a capsulotomy along the intertrochanteric line was performed to view the femoral neck (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1), and immediately, a mass of hematoma gushed out. In all cases, proximal rotation and fracture fragments firmly driven into each other were observed. After the release of the hematoma at the fracture site, two 2.5 mm Kirschner needles were inserted into the femoral head to control the movement of the proximal fracture (Fig. 1).

During the reduction procedure, the needles functioned as an auxiliary joystick, allowing manipulation of the proximal fragment to facilitate separation from the distal femur by holding the proximal fracture in place while slightly rotating and pulling the distal femur inward and outward. Then, the surgeon rotates the proximal fracture in horizontal, sagittal, or coronal positions, while the assistant rotates and extends the distal femur in the opposite direction so that the proximal and distal fractures are aligned in a three-dimensional direction. The reduction is held in a stable position by the assistant while the surgeon provisionally fixes the fracture using pins under fluoroscopy. Subsequently, a standardized anteroposterior (AP) and lateral fluoroscopy was taken to evaluate reduction (Fig. 2). If acceptable, 3 cannulated screws are scheduled to stabilize the fracture in a parallel inverted triangle configuration with the aid of 3 guide pins. When the screw reaches the fracture lines, the pins are withdrawn, and the allograft bone, BMP-2, and allograft bone are sequentially implanted through a hollow channel of screws to expedite bone union. This bone graft procedure is what we call “Sandwich Mode” (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Movie S_01–03). Quantitative bone grafting was carried out based on preoperative CT evaluation: comminuted femoral neck fractures received implants of 1 mg of BMP-2 and 2 cm3 of allograft, while non-comminuted fractures were implanted with 1 mg of BMP-2 and 1 cm3 of allograft. Finally, the stability of fracture fixation and the alignment of the screws were rechecked using fluoroscopy in the AP and lateral planes. After hemostasis of the incision edge and muscle, the fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin are closed in sequence. Detailed trajectories of the treatment process of the affected limb of a patient were shown in Fig. 3.

Postoperatively, a low dose of heparin (5000 IU) was prescribed for at least 3 weeks. Immediate post-surgical mobilization of the affected limb and isometric contraction muscle exercises were encouraged. Gradually, partial weight bearing with crutches was initiated at 3 months after surgery. When clinical and radiological evidence confirm fracture healing, full weight-bearing activities were permitted. All patients were followed for at least 2 years.

The quality of reduction according to Garden index [20], fracture healing time, nonunion, and AVN was recorded and evaluated as primary outcomes. Harris Hip Score (HHS) was recorded and assessed as functional outcomes [21]. Other complications, including infection, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), and paresthesia of the skin were also noted.

Results

Fracture reduction was successfully accomplished in all cases through the described intertrochanteric line approach. Intraoperatively, all patients underwent "sandwich mode" bone graft. The mean operative duration was 73.46 min (range, 60–95 min). Garden Index evaluation showed that 37/52 (71.15%) patients achieved grade-I, and 15/52 (28.85%) achieved grade-II reduction on the two plain radiographs (AP and lateral views) (Table 2).

Intraoperative observation, postoperative outcomes, and complications of 52 patients (n = 52)

All patients were followed with an average of 32.37 ± 7.49 months (range, 24–56 months). Radiographically, unions were achieved in 52/52 (100%) patients with a mean duration of 4.35 ± 0.85 months (range, 3–6 months). Unfortunately, three (5.77%) cases, a 21-year-old man, a 50-year-old man, and a 39-year-old man, all suffered a femoral neck fracture from a height fall and subsequently achieved Garden Grade I reduction as well as secure internal fixation, yet developed avascular necrosis (AVN) at 23, 18, and 13 months of follow-up, respectively. They were finally re-operated on for total hip arthroplasty. Functional outcomes of 52 hips were evaluated by HHS, of which 39/52 (75.00%) were excellent, 7/52 (13.46%) were good, 3/52 (5.77%) were fair, and 3/52 (5.77%) who developed AVN were poor. Therefore, the rate of excellent and good results was up to 88.46% (Table 2).

Additionally, of the 52 patients, the neck-shaft angle of the affected side was restored to a mean of 128.83° (range, 119.21°–138.32°). Notably, no significant difference was observed between the injured side and the healthy side (mean: 128.75°; range: 115.43°–137.37°) (Table 2).

No other complications, such as infection, and deep vein thrombosis (DVT), occurred in those cases. Besides, no positive Trendelenburg test was found in all patients. Moreover, none of the patients complained of thigh skin paresthesia.

Discussion

In this study, we describe a novel intertrochanteric line approach, a true neurovascular-sparing approach associated with avoidance of nerve traction and vascular ligation, for the treatment of irreducible femoral neck fractures. This open approach shows many advantages, including capsular decompression for vascular recanalization, monitoring fracture reduction under direct vision for the detection of bone defects, and the use of a single incision for reduction and internal fixation implantation. Additionally, we designed a new bone graft model of “sandwich mode” with sequential implantation of allograft bone—BMP2- allograft bone at fracture sites to motivate fracture healing. Patients with irreducible femoral neck fractures can achieve encouraging results via this approach combined with "sandwich mode" bone grafting, with a lower incidence of nonunion and AVN than reported in the literature.

Femoral neck fracture is also known as “unsolved bone fracture” because of the higher prevalence of nonunion and AVN [22, 23]. According to current consensus, the quality of reduction determines the outcomes of femoral neck fracture [24, 25]. The misalignment of the fracture causes continuous tension in nourishing vessels of the femoral neck, resulting in arteries' spasm and blockage [7]. Moreover, the large shear force caused by poor reduction prevents the reconstruction of trabeculae that increase the risk of AVN [26, 27]. Clinically, this reduction dilemma of IFNF may result from the complete disconnection of the proximal fragment from the distal femur, creating a "floating" femoral head, or a moving whole caused by the striking of the rotated proximal segment and the distal [18]. Although various closed methods are reported, the complication rate of IFNF has not been significantly reduced [2, 18]. To our knowledge, there are few reports of open reduction for the treatment of IFNF. Unlike other studies, in our series, open reduction was proceeded immediately once IFNF was diagnosed; even though this has been suspected with incremental risk of infection. Studies have found that the displacement of the fracture leads to intra-articular hematoma, resulting in a significant increase in intra-articular pressure, up to 19.95kpa [28], which directly compresses the blood vessels in the femoral neck and reduces the blood supply to the femoral head [29]. Intra-operatively, we observed that a large amount of hematocele drained out after capsulotomy, which meant that the capsular pressure was relieved. Resultantly, anatomical reduction and union were successfully achieved in all patients. Despite the long-term complication of AVN occurring in three cases (5.77%), the data was significantly lower than other literature for Garden Ⅲ/Ⅳ and even for Garden I/II fractures [18, 30]. Therefore, intracapsular decompression is beneficial to reduction and reduces the incidence of complications [31]. This is supported by a clinical investigation that open reduction with internal fixation (ORIF) in the treatment of Garden III and IV fractures results in a lower incidence of nonunion and AVN [4]. Sometimes, although a perfect reduction may be achieved by closed procedures, further vascular injury and increased intracapsular pressure resulting from multiple operations raise the risk of AVN and nonunion. A meta-analysis based on clinical studies concluded that DFNF should be prioritized for ORIF [3].

Surgical approaches to the hip, including Smith-Peterson (S-P), Direct Anterior approach (DAA), Watson-Jones (W-J), and other modified approaches, are an ongoing area of debate among orthopedic surgeons. Moreover, available evidence does not support an advantage of one approach regarding the quality of reduction attained or the final surgical outcome. The S-P approach, the anterosuperior iliac spine and parallel to the palpable interval between the tensor fascia lata and the sartorius, was first described by Smith-Petersen [32]. Although providing superior visualization of the femoral neck, the S-P approach presents a long incision and may necessitate the amputation of the rectus femoris, carrying a high risk of intraoperative neurovascular injury and postoperative heterotopic ossification [13]. Hueter interval, a neurovascular interval between the tensor fascia lata and the sartorius, was first depicted by Hueter in 1881 [33]. Smith first reported on the use of the Hueter interval for hip arthroplasty in 1949 [34]. Subsequently, the Hueter interval has been widely used to treat hip fractures and is known as the DAA. In contrast to the S-P approach, the DAA leaves out a partial incision in the iliac spine, resulting in a smaller surgical incision and less tissue dissection. Since the DAA is anatomically adjacent to the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN) and the branches of the lateral circumflex femoral artery (LCFA), several observational studies have shown that the use of the DAA often results in injury to the main LFCN trunk near the medial margin of the tensor fascia lata with abnormal sensory function [35, 36], nonetheless, exposure of the rectus femoral aponeurosis leads to the injury and ligation of the LCFA, above which makes it technically difficult and has a long learning curve. Alternatively, the DAA and S-P approaches require an additional incision for internal fixation implants. The W-J approach, between the tensor fasciae latae and the gluteus medius muscles, outlined by Watson–Jones, allows fracture reduction and implant placement through a single incision [5]. It exhibits inferior exposure of the femoral neck and may be preferred to address basicervical fracture patterns. It is also associated with superior gluteal nerves injury and Trendelenburg gait [16]. In this study, we introduced this novel intertrochanteric line approach, a true neurovascular-sparing plane between the Hueter and W-J interval formed through blunt separation of muscle fibers of the tensor fascia lata that avoids the neurovascular injury involved in the DAA and W-J approach. Theoretically and clinically, this approach, about 6–8 cm in length, is a relatively safe surgical incision avoiding nerve damage and vascular ligation reported by the S-P or DAA. Compared with the W-J approach, the intertrochanteric line approach is more anterolateral and provides a more excellent exposure of the femoral neck. Furthermore, distinct from the W-J approach which necessitates an incision traversing the greater trochanter, the intertrochanteric line approach, entailing a 6–8 cm incision situated between the greater and lesser trochanters, seemingly embodies a more minimally invasive technique. Aside from safety, the intertrochanteric line approach completes the internal fixation implants without a second incision compared to the DAA. The capsule is incised along the intertrochanteric line instead of capsulotomy in a "T" shape to minimize the injury of capsular vessels. Though the reported incidence of LFCN injury ranges from 1 to 67% [37–39] in the DAA and positive Trendelenburg test about 1.8% to 31% in the W-J approach [16, 40, 41], no Trendelenburg gait and paresthesia were observed in our cases. In addition to surgical visualization and fracture reduction, the intertrochanteric line approach also affords minor tissue damage, lowers complications, and has a shorter learning curve.

In order to motivate fracture healing, we designed a new "sandwich mode" bone grafting with sequential implantation of allograft bone—BMP2—allograft bone at fracture sites. Radiological and clinical studies have identified a bone defect in DFNF that interferes with the healing progress; for such cases, bone grafts have been commonly used to address these issues [17, 42, 43]. Advancements in allografts have made them a favorable alternative to other grafts and exhibit good osteogenic effects [42]. Besides, clinical practice and previous literature reveal that the application of BMP-2 can further optimize patient outcomes. In our clinical work, the "sandwich mode" bone grafting with the synergistic effect of osteogenesis and osteoinduction can solve this clinical challenge caused by DFNF to allow for encouraging results. This may explain the lower prevalence of nonunion and AVN in our case series compared to other studies.

Given the routine use of BMP-2, its complications and cost-effectiveness must be taken into consideration. As the only FDA-approved osteoinductive factor, BMP-2 exhibits potent bone-inducing properties and is commonly employed as a bone repair adjunct. Extensive clinical studies and animal experiments have demonstrated that BMP-2 promotes osteogenic differentiation of stem cells and enhances bone healing, with no reported complications such as infection or rejection reactions during the bone repair process [44, 45]. However, a small number of cases have reported that BMP-2 may lead to heterotopic ossification. We believe that, compared to its significant benefits in promoting the healing of femoral neck fractures, this complication is relatively minor. Furthermore, in terms of cost-effectiveness, the current market price for 1 mg of BMP-2 is approximately $470. In comparison, if non-union occurs in femoral neck fractures, subsequent surgical interventions become necessary. For instance, at our center, a revision surgery utilizing microsurgical bone flap transport to address femoral neck non-union costs at least $3,500, while hip arthroplasty would require a minimum of $5,000. These costs for revision surgery far exceed the $470 price of BMP-2, and do not even include the additional expenses associated with postoperative care for multiple surgeries due to non-union. If the use of BMP-2 can reduce the incidence of femoral neck fracture non-union, it would not only decrease potential hospitalization costs but also alleviate the psychological burden on patients. Therefore, from a comprehensive cost–benefit perspective, we propose that BMP-2 can be routinely incorporated into the clinical management of femoral neck fractures.

Our study has some limitations. First, our series included a small number of patients and was a retrospective single-center study. Second, the series is also noncomparative and has some selection bias in terms of treatment. Future studies comparing this approach with others are also warranted. Third, using HHS as an indicator of hip function is subjective because of differences in patients’ pain tolerance.

Conclusions

For patients with IFNF, anatomic reduction through a surgical approach should be performed decisively. The intertrochanteric line approach has advantages of safety, minor tissue damage, excellent surgical visualization, and a short learning curve; hence, it can be a viable alternative to other approaches for the treatment of IFNF and suitable for promotion in primary hospitals. The "sandwich mode" bone grafting fully embodies the characteristics of osteogenesis and osteoinduction, which combined with the intertrochanteric line approach yield favorable mid-term outcomes.
