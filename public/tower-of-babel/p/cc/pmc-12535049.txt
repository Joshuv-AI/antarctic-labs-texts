Nasopharyngeal carcinoma in sub-Saharan Africa: clinicopathological profiles, patterns of care, and survival outcomes of patients treated at a leading radiotherapy center
Authors: Kofi Adesi Kyei, Joseph Daniels, Tigist Tefera Degefu, Andrew Yaw Nyantakyi, Judith Naa Odey Tackie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn sub-Saharan Africa, the clinicopathological features, management strategies, and survival outcomes of nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) remain underreported, posing challenges to improving patient care and treatment outcomes. The aim of the study was to evaluate the clinicopathological profiles, patterns of care, and survival of patients with NPC treated with curative intent at a major radiotherapy center in sub-Saharan Africa.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed 83 patients with histopathologically confirmed NPC treated over a 10-year period. Data on demographics, clinical features, management, and treatment outcomes were extracted from patients’ medical records. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan-Meier curves, whilst associations between clinicopathological variables& survival were assessed using Cox proportional hazard models.ResultsThe mean age was 32.8 ± 18.3 years whilst the male-to-female ratio was 2.3:1. In all, 8.4% had EBV-positive NPC, whilst 9.6% were tobacco smokers. Most participants (94%) had a good performance status. Overall, 38.6%, 26.5%, and 20.5% were diagnosed with stages IVB, IVA and III NPC respectively. In all, 75.9% received definitive chemo-radiation without either induction or adjuvant chemotherapy. A total of 78.3% were treated to a standard radiotherapy dose of 70 Gy in 35 fractions delivered over 7 weeks, whilst other patients were treated to lower doses of 68 Gy in 34 fractions (10.8%), 66 Gy in 33 fractions (4.8%) or 64 Gy in 32 fractions to meet critical organ constraints. There were low acute (25.3%) and long-term (15.7%) treatment-induced toxicity rates. The 1-, 3- and 5-year disease-free survival rates were 75%, 45%, and 25%, respectively whereas overall survival rates at the same time points were 90%, 65%, and 35%, respectively.ConclusionAdvanced-stage diagnosis remains a major challenge due to delayed detection and limited healthcare access. Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a unique malignancy originating from the epithelial lining of the nasopharynx that is distinguished from other head and neck cancers (HNC) by its epidemiology, etiology, clinical behavior, and therapeutic response [1]. Additionally, NPC exhibits a significant geographical variation in incidence with the highest prevalence observed in Southeast Asia, particularly southern China, where incidence rates exceed 30 cases per 100,000 annually [2]. However, NPC remains rare in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), with an overall incidence of less than 1 per 100,000 [3]. Despite its rarity, NPC represents a disproportionately high percentage of HNC in certain SSA regions, emphasizing the need for region-specific research and tailored healthcare strategies [4].

The etiology of NPC is multifactorial, involving a complex interplay of genetic predisposition, environmental influences, and viral oncogenesis. The Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) is a pivotal oncogenic driver, particularly in endemic regions where nearly all cases are EBV-positive [5]. Dietary habits, such as consumption of salted fish containing nitrosamines, are also implicated, particularly in endemic regions [6]. In non-endemic areas, sporadic cases are more frequently associated with lifestyle factors such as tobacco and alcohol use.

Clinically, NPC often manifests with nonspecific symptoms, including nasal obstruction, epistaxis, and cervical lymphadenopathy. These subtle initial symptoms frequently lead to delayed diagnoses, with many patients presenting at advanced stages [7]. Also, NPC has a high metastatic potential, which further complicates its management. Common metastatic sites include the lungs, liver, and bone. The aggressive nature of NPC underscores the importance of timely diagnosis and effective management [8]. Intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) has revolutionized treatment, offering enhanced precision, improved local control, and better survival outcomes, particularly in high-resource settings [9]. However, in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including SSA, limited access to IMRT and systemic therapies presents significant barriers to optimal care. In SSA, these challenges are further exacerbated by inadequate diagnostic infrastructure, resulting in poorer outcomes compared to high-income regions [10].

In Ghana, NPC is the second most frequently diagnosed HNC, accounting for 1.2% to 1.3% of all cancers [11]. It predominantly affects younger individuals, with 34.5% of cases occurring in patients aged 10 to 19 years [12]. Despite its clinical significance, data on the clinicopathological characteristics and survival outcomes of NPC in Ghana are sparse. Limited awareness and diagnostic delays contribute to late-stage presentation, with approximately 47.6% of patients presenting at stage IV [12]. Advances in diagnostic imaging, radiotherapy techniques, and systemic therapies have dramatically improved NPC outcomes worldwide. In high-income countries, the availability of IMRT has improved five-year disease-specific survival (DSS) rates to over 85% in endemic regions [13]. Globally, survival outcomes for NPC vary significantly depending on geographic location, stage at diagnosis, and available treatment modalities. In SSA, survival rates remain poorly documented but are believed to be substantially lower due to delayed diagnoses and limited treatment options [14].

Concurrent chemoradiation is the cornerstone for the treatment of locally advanced NPC, with meta-analyses confirming its superiority over radiotherapy alone [13]. The integration of induction or adjuvant chemotherapy further enhances tumor control and survival. However, the implementation of these advancements in SSA is constrained by infrastructural and financial challenges [10]. Addressing these challenges requires a nuanced understanding of the unique factors influencing NPC outcomes in the sub region. The study sought to address critical knowledge gaps in NPC management in SSA. Specifically, the study aimed to evaluate the clinicopathological profiles and patterns of care of patients with NPC who were treated over a 10-year period at the leading radiotherapy and oncology centre in Ghana. Additionally, the study assessed survival outcomes, including OS and disease-free survival (DFS) rates, and analyzed the impact of clinicopathological characteristics on patients’ survival.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective cohort study with a descriptive correlational design involving patients managed for NPC between January 2012 and December 2021 at a leading radiotherapy and oncology centre in SSA. This facility is uniquely equipped for the management of NPC, operating both a 1.25 MeV Cobalt-60 teletherapy machine and a 6MV linear accelerator. The center’s reputation, coupled with its strategic location, underscores its suitability as an ideal setting for this retrospective cohort study.

Participants

Study participants comprised patients diagnosed clinically by using imaging materials such as CT scan and MRI and histopathologically confirmed NPC who were treated with curative intent with definitive radiation/chemoradiation with or without induction or adjuvant chemotherapy. The tumor was staged using WHO tumor staging and AJCC 7th and 8th edition TNM staging methods for the entire patients. Patients received three cycles of Cisplatin chemotherapy on days 1, 22 and 43 of radiotherapy. Patients with HNC of other subsites who were also treated with curative chemoradiation were not included in the study. Patients with confirmed NPC who underwent palliative treatment were also excluded, likewise patients with NPC who were managed with best supportive care only. Patients with significant missing/unrecoverable clinicopathological data were also excluded. Total population sampling was used to select participants for the study. Out of 238 patients screened, only 83 were found to be eligible.

Study variables

Independent variables of the study included patients’ age, sex, stage at the time of diagnosis, and histology as well as the prescribed radiotherapy dose & technique used. Dependent variables were OS and DFS rates. Disease-free survival was defined as the time interval (measured in months) from the date of first presentation without evidence of disease after treatment completion to the first documented recurrence of NPC, death, or the date of the last follow-up for patients who remained disease-free. Overall survival was calculated from the date of confirmed histological diagnosis of NPC to the date of death from any cause.

Data collection

A structured, standardized data collection tool was used to retrospectively extract patients’ data from hospital-based medical records. Data extracted included demographic information, clinical features, treatment details, and follow-up outcomes such as the date of last follow-up, date of recurrence or death. Data quality and completeness were assessed to minimize biases.

Data management and analysis

The data collected were systematically coded, cleaned, and entered into the SPSS software for statistical analysis. Descriptive and inferential statistical methods were employed to analyze the data comprehensively. Descriptive statistics included measures of central tendency (mean) and measures of variability (standard deviation). Frequency distributions were generated to summarize categorical data. Data relationships were assessed using multivariate analysis to explore potential associations. The results were presented using tables and graphs to enhance interpretability. Descriptive methods were applied to summarize clinicopathologic and treatment-related factors. Survival rates were estimated using Kaplan–Meier curves, with comparisons between survival curves tested for significance using the two-sided log-rank test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted in accordance with standard ethical principles, and adhered to all relevant institutional and national research guidelines. The consent to participate in the study by participants was waived by the Institutional review board of the University of Ghana, School of Biomedical and Allied Health Sciences (cal and Allied Health Sciences (SBAHS/AA/RAD//10943325/2022-2023).

Treatment related characteristics

In all, 21.7% were treated on a cobalt-60 teletherapy machine whereas 78.3% received treated with a 6MV linear accelerator. Three radiotherapy techniques were employed in the definitive treatment of the patients with NPC, namely; two-dimensional (2D) (21.7%), three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3D-CRT) (45.8%), and IMRT (32.5%).Most of the patients received concurrent Cisplatin-based chemotherapy (98.8%), whereas only 1.2% received radiotherapy alone (without chemotherapy). Also, 22.9% received two to three cycles of induction chemotherapy prior to undergoing concurrent chemoradiation whereas none received adjuvant chemotherapy. Due to hematological toxicity or performance status, treatment non-compliance was witnessed in 3.6% of patients who underwent induction chemotherapy. In all, 75.9% received definitive chemoradiation without either induction or adjuvant chemotherapy as summarized in Table 3. A total of 78.3% were treated to a standard radiotherapy dose of 70 Gy in 35 fractions delivered over 7 weeks. However, the other patients were treated to lower doses of 68 Gy in 34 fractions (10.8%), 66 Gy in 33 fractions (4.8%) or 64 Gy in 32 fractions (2.5%) to meet critical organ constraints; and in 3.6% of the cases treatment breaks ranging from 3 to 7 days occurred due to toxicities. For those patients who were treated with 2D/3D techniques, the treatment was delivered in multiple phases to minimize the risk of exceeding the spinal cord’s tolerance dose. In the first phase, the treatment volume included the primary tumor along with the bilateral cervical lymph nodes by using 3–4 fields, receiving a total dose of 46 Gy administered in 23 fractions. In the second phase, the spinal cord was shielded and a reduced treatment field was used to deliver additional dose of 18 to 24 Gy depending on the individual case. The IMRT treatment delivery was employed with different treatment volumes (GTV, CTV, and PTV). The gross tumor volume (GTV) was the palpable or visible part of the tumor. The clinical target volumes (CTV) had 3 categories such as the high risk CTV included the gross tumor volume with additional margin of 0.5–10 mm; the intermediate risk CTV covered areas considered at risk of microscopic disease; and the low risk CTV covered elective nodal regions. The planning target volumes (PTV) were generated by applying 5–10 mm margin to respective CTVs accounting for institutional uncertainties. The treatment planning was performed using the Eclipse treatment planning system with Varian Medical System version 13.6, and plans typically involved 7–9 radiation fields to achieve optimal dose distribution while sparing surrounding normal tissues. Acute and Late toxicities experienced by the patients were stated based on RTOG (Radiation Therapy Oncology Group) and CTCAE (Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events) Version 4 criteria. Overall patients, Acute toxicities such as Grade 3 Dermatitis (moist desquamation in areas other than skin folds; bleeding induced by minor trauma), Grade 3 Mucositis (sever pain interfering with oral intake), Grade 2 to 3 Dysphagia (by experiencing moderate to severe pain during eating/swallowing) was seen in 25.3% of the cases and 3.6% of the patients were forced to interrupt the treatment for several days or a week due to severe Anemia (blood transfusion indicated). Whereas Late Toxicities like Grade 3 Xerostomia (inability to adequately alignment orally; tube feeding) and Grade 2 Fibromas (stiffness on the neck and trismus (limited jaw opening) were experienced by 15.7% of the cases.

Discussion

The study aimed to fill knowledge gaps in NPC management in a limited-resource setting by evaluating the clinicopathological profiles, treatment patterns, and survival outcomes of patients treated over a decade at Ghana’s leading oncology center. The mean age was 32.8 ± 18.3 years whilst the male-to-female ratio was 2.3:1. In all, 44% were students, whilst 8.4% had EBV-positive NPC. Also, 4.8% had a positive family history of cancer, whilst 19.3% had history of alcohol intake. Only 9.6% were tobacco smokers. Most of the patients (94%) had a good ECOG performance status (0–1). Overall, 38.6%, 26.5%, and 20.5% were diagnosed with stages IVB, IVA and III NPC respectively. Most patients (63.9%) were diagnosed with non-keratinizing undifferentiated SCC. In total, 78.3% were treated with the 6MV linear accelerator using either 3D-CRT or IMRT (32.5%) techniques. In all, 75.9% received definitive chemo radiation without either induction or adjuvant chemotherapy. A total of 78.3% were treated to a standard radiotherapy dose of 70 Gy in 35 fractions delivered over 7 weeks. The other patients were treated to lower doses of 68 Gy in 34 fractions (10.8%), 66 Gy in 33 fractions (4.8%) or 64 Gy in 32 fractions to meet critical organ constraints. There were low acute (25.3%) and long-term (15.7%) treatment-induced toxicity rates. The mean DFS time was 32.4 ± 27.3 months with 75%, 45%, and 25% DFS rates at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively. The mean OS time was 43.5 ± 27.1 months with 90%, 65%, and 35% OS rates at 1, 3, and 5 years respectively. Multivariate analysis of tumor histology and radiotherapy technique showed a significant association with both DFS (p = 0.013 and 0.0001 respectively) and OS (p = 0.0001 and 0.0001 respectively). The 3D-CRT group was associated with the highest survival probability whilst patients who were treated with the IMRT technique had intermediate survival outcomes compared to those treated with 2D radiotherapy who had the poorest survival outcomes. Also, N-k Undifferentiated SCC was associated with the worst survival whereas Differentiated SCC had slightly better survival outcomes with N-k Differentiated SCC showing intermediate survival.

The mean age was 32.8 years, which is consistent with findings showing that NPC in SSA tends to affect younger populations compared to high-incidence regions like East Asia, where the median age at diagnosis is typically between 45 and 55 years [5, 15]. Previous research at the study site, involving patients managed in 2004 and 2017, reported mean ages of 36.7 and 34.1 years, respectively, with 60.4% of the patients ≤ 40 years [11, 16]. The younger age at presentation in SSA has also been attributed to differences in genetic susceptibility, EBV exposure, and environmental factors [17]. The finding that a considerable proportion were students (44%), highlights significant educational, socioeconomic, and psychological consequences. The diagnosis and treatment of NPC often requires frequent hospital visits, long treatment durations (radiotherapy, chemotherapy), and recovery periods, potentially leading to academic setbacks such as missed classes, poor academic performance, and potential school dropouts [18]. Educational disruptions may also result in delayed graduation or early withdrawal from school, limiting future employment prospects and reducing earning potential. Given the high proportion of affected patients being students, educational institutions should integrate cancer awareness programs to promote early detection. Specialized academic support can help affected students keep up with their studies. NPC in students jeopardizes education, imposes financial hardships, and affects psychological well-being [19]. Addressing these issues requires a multi-sectoral approach involving healthcare providers, educational institutions, and policymakers to mitigate the disease’s impact on young patients.

The observed male predominance (male-to-female ratio of 2.3:1) aligns with global patterns, reflecting a higher susceptibility in males likely due to genetic, hormonal, and environmental factors [6]. Similar male to female ratios of patients with NPC have been reported in Nigeria (2:1) [12] and Ghana (2:1 [11] and 2.1:1 [16].The male predominance may be attributed to hormonal influences, differential EBV immune responses, and occupational or environmental exposures that increase risk [20]. Additionally, exposure to potential risk factors such as tobacco smoke, industrial pollutants, and carcinogenic dietary habits (e.g., salted fish and preserved foods), which have been linked to NPC, may be more prevalent among men [21]. The higher prevalence of NPC in men could also be indicative of gender-based differences in healthcare-seeking behaviors, where women may face more barriers to accessing diagnostic and treatment services [22].

Only 4.8% had a positive family history of cancer, suggesting that genetic predisposition plays a relatively minor role in NPC development in this population. While familial clustering of NPC has been well-documented in endemic regions, such as Southeast Asia, where genetic susceptibility interacts with Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) infection [23], the low prevalence of familial cancer history in this study may indicate that environmental and lifestyle factors play a more significant role in NPC development in SSA. Additionally, underreporting due to limited cancer awareness and poor documentation of family medical history in SSA could contribute to this observation [24]. A history of alcohol intake was reported in 19.3% of the patients, while only 9.6% were tobacco smokers. Unlike other HNC, NPC has a weaker association with tobacco and alcohol use, but these risk factors may still play a synergistic role in disease progression, particularly in non-endemic regions [25]. Histologically, non-keratinizing undifferentiated carcinoma was the most common cell type, which was observed in 63.9% of the patients, consistent with data of the World Health Organization that classify this subtype as the most common globally, particularly in endemic areas [26].

EBV is strongly associated with NPC, and has been implicated in increasing NPC risk at a younger age [27].The low proportion of EBV-positive NPC (8.4%) contrasts with endemic regions like Southeast Asia, where EBV positivity exceeds 90%, suggesting possible regional epidemiological variations or under diagnosis [28]. One possible explanation for the low EBV positivity rate is geographic variation in EBV subtypes and their oncogenic potential. Studies have shown that EBV subtypes prevalent in SSA (such as EBV type 2) may have a weaker oncogenic effect compared to EBV type 1, which is dominant in endemic regions and strongly linked to NPC [29].

The high prevalence of advanced-stage disease (IVB: 38.6%) is reflective of delayed presentation, which is a critical challenge in low-resource settings. In SSA, delayed presentation of NPC is primarily attributed to several interconnected barriers, including limited healthcare access, lack of awareness, and diagnostic challenges [30]. Many individuals in the region face geographic and financial barriers to accessing healthcare services, especially in rural areas where oncology services and radiotherapy centers may be few and far between [31]. This situation leads to longer treatment delays, where patients may seek care only after the disease has progressed to advanced stages [24]. Additionally, low levels of cancer awareness contribute significantly to the delay in seeking medical attention. Many individuals may misinterpret the early symptoms of NPC, such as nasal obstruction, ear pain, and swelling, as minor illnesses, and thus, do not seek timely medical care. In many limited-resource settings, there is also a general lack of education on the importance of early cancer detection, resulting in individuals presenting only when symptoms become more severe or debilitating. Policies aimed at enhancing early referral systems, increasing the availability of diagnostic tools, and promoting public awareness about NPC symptoms are critical to addressing the delays in diagnosis.

Most patients (94%) presented with a good ECOG performance status (0–1), indicating that most individuals were in relatively good physical condition at diagnosis. An ECOG score of 0–1 indicates that the individual can perform normal daily activities, with only slight limitations in physical activity. Patients with good ECOG scores are more likely to be eligible for aggressive treatment options such as radiotherapy and chemotherapy, which are crucial for managing advanced NPC. However, while the good ECOG status in this cohort may suggest that patients were relatively fit for treatment, it also highlights a critical gap in timely diagnosis. The fact that the majority of patients were in good physical condition despite their presentation with advanced-stage disease suggests that disease progression may have been insidious, with no apparent symptoms or mild symptoms that were easily ignored or misinterpreted. This further underscores the need for early screening programs, as individuals with good performance status may still be suffering from the effects of advanced disease, which requires prompt intervention to improve prognosis [32]. Furthermore, the fact that most patients had ECOG scores of 0–1 suggests that most were likely diagnosed during a phase when NPC symptoms were not severely debilitating, which may also be a result of better treatment options and good supportive care once diagnosis is made. However, the delayed diagnosis, coupled with advanced-stage disease, means that despite having a good performance status, these patients are likely to experience worse outcomes compared to those diagnosed earlier.

The predominant use of a linear accelerator (78.3%) and advanced radiotherapy techniques such as 3D-CRT (45.8%) and IMRT (32.5%) signifies progress in radiotherapy capabilities in SSA, where access to advanced radiotherapy technology has historically been limited [20]. The IMRT technique allows for more precise dose delivery to the tumor while sparing critical organs such as the brainstem, spinal cord, and salivary glands [25]. However, the continued reliance on cobalt-60 machines (21.7%) highlights persistent infrastructural constraints. The relatively low proportion of IMRT utilization (compared to high-income countries) may be due to cost constraints, and equipment availability. Expanding access to IMRT technology and training healthcare professionals in its optimal use would facilitate better outcomes for NPC patients in the region.

The near-universal use of concurrent cisplatin-based chemotherapy (98.8%) demonstrates adherence to global treatment standards for locally advanced NPC. In high-risk patients, induction or adjuvant chemotherapy can provide additional benefits by reducing distant metastases and improving local control. The absence of adjuvant chemotherapy in this cohort may limit potential survival benefits observed in other settings [33]. The low utilization of induction and adjuvant chemotherapy may reflect several challenges such as resource constraints, high cost of additional chemotherapy, toxicity concerns and limited supportive care.

In all, 78.3% of patients received the standard NPC radiotherapy dose of 70 Gy in 35 fractions over 7 weeks, which aligns with global treatment guidelines [13]. However, some patients received lower doses, including 68 Gy (10.8%), 66 Gy (4.8%), and 64 Gy (2.4%), to meet critical organ dose constraints. The reduction in prescribed radiotherapy doses in select cases reflects a pragmatic approach to balancing efficacy and toxicity which is a fundamental principle of radiotherapy planning. Lowering the prescribed dose is often necessary when tumors are located close to radiosensitive structures such as the optic chiasm, brainstem, or spinal cord. While dose reduction helps minimize severe toxicities, it may also result in slightly reduced tumor control, particularly in locally advanced cases. The study reported low acute (25.3%) and long-term (15.7%) treatment-induced toxicity rates, which is an encouraging finding. The low toxicity rates observed may be attributed to good performance status at diagnosis (94% ECOG 0–1), lower radiotherapy doses for some patients, and the utilization of IMRT in a subset of patients. It is also possible that toxicity rates were underreported due to limited follow-up, inadequate documentation, or lack of systematic toxicity assessment, common in resource-constrained centers [28]. Future research should include comprehensive long-term toxicity assessments to better understand the impact of NPC treatment on quality of life and functional outcomes.

The 3-year OS and DFS rates were 65% and 45%. These survival metrics are lower than those reported in high-income countries, where 3-year OS rates often exceed 80% for similar cohorts [34]. The Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed significant differences in survival based on radiotherapy technique and tumor histology. The superior outcomes observed with 3D-CRT over 2D radiotherapy align with findings from other LMICs where 3D-CRT has been associated with improved loco-regional control and survival [35]. Similarly, the poor prognosis of undifferentiated SCC compared to differentiated subtypes mirrors global trends and underscores the aggressive biology of undifferentiated tumors.A Nigerian study assessed treatment outcomes of NPC patients treated with CRT, the overall DFS rates were 67% and 46% at 1 year and 2 years, respectively, and further showed a significant association between the stage of the disease at presentation and the overall 2-year survival. In particular, the study reported longer survival of NPC patients with stage II disease than those with stage III and IV, with a 2-year survival rate of 58%, 52%, and 20%, respectively. The survival rates of patients based on the three different histological types were also different. Patients with non-keratinizing differentiated carcinoma had a higher overall DFS rate (65%), followed by patients with keratinizing SCC (51%) and non-keratinizing undifferentiated carcinoma (40%). This study was relatively comprehensive in addressing the clinic-pathological characteristics of patients determining the OS rates [12]. This study presented a long-term survival outcome of NPC patients treated using 3D-CRT which was followed by IMRT. Possible reasons for this can be the short-term treatment practice using IMRT technique in the hospital, which was first introduced and used in 2019, and maximum survival period of still alive patients were not considered statistically in the study so that the maximum survival period considered couldn’t exceed 48 months for those patients treated with IMRT; it was relatively recent addition to the institutional treatment protocols which may have involved a learning curve relating to target volume delineation, treatment planning and quality assurance practices and early IMRT cases may not have fully benefited from peer-review, adaptive planning or standardized protocols that were late adopted, which may need further investigation in the future. IMRT, although not the most utilized technique, was associated with better survival outcomes compared to 2D radiotherapy. This finding aligns with international evidence showing IMRT’s superiority in sparing critical organs and improving tumor control [9].

Histological subtypes of NPC, including keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma (KSCC) and non-keratinizing carcinoma (NKC), have been linked to distinct clinical characteristics and survival outcomes. According to a study conducted by Mila et al. [36], best survival outcome was achieved treating non-keratinizing undifferentiated carcinoma followed by non-keratinizing differentiated carcinoma. A study by Zhang et al. [37] found that patients with non-keratinizing SCC had better 5-year DFS and OS rates than those with keratinizing SCC [36, 37]. Also, in this study the survival rates of the patients based on the three different histological types were also different. Patients with non-keratinizing undifferentiated carcinoma had a higher overall DFS rates, followed by patients with non-keratinizing differentiated SCC. Most of the patients in this study presented with late-stage disease, 85.5% with stage-III and above and only one patient had stage-I disease. The stage at presentation is considered the most important prognostic factor of NPC. The mean OS time for stage I, II, III, IVA, and IVB were 56, 50, 45, 44, and 39 months, respectively, which indicated relatively better OS for early disease stage. In the present study, the log-rank test showed a significant association between DFS & OS and RT dose with p-value < 0.018 and < 0.01, respectively. The survival rates were improved with the use of increased radiation dose for NPC patients. This is supported by a study conducted by Kazemian et al. [38], which showed that patients who received total planned radiotherapy dose of at least 70 Gy demonstrated improved OS. Also, Mila et al. [36], demonstrated that a lower total RT dose was a significant prognostic factor for worse DSS (p-value < 0.0001).

Limitations

The retrospective design may introduce selection bias. Also, the relatively small sample size limits the generalizability of our findings to other SSA settings. The limited availability of EBV testing may underestimate its role in NPC pathogenesis in this cohort.

Conclusions

The low EBV positivity rate underscores regional variations in NPC etiology, suggesting that EBV-independent mechanisms may play a greater role in SSA compared to endemic regions. The high prevalence of advanced-stage NPC (IVB) highlights the serious challenges related to delayed diagnosis and limited access to healthcare in SSA, emphasizing the urgent need for targeted interventions aimed at improving early detection and cancer awareness. The high utilization of Linac-based radiotherapy, including IMRT in some cases, reflects the growing adoption of modern cancer treatment techniques in SSA. However, the limited use of induction and adjuvant chemotherapy, along with occasional dose reductions to protect critical organs is worrisome. The low acute and long-term toxicity rates indicate that treatment was well-tolerated, but ongoing efforts are needed to optimize treatment strategies, expand access to IMRT, and improve supportive care. The survival of patients with NPC varied with stage of the disease, the type of tumor, the technique used in radiotherapy, whether induction chemotherapy was given, and the dose of radiotherapy. Specifically, the technique and dose of radiotherapy are particularly significant in determining the DFS and OS of NPC patients. Future research should focus on prospective multicenter studies to validate these findings and inform region-specific guidelines for NPC management.
