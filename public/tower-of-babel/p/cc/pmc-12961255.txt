First genome and transcription factor profile for Asimina triloba, a native North American fruit tree
Authors: Gabdiel E. Yulfo‐Soto, Hannah Toth, Sarah E. Francino, Jason Leung, Lyndel W. Meinhardt, G. Matt Davies, Jonathan M. Jacobs, Stephen P. Cohen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPawpaw (Asimina triloba) is the only fruit‐producing tree of the soursop (custard apple) family Annonaceae that is native to temperate North America. Pawpaws are extensively cultivated in the northeast United States, but to date, there are few genetic resources and no publicly available genome assemblies. Here, we present the first high‐quality genome assembly and annotation of pawpaw (cultivar Mango), derived from high‐fidelity third‐generation sequencing. The 851.7‐Mbp assembly consists of 68 contigs with a scaffold N50 of 28.5 Mbp, guanine‐cytosine content of 37%, and a 96.1% benchmarking universal single‐copy ortholog completeness score (eudicots). We profiled agronomically relevant transcription factors in the transcription factor family with the DNA‐binding WRKY amino acid domain and no apical meristem/Arabidopsis transcription activation factor/cup‐shaped cotyledon transcription factor families, which have functions related to environmental and pathogen immunity responses and regulation of fruit traits. Our resource facilitates future genetic and breeding research for this culturally important fruiting tree, expanding its economic and commercial potential.

INTRODUCTION

Asimina triloba and the members of its genus (Asimina Adans.) are the only temperate fruiting trees in the soursop (custard apple) family, Annonaceae (Cronquist, 1981). Within the soursops, there are several agriculturally significant species, such as cherimoya (Annona cherimola), sugar apple (Annona squamosa), guanábana (Annona muricata L.), and custard apple (Annona reticulata; Hormaza, 2014; Losada et al., 2017; Peña‐Ramírez et al., 2024). Asimina triloba, or pawpaw, is the largest tree fruit native to North America, with a distribution range from northern Florida to southern Ontario, and as west as Nebraska (Darrow, 1975). Flowering in April to May, one flower produces one to thirteen fruit per cluster, which can range in size from 50 to 1000 g (Pomper et al., 2003). The fruit is oblong shaped with green skin, orange to yellow to white flesh, large black to brown seeds (15–25 mm), and a flavor resembling mango, banana, and pineapple (Layne, 1996; Pomper et al., 2003, 2008). The climacteric fruit ripens asynchronously and, at peak ripeness, abscise; this coincides with the flesh softening (Lagrange & Tramer, 1985; Layne, 1996). The fruit has a short shelf life, ca. 5 days, which can be extended to around a month with refrigeration, but all other shelf life extension methods have failed (Archbold et al., 2003). The most common disease in pawpaw is leaf and fruit fungal spot, caused by a complex of Phyllosticta asiminae Ellis & Kelerm and two other isolates (Farr et al., 1989; Lowe et al., 2014). This complex produces blemishes on the leaves and skins of the fruit, which in the correct conditions can produce small cracks or fissures opening to the seeds. These inherent pathological and physiological facts have impeded large‐scale commercial production of pawpaw.

Asimina triloba is the standout species, producing the largest and most desirable fruit, in the genus Asimina, a North American genus of Annonaceae collectively referred to as the pawpaws. There are several other pawpaw species and hybrids, including Asimina incana (woolly pawpaw), Asimina longifolia (polecat‐bush), Asimina obovate (big‐flowered pawpaw), Asimina parviflora (small‐flowered pawpaw), Asimina pygmaea (dwarf pawpaw), Asimina reticulata (netted pawpaw), Asimina tetramera (four‐petal pawpaw), and Asimina × nashii (Kral, 1960). Even though over 50 cultivars have been lost due to the neglect of collections in between 1900 and 1960, parent material from historical collections were used by Peterson and other breeders to develop new cultivars (Peterson, 1991, 2003). From 2009 to 2020, the number of commercially available varieties increased from 45 to 140, and new cultivars are chosen from wild population every year by specialized collectors (Cothron, 2021; Pomper et al., 2010; Signorini & Francino, 2024). Since 1994, Kentucky State University has been designated as the U.S. National Plant Germplasm System's repository for Asimina preservation, where 1700 wild and commercial accessions are maintained to preserve genetic diversity (Pomper et al., 2003). Phenotypic differences between cultivars can vary significantly. For example, variations occur in tree height, growth, fruit size, yield, flavor, secondary compounds, and color (Adainoo et al., 2022; Francino, 2019, Lolletti et al., 2021, Pomper et al., 2003). Global interest to establish this American crop has spread to Austria, Belgium, China, Croatia, Israel, Italy, Japan, Korea, Portugal, Romania, and Ukraine (Bellini et al., 2003; Hrabovetska et al., 2006; Lehner et al., 2022; Licitar Osmičević, 2021; Lolletti et al., 2021; Tabacu et al., 2020; Xinkun et al., 2021).

Despite the fruit's large size, palatable flavor, and nutrition, the crop has not been a widely successful product for the US food market. Reasons for this include post‐harvest management issues (such as short shelf life and bruising), lack of marketable traits (such as seedless fruit and consistent size), and the time investment from planting to full production (between 5 and 7 years; Pomper & Layne, 2003). The pawpaw supply chain has major hurdles to overcome for it to become a widespread, mainstream crop, including the supply of high‐quality fruit, post‐harvest management for the delicate fruit, and processing techniques to increase safe and reliable secondary products (Cai et al., 2018; Callway, 1992; Layne, 1996; Peterson, 2003; Pomper & Layne, 2003; Signorini & Francino, 2024). Despite these facts, pawpaw is an emerging crop showing great commercial potential (Hummer et al., 2012). Currently all pawpaw breeding has been conducted using traditional techniques (Peterson, 2003). Impeding modern breeding approaches, no assembled and annotated pawpaw genome has been made publicly available, and there is evidence that much of the existing pawpaw genetic data are imprecise and that re‐analysis is necessary (Frost, 2023). A genotyping‐by‐sequencing approach has been used to study the evolution of wild pawpaw across North America (Friel, 2023).

Plants have developed sophisticated regulatory networks of genes that act in several plant responses, such as the immune response against pathogens and defense against environmental pressures and during fruit development (J. D. G. Jones & Dangl, 2006). Transcription factor (TF) families including TFs with the DNA‐binding WRKY amino acid domain (WRKYs) and no apical meristem/Arabidopsis transcription activation factor/cup‐shaped cotyledon family TFs (NACs) play important regulatory roles in these processes (Eulgem & Somssich, 2007; G. S. Liu et al., 2022). WRKYs are highly induced during pathogen infection, abiotic stress, such as drought and cold, triggering plant immune responses like stomatal closure and dormancy (Chen et al., 2016; Dong et al., 2003; He et al., 2016). Phytohormones such as salicylic acid, jasmonic acid, abscisic acid, and brassinosteroids can also influence the expression of WRKY TFs, which can also influence ripening (Y. Cheng et al., 2016; Luo et al., 2017). NACs, divided into five subfamilies including Group I, Group IIa, Group IIb, Group IIIa, and Group IIIb based on their domains (Jin et al., 2020), are also involved in regulating biotic and abiotic stress response and fruit development. Some NACs are induced at different plant growth stages, including fruit maturity and seed development. Studies in tomato have shown that homologous genes like SlNAC1 and SlNAC4 affect fruit by delaying ripening by 14 days (Gao et al., 2018). The manipulation of NACs affects ripening in different ways, including ripening acceleration, delay, and induction of resistance (Kou et al., 2016; Ma et al., 2014; Meng et al., 2016; Zhou, Jiang, et al., 2015; Zhu et al., 2013). In climacteric and non‐climacteric fruits, NACs interact with biosynthesis pathways such as carotenoids and anthocyanins, resulting in changes in traits that influence consumer preferences, such as color and sugar content (C. C. Fu et al., 2017; B. L. Fu et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021; H. Zhou, Lin‐Wang, et al., 2015).

Currently, genomes are available for only four Annonaceae species: An. cherimola, An. montana, An. Muricata, and An. squamosa (Bisht et al., 2025; H. Li et al., 2024; Peña‐Ramírez et al., 2024; Strijk et al., 2021; Talavera et al., 2023). The lack of whole‐genome assembly and annotation for A. triloba limits the knowledge of the molecular mechanisms involved in fruit quality and stress resistance. We were motivated to generate a high‐quality genome for A. triloba to facilitate pawpaw improvement and commercialization, and to better understand pawpaw genetic variation. Here, we describe our assembly and annotation of the A. triloba genome for var. Mango, a pawpaw variety that is popular due to its flavor, using third‐generation sequencing data from the PacBio HiFi sequencing platform. We provide profiles for WRKY and NAC TFs found in pawpaw to highlight targets for future studies on plant pathogen defense, environmental stress tolerance, and fruit trait improvement.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample collection, DNA extraction, and sequencing

We collected A. triloba var. ‘Mango’ leaves from an experimental orchard at Waterman Agriculture and Natural Resources Laboratory at The Ohio State University during June 2023. Trees were 5 years of age and healthy. DNA extraction was performed using the Monarch Genomic DNA Purification Kit (T3010; New England Biolabs) with 29 mg of tissue following the animal tissue lysis protocol with the following modifications. Prior to extraction, we ground the leaf tissue in liquid nitrogen with mortar and pestle. For tissue lysis, we incubated the sample at 56°C for 45 min, vortexing every 5 min. The optional step of centrifugation and transfer of the supernatant to a new tube was performed. After the addition of RNAse A, we incubated the sample at 56°C for 5 minutes, vortexing once every minute. For the second stage of the extraction, Genomic DNA Binding and Elution steps were followed with no modifications. Library preparation and sequencing were conducted by the University of Wisconsin–Madison Biotechnology Center's DNA Sequencing Facility (Research Resource Identifier: RRID:SCR_017759). The Pacific Biosciences (PacBio) HiFi library was prepared according to PN 102‐166‐500 Version 04 (Pacific Biosciences) with the following modifications: shearing with Covaris gTUBEs and size selection with Sage Science BluePippin. The library was sequenced on a PacBio Sequel II using Sequel Polymerase Binding Kit 2.2 (Pacific Biosciences). Read quality was assessed with seqkit v2.4.0 with the stat subcommand (Shen et al., 2024).

Genome assembly

We predicted genome size using GenomeScope (Vurture et al., 2017) with Jellyfish v2.2.9 (Marçais & Kingsford, 2011), with k = 25 and the PacBio HiFi reads as input. The assembly of the reads was performed using SMARTdenovo v1.0.0 with arguments ‐c 1 ‐t 24 (H. Liu et al., 2021) and HiFiasm v0.25.0 with argument –primary (H. Cheng et al., 2021), followed by purging with PurgeHaplotigs v1.1.2 with argument a = 50 (Roach et al., 2018). We merged the SMARTdenovo and HiFiasm assemblies with Quickmerge v0.3 with argument ‐pre (Chakraborty et al., 2016), with the purged SMARTdenovo assembly as the backbone. We purged duplicated sequence from the merged assembly with PurgeHaplotigs v1.1.2 with argument a = 60. To detect and remove duplicated sequences not identified by PurgeHaplotigs, we inspected the merged assembly for large duplicate contigs using the D‐GENIES web tool (Cabanettes & Klopp, 2018) and manually removed duplicates. Quality was assessed with qqual v2024‐07‐08 (Cohen, 2025) and BUSCO v6.0.0 (Manni et al., 2021; Tegenfeldt et al., 2025) with “eudicotyledons_odb12” and “embryophyta_odb12.” All pipelines steps used default parameters unless otherwise noted.

Genome annotation

RepeatModeler v2.0.4 (Flynn et al., 2020) was used to discover novel repetitive elements, which were masked, along with RepBase v21.09 repeats (Bao et al., 2015) and simple repeats, by RepeatMasker v4.1.5 (Smit et al., 2013). The Funannotate v1.8.16 (Palmer & Stajich, 2020) predict pipeline was used with default parameters and the repeat‐masked assembly FASTA file as input to predict genes from the final assembly, which utilizes AUGUSTUS v3.5.0, CodingQuarry v2.0, GeneMark‐ES v4.71, glimmerHMM v3.0.4, Snap v2006‐07‐28, tRNAscan‐SE v2.0.9, and EvidenceModeler v1.1.1 (Chan et al., 2021; Haas et al., 2008; Korf, 2004; Majoros et al., 2004; Stanke et al., 2008; Ter‐Hovhannisyan et al., 2008; Testa et al., 2015). InterProScan v5.60–92.0 was used with arguments –goterms –iprlookup –disable‐precalc for functional annotations, which were incorporated into the annotations along with the funannotate annotate pipeline, which utilized EggNOG‐mapper v2.1.12 (Cantalapiedra et al., 2021; P. Jones et al., 2014). Repeat density and proportion of repeat classes were plotted via Circos v0.69.9 (Krzywinski et al., 2009). Annotation completeness was assessed with BUSCO v6.0.0 (Manni et al., 2021; Tegenfeldt et al., 2025) with “eudicotyledons_odb12” and “embryophyta_odb12” using transcriptome mode with the coding DNA sequences (CDS) and proteome mode with the predicted amino acid sequences as input.

Genome comparison and synteny analysis

To assess synteny between An. squamosa and A. triloba, raw reads from An. squamosa (NCBI GenBank accession GCA_035584095.1) were aligned to the final A. triloba assembly. BWA v0.7.17 (H. Li, 2013) was used to index the genome, and each paired end file was aligned separately using BWA aln with default parameters. The aligned reads were paired to generate a SAM alignment file using BWA sampe, followed by converting to binary alignment map (BAM) and sorting using SAMtools v1.17 (T. Li et al., 2009). Coverage of aligned reads was plotted via Circos v0.69.9 (Krzywinski et al., 2009).

Phylogenetic and synteny analyses

To generate a phylogenetic tree, to see how A. triloba relates to other plant species, the A. triloba genome was compared to the genomes of 27 class Magnoliopsida species available on the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI; Table S1). Benchmarking universal single‐copy orthologs (BUSCOs) were identified using BUSCO v5.7.1, using the “eudicots_odb10” database (Manni et al., 2021). The predicted amino acid sequences for the 154 BUSCOs that were complete and present as single copy genes in all 28 genomes were used for generating alignments. Alignments were generated separately for each gene's predicted amino acid sequences with MUSCLE v5.1.0 (Edgar, 2022). The alignments were trimmed with trimAl v1.4.rev22 using arguments ‐gt 0.8 ‐st 0.001 ‐cons 60 (Capella‐Gutiérrez et al., 2009). After sorting by name with seqkit v2.4.0 (Shen et al., 2024), the trimmed alignments were concatenated to generate one sequence for each genome. RAxML‐NG v1.2.2 (Kozlov et al., 2019) was used to generate 10 starting parsimony trees each for nine different evolutionary models (Blosum62, Dayhoff, DCMut, JTT, JTT‐DCMut, LG, PMB, and WAG) to identify the best scoring evolutionary model (JTT) via BIC scores. A maximum‐likelihood tree was generated with support values from 100 starting trees (50 parsimony and 50 random trees) and 500 bootstrap trees with the model JTT+I+G+F. The tree was visualized in FigTree v1.4.4 (https://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree) and rooted with the Chloranthales clade, the only order used in the analysis outside of the Magnoliidae clade. Another tree was generated using the same methods with only species in Annonaceae. Of the 1891 shared complete single‐copy BUSCOs, a random subset of 250 was selected and the tree was generated as above (with the same model), and the final tree was rooted with A. triloba and displayed as a cladogram.

Genome assemblies for chromosome‐level Annonaceae genomes were downloaded from the NCBI, which included An. cherimola, accession GCA_030490835.1, and An. montana, accession GCA_050575065.1. Unplaced contigs were removed to prepare reduced assemblies. Nucleotide synteny blocks were identified via ntSynt v1.0.2 (Coombe et al., 2024) with argument ‐d 15 and the two reduced assembly FASTA files and the A. triloba assembly FASTA file as input. Synteny was visualized with ntSynt_viz v1.0.0 with the An. cherimola reduced assembly as the –target‐genome option (Coombe et al., 2025).

Identification and alignment of transcription factor families

Identification of genes coding for potential WRKY TF proteins was conducted using multiple methods. First, the protein sequences for 72 Arabidopsis WRKYs were downloaded from the Arabidopsis Information Resource using the protein search tool (Berardini et al., 2015). The proteins were mapped to the A. triloba genome with miniprot v0.13‐r248 (H. Li, 2023). Predicted coding sequences for funannotate‐identified A. triloba genes overlapping with the miniprot predictions were identified by manually inspecting the miniprot predictions and comparing to funannotate predictions. Protein sequences for additional A. triloba genes containing the WRKY DNA‐binding Pfam domain (PF03106) or the WRKY domain superfamily (IPR036576) annotations from InterProScan v5.73‐104.0 (P. Jones et al., 2014) were also identified.

To identify genes coding for NACs transcription factors, Solanum lycopersicum (tomato) was used as a reference. The reference genome protein FASTA was downloaded directly from the SOL Genomics Network (Fernandez‐Pozo et al., 2015). We used InterProScan (as above) to annotate the tomato and pawpaw genomes, predicting NAC TFs based on the presence of the nascent polypeptide‐associated complex NAC domain (IPR002715). The tomato proteins were also mapped to the pawpaw genome with miniprot (as above). Predictions were manually verified following the same abovementioned WRKY methods.

Multiple sequence alignments were generated for both WRKYs and NACs separately, with the protein coding sequences of the predicted A. triloba genes and the Arabidopsis thaliana WRKYs and S. lycopersicum NACs using MUSCLE v5.1.0 (Edgar, 2022). For both alignments, evolutionary models for proteins were trialed with RAxML‐NG v1.2.2 by generating 10 parsimony trees with each of the following models: Blosum62, Dayhoff, DCMut, JTT, JTT‐DCMut, LG‐ PMB, Q.pfam, Q.plant, WAG, and PROTGTR (Kozlov et al., 2019). The model with the best BIC score, JTT+I+G+F, was selected, and 100 new starting trees (50 parsimony and 50 random trees) were generated to identify the best starting tree for each alignment, with the support values from 500 bootstrap trees added to the best tree. The resulting tree was visualized in FigTree (as above).

Heterozygous variant calling

Heterozygous sites were identified by using GATK v4.0.9.0 (Poplin et al., 2017) with the PacBio HiFi reads, following the GATK best practices workflows (DePristo et al., 2011) for data pre‐processing for germline short variant discovery. BWA v0.7.17 (H. Li, 2013) was first used to index the final A. triloba assembly, followed by SAMtools (T. Li et al., 2009) to sort the resulting aligned and unaligned BAM files, before being merged into one file. The duplicates in the BAM file were marked using GATK MarkDuplicatesSpark and then used for GATK HaplotypeCaller to generate GVCF (Van der Auwera & O'Connor, 2020). SNPs were extracted from GVCF using GATK SelectVariants with default parameter, followed by annotation using GATK GenotypeGVCF. Raw variant calls were filtered using GATK VariantFiltration with quality parameters: Quality by Depth < 2.0, Mapping Quality < 30.0, and Fisher Strand Bias > 60.0. These thresholds were applied to reduce false‐positive variants by removing low confidence calls. Variants that passed the filter were plotted via Circos v0.69.9 (Krzywinski et al., 2009).

RESULTS

Generation of a high‐quality and highly contiguous A. triloba genome

The total PacBio HiFi sequencing yielded 5.86 million zero‐mode waveguides (ZMWs), with 2.36 million ZMWs passing quality filters. The remaining ZMWs comprised 2.36 million reads representing 20.38 Gbp, or approximately 23.9× coverage, with N50 = 9668 bp, Q20% = 98.83, and Q30% = 97.34. The primary genome generated from HiFiasm and the assembly from SMARTdenovo were merged to generate a highly contiguous assembly with final length of 851.73 Mbp, a total of 68 contigs, N50 = 28.64 Mbp, and guanine‐cytosine content = 37.1% (Table 1). BUSCO completeness was 98.7% using the embryophyta (land plants) database and 96.1% using the eudicots (flowering plants) database. Total interspersed repetitive sequences accounted for 74.8% of the genome. There were 41,468 predicted protein‐coding sequences. The completeness of the predicted transcriptome was assessed, with 83.2% detected complete or fragmented BUSCOs (56.6% single, 1.8% duplicate, 24.8% fragmented, and 16.7% missing) via the embryophyta database and 78.1% complete or fragmented BUSCOs (53.6% single, 3.2% duplicate, 21.2% fragmented, and 22.0% missing) via the eudicotyledons database. The completeness of the protein annotation was assessed, with 83.7% complete or fragmented BUSCOs (57.3% single, 2.1% duplicate, 24.4% fragmented, and 16.6% missing) via the embryophyta database and 78.5% complete or fragmented BUSCOs (54.7% single, 3.4% duplicate, 20.4% fragmented, and 21.5% missing) via the eudicotyledons database. Assembly and annotations were uploaded to the NCBI and are available via BioProject accession PRJNA1211107.

Phylogenetic and synteny analyses confirms A. triloba placement and similarity to Annonaceae family members

The phylogenetic analysis based on the 155 shared single‐copy BUSCOs of available Magnoliids genomes confirmed the placement of the A. triloba genome within the Annocaeae family of the order Magnoliales (Figure S1). All major clades were supported with most nodes receiving 100% bootstrap support, indicating high confidence. The separate analysis based on 250 randomly selected single‐copy BUSCOs shared among Annonaceae genomes confirmed the placement of A. triloba as an outgroup compared to An. cherimola, An. squamosa, and An. montana (Figure 1). Syntenic comparison of nucleic acid sequences showed a high degree of shared contiguity among the available chromosome‐scale Annona genomes and A. triloba (Figure 1). Of the 68 A. triloba contigs, 59 contained homology to the seven linkage groups from An. montana and An. cherimola.

Profiles of WRKY and NAC transcription factors give insights into A. triloba regulatory networks

There were 47 WRKY domain‐binding transcription factors identified in A. triloba, compared to the 72 WRKYs in Arabidopsis (Table S2). Six WRKYs appeared to be unique to Arabidopsis, not clustering with any similarity to A. triloba (Figure 2). Predicted WRKYs from A. triloba grouped with all known Arabidopsis WRKY groups, with three not being characterized in any group (Table S2, Figure 2). One A. triloba WRKY, ACLOJK_001583, shared no identity with any Arabidopsis WRKY. The regulatory function was noted for Arabidopsis WRKYs that shared similarity with A. triloba WRKYs, indicating putative regulatory roles of A. triloba WRKYs.

Cladogram of Arabidopsis and A. triloba transcription factor family with the DNA‐binding WRKY amino acid domain (WRKYs) showed similarities among WRKYs. All branches had ≥50% support from 500 bootstrap trees, except where noted. WRKY subgroups are shown for Arabidopsis WRKYs. The orange‐highlighted clade of Arabidopsis WRKYs was distinct from all predicted A. triloba WRKYs.

Tomato, one of the most‐characterized fruit crops, was used as a reference to identify NACs in A. triloba (Table S3). A large group containing 22 tomato NACs, all belonging to the superfamily Group I, grouped separately from A. triloba NACs (Figure 3). Asimina triloba NACs clustered with tomato NACs in four out of the five NAC groups. Three of the tomato NACs did not have a specific grouping (Table S3).

Visualization of A. triloba genomic arrangement revealed patterns of genomic variants and repeat content

We visualized the landscape of A. triloba genomic features to characterize WRKY and NAC TF localization, diploid SNPs, and repetitive genomic content (Figure 4; Figure S2). The genomic position of WRKYs and NACs was distributed throughout most, but not all, contigs. Both families are dispersed across the genome and show some clustering patterns, showing a greater accumulation on contigs 1, 4, 5, 7, 9, 10, 13, 18, and 19 (Figure 4). Genetic variant density varied through the genome, with some contigs showing few variants (e.g., contigs 5 and 12). The highest variant density was on the 5′‐termini of contigs 4 and 9, the 3′‐termini of contigs 3 and 8, and in the middle of contigs 2 and 10. Repeat density varied across the genome, with contigs 6, 12, 20, 22, and 24 containing notably low repeat density. Contigs 5 and 13 showed notable high repeat density, with repeats distributed throughout the entire contigs. Repeat density also varied within contigs; for example, contigs 1 and 10 contained high repeat density at the 5′‐termini and contigs 2, 11, and 15 contained high repeat density at the 3′‐terminus. The types of repeats also varied across the genome with Retroelements and DNA Transposons being the most stable in number on all contigs, with the most across‐contig variation being found in unclassified, simple, and low complexity repeats.

DISCUSSION

We generated a high‐quality, highly contiguous genome resource for A. triloba using PacBio HiFi. The resulting assembly was a collapsed/haploid (n = 1) assembly for a species with a diploid (n = 2) genome. PacBio HiFi technology was previously used to generate a highly contiguous genome for the related species, An. cherimola (H. Li et al., 2024), which was further improved to a chromosome‐level assembly with scaffolding via chromatin conformation capture. This genome, from the A. triloba cultivar Mango, is the first publicly available genome for A. triloba, the tree species that produces pawpaw fruit. The large size and high repeat density of the A. triloba genome did not enable a chromosome‐level assembly. This high amount of repetitive content is expected because repeats commonly cover 30%–85% of plant genomes (Schnable et al., 2009; Y. Zhou et al., 2023). The total percentage of repeats sequences in A. triloba is higher than An. cherimola (68.23%) and An. muricata (54.87%; H. Li et al., 2024; Strijk et al., 2021). There is still a dearth of genomic information for members of Annonaceae despite their global cultivation for edible fruits.

Genomic variants were high frequency and not evenly distributed throughout the A. triloba genome, with there being apparent variation hotspots on several contigs noted above. This indicates a high number of heterozygous sites within the Mango cultivar. Mango was originally collected from the wild and has been propagated from that source; the distribution and frequency of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are generally higher in wild specimens compared to domesticated. While our variant analysis sheds light on variation within the Mango cultivar, these sites may not represent a good selection of variants for breeding SNP markers, and more broad sequencing is needed for breeding marker development.

Identifying WRKY TFs in A. triloba helps us understand this plant species’ defense network. We identified several WRKYs from all subgroups in A. triloba, except for a small subset of Group III unique to Arabidopsis (Figure 2). Genomic characterization of regulatory targets such as WRKYs is foundational information that lays the groundwork for breeding studies that focus on improving varietal traits; increased access to genomic information such as this supports the work being conducted to lead to the increased adoption of this emergent crop (Peterson, 2003; Signorini & Francino, 2024). The most common disease on pawpaw is leaf and fruit fungal spot, which is caused by a fungal species complex containing Mycocentrospora asiminae, Rhopaloconidium asiminae Ellis and Morg, and P. asiminae Ellis and Kellerm (Farr et al., 1989; Lowe et al., 2014). This disease causes cracks and blemishes on the fruit that are unpleasant to consumers (Francino, 2019). Chemical disease management practices are not available, and the basic removal of the diseased material at the end of the growing season has not been adopted by growers (Signorini & Francino, 2024). Other pawpaw diseases include tobacco ringspot virus causing viral disease and Phytophthora citrophthora causing leaf necrosis and terminal shoot dieback (Bily & Gyatso, 2024; Maclot et al., 2023).

Some WRKYs act as master regulators for plant response to biotic diseases, so the selection of active disease‐responsive WRKYs during breeding offers a non‐chemical option to control diseases. In Arabidopsis, AtWRKY3 (A. triloba orthologs ACLOJK_001582, ACLOJK_004015, and ACLOJK_004016) is rapidly induced within 2–24 h after inoculation with Botrytis (Lai et al., 2008). Together with AtWRKY4 (closest A. triloba orthologs ACLOJK_018126 and ACLOJK_013840, orthologs of AtWRKY19), mutant and double mutant Arabidopsis lines were deficient in Botrytis resistance, indicating their importance in plant resistance to a necrotrophic pathogen (Lai et al., 2008). Identifying potential Botrytis‐responsive WRKYs in pawpaw is particularly important for the predicted future expansion of pawpaw cultivation because Botrytis spp. broadly cause fruit rot, affecting up to 50% of strawberries, 20%–50% of pears and apples, and 20% of kiwifruits (Amiri & Acosta, 2020; Michailides & Elmer, 2000). Another WRKY of interest is AtWRKY72 (A. triloba orthologs ACLOJK_019781 and ACLOJK_031769), which encodes the WRKY72 protein that contributes to basal defense against bacterial pathogens such as Pseudomonas syringae in tomato (Bhattarai et al., 2010). SlWRKY72a and SlWRKY72b (orthologs of Arabidopsis AtWRKY72) were also upregulated during aphid infestation and root‐knot nematodes (Bhattarai et al., 2010). Expanding the market for pawpaw and distributing plants will inherently bring new stresses from abiotic and biotic sources (Signorini & Francino, 2024), so further studies are needed to test the roles of the A. triloba WRKYs during responses to different environmental challenges.

Some challenges that keep pawpaw from mainstream markets include the fruit's short shelf life, easy bruising, large seeds and seed density, and inconsistent size (Archbold et al., 2003; Peterson, 1991; Pomper et al., 2008). These properties are influenced and regulated by NAC TFs. NACs interact with phytohormones such as ethylene, abscisic acid, and auxin to regulate fruit ripening by modulating the expression of their hormone biosynthesis pathways and signaling genes (G. S. Liu et al., 2022). This is a timely and orchestrated process that ensures the proper development of fruits such as softening, color changes, and flavor profiles. As a climacteric fruit, pawpaw continues to ripen after harvesting, and the fruit are low ethylene producers during this process (Koslanund et al., 2005), suggesting that the ripening pathways may be divergent from other fruit crops, necessitating the need to identify regulators of these pathways. We identified 43 NACs in A. triloba (Figure 3), including all subgroups except the tomato‐exclusive Group I, similar to the Arabidopsis and tomato NAC comparison by Jin et al. (2020). Experiments on overexpressed SlNAC1, also known as SlNAC033 (A. triloba ortholog ACLOJK_014076), significantly inhibited ethylene synthesis, and silencing of SlNAC1 caused slow ripening and a higher‐than‐normal ethylene peak and color change (Ma et al., 2014; Meng et al., 2016). The wide range of North American pawpaw covers an array of microclimates and climates with many different soils and environmental conditions that could influence growth and development (Adainoo et al., 2022; Francino, 2019; Lolletti et al., 2021). Some NACs respond to these environmental challenges; SlNAC3, an ortholog of Arabidopsis NAC3 also known as SlNAC063 (A. triloba ortholog ACLOJK_015289), enhances drought tolerance and salt stress in tomato (Al‐Abdallat et al., 2015). SlNAC3 overexpression lines also show delayed wilting in extended drought and higher germination rates when seeds were sowed in salt‐treated water (Al‐Abdallat et al., 2015). NACs related to SlNAC3 also confer salt tolerance and drought in tomato and Arabidopsis (Su et al., 2014; Tran et al., 2004), indicating that the A. triloba SlNAC3 ortholog, ACLOJK_015289, may play a similar role.

We present the first publicly available, high‐quality genome assembly of A. triloba ‘Mango,’ a horticulturally significant species in the Annonaceae family. This resource lays the foundation to explore the evolution of Asimina alongside other Annonaceae and provides insight into the complement of TFs that regulate desirable horticultural traits. As the interest in developing pawpaw for commercial production is increasing worldwide, this genomic resource will serve as a platform for accelerating new research in pawpaw cultivation.
