Systematic Review of Bilateral Ocular Trauma: Considerations for Future Whole-eye Transplantation
Authors: Raymond B. Kroma, Alexis K. Gursky, Hailey P. Wyatt, Abigail M. Royfman, Sofia M. Ghani, Sachin R. Chinta, Alay R. Shah, Eduardo D. Rodriguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Bilateral ocular trauma (BLOT) is a rare but severe injury pattern associated with high-energy mechanisms and poor visual outcomes. As interest in vascularized composite allotransplantation grows, particularly following the first combined face transplantation (FT) and whole-eye transplantation (WET), a clearer understanding of BLOT injury patterns, interventions, and outcomes is needed to provide context for future discussions of bilateral WET.Methods:A systematic review was conducted according to PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) 2020 guidelines using PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, Cochrane, and MEDLINE. Studies published through June 2024 were included if they reported patient-level data on BLOT mechanisms, injury type, outcomes, and surgical management.Results:A total of 268 patients with BLOT were identified, predominantly young men (72.4%) with a mean age of 29.9 years. Motor vehicle accidents (31.6%), explosions (21.4%), and fireworks (9.8%) were the leading mechanisms. Open-globe injuries (51.8%) were more frequent than closed-globe injuries, and the cornea (26.5%), limbus (15.5%), and lens (11.6%) were the most affected ocular regions. Globe-sacrificing procedures such as enucleation (2.1%) and evisceration (1.6%) were infrequent, and among studies reporting visual outcomes, bilateral blindness occurred in 38.7% of cases.Conclusions:Patients with BLOT were predominantly young men exposed to motor vehicle accidents and explosive trauma and, despite a high rate of bilateral blindness, often retained intact globes. Although vision restoration has not yet been achieved, these findings provide context for ongoing discussions of bilateral WET, whether performed in isolation or in combination with FT, within the evolving field of vascularized composite allotransplantation.

Takeaways

Question: What are the mechanisms, treatments, and outcomes of bilateral ocular trauma (BLOT), and how do these findings provide clinical context relevant to future discussions of bilateral whole-eye transplantation?

Findings: BLOT primarily affected young men following high-energy trauma, often from motor vehicle accidents, explosions, or fireworks. Open-globe injuries were most frequent, and enucleation rates were low; however, nearly 40% of patients sustained bilateral blindness.

Meaning: Our findings provide clinical context relevant to discussions of bilateral whole-eye transplantation in patients with BLOT within the evolving field of vascularized composite allotransplantation.

INTRODUCTION

Ocular trauma remains a significant contributor to the global burden of preventable visual impairment and blindness.1 Each year, an estimated 55 million ocular injuries occur worldwide, including nearly 2.5 million reported cases in the United States.2 These injuries range in severity and may result in temporary or permanent vision loss. Historically, traumatic ocular injuries have been a leading cause of monocular blindness and disability.3 Although unilateral ocular injury is more common, bilateral ocular trauma (BLOT) is associated with disproportionately more severe injuries and worse outcomes. Previous studies describe high-energy injury mechanisms, including motor vehicle accidents, assault, and combat-related injuries, as key contributors to BLOT, with the incidence of these cases increasing over time.4–7

The relevance of BLOT extends beyond acute ophthalmologic management and has growing implications for the field of plastic and reconstructive surgery. Advances in vascularized composite allotransplantation (VCA), including face transplantation (FT) and whole-eye transplantation (WET), have expanded the discussions of reconstructive options beyond the limits of autologous repair.8 Although bilateral WET has not yet been performed, primarily due to the current limitations in vision restoration and the risks associated with lifelong immunosuppression, interest in this field has increased.8–11 However, in patients with BLOT and concomitant facial trauma, for whom FT may already be indicated, the inclusion of bilateral WET may be a future option. Moreover, as advances in retinal ganglion cell and optic nerve regeneration continue, isolated unilateral or bilateral WET may eventually emerge as a reconstructive option for select patients with profound vision loss, even in the absence of facial trauma.

Despite the rising incidence and severity of BLOT, the literature remains heterogeneous, with inconsistent outcome reporting, and this patient population has not been previously well characterized. This systematic review aims to define the epidemiology, injury patterns, management strategies, and visual outcomes associated with BLOT. By clarifying the clinical features and outcomes of BLOT, these findings inform future reconstructive discussions regarding isolated bilateral WET or bilateral WET combined with an already indicated FT.

METHODS

Search Strategy

A systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines.12 Five databases were queried: PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, and MEDLINE. A search was performed using Medical Subject Headings terms and keywords including “bilateral ocular trauma” or “ocular trauma” in addition to “treatment,” “therapy,” “management,” and/or “outcomes.” The full search query is reported in Supplemental Digital Content 1. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the general search strategy, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E931.) There was no restriction on publication date, and all searches were conducted through June 2024. The resulting studies were uploaded into Covidence, a website used to streamline screening and reduce bias.13

Selection Criteria

Duplicate studies were removed, and 3 independent reviewers (R.B.K., A.M.R., S.M.G.) performed title and abstract screening, followed by a full-text review of the resulting studies. Any conflicts encountered were resolved by a fourth reviewer (A.R.S.). Eligible studies were those that included patients with BLOT and reported at least one of the following combinations of data: (1) mechanism of injury and specific injuries sustained; (2) mechanism of injury and corresponding management; or (3) type of management and a visual outcome. BLOT was defined as simultaneous traumatic injury to both globes and/or adnexal structures resulting from the same incident. Studies describing sequential injuries, nontraumatic bilateral vision loss, or cases not meeting these criteria were excluded. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria are listed in Table 1.

Data Extraction and Injury Classification

Extracted data included study characteristics, number of patients with BLOT, demographics, injury details, management, and visual outcomes. Variables absent from a study were documented as “not reported.” Additionally, if group statistics were provided and could not be attributed to individual BLOT patients, these data were also documented as “not reported.”

Globe injuries were classified using the Birmingham Eye Trauma Terminology (BETT) system. Closed-globe injuries, defined as non–full-thickness scleral and corneal wounds, included 2 subtypes: contusions, defined as blunt injuries without a wound, and lamellar lacerations, defined as partial-thickness sharp injuries. Open-globe injuries, defined as full-thickness scleral and corneal wounds, were categorized as ruptures from blunt trauma or lacerations from sharp trauma. Lacerations were further classified as penetrating injuries, intraocular foreign bodies, or perforating injuries. Injuries not captured by the BETT system, such as adnexal trauma and nonmechanical injuries, were included but not formally classified, as the BETT system primarily addresses mechanical injuries.14

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using R (RStudio version 12.1; R Core Team, 2025),15 and descriptive statistics were used to summarize study characteristics, injury mechanisms, setting, injury type and zone, outcomes, and surgical procedures, which were reported as frequencies and percentages or as mean ± SD.

Quality and Risk of Bias Assessment

Study quality and risk of bias were assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools for case reports and case series. Each study was assessed independently by 3 reviewers using the appropriate JBI study-type checklist, with checklist items recorded as yes, no, unclear, or not applicable. Based on consensus appraisal, studies were classified as include, exclude, or seek further information.16,17

After removing duplicates, the initial search yielded 2898 unique studies. Following title and abstract screening, 169 studies were selected for full-text review. Ultimately, 53 studies met all inclusion criteria (Fig. 1).18–70 Of the included studies, 34 were case reports and 19 were case series. Type and Zone of Injury

A total of 239 ocular injuries were identified among patients with BLOT. According to the BETT system, open-globe injuries (51.8%) were more common than closed-globe injuries (48.2%). Open-globe injuries predominantly consisted of lacerations (84.5%) compared with ruptures (3.4%), with penetrating injuries (81.6%) and intraocular foreign bodies (18.4%) representing the specific laceration subtypes. Nonmechanical injuries not classified by the BETT system included burns, which were more prevalent than mechanical injuries (53.1% versus 46.9%). Most burns were chemical (89.0%) (Table 5). The zone of injury was reported for 110 cases, with zone I (88.1%) being the most common, involving the conjunctiva, sclera, and cornea. Zone II, which includes the lens, zonules, and pars plicata, accounted for 18.2%, whereas zone III, involving the vitreous, retina, optic nerve, choroid, and ciliary body, accounted for 22.7% (Table 6).

Ocular and Adnexal Injury Outcomes

In total, 181 injury outcomes were recorded. The cornea (26.5%), limbus (15.5%), and lens (11.6%) were the most frequently injured regions. Among all injuries, limbal stem cell deficiency was the most common outcome (15.5%), followed by corneal abrasion (14.4%) and traumatic cataract (9.4%). General globe injury outcomes, including globe luxation, secondary glaucoma, and phthisis bulbi, accounted for 7.7% of all injuries. Optic nerve injuries were less frequent (5.5%), and adnexal injuries were even less common (4.4%) (Table 7).

Surgical Procedures

Across all studies, 193 surgical procedures were reported. The most common procedures were both cataract surgery (14.5%) and keratoplasty (14.5%), followed by limbal stem cell transplant (13.0%). Postinjury Visual Acuity

Visual acuity was reported at presentation, discharge, or last follow-up in 36 studies. Among the 93 cases reported, 65 (69.9%) experienced bilateral improvement in visual acuity, whereas 9 (9.7%) showed unilateral improvement, with the contralateral eye remaining blind. A total of 19 (20.4%) patients showed no improvement in either eye. Overall, 36 (38.7%) patients were left with bilateral blindness.

DISCUSSION

Although rare, BLOT represents a severe injury pattern associated with high rates of visual impairment and blindness. Although unilateral ocular trauma is well reported in the literature, BLOT remains poorly defined. This systematic review aims to characterize the epidemiology, injury mechanisms, treatments, and outcomes of BLOT to inform future reconstructive discussions and patient presentations relevant to isolated bilateral WET or bilateral WET in the setting of an already indicated FT. Individuals with bilateral vision loss face substantial psychosocial burdens and reconstructive needs, making this population an important group for these discussions. Currently, isolated unilateral WET is not justified, as patients with a remaining functional eye would have limited benefit, and the risks of lifelong immunosuppression outweigh potential benefits. However, the landmark case of combined FT and WET represents a pivotal step toward feasibility, as this WET was performed in the setting of an already indicated FT, and systemic immunosuppression was required regardless.8

Considerations for Bilateral WET

The population affected by BLOT consisted primarily of young men in their 30s, often injured by motor vehicle accidents and explosions. Nearly 40% of these individuals were blind in both eyes. Interestingly, many retained their globes despite traumatic vision loss, with few undergoing enucleation or evisceration. Most injuries were open globe in nature, and in terms of injury outcomes, limbal stem cell deficiency, which is often associated with chemical or thermal insults, was the most common. These findings highlight the continued central role of established reconstructive approaches for superficial ocular injury patterns, while reserving discussion of bilateral WET for rare, highly selected scenarios in which conventional options are limited.

Individuals with traumatic bilateral blindness, such as those with BLOT and enucleated or eviscerated globes, may be among the earliest considered for WET, given the lack of remaining ocular tissue and limited reconstructive options. Importantly, WET would only be considered in the postacute setting, once irreversible blindness has been established, rather than during the acute phase of injury. Our study examined traumatic injuries; however, if functional vision restoration becomes a reality, similar considerations may eventually extend to individuals with nontraumatic congenital blindness or end-stage ocular disease. This distinction is important, as patients with intact but blind globes may still benefit from other vision-restoring interventions and therefore do not represent an immediate anatomical indication for WET.

Prevention

The predominance of motor vehicle collisions, explosions, and fireworks as mechanisms of injury highlights opportunities for prevention. Public health measures, such as road-safety enforcement, including mandatory seatbelt use, are associated with reductions in facial and ocular injuries.71 Strict firework regulations, including bans on consumer fireworks and targeted restrictions on high-risk types of fireworks, have also been shown to lower rates of injury, especially among young men.72–74 Collectively, these preventive strategies underscore the critical role of public health policy in reducing the incidence and severity of ocular trauma, including BLOT.

Psychosocial Impact

Vision loss is associated with reduced quality of life, increased dependence, and elevated rates of depression and anxiety compared with those with normal vision.75–78 These effects are amplified in cases of bilateral globe and periorbital tissue loss, where disfigurement contributes to appearance-related distress, avoidance behaviors, and disruptions in self-image, with an increased risk of posttraumatic stress disorder.10,78–80 Restoring form and function may therefore be essential to long-term psychological well-being. Although psychiatric or rehabilitation interventions were rarely reported in the included studies, evidence from related populations demonstrates the value of multidisciplinary care, including structured psychiatric evaluation, psychological interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapy, social support, and timely rehabilitation referrals. Early identification and intervention are critical for mitigating long-term adverse outcomes and supporting adjustment to vision and/or globe loss.78,81 Prior experience with VCA highlights that comprehensive pre- and postoperative psychiatric assessment and longitudinal follow-up can improve depression scores and mental health-related quality of life.82–84

Reconstructive Implications and the Role of VCA

Although this study did not capture rates of concomitant facial trauma, such injuries are likely given the motor vehicle accidents and high-energy mechanisms commonly associated with BLOT.85 In this cohort, injuries extended beyond the globe to involve the ocular adnexa, suggesting broader periocular and facial involvement. Traumatic facial injuries can require complex soft tissue reconstruction and, in severe cases, autologous reconstruction using free flaps to address the defects.86 Even with multiple reconstructions or the use of ocular prostheses for patients with globe loss, aesthetic and functional outcomes may remain limited for some patients. No single specialty can comprehensively address the full spectrum of functional and aesthetic deficits these patients face, highlighting the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration involving ophthalmology, plastic and reconstructive surgery, and, at times, neurosurgery.87,88 For these individuals, VCA, specifically FT with bilateral WET, may warrant consideration in future reconstructive discussions when conventional autologous reconstruction is insufficient.

The landmark 2023 case of combined FT and WET demonstrated the technical feasibility of transplanting an entire eye as part of a composite facial allograft, along with the immunologic viability and sustained perfusion of the transplanted globe. At 1 year posttransplant, light perception and visual acuity were not observed; however, the procedure should be interpreted as establishing the technical and procedural feasibility of WET rather than as an attempt to restore vision. In addition to vision restoration, areas that remain active topics of research include achieving coordinated ocular motility and reflexes, particularly because the index case did not include the coaptation of cranial nerves III, IV, or VI, resulting in the absence of extraocular movement, eyelid motion, and autonomic function.8

Advances in WET Research

Although restoring vision through isolated WET remains a goal, recent scientific advances are mostly preclinical, and a gap remains between concept and clinical reality.9 Rodent studies have demonstrated WET graft survival with maintenance of intraocular pressure and preserved retinal perfusion. However, only minimal electroretinography responses have been observed, with no evidence of functional visual recovery. Histologic analysis also revealed increased corneal thickness, decreased retinal thickness, and optic nerve degeneration following transplantation.9,89 Nonhuman primate models have further advanced the field, showing up to 19 days of graft survival with preserved ocular structure and partial retinal function under immunosuppression. However, immune rejection and retinal ganglion cell loss remain major barriers.90 Human cadaveric work has refined microsurgical techniques, establishing benchmarks for anastomoses, such as the ophthalmic artery to the superficial temporal artery. Still, translation to clinically isolated WET remains limited by unresolved challenges in neuroregeneration and vision restoration.10,91

Research on optic nerve crush injury, axonal regeneration, and immunologic modulation is ongoing to address these barriers to restoring vision.92–94 Strategies under investigation include stimulating retinal ganglion cells to promote full-length axonal regrowth, modulating retinal ganglion cell signaling to increase cell survival, and using electric field stimulation to guide target-specific axonal growth.92–94 Collectively, these advances demonstrate meaningful scientific progress; however, clinically significant visual recovery has not been achieved, and the timeline to successful isolated WET remains uncertain.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations. Reliance on aggregate data limited patient-level analysis of management strategies and outcomes, underscoring the need for standardized reporting. Although several major databases were searched, relevant non-English studies may have been missed, and underreporting from low-resource or conflict-affected regions likely contributes to underestimation of the true global burden of BLOT. Risk of bias was present, as the predominance of case reports and case series favored severe or atypical presentations and limited generalizability. Patient-reported outcomes and long-term functional data were infrequently reported. This study was descriptive in nature and focused on characterizing injury patterns and outcomes relevant to discussions of bilateral WET, and substantial heterogeneity and a limited pooled sample size precluded statistically powered analyses. Most studies focused solely on ocular injuries, limiting assessment of associated facial and orbital trauma, which is an important consideration when discussing combined FT and bilateral WET. Although such scenarios are rare, particularly given the limited number of approximately 50 FTs performed worldwide,95 combined FT and bilateral WET should not be discounted as a future reconstructive option in selected patients. Finally, the reported cases represent only a subset of published data and exclude nontraumatic causes of bilateral blindness, which were outside the scope of this review.

CONCLUSIONS

This systematic review offers a comprehensive assessment of BLOT, a rare but severe injury pattern that predominantly affects young men involved in motor vehicle accidents, explosions, and fireworks. These patients experience elevated rates of bilateral blindness yet often retain structurally intact globes. Although isolated WET remains experimental and has not achieved vision restoration, characterization of severe BLOT injury patterns and outcomes provides important context for future VCA discussions of isolated bilateral WET or bilateral WET in the setting of an already indicated FT.
