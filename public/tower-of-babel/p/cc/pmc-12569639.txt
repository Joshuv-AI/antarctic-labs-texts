Feasibility of Aorto-uni-iliac Endovascular Aortic Repair Using the Gore Excluder for Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm
Authors: Takumi Sugiura, Akira Yokka, Toru Yamamoto, Kazunori Koyama, Shintaro Takago, Satoru Nishida, Nobuhiko Ogawa, Hiroshi Ikeno, Kotaro Yoshida, Jun Yoshikawa, Shiro Miyayama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose: To evaluate the feasibility of aorto-uni-iliac endovascular aortic repair using the Gore Excluder in selected patients.Material and Methods: This retrospective study reviewed five cases of aorto-uni-iliac endovascular aortic repair for abdominal aortic aneurysm using the Gore Excluder between January 2014 and January 2024. Patient demographics, procedural details, and postoperative outcomes were evaluated. Study endpoints included technical success, overall survival, aneurysm sac changes, endoleak occurrence, secondary interventions, and aneurysm-related death to evaluate feasibility and mid-term durability. Technical success was defined as successful stent graft deployment without conversion to open repair.Results: The cohort included one elective case in which a dedicated aorto-uni-iliac device was deemed unsuitable, three cases converted intraoperatively from bifurcated to aorto-uni-iliac configuration, and one case in which the dedicated device was unavailable. The aorto-uni-iliac configuration was achieved through off-label use of aortic extenders or an upside-down contralateral leg. All cases were technically successful. Three cases were emergency procedures. All cases required femoro-femoral crossover bypass. Slight type III endoleaks were detected in two cases on completion aortography but had resolved on postoperative computed tomography on days 11 and 16, respectively. No type III endoleaks were observed in any case during follow-up. No aneurysm-related deaths occurred. Over a median follow-up of 35 months (range: 1-62; excluding one same-day death), no sac enlargement was observed, suggesting acceptable durability. Median overall survival was 1,100 days (range: 0-1,966).Conclusions: Aorto-uni-iliac endovascular aortic repair using the Gore Excluder for abdominal aortic aneurysm is feasible when dedicated aorto-uni-iliac devices are unavailable or unsuitable, particularly in emergencies.

Introduction

EVAR is a well-established minimally invasive treatment for AAA that is superior to open surgical repair (OSR) in terms of reducing early morbidity and mortality [1]. It is recommended as a first-line treatment for ruptured AAA [2].

In patients in whom standard bifurcated devices are unsuitable, such as those with unilateral iliac artery occlusion, aorto-uni-iliac (AUI) EVAR combined with femoro-femoral crossover bypass (F-F bypass) provides a viable alternative [3]. AUI EVAR is typically performed using dedicated AUI devices, such as the Endurant AUI (Medtronic, Santa Rosa, CA, USA).

However, in some situations, particularly emergency settings, the unavailability of dedicated AUI devices may necessitate OSR, despite its associated risks. Furthermore, intraoperative challenges, such as contralateral gate cannulation failure, may require bailout conversion from a bifurcated to an AUI configuration when technically feasible [4]. We hypothesized that the Gore Excluder stent graft (W.L. Gore & Associates, Flagstaff, AZ, USA), when configured in an AUI fashion (hereafter referred to as the Excluder AUI), may provide a viable solution to the aforementioned challenges in both elective and emergency settings. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the feasibility and durability of the Excluder AUI in selected patients undergoing either elective or bailout AUI EVAR procedures.

Material and Methods

This retrospective study analyzed five consecutive cases of AUI EVAR for AAA performed using the Gore Excluder main body between January 2014 and January 2024 at our institution. The AUI configuration was achieved using either one or two aortic extenders or an upside-down contralateral leg inside the main body, as shown in Figure 1 [5]. It should be noted that this technique constitutes an off-label use of the Gore Excluder stent graft. The ipsilateral limb was placed in either the CIA or EIA in all cases, and F-F bypass was subsequently performed.

Clinical data, including patient demographics, comorbidities, procedural details, and follow-up outcomes, were collected from medical records. The follow-up included assessments of overall survival, aneurysm sac changes, endoleak occurrence, aneurysm-related death, complications associated with F-F bypass, and the need for secondary interventions.

In principle, patients were followed up with physical examinations and unenhanced or dynamic contrast-enhanced CT scans at approximately 1-2 weeks, 6 months, and 1 year post-procedure and then at 6-month or annual intervals. Aneurysm sac enlargement and shrinkage were defined as a change of ≥5 mm from preoperative diameter.

Technical success was defined as successful stent graft deployment without complications or intraoperative conversion to OSR. Durability was assessed based on the absence of aneurysm-related death, sac enlargement, and postoperative type III endoleak during follow-up.

The present study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to its retrospective design, written informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board, and an opt-out method was adopted. Written informed consent for EVAR was obtained prior to treatment.

Technique for the Excluder AUI.

To achieve an aorto-uni-iliac configuration, the contralateral gate was occluded, redirecting blood flow exclusively from the main trunk to the ipsilateral limb. This was accomplished by deploying either aortic extenders or a contralateral leg using the upside-down technique from the main trunk to the ipsilateral limb.

A. The middle of the first aortic extender was positioned at the level of the flow divider, and the distal end of the second aortic extender was deployed at the level of the flow divider.

B. When using the upside-down contralateral leg, the distal end of the leg was deployed at the proximal end of the main trunk.

Results

Patient demographics and technical aspects of the Excluder AUI

Patient demographics are summarized in Table 1. Two patients were male, with a median age of 83 years (range: 76-92). All EVAR procedures were performed under general anesthesia. Two cases were elective, while three were emergency cases. Among emergency cases, one had impending rupture, while the other two presented with rupture classified as Fitzgerald grade 3/Rutherford grade 4 and Fitzgerald grade 3/Rutherford grade 2, respectively. In all cases, EVAR was selected over OSR, due to hemodynamic instability or advanced age.

The indications for the Excluder AUI were as follows: (1) an unsuitable aortic configuration for the dedicated AUI device (severe infrarenal angulation) (case 1); (2) failure to cannulate the contralateral gate of the bifurcated device (cases 2, 3, and 4); and (3) unavailability of dedicated AUI devices due to emergency conditions (cases 5). Contralateral gate cannulation in the rupture cases was performed without using an aortic occlusion balloon.

F-F bypass was performed in all cases. No complications related to F-F bypass were observed. Case 1 had a narrow terminal aorta, which provided an adequate distal sealing zone, and embolization of the contralateral CIA was omitted at the discretion of the operators. In the other cases (cases 2, 3, and 4) with patent contralateral CIAs, embolization was performed using coils or a vascular plug.

The AUI configuration was achieved using the upside-down contralateral leg in one case, two aortic extenders in three cases, and a single aortic extender in one case.

Outcomes of the Excluder AUI

The postoperative course is shown in Table 2.

Completion aortography detected type III endoleaks in two cases, which resolved on initial dynamic contrast-enhanced CT on days 11 and 16 postoperatively.

The technical success rate was 100% and no perioperative complications were observed. Median overall survival was 1,100 days (range: 0-1,966). Among ruptured cases, one patient died 5.5 hours postoperatively (case 4). This patient had been diagnosed at another hospital and experienced cardiac arrest during transfer to our hospital. Following resuscitation, EVAR was performed. Despite the successful exclusion of AAA, the patient died from multiple organ failure. Of the remaining four cases, three patients died from causes unrelated to aortic disease, while one patient was presumed alive during the follow-up.

Sac enlargement was not observed in any patient on postoperative CT. Excluding the patient who died on the same day, the median CT follow-up was 35 months (range: 1-62). Two cases showed aneurysm sac shrinkage, while two remained stable. To date, no additional procedures have been performed. Of the four patients who underwent postoperative contrast-enhanced CT, none exhibited type III endoleaks. No type I endoleak was observed. Type II endoleak was observed in case 3, involving lumbar arteries.

One emergency case (case 5) had only a 1-month CT follow-up. Although he was not inclined to continue regular follow-ups due to preexisting frailty, he was confirmed to be alive 888 days postoperatively.

Case Presentation

Case 1

An elective case involved a markedly narrow terminal aorta, increasing the risk of limb occlusion with a bifurcated configuration. Therefore, AUI EVAR with F-F bypass was selected (Figure 2). The use of the Endurant AUI was considered; however, severe infrarenal angulation (>90°) posed a risk of suprarenal stent collapse and entrapment of the Endurant device's delivery system [6, 7]. The access route was also severely stenotic. The Aorfix stent graft (Lombard Medical, Oxfordshire, UK), an alternative for an angulated proximal neck, was also deemed unsuitable due to the large diameter of its delivery system. As a result, the Excluder stent graft, which does not have a suprarenal stent, was selected, and an upside-down contralateral leg was used for AUI EVAR.

Imaging findings of case 1.

The patient had AAA with a maximum diameter of 74 mm. The infrarenal aorta was severely angulated at >90°, and the terminal aorta was narrow, with the narrowest diameter measuring 9.5 mm. The access route was suboptimal, with the narrowest portion of the external iliac arteries measuring 4.2 mm on the right and 3.4 mm on the left. Elective aorto-uni-iliac EVAR using the Gore Excluder stent graft was planned.

An emergency case with severe proximal neck angulation, which resulted in obstruction of the contralateral gate, making it inaccessible (Figure 4). Attempts to pass the contralateral gate in both retrograde and antegrade direction were unsuccessful. As the contralateral gate was nearly occluded, a single aortic extender was used, successfully stabilizing hemodynamics.

Discussion

A recent case report described the use of the Excluder AUI as a bailout option for intraoperative complications involving a CIA aneurysm [8]. Building on this, the present study demonstrated that the Excluder AUI is a feasible and effective treatment option for AAA in both elective and emergency cases, particularly when intraoperative conversion is required due to contralateral gate cannulation failure or when a dedicated AUI device is unsuitable or unavailable. Technical success was 100%. During follow-up, no aneurysm-related deaths or sac enlargement were observed, and no type III endoleaks were detected on postoperative CT in any case, suggesting acceptable durability. Intraoperative type III endoleaks, observed on completion aortography in two cases, resolved on CT on days 11 and 16 postoperatively. Among the four patients with follow-up CT, sac shrinkage ≥5 mm was noted in two cases, while the remaining two remained stable.

AUI EVAR is generally indicated when the deployment of a conventional bifurcated stent graft is technically challenging or poses a risk of limb occlusion, such as in cases of unilateral iliac artery occlusion, severe iliac occlusive disease, severe iliac tortuosity, complex iliac aneurysms, and a severely narrow or calcified aortic bifurcation [3]. Additionally, previous studies highlighted the advantages of AUI EVAR for ruptured AAA, even when a bifurcated configuration is feasible, because it allows for the rapid exclusion of extravasation in ruptured aneurysms without the need to complete the contralateral limb, thereby potentially reducing the procedure time [9].

The Endurant AUI is the only device approved for AUI EVAR in Japan; however, its availability is limited in emergency scenarios or specific institutional settings. Moreover, its suprarenal stents may collapse and entrap the delivery system, particularly in patients with highly angulated proximal necks, as observed in case 1 [6, 7]. Conversion to AUI EVAR is also required during the procedure when contralateral gate cannulation fails after the deployment of a bifurcated main body. Some stent graft manufacturers provide dedicated devices for converting a bifurcated device into an AUI configuration (e.g., the Zenith Abdominal Converter [Cook Incorporated, Bloomington, IN, USA]); however, these remain subject to availability constraints. Hongo et al. [10] reported a technique for the back table modification of a bifurcated Endurant stent graft to AUI during the procedure. However, this modification may carry a risk of fabric damage and requires operator expertise and an extended preparation time, limiting its applicability in emergencies. AUI EVAR using an upside-down contralateral leg without a main body has also been reported [11]. This approach may be feasible in cases with an adequate proximal neck diameter and sufficient landing zone length, such as in saccular aneurysms. However, since the distal end of the contralateral leg is not originally designed for proximal use in the aorta, the risk of type I endoleak or migration may increase due to its insufficient radial force. The misalignment of the upside-down contralateral leg may also occur in cases with severe angulation. Therefore, the Excluder AUI technique represents a feasible alternative, particularly for patients with a challenging anatomy or emergencies.

AUI configurations were achieved using one or two aortic extenders in four cases and an upside-down contralateral leg in one case. The selection of aortic extenders or an upside-down contralateral leg depended on anatomical factors and operator experience. To prevent type III endoleaks, a longer sealing length is generally preferable, making the upside-down contralateral leg a better option when the proximal neck diameter is suitable. A type III endoleak was not observed in the case where the upside-down contralateral leg was used. If operators are not prepared for or familiar with the upside-down technique, particularly in unexpected intraoperative conversion, the use of aortic extenders needs to be considered. In our cohort, aortic extenders were preferred under bailout conditions where contralateral gate cannulation failure necessitated intraoperative conversion to AUI.

A major concern with the Excluder AUI is the potential for type III endoleaks due to insufficient occlusion of the contralateral gate, which may lead to sac enlargement. In the present study, two cases using aortic extenders exhibited type III endoleaks on completion aortography. No additional procedures were performed in either case because minimal leakage was detected, thrombosis was expected to result in spontaneous resolution of the endoleak, and dynamic contrast-enhanced CT confirmed the resolution on days 11 and 16 postoperatively. Completion aortography performed before the removal of the large-bore sheath in the ipsilateral iliac artery may have emphasized type III endoleaks, as contrast media stagnation caused by limited outflow could have accentuated the appearance of minor leakage. Under these conditions, the application of our standard contrast injection protocol for bifurcated EVAR (contrast media flow rate: 10 mL/sec, total volume: 20 mL) may have resulted in the overestimation of type III endoleaks, forcibly highlighting leakage that was in the process of thrombosis. To increase accuracy, completion aortography needs to be performed following F-F bypass and after the removal of the large-bore sheath. Based on these considerations, intraoperative type III endoleaks were not regarded as immediate technical failures in the present study, although this deviates from the reporting standards of the Society for Vascular Surgery [12]. If a type III endoleak persists despite these steps, additional stent graft placement should be considered, or in certain cases, adjunctive transcatheter embolization may also be considered.

The necessity of F-F bypass remains a concern in AUI procedures. F-F bypass carries risks of graft thrombosis, infection, and pseudoaneurysm [13]. Although no F-F bypass-related complications were observed in our series, the invasiveness of bypass creation should be carefully considered.

The present study has some limitations that need to be addressed. This was a retrospective study with a small sample size. Furthermore, the cohort included one patient who had limited follow-up, with only a contrast-enhanced dynamic CT 1 month after the procedure. Therefore, the clinical outcomes of the Excluder AUI may have been overestimated. In addition, compared to the Endurant AUI, the Excluder AUI requires a longer proximal sealing zone, which can be considered one of its limitations. Further research is needed to evaluate the long-term durability of this technique.

Conclusions

The Excluder AUI appears to be a feasible alternative for AAA in cases where dedicated AUI devices are either unavailable or unsuitable or when bailout conversion from a bifurcated configuration is required. No sac enlargement, postoperative type III endoleaks, or aneurysm-related deaths were observed during follow-up, suggesting acceptable durability.
