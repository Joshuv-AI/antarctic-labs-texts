Bioinspired and Biomimetic Wetting Properties
Authors: Frédéric Guittard, Sonia Amigoni, Thierry Darmanin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We review the bioinspiration and biomimetism, for which wetting properties are involved. Nature is able to capture or repel liquids such as water, following the needs. The most famous example of water repellency is the Lotus leaves, but numerous other species are also able to capture water. On the contrary, springtails are able to repel low-surface-tension liquids, while pitcher plants are able to capture prey.

Introduction

Nature is an unlimited source of inspiration for surpassing the limits of Science. We believed that only intrinsically hydrophobic and oleophobic compounds, such as perfluorinated chains, can lead to super liquid-repellent surfaces. By the observation of lotus leaves, the key role of surface roughness was discovered. The combination of intrinsically hydrophobic compounds (waxes) with both micro/nanostructures leads to superhydrophobic surfaces with ultralow water adhesion. However, it is now possible to reach superhydrophobicity even from intrinsically hydrophilic rough surfaces.

For repelling liquids of ultralow surface tensions (superamphiphobicity), the study of springtails showed the key role of surface structures but with re-entrant curvatures. Now, it is even possible to repel liquids of ultralow surface tensions even if the surface is superhydrophilic and superoleophilic with doubly re-entrant surface structures.

In this review, we will review the superhydrophobicity found in plants, insects (eyes, wings, legs), and then with anisotropicity (plants, butterflies, fishes) and sticky superhydrophobicity (roses, geckos, Salvinia molesta). Then, we will review the superamphiphobicity found in springtails and slippery liquid-infused porous surfaces (SLIPS) found in Nepenthes pitcher plants. The last chapter will be dedicated to one of the most studied fields: water-harvesting systems present in plants, cacti, beetles, or spider silk.

Superhydrophobicity

Plants

Superhydrophobicity is characterized by apparent contact angles (θ) above 150° and ultralow adhesion between water droplets and the surface. − The most used method to determine the adhesion between a water droplet and a superhydrophobic surface consists of the deposition of a water droplet and the inclination of the surface until the water droplet rolls off it. The inclination inducing a droplet deformation, the advanced contact angle (θadv) and receding contact angle (θrec), and as a consequence, the hysteresis H = θadv – θrec, are determined, but just before the droplet moves. The maximum inclination angle is called the sliding angle (α). A surface is superhydrophobic if H < 10° and α < 10°.

The most famous example of superhydrophobicity in Nature is the lotus leaves, such as Nelumbo nucifera, with the Lotus effect discovered by Barthlott and Neinhuis (Figure ). − They analyzed 200 water-repellent leaves and, more precisely, their epidermal relief (convex to papillose), the presence and density of wax crystals, and their hydrophobicity. Lotus leaves are both superhydrophobic and self-cleaning. Dust also has ultralow adhesion. All superhydrophobic surfaces are structured. The superhydrophobicity of Nelumbo nucifera is due to a combination of both micro and nanostructures (micropapillae and nanotubules), which are chemically composed of hydrophobic waxes. However, natural surfaces have defects, leading sometimes to high contact angle hysteresis. The ultralow water adhesion is explained by the Cassie–Baxter equation if a high amount of air is trapped between the water droplet and the rough surface.

There are an impressive number of publications replicating the lotus leaves. − One of the biomimetic approaches was to dissolve Eucalyptus gunnii ß-diketone tubules and recrystallize them to form nanocrystals on any substrate. Another biomimetic strategy was to replicate directly a lotus leaf by lithographic processes.

Otherwise, for oil/water separation, both superhydrophobic and superoleophobic membranes were also reported bioinspired by lotus leaves. −

Finally, numerous other superhydrophobic leaves were also studied and reported (Figure ). − For each leaf, the surface structures and chemistries are different.

Insect Eyes

The group of Jiang demonstrated that mosquito and fly eyes are both superhydrophobic and with antifogging properties providing an effective protective mechanism for maintaining clear vision in a humid habitat (Figure ). − Fogging occurs when moisture condensation takes the form of accumulated droplets with diameters larger than 190 nm or half of the shortest wavelength (380 nm) of visible light. Here, the mosquito's eyes have extremely ordered surface structures, but especially nanostructures. More precisely, hexagonally packed nanoprotuberances prevent fog drops from condensing. Different strategies were used to replicate the surface structures (Figure ). − Moth eyes also have antireflection properties. Their nanoprotuberances behave as a gradation of refractive index, reducing the reflectance. For instance, coatings improving the transmittance of the glass substrate by around 4% were prepared when coated on a single side of the glass. Moreover, microlens arrays with high-quality imaging were also prepared, even with flexible materials.

Insect Wings

The wings of 15 different species of cicadas were investigated (Figure ). If their hydrophobicity were different, nanoprotuberances were found in all of these wings. Their diameter Ø was 82–148 nm, their height h was 159–446 nm, and the spacing s between them was 44–117 nm. The maximum hydrophobicity was CA = 146.0° for Terpnosia jinpingensis (Ø = 141 nm, h = 391 nm, and s = 46 nm). However, the water adhesion was extremely strong, and a water droplet remained stuck on them, whatever the sliding angle, except for high droplet sizes. Here, the high water adhesion was due to the presence of only nanostructures. − However, it was also demonstrated that some Cicada wings are truly superhydrophobic (CA > 150°) with low contact angle hysteresis (H < 5°). − It was even possible to obtain efficient fog harvesting systems.

Finally, Cicada or dragonfly wings also have antibacterial effects. − They were extremely effective at killing Pseudomonas aeruginosa cells but especially using a physicomechanical effect (Figure ). The size of the nanostructures also affects the antibacterial effect. Surfaces with both self-cleaning, antifogging, and antibacterial properties were also prepared. ,

Insect Legs

Water striders (Gerris remigis) can move very quickly on water due to nonwetting legs. − Their legs are covered by large numbers of oriented tiny hairs (microsetae) with fine nanogrooves, which stabilize the Cassie–Baxter regime. The legs could support a maximum force of about 15 times the total body weight of the insect. − However, the nonadhesion is directional as observed for cactus spines. The water striders also support raindrop collision forces. Microrobots capable of walking on water were also fabricated. −

Other insects or animals can also work on water: water bugs, fire ants or basilisk lizards (Figure ). − By contrast, ladybird beetles can walk underwater.

Anisotropic Superhydrophobicity

Plants

The rice, reed, and bamboo leaves exhibit anisotropic sliding behavior, similar to Morpho aega (Figure ). , However, this is caused by the presence of parallel microgrooves. − The surfaces are also nanostructured. If the roughness geometry is not isotropic (parallel grooves), then the apparent contact angle will no longer be uniform along the contact line. The apparent contact angles perpendicular and parallel to the direction of the grooves are different. Numerous strategies were used to prepare microgrooves in the laboratory. − One of the most used methods is laser treatment. The presence of nanostructures enhanced the properties. The formation of the drag of fluids inside microgrooves was also studied. −

Butterflies

If the iridescent blue color of the butterfly Morpho aega is due to ordered nanostructured wing scales, it was demonstrated that their wings also possess directional water adhesion (Figure ). − A water droplet can roll off the wing only if it is inclined in the radial outward (RO) direction. This is due to the periodic arrangement of overlapping microscales, resembling stairs. The scales also have nanostripes. Ratcheted superhydrophobic surfaces were studied (Figure ). − Due to the anisotropic geometry of the surface patterning, the contact line could preferentially depin from one side of the ratchets, leading to a novel, partially suspended, superhydrophobic state. With ratchets, it was also possible to transport water and fog drops directionally. As demonstrated for shark skin, these surfaces could also reduce the drag underwater. ,

Fishes

Fish, and more precisely sharks, have an exceptional capacity to swim underwater due to enhanced aerodynamic performances. − For instance, the skin of sharks is covered by tooth-like denticles that allow a huge reduction of drag underwater (Figure ). Many fish are also underwater superoleophobic, allowing the prevention of contamination in oil-polluted water (Figure ). The presence of surface roughness is indispensable to reach underwater superoleophobicity. − It is also preferable to intrinsically use both hydrophilic and oleophobic materials. For instance, in the mucus of fish, the presence of hydroxyl, amine, and carbonyl compounds is observed.

Numerous superhydrophilic and underwater superoleophobic membranes were prepared and tested for separating oil–water mixtures. − Exceptional separation capacities were obtained, even with emulsions.

Using superhyphophilic materials, it was also possible to reach membranes with underwater superaerophobicity, which means repelling gas bubbles (Figure ). , Hence, such materials are excellent for the control of bubbles’ behavior on a solid surface in water.

Sticky Superhydrophobicity

Rose

Rose petals have hierarchical structures consisting of micropapillae and nanofolds (Figure ). − Petal roses are extremely hydrophobic but with an extremely high adhesive force with water. A water droplet cannot roll off even when the petal is turned upside down (Petal effect). Petal effect is an intermediate state between Wenzel and Cassie–Baxter states. , However, a dual-scale roughness is not necessary for the Petal effect, and surfaces possessing a single-scale roughness, either at the nanoscale or the microscale alone, can lead to this effect.

Numerous strategies were used to fabricate these kinds of surface structures (Figure ). − With these surfaces, it is also possible to capture and move water droplets even with lossless transfer. , Surfaces reversible from the lotus effect to the rose effect were also reported. ,

Geckos

Geckos are able to climb rapidly on smooth and vertical surfaces. − Their feet are covered by vertically aligned and densely packed microhairs called setae (Figure ). The authors measured with a microelectromechanical system the adhesion force of a setae and found a force ten times higher. The hypothesis is that individual seta operates by van der Waals forces, even if other interactions exist. The adhesion can be improved in a humid environment. − A water droplet can also move if it is super paramagnetic. ,

Surfaces were prepared on mushroom-type microstructures with enhanced adhesion (Figure ). − Other surface structures were also studied.

Gecko-inspired tapes were also prepared (Figure ). − They compared friction (and adhesion), also called dry adhesives, to wetting by a liquid, occurring at solid–solid and solid/liquid interfaces. Smart grippers were also prepared and studied.

Salvinia Molesta

Salvinia molesta is capable of spreading very rapidly, causing environmental problems. , It was demonstrated that the surface of the plant is covered by highly complex and textured structures (Figure ). Hairs are grouped together, forming an eggbeater-shaped structure. Underwater, the leaves are capable of holding an air layer for several weeks, presumably limited by the lifetime of the leaves. − These surfaces are extremely hydrophobic, close to superhydrophobicity, but the sliding angles are dependent on the size of the water droplets. However, they have hydrophilic tips at their ends, pinning the air–water interface.

Superamphiphobicity

Surfaces that can repel low surface-tension liquids are called superamphiphobic or superoleophobic. It is extremely difficult to repel low surface-tension liquids (γLV), such as oils, because the ability to wet increases as the liquid surface-tension decreases (Young equation). Superamphiphobicity is related to superhydrophobicity. The only difference is the surface tension of the liquids repelling by the surface: γLV = 72 mN m–1 for water, while γLV < 35 mN m–1 for oils or various other liquids. The first superamphiphobic surface was reported by Prof. Tsujji. However, most of the superamphiphobic surfaces reported in the laboratory used perfluorinated compounds in order to reduce the surface energy.

The group of Werner first reported the most impressive liquid repellency in Nature (Figure ). − Springtails (Collembola) are wingless arthropods living in soil that are adapted to cutaneous respiration. They immediately form a plastron, protecting them against suffocation upon immersion into water and even low-surface-tension liquids, including alkanes. The plastron is stable even at elevated pressures. The entire skin of the animal is covered by a comb structure with hexagonally arranged cavities. They also calculated the breakthrough pressure, that is, the barrier against plastron collapse. Chemically, the cuticle is composed of a chitin-rich lamellar base structure overlaid by protein-rich nanostructures and a lipid-rich envelope.

Springtail colony of Orthonychiurus stachianus. Stachianus (a). The skin ornamentation is composed of granules at the intersections of the underlying comb structure (b). Plastron surrounding the entire animal upon immersion into water (c) and olive oil (d). Illustration of two distinctive pressure-induced wetting transition phenomena (e–i). Reprinted with permissions from ref , Copyright American Chemical Society 2013.

The cuticle structures of 40 different species of springtails (Collembola) were analyzed. , Their main comb structure was either hexagonal, rhombic, or irregular.

By designing surfaces with microhoodoos, that means with re-entrant surface curvature, it was for the first time demonstrated the possibility to repel liquids with surface tensions up to octane (surface tension 21 mN/m). − However, post-treatment with a perfluorinated compound was employed, reducing the surface energy. Re-entrant surface curvature is crucial for the oleophobicity because it enhances the stability of the composite interface. ,

It was demonstrated that surfaces with doubly re-entrant nanohoodoos can repel ultralow-energy liquids, even fluorinated solvents (perfluorohexane: surface tension 10 mN/m), even without perfluorinated post-treatment (Figure ). , They also showed that even a completely wetting liquid (Young angle θY = 0°) may be super-repelled (θ > 150°) if the solid fraction of the Cassie–Baxter equation fs <6%.

Slippery Liquid-Infused Porous Surfaces (SLIPS)

SLIPS are bioinspired by Nepenthes pitcher plants. − They capture prey with a pitfall trap. The peristome is fully wettable (superhydrophilic) and causes insects to slip by aquaplaning on a thin water film (Figure ). The surface microstructures consist of radial ridges and are filled with a hygroscopic nectar.

Heliamphora nutans pitchers and trichomes on the pubescent inner wall surface. Reprinted from ref , available under a CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 Copyright 2004, Bohn et al.

SLIPS were prepared by infusing a lubricating fluid inside a porous surface. , This surface could repel various liquids but especially with extremely low contact angle hysteresis (<2.5°). It could also resist ice adhesion because no gas is present in the pores. Numerous other properties were reported, including self-cleaning, , self-healing, , anticorrosion, , antibiofouling, , with drag reduction , or for water control (Figure ). ,

Water Harvesting

One of the applications for which bioinspiration is everywhere is water collection. Water-harvesting systems consist of the collection and the distribution of water.

Plants

Badyal et al. observed that plants, such as Cotula fallax plants, possess exceptional fog harvesting properties (Figure ). Their 3D arrangement formed by their leaves and the fine hairs covering them has been found to underpin the collection and retention of water droplets on the foliage for extended periods of time. Water is retained throughout the entirety of the plant and is held within the foliage itself. Surfaces were replicated by writing a tree by laser on a superhydrophobic surface (Figure ). − The efficiency of water collection was ∼36 times higher than the superhydrophobic film. The water is transported spontaneously in one direction owing to the continuous Young–Laplace pressure difference. Various strategies were used to reproduce these patterns in the laboratory. −

Desert mosses Syntrichia caninervis Mitt. (Pottiaceae) is one of the most abundant mosses in the desert. To collect and transport water, they use tiny hairs (awns) present on the ends of each leaf.

Green bristlegrass bristle has an exceptional capacity to collect tiny water droplets (Figure ). The presence of ratchet structures facilitates the coalescence and directionally transports the tiny droplets.

Various barley awns were also studied. A specific hierarchical surface architecture was found for barley Hordeum vulgare awns, which is extremely hydrophilic (Figure ). The presence of barbs facilitates fog collection.

Cacti

Cacti are known to live in hot and dry environments. Jiang et al. studied the cactus Opuntia microdasys from the Chihuahua Desert for fog collection (Figure ). They studied, especially, the spine of each stem. The spines are not smooth but are highly structured with microgrooves and submicrogrooves. The proposed mechanism was the following: a drop on a conical-shaped surface will be unidirectionally driven to the side with the larger radius due to the gradient of the Laplace pressure. − If the cactus is tilted, fast droplet transport without an additional energy expenditure could be achieved. Tests to collect dew were also performed on four different cacti. , Copiapoa cinerea could collect most of the dew. This one was also hydrophilic.

Cactus Opuntia microdasys is covered with well-distributed clusters of spines and trichomes. Reprinted with permissions from ref , Copyright Nature 2012.

Of course, numerous strategies were used to reproduce cactus spines in the laboratory. − Arranged cone arrays were fabricated in PDMS, a soft and hydrophobic material, and compared to cactus spines (Figure ). Hexagonal arrangement proved to be more efficient due to the more turbulent flow field around the staggered cones and the rapid directional movement of water drops along each cone.

Cactus-like structures were also deposited on surfaces already textured, such as meshes. , The resulting superhydrophilic meshes could enhance the coalescence and transport of droplets. Janus meshes were also prepared with cactus-like structures. The fog collection was ∼7.05 g/cm–2 h–1.

For the first time, it was also demonstrated that gas bubbles can be generated by a Pitaya thorn when it is immersed underwater (Figure ). , Extensive bubbles are generated underwater within a few seconds on the thorn surface. The microgrooves accelerated gas diffusion. This discovery is exceptional because the plants need gas to live, and thanks to their thorns, they could survive at least for a certain time after complete immersion underwater.

Beetles

Beetles in the Namid desert can capture water droplets even in arid and dry environments. The group of Parker demonstrated that the forwarding of water droplets is due to alternating hydrophobic and hydrophilic domains (Figure ). Water droplets will preferably be captured and grow in the hydrophilic domains; they will avoid the hydrophobic domains. The volume of the water droplets is an important parameter for detaching them.

Water-capturing surface of the elytra of the desert beetle Stenocara species. Reprinted with permissions from ref , Copyright Nature 2001.

Numerous strategies were used to prepare surfaces with both hydrophobic and hydrophilic domains. However, in order to understand the effect of surface structures on water-harvesting properties, it is important to prepare homogeneous structures. For instance, superhydrophobic bumps surrounded by a hydrophilic patch were numerically tested using the lattice Boltzmann method (Figure ). The water droplet grows on the top patch due to condensation and then sheds from the bump due to gravity. Bump shape, diameter, height, inclination, and wettability affect the shedding volume of the droplet. The shedding volume decreases with the bump height, the patch contact angle, and the surface inclination angle. It increases with the bump diameter. It is smaller on the bump with a hemispherical top compared to the bump with a circular cylindrical top.

Shedding of a condensing droplet from beetle-inspired bumps. Reprinted with permissions from ref , Copyright Elsevier 2019.

Similarly, hydrophilic bumps surrounded by superhydrophobic walls were tested. An increase in the fog harvesting efficiency of 88.3% was measured. Hydrophilic plots were also tested. A high water collection capacity of 2050 mg cm–2 h–1 and its efficiency remained even after 50 test cycles. Higher collection efficiencies were also measured with a square pattern compared with a triangular one. The pinning and dewetting mechanism of a tilted drop pinned by a designed patch was also studied. The inclined angle was also found as a key parameter, but the variations observed were not the same with the shape of the structures.

Due to their controlled porosity, meshes were also used. − For instance, superhydrophobic meshes with superhydrophilic bumps were able to capture emulsified water droplets, even from emulsions. Another strategy was to deposit a superhydrophobic mesh on a superhydrophilic surface. A maximum water harvesting efficiency of about 5.3 g cm–2 h–1 was measured.

Bioinspired by the anisotropicity of rice leaves or wings of the butterfly Morpho aega, hydrophilic channels surrounded by superhydrophobic walls were prepared. ,− This channel demonstrated unidirectional water transport properties (Figure ). When water was injected into the channel, it tended to spread in the direction of the channel due to the presence of superhydrophobic walls. The included angle was the key parameter. Following the same strategy, interconnected hydrophilic microchannels surrounded by superhydrophobic walls were also tested even for fog harvesting. , The optimized water collection rate from mild fog could reach 6.731 mg cm–2 h–1.

Water entering the hydrophilic channel surrounded by superhydrophobic walls. Reprinted with permissions from ref , Copyright Royal Chemistry of Society 2021.

Pursuing this strategy even beyond Janus membranes or fabrics, with superhydrophobicity on the one side and superhydrophilicity on the other side, was developed bioinspired by the lotus leaf, which is also underwater superoleophobic. − These Janus membranes have a unidirectional liquid transport property, as observed with channels. The enhancement in the fog collection of these membranes is really impressive.

Lizards and Snakes

Several lizard species that live in arid areas have developed special abilities to collect water and moisture with their bodies’ surfaces. − Three lizards of different regions were investigated: Moloch horridus, Phrynocephalus arabicus, and Phrynosoma cornutum (Figure ). Their integuments are structured with honeycomb-like micro-ornamentation, close to rose petals. These surfaces were superhydrophilic with an improvement in the condensation of air humidity. For Phrynosoma cornutum, the water droplets are directed to the mouth.

Three studied lizard species with different sizes and morphologies of scales. Reprinted from ref , Available under a CC-BY 2.0. Copyright 2011, Ensikat et al.

During storms, several rattlesnake species have been observed drinking rain droplets collected on their dorsal scales (Figure ). − These scales are also nanostructured. These surfaces are also extremely hydrophobic but with sticking behavior comparable to rose petals. When water droplets impact the surface, they will stick to the surface.

Spider Silk

Spider silks were also able to capture water from the humid air. The silk of the spider Uloborus walckenaerius is textured by periodic spindle-knots while it is composed of nanofibrils (Figure ). Directional water collection was observed along the fibers due to a difference in Laplace pressure. − Artificial fibers were also prepared and evaluated. Hydrophobic fibers were to be more efficient than hydrophilic ones.

Structures of dry capture silk of the cribellate spider and mechanism of directional water collection. Reprinted with permissions from ref , Copyright Nature 2010.

A spider web with a spider with extremely high-volume directional water collection was also reported. It could collect a maximum water volume of 6.6 μL and had a 100 times higher water collection efficiency.

Conclusions

Bioinspiration is and will be an immeasurable source of inspiration for exceeding research limits. This is particularly true for the wetting properties. Here, we reviewed the main sources of bioinspiration to reach (a) superhydrophobicity, including anisotropicity; (b) sticky superhydrophobicity; (c) superamphiphobicity with the possibility to repel even the ultralow surface tension liquids without perfluorinated compounds; (d) slippery liquid infused porous surfaces (SLIPS). The last section was also fully dedicated to water collection.

For superhydrophobicity with self-cleaning properties, it was preferable to have both micro- and nanostructures (lotus leaf Nelumbo nucifera) while using low-surface energy materials (hydrophobic waxes). For antifogging and antireflection properties, it is preferable to have only ordered nanostructures with high transparency (insect eyes). Some insects or animals are able to walk on the water surface (gerris), but the body weight is a key parameter. Underwater, others are able to leave by stabilizing an air layer (Salvinia) or to swim quickly (sharks). For all of these natural species, the surface structures are different.

For anisotropicity, it was preferable to have parallel microgrooves (rice, reed, or bamboo leaves) or microratchets (butterflies).

For sticky superhydrophobicity, the surfaces highly both micro- and nanostructured should be avoided (rose, gecko). It was preferable to have only micro- or nanostructures. Low-surface-energy materials should also be avoided.

Superamphiphobicity can be obtained with surface structures with re-entrant curvatures (springtails). However, if we want to repel ultralow surface tension liquids and avoid the use of perfluorinated compounds, doubly re-entrant curvatures are requested.

SLIPS surfaces could be formed by infusing an oil on a porous rough surface (Nepenthes pitcher plants).

By contrast, if we want to capture and control water droplets for water harvesting systems, sticky superhydrophobic surfaces are preferable in order to capture and release a high number of water droplets (rose petals). For the control of water droplets, it is possible to use a wettability contrast (desert beetles) or a gradient of roughness (cacti).

Finally, this review allows researchers to know in advance which surface structure is preferable for all these wetting properties. However, for practical applications, other properties are also required, such as high mechanical or chemical resistance.
