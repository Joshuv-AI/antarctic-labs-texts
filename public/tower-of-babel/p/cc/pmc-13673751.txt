Incidence and risk factors of pediatric post-tonsillectomy hemorrhage: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xi Zhang, Qicheng Deng, Ling Yang, Min Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTonsillectomy ranks among the most frequently performed surgical procedures in the pediatric population. Post-tonsillectomy hemorrhage (PTH) represents one of its most severe complications, potentially resulting in anemia, hemodynamic instability, reoperation, and even mortality. Nevertheless, the incidence of and risk factors for PTH are not well defined. Consequently, we performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to examine and identify risk factors for this postoperative complication.MethodsAn extensive literature search was executed across six electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, Cochrane Library, and Ovid MEDLINE) from their inception until June 1, 2025. We included observational studies (case–control, cohort, cross-sectional) that enrolled pediatric patients (aged 0–18 years) undergoing tonsillectomy. Data analysis was performed using Stata 16.0 and Review Manager 5.4.1.ResultsA total of 18 studies, encompassing 844,123 children, were included in the analysis. The pooled incidence of PTH was 3% (95% CI 3%-4%; I2 = 98.61%). Background

Tonsillectomy ranks among the most common surgical procedures in otorhinolaryngology-head and neck surgery, particularly in the pediatric population worldwide [1, 2]. In the United States alone, over 550,000 children undergo this operation each year for tonsil-related ailments [3]. Common indications for tonsillectomy include chronic or recurrent tonsillitis, peritonsillar abscess, upper airway obstruction, dysphagia, or sleep-disordered breathing secondary to tonsillar hypertrophy [4]. In contrast to adults, the primary indications for tonsillectomy in children have shifted from recurrent streptococcal tonsillitis toward adenotonsillar hypertrophy and sleep-related breathing disorders, most notably obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) [5].

Despite being a common procedure, tonsillectomy is not without risks, and postoperative complications pose a major challenge to recovery. It is estimated that 5% to 15% of patients require revisit due to complications, among which post-tonsillectomy hemorrhage (PTH) is the most frequent [6, 7]. The reported incidence of PTH varies widely, from 2.41% to 16.22%. Although not exceedingly common, PTH carries a considerable risk of complications such as anemia, hemodynamic instability, compromised recovery, and potential need for surgical re-intervention, all of which impose a substantial physical and psychological burden on patients [8–10]. Notably, PTH is a potentially fatal complication, especially in the pediatric population, where it represents the primary cause of tonsillectomy-related mortality [11, 12]. Consequently, the identification of risk factors for pediatric PTH is paramount for early detection and prevention strategies to reduce its occurrence. To date, however, a comprehensive systematic review synthesizing evidence on risk factors for PTH specifically in children is lacking. Elucidating these factors is critical for stratifying patient risk and guiding targeted perioperative management. This study therefore aims to fill this knowledge gap by performing a systematic review and meta-analysis to identify and evaluate the potential risk factors for PTH in children, thereby providing a reference for clinicians to early identify high-risk individuals and implement effective preventive measures.

Methods

The protocol for this systematic review and meta-analysis was registered with PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews; registration number CRD420251066627). The reporting of this study adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [13].

Literature search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted independently by two investigators following the PRISMA guidelines. We identified case–control, cohort, and cross-sectional studies reporting on the incidence and risk factors of PTH by searching six electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, Cochrane Library, and Ovid MEDLINE. The search was current through June 1, 2025. The search strategy utilized a combination of controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms) and free-text keywords, including but not limited to: tonsillectomy, adenotonsillectomy, hemorrhage, bleed, risk factor, and mortality. The complete, reproducible search syntax for each database is available in Supplementary Appendix S1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study selection criteria were as follows. For inclusion, studies had to fulfill all of the following criteria: (1) case–control, cohort, or cross-sectional study design; (2) enrollment of patients aged 0–18 years who underwent tonsillectomy; (3) reporting of the incidence and/or risk factors for PTH; (4) availability of complete data for extraction, including adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for risk factors from multivariate analyses; and (5) publication in English in a peer-reviewed journal. Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) publication types such as conference abstracts, reviews, case reports, case series, editorials, letters, or brief communications; (2) sample size smaller than 100; (3) duplicate publications from the same patient population; (4) studies that did not employ multivariate logistic regression analysis to assess predictors of PTH; and (5) studies with unavailable full text or insufficient data for analysis. Studies were eligible for final inclusion only if they met all inclusion criteria simultaneously and did not meet any of the exclusion criteria.

Literature screening

The study selection process was performed independently by two reviewers. After removing duplicate records using EndNote, the reviewers screened titles and abstracts to exclude obviously irrelevant studies. The full text of the remaining potentially eligible articles was then retrieved and thoroughly assessed against the predefined eligibility criteria. In cases of insufficiently reported data, the corresponding authors were contacted for clarification. Furthermore, the reference lists of all included studies and relevant review articles were manually searched to identify any additional eligible publications that may have been missed in the electronic database search.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two reviewers independently performed the data extraction and quality assessment. Data extraction was conducted using Microsoft Excel 2021, capturing the following variables: first author, publication year, country, study design, patient demographics (age, sex), sample size, type of PTH, PTH incidence, and reported risk factors. Given the observational nature of all included studies, the methodological quality was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), a validated tool for assessing the quality of non-randomized studies [14]. The NOS assesses studies across eight domains, with a maximum possible score of 9. Articles achieving a total score of ≥ 6 were considered to be of high methodological quality [15].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 16.0 and Review Manager 5.4.1. The pooled incidence of PTH and its 95% CI were calculated. For the incidence outcome, only cohort studies were included in the pooled analysis to ensure methodological validity. For risk factors reported in two or more studies, a meta-analysis was conducted to generate pooled ORs with 95% CIs; factors reported in fewer studies were descriptively synthesized. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic and Cochran's Q test [16]. The degree of heterogeneity was interpreted as follows: I2 = 0–25%, low; I2 = 26–50%, moderate; and I2 = 51–100%, substantial. A fixed-effects model was applied when heterogeneity was not significant (Q test P value ≥ 0.1 and I2 ≤ 50%); otherwise, a random-effects model was used. To assess the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially excluding each study (leave-one-out method) and by altering the effect model. Subgroup analyses by geographic region, study period, study quality, and sample size were performed to explore heterogeneity sources; subgroup analyses by PTH type and surgical technique were not feasible due to incomplete reporting in the included studies. Publication bias was assessed visually with funnel plots and statistically using Begg's and Egger's tests for outcomes encompassing 10 or more studies [17, 18]. The certainty of evidence for all significant risk factors was assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) framework for observational studies.

Results

Literature search and screening results

The initial database search yielded 9,967 records from PubMed (n = 1,058), Embase (n = 2,181), Web of Science (n = 2,923), Scopus (n = 2,575), Cochrane Library (n = 245), and Ovid MEDLINE (n = 985). After the removal of 5,387 duplicate records through EndNote, 4,580 unique articles were retained for screening. Based on title and abstract review, 4,501 studies were excluded. The full texts of the remaining 79 potentially eligible studies were retrieved and thoroughly evaluated. Finally, 18 [19–36] studies fulfilled all the predefined inclusion criteria and were included in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1). No cross-sectional studies fulfilled all predefined inclusion criteria after full-text evaluation, and thus no studies of this design were included in the final quantitative analysis.

Incidence of PTH

The incidence of PTH was reported in all included studies, with individual estimates ranging from 1.5% to 12.8%. The meta-analysis of proportions yielded a pooled incidence of 3% (95% CI 3% to 4%), with considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 98.61%) (Fig. 2). Subgroup analyses by geographic region, study period, NOS score, and sample size were conducted. Geographic region and sample size were identified as partial sources of heterogeneity, while study period and study quality showed no significant between-group differences (Fig. 3). Subgroup analyses stratified by primary/secondary PTH and surgical technique were not performed due to insufficient data across included cohort studies. Sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out method confirmed the robustness of the pooled estimate, as no individual study's exclusion materially changed the result (Fig. 4). Assessment of publication bias using a funnel plot and statistical tests (Begg’s and Egger’s) revealed statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry (t = 3.48, P = 0.008), suggesting potential small-study effects and publication bias in the included cohort studies (Fig. 5).

Preoperative factors

The meta-analysis identified several significant risk factors for PTH. These included increasing age (OR = 1.12, 95% CI 1.10–1.13; P < 0.00001), age ≥ 9 years (OR = 2.47, 95% CI 2.05–2.99; P < 0.00001), age ≥ 12 years (OR = 2.40, 95% CI 1.83–3.03; P < 0.00001), male sex (OR = 1.19, 95% CI 1.08–1.31; P = 0.0005), abnormal coagulation parameters (OR = 3.14, 95% CI 1.15–8.56; P = 0.03), and a history of chronic tonsillitis (OR = 1.55, 95% CI 1.10–2.19; P = 0.01). Conversely, no statistically significant association was found between PTH development and obesity (OR = 0.73, 95% CI 0.26–2.07; P = 0.55), White race/ethnicity (OR = 1.97, 95% CI 0.85–4.59; P = 0.11), OSA (OR = 1.62, 95% CI 0.40–6.60; P = 0.50), or pulmonary disease (OR = 0.97, 95% CI 0.66–1.43; P = 0.88) (Table 3 and Supplementary Information).

Intraoperative factors

The meta-analysis identified the following significant procedural and surgical risk factors for PTH: severe tonsillar embedment (OR = 2.74, 95% CI 1.66–4.50; P < 0.00001), surgery performed by a junior surgeon (OR = 1.74, 95% CI 1.36–2.22; P < 0.00001), and use of cold steel dissection techniques (OR = 1.87, 95% CI 1.41–2.48; P < 0.0001) (Table 3 and Supplementary Information).

Postoperative factors

The meta-analysis further identified significant postoperative risk factors for PTH: use of postoperative analgesics (OR = 4.82, 95% CI 1.25–18.58; P = 0.02) and poor postoperative diet (OR = 4.60, 95% CI 2.22–9.52; P < 0.0001) (Table 3 and Supplementary Information). Notably, the definition of poor postoperative diet varied across the 3 included studies, and the number of supporting studies was limited.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the findings, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by switching the effect model (fixed-effect vs. random-effects). The pooled odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals for all 15 risk factors were recalculated using both models. The analysis revealed no statistically significant change in the effect estimates between the two modeling approaches, indicating the stability of the results (Table 4).

Discussion

The GRADE assessment revealed varying levels of evidence certainty for the identified associations. Factors related to age and surgeon experience had relatively higher certainty, whereas factors such as postoperative diet, analgesic use, and abnormal coagulation parameters had lower certainty, mainly due to limited study numbers, substantial heterogeneity, and wide confidence intervals. These differences should be considered when interpreting the clinical implications of the findings.

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to comprehensively investigate the incidence and risk factors for post-tonsillectomy hemorrhage (PTH) specifically in the pediatric population. Our analysis, which synthesized data from 18 studies encompassing 844,123 patients, yielded a pooled PTH incidence of 3% and identified 11 significant risk factors. Furthermore, a descriptive synthesis highlighted 10 additional risk factors (e.g., longer operative duration, ICU admission) that were reported in individual studies but could not be meta-analyzed due to insufficient data. While these latter findings require validation, they indicate promising avenues for further investigation. The identified risk factors comprehensively cover the perioperative continuum and can be conceptually organized into preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative domains, providing a structured framework for clinical risk assessment.

Incidence of pediatric PTH

To ensure methodological accuracy, our pooled incidence estimate was derived exclusively from cohort studies. The pooled PTH incidence of 3% in children is slightly lower than but broadly comparable to the previously reported estimate of approximately 4.5% [37]. This minor discrepancy may be partly attributed to the exclusion of case–control studies, which tend to overestimate incidence due to their design characteristics. However, the incidence rates reported in the 18 individual studies included in our study exhibited considerable variation, ranging from 1.5% to 12.8%. This heterogeneity may be partly attributed to differences in how PTH was defined and classified across studies. PTH is conventionally categorized as primary (occurring within the first 24 h postoperatively) or secondary (occurring after 24 h) [38]. Literature suggests that secondary PTH (incidence range: 0.1%−4.8%) is generally more common than primary PTH (incidence range: 0.2%−2.2%), a pattern that was corroborated in our review [39–41]. Among the included studies, three [19, 22, 27] exclusively focused on secondary PTH, four [23, 24, 30, 36] reported data for both primary and secondary PTH separately, and the remaining eleven did not specify the type of PTH included. In the four studies that provided discrete data, the number of secondary PTH cases invariably surpassed that of primary PTH, confirming the higher frequency of secondary hemorrhage. An important methodological consideration is that studies restricting enrollment to either primary or secondary PTH alone likely underestimate the true overall incidence, as the calculation denominator (total tonsillectomies) remains constant while the numerator (hemorrhage events) is selectively limited. Beyond this selection bias, the observed variation in incidence rates underscores the influence of numerous other factors. The identification of these factors and the specific risk profiles for PTH are therefore paramount for developing effective preventive strategies.

Preoperative factors

The fact that increasing age, along with the specific thresholds of age ≥ 9 and ≥ 12 years, was all significant risk factors indicates a strong correlation between age and PTH incidence. Specifically, older children within the pediatric population was associated with increased odds of hemorrhage. This may be attributed to larger tonsillar blood vessels, a greater tonsillar surface area, and consequently a larger postoperative wound bed in older children [19, 20]. We found that male children had a 1.19-fold higher risk of PTH compared to females (OR = 1.19), a finding consistent with previous reports such as that by Windfuhr et al. The association between male sex and increased surgical risk is not unique to tonsillectomy but has been observed across various pediatric surgical procedures [42]. For instance, Stone et al. reported that male sex is associated with a higher incidence of postoperative complications after appendectomy, pyloromyotomy, and other abdominal surgeries [43]. The underlying reasons for this sex-based disparity remain unclear but may involve differences in temperament, physiological development, or other unmeasured factors, warranting further exploration.

Furthermore, our meta-analysis demonstrated a substantial association between abnormal coagulation parameters and PTH, with an odds ratio of 3.14. This elevated risk is pathophysiologically grounded, as underlying coagulopathies—such as hemophilia or von Willebrand disease—compromise hemostasis by impairing clotting factor function or platelet activity. These deficiencies inherently increase the propensity for bleeding, both spontaneously and following surgical interventions like tonsillectomy [44–46].

Our analysis found that a history of chronic tonsillitis was associated with a 1.55-fold increased odds of PTH, aligning with reports such as that by Achar and colleagues [47]. The pathophysiological basis for this association may involve chronic inflammation leading to peritonsillar fibrosis and the loss of clear anatomical dissection planes. This can result in more challenging surgery, larger tissue wounds, and potential injury to a greater number of vessels during tonsillectomy [40, 48].

Intraoperative factors

Our meta-analysis identified severe tonsillar embedment as a significant intraoperative risk factor, associated with a 2.74-fold increased risk of PTH. This corroborates the findings of Kim et al., who also recognized the degree of tonsillar embedment as a risk factor in their cohort of 1,397 pediatric patients [49]. The elevated risk associated with severe embedment may be attributed to several mechanisms. First, mild embedment allows for adequate tonsillar exposure, facilitating easier dissection, shorter operative time, and reduced intraoperative blood loss. In contrast, severe embedment is associated with a more restricted surgical field, greater technical difficulty, longer operative time, and consequently increased odds of hemorrhage. Additionally, the dissection of severely embedded tonsils often results in greater tissue trauma, further predisposing to postoperative hemorrhage.

The significant association between surgeon experience and PTH risk is corroborated by existing literature. Hinton-Bayre et al. identified a strong correlation in an analysis of 1,396 pediatric cases [50]. This is supported by Bugten et al., who reported that PTH rates among junior surgeons could be reduced through structured training, such as reviewing videos of procedures performed by senior surgeons, a finding further validated by Shotts et al. [51, 52]. Our meta-analysis aligns with these observations, quantifying the risk by demonstrating that surgeries performed by junior surgeons were associated with a 1.74-fold higher odds of PTH compared to those by senior surgeons. This disparity can be attributed to the technical challenges posed by conditions like severe tonsillar embedment and chronic inflammation, which obscure anatomical planes. Senior surgeons' proficiency allows for more efficient dissection with minimal tissue trauma, whereas procedures performed by junior surgeons are often characterized by longer operative times and less precise hemostasis, which may underlie the observed association with increased hemorrhage odds.

Our analysis also indicated that the choice of surgical technique was associated with differences in PTH odds, with cold steel or cold knife dissection associated with significantly higher odds (OR = 1.87). This finding, however, exists within a context of ongoing debate. Some evidence suggests electrocautery techniques may pose a greater risk, potentially due to increased eschar formation and subsequent sloughing leading to secondary hemorrhage [53, 54]. Furthermore, a small randomized controlled trial by Yilmazer et al. reported comparable outcomes for coblation and cold dissection in terms of wound healing and complications [55]. Thus, the literature currently lacks a clear consensus on the comparative hemorrhage risks of different tonsillectomy techniques. The specific association we observed with cold techniques may be influenced by the nature of our included evidence. As our meta-analysis was restricted to observational studies, the absence of data from interventional trials may have introduced a selection bias, potentially affecting the generalizability of this particular result.

Postoperative factors

A significant association was identified between postoperative analgesic use and PTH risk. Common analgesics administered after tonsillectomy include acetaminophen, ibuprofen, and opioids [56]. The role of ibuprofen, a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug, is particularly debated. Its mechanism of action involves non-selective inhibition of cyclooxygenase enzymes, thereby reducing prostaglandin synthesis. Crucially, this non-selectivity also inhibits thromboxane A2 production—a key mediator of platelet aggregation [57, 58]. This antiplatelet effect provides a plausible biological mechanism for an increased systemic bleeding tendency, which may underlie the association between analgesic use and elevated PTH odds observed in our analysis. Notably, this association was based on a small number of studies with wide confidence intervals and very low certainty of evidence, and should be interpreted with caution.

Bannister et al. confirmed that postoperative dietary regimens had no significant impact on the hemorrhage rate following tonsillectomy [59]. They emphasized the importance of encouraging children to eat after surgery, noting that early appropriate dietary intake and adequate fluid intake are crucial for recovery. Gabrawi et al. reported that dietary restrictions provided no significant benefit and recommended a prompt return to a normal diet [60]. In our study, poor postoperative diet was associated with higher odds of PTH. However, this finding must be interpreted with great caution for three main reasons. First, the definition of poor postoperative diet was not standardized across included studies, ranging from non-adherence to soft diet recommendations to insufficient caloric intake due to postoperative pain. Second, reverse causality cannot be excluded: early minor bleeding or severe throat pain may reduce dietary intake, rather than poor diet being a direct cause of hemorrhage. Third, this association was based on only 3 studies with moderate heterogeneity, limiting the robustness of the pooled estimate. The potential biological mechanism may involve malnutrition and decreased immunity affecting cell metabolism and postoperative pseudomembrane formation, but this hypothesis requires further validation in prospective studies.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies. Although subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed, the exact origins remained largely unexplained, and incomplete reporting of PTH classification and surgical techniques precluded more comprehensive subgroup exploration. Second, most included cohort studies were retrospective, carrying inherent recall bias despite high NOS scores. Third, Egger's test revealed significant funnel plot asymmetry, suggesting potential publication bias and small-study effects, while our restriction to English-language articles may have introduced language bias. Fourth, the inclusion of only observational studies limits comparison with interventional trials. Fifth, the multivariable models varied substantially in their covariate adjustment sets, ranging from basic demographics to comprehensive perioperative variables, which may introduce methodological heterogeneity and residual confounding; thus, the identified associations should be interpreted as observational correlates rather than definitively independent causal factors. Sixth, requiring multivariable logistic regression for inclusion may have excluded relevant incidence-only studies, potentially affecting the generalizability of the pooled incidence estimate. Finally, this review identifies risk factors but does not evaluate preventive strategies, which warrants future research.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis quantified the incidence of PTH in children and identified several factors associated with PTH in multivariable-adjusted observational studies. These findings underscore the importance of clinicians integrating risk assessment into the perioperative management of pediatric patients undergoing tonsillectomy. Early identification of high-risk individuals and the implementation of proactive preventive strategies are crucial to reducing the incidence of PTH and facilitating expedited recovery. Furthermore, this study highlights the imperative for future rigorously designed prospective studies to validate the predictive utility of these risk factors and to evaluate the efficacy of targeted interventions aimed at preventing PTH.
