A Cross-Sectional Analysis of Psychological and Dermatologic Manifestations Post-February 6 Earthquakes in Dermatology Patients
Authors: Şule Gençoğlu, Neslihan Cansel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarthquakes are major natural disasters that can trigger both psychological and dermatological disturbances. The complex interaction between post-disaster mental health and skin disease remains underexplored, particularly beyond the acute phase.ObjectiveTo investigate the prevalence of anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and their associations with dermatologic diagnoses in patients attending a dermatology clinic one year after the February 6, 2023 earthquakes in Türkiye.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 673 dermatology outpatients who had resided in Malatya during the earthquakes. Sociodemographic and clinical data were collected. Psychological distress was assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale and Posttraumatic Stress Disorder-Short Scale.ResultsClinically significant anxiety, depression, and PTSD were observed in 44.0%, 69.2%, and 15.8% of participants, respectively. The most frequent dermatologic diagnoses were telogen effluvium (18.7%), pruritus (16.0%), and urticaria (10.1%). Telogen effluvium was more common among patients with anxiety (p=0.004) and PTSD (p=0.009), whereas alopecia areata and psoriasis showed higher rates in patients with anxiety and depression (p<0.05). Female sex, a history of psychiatric illness, and high education level were associated with the presence of PTSD.ConclusionHigh levels of psychological distress persist among dermatology patients even one year after the earthquakes, underscoring the importance of integrated dermatologic and mental health assessment.Keywords: Anxiety; Depression; Earthquake; Skin Diseases; Stress Disorders, Post-Traumatic

INTRODUCTION

Earthquakes are sudden, unpredictable geophysical events resulting from fractures in the earth’s crust, releasing large amounts of seismic energy that propagate as waves and violently shake the surface. They are universally regarded as among the most destructive natural disasters, not only because of their immediate toll in terms of infrastructure collapse and loss of life, but also due to their prolonged and multifaceted health consequences. With its location along the Alpine–Himalayan earthquake belt, Türkiye is one of the most seismically vulnerable countries in the world, with nearly half of its landmass classified as a first-degree earthquake zone1,2. In this geographical context, where catastrophic earthquakes strike approximately every decade, it is no longer sufficient to view earthquakes as acute events with temporary effects; rather, they must be recognized as chronic public health challenges with implications for physical, psychological, and dermatologic well-being.

Natural disasters exert complex health impacts that extend beyond the acute injuries sustained during the event itself. Survivors are frequently subjected to secondary stressors including forced displacement, exposure to unsanitary environments, poor nutrition, loss of social support systems, and limited access to healthcare3,4,5. Each of these factors independently contributes to the onset or exacerbation of dermatological conditions, either through direct biological pathways such as immune dysregulation or indirectly through psychological stress mechanisms6,7,8,9. Stress-induced alterations in neuroendocrine and immune responses—particularly activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and dysregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines—are now well established as critical mediators linking traumatic life events to dermatologic disease activity10.

The interplay between psychological trauma and dermatologic disease is particularly significant because it is inherently bidirectional. On the one hand, stress, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) can precipitate or worsen dermatologic conditions such as psoriasis, alopecia areata, and telogen effluvium. On the other hand, the visible and often stigmatizing nature of these skin diseases can generate psychological distress, fueling a cycle that perpetuates both psychiatric and dermatologic morbidity11. This bidirectionality highlights the importance of integrated care approaches, as failure to address psychological components may compromise dermatologic treatment outcomes, and vice versa.

On February 6, 2023, Türkiye experienced two catastrophic earthquakes, measuring 7.7 and 7.6 on the Richter scale, that devastated eleven provinces in the Eastern and Southeastern Anatolia regions, including Malatya. These events, followed by numerous aftershocks, displaced millions, caused tens of thousands of deaths, and generated widespread fear and uncertainty. Beyond the immediate destruction, survivors faced long-term psychosocial hardships—bereavement, housing instability, disrupted hygiene and nutrition, and exposure to harsh winter conditions—all of which may act as potent triggers for dermatologic disease expression. Importantly, psychological sequelae such as PTSD are known to persist for months or even years after the initial trauma, potentially exerting delayed effects on physical health.

Previous research has provided some evidence regarding the relationship between psychological stress and dermatological diseases in the context of disasters. However, studies investigating the outcomes in patients with dermatological complaints following the 2023 Turkey earthquakes, one of the most devastating earthquakes globally in recent years, are quite limited12,13. Most existing studies have focused solely on physical and environmental factors and have not systematically evaluated the potential relationship between the psychological effects of the disaster and dermatological conditions.

This study aims to investigate the potential relationships between depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress levels, demographic characteristics, and dermatological diagnoses in patients presenting to dermatology outpatient clinics one year after the February 6, 2023 earthquakes. Given the limited number of studies that systematically evaluate the co-occurrence of mental distress and dermatological diseases in the post-disaster period, the study aims to fill an important gap in the literature. Additionally, by shedding light on comorbidity patterns that may emerge in the context of disasters, it aims to emphasize the need for the integration of dermatological and psychiatric care in post-disaster healthcare services.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted between February and March 2024 on patients who presented to the Dermatology clinic of Malatya Özel Gözde Hospital, located in one of the regions most severely affected by the February 6, 2023 earthquakes. The inclusion criteria for the study were: being 18 years or older, direct exposure to the earthquake, the onset of dermatological complaints after the earthquake, the ability to provide informed consent, and willingness to participate in the study. Individuals with dermatological diseases that had been diagnosed before the earthquake and who presented for follow-up/continuation of treatment, those currently receiving psychiatric treatment, those who refused to participate, individuals with serious cognitive impairments or communication barriers, and those who completed the questionnaires incompletely were not included in the study.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data were collected through a structured questionnaire designed by the investigators. Information on sociodemographic and clinical variables (including age, sex, educational level, history of temporary migration after the earthquake, prior dermatologic and psychiatric disease, etc.) was obtained. Dermatological complaints, for which diagnoses were established by a board-certified dermatologist, were classified into 15 groups according to their clinical characteristics, including psoriasis, acne, erythematous–squamous disorders, and other conditions, etc.

Psychological assessment tools

Depression and anxiety levels of the participants were assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). Post-traumatic stress levels were measured with the Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder-Short Scale (PTSD-SS).

1) The HADS

HADS is a four-point Likert-type self-assessment scale developed by Zigmond and Snaith to assess the severity of anxiety and depression in patients with medical illnesses14. The scale consists of 14 questions seven for anxiety (HADS-A) and seven for depression (HADS-D). The lowest possible score on the scale was 0 and the highest possible score was 21. In the validity and reliability research, the cutoff thresholds for the anxiety and depression subscales were set at 10 and 7, respectively, with scores over these levels deemed abnormal15.

2) PTSD-SS

The PTSD-SS was developed to evaluate the severity of symptoms emerging after exposure to an extremely stressful event or experience16. The scale consists of nine items reflecting DSM-5 criteria for PTSD, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 4 (“extremely”). The Turkish validity and reliability study of the short form was conducted by Evren et al.17 The total score ranges from 0 to 36, with a score of 24 or higher considered indicative of clinically significant PTSD.

Sample size

The sample size was determined a priori using power analysis with the G*Power software based on a one-sample t-test. Assuming a Type I error (alpha) of 0.05, a test power (1–β) of 0.80, an effect size of 0.51 (medium), and a two-tailed alternative hypothesis (H1), the minimum required sample size to detect a significant difference was calculated as 38618.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for Social Science (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) version 27.0. Graphpad Prism 8.3.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for graph drawing. The conformity of the variables to a normal distribution was examined using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers (n) and percentages (%), and continuous variables are expressed as median (1st–3rd quartile). The χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the categorical variables. Multivariate logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress, and dermatologic diseases. A p-value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Inonu University Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2024/5508). All procedures were carried out in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study.

RESULTS

Analyses were performed using the data of 673 patients who met the inclusion criteria during the study period. The median age of the patients was 33 years (26–41). The majority were young to middle-aged adults (56.6% aged 18–35 years, 38.6% aged 36–54 years, and 4.8% aged 55–72 years). Women comprised 60.2% of the sample, more than half were university graduates (55.6%), and 60.0% were employed. Most participants were married (59.6%), while 34.0% reported current smoking. A psychiatric family history was present in 8.6%, a personal psychiatric history in 10.3%, and 12.0% reported prior dermatologic disease. In addition, 496 participants (73.7%) had temporarily migrated due to the earthquake.

Distribution of dermatologic diagnoses

Regarding dermatologic presentations, the most common diagnoses were telogen effluvium (18.7%), pruritus (16.0%), urticaria (10.1%), seborrheic dermatitis (8.6%), acne (9.2%), and lichen simplex chronicus (9.4%) were also frequent. Less common conditions included acne (%9.2), alopecia areata (5.1%), psoriasis (2.5%), vitiligo (2.2%), lichen planus (1.8%), and herpes zoster (4.0%), rosacea (3.3%), pityriasis rosea (2.7%), herpes simplex infections (1.0%), others (contact dermatitis, atopic dermatitis, erythema multiforme, pemphigus vulgaris, Behçet disease, discoid lupus erythematosus, androgenetic alopecia, tinea pedis, and tinea corporis, etc.). Distribution of dermatological diagnoses by anxiety, depression, and PTSD

The relationships between psychological symptom severity and dermatologic conditions are summarized in Table 3. Telogen effluvium was significantly more common among patients with PTSD (28.3%) compared to those without PTSD (16.9%; p=0.009). Anxiety-positive patients (HADS-A >10) showed higher rates of telogen effluvium (23.6% vs. 14.9%; p=0.004), alopecia areata (7.4% vs. 3.2%; p=0.020), and psoriasis (4.7% vs. 0.8%; p=0.003). Factors affecting anxiety, depression, PTSD, and dermatological disorders

Logistic regression analysis was applied to identify the determinants of psychological and dermatologic disorders.

According to the multivariate logistic regression of demographic factors affecting the PTSD: female sex was independently associated with an approximately 1.7-fold higher likelihood of PTSD symptoms (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.746; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.106–2.755; p=0.017), while a personal history of psychiatric illness was linked to a nearly 2.4-fold increase (aOR, 2.408; 95% CI, 1.343–4.317; p=0.003). Compared with university graduates, individuals with a high school education had about 40% lower odds of PTSD symptoms (aOR, 0.596; 95% CI, 0.371–0.957; p=0.032), whereas no significant difference was observed among primary school graduates (p=0.125) (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

This cross-sectional study investigated the prevalence of post-disaster psychological distress and its association with dermatologic diagnoses among survivors of the February 6, 2023 earthquakes in Turkey. Using validated screening tools, we found that 44.0% of patients reported clinically significant anxiety, 69.2% reported depressive symptoms, and 15.8% screened positive for PTSD. Dermatologically, telogen effluvium, pruritus, and urticaria were the most frequent diagnoses. Telogen effluvium was more prevalent among patients with PTSD, while alopecia areata and psoriasis were more frequently observed in patients with anxiety and depression.

Earthquakes are among the most destructive natural disasters, exerting profound physical and psychological effects on survivors19,20. Numerous studies have demonstrated that disasters lead to increased psychiatric morbidity, including anxiety, depression, and PTSD, and may exacerbate pre-existing medical conditions21,22,23,24,25. The nature and severity of post-disaster symptoms depend on a range of factors, including earthquake magnitude, losses sustained, injury, demographic factors such as gender, age, and history of psychiatric diseases, and availability of timely shelter and medical support5,26.

Longitudinal studies have reported that mental health problems following disasters typically peak within the first year and subsequently decline. Nevertheless, some research indicates that adverse psychological symptoms may persist for months or even years27. The general trend suggests a marked reduction in post-traumatic stress symptoms within 12 months of a high-impact disaster, whereas the prevalence of depression and anxiety tends to remain stable. For instance, a study conducted three months after the February 6 earthquakes reported a PTSD rate of 51.4%28, while research by Gökkaya et al.29 covering the 9th to 11th months found that PTSD prevalence had decreased to 17.4%, yet rates of depression (61.2%) and anxiety (79.4%) remained elevated. Consistent with these findings, our study—conducted one year after the earthquake—showed that while PTSD prevalence among individuals with dermatological conditions was relatively low (15.8%), rates of anxiety (44.0%) and depression (69.2%) remained high.

Our demographic findings were consistent with prior reports: being female, a history of psychiatric illness were significant predictors of psychological burden26,30,31,32. The heightened vulnerability of women has been attributed to neuroendocrine differences, hormonal changes, greater exposure to adverse experiences, and differential coping mechanisms31,32. A history of psychiatric illness may increase vulnerability to psychiatric morbidity in the aftermath of such a severe earthquake by weakening both neurohumoral systems that regulate stress responses and psychological coping capacity33.

Contrary to studies suggesting that higher education exerts a protective effect against psychological stress34, this study identified a significant association between higher educational attainment and PTSD symptoms. Although seemingly counterintuitive, this finding may be explained by greater awareness and reporting of psychological symptoms among highly educated individuals. Furthermore, higher education is often accompanied by greater occupational and social responsibilities, which may amplify perceptions of loss and disruption after a disaster. Increased media exposure, heightened sensitivity to risks, and stronger expectations for safety and stability may also contribute to the elevated distress observed in this group.

Dermatologic manifestations vary according to the post-disaster phase. In the early period, infectious dermatoses such as scabies, leishmaniasis, and fungal infections predominate due to inadequate hygiene and overcrowding7,35,36. Over time, psycho-emotional stress emerges as a significant contributor to dermatologic morbidity.

In Hatay, one of the provinces affected by the Kahramanmaraş earthquakes, the monthly dermatologic symptoms of 5,103 patients presenting to the dermatology outpatient clinic between April 2023 and January 2024 were analyzed in three-month intervals. Early-phase findings indicated a predominance of skin infections, particularly scabies. In the later phase, excluding infections/infestations, the most frequently observed groups included erythematous–squamous disorders (psoriasis, pityriasis rubra pilaris, seborrheic dermatitis, pityriasis rosea, lichen, etc.), neurocutaneous disorders (pruritus, lichen simplex chronicus, prurigo), pigmentary disorders, hair disorders (androgenetic alopecia, telogen effluvium), and autoimmune skin diseases (alopecia areata, vitiligo)12.

Our study, covering the early months of the first year after the earthquakes, supports this pattern. The most common conditions were neurocutaneous disorders (pruritus, lichen simplex chronicus, 25.4%), hair disorders (primarily telogen effluvium, 18.7%), erythematous–squamous disorders (psoriasis, seborrheic dermatitis, pityriasis rosea, lichen planus, 15.6%), acne (acne vulgaris, rosacea, 12.5%), urticaria (10.1%), and autoimmune skin diseases (vitiligo, alopecia areata, 7.3%).

Notably, certain dermatoses were more prevalent among patients reporting psychological distress. The prevalence of telogen effluvium was significantly higher among individuals diagnosed with PTSD and anxiety, whereas alopecia areata and psoriasis were more frequently observed in those with anxiety and depression. Even though not fully confirmed by logistic regression analysis, the observed findings are consistent with prior evidence suggesting a potential specific influence of psychological stress on selected dermatoses.

The stress response involves the HPA axis, autonomic nervous system, and immune system. Stress disrupts negative feedback within the HPA axis, leading to CRH and cortisol imbalance, Substance P release, mast cell activation, and elevated proinflammatory cytokines. These mediators not only drive cutaneous inflammation but also suppress serotonin synthesis, thereby increasing vulnerability to anxiety and depression. Genetic predispositions, such as ACTH receptor polymorphisms, may further facilitate autoimmune responses under stress. At the cutaneous level, stress impairs barrier integrity, promotes keratinocyte proliferation, and delays wound healing. These alterations may contribute to telogen effluvium, psoriasis, and alopecia areata through distinct but overlapping pathways37,38,39,40,41,42,43,44. Therefore, the acute stress of the earthquake and subsequent chronic stressors such as adverse living conditions, economic hardship, and uncertainty may have contributed to the development of these three dermatologic conditions through shared biological mechanisms. In addition, the visible and stigmatizing characteristics of these conditions may have further aggravated the psychological burden by leading to social isolation and loss of self-confidence41.

Nevertheless, due to the cross-sectional design of our study, it remains uncertain whether depression and anxiety emerged de novo after the earthquake or reflected an exacerbation of preexisting conditions, and the findings should therefore be interpreted with caution.

In contrast, no significant associations were observed with other dermatoses such as pruritus, seborrheic dermatitis, urticaria, acne, rosacea, vitiligo, lichen planus, and herpes zoster, possibly owing to relatively small case numbers, the predominance of non-stress-related etiologies, and the inherent limitations of a cross-sectional design.

From a clinical perspective, these findings emphasize the value of dermatology clinics as potential screening points for psychological distress in post-disaster populations. The association of PTSD with telogen effluvium suggests that dermatologic complaints may act as sentinel signs of underlying psychological trauma. Incorporating psychiatric evaluation and counseling into dermatology care pathways could improve both mental health and dermatologic outcomes. At the public health level, these results argue for the development of multidisciplinary, gender-sensitive, and long-term intervention strategies in disaster-affected regions. Special attention should be directed toward women, highly educated individual, and those with prior psychiatric histories, as they are at highest risk for sustained distress.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single private dermatology clinic, which may limit generalizability due to potential socioeconomic bias in healthcare access. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes assessment of causality and temporal dynamics. Third, reliance on self-reported questionnaires, while validated, introduces the possibility of reporting bias. In addition, earthquake-related specific stressors (e.g., fear of the earthquake, injury, bereavement) were not assessed in this study, making it impossible to directly measure their impact on individual stress levels. Finally, potential confounders such as comorbid medical conditions, pharmacologic treatments, and seasonal variation in dermatologic disease were not controlled. Future longitudinal, multicenter studies incorporating biological stress markers (e.g., cortisol levels, cytokine profiles) and enrolling large, diverse populations could provide clearer insights into the mechanistic pathways linking psychological trauma and skin disease. Despite these limitations, this is, to our knowledge, the first study to systematically examine both psychiatric and dermatologic symptoms among earthquake survivors one year after the February 2023 disaster.

In conclusion, psychological distress remained high among dermatology patients one year after the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes, with anxiety and depression especially prevalent. Telogen effluvium was the most common dermatologic diagnosis and was significantly higher among patients with PTSD, while alopecia areata and psoriasis were more common in patients with anxiety and depression. These findings once again highlight the complex and bidirectional relationship between psychological stress and dermatologic disease, and underscore the need for integrated, multidisciplinary care models in post-disaster contexts.
