Efficacy and safety of the HIV-1 maturation inhibitor GSK3640254 plus two NRTIs in adults naive to antiretroviral therapy (DOMINO): 24-week results from a randomised phase 2b study
Authors: Samit R Joshi, Ezequiel Cordova, Essack Mitha, Antonella Castagna, Moti Ramgopal, Josep M Llibre, Anja Potthoff, Oksana E Chernova, Sebastian A Nuñez, Choy Man, Jerry L Jeffrey, Cindy Fishman, James Oyee, Aditya Joshi, Ying Zhang, Veronica Bainbridge, Brian Wynne, Max Lataillade

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThere is an unmet need for antiretrovirals with novel mechanisms of action that can provide additional options for people with HIV-1. DOMINO evaluated the efficacy and safety of the maturation inhibitor GSK3640254 (GSK’254) plus two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) in adults naive to antiretroviral therapy.MethodsThis partially blinded, multicentre phase 2b trial enrolled people with HIV-1 from 54 centres in ten countries (Argentina, Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Portugal, Russian Federation, South Africa, Spain, and the United States). Eligible participants were adults with plasma HIV-1 RNA ≥1000 copies/mL and CD4+ T-cell count ≥250 cells/mm3 who were naive to antiretroviral therapy. Participants were centrally randomised to receive once-daily oral GSK’254 100, 150, or 200 mg (double-blinded dose) or open-label dolutegravir (DTG) 50 mg, each with open-label NRTIs. The primary endpoint was proportion with Snapshot HIV-1 RNA <50 copies/mL at Week 24 (intention-to-treat–exposed population). Safety was assessed through Week 24. This trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT04493216 (trial closed).FindingsScreenings occurred between November 18, 2020, and January 31, 2022; 161 participants were randomised (sex assigned at birth: 24% female; 76% male). At Week 24, proportions with HIV-1 RNA <50 copies/mL were 83% (33/40), 91% (39/43), and 76% (32/42) in the GSK’254 100-mg, 150-mg, and 200-mg groups, respectively, vs 92% (33/36) in the DTG group. One participant receiving DTG and eight participants receiving GSK’254 had protocol-defined virologic failure (no resistance detected). Research in context

Evidence before this study

We conducted a literature search in PubMed for clinical trials published between January 1, 2014, and February 19, 2024, reporting maturation inhibitor (MI) use in people with HIV-1. The search string was “maturation inhibitor AND HIV AND (clinical trial OR phase 1 OR phase 2 OR phase 3 OR clinical study)”. Overall, 48 articles were retrieved. Of these, those describing preclinical data, reviews, non-clinical trials, studies that only included participants without HIV-1, and those not reporting on MIs were excluded. Four articles were identified, of which three were phase 2 studies of previous generation MIs, GSK3532795 and GSK2838232, in people with HIV-1. Findings from these studies demonstrated antiviral activity of the MI class. One article was a phase 2a proof-of-concept study of monotherapy with GSK3640254. This study established the potency of GSK3640254 monotherapy (a 2-log10 maximum change) and dose– and exposure–antiviral response relationship with no major tolerability concerns. Data are needed on the efficacy and safety of GSK3640254 in a complete antiretroviral therapy regimen in people with HIV-1.

Added value of this study

The DOMINO study is the first clinical study evaluating the efficacy and safety of once-daily oral GSK3640254 as a complete three-drug antiretroviral regimen as compared with a standard-of-care dolutegravir-based regimen. People with HIV-1 who were naive to antiretroviral therapy received once-daily oral GSK3640254 dosed at 100 mg, 150 mg, or 200 mg plus two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) vs dolutegravir 50 mg plus two NRTIs. At Week 24, GSK3640254 plus two NRTIs demonstrated generally high and comparable efficacy to that of dolutegravir (DTG) plus two NRTIs without treatment-emergent resistance. All GSK3640254 doses had an acceptable safety profile and GSK3640254 100-mg plus two NRTIs had a safety and tolerability profile comparable to that of dolutegravir plus two NRTIs.

Implications of all the available evidence

There is an unmet need for antiretrovirals with novel mechanisms of action that can provide additional options for people with HIV-1, especially for those with concerns of comorbidities, drug–drug interactions, tolerability, or pre-existing resistance. The MI GSK3640254 plus two NRTIs demonstrated virologic efficacy comparable to that of DTG plus two NRTIs, with no treatment-emergent resistance, and an acceptable safety profile. Our findings add to the body of literature supporting the therapeutic potential of MIs and support their continued evaluation, in future research, as a novel antiretroviral class that may expand HIV-1 treatment options.

Introduction

Guidelines recommend first-line antiretroviral therapy (ART) regimens contain two or three drugs targeting HIV-1 reverse transcriptase, protease, and/or integrase proteins to inhibit multiple facets of viral replication.1, 2, 3, 4 However, certain regimens may not be an option for all people with HIV-1 due to comorbid conditions, undesirable side effects, drug–drug interactions, or risk of decreased efficacy in the presence of pre-existing resistance.1,3,4 Thus, there is an unmet need for antiretroviral drugs with novel mechanisms of action to expand therapeutic options for these populations.

The HIV-1 maturation inhibitor (MI) class has a distinct mechanism of action in that it disrupts the maturation process, which is among the final steps of the HIV-1 virus life cycle. The HIV Gag polyprotein precursor (Pr55Gag) facilitates the assembly and ultimate release of infectious virions after Gag–Gag, Gag-lipid, and Gag-RNA interactions and numerous proteolytic cleavage steps.5,6 As opposed to protease inhibitors that directly inhibit the HIV-1 protease enzyme, MIs bind to the Gag protein substrate and block the last step of proteolytic cleavage. The inhibition of the capsid-spacer protein 1 junction site prevents p24 from forming, which results in the release from host cells of immature and non-infectious virions.5 Maturation inhibitors have demonstrated antiviral activity in phase 2 clinical studies of people with HIV-1 who are naive to ART, suggesting therapeutic potential.7, 8, 9, 10

The HIV-1 MI GSK3640254 (GSK’254) demonstrated a 2-log viral load reduction in a phase 2a proof-of-concept monotherapy study in adults naive to ART.8 No drug–drug interactions of clinical relevance were observed with dolutegravir (DTG), tenofovir alafenamide (TAF), or emtricitabine (FTC) co-administration, supporting concurrent use of GSK’254 with these antiretroviral agents.11,12 Here we evaluate the efficacy, safety, and tolerability of GSK’254 plus two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) in adults naive to ART through 24 weeks.

Methods

Study design and ethics

DOMINO was a phase 2b, randomised, multicentre, parallel-group, partially blinded (only to GSK’254 dose), dose-range finding study evaluating the efficacy and safety of GSK’254 compared with DTG, each in combination with two co-formulated NRTIs, in adults with HIV-1 who were naive to ART.

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki at 54 sites in ten countries: Argentina, Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Portugal, Russian Federation, South Africa, Spain, and the United States. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by an investigational centre ethics committee or institutional review board before study initiation. The names of the authorities who granted ethical approval for the study are available in Supplementary Table S1, along with the respective reference/approval numbers. All participants provided written informed consent. The study protocol is available online at: https://classic.clinicaltrials.gov/ProvidedDocs/16/NCT04493216/Prot_002.pdf.

Study participants

Eligible participants were adults who had not received any antiretroviral agents in combination or as monotherapy after HIV-1 diagnosis, had plasma HIV-1 RNA ≥1000 copies/mL (with no upper limit) and CD4+ T-cell count ≥250 cells/mm3 at screening, and demonstrated antiviral susceptibility to the investigator-selected background NRTIs (based on the participants history including HLA-B∗5701 allele testing and genotypic and phenotypic NRTI resistance testing conducted at screening). Participants weighed ≥50 kg (male at birth) or ≥45 kg (female at birth) and had body mass index >18.5 kg/m2. Key exclusion criteria included evidence of hepatitis B or C virus co-infection, positive results for Helicobacter pylori unable to be eradicated with treatment during the screening period (to facilitate a gastric sub-study), any significant psychiatric disorder, pre-existing conditions that could interfere with gastrointestinal anatomy or motility, or any current or historical cardiac condition associated with risk of QT prolongation. Participants were ineligible if they had historical evidence (before study screening) of MI resistance-associated mutations A364V or A364A/V in available genotypes, or any evidence of resistance to the backbone NRTIs.

Randomisation and masking

Participants were centrally assigned to randomised treatment groups (40:40:40:30 aligned with the target enrollment of 150 participants) using the Interactive Voice/Web Response System (the Randomisation and Medication Ordering System) following permuted block randomisation with block sizes of 7 or 8. Participant randomisation was stratified by screening plasma HIV-1 RNA (<100,000 vs ≥100,000 copies/mL) and choice of background NRTI. GSK’254 doses were blinded to all participants and study personnel, and blinded to the sponsor until database lock for the Week 24 analysis. The Randomisation and Medication Ordering System was used to dispense a 1-month supply of three high-density polyethylene bottles of blinded GSK’254 at study sites; treatment blinding was achieved using tablets with similar appearance. Allocation concealment was performed using centralised randomisation.

Procedures

Participants were enrolled by principal investigators at each study site. Eligible participants were randomised to receive once-daily oral doses of GSK’254 100, 150, or 200 mg or open-label DTG 50 mg, all in combination with open-label abacavir/lamivudine (ABC/3TC) or TAF/FTC. Background NRTIs were selected by the investigator based on the participant's history and current medical conditions, including HLA-B∗5701 allele testing. All GSK’254 doses were to be taken with a low-fat meal (400–500 calories, with 25% of calories from fat).

GSK’254 doses were selected on the basis of clinical trial simulations of antiviral effect conducted using population pharmacokinetic and exposure-response modeling. These models were developed using data from phase 1 and 2a clinical studies evaluating doses of GSK’254 in adults without HIV-1 and people with HIV-1 who were naive to ART.8,13 The selected doses were predicted to achieve ≥75% of the median maximum antiviral effect for GSK’254 after 8 days and to maintain steady-state trough concentrations >3 times the protein-binding adjusted 90% effective concentration target (0.11 μg/mL)8 in >95% of participants. It was predicted that the virologic response of GSK’254 plus two potent NRTIs would result in a Week 24 virologic response similar to or better than that of the previous MI GSK3532795 (GSK’795), which was dosed at 180 mg once-daily.7

Study assessments

Study visits were planned at baseline (Day 1) and Weeks 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 36, 40, 44, 48, 52, 56, 64, 72, 80, 88, and 96. Participants who continued to derive benefit from the treatments were planned to remain on study beyond Week 96 until GSK’254 became commercially available. Sex (female or male assigned at birth), race (American Indian or Alaska Native; Asian; Black or African American; Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander; White; or multiple races), and ethnicity (Hispanic/Latin American or not Hispanic/Latin American) were self-reported by participants. Plasma HIV-1 RNA samples were to be assessed at each visit. Repeat viral load testing was performed within 2–4 weeks if HIV-1 RNA was ≥50 copies/mL after previous HIV-1 RNA <50 copies/mL. Protocol-defined virologic failure (PDVF) required two consecutive plasma HIV-1 RNA measurements at least 2 weeks apart that met either virologic rebound criterion (confirmed rebound in HIV-1 RNA ≥200 copies/mL after confirmed consecutive HIV-1 RNA <50 copies/mL) or any of the following virologic non-response criteria: (one) HIV-1 RNA <1.0 log10 copies/mL decrease from baseline, unless HIV-1 RNA <200 copies/mL by Week 12; (two) HIV-1 RNA ≥200 copies/mL at or after Week 24; (three) HIV-1 RNA ≥50 copies/mL on repeat testing of Week 24 results and before Week 28. Plasma samples for resistance testing were collected at screening and planned to be collected at each study visit, except Week 2. Resistance was assessed in the GSK’254 groups on Day 1, at Week 4, and at time of suspected PDVF (the first viral load meeting PDVF criteria) using PhenoSense® GT, GeneSeq® Integrase, PhenoSense® Integrase, PhenoSense® Gag assays for GSK’254, and a next-generation sequencing platform for Gag genotype (Monogram Biosciences, South San Francisco, CA). CD4+ T-cell counts were planned to be assessed via flow cytometry at all visits except Week 2.

Safety was planned to be assessed at screening (serious adverse events [AEs] and Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale only) and all visits with the addition of virtual visits at Weeks 60, 68, 76, 84, 92, and 100. Adverse events were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (version 25.0) and suicidality monitoring was performed using the electronic Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale. Laboratory assessments were planned to be performed at all visits except Week 2, and electrocardiograms were planned to be acquired on Day 1 and at Weeks 2, 12, 24, 36, 48, 72, and 96. To evaluate potential gastric toxicity, an optional prospective stomach safety sub-study was planned assessing evidence of translation of non-clinical toxicology findings (eg, parietal cell and chief cell changes). Serial esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) with biopsies of the greater curvature and transitional zone of the stomach were planned on Day 1 (before treatment initiation), Week 24, and Week 96. Biopsies were evaluated microscopically from digitally scanned slides of sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin as well as one slide each stained for either periodic acid Schiff or Alcian blue histochemical stains. Serum biomarkers of stomach function, including gastrin, pepsinogen I, and pepsinogen II, were evaluated on Day 1 and Week 24.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was the proportion of participants with plasma HIV-1 RNA <50 copies/mL at Week 24 centrally assessed using the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Snapshot algorithm. Secondary efficacy endpoints were the proportion of participants with HIV-1 RNA <50 copies/mL at Weeks 48 and 96 using the FDA Snapshot algorithm; absolute values and changes from baseline in HIV-1 RNA and CD4+ T-cell count through Weeks 24, 48, and 96; and development of genotypic or phenotypic resistance through Weeks 24, 48, and 96. Secondary safety endpoints included incidence and severity of AEs and frequency of gastrointestinal AEs, psychiatric AEs, central nervous system AEs, serious AEs, deaths, and AEs leading to discontinuation at Weeks 24, 48, and 96. An exploratory endpoint included a stomach safety sub-study to look for evidence of translation of non-clinical toxicology findings (eg, parietal cell and chief cell changes). A secondary pharmacokinetic endpoint was the steady-state plasma pharmacokinetic parameters of GSK’254 at Weeks 24 and 48. The present report presents efficacy and safety data through Week 24. For secondary outcomes, changes from baseline data are presented for ease of interpretation.

Statistical analyses

The intention-to-treat–exposed (ITT-E) population consisted of all randomised participants who received ≥1 dose of study treatment and was the primary population for efficacy analyses. The safety population consisted of all randomised participants but excluded those with documented evidence of not receiving study treatment. The per-protocol population consisted of the ITT-E population but excluded those with important protocol violations. The EGD sub-study population consisted of participants with evaluable post-baseline EGD assessments. Prespecified descriptive subgroup analyses were conducted, categorised by baseline disease characteristics and demographics.

Simulations were conducted with varying numbers of participants in each group to assess how changes in sample size might affect the study design. The planned sample size of each GSK’254 group was decreased from 60 to 40 participants after the start of the COVID-19 pandemic (no changes were made to the DTG group), via substantial protocol amendment. Sensitivity analysis simulation results are available in the study protocol and determined that decreasing the GSK’254 group sizes from 60 to 40 participants due to the COVID-19 pandemic had a minimal-to-modest impact on the probability of making a correct positive efficacy decision. The planned sample size was determined based on simulations aimed to maximise the probability of making a correct positive efficacy decision and minimise the probability of making an incorrect positive efficacy decision when selecting the most efficacious GSK’254 dose. A positive efficacy decision was defined as ≥1 GSK’254 dose having a high (≥0.85) posterior probability of virologic response rates within a 10% margin of the estimated 92% response rate of the DTG reference group at Week 24, approximated from response rates with similar regimens in other trials.14, 15, 16, 17 The simulated probabilities of making a positive efficacy decision with 80%, 85%, and 90% most efficacious GSK’254 dose response rates were 0.167, 0.484, and 0.861, respectively, assuming a 2% difference in response rates between each dose; these simulated probabilities support the robustness of the assumptions made for sample size and response rate of the DTG reference group.

The adjusted difference between treatment groups in proportion of participants with HIV-1 RNA <50 copies/mL (Snapshot) at Week 24 and associated 95% confidence intervals were analysed using a Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test adjusting for screening HIV-1 RNA (<100,000 or ≥100,000 copies/mL) and initial NRTI backbone (ABC/3TC or TAF/FTC). A sensitivity analysis assessing proportion of participants in the per-protocol population with HIV-1 RNA <50 copies/mL (Snapshot) was also performed.

Although no formal statistical analyses were performed, Bayesian analyses were used to evaluate the probability that the virologic response of GSK’254 doses had comparable efficacy to that of DTG at Week 24. A Bayesian hierarchical model was fitted using Markov chain Monte Carlo methods18 to estimate the posterior probability of virologic response rate for each treatment group, adjusting for baseline analysis stratification factors. Model convergence was checked by following GSK best practices, which include visual checks of trace plots and autocorrelations and are not limited to checks on diagnostics, such as effective sample size or Gelman–Rubin plots. The probability that ≥1 GSK’254 dose had similar efficacy to the estimated 92% response rate of the DTG reference group was assessed using a 10% margin. Posterior distributions of the difference in proportions of participants with HIV-1 RNA <50 copies/mL were produced comparing each GSK’254 dose group with the reference group. Relative risk analyses assessed the risk of common AEs (≥2%) in each GSK’254 group compared with DTG; 95% CIs were calculated via the Wald method using SAS® software, and significance was determined by a 95% CI that did not overlap with 1. Analyses were performed using SAS® software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC). The statistical analysis plan is available online at: https://classic.clinicaltrials.gov/ProvidedDocs/16/NCT04493216/SAP_003.pdf.

An independent data monitoring committee conducted an interim analysis after 33% of enrolled participants completed their Week 12 visit to determine if any of the GSK’254 doses are suboptimal. This trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT04493216.

Role of the funding source

ViiV Healthcare is the funder and study sponsor. The sponsor had a role in the study design, the collection, analysis, and interpretation of data, and the writing of the report.

Results

Study population

Screening occurred between November 18, 2020, and January 31, 2022; the Week 24 cutoff date was September 26, 2022. Of 369 individuals screened, 161 were randomised to receive one of three GSK’254 doses (n = 125) or DTG (n = 36), each in combination with two NRTIs (Fig. 1). The most common reasons for not meeting inclusion or exclusion criteria were CD4+ T-cell count <250 cells/mm3 at screening (n = 62) and positive results for H. pylori (n = 43). No participant failed screening due to historical evidence of MI resistance-associated mutations A364V or A364A/V. Participant disposition was similar between treatment groups, and no participants from any group withdrew because of COVID-19 or fatal AEs. Demographics and baseline characteristics were well distributed across treatment groups, with a mean (SD) age of 35.0 (10.3) years; in the overall population, the majority of participants were White (77%), male sex assigned at birth (76%), and had baseline HIV-1 RNA <100,000 copies/mL (88%). The GSK’254 100-mg group had no participants aged ≥50 years, numerically higher proportions of participants who were male sex assigned at birth and Hispanic/Latin American, and a lower proportion with a psychiatric disorder at baseline compared with the other treatment groups (Table 1).

Efficacy

At Week 24, the proportions of participants with Snapshot HIV-1 RNA <50 copies/mL were 83% (33/40), 91% (39/43), and 76% (32/42) in the GSK’254 100-mg, 150-mg, and 200-mg groups, respectively, vs 92% (33/36) in the DTG group in the ITT-E population (Table 2; Supplementary Figure S1). Results were similar in the per-protocol population (Supplementary Table S2). Compared with the DTG group, the adjusted differences (95% confidence interval) in the proportion of participants with Snapshot HIV-1 RNA <50 copies/mL at Week 24 were −11.9% (−26.0%, 2.2%), −0.9% (−13.5%, 11.6%), and −16.3% (−31.7%, −0.9%) in the GSK’254 100-mg, 150-mg, and 200-mg groups, respectively. The proportion of participants with HIV-1 RNA ≥50 copies/mL were 15% (6/40), 0 (0/43), and 14% (6/42) in the GSK’254 100-, 150-, and 200-mg groups, respectively vs 3% (1/36) in the DTG group (Table 2). Among participants with HIV-1 RNA ≥50 copies/mL, 3/6 (50%) in the GSK’254 100-mg group and 2/6 (33%) in the GSK’254 200-mg group achieved virologic suppression post-Week 24. Only the GSK’254 150-mg group had a high (≥85%) posterior probability of significant treatment difference in response rates from the DTG reference group at Week 24 in both the ITT-E population analysis and per-protocol population sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S3).

Overall proportions with HIV-1 RNA <50 copies/mL for each treatment group at Week 24 were generally consistent across subgroups based on demographic and baseline characteristics but varied in subgroups with smaller sample sizes (Supplementary Figure S2). Of note, 3/3 participants in the GSK’254 100-mg group with baseline HIV-1 RNA ≥100,000 copies/mL were classified as Snapshot non-responders yet only one met PDVF criteria and two had viral blips (a transient viral load ≥50 copies/mL but <200 copies/mL after achieving virologic suppression) at Week 28 and later re-suppressed. One participant in the GSK’254 200-mg group had missing data for race and was included in the subgroup analysis in the “other races” subgroup.

In resistance testing conducted in all participants at Week 4, no clinically relevant treatment-emergent resistance mutations were detected. In the GSK’254 groups, eight participants met PDVF criteria (100 mg, n = 3/40 [8%]; 150 mg, 1/43 [2%]; 200 mg, 4/42 [10%]). Of these, seven participants with PDVF met criteria at or before the Week 24 visit. Four participants met PDVF criteria with HIV-1 RNA <200 copies/mL in the 24-week window (HIV-1 RNA values at suspected/confirmed PDVF: 100 mg, 125/61 and 155/83 copies/mL; 150 mg, 57/62 copies/mL; 200 mg, 67/138 copies/mL; Supplementary Figure S3); of these, one participant from the GSK’254 100-mg group had baseline HIV-1 RNA >100,000 copies/mL. Three participants were from the GSK’254 200-mg group and met PDVF criteria due to change from baseline in HIV-1 RNA <1.0 log10 copies/mL at Week 12 (0.97, 0.26, and 0.06 log10 copies/mL). The remaining participant from the GSK’254 100-mg group met virologic rebound criterion at Week 32 (HIV-1 RNA values at suspected/confirmed PDVF, 361/524 copies/mL). In the DTG group, one (3%) participant met PDVF virologic rebound criteria at Week 28 (HIV-1 RNA values at suspected/confirmed PDVF, 861/687 copies/mL). No treatment-emergent resistance was detected at PDVF or before Week 24 in any group. Across GSK’254 groups, resistance tests were not conducted in four of the eight participants because HIV-1 RNA was <200 copies/mL at suspected virologic non-response, no resistance was detected in three participants, and assays failed for one participant. No resistance was detected in the participant with PDVF who received DTG plus ABC/3TC.

CD4+ T-cell counts increased after treatment initiation and remained elevated vs baseline levels across visits through Week 24 in all groups, with no meaningful difference in increases between groups (Fig. 2). Mean (SD) CD4+ T-cell count increased from baseline to Week 24 by 241.3 (191.3), 129.3 (233.1), and 202.3 (272.0) cells/mm3 in the GSK’254 100-, 150-, and 200-mg groups, respectively, and 198.5 (285.0) cells/mm3 in the DTG group.

Safety

By Week 24, 86% (138/161) of all participants reported AEs. Incidence of overall AEs and grade 2 to 5 AEs was numerically higher in all GSK’254 groups compared with the DTG group (Table 2). In the GSK’254 vs DTG groups, the most common AEs were COVID-19 (14%–23% vs 28%), diarrhoea (12%–14% vs 17%), and headache (7%–17% vs 8%). In relative risk analyses, there was no significant increase in risk observed for any common AE (≥2%) in any GSK’254 group relative to the DTG group, including gastrointestinal AEs (Supplementary Figure S4). No AEs related to QT prolongation were reported.

The proportion of participants with drug-related AEs were comparable between the GSK’254 100-mg and DTG groups (13% and 19%, respectively), whereas drug-related AEs were numerically highest in the GSK’254 150-mg and 200-mg groups (30% and 31%, respectively). Drug-related serious AEs were rare, with one instance of allergic dermatitis and one instance of pancreatitis in the GSK’254 150-mg and 200-mg groups, respectively. Drug-related AEs leading to withdrawal appeared to increase with GSK’254 dose; no drug-related AEs led to withdrawal in the GSK’254 100-mg group, whereas 5% and 10% of participants withdrew due to drug-related AEs in the GSK’254 150-mg and 200-mg groups, respectively, compared with 3% in the DTG group. In the GSK’254 150-mg group, allergic dermatitis (n = 2) led to withdrawal. In the GSK’254 200-mg group, mood alteration (n = 1); maculopapular rash (n = 1); pancreatitis (n = 1); and diarrhea, heartburn, rash, stomach cramps, and loss of appetite (n = 1) led to withdrawal. Pruritus and exanthema (n = 1) led to withdrawal in the DTG group. Among AEs of special interest, the proportion with gastrointestinal intolerability ranged from 29% to 42% across GSK’254 groups, a generally comparable frequency to the 31% in the DTG group (Table 3). Gastrointestinal intolerability were all grade 1 or 2 with generally comparable frequency of grade 2 gastrointestinal intolerability between GSK’254 and DTG groups. Drug-related gastrointestinal intolerability was similar between GSK’254 100-mg group and the DTG group (10% and 8%, respectively) but higher in the GSK’254 150-mg and 200-mg groups (19% and 17%, respectively). Two participants withdrew due to gastrointestinal intolerability (one in the GSK’254 200-mg group assessed as related to GSK’254; one in the GSK’254 150-mg group assessed as unrelated to GSK’254). There were similar and low proportions of participants who experienced psychiatric AEs across GSK’254 groups (3%–7%) and the DTG group (6%). One grade 3 psychiatric AE of suicidal ideation assessed as not related to GSK’254 was observed in the GSK’254 100-mg group and resulted in withdrawal. All other psychiatric AEs were grade 1 or 2. Nervous system AEs ranged from 9% to 17% across GSK’254 groups vs 8% in the DTG group; all were grade 1 or 2.

Discussion

The MI GSK’254 and the integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI) DTG, each in combination with two co-formulated NRTIs, demonstrated efficacy, tolerability, and a high barrier to resistance through 24 weeks in people with HIV-1 who are naive to ART. High proportions of participants achieved and maintained virologic suppression at Week 24 in the GSK’254 100-mg and 150-mg groups by Snapshot analysis in both the ITT-E and per-protocol populations. Eight participants across all GSK’254 groups and one participant in the DTG group met PDVF criteria, with no resistance detected. All GSK’254 doses were generally well tolerated, with few participants reporting drug-related serious AEs and GSK’254 100 mg plus two NRTIs had an overall safety and tolerability profile comparable to that of DTG plus two NRTIs.

GSK’254 plus two NRTIs demonstrated generally high and comparable efficacy to that of DTG plus two NRTIs. Among the GSK’254 groups, the 150-mg dose demonstrated the highest virologic response rate at Week 24 (91%), an efficacy similar to the DTG reference regimen. Although the GSK’254 100-mg group had five participants with Snapshot HIV-1 RNA ≥50 copies/mL in the Week 24 window, of these, two had viral blips and later re-suppressed while remaining on study (n = 1 each at Weeks 32 and 36). The GSK’254 200-mg group had the lowest proportion of participants with Snapshot HIV-1 RNA <50 copies/mL (76%) but 10% of participants had no virologic data, primarily as a result of discontinuations due to AEs. The efficacy in the GSK’254 100-mg and 150-mg groups more closely matched the efficacy in the DTG group at Week 24 (83% and 91% vs 92% response rates, respectively) and were therefore consistent with efficacy expected of contemporary antiretroviral agents. Response rates for the DTG group were similar to Week 24 results from other studies evaluating second-generation INSTIs (DTG or bictegravir) paired with two NRTIs in people with HIV-1 who are naive to ART.14, 15, 16, 17,20,21 However, higher rates of virologic suppression were observed at earlier time points for participants receiving DTG plus two NRTIs vs GSK’254 plus two NRTIs in the DOMINO study (Supplementary Figure S1), likely reflecting the known rapid antiviral decay kinetics of second-generation INSTIs (relative to MIs in comparable phase 2a proof-of-concept studies).8,9,22

The phase 2b DYNAMIC study assessed the same dose range of GSK’254 but as a two-drug regimen paired with DTG.23 The efficacy findings at Week 24 from DOMINO and DYNAMIC were consistent with one another, with both showing higher rates of virologic suppression in the GSK’254 100-mg (DOMINO, 83%; DYNAMIC, 95%) and 150-mg groups (DOMINO, 91%; DYNAMIC, 85%) and the lowest rates in the 200-mg groups (DOMINO, 76%; DYNAMIC, 77%). In DOMINO, lower rates of virologic suppression in the GSK’254 200-mg group were due to missing virologic data in the Week 24 Snapshot window, because of discontinuations due to AEs and participants with virologic non-response. In DYNAMIC, Snapshot virologic response for the GSK‘254 200-mg group was slightly lower because five participants had virologic non-response, of which four were blips; unlike DOMINO, no participants discontinued due to AEs.23

In the DOMINO study, resistance testing was proactively assessed at Week 4 in the GSK’254 groups even in the absence of clinical resistance (elevated HIV-1 RNA) as well as at PDVF. Eight participants met PDVF criteria after initiating GSK’254, seven of which were within the 24-week window. No resistance mutations were observed at PDVF or at Week 4 testing. Our findings support the high barrier to resistance of GSK’254 when used as a core agent with two NRTIs in adults naive to ART. Similarly, no treatment-emergent resistance was detected by Week 24 of the DYNAMIC study.23 In contrast, 10/12 people with HIV-1 who were naive to treatment and initiated the previous MI GSK’795 plus tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/FTC developed treatment-emergent resistance to FTC and 4/8 developed treatment-emergent Gag substitutions through Week 24 in a phase 2b study.7 Moreover, in a phase 2a study of 10-day GSK’795 monotherapy, 13/37 participants had treatment-emergent genotypic and phenotypic changes.24 An improved barrier to resistance of GSK’254 compared with GSK’795 is also consistent with data on in vitro antiviral profiles.25

GSK’254 appears to have an improved gastrointestinal tolerability profile relative to the structurally similar MI GSK’795. In a phase 2b trial, there was a higher incidence of gastrointestinal AEs through 24 weeks in participants receiving GSK’795 vs efavirenz (65% vs 30%).7 In DOMINO, overall gastrointestinal intolerability incidence rates were generally similar between the GSK’254 and DTG groups. A low and similar incidence of drug-related gastrointestinal intolerability AEs of special interest was observed between the GSK’254 100-mg and DTG groups, but numerically higher rates were observed in the GSK’254 150- and 200-mg groups. Though the majority of AEs leading to withdrawal across the GSK’254 groups in DOMINO were drug-related (6/9 [67%]), treatment was generally well tolerated overall, with few participants reporting drug-related serious AEs. There was no evidence of translation of non-clinical toxicology findings following evaluation of serum biomarkers of stomach function and stomach biopsies in a subset of participants in all groups.

The DOMINO study has some limitations, including a study population with disproportionately high White and male sex assigned at birth demographics. Though each treatment group had relatively small sample sizes, they were sufficiently powered to maximise the probability of making a correct positive efficacy decision using posterior distributions of response rates; further, Snapshot analysis results were comparable between the ITT-E population and per-protocol population. As a phase 2 study with four arms, the subgroup efficacy analyses had small sample sizes, limiting conclusions drawn from these data. In relative risk analyses assessing the risk of common AEs, wide confidence intervals were observed for some AEs due to sparse events.

Ultimately, it was determined that the intended fixed-dose combination of GSK’254-containing daily oral regimen would not be differentiated enough from existing two-drug daily oral regimens; thus, the DOMINO study was stopped by the sponsor.

There is an unmet need for new ART regimens with novel mechanisms of action that provide more choices for people with HIV and may accommodate their unique needs, especially for individuals with concerns of drug–drug interactions, challenges with ART tolerability, prior drug resistance, or treatment-related comorbidities.1,3,4 The MI GSK’254 plus two NRTIs demonstrated virologic efficacy comparable to that of DTG plus two NRTIs, with no treatment-emergent resistance and an acceptable safety profile. Furthermore, GSK’254 100 mg plus two NRTIs had an overall safety and tolerability profile comparable to that of DTG plus two NRTIs. These data support the continued evaluation of MIs as a novel antiretroviral class that may expand HIV-1 treatment options.
