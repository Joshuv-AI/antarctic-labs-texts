Biodistribution and dosimetry of 89Zirconium‐labeled microbiota transplants in the pig gut
Authors: Praveen Dassanayake, Diksha Diksha, Gabriel Varela‐Mattatall, Qin Sun, Sarah C Donnelly, Mojmir Suchy, Dianne Bartolome, Shaena Furlong, Lela Deans, Heather Biernaski, Yvonne Huston, R Terry Thompson, Jeremy P Burton, Gerald Moran, Neil Gelman, Frank S Prato, Mike S Kovacs, Jonathan D Thiessen, Donna E Goldhawk, James Schellenberg, Matthew S Fox
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe gastrointestinal (GI) microbiota, composed of diverse microbial communities, is essential for physiological processes, including immune modulation. Strains such as Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 support gut health by reducing inflammation and resisting pathogens. Microbial therapies using such strains may restore GI balance and offer alternatives to antibiotics, whose overuse contributes to antibiotic resistance. However, effective treatment will require optimizing delivery and understanding microbial dissemination and engraftment.PurposeWe developed a method to monitor microbial migration and GI permeability post‐ingestion using hybrid PET/MRI. To simulate probiotic therapy, bacteria were radiolabeled with 89Zr, encapsulated, and administered to pigs. Organ level and whole‐body dosimetry was determined from the time activity curves recorded over 7 days post ingestion.MethodsWe administered 89Zr‐labeled Lactobacillus crispatus ATCC33820 (Gram‐positive) to six female Duroc pigs (weight = 33.3 ± 4.6 kg) and E. coli Nissle 1917 (Gram‐negative). Scans were performed between 6 h and 7 days post‐ingestion using a hybrid PET/MRI system. The mean administered dose was 74.7 ± 12.9 MBq. Whole‐body PET scans were acquired simultaneously with MRI using a T2‐weighted HASTE sequence. Images were processed using 3D‐Slicer co‐registering PET with MRI and semi‐automated organ segmentation was performed. Gender‐averaged human equivalent organ‐level effective doses (ED) and whole body ED were calculated using OLINDA.ResultsPET imaging showed 89Zr‐labeled L. crispatus and E. coli post‐ingestion localized primarily within the GI tract before excretion within feces. The highest mean ED for 89Zr‐labeled L. crispatus and E. coli were in the distal colon (26.8 ± 4.9 µSv/MBq and 28.4 ± 7.9 µSv/MBq, respectively) and proximal colon (17.9 ± 3.7 µSv/MBq and 18.4 ± 5.1 µSv/MBq, respectively). EDs in other organs were low. Whole body ED were 60.5 ± 9.5 µSv/MBq (L. crispatus) and 66.7 ± 14.9 µSv/MBq (E. coli).ConclusionsThe whole‐body ED for L. crispatus and E. coli is lower than reported values for ingested tracers, such as that from 89Zr labelled antibodies and 111In labelled “meals” used to determine gut transit times. Hence ingestion of 89Zr labelled bacteria shows promise for becoming a human nuclear‐medicine procedure to determine the effectiveness of probiotic therapies.Keywords: 89Zr‐labeled bacteria, PET/MRI, probiotic imaging

INTRODUCTION

By 2050, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is projected to account for approximately 10 million deaths annually worldwide, necessitating innovative approaches. 1 To date, the contribution of medical imaging to detecting infection has been limited primarily because: (1) imaging modalities typically detect advanced infections that have already caused damage to mammalian host tissues 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 ; (2) most imaging techniques rely on inflammation markers, thus failing to distinguish between septic and aseptic inflammation 6 ; and (3) imaging host responses alone does not allow for identification of bacterial species or strains. Addressing these limitations could enable earlier deployment of narrower‐spectrum therapies, including novel non‐antibiotic treatments such as bacteriophage therapies designed to preserve commensal microbiota. 7 , 8 , 9 Currently, medical imaging research aimed at directly detecting bacteria is ongoing in various centers. Existing clinical imaging agents, such as radiolabeled leukocytes 10 , 11 or [18F]F‐FDG (2‐fluoro‐2‐deoxy‐D‐glucose) 5 , 12 are non‐specific and may accumulate at sites of sterile inflammation in the absence of infection. 13 , 14 Consequently, the development of bacteria‐specific probes for direct nuclear imaging of infection is actively pursued. For instance, probes utilizing antibiotics, such as [18F]F‐fluoropyrazinamide and [99mTc]Tc‐fluoroquinolone are currently being explored for their potential. 15 , 16 Moreover, PET tracers such as [18F]F‐fluorodeoxysorbitol ([18F]F‐FDS) have been developed to track select bacterial families specifically. 17 Studies have shown that [18F]F‐FDS can effectively differentiate bacterial infection from inflammation, providing crucial data for targeted microbial therapies. 18 , 19

In this context, our research explores an innovative application of medical imaging to combat AMR by developing techniques to track probiotic bacteria within the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, potentially enabling infections to be treated without antibiotics. 20 The GI microbiota, a complex and diverse microbial ecosystem, plays a vital role in physiological functions, including modulating immune responses. Certain bacterial strains within the GI microbiota significantly contribute to maintaining gut homeostasis and functionality due to their unique interactions with the host. 20 , 21

One such strain is Escherichia coli Nissle 1917, a probiotic strain recognized for its health‐promoting properties. Probiotics are non‐pathogenic microorganisms that confer health benefits through various mechanisms 22 and E. coli Nissle 1917 is particularly important for digestive health. In cases of compromised gut health, E. coli Nissle 1917 is often administered as an ingestible probiotic, however, its effectiveness in modifying gut flora remains largely unknown due to the inability to track its engraftment. 23

In this study, we developed a medical imaging method to track probiotic therapy and determine its degree of engraftment. As E. coli Nissle 1917 is a gram‐negative bacterium, we also investigated a gram‐positive commensal strain, Lactobacillus crispatus ATCC33820, to assess whether our tracking methodology could be broadly applied to both bacterial classes, covering the majority of bacteria relevant to AMR.

Current methods to evaluate the gut microbiome, such as metagenomics and bioinformatics, are slow and complex in clinical settings, highlighting the need for complementary techniques like imaging. As microbial therapeutics continue to evolve, optimizing microbial delivery methods, understanding pathways of microbial migration, and localizing the engraftment of key microbes in vivo remain critical. To address this, we developed a protocol for the in vivo monitoring of bacteria using hybrid PET/MRI. Our objective was to radiolabel bacteria with 89Zr (half‐life 78.4 h) and utilize a porcine model of bacterial ingestion to estimate organ‐level dosimetry following ingestion of digestible capsules containing 89Zr‐labeled bacteria. Accurate dosimetry assessment is essential for ensuring the safety of radiolabeled microbial therapies and is a critical step toward translating this approach to clinical use in humans.

METHODS

Bacterial culture

Gram‐negative Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 were cultured aerobically with agitation in tryptic soy broth for 16–18 h at 37°C. Gram‐positive Lactobacillus crispatus ATCC33820 were cultured anaerobically in deMan, Rogosa and Sharpe medium for 20 h at 37°C.

Labeling bacteria with 89Zr

Bacterial cultures were centrifuged at 4500 × g for 10 min to pellet cells and remove broth, then washed three times with phosphate buffered saline pH 7.4 (PBS, 137 mM NaCl/2.7 mM KCl/10 mM Na2HPO4). The cells were then mixed with neutralized solution of [89Zr]Zr‐desferrioxamine‐isothiocyanate([89Zr]Zr‐DBN) in PBS 24 ; resuspended to a total volume of 1 mL and incubated at 37°C for 1 h on a heated rocking platform, with additional manual agitation every 15 min. This step permitted [89Zr]Zr‐DBN to attach to primary amines on the bacterial cell surface. After incubation, [89Zr]Zr‐DBN‐labeled bacteria were pelleted at 10,000 × g for 2 min, and washed with PBS to remove unbound [89Zr]Zr‐DBN. Pellets of [89Zr]Zr‐DBN‐labeled bacteria were washed until radioactivity in the supernatant reached a plateau (typically < 1 MBq) and radioactivity in all supernatants was assessed with a dose calibrator (Capintec, Mirion, Florham Park, New Jersey, USA). The activity for 89Zr labeled Lactobacillus crispatus ATCC33820 was 0.007 ± 0.005 Bq/CFU and for 89Zr labeled Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 was 0.01 ± 0.01 Bq/CFU as estimated by colony forming units (CFUs) after drop plating. Labelling efficiency for L. crispatus ATCC33820 and E. coli Nissle 1917 was 60.6 ± 11.0% and 71.6 ± 13.6%, respectively. Radiolabeled bacteria were mixed with unlabeled bacteria and 15% glycerol, in a ratio of 2.5:97.5 for E. coli and 5:95 for L. crispatus, then double‐encapsulated in size 1 and 0 gelatin capsules, and frozen on dry ice until deposited in the pig stomach.

Bansal et al., 2015 showed that 0.5 Bq of 89Zr labelled to a mammalian cell did not cause radio‐toxicity. To reduce the possibility of radiotoxicity to the bacterium, we normalize the amount of 89Zr labelled to the cell surface by the cell surface area. Hence 0.5 Bq per mammalian cell with a cell surface area of approximately 300 µm2 (radius of 5 µm) was reduced to 0.005 Bq for bacterial cells (assuming a radius of 0.5 µm). Note this was the target value that was at times exceeded but was not expected to cause radiotoxicity.

Animal model

Pig experiments to image biodistribution of 89Zr‐labeled L. crispatus ATCC33820 and E. coli Nissle 1917 were conducted according to guidelines set by the Canadian Council on Animal Care. Approximately 6–11 capsules of either 89Zr‐labeled L. crispatus or E. coli were administered to twelve female Duroc pigs (mean weight = 33.3 ± 4.6 kg, mean (± SD) dose = 74.7 ± 12.9 MBq) using a feeding tube (89Zr‐labeled L. crispatus (n = 6; mean dose = 79.9 ± 8.5 MBq) and 89Zr‐labeled E. coli (n = 6); mean dose = 71.8 ± 16.9 MBq). To estimate radiation dose, animals were scanned 2 or 3 times over the course of 1 week, with the first scan occurring between 6 and 24 h post‐ingestion, and subsequent scans repeated at 2–3 day intervals.

PET/MRI

To image the biodistribution of 89Zr‐labeled L. crispatus ATCC33820 and E. coli Nissle 1917, pigs were longitudinally scanned between approximately 6 h and 7 days post‐ingestion on a simultaneous 3T PET/MRI (Biograph mMR, Siemens Healthcare GmbH, Erlangen, Germany). PET/MRI was conducted using PET‐compatible MRI receive coils: a 4‐channel body array (with 3 body arrays used in total for whole‐body imaging), 5 table positions for whole‐body imaging, and 1 table position for dedicated abdominal imaging with the body array coil. PET acquisition in list‐mode was performed for 20 min per bed position. Whole body T2 ‐weighted Half‐Fourier Acquisition Single‐shot Turbo spin Echo (HASTE) MRI was acquired coronally as 35 slices with in plane matrix size of 320 × 250 for anatomical information (voxel size = 1.2 × 1.2 × 5.0 mm3). The list‐mode PET data was reconstructed into 127 slices (matrix size = 172 × 172; voxel size = 2.3 × 2.3 × 5.0 mm3) using Siemens e7 tools and an iterative algorithm (ordinary Poisson‐ordered subset expectation maximization; 3 iterations, 21 subsets and 3D Gaussian filter with a full‐width‐half‐maximum of 4 mm). Scatter, decay and dead‐time corrections were applied while attenuation correction was performed using the vendor‐provided Dixon sequence.

Image analysis and tissue activity curves

To quantify organ activity, images obtained from PET/MRI of the pigs were processed utilizing 3D‐Slicer (version 5.2.6). The initial step involved co‐registering PET with MRI, followed by the execution of organ segmentation. The latter was primarily achieved through the level tracing algorithm, which identifies a closed path within the current slice that traces back to the starting point following a consistent intensity value. To mitigate spill‐out effects, the segments were smoothed and eroded before the extraction of organ tissue activity curves (OAC) in Bq/ml over time, using region of interest statistics. Subsequently, the extracted OAC was transformed into total organ activity (in Bq) by using equation 1.

where A is the organ activity concentration at time t and V is the volume of the segmented organ at time t. For the purpose of determining internal dosimetry, the activity of pig organs over time was extrapolated to human organ activity employing the Kirschner approach. 25 Organ‐specific time‐integrated activity coefficients (COE) were computed by integrating the respective OAC for each organ using a MATLAB script, applying the trapezoidal method for integration. The residence time was determined by normalizing the COE by the administered dose as shown in equation 2. 25

Dosimetry

Dosimetry calculations were performed using the OLINDA/EXM software package (version 2.2.3), widely used for internal dose assessment in nuclear medicine. 26 In brief, the software utilizes the Medical Internal Radiation Dose schema to calculate absorbed doses to various organs. 27 Absorbed dose was calculated by inputting the residence times calculated per organ. Subsequently the equivalent doses (in sieverts) were calculated. Radiation weighting factors were all set to one as 89Zr emissions are either x‐rays, gamma‐rays, electrons or positrons. Tissue weighting factors, as defined to reflect the impact of individual organs and tissues on the overall risk of radiation‐induced stochastic effects, were then applied to calculate the gender‐averaged human equivalent organ‐level effective doses (ED). 28

Statistics

To compare the residence times and dosimetry results of 89Zr‐labeled L. crispatus ATCC33820 and E. coli Nissle 1917, we employed a two‐way mixed‐effects ANOVA. This analysis was used to determine if there were significant differences in organ doses between the two types of bacteria and within each type of bacteria across different organs. The model included bacterial strain (fixed effect) and organ type (random effect) as factors, given that the number of organs analyzed was more than three for each type of ingested bacteria. Total whole‐body ED between 89Zr‐labeled L. crispatus and E. coli were compared using a two‐sample t‐test. Post‐hoc tests were conducted to correct for multiple comparisons, for two‐way mixed‐effects ANOVA analysis Tukey's test and for two sample t‐tests a false discovery rate of 5% was used were conducted to further investigate significant interactions, with a significance level set at alpha = 0.05.

RESULTS

This study investigated the biodistribution and dosimetry of, Lactobacillus crispatus ATCC33820 and Escherichia coli Nissle 1917, labeled with 89Zr. The biodistribution of 89Zr‐labeled L. crispatus or E. coli was primarily localized within the GI tract for up to 3 days before progressing to the rectum and excretion within feces (Figure 1). 89Zr was not detected in blood or urine. The greatest PET activities were 32.4 ± 10.5 MBq and 30.5 ± 19.8 MBq, found at 12 h post‐ingestion in the large intestine for pigs ingesting 89Zr labeled L. crispatus and E. coli, respectively. Furthermore, at days 3 ‐ 4 post‐ingestion, 0.4 ± 0.2 MBq of 89Zr‐labeled L. crispatus and 0.4 ± 0.7 MBq of 89Zr‐labeled E. coli were found primarily in the large intestine. Little or no PET signal was detected in the bladder (Figure 1a, red arrow). Furthermore, minimal radioactivity in the liver, lungs and brain was observed for all animals.

Organ activity curves indicate the change in 89Zr‐labeled L. crispatus (Figure 2a) and E. coli (Figure 2b) organ radioactivity, in Bq as a function of time. The estimated human equivalent organ residence time for both E. coli (blue) and L. crispatus (orange) was highest in the large intestine: 1.3 ± 0.5 h and 1.3 ± 0.4 h, respectively (Figure 3). By comparison, the residence times for both E. coli and L. crispatus in the brain were 0.01 ± 0.01 h and 0.001 ± 0.001 h, respectively and significantly lower than large intestine (p < 0.001). Similarly, the residence times for all other organs were approximately 20 times lower than that of the large intestine. There was no significant difference in any organ residence times between the 89Zr‐labeled E. coli (n = 6) and L. crispatus (n = 6) groups.

The effective dose after ingestion of L. crispatus ATCC33820

The highest mean ED for 89Zr‐labeled L. crispatus (Table 1) was in the distal colon (26.8 ± 4.9 µSv/MBq) and proximal colon (17.9 ± 3.7 µSv/MBq). The stomach wall had a mean ED of 5.7 ± 3.3 µSv/MBq, while the lowest mean ED in the GI tract was observed in the salivary glands (0.004 ± 0.001 µSv/MBq). For the endocrine and reproductive systems, the highest mean ED was 0.9 ± 0.6 µSv/MBq in the ovaries and the lowest in the thymus (0.03 ± 0.01 µSv/MBq). The mean ED for the kidneys and bladder wall in the urinary system were significantly lower compared to the mean ED for the rectum (p < 0.001) in the digestive system. The mean ED for red marrow, lungs, adrenal glands, heart, and thymus was significantly lower than for the proximal colon, distal colon, and stomach (p < 0.001 for all comparisons), further indicating that 89Zr‐labeled bacteria mainly remained in the gut. The whole‐body ED for L. crispatus was 60.5 ± 9.5 µSv/MBq.

DISCUSSION

The results of this study provide insight into the biodistribution and dosimetry of 89Zr‐labeled Lactobacillus crispatus ATCC33820 and Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 in a porcine model. The model was selected due to its physiological and anatomical similarities to humans, including comparable organ size and digestive processes, despite some differences. 29 In healthy female pigs, repetitive hybrid imaging using PET/MRI after a single ingested dose showed that both Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative 89Zr‐labeled bacteria primarily localize within the GI tract before descending through the rectum for excretion within feces over the course of 3 to 4 days post‐ingestion. This behavior aligns with the known colonization patterns of these commensal bacteria. 29

The highest residence time was observed in the large intestine for both E. coli and L. crispatus (1.3 ± 0.5 h and 1.3 ± 0.4 h, respectively), reflecting the colon's role as the primary site of bacterial colonization. 30 , 31 Similarly, the higher activity in the small intestine compared to rectum and spleen are consistent with the differential distribution and retention of bacteria in various gut regions. 32 The high activity observed in the large intestine at 12 h post‐ingestion suggests that radiolabeled bacteria are retained longer in this region, consistent with the longer transit time of ingested material passing through the large intestine compared to other parts of the digestive system. 33 However, it is important to note that the maximum retention time between 0 and 12 h is unknown, which represents a limitation of this study. The decay of organ radioactivity to background levels by days 3–5 post‐ingestion indicates natural clearance and metabolic processing of both radiolabeled and non‐radiolabeled bacteria, consistent with studies on 89Zr‐labeled antibodies. 34 , 35 Lower activities in the bladder, kidney, and liver suggest minimal systemic radiation absorption, reducing potential off‐target effects and radiotoxicity.

The highest mean ED for both bacteria was observed in the distal and proximal colon, consistent with their high residence time in these regions. Conversely, ED estimates for other organs such as the brain, lungs, heart, liver, kidneys, small intestine, bladder, and rectum remained low, below 5 µSv/MBq. Although the radiolabeled bacteria primarily stay within the GI tract, neighbouring organs like the lungs and gonads may still receive doses due to their proximity. Also, in the endocrine and reproductive systems highest mean ED was observed in the ovaries for both L. crispatus and E. coli, suggesting potential sex/gender implications on microbial therapy and highlighting the need to consider sex‐specific responses in treatment development. Furthermore, the weighting factors, which vary based on radiosensitivity, also contribute to higher ED in certain digestive organs. For example, the colon, lungs, and stomach have higher weighting factors (0.12), while the liver, esophagus, and bladder have lower factors (0.04). Higher weighting factors indicate greater sensitivity to radiation and contribute more to the effective dose. It is important to note that there were no significant differences in the residence times or ED estimated between E. coli and L. crispatus. While these bacteria may not significantly impact the biodistribution or dosimetry of 89Zr‐labeled bacteria in the gut, it is important to avoid overgeneralizing, as not all bacteria are commensal. Tracking 89Zr‐labeled bacteria can provide valuable insights into their behavior and utility in the gut. This model could be applied to study more complex microbial therapies and the in vivo mechanisms of bacterial interactions within the gut.

The estimated ED of 4.6 ± 1.0 mSv (∼60.5μSvMBq−1×71.4MBq) and 4.6 ± 0.7 mSv (∼66.7μSvMBq−1×71.4MBq) observed for L. crispatus and E. coli, respectively, were comparable to the whole‐body ED from established nuclear medicine gut transit studies 36 such as those using [111In]In‐DTPA‐labeled liquids, where whole‐body EDs can reach up to 12.6 mSv. For example, Srinivasan et al. (2020) reported a whole‐body ED of 6.8 ± 0.6 mSv after orally administering 370 MBq of [18F]F‐FDG and Kjærgaard et al. (2016) observed effective doses of 6.5 mSv and 6.9 mSv after orally administering 57.3 and 61.3 MBq [64Cu]CuCl2 in human controls. 26 , 37 However, both these tracers were absorbed by the intestine. As reported by Srinivasan et al. (2020), this led to an ED of 10.9 ± 1.9 mSv for the brain. In contrast, our model showed 89Zr‐labeled bacteria localized mainly in the gut, with low doses to organs like the brain (mean ED of 0.9 ± 1.1 mSv for L. crispatus and 0.3 ± 0.1 mSv for E. coli).

CONCLUSION

This study provides valuable data from a live porcine model on the biodistribution and dosimetry of ingested 89Zr‐labeled L. crispatus ATCC33820 and E. coli Nissle 1917. We found that the effective dose (ED) was significantly higher in the gut compared to other organs, while extremely low levels of ED were observed in the brain. These findings suggest that use of these bacteria in nuclear medicine could be beneficial for tracking and studying the behavior of probiotics in the gut. This has potential applications in microbial therapeutics, where understanding the biodistribution and dosimetry of administered bacteria is crucial for optimizing treatment efficacy and ensuring patient safety. Currently, there are limited studies utilizing imaging techniques to track microbial therapeutics. Incorporating imaging could provide deeper insights into the mechanisms of action and improve the precision of these therapies. Furthermore, the long half‐life of 89Zr is suitable for extended imaging studies but requires consideration of prolonged radiation exposure. However, our analysis after one week indicates that the effective doses are well below safety limits for ingestion models. Further studies are needed to confirm these results in healthy human volunteers and explore the potential clinical implications of real time bacterial imaging throughout the gut, including long‐term effects of therapeutic microbes and their interactions in vivo.
