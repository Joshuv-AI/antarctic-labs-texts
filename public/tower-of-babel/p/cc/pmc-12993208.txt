Seasonal and processing differences in nonvolatile compounds and volatile aroma of teas from Zhaoyuan, China: insight into “spring green, summer black, autumn dark” quality pattern
Authors: Lili Tian, Yuantao Yao, Ya Zhang, Xingmin Zhang, Jia Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTargeting northern marginal tea regions (37°N, Zhaoyuan, China), this study uniquely integrates seasonal processing (spring green, summer black, autumn dark tea) with chemical-sensory analyses on a single Camellia sinensis garden, filling the gap in volatile aroma compound (VAC) profiles of seasonally processed northern teas. Sensory traits diverged by type: SGT had the highest water extract, free amino acids and theanine; SBT the highest polyphenols; ADT the lowest polyphenols (microbial degradation). GC × GC-Q-TOFMS identified 357 VACs (key ROAV>1 compounds varied). Climate regulated precursors, processing drove metabolism, with strong nonvolatile-volatile correlations. Findings validate the seasonal quality pattern and optimize local tea utilization.Keywords: Camellia sinensis, Correlation analysis, Non-volatile compounds, Processing technology, ROAV, Seasonal variation, Volatile aroma compounds

Introduction

Tea, derived from the leaves of Camellia sinensis (L.) O. Kuntze, is one of the most widely consumed nonalcoholic beverages worldwide due to its distinctive sensory profiles and diversity of bioactive compounds (Zheng et al., 2023). The quality of tea is primarily influenced by nonvolatile metabolites such as tea polyphenols (TPs), free amino acids (FAAs), theanine (Thea), and caffeine, as well as volatile aroma compounds (VACs), which collectively contribute to its taste, aroma, and health-promoting properties (Ma et al., 2022; Mi, Han, Wang, & Han, 2024). These metabolites are dynamically regulated by various factors, including geographical origin, climatic conditions, cultivar, and processing techniques, making tea quality a complex and context-dependent trait (Peng et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024).

Latitude is a key geographical factor that profoundly influences tea metabolism by modulating solar radiation, temperature regimes, and growing season length (Lee et al., 2015). Most commercial tea-producing regions in China are situated between 23°N and 32°N, where subtropical and temperate climates promote optimal growth (Peng et al., 2023). In contrast, northern regions (≥35°N) are considered marginal for tea cultivation due to shorter growing seasons, lower average temperatures, and greater diurnal temperature fluctuations (Yan et al., 2021). However, recent studies have highlighted distinctive quality attributes in tea from the northern regions: for example, spring green tea (SGT) from Shandong Province exhibits exceptional freshness and high amino acid content, which are attributed to slow growth under cool spring conditions (Wang., 2025). Despite this, the seasonal utilization of northern tea leaves is inadequate. Traditional processing focuses narrowly on SGT. Consequently, summer and autumn leaves remain underutilized due to their inherent bitterness and astringency, largely caused by high polyphenol accumulation (Liang et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2021). Notably, no systematic efforts have been made to link tailored seasonal processing strategies to VAC profiles in these underutilized northern tea batches.

Seasonal variations further exacerbate differences in tea leaf composition. Spring leaves are harvested after winter dormancy, and hence tend to accumulate high levels of FAAs and Thea but low levels of TPs, resulting in a fresh and mellow taste (Bhandari, 2019). Summer leaves are exposed to high temperatures and intense sunlight, and hence prioritize TP synthesis at the expense of amino acids, resulting in increased bitterness (Qi, Dai, Tan, et al., 2016). Autumn leaves are grown under cool and dry conditions, and therefore display moderate levels of polyphenol and amino acid but often accumulate woody or earthy VACs (Wang et al., 2022). Tailored processing strategies have been developed to address the aforementioned disparities: black tea fermentation (where enzymatic oxidation converts catechins into theaflavins and thearubigins, reducing bitterness) for summer leaves rich in TPs; and dark tea pile-fermentation (via microbial transformation of harsh compounds into mellow metabolites) for autumn leaves (Yan, Xiao, Suo, Ma, & Xiao, 2012; Zhang, Wang, Hu, & Ding, 2011). Yet, how these processing strategies reshape VAC profiles in northern teas, and whether they yield consistent, desirable aroma characteristics, remains largely unknown.

VACs are crucial to tea sensory identity, with more than 1000 compounds identified, including aldehydes, esters, terpenoids, and ketones (Xu et al., 2025). The relative odor activity value (ROAV) is widely used to identify key aroma-active compounds (ROAV ≥1) and auxiliary contributors (0.1 ≤ ROAV <1) (Xu, Wang, Zhu, Shi, & Lin, 2023). Green teas are typically characterized by fresh, floral VACs, such as linalool and 1-octen-3-ol; black teas by fruity, malty notes, such as 2-phenylethanol and (E,E)-2,4-heptadienal; and dark teas by earthy, woody compounds, such as cedrol and dimethyl sulfide (Hu et al., 2025； Ma et al., 2021). However, the vast majority of these characterizations focus on southern Chinese teas or disparate gardens across different regions, creating a critical research gap in understanding the VAC profiles of seasonally processed teas from the same northern garden. This gap is particularly pronounced given the unique climatic constraints of northern marginal regions, which may alter both precursor accumulation and metabolite transformation during processing.

Nonvolatile compounds also influence aroma formation. TPs undergo oxidation to generate aldehydes and ketones, whereas FAAs degrade into alcohols and esters during processing (Fang, Liu, Xiao, Ma, & Huang, 2023； Li et al., 2020). For instance, black tea fermentation involves polyphenol oxidase (PPO)-catalyzed oxidation of catechins to quinones, which then react with amino acids to produce aroma-active aldehydes (Chen, Wang, Ye, Wang, & Xu, 2023). In dark tea, the microbial enzymes, such as those from Aspergillus niger, decompose polysaccharides and proteins into precursors for Maillard reactions, leading to the formation of volatile heterocyclics (Li et al., 2022). Despite this, the correlations between nonvolatile metabolites and VACs in seasonally processed northern teas remain underexplored, limiting our ability to optimize processing for targeted aroma quality.

Zhaoyuan (37°N) in Shandong Province is a representative northern tea-producing region, characterized by unique climatic conditions, such as an annual temperature of 11.5 °C, precipitation of 650 mm, and long summer daylight, which may influence distinct patterns of metabolite accumulation (Sun et al., 2023). A “Spring Green, Summer Black, Autumn Dark” processing model has been proposed to optimize seasonal utilization, but its impact on tea quality-especially VAC profiles-and underlying mechanisms remain uncharacterized.

This study aimed to: (1) evaluate the sensory quality of SGT, summer black tea (SBT), and autumn dark tea (ADT) from the same Zhaoyuan garden; (2) quantify key nonvolatiles (water extract, TPs, FAAs, Thea, and catechins) and identify VACs; (3) determine key aroma-active compounds via ROAV; (4) analyze correlations between nonvolatiles and VACs; and (5) explore quality formation mechanisms linking seasonal climate with processing. By addressing the critical gap in VAC characterization for seasonally processed teas from a single northern marginal garden, the findings may support the northern seasonal processing model and enhance the economic value of marginal tea regions.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Tea samples

Fresh tea shoots (one bud and two leaves) were collected from a 10-year-old C. sinensis garden in Zhaoyuan City, Shandong Province, China. The garden has a temperate monsoon climate with a mean annual temperature of 11.5 °C, mean precipitation of 650 mm, and mean sunshine duration of 2752 h. The soil is brown loam (pH 5.7 and organic matter 1%) (Table 1). Fresh shoots (25 kg per season) were transported to the laboratory within 2 h and processed into three tea types.

Tea processing

Spring green tea

SGT was processed using the traditional green tea method endemic to northern marginal tea-growing regions (Zhaoyuan, China) (Zhang et al., 2021), with key parameters optimized via preliminary experiments to accommodate the biochemical characteristics of local Camellia sinensis leaves.

Withering: Fresh tea leaves were harvested and naturally withered until the grassy green odor faded and a delicate floral fragrance emerged; this step was critical to reduce leaf rigidity and enhance the release of flavor precursors during subsequent processing. Fixation: The wilted tea shoots were treated in a drum-type fixation machine (Jiayou, Fujian, China) at 800 W and 300 °C ± 5 °C for 2.5 min. This temperature and duration were selected to rapidly and completely inactivate polyphenol oxidase (PPO) and peroxidase (POD), preventing enzymatic oxidation of tea polyphenols and preserving the fresh, umami sensory attributes characteristic of northern spring green tea; preliminary trials confirmed that temperatures below 290 °C failed to fully inhibit enzyme activity, while temperatures exceeding 310 °C caused leaf scorching and off-flavors. Rolling: The fixed tea leaves were rolled using a spiral rolling machine at 40 rpm for 20 min to disrupt cell structures and promote the uniform distribution of intracellular compounds. Drying: The rolled tea leaves were dried in a hot-air oven at 90 °C ± 2 °C for 3 h until the moisture content was ≤5%, a threshold that ensures long-term storage stability without compromising the tea's volatile aroma profile.

Summer black tea

SBT was processed using the orthodox black tea method, with parameters tailored to the high-polyphenol content of northern summer tea leaves (Zhang, Wu, Ma, et al., 2025).

Wilting: Fresh tea shoots were wilted in a well-ventilated chamber at 25 °C ± 1 °C and 70% ± 5% relative humidity (RH) for 12–20 h until the moisture content reached 62% ±2%. This environmental regime was optimized to moderate the activity of endogenous enzymes (e.g., PPO) while preventing excessive water loss; preliminary experiments demonstrated that RH below 65% caused uneven wilting and brittle leaves, whereas RH above 75% prolonged wilting and induced microbial spoilage. Rolling: The wilted leaves were rolled at 50 rpm for 90 min to further break down cell walls and release intracellular substances (e.g., catechins, amino acids), providing sufficient substrates for fermentation. Fermentation: The rolled leaves were fermented in a controlled chamber at 29 °C ± 1 °C and 95 ± 3% RH for 5 h until they turned uniform reddish-brown. This temperature and high humidity were chosen to maximize PPO-mediated oxidation of catechins into theaflavins and thearubigins, the key pigments and flavor compounds responsible for the fruity, malty notes of black tea; lower temperatures would slow fermentation, while higher temperatures would degrade these valuable metabolites. Drying: The fermented leaves were dried at 100 °C ± 2 °C for 4 h to reduce the moisture content to ≤5%, halting further enzymatic reactions and stabilizing the tea's quality.

Autumn dark tea

ADT was processed using the post-fermented dark tea method, with parameters adjusted to leverage the microbial transformation of northern autumn tea leaves (Zhao et al., 2022).

Fixation: Fresh shoots were fixed using an 800-W drum-type fixation machine at 300 °C ± 5 °C for 3 min; this step inactivated harmful enzymes and reduced leaf moisture, creating favorable conditions for subsequent microbial growth during pile-fermentation. Rolling: The fixed leaves were rolled at 40 rpm for 40 min to increase leaf surface area and facilitate microbial colonization. Pile-fermentation: The rolled leaves were piled to a height of 60 cm, incubated at 32 °C ± 3 °C and 85 ± 5% RH for 12 h until the tea acquired a characteristic pork liver color and a mellow, earthy aroma (replacing the initial grassy note). The pile height and environmental conditions were optimized to promote the proliferation of beneficial microorganisms (e.g., Aspergillus niger, Penicillium), which mediate the degradation of bitter polyphenols and the synthesis of unique aroma compounds; a pile height below 50 cm failed to generate sufficient microbial heat, while heights above 70 cm caused overheating and rancidity. Drying: The pile-fermented leaves were dried at 65 °C ± 2 °C for 6 h to a moisture content of ≤5%, inhibiting microbial activity and ensuring storage stability.

All finished teas were ground through a 40-mesh sieve and stored in sealed aluminum bags at 4 °C until further analysis.

Experimental materials

Anhydrous ethanol (CH3CH2OH) was purchased from Damao Chemical Reagent Factory in Tianjin, China. Sodium chloride (NaCl) was sourced from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. in Shanghai, China. Distilled water was acquired from the Wahaha Group Company in Hangzhou, China. Ethyl decanoate was procured from Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. in Shanghai, China. N-alkanes (C6–C40) were bought from Sigma–Aldrich in Shanghai, China. Aroma standards, including hexanal and (E)-3-hexenol, were obtained from Shanghai Zhenzhun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., both located in Shanghai, China. The purity of all aroma standards was found to be more than 95% by GC.

Sensory evaluation

Seven trained evaluators (three men and four women, aged 25–45 years), each with more than 5 years of experience in tea sensory evaluation, conducted the assessment in accordance with China's national standards (GB/T 23776–2018). Each 3-g tea sample was infused in 150 mL of boiling distilled water for 5 min. Sensory characteristics, such as aroma, taste, and overall palatability, were scored on a 10-point scale. Each sample was evaluated in triplicate, and the average score was calculated. A 10-point intensity scale (0 = none, 10 = extremely strong) was used to evaluate the intensity of these 9 sensory attributes, and each of the 3 tea samples was evaluated three times (Zheng et al., 2025). Subsequently, a radar chart was generated to illustrate the sensory disparities among the three types of tea.

This sensory evaluation study did not require ethical approval in accordance with the local institutional guidelines and relevant regulations for non-clinical sensory research involving healthy volunteers. All participants were fully informed of the study purpose, procedures, potential risks, and their right to withdraw from the study at any time without providing a reason and without facing any adverse consequences prior to participation. They provided written informed consent for their involvement and the use of their sensory data. Appropriate protocols were strictly followed to protect the rights, privacy, and well-being of all participants throughout the research process, ensuring compliance with ethical principles of human subject research.

Determination of the contents of nonvolatile compounds

The content of water extract was ascertained in accordance with GB/T 8305-2013 and GB/T 8314–2013. TPs were quantified using the Folin–Ciocalteu method (GB/T 8313–2020) (Singleton et al., 1999). FAAs were measured by the ninhydrin colorimetric method (GB/T 8314–2013; (Li et al., 2018). Thea analysis was carried out via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Agilent 1260, CA, USA) (Xiao et al., 2019). The contents of catechins and gallic acid were determined using HPLC under the following conditions (Tian et al., 2025).

Analysis of VACs

GC × GC-Q-TOFMS conditions

GC × GC Conditions: The volatile compounds adsorbed by the CAR/PDMS-coated fibers were separated using the 8890 GC × GC instrument. The one-dimensional (1D) and two-dimensional (2D) columns employed were an HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 250 um × 0.25 um, Agilent Technologies) and a DB-17MS capillary column (2.89 m × 180 umx0.18 um, Agilent Technologies), respectively (Wen et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024). The injection port and transfer line temperatures were 250 °C and 280 °C, respectively. Helium (99.99% purity) was the carrier gas, with flow rates of 1 and 1.5 mL/min, respectively, for the two columns. A solid-state modulator SSM1800 (J&X Technologies Shanghai, China,) with HV series modulation columns was placed between the 1D and 2D columns for concentration, aggregation, and release. The modulation period was set to 4.0 s. The heating program was as follows: 40 °C for 1 min, which was then increased to 180 °C at the rate of 4 °C/min and further to 250 °C at 20 °C/min, held for 1 min, and finally increased to 350 °C at the rate of 20 °C/min and maintained for 0.5 min. The split ratio was 10:1 (Wen et al., 2023).

Time-of-flight mass spectrometry (TOFMS) conditions: The 7250 TOFMS system (Agilent Scientific, CA, USA) was used to obtain volatile mass spectral information (Wen et al., 2023). A full-scan mode was used during acquisition, with a mass range of 45–450 amu, a scanning frequency of 50 spectra per second, and a filament current of 5 μA. An electron impact ion source was used for ionization, with an ion source temperature of 200 °C and an electron energy of 70 eV. The interface temperature of the mass spectrometer was set at 280 °C. GC × GC-Q-TOFMS was used to analyze volatile compounds in three samples. A total of 357 volatile compounds were identified after comparing MS spectra and retention indices (RI) of 3 tea samples (Table S1).

Identification and quantification of VACs

The data from GC × GC-Q-TOFMS were analyzed using Canvas software (version 1.0.0.25117) (Bao et al., 2025). The qualitative analysis of volatile compounds involved the use of n-alkanes (C7–C28) for calibration and retention index (RI) calculation. Then, the mass spectra of each chromatographic peak were compared with “mainlib’,” “replib,” and “nist_ri” in the NIST20 mass spectral library. The peak tables of samples were then compared using the statistical comparison function in Canvas software. Volatile compounds were identified based on a positive match score > 750, a reverse match >800, and an RI deviation <30. The relative concentration of tea in the sensory evaluation approach was calculated by the internal standard method using the following formula:C=R×10μlL×10mg·L−1M, where C is the relative concentration (μg/kg) of each odor compound in tea; R is the ratio of the compound's peak area to the internal standard's peak area; and M is the mass of the tea sample (0.5 g). Each sample was detected three times.

Calculation of ROAV

Referring to the white sensory evaluation method in GB/T 23776–2018, a tea–water ratio of 1:50 with 3 g tea samples was used to calculate the concentration of each volatile compound. Combined with the reported OT of each compound in water, the ROAV was equal to the ratio of the content of each compound in teas to its reported minimum odor threshold (Chen et al., 2024).

Statistical analysis

The experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The sample differences were determined using one-way analysis of variance, followed by Duncan's multiple range test at P < 0.05 to identify significant variations among spring green tea (SGT), summer black tea (SBT), and autumn dark tea (ADT) samples. Principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were performed using SIMCA 14.1 to visualize tea sample differences; PCA was selected as an unsupervised dimensionality reduction method to objectively reveal the natural clustering trend of the three tea types based on their comprehensive metabolite profiles, without prior grouping information. In contrast, OPLS-DA was employed as a supervised discriminant analysis approach to maximize the separation between sample groups and identify the key differential variables responsible for the metabolic distinctions among SGT, SBT, and ADT, which is more robust than PCA for targeted pattern recognition in metabolomics-based tea quality studies. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to analyze the linear correlations between nonvolatile metabolites and key aroma-active compounds at P < 0.05 or P < 0.01. Graphs were generated using Origin 2023 (OriginLab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA).

Results

Sensory quality of the three types of tea

The radar chart (Fig. 1A) displaying sensory evaluation results revealed notable differences in flavor attributes such as sweetness, roastiness, umami-like, aged-like, chestnut-like, and floral among SGT, SBT, and ADT, which were consistent with seasonal leaf traits and processing effects.

(A) Quantitative descriptive analysis results of SGT, SBT, and ADT; (B) Quantitative analysis results(Water, Water extract, Tea polyphenol, Amino acid, Theanine) of SGT, SBT, and ADT; (C) Quantitative analysis catechins (Theobromine, Gallic acid, Theophy lline, CAF, EGC, DL-C, EC, EGCG, GCG, ECG) of SGT, SBT, and ADT.

SGT scored the highest in fresh and floral aspects. SGT leaves had abundant fresh-related VACs such as linalool and geraniol. These were well-preserved during the light processing, such as pan-firing, of green tea, which contributed to the fresh and floral flavor. This was consistent with previous findings indicating that spring green teas retained high levels of aromatic terpenoids because of mild growth conditions and minimal processing (Qiao et al., 2021; Fang et al., 2025).

SBT performed the best in terms of sweetness and roastiness. Reactions such as polyphenol oxidation by PPO and amino acid degradation occurred during the fermentation and processing of SBT, yielding sweet-tasting substances and roasted-flavor compounds, thus enhancing the sweet and roasted notes of tea. Studies have shown that black tea fermentation promotes the formation of flavor-active compounds, resulting in sweet and roasted aromas (Supriyadi et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2023).

ADT was prominent in aged and chestnut flavors. The post-fermentation microbial activities (e.g., Aspergillus spp.) and long-term storage of ADT led to the formation of aged-flavor compounds (e.g., heterocyclic compounds) and chestnut-like aroma substances (e.g., specific aldehydes and ketones). Research indicates that microbial metabolism during dark tea processing plays a crucial role in the formation of the aforementioned unique flavors (Li et al., 2020).

All three types of tea performed well in various flavor dimensions. This suggested that processing methods for different seasons effectively optimized the sensory quality of seasonal tea leaves.

Nonvolatile bioactive compounds in SGT, SBT, and ADT

Nonvolatile compounds (Fig. 1B—C) -including water extract, TPs, FAAs, Thea, catechins, and purine alkaloids (caffeine, theobromine, theophylline)-are crucial for tea taste (e.g., bitterness, umami) and precursors for VAC biosynthesis, while purine alkaloids also mediate key physiological activities (e.g., neurostimulation, antioxidation) (Ren et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024; Ashihara & Crozier, 2001). Their variations in SGT, SBT, and ADT result from seasonal physiological regulation and processing-related biochemical dynamics, as detailed in the following text.

Water extract

Water extract content reflects the total soluble substances in tea and directly influences taste richness. As shown Fig. 1B, SGT exhibited the highest water extract content (46.21% ± 1.03%), followed by SBT (41.58% ± 0.87%) and ADT (38.92% ± 0.95%). This trend was consistent with the findings of Liu et al. (2022), who reported that low temperatures (14.2 °C ± 2.1 °C) in the spring season slowed tea shoot growth, promoting the accumulation of soluble sugars, amino acids, and low-molecular-weight polyphenols. In contrast, the high temperatures (28.5 °C ± 3.2 °C) in the summer season accelerated leaf senescence, reducing the synthesis of soluble substances. For ADT, pile-fermentation induced dual losses: microbial communities (e.g., Aspergillus and Rhizopus) degraded polysaccharides via enzymatic hydrolysis, and thermal evaporation during processing further decreased soluble compound levels (Feng et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2023).

Tea polyphenols

TPs were the most abundant contributors to tea bitterness and astringency, displaying distinct patterns: TP content was the highest in SBT (22.56 ± 0.61 mg/g), moderate in SGT (18.75 ± 0.52 mg /g), and the lowest in ADT (15.32 ± 0.48 mg/g). The high TP content in SBT can be attributed to the intense sunlight and high temperatures of the summer season, which upregulate the expression of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL)-a key enzyme in the shikimate pathway for polyphenol biosynthesis (Zhou et al., 2022). Moreover, black tea processing (withering and fermentation) preserves polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity in TPs, which primarily oxidizes catechins into theaflavins and thearubigins (still quantified as TPs) rather than fully degrading them (Xue et al., 2019). The moderate TP content in SGT can be attributed to the low temperatures in the spring season, which inhibit PAL activity, reducing de novo TP synthesis (Guo et al., 2015). The low TP content in ADT can be attributed to pile-fermentation in ADT, during which microbes secrete polyphenol-degrading enzymes (e.g., laccase from Penicillium) that break down TPs into small-molecule phenols (e.g., catechol). This process reduced the TP content by 20%–30% compared with that in raw materials, which was consistent with dark tea fermentation dynamics (Chen et al., 2023).

FAAs and Thea

FAAs, especially Thea, dominate tea umami and freshness. SGT had the highest contents of FAAs (3.12 ± 0.08 mg /g) and Thea (1.85 ± 0.05 mg/g), which is a hallmark of high-quality northern Chinese spring tea.

The high FAAs/Thea ratio in SGT can be attributed to winter dormancy, when tea plants store nitrogen in roots. This nitrogen is then translocated to new shoots in spring for amino acid synthesis (Ma et al., 2019). Moreover, low temperatures in the spring season suppressed protease activity and reduced amino acid conversion to secondary metabolites (e.g., polyphenols), promoting accumulation (Wang et al., 2015).

The low FAAs/Thea ratio in SBT can be attributed to the high temperatures of the summer season, which accelerate amino acid catabolism. Thea is hydrolyzed into ethylamine and glutamic acid by Thea hydrolase, whereas other FAAs are used as carbon sources for rapid growth (Wang et al., 2025). Furthermore, black tea fermentation triggered reactions between amino acids and catechin-derived quinones, forming aroma compounds, such as aldehydes, and further depleting FAAs (Jiao et al., 2019).

The moderate FAA/Thea ratio in ADT can be attributed to milder temperatures (16.8 °C ± 2.5 °C) in the autumn season, which slows amino acid degradation. Pile-fermentation exerted dual effects: early-stage microbial proteases decomposed proteins into FAAs (increasing content), whereas prolonged fermentation (7 days) induced Maillard reactions that partially consumed FAAs in the late stage (Wei et al., 2025).

Catechins profiles across seasons

The Fig. 1C illustrates catechin variations (e.g., theobromine, gallic acid, and catechin isomers) across seasonal teas (spring, summer, and autumn). Spring tea (green tea) had the highest contents of most catechins (e.g., EGCG and ECG), which was attributed to: (1) low temperatures in the spring season favoring flavonoid precursor biosynthesis via the phenylpropanoid pathway; and (2) minimal enzymatic oxidation during green tea processing, preserving catechin integrity. Summer tea (black tea) displayed altered catechin profiles: oxidation by PPO and peroxidase converted catechins into theaflavins and thearubigins, reducing the contents of individual catechins while elevating the contents of oxidized polyphenol derivatives. Autumn tea (dark tea) exhibited unique catechin dynamics: microbial metabolism during pile-fermentation transformed catechins into novel metabolites (e.g., methylated catechins, phenolic acids), resulting in reduced levels of parent catechins but increased diversity of microbial-derived phenolic compounds (Chen, Sun, Qu, et al., 2020).

Seasonal variation and processing modulation of caffeine, theobromine, and theophylline in spring green, summer black, and autumn dark teas

Purine alkaloids (caffeine, theobromine, theophylline) are key determinants of tea bitterness and physiological activities (e.g., neurostimulation, antioxidation) (Ashihara & Crozier, 2001; Tang et al., 2025). The bitterness intensity of tea is closely correlated with the composition and content ratio of these three methylxanthines: caffeine contributes the most to the bitter taste due to its high content and strong bitter threshold, while theobromine and theophylline enhance the bitter aftertaste and coordinate with other taste substances (e.g., catechins, amino acids) to form the unique flavor of different tea types (Liu et al., 2016; Rui et al., 2024). In terms of physiological activities, these methylxanthines exert synergistic effects: caffeine mainly acts on the central nervous system to relieve fatigue, theobromine has mild cardioprotective effects, and theophylline shows significant anti-inflammatory and bronchodilatory activities, which together constitute the important functional value of tea (Ashihara & Crozier, 2001; Tang et al., 2025).To quantify these methylxanthines in teas, we adopted HPLC (titania-based reverse-phase column) (Liu et al., 2016) with good linearity (R2 > 0.9990), LODs 0.03–0.08 mg/mL, recoveries 96.7%–106.7% (Liu et al., 2019); UPLC/UPLC-MS/MS are also widely used (Ahmad et al., 2025; Gamze et al., 2025).

Their contents in spring green tea (SGT), summer black tea (SBT), and autumn dark tea (ADT) showed distinct seasonal and processing-dependent patterns (Fig. 1C). SGT exhibited the highest theobromine (0.58%) and theophylline (0.042%) due to winter nitrogen reserve mobilization and low spring temperatures inhibiting alkaloid degradation (Ashihara, Sano, & Crozier, 2008; Liu, Qi, Zou, Li, & Huang, 2015). This high content of theobromine and theophylline in SGT is one of the important reasons for its fresh and mellow taste with a slight bitter aftertaste, which is consistent with the results of Rui et al. (2024) who found that spring green tea has a higher ratio of theobromine to caffeine, contributing to its unique flavor profile.SBT contained the highest caffeine (4.0%), driven by high summer temperatures upregulating caffeine synthase (TCS) expression (Zhou et al., 2024). The high caffeine content in SBT leads to a more intense bitter taste compared to SGT and ADT, which is in line with the study of Ahmad et al. Zheng wei et al. (2025) that reported a positive correlation between caffeine content and bitter intensity in black tea. ADT had the lowest levels of all three alkaloids, as moderate autumn temperatures slowed biosynthesis and pile-fermentation triggered microbial demethylation and thermal evaporation (Chen et al., 2023; Wen et al., 2023). The significant reduction of methylxanthines in ADT during pile-fermentation not only weakens its bitter taste but also reduces the neurostimulation effect, making it suitable for long-term consumption, which is consistent with the findings of Determination of methylxanthine contents and risk characterization for various types of tea in Korea (Baek et al., 2022).

Processing further modulated the stability of these methylxanthines: green tea fixation preserved their contents, black tea fermentation promoted limited theobromine-to-caffeine conversion, and dark tea pile-fermentation induced profound microbial degradation (Guo, Cui, Wang, et al., 2010). Our HPLC analysis confirmed the theobromine-to-caffeine conversion in early black tea fermentation, with a slight decrease (0.03%–0.05%) in theobromine and a corresponding increase (0.1%–0.2%) in caffeine, consistent with the methylxanthine metabolic pathway in tea fermentation reported by Guo et al. (2010) and Gamze et al. Zheng wei et al. (2025). In contrast, microbial degradation of methylxanthines in ADT mainly involved caffeine demethylation to theobromine and then xanthine, verified by detecting the intermediate metabolite xanthine via UPLC-MS/MS qualitative analysis (referring to Ahmad et al., 2025).

Overall characteristics, identification, and classification of VACs

GC × GC-Q-TOFMS was used to analyze volatile compounds in three samples. A total of 357 volatile compounds were identified after comparing MS spectra and retention indices (RI) of 3 tea samples (Table S1). Further, 203, 176, and 166 volatile compounds were identified in SGT, SBT, and ADT tea samples, respectively. The Venn diagram (Fig. 2B) showed that the three samples shared 50 common aroma compounds. SGT and SBT shared 31 unique compounds, SBT and ADT shared 26 unique compounds, and SGT and ADT shared 64 unique compounds. SGT had 97, SBT had 70, and ADT had 64 unique compounds. This underscored that seasonal metabolism and processing (fixation for SGT, oxidation for SBT, and fermentation for ADT) shaped their aroma profiles. The three samples had both unique and overlapping aroma compound compositions to some extent.

Correlation between nonvolatile compounds and aroma-active compounds with VIP>1

The Pearson correlation analysis illustrated in Fig. 4 elucidates the intricate interplay between nonvolatile compounds (water extract, TPs, FAAs, Thea, and catechins) and aroma-active compounds (VIP > 1), which is fundamental to tea quality. Nonvolatile compounds serve as crucial precursors for VACs. TPs, for example, showed positive correlations with floral and malty VACs, aligning with previous findings by Kim et al., 2022 who showed that the enzymatic oxidation of TPs during processing generates aroma-active aldehydes and ketones, contributing to the characteristic aromas of teas such as black tea. FAAs and Thea correlate positively with fresh and fruity VACs because amino acids serve as precursors for ester synthesis (Wen et al., 2024), and Thea enhances the stability of fresh VACs (Wen et al., 2024). This explains the fresh and floral profiles observed in teas with high FAA and Thea content, such as SGT.

Beyond serving as aroma precursors, nonvolatile compounds directly influence taste. Thea imparts umami flavor (Yu et al., 2025), whereas TPs contribute bitterness and astringency. Their interactions with other compounds lead to complex flavor profiles, such as balanced bitterness and astringency in SBT. Water extract content, which reflects the total amount of soluble substances, correlates positively with many VACs. This suggests it provides essential substrates for VAC formation and enhances taste richness, as demonstrated in the high-quality profile of SGT.

This interplay is crucial for overall tea quality. Understanding these correlations can help optimize processing conditions (e.g., temperature and microbial activity in dark tea fermentation) to customize aroma and taste, thereby improving both scientific understanding and practical tea production.

Core aroma compounds and their differential distribution

The data revealed striking specificity in volatile composition across seasons and processing methods. SGT was dominated by fruity and fresh compounds: hexanoic acid, ethyl ester (ROAV 675.74) imparted a pineapple-like note, whereas butanoic acid, 2-methyl-, ethyl ester (ROAV 674.92) enhanced fruity nuances, consistent with spring's low-temperature conditions that preserved ester precursors (Wang et al., 2022). Levomenthol (ROAV 37.51), unique to SGT, contributed a cool, minty freshness, which was likely derived from terpenoid metabolism in slow-growing spring shoots (He et al., 2012).

SBT, shaped by enzymatic oxidation, was characterized by malty and green volatiles. The dominant compounds included (E,Z)-2,6-nonadienal (ROAV 5106.15) and (E)-2-buten-1-one (ROAV 1166.23), which contributed to cucumber and cooked apple notes. These arose from lipoxygenase (LOX)-mediated fatty acid oxidation at high temperatures in the summer season (Liang et al., 2024). Additionally, (E,E)-2,4-Heptadienal (ROAV 29.03) added nutty undertones, a hallmark of black tea fermentation (Chen et al., 2022).

ADT, undergoing microbial fermentation, was characterized by earthy and fatty compounds. (Z)-4-Heptenal, (ROAV 21331.37) and (Z)-2-decenal, (ROAV 1219.63) contributed dairy and fatty aromas, which were likely derived from the microbial degradation of lipids during pile-fermentation (Ma et al., 2021). Dimethyl sulfide (ROAV 32.29) reinforced earthy notes, which were derived from methionine metabolism by fungi such as A. niger (Liu et al., 2013).

Shared compounds such as linalool (ROAV 243.87–197.39) and β-myrcene (ROAV 245.67–162.61) provided a floral-herbaceous base across all teas, acting as “bridge volatiles” balancing unique notes. However, their concentrations aligned with seasonal precursor accumulation: higher in SGT and SBT due to terpenoid synthesis in the spring/summer season, and moderate in ADT due to microbial transformation (Wu et al., 2023; Rawat and Gulati, 2008).

Correlations with nonvolatile compounds (Fig. 8) further explained these patterns: high FAAs/Thea ratio in SGT correlated with ester formation, TP content in SBT drove aldehyde production via oxidation, and microbial activity in ADT was linked to sulfur-containing volatiles. This interplay mirrored findings in honey-related teas, where precursor availability and processing enzymes dictated aroma complexity (Zeng, Watanabe, & Yang, 2018).

Seasonal climate regulated precursor pools. Low PAL activity in the spring season preserved amino acids for esters, high LOX activity in the summer season sped up fatty acid breakdown for aldehydes, and long-term growth in the autumn season led to the accumulation of terpenoids for microbial conversion. Processing magnified these differences. High-temperature fixation in SGT kept esters, fermentation of SBT activated PPO for aldehyde formation, and pile-fermentation of ADT promoted microbial volatile synthesis. These factors jointly created the unique aroma of northern seasonal teas.

Discussion

Mechanisms underlying seasonal differences in tea quality

The distinct quality characteristics of SGT, SBT, and ADT are primarily driven by seasonal climate variations at 37°N latitude, which regulate the accumulation of nonvolatile compounds and aroma precursors in tea leaves.

Spring (SGT): Low temperatures (14.2 °C ± 2.1 °C) and moderate precipitation (85 ± 12 mm) create optimal conditions for amino acid accumulation. During winter dormancy, tea plants store nitrogen reserves, which are transported to new shoots in spring to support the synthesis of free amino acids (FAAs) and theanine (Thea) (Zhu, Qiao, Li, Cheng, & Ma, 2023). Beyond serving as precursors for fruity ester volatiles, these nonvolatile compounds directly modulate taste perception: theanine, as the dominant amino acid in SGT, contributes a distinctive umami and sweet aftertaste, while also antagonizing the mild bitterness of caffeine via competitive binding to taste receptors (Hou et al., 2022). Meanwhile, the low spring temperatures inhibit phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) activity, reducing tea polyphenol (TP) synthesis and minimizing inherent bitterness. For volatile aroma compounds (VACs), the mild climate preserves fatty acid ester precursors (e.g., hexanoic acid and ethanol), which are enzymatically converted into fruity esters (ethyl hexanoate) during green tea processing. Additionally, high-temperature fixation (220 °C) inactivates esterases and lipoxygenases (LOXs), preventing VAC degradation and maintaining the fresh aroma of SGT (Zhang et al., 2021). Collectively, the high FAA/theanine levels and low TP content synergistically shape SGT's clean, fresh, and umami taste profile, with minimal bitterness interfering with sensory acceptance.

Summer (SBT): High temperatures (28.5 °C ± 3.2 °C) and intense sunlight upregulate PAL and LOX activity. PAL promotes TP synthesis (22.56 ± 0.61 mg GAE/g), whereas LOX accelerates fatty acid oxidation, generating aldehyde precursors (e.g., linoleic acid → (E,Z)-2,6-nonadienal) (Chen et al., 2020). Without targeted processing, the high TP content (dominated by catechins) would result in harsh, persistent bitterness and astringency, as catechins bind to salivary proteins and induce oral dryness. The balanced bitterness characteristic of SBT arises from two key nonvolatile interaction mechanisms during fermentation: First, polyphenol oxidase (PPO)-catalyzed oxidation converts catechins into theaflavins (TFs) and thearubigins (TRs); TFs contribute a mild, brisk bitterness and bright red-orange color, while TRs impart a smooth, mellow mouthfeel (Chen et al., 2022). The ratio of TFs to TRs (optimized under the 29 °C, 95% RH fermentation conditions) is critical-excessive TFs would retain sharp bitterness, while overaccumulated TRs would lead to dullness. Second, residual FAAs in summer leaves interact with TFs via hydrogen bonding, mitigating the astringent effect of TF-catechin complexes and softening the overall bitter taste. The high precipitation in summer (150 ± 18 mm) increases leaf moisture, facilitating these enzymatic and molecular interactions during withering and fermentation. Maillard reactions between amino acids and reducing sugars further generate malty compounds ((E)-2-buten-1-one), whose aromatic notes complement the balanced bitter taste, resulting in SBT's characteristic fruity/malty profile with pleasant, non-irritating bitterness.

Autumn (ADT): Cool temperatures (16.8 °C ± 2.5 °C) and low precipitation (45 ± 8 mm) slow leaf growth, resulting in moderate levels of free amino acids (FAAs) and theanine (Thea) contents. Autumn leaves accumulate more terpenoid glycosides (e.g., cedrol precursors) due to prolonged daylight hours; these glycosides are later converted into woody terpenoids during processing via microbial enzyme catalysis (He et al., 2012). In dark tea production, pile-fermentation at 35 °C and 85% RH activates a complex, synergistic microbial consortium-far beyond Aspergillus niger and Penicillium-that underpins ADT's polyphenol reduction, taste modulation, and aroma formation. This mechanistic detail aligns with our acknowledged limitation (lack of systematic microbial community analysis) and highlights it as a key future research direction.

This microbial community comprises functional fungi, bacteria, and yeasts, each with distinct metabolic roles. Among fungi, Aspergillus niger secretes laccase to oxidize and degrade tea polyphenols (TPs, primarily catechin polymers) into low-molecular-weight fragments (e.g., gallic acid), effectively reducing astringency; Penicillium simplicissimum further contributes by producing cellulases and hemicellulases to break down leaf cell wall polysaccharides into fermentable sugars, serving as substrates for subsequent microbial reactions (Li et al., 2025). Complementing these, Mucor circinelloides regulates starch hydrolysis to generate maltose, enhancing the infusion's mellow mouthfeel.

Bacteria and yeasts play pivotal roles in flavor refinement: Lactic acid bacteria (LAB, e.g., Ligilactobacillus plantarum) ferment sugars to produce lactic acid and succinic acid, which soften residual bitterness via hydrogen bonding with TP fragments; acetic acid bacteria (e.g., Acetobacter pasteurianus) oxidize yeast-derived ethanol into acetic acid, adding a subtle sour note that balances earthy aromas (Guo et al., 2025). Yeasts such asWickerhamomyces anomalus and Saccharomyces cerevisiae ferment sugars into ethanol, which reacts with fatty acids to form ethyl esters (e.g., ethyl octanoate) and interacts with amino acids to enhance sweet aftertaste.

Microbial metabolism directly drives ADT's characteristic earthy/woody aroma profile: Aspergillus niger mediates methionine degradation to produce dimethyl sulfide; LAB catalyze fatty acid oxidation to generate (Z)-2-decenal; and fungal β-glucosidases (secreted by Aspergillus and Penicillium) hydrolyze terpenoid glycosides into cedrol (Li et al., 2023). Additionally, microbial proteases decompose leaf proteins into FAAs, which synergize with degraded TP fragments to further soften taste. Collectively, these coordinated microbial metabolic pathways-shaped by autumn's intrinsic leaf composition and pile-fermentation conditions-forge ADT's distinctive mellow, earthy sensory profile with no lingering bitterness or astringency.

Impact of processing technology on quality optimization

The “Spring Green, Summer Black, Autumn Dark” processing model is scientifically validated for marginal tea-growing regions in northern China, as it matches processing methods to seasonal leaf characteristics, thus maximizing quality and resource utilization.

Spring leaves for green tea: The high amino acid and low TP contents of spring leaves are ideal for green tea, where minimal processing (fixation, rolling, and drying) preserves umami and fresh aromas. High-temperature fixation is critical, as it inactivates enzymes that degrade FAAs and VACs, thus maintaining high sensory acceptability of SGT(8.6 ± 0.2) (Wang et al., 2021).

Summer leaves for black tea: The high TP content of summer leaves is a limitation for green tea (excessive bitterness) but an advantage for black tea. Fermentation converts catechins into theaflavins (which contribute brightness) and thearubigins (which add body), whereas amino acid degradation generates fruity and malty VACs (Wei et al., 2022). This transforms bitter summer leaves into SBT with balanced taste and rich aroma (overall acceptability: 8.3 ± 0.3).

Autumn leaves for dark tea: The moderate metabolite content and mature structure (thicker cell walls) in autumn leaves are suitable for long-term pile-fermentation of dark tea. Microbial activity breaks down compounds (e.g., high-molecular-weight TPs) with harsh attributes and generates metabolites with mellow attributes, whereas terpenoid release enhances woody notes (Zhang et al., 2023). The overall acceptability (8.1 ± 0.4) of ADT confirms that dark tea processing effectively uses autumn leaves, which are often discarded in traditional systems.

Comparison of SGT, SBT, and ADT with teas from southern China

Teas from 37°N latitude (Zhaoyuan) exhibit unique quality traits compared with southern Chinese teas (23°N–32°N), which is attributed to the distinct climate in northern China.

Nonvolatiles: The Thea content in SGT (1.85 ± 0.05 mg/g) is 20%–30% higher than that in southern spring green teas (e.g., Longjing tea: 1.4 mg/g) (Chen et al., 2021). This is due to larger diurnal temperature variations (10%–12 °C vs 5%–7 °C in the south) in the northern spring season, which slows amino acid degradation (Deka et al., 2023). In contrast, the TP content in SBT (22.56 ± 0.61 mg/g) is lower than that in southern black teas (e.g., Lapsang Souchong: 26 mg/g) (Deka et al., 2023) because a shorter growth period in northern summer limits TP accumulation.

Volatiles: The ethyl hexanoate content (1486.63 mg/kg) in SGT is remarkably higher than that in southern green teas (e.g., Biluochun: 800 mg/kg) (Zhao et al., 2021), contributing to stronger fruity notes. The dimethyl sulfide content (9.69 mg/kg) in ADT is lower than that in southern dark teas (e.g., Pu-erh: 15 mg/kg) (Hu et al., 2021), resulting in a milder earthy aroma. This is likely due to lower humidity during the northern autumn season, which slows microbial sulfur metabolism.

These differences highlight the “terroir” effect of 37°N latitude, providing a niche market for northern teas with unique sensory profiles.

Implications for tea production in marginal regions

This study provides the following valuable insights for optimizing tea production in northern China and other marginal tea-growing regions (e.g., northern Japan, Korea).

Seasonal processing adjustment: The “Spring Green, Summer Black, Autumn Dark” model should be adopted to reduce waste of summer and autumn leaves, thus increasing farmers' income by 30%–40% (based on local market prices: SGT 20 yuan/kg, SBT 12 yuan/kg, and ADT 8 yuan/kg vs unprocessed summer leaves 2 yuan/kg).

Cultivar selection: Cold-tolerant cultivars (e.g., “Huangshan Quntizhong”) adapting to northern climates should be promoted, ensuring stable metabolite accumulation.

Microbial regulation for dark tea: High-efficiency strains (e.g., A. niger CGMCC 3.316) should be inoculated during ADT pile-fermentation to enhance the content of earthy aroma compounds and reduce fermentation time from 7 to 5 days (Heidarzadeh, Amani, & Javadian, 2019).

Quality standardization: Regional standards should be established for northern teas, focusing on high Thea content (≥1.5 mg/g for SGT) and characteristic VACs (ethyl hexanoate ≥1000 mg/kg for SGT, (E,Z)-2,6-nonadienal ≥20 mg/kg for SBT) to enhance market competitiveness.

Limitations and future directions

This study had several inherent limitations that warrant further investigation: (1) Only a single tea cultivar (“Huangshan Quntizhong”) was investigated, which restricts the generalizability of the seasonal processing strategy to other germplasms adapted to northern marginal tea-growing regions. (2) The structural composition and dynamic changes of the microbial community during ADT pile-fermentation were not characterized, leaving the mechanistic link between specific microbial taxa and volatile aroma compound (VAC) biosynthesis unelucidated. (3) Sensory evaluation was solely conducted by a professionally trained panel, and the results thus lack the market-oriented insights of consumer preference perception from different demographic groups.

To address these limitations and advance the understanding of northern tea quality formation, future studies will adopt targeted experimental designs and advanced analytical techniques, with the following research directions proposed: First, multi-cultivar comparative studies will be performed by collecting major commercial tea cultivars suitable for northern cultivation, systematically evaluating their sensory and chemical characteristics after the “Spring Green, Summer Black, Autumn Dark” processing, and screening out the optimal germplasms with the most prominent seasonal quality advantages for northern marginal tea regions. Second, integrated metagenomic sequencing (including 16S rRNA/ITS high-throughput sequencing and whole-genome shotgun metagenomics) will be applied to ADT fermentation samples to clarify the core functional microbial taxa, their dynamic metabolic pathways, and direct regulatory relationships with VAC and nonvolatile compound formation; combined with aroma recombination and omission experiments, the contribution of key microbially derived aroma-active compounds to ADT's sensory profile will be further verified. Third, consumer preference tests with stratified sampling by age, region and tea consumption habits will be conducted to bridge the gap between professional sensory evaluation and market demand, providing a practical basis for the industrialization of northern seasonal tea processing. Fourth, advanced extraction technologies such as ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) will be explored to enhance the recovery efficiency of bioactive compounds (e.g., theanine, polyphenols, microbial metabolites) from northern teas (Wu et al., 2024), which will lay a foundation for in-depth functional studies. In addition, the health benefits of northern teas should be systematically explored: for instance, the high theanine content in SGT may exert neuroprotective effects (Deb et al., 2019), while the unique microbial metabolites in ADT may possess anti-inflammatory properties (Liu, Jian, Li, et al., 2024). Integrating seasonal processing optimization, bioactive compound efficient extraction and functional mechanism verification will further enrich the theoretical system of northern tea quality regulation and maximize the economic and industrial value of marginal tea production.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated three tea types (SGT, SBT, ADT), all sourced from the same C. sinensis garden in the northern marginal tea-growing region. The study employed integrated sensory evaluation, chemical analysis, and multivariate statistical approaches. Sensory profiles revealed distinct characteristics: SGT exhibited fresh, floral, and umami characteristics; SBT displayed fruity, malty, and balanced bitter traits; and ADT was characterized by earthy, woody nuances, thereby validating the “Spring Green, Summer Black, Autumn Dark” model. The chemical analysis revealed significant seasonal and processing-driven variations in nonvolatile compounds. SGT had the highest contents of water extract, FAAs, and Thea, contributing to its umami and freshness. SBT contained the highest content of TPs, whereas ADT had the lowest content of TPs due to microbial degradation during pile-fermentation. GC × GC- Q–TOFMS analysis identified 357 VACs, with 46 key aroma-active compounds characterizing the tea. SGT was dominated by fruity esters and floral terpenoids; SBT featured malty aldehydes and green volatiles; and ADT was enriched with earthy fatty aldehydes and microbial metabolites. PCA and OPLS-DA separated the three types of tea, with VIP >1 compounds as discriminators. Pearson correlation analysis confirmed strong associations between nonvolatiles and VACs, highlighting nonvolatiles as key precursors for aroma formation. These findings elucidated the synergistic effects of seasonal climate and processing techniques on tea quality in northern marginal regions, providing a scientific basis for optimizing processing strategies and enhancing the economic value and sustainability of local tea production. However, this study focused on a single cultivar. Future studies should include multiple cultivars, microbial community analysis during ADT fermentation, and aroma recombination/omission experiments to further validate key aroma compounds and enhance the understanding of quality formation mechanisms in marginal tea regions.
