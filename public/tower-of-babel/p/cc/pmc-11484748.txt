Genetic characteristics of clinical carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae: epidemic ST11 KPC-2-producing strains and non-negligible NDM-5-producing strains with diverse STs
Authors: Yi Yuan, Yanjun Lu, Li Cao, Yu Fu, Ying Li, Luhua Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractKlebsiella pneumoniae is among the most important Gram-negative pathogens that can cause serious nosocomial infections. The emergence and prevalence of hypervirulent carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (Hv-CRKP) pose a significant challenge to public health. In this study, we characterized thirty carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP) strains from a tertiary care hospital in Sichuan province, China, by whole-genome sequencing and genome analysis. These strains were all highly resistant to carbapenem but remained susceptible to tigecycline. Of the 30 tested CRKP strains, 23 were positive for blaKPC−2 and seven for blaNDM−5. These blaKPC−2-positive strains all belonged to ST11, while blaNDM−5-positive strains belonged to five distinct STs. Phylogenetic analysis revealed a predominant intra-hospital transmission of ST11-KL64 in KPC-2-producing CRKP, and that both clonal and horizontal transmission of blaNDM−5 have occurred among NDM-5-producing CRKP strains in this hospital. Hypervirulence genes were commonly detected in the CRKP. The prevalent pLVKP-like plasmid and ICEKp seem to have contributed largely to the transmission of virulence genes in them. blaNDM−5 was located on highly similar IncX3 plasmids in the collected strains, and its truncated vision was highlighted. blaKPC−2 was primarily carried by IncFII/IncR plasmids in our collection. At least two IncFII/IncR plasmid subtypes were identified, exhibiting high similarity to many previously reported blaKPC−2-bearing plasmids from different parts of China. The findings provide an expanded knowledge of the genetic characteristics of CRKP, the transmission pattern of carbapenem-resistance genes, and also the convergence of Hv-CRKP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74307-6.Keywords: CRKP, blaKPC−2, blaNDM−5, Hypervirulence, IncFII/IncR plasmidSubject terms: Microbiology, Antimicrobials, Bacteria, Clinical microbiology

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae is a clinically important bacterial pathogen that is capable of causing serious nosocomial infections1. In particular, carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP) is usually associated with longer hospital stays and higher mortality rates for infected patients2. CRKP has emerged worldwide as a great challenge to public health and anti-infection therapy and was listed as a critical-priority bacteria by the World Health Organization in 20173. It was reported that CRKP accounts for roughly 64% of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales CRE infections in China, and ST11 CRKP represents the dominant clonal group4–6. More worrying, a large proportion of CRKP strains were found to carry a virulence-encoding plasmid and some of them exhibited a hypervirulent phenotype7. The convergence of hypervirulence and carbapenem resistance in a single K. pneumoniae strain, most likely caused by the dissemination of the virulence plasmid among CRKP, drives a global crisis8.

Generally, the hypervirulent phenotype of Hv-CRKP was commonly associated with the carriage of a pLVPK-like virulence plasmid that harbors capsular regulator genes (rmpA, and rmpA2), aerobactin (iutA), and a number of siderophore gene clusters (iroBCD)5. The pLVPK-like virulence plasmids are generally non-conjugative, while Yang et al. reported a rare case of a conjugative virulence plasmid, p15WZ-82_Vir, which was formed due to the integration of a 100-kb fragment of the pLVPK virulence plasmid into a conjugative IncFIB plasmid9. The conjugative virulence plasmids are very likely to spread the virulence among K. pneumoniae and produce hypervirulent K. pneumoniae. Besides, the integrative and conjugative element ICEKp is also a common virulence-associated mobile genetic element in K. pneumoniae that was first described in hypervirulent strain NTUH-K2044 and was later identified in many carbapenem-resistant strains10–12.

blaKPC−2 is the main genetic determinant accounting for carbapenem resistance in K. pneumoniae, followed by blaNDM−1 and blaNDM−5 in China6. KPC-2 is the prevalent form in ST11 CRKP7. ST11 hv-CRKP-producing KPC-2 carbapenemase is the most transmissible clone in Chinese hospitals and causes serious infections, including some lethal cases5,7,13. Studies showed that IncFII plasmids contributed significantly to the global prevalence of blaKPC−2 among K. pneumoniae14. A cointegrate blaKPC−2-bearing IncFII/IncR plasmid lineage with sizes of ~ 120 to ~ 170 kb has emerged in K. pneumoniae ST11 and is seemingly entrenched in hospital-associated populations in China15. Besides, Chen et al. reported a small IncN-type blaKPC−2-harboring plasmid (~ 26 kb) in the ST11 clone, which is thought to be attributed to cross-species transfer of KPC-2 carbapenem resistance between K. pneumoniae and Proteus mirabilis13. In recent years, dissemination of NDM-5-producing K. pneumoniae has been sporadically reported in neonates and children in China16,17. Unlike the overwhelming dominance of KPC-2-producing ST11 CRKP, NDM-5 was detected in K. pneumoniae of different ST types, such as ST78918, ST4819, and ST6520, and they carried a common blaNDM−5-bearing IncX3 plasmid (~ 45 kb). Some of these NDM-5-producing K. pneumoniae strains, such as SCNJ1, RJY9645, and KP22937, have been confirmed to be hypervirulent12,20,21. This study aims to genomically characterize thirty KPC-2- or NDM5-producing CRKP strains causing clinical infections from a tertiary care hospital in Sichuan province of China during 2018–2022, with a focus on the genetic features of acquired carbapenemase resistance and hypervirulence.

Methods

Bacterial isolates

We collected 30 CRKP clinical isolates from the First People’s Hospital of Neijiang in eastern Sichuan Province, China, between November 2018 and November 2022. The initial species identification was performed using the Vitek-2 compact system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France). These CRKP strains were cultured on either Luria-Bertani (LB) agar plates or LB liquid medium at 37 °C.

Antimicrobial susceptibility tests

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of ceftazidime, cefepime, tobramycin, amikacin, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, aztreonam, ceftriaxone, gentamicin, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, piperacillin/tazobactam, ampicillin/sulbactam, and imipenem were determined by using Vitek-2 system. MICs of meropenem, tigecycline, and colistin against the isolate were determined using the microdilution broth method following recommendations of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 was used as a quality control strain. Breakpoints of tigecycline and colistin for Enterobacteriaceae were interpreted according to the FDA and the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) criteria (http://www.eucast.org/clinical_breakpoints/), respectively. Otherwise, we applied those defined by the CLSI.

String test and low-speed centrifugation assay

To determine the phenotypic mucoviscosity, a string test was carried out as described previously21. Bacterial colonies of K. pneumoniae on a blood agar plate were scratched with a standard inoculation loop and the string test was deemed positive when a viscous string of > 5 mm length could be generated by the strain. Mucoviscosity was measured as previously described22. Briefly, strains were grown shaking for 24 h at 37 °C, and the OD600 of bacterial culture was adjusted to (pre spin OD600) with either LB broth as appropriate. The adjusted culture (1.5 ml) was then centrifuged at 1,000× g for 5 min before the OD600 of supernatant was measured (postspin OD600). Mucoviscosity was recorded as the ratio of post/prespin OD600. This experiment was performed for three independent times in triplicate, and the data were reported as means ± SD.

Whole genome sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

All the CRKP isolates were subjected to whole-genome sequencing on the HiSeq 2000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, United States) platform using a paired-end library with an insert size of 150 bp by the Beijing Tsingke Bioinformatics Technology Co. Ltd. Reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic 0.3323 and were de novo assembled into contigs using SPAdes v. 3.15.3 with the careful mode24. Pilon v1.22 was used to correct the assembled contigs and sequences were annotated using Prokka v1.14 and BLASTp/BLASTn searches against the UniProtKB/SwissProt database25–27. Species identification, sequence types, capsular and lipopolysaccharide typing, and identification of virulence genes of these strains were determined using Kleborate 2.3.228. Plasmid incompatibility types and antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) as well as chromosomal mutations mediating antimicrobial resistance were predicted using PlasmidFinder v2.1 and ResFinder v4.1 at the online service of the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (http://genomicepidemiology.org/)29,30, respectively. Insertion elements (ISs) and integrons were predicted using ISfinder31 and INTEGRALL32.

The complete sequence of pNDM5-SCNJ1 (45,255 bp, GenBank accession no. MK715437) was used as a reference to cyclize the blaNDM−5-bearing plasmids. The complete sequences of p3_L39 (133,772 bp, CP033956), pCRKP-F1-KPC (133,859 bp, CP132044), pKPC-2_FZKP4523 (141,697 bp, NZ_CP101534) were used as references to cyclize the blaKPC−2-bearing plasmids. Assembled contigs were aligned to the reference sequences by local BLASTn and gaps between contigs were filled using PCR and Sanger sequencing. To detect the presence of the virulence plasmid, assembled contigs were aligned to a reference plasmid pLVPK (219,385 bp, AY378100) using the local BLASTn with threshold of > 90% identify and > 50% coverage.

Phylogenetic analysis

The phylogenetic tree was constructed on core genome single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in their core genomes as previously described33. Briefly, genomes were annotated by Prokka v1.14, and the generated GFF3 files were piped into Roary to create a core genome alignment34. SNPs were extracted by using snp-sites v2.3.235. A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using FastTree v2.1.10 with the GTR model based on the SNPs36. The phylogenetic analysis of genome sequences of ST11 strains in this study and other ST11 isolates from China was carried out using the online pathogenwatch server (https://pathogen.watch/).

Conjugation experiments

Mating experiments were performed using a broth-based method with the rifampin-resistant E. coli strain EC600 as previously described with minor modification33. Donor and recipient strains in the exponential stage (OD600 ~ 0.5) were mixed at a ratio of 1:1, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. Putative transconjugants were selected on LB agar plates containing 4 µg/ml meropenem plus 400 µg/ml rifampin.

Results and discussion

Resistance phenotype and genotype of CRKP strains

Thirty non-duplicate CRKP clinical isolates (one from our previous study21 and 29 from this investigation) were sampled from different patients admitted into general ICU, pediatrics, urology, neurosurgery, gastroenterology, neurology, and emergency. These CRKP isolates were mainly recovered from sputum (n = 24), followed by urine (n = 3), blood (n = 2), and excreta (n = 1) (Fig. 1, Table S1). All the isolates were resistant to the tested β-lactam antibiotics and inhibitors (except aztreonam), including cefepime, ceftazidime, ampicillin/sulbactam, piperacillin/tazobactam, ceftriaxone, imipenem, meropenem and 25 (83.3%) were resistant to aztreonam (Table 1, Table S2). Most strains also showed frequent resistance to levofloxacin (80.0%), and ciprofloxacin (80.0%), followed by gentamicin (70.0%), amikacin (70.0%), tobramycin (70.0%), and sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (60.0%). 13.3% of the isolates were resistant to colistin, while all the isolates remained sensitive to tigecycline.

Molecular epidemiology of CRKP strains

Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) analysis revealed that these KPC-2-producing K. pneumoniae (KPC-2-KP) all were ST11. Seven NDM-5-producing isolates (NDM-5-KP) belonged to five distinct STs, including ST29 (n = 2), ST2407 (n = 2), ST789 (n = 1), ST307 (n = 1), and ST412 (n = 1) (Fig. 1). Previous studies showed that ST789 and ST2407 NDM-5-KP have become two new emerging CRKP lineages threatening the neonates by clonal expansion in some hospitals in Chengdu, in Sichuan province18,37. One ST789 (SCNJ29) and two ST2407 (SCNJ24 and SCNJ34) were isolated from the pediatrics department in this hospital (approximately 176 km apart in geographical distance from Chengdu), highlighting a need for intensive monitoring of these two NDM-5-KP lineages in local and other regions of China.

Capsular polysaccharides and lipopolysaccharides are important factors contributing to the hypervirulence of K. pneumoniae38. Analysis of capsule loci (KL) and O-antigenic polysaccharide in our collection revealed that 78.3% (18/23) of the ST11 KPC-2-KP were O1/O2v1:KL64 type, with the remaining five strains being OL101: KL47 (Fig. 1). These findings are consistent with current epidemiology features of CRKP in China that ST11 KPC-2-KP dominated the CRKP isolates and that the previously prevalent subclone OL101:KL47 had been replaced by O1/O2v1:KL6439.

Regarding to the diverse NDM-5-KP, each ST is associated with specific KL + O loci: KL54 + O1/O2v2 with ST29; KL25 + O5 with ST2407; KL18 + O1/O2v1 with ST789; KL102 + O1/O2v2 with ST307; and KL57 + O3b with ST412 (Fig. 1). Compared to the high diversity (more than 80 types) of the K-Locus in K. pneumoniae, a notably lower number (eight types) of O serotypes have been reported, among which O1 and O2 strains are the most common serotypes globally and have been closely related to human disease, contributing to invasive tissue infection40,41. Although we also observed the dominant distribution of O1/O2 serotypes in our collection of CRKP isolates, three NDM-5-producing strains were identified to be O5 (n = 2) or O3b (n = 1). More data is needed to better understand the epidemiology of CRKP O5 and O3 strains in China.

The core genome-based phylogenetic analysis showed that KPC-2-KP clustered together and formed two subclades, comprising isolates of ST11-KL64 and ST11-KL47, respectively (Fig. 1). All ST11-KL64 isolates were clustered with limited SNP (0-163) divergences between each other (Table S3), suggesting that they were derived from the same ancestor and transmissible within the hospital from 2018 to 2022. The identification of two sublineages (SCNJ2/SCNJ3/SCNJ4, and SCNJ42/SCNJ49) of ST11-KL47 strains reveals an evolution while circulating of them. When placed the genome sequences of ST11 strains in the context of nationwide ST11 isolates (n = 1689), the KL64 strains from this study were distributed in two distinct subclades, with SCNJ8, SCNJ17, SCNJ11, SCNJ10, SCNJ12, SCNJ26, and SCNJ13 in subclade I and the remaining KL64 strains in subclade II (Figure S1). The KL47 strains were tightly clustered with many isolates from other parts of China in subclade III but are less related to the KL64 strains from this study, just as expected (Figure S1).

The NDM-5-KP were clustered according to ST types. The different genetic backgrounds between these NDM-5-KP suggest their independent acquisition of blaNDM−5. Notably, the clonal relationship was noticed between some of the NDM-5-producing strains, including SCNJ24/SCNJ34 (22 SNPs), and SCNJ1/SCNJ9 (4 SNPs), hinting that clonal spread of NDM-5-KP has occurred in this hospital.

Analysis of acquired virulence determinants in CRKP strains

We examined the presence of key hypervirulence genes42, including siderophores yersiniabactin (ybt), aerobactin (iuc), and salmochelin (iro), and regulator of mucoid phenotype A (rmpA and rmpA2) among the tested strains. We found that 27/30 (90%) of the CRKP strains carried at least one type of these five virulence genes (Table 2). ICEKp is a major vehicle for the spreading of yersiniabactin in K. pneumoniae10. In our collection, the ICEKp3 variant related to ybt 9 lineage is the most commonly (25/30, 83.3%) detected, and is associated with strains of diverse STs, revealing the possible circulation and transmission of ICEKp3 in the CRKP population. In addition, we also identified ICEKp11 (ybt 15 lineage) in two ST2407 isolates.

pLVKP-like virulence plasmids are closely associated with hypervirulence in K. pneumoniae43. Blast analysis showed that pLVKP was present in 50% (15/30) of the genomes with coverage > 50% and identity > 90%. A previous study showed that some pLVPK-like derivatives had lost approximately 50 kb region encoding rmpA and iroBCDN by a genetic deletion event in response to an unknown selective pressure15. Similar scenarios were also seen in this study in that all of these pLVKP-positive strains harbored rmpA2 and iuc genes, but only five of them had rmpA genes (Table 2). The deletion of key mucoid regulator-encoding gene rmpA may cause non-hypermucoviscosity and non-hypervirulence despite the acquisition of pLVPK-like derivative. Consistent with this, four strains (SCNJ1, SCNJ9, SCNJ48, and SCNJ19) that possess intact hypermucoviscous-associated gene rmpA exhibited positive for string test (Fig. 1). Mucoviscosity was quantified by low-speed centrifugation assay, with K. pneumoniae NTUH-K2044 as a hypervirulent comparator strain, which also confirmed the hypermucoviscosity of these four strains (Figure S2). We noticed that SCNJ43 also carried intact rmpA but showed negative for the string test and low mucoviscosity. Compared to the wild-type sequence in pLVPK plasmid, deletion and nonsynonymous mutations are identified in rmpA (G deletion at 42) and rmpA2 (CC deletion at 276–277, T deletion at 345, G301T mutation ) genes in SCNJ43. Notably, identical mutations of the rmpA and rmpA2 genes were also found in strain SCNJ19, which did exhibit a positive string test and hypermucoviscous phenotype. Recently, L. Liu et al. showed that an active P11T rmpA promoter determines phenotypic mucoviscosity44. Based on this, we analysed the full rmpA promoter in SCNJ43 and SCNJ19, respectively, and found that both contained an intact PT11. This finding indicates that mechanisms other than rmpA may be also involved in regulating the expression of the hypermucoviscous phenotype.

The conserved IncX3 plasmids facilitated the spread of blaNDM−5 in K. pneumoniae

It has been known that IncX3 type plasmid is an important vehicle in mediating the dissemination of blaNDM45. In K. pneumoniae, the blaNDM−5 gene was located on a highly similar IncX3 plasmid in all the strains in this study, as well as those from previous studies17,18,20,46–51, revealing a dominant role of IncX3 plasmid in facilitating the dissemination of blaNDM−5 in K. pneumoniae. Moreover, these blaNDM−5-bearing K. pneumoniae isolates usually belonged to distinct STs, indicating that horizontal transfer mediated by IncX3 plasmid is the major transmission route for blaNDM−5 among K. pneumoniae strains.

By sequence alignment to a reference plasmid, we successfully obtained eight complete sequences of IncX3 blaNDM−5-carrying plasmids from the tested strains (Table 3), which vary in size from 26,521 bp to 54,004 bp. blaNDM−5 was the sole ARG in these plasmids and it was present in a similar genetic context with core gene arrangement ΔcutA1-dsbC-trpF-ble-blaNDM−5-ΔISAba125-IS5-ΔISAba125-IS3000 (Fig. 2A). These plasmids, except pNDM5-SCNJ24 and pNDM5-SCNJ34, were almost identical to the reference plasmid pNDM_MGR194 (GenBank accession no. KF220657), with the varied length of the ΔISAba125 fragment upstream of blaNDM−5 being the major differences. For pNDM5-SCNJ34 (26,521 bp) and pNDM5-SCNJ24 (27,668 bp), their ~ 20.6 kb backbone structure (hns-topB-vir-taxAB) was deleted during the evolution (Fig. 2B). Two similar small blaNDM−5-bearing plasmids, pNDM-5-SCLZ494 (GenBank accession no. CP095160) and p3NDM-5 (CP089991), both recovering from clinical K. pneumoniae isolates in Luzhou city (a neighboring city of Neijiang), were identified in the GenBank database. These four plasmids were almost identical, except for a deletion of ΔISAba125 fragment downstream of IS3000 in pNDM5-SCNJ34 (Fig. 2B). Conjugation assays showed that pNDM5-SCNJ34 was not transferable, just as expected, due to the absence of tra region. The MLST results and phylogenetic analysis in our study revealed that SCNJ24 and SCNJ34 isolates were clonally related. These findings reveal that clonal expansion of strains after acquiring a blaNDM−5-carrying self-transmissible IncX3 plasmid and subsequent deleting the ~ 20.6 kb backbone region is most likely to contribute to the emergence of this plasmid lineage.

Two IncFII/IncR plasmid subtypes contributed significantly to the dissemination of blaKPC−2 in K. pneumoniae ST11

In our collection, eighteen of the 23 (78.26%) KPC-KP were positive for IncFII/IncR plasmids, confirming that blaKPC−2-bearing IncFII/IncR plasmid lineage is increasingly prevalent in K. pneumoniae ST11 strains15,52. By sequence alignment to reference plasmids, we successfully obtained 12 complete sequences of blaKPC−2-carrying plasmids (Table 3). Unlike blaNDM−5, blaKPC−2 usually coexists with many other antibiotic resistance determinants on the MDR plasmid, which may increase the transmission risk of blaKPC−2 by co-selection.

These IncFII/IncR plasmids, except pKPC2_SCNJ17 and pKPC2_SCNJ26, shared ≥ 99.98% identity with each other with query coverage ≥ 98%, and are almost identical to the epidemic blaKPC−2-bearing plasmids recovering from clinical K. pneumoniae strains from different parts of China (Fig. 3A), such as p3_L39 (CP033956, Zhejiang, 2018), p16ZR-187-IncFII-133-CR (MN182746, Hong Kong, 2019), pKPC84_130120 (CP071162, Sichuan, 2019). This finding reveals that these p3_L39-like plasmids are a major vehicle in mediating the dissemination of blaKPC−2 in CRKP strains on a nationwide scale53. In these plasmids, blaKPC−2 was located in a Tn1721-related structure ΔTn1721-blaKPC−2-IS26-ΔTn3 (Fig. 3A). It was demonstrated that the replicative transposition of Tn1721 and IS26 facilitated the transfer of blaKPC−254.

pKPC2_SCNJ17 is similar to pKPC2_SCNJ26, sharing 99.94% identity with 91% coverage. Sequence analysis showed that they also exhibited high similarity (> 99.9% identity, ≥ 91% coverage) to many previously reported blaKPC−2-bearing plasmids, such as pKPC2_015093 (CP036301, Sichuan, 2017)52 and pKPC-2_FZKP4523 (CP101534, Sichuan, 2021)55 (Fig. 3B). These pKPC2_015093-like plasmids represent another blaKPC−2-bearing IncFII/IncR plasmid subtype that is circulating in clinical K. pneumoniae isolates in China. In these plasmids, the core blaKPC−2 unit ΔISKpn6-blaKPC−2-ISKpn27-ΔtnpR was bracketed by two copies of IS26 in the same orientation (Fig. 3B). An IS26-mediated recombination event is most likely to contribute to the dissemination of blaKPC−2 in these situations55. Conjugation experiments showed that both IncFII/IncR plasmid subtypes could not be transferred to reception E. coli. The dissemination of blaKPC−2 might be attributed to the clonal expansion of CRKP strains. Moreover, blaKPC−2-bearing IncFII/IncR plasmids continue to evolve while circulating in bacterial populations.

Conclusion

We are aware of the limitations of the current study. First, this study presented genomic characteristics of CRKP in the form of a single center and the number of clinical isolates collected for genomic analysis was low, which did not allow a full assessment of the epidemic characteristics of CRKP in the local region. Second, a long sequencing approach for representative plasmids could be a valuable add to the present work. Third, virulence genes in the CRKP strains were predicted by bioinformatics, which did not guarantee phenotypic hypervirulence. Animal infection experiments are conducive to revealing the virulence of these potentially hypervirulent strains.

In summary, the data presented in this study expand the understanding of molecular epidemiology and genomic features of clinical CRKP, demonstrating a significant clonal expansion of blaKPC−2-positive ST11 strains, and both horizontal and clonal transfer of blaNDM−5 in CRKP. The observed convergence of hypervirulence and carbapenem resistance in these CRKP strains highlights that adequate control measures are needed to prevent such superbugs from further dissemination in hospitals and the community. Also, the implementation of genomic surveillance to trace evolving CRKP in the nosocomial settings is warranted.
