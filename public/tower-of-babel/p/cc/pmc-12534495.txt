Regularized CCA identifies sex-specific brain-behavior associations in adolescent psychopathology
Authors: Leo Milecki, Camila Gonzalez, Ehsan Adeli, Kate B Nooner, Mert R Sabuncu, Amy Kuceyeski, Qingyu Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdolescence is a critical period of neural development and a sensitive window for the emergence of psychiatric symptoms. Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) provides a unique opportunity to investigate brain-behavior associations. However, the role of sex-specific differences in these associations remains underexplored, despite their potential to reveal heterogeneous neurobiological mechanisms and guide personalized interventions. In this study, we analyzed data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, comprising 7,892 adolescents (9–10 years old, 3,896 females). Using Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) and a rigorous cross-validation framework, we identified associations between cortical-to-cortical (Cor-Cor) and cortical-to-subcortical (Cor-Sub) functional connectivity and eight symptom domains from the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL). Unlike previous approaches, we directly examined sex differences within the brain-behavior mappings by applying separate CCA models in boys and girls to uncover differential connectivity-behavior relationships. Our analysis uncovered two reproducible components for both Cor-Cor and Cor-Sub mappings on the whole cohort (r1 = 0.130, p < 0.001, r2 = 0.122, p < 0.01 for Cor-Cor; r1 = 0.157, p < 0.001, r2 = 0.115, p < 0.01 for Cor-Sub). Importantly, sex-stratified analyses revealed distinct patterns of brain-behavior associations. Among females, high loadings on attention and thought problems were linked to high loadings on default mode network, whereas in males, attention and thought problems were linked to sensorimotor networks. Compared to females, males also had higher loadings on internalizing symptoms, such as anxious/depressed and withdrawn/depressed symptoms, coupled with lower loadings on putamen and hippocampus functional connectivity. These findings suggest there may be fundamentally different brain-behavior mappings across the sexes in adolescence, in addition to previously reported sex differences in functional connectivity and behavioral profiles. By revealing sex-specific neural correlates of psychiatric symptoms in early adolescence, this study paves the way for sex-informed strategies in clinical risk assessment and personalized treatment design.Subject terms: Human behaviour, Neuroscience

Introduction

Early adolescence is a critical risk window for the emergence of psychopathology [1]. These developmental periods are marked by profound changes in brain structure and function, as well as increased susceptibility to mental health disorders. Neuroimaging-based brain-behavior association studies offer a powerful tool for uncovering the neurobiological underpinnings of mental health challenges and individual variability in neural mechanisms that predict behavioral phenotypes [2–6]. However, identifying reliable brain-behavior relationships has proven challenging [7–9], in part due to limited statistical power from small sample sizes [10, 11] and methodological variability [12–14].

One notable issue is the high degree of symptom heterogeneity and comorbidity in youth, who usually exhibit less clearly defined psychopathology and heterotypic continuity of symptoms and phenotypes [15]. Traditional univariate predictive models, which attempt to link imaging features to each symptom domain independently, often fall short in capturing the multidimensional and overlapping nature of psychiatric symptoms. In contrast, multivariate-to-multivariate frameworks, such as canonical correlation analysis (CCA), explicitly model the covariance between sets of brain and behavioral variables, making them well-equipped to discover shared neural substrates across behavioral domains and the transdiagnostic and multidimensional nature of psychopathology.

The complexity of symptom heterogeneity is further compounded by sex differences in mental health outcomes, including differences in prevalence, symptom presentation, risk factors, and trajectories of disorders, such as variations in the onset, progression, and behaviors like alcohol use [16–21]. Sex differences in mental health outcomes have been extensively studied in youth [22]. Girls generally report slightly lower subjective well-being than boys at age 11–12, with a progressive decline over time, whereas boys’ well-being remains relatively stable [23, 24]. For example, phenotyping of 454 girls and 295 boys aged 9–18 years with conduct disorder revealed that girls had higher rates of comorbid lifetime mental disorders and functional impairments and might have a later age of onset [25]. These behavioral variations coincide with pronounced sex differences in brain structure and function during adolescence [26–28]. Research indicates males exhibited greater variability in functional network connectivity, while females’ connectivity patterns stabilized around the ages of 9–11 years [19]. Early in development, boys exhibited greater between-module connectivity, whereas girls showed more within-module connectivity, contributing to sex-specific differences in cognitive performance [29]. Despite well-documented evidence of sex differences in brain structure, functional connectivity, and behavioral outcomes, few studies have explicitly examined how these differences shape sex-specific brain-behavior associations [30, 31]. In other words, the heterogeneity of interest lies not only in the neural and behavioral data themselves but also in the mapping function between them [32].

The current study aimed to explore such relational heterogeneity by mapping sex-specific brain-behavior relationships [33–36] using CCA in a large, well-characterized cohort from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study. However, CCA is sensitive to outliers and prone to overfitting, especially in high-dimensional settings. To mitigate these issues, we employed a regularized variant of CCA (rCCA) with L2-penalization and optimized its hyperparameters through nested cross-validation. This approach improves model stability and generalizability by controlling coefficient shrinkage and preventing overfitting to noise in the data. Specifically, we examined the relationships between resting-state functional connectivity and a broad set of behavioral measures from the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL). Given the distinct roles of cortical and subcortical networks in cognitive and emotional functions, we separately analyzed cortical-to-cortical (Cor-Cor) and cortical-to-subcortical (Cor-Sub) connectivity in our brain-behavior mappings. More importantly, our study directly examines sex differences in brain-behavior mappings, in contrast to prior studies that typically assess sex differences separately in brain connectivity or behavioral measures without analyzing their integrated relationship. We hypothesize that major modes of brain-behavior mapping differ by sex, driven by distinct brain patterns and different behavioral constructs. Our findings highlight the need to expand data-driven approaches beyond brain subtyping and behavioral phenotyping to analyses that capture heterogeneity in brain-behavior relationships [32]. Understanding sex-specific brain-behavior associations has the potential to inform early identification of at-risk children and guide the design of targeted prevention/interventions during critical developmental periods.

Materials & methods

Participants

The Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study is a longitudinal prospective study that tracks participants from ages 9–10 over a 10-year period. This diverse cohort includes 11,868 adolescents enrolled from 21 research sites across the United States (https://abcdstudy.org). Informed consent was obtained from parents, and assent was obtained from all children. Recruitment procedures and details are fully documented in prior publications [37, 38]. The ABCD consortium employs standardized, harmonized assessments across multiple domains: neurocognition, physical and mental health, social and emotional function, and cultural and environmental context, along with multimodal neuroimaging, structural and functional, and bioassays. For this study, we utilized data from baseline visits from the ABCD 5.1 Data Release, available on the NIMH Data Archive. Participants were excluded if they (i) had missing CBCL scores (Nexcl = 2), (ii) failed rs-fMRI quality assessment [39] (Nexcl = 1650), or (iii) had a sibling in the dataset, in which case one sibling per family was randomly retained (Nexcl = 2061). Applying these criteria ensured data quality and independence across participants and resulted in a final sample size of N = 7892 subjects. Table 1 provides summary statistics on the demographic variables, including sex at birth, race/ethnicity, and parental education (see also Supplementary Methods A.1).

Psychiatric symptoms and neuroimaging data

As part of the Achenbach System of Empirically Based Assessment (ASEBA) [40], the CBCL is a well-established tool for assessing behavioral and emotional problems in children and adolescents. Based on 113 items rated by parents on a three-point Likert scale (0=absent, 1=occurs sometimes, 2=occurs often), it assesses eight syndrome scales: anxious/depressed, withdrawn/depressed, somatic complaints, social problems, thought problems, attention problems, rule-breaking behavior, and aggressive behavior. Scores from the CBCL are organized into two higher-order factors, internalizing and externalizing behaviors [41]. Table 1 provides the means and standard deviations (m (std)) of these raw scores. To adjust for potential confounding factors, each CBCL score underwent linear residualization with respect to sex, race/ethnicity, and parental education. To avoid data leakage [42], this residualization was performed separately within each train/test split by fitting the residualization model on the training set and applying it to the test set.

The resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) data were downloaded from the official data release 5.1 (https://nad.nih.gov/study.html?id=2313), preprocessed by the standardized protocols established by the ABCD study [37, 38] and including quality control metrics to allow for identification of high-quality rs-fMRI scans.

The data release contains 169 cortical-to-cortical (Cor-Cor) connectivity and 247 cortical-to-subcortical (Cor-Sub) connectivity features. Specifically, the rs-fMRI preprocessing pipeline [39] included head motion correction [43], B0 distortion correction [44], gradient nonlinearities correction [45], and between-scan motion correction [46]. The average time courses were calculated for 19 subcortical regions [47] and 13 cortical networks defined by the Gordon parcellation [48]. The functional connectivity within a network was calculated as the average of the Fisher-transformed correlations of time courses between pairs of ROIs belonging to that network. The connectivity between one network and another was calculated by averaging the correlations between each ROI in the first network and each ROI in the second. This process resulted in 169 Cor-Cor connectivity features. Additionally, the Cor-Sub connectivity features between each cortical network and subcortical gray matter regions [49] were computed by averaging correlations between all ROIs in a cortical network and each subcortical ROI. Finally, each connectivity feature underwent a similar residualization procedure as in the CBCL scores, i.e., by regressing out the effects of sex, race/ethnicity, and parental education. In addition, the site variable was included in the regression as an additional covariate to remove potential confounding from data collection site variability. These residualized connectivity measures were used in the following CCA analysis.

Brain-behavior mapping

In this study, we leveraged Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) [50, 51] to explore the multivariate brain-behavior relationships. In particular, we used Regularized CCA (rCCA) with l2-norm regularization to derive stable linear combinations of the 8 CBCL scores that maximally correlated with linear combinations of the 169 Cor-Cor connectivity features (see detailed formulation in Supplementary Methods A.2), which was shown to have less overfitting on the ABCD data (Supplementary Table B.1). To train and evaluate the rCCA model, we conducted leave-3-sites-out cross-validation by partitioning the data 10 times, where each partition separated the 21 sites into 18 sites for training and 3 sites for testing (see training and testing partitions in Supplementary Table A.1). Regularization hyperparameters of the rCCA model were selected based on a 50-fold inner cross-validation within the training set. Specifically, we searched 13 values for each hyperparameter with a log-spaced grid ranging from 10−3 to 101. The optimal hyperparameter combination was selected based on the highest first canonical correlation averaged across the 50 inner validation sets. After determining the best regularization hyperparameters, the rCCA model was trained on the whole training set, and the final model was applied to the test set, resulting in 8 canonical components ordered by the strength of their canonical correlations. We repeated the leave-three-site out validation 10 times and assessed the statistical significance of the mean canonical correlation (over the 10 testing runs) of each component through permutation testing (Supplementary Methods A.3). For each significant component (p < 0.05), we interpreted the contributions of each original CBCL and connectivity feature to the correlation by computing the average canonical loadings from the rCCA models over the 10 testing runs. The statistical significance of the loadings was again assessed by permutation tests with Bonferroni correction. For connectivity features, we regarded the top 10 canonical loadings as the strongest contributors to the brain-behavior associations. Finally, all steps in the Cor-Cor analysis were repeated by relating CBCL scores with Cor-Sub functional connectivity to enable separate interpretation of the contributions of subcortical regions to psychopathology. In parallel, we also conducted an analysis to relate CBCL scores to all cortical and subcortical connectivity in a single rCCA model.

Sex-specific mappings

To examine sex differences in brain-behavior relationships (in either Cor-Cor or Cor-Sub analysis), the optimal rCCA regularization hyperparameters were applied separately to males and females to retrain two models within each train/test split. Following the same approach used in the whole cohort, we analyzed the canonical correlations and canonical loadings for both behavioral and brain connectivity features in each sex. To assess whether the brain-behavior relationships differed by sex, we applied permutation testing, where the data were randomly partitioned into two sub-groups in both training and test sets. Then, we retrained the rCCA models with the same regularization hyperparameters to evaluate the absolute difference between subgroup-specific CBCL canonical loadings. For each of the 10 train/test splits, 1,000 permutations were performed to build a null distribution, from which the statistical significance of the observed sex differences in CBCL canonical loadings was measured. This approach allowed us to quantify whether the observed sex differences in CBCL canonical loadings deviated significantly from those expected under the null hypothesis of no sex effect. Similarly, we tested whether the brain patterns that defined the canonical correlation were different between males and females. To do so, we used the same permutation procedure to quantify the sex differences in canonical loadings among the top 10 connectivity features identified by the whole-population model. To further interpret the sex differences on the relational level, we predicted each CBCL score with significant sex differences (in loadings) from connectivity features with sex differences by fitting linear regression models independently in males and females. Finally, to determine whether the predictive power of the regression models differed between sexes, we used Fisher’s r-to-z transformation to statistically compare the correlation between predicted and observed CBCL scores between male and female subgroups.

Results

Population-level analysis

Cross-validating the joint rCCA that related 8 CBCL scores with all Cor-Cor or Cor-Sub connectivity (Supplementary Table B.2 and Supplementary Figure B.1) revealed that the extracted components were automatically separated by Cor-Cor connectivity and Cor-Sub connectivity, suggesting that the two types of connectivity measures could be separately analyzed. Thus, the following results focused on separately cross-validating the rCCA model specific to Cor-Cor and the model specific to Cor-Sub (Table 2 and Fig. 1). All p-values were Bonferroni corrected for the number of CBCL scores or the number of connectivity features. Permutation testing confirmed significant canonical correlations for the first two components in both Cor-Cor (p < 0.001 for component 1, p = 0.001 for component 2) and Cor-Sub (p < 0.001 for component 1, p = 0.005 for component 2) analyses (Fig. 1 I). In contrast, subsequent components (components 3–8) exhibited weaker correlations and did not reach statistical significance (Table 2).

Next, we examined patterns of brain-behavior correlation defined by the first two canonical components of Cor-Cor and Cor-Sub analyses. The behavioral canonical loadings were highly stable across cross-validation folds given the narrow standard deviation of the behavioral loadings in Fig. 1 II. For both Cor-Cor and Cor-Sub analyses, the first canonical component (blue) was driven by rule-breaking behaviors (p < 0.001 for both Cor-Cor and Cor-Sub) and social problems (p = 0.004 for Cor-Cor, p = 0.026 for Cor-Sub), whereas Component 2 (green) was primarily driven by attention (p = 0.001 for Cor-Cor, p < 0.001 for Cor-Sub) and thought problems (p = 0.004 for Cor-Cor, p < 0.001 for Cor-Sub). The difference between Cor-Cor and Cor-Sub analyses was that Component 1 of the Cor-Cor analysis was associated with higher loadings for attention problems, while Component 2 of the Cor-Sub analysis was more strongly associated with depression symptoms.

Figure 1 III highlights key brain connectivity that drove significant brain-behavior correlations. Chord diagrams represent the top 10 functional connections of the identified significant components with the strongest canonical loadings in cortical (Cor-Cor) and subcortical (Cor-Sub) analyses. For Component 2, sensorimotor networks, associated with both hands and mouth, dominated the correlation. On the other hand, brain-behavior correlations were driven by widespread connectivity in subcortical regions: Component 1 of the Cor-Sub analysis prominently involved functional connectivity to the pallidum region, whereas Component 2 emphasized connections to the caudate and putamen.

Sex differences

Cross-validation of rCCA models independently within boys and girls confirmed that the first two canonical components in both Cor-Cor (r1 = 0.123, p < 0.001, r2 = 0.103, p = 0.006 for boys, r1 = 0.089, p = 0.003, r2 = 0.108, p < 0.001for girls) and Cor-Sub (r1 = 0.163, p < 0.001, r2 = 0.097, p = 0.017 for boys, r1 = 0.119, p < 0.001, r2 = 0.094, p = 0.022 for girls) analyses were still significant. However, the behavioral and connectivity patterns underlying these canonical correlations exhibited notable sex differences. Figure 2 I displays the behavior loadings for all significant components separately for boys (purple) and girls (orange). In Component 1 of the Cor-Cor analysis (Fig. 2 a), while both sexes had high loadings for rule-breaking behaviors, girls had higher loadings for thought (|∆|=0.300, p < 0.001) and attention (trend-level, |∆|=0.332, p = 0.010) problems compared to boys. On the other hand, boys had significantly higher loadings in Component 2 (Fig. 2 c) for attention problems (|∆|=0.380, p < 0.001) and anxious/depressed symptoms than girls (|∆|=0.280, p = 0.010). Lastly, in Component 2 of the Cor-Sub analysis (Fig. 2 d), boys also had higher loadings for anxious/depressed (|∆|=0.373, p < 0.001), social problems (|∆|=0.328, p < 0.001), and withdrawn/depressed (|∆|=0.269, p = 0.030) than girls.

Discussion

This study provides novel insights into sex differences in neurobiological correlates of psychiatric symptoms in early adolescence. Using a large, population-based sample from the ABCD study, we identified four reproducible components linking resting-state functional connectivity to psychiatric symptom dimensions from the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL). By separately analyzing cortical and subcortical connectivity, this work provides a granular understanding of connectivity patterns underlying adolescent psychopathology. Results suggest the two major modes of brain-behavior mapping are anchored in rule-breaking behaviors and attention and thought problems. Importantly, sex-stratified analyses revealed distinct patterns of brain-behavior associations involving functional connectivity in default mode, salience, sensorimotor networks, and multiple sub-cortical regions. Critically, boys exhibited stronger associations between salience-to-sensorimotor connectivity and attention and thought problems than girls. These findings emphasize that brain-behavior relationships are not homogeneous across sexes and highlight the need to investigate sex-specific neural mechanisms underlying youth psychopathology.

At the population level, the two modes of brain-behavior mapping are highly similar in CBCL loadings between Cor-Cor and Cor-Sub analyses, with one mode focusing primarily on attention problems and the other on rule-breaking behaviors. Notably, these two behavioral constructs were also identified as the determinants of the two strongest brain-behavior mapping modes in a prior CBCL-based CCA analysis [50], despite their substantial differences in cohort construction and rs-fMRI processing pipelines. When examining critical underpinning functional connectivity patterns, our Cor-Cor analysis suggests rule-breaking behaviors were linked to functional connectivity in sensorimotor (hand and mouth), default, dorsal attention, and cingulo-opercular networks, and 4 out of these 5 networks were also identified as key connectivity patterns by Xu et al. [51] In particular, the strongest loading was recorded between the default mode network and the dorsal attention network. These two networks have been previously shown to exhibit heightened functional connectivity in impulsive juveniles [52]. Typically, these networks function in an anti-correlated manner [53, 54], with the default mode network supporting self-referential thought processes (e.g., mind-wandering, introspection) and the dorsal attention network engaging in top-down, goal-directed attention to external stimuli. Dysregulation and imbalance between the two networks have been implicated in impulsivity and risk-taking behaviors [55, 56]. The second major brain-behavior correlation in our Cor-Cor analysis was represented by the coupling between attention/thought problems and sensorimotor connectivity, which was also identified as a critical network by Xu et al. [51] As the sensorimotor network often integrates and coordinates between sensory inputs, motor responses, and higher-order cognitive processes, its disruption has been frequently observed in children with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, or ADHD [57], and impaired attention following traumatic brain injury [58].

Subcortical structures play a crucial, though often underappreciated, role in cognition [59], emotion [60], and social functions [61]. For example, despite highly similar findings in cortical networks in Xu et al. [51], subcortical regions did not play a significant role in rule-behavior problems in their analysis. This missed identification likely stems from collinearity between cortical and subcortical connectivity, which can obscure the unique contribution of subcortical circuits. Supporting this interpretation, our joint CCA analysis using all cortical and subcortical connectivity yielded only two significant components (Supplementary Table B.2), whereas separate Cor-Cor and Cor-Sub analyses each revealed two distinct components. Notably, the behavioral loadings were highly similar across Cor-Cor and Cor-Sub models, suggesting that each behavioral construct involves distributed functional connectivity across both cortical and subcortical regions. The Cor-Sub analysis revealed that the major modes of behavioral constructs are primarily linked to the putamen and pallidum, which collectively form the lentiform nucleus and are critical for motor function [62], inhibitory control [63], and reward processing [64]. Their interaction with sensorimotor and dorsal attention networks together contributes to the strongest brain-behavior mapping in the adolescent population.

Our analysis also furthered understanding of sex differences in brain-behavior mapping, which traditionally relies on identifying sex differences in either brain connectivity or behavioral symptoms separately [26, 65, 66]. Instead, we identified sex-specific brain-behavior mappings and significantly different CBCL and functional loadings that defined the canonical correlations. Such relational heterogeneity was mainly driven by the differential mapping between CBCL and cortical connectivity (Cor-Cor). While boys show two separable dimensions of psychopathology (Fig. 2 a,c) that are similar to the whole-population results, girls seem to have integrated neural underpinnings across attention and thought problems and rule-breaking behaviors (similarly high CBCL loadings in Fig. 2 a,c). This difference was likely driven by sex differences in functional connectivity in default mode, salience, and sensorimotor networks (Fig. 2 e,g). Notably, the salience and sensorimotor networks cooperate to align sensory and motor responses with attentional and emotional processing, enabling adaptive responses to environmental demands [67]. Disruptions in their connectivity can result in difficulties in sensory processing and motor coordination (as often observed in Autism Spectrum Disorder [68]) and hyperactivity or impulsive motor behaviors (often observed in conditions like ADHD [69]). Thus, the elevated salience-sensorimotor reactivity coupled with greater attention/thought problems in boys might reflect prior research suggesting that certain neurodevelopmental disorders such as autism spectrum disorder and ADHD are diagnosed more frequently in boys [70–72].

Limitations

Despite the robustness of our methodological framework, certain limitations warrant consideration. First, the focus on baseline visits of ABCD samples (ages 9–10) limited the investigation of symptoms with later onset and may also be influenced by sex differences in pubertal development, as girls at this age are typically more advanced than boys in pubertal stage progression [73]. Other than pubertal development, social and environmental factors may also play a role in shaping brain-behavior relationships [74]. To explore this, we examined differences based on exposure to adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), which are known to exert long-term effects on mental health. However, our workflow detected no significant differences in brain-behavior mappings between participants with or without ACEs at this early developmental stage. Another concern is that this study relies on binary sex categories as reported in the ABCD dataset, which may not fully capture the nuanced interplay between biological sex and gender identity. While reported gender [75] in our sample differed from biological sex for only 21 out of 7892 participants, this distinction has been highlighted in prior work on mental illness [76], neuroimaging [77], and brain networks in children [78], as social and environmental factors related to gender could influence brain-behavior relationships and warrant consideration in future studies. Lastly, while regularized CCA is a powerful tool for identifying linear multivariate associations, it cannot identify non-linear brain-behavior associations. To this end, deep CCA may uncover more complex connectivity patterns that remain undetected by linear approaches.

Conclusion

This study advances our understanding of sex-specific neurobiological correlates of psychiatric symptoms in early adolescence. Leveraging baseline neuroimaging and behavioral data from the ABCD Study, a regularized CCA model identified reproducible associations between resting-state functional connectivity and psychiatric symptom dimensions. Sex-stratified analyses further revealed distinct brain-behavior mapping patterns, with boys showing stronger associations between salience-to-sensorimotor connectivity and attention and thought problems than girls. By revealing sex-dependent neurobiological mechanisms, this work lays the foundation for developing personalized interventions and risk assessment tools tailored to the unique developmental and clinical needs of boys and girls. Such efforts set the path to ultimately improving the prevention and treatment of adolescent psychiatric disorders.
