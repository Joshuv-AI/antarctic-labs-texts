Tetrandrine enhances cognition in SAH rats via gut microbiota CYP51 and TLR4 signaling
Authors: Huan Liu, Qin Zou, Tao Xiang, Rui Zhu, Zigui Chen, Rui Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSubarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is a common cerebrovascular disease that can lead to cognitive impairment. Although tetrandrine (Tet) has been proposed as a potential therapeutic agent, its efficacy in the treatment of SAH has not been fully explored. To investigate Tet effects on SAH, a rat model was established and divided into an SAH + vehicle and an SAH + Tet group. Cognitive function and behavioral performance were assessed using the Morris water maze and open field tests. Inflammatory cytokine levels were measured by ELISA, and hippocampal injury was evaluated by hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining. Neuronal loss was quantified using Nissl staining, while neuronal density was assessed via immunofluorescence. The expression of the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway related-proteins was examined by Western blotting, and 16 S rRNA sequencing was conducted to determine differences in gut microbiota composition across the groups. Tet treatment significantly reduced mNSS and SAH assessment scores in SAH rats, suggesting an improvement in neurological and cognitive function. Behavioral analysis demonstrated that Tet increased escape latency, movement speed, and total distance in the Morris water maze. Histological staining revealed attenuated hippocampal damage and decreased neuronal death in the Tet-treated group. These neuroprotective effects were accompanied by reduced expression of TLR4 and NF-κB pathway components, as well as decreased secretion of LPS, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. Notably, Tet modulated the gut microbiota, restoring microbial diversity and abundance, and this modulation was associated with changes in the CYP51 metabolic pathway. Introduction

Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is a cerebrovascular condition characterized by the extravasation of arterial blood into the subarachnoid space of the brain1. It is a serious neurological disorder with an annual incidence of 6–7 cases per 100,000 individuals and a high mortality rate approaching 50%12. Current treatment strategies for SAH primarily focus on preventing the rupture of intracranial aneurysms, a major cause of early brain injury (EBI), and managing cerebral vasospasm3. Among the various pathological processes involved, inflammation has been recognized as a key contributor to the severity of brain injury following SAH4. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify therapeutic agents capable of attenuating inflammatory brain injury.

Tetrandrine (Tet), a bisbenzylisoquinoline alkaloid extracted from the root of Stephania tetrandra S. Moore5, has demonstrated protective effects against ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury in several organs, including the heart, liver, and small intestine6. Its cardioprotective effects are thought to be mediated by inhibition of calcium influx, reduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in both serum and tissues, and suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines7. In cerebral ischemia models, Tet has been reported to reduce inflammation and neuronal apoptosis by inactivating the TLR4 signaling pathway8. However, the effects of Tet on SAH have not yet been reported, and it remains unclear whether Tet can improve cognitive function in SAH through modulation of the TLR4 signaling pathway.

Studies have shown that alterations in gut microbiota composition may increase the risk of SAH9,10. A stable gut microbial community is essential for maintaining intestinal homeostasis and regulating immune responses11–13, and disruption of this balance can trigger chronic inflammation at the intestinal epithelium and promote neuroinflammation through the microbiota-gut-brain axis14,15. Chronic intestinal inflammation resulting from microbial imbalance compromises the intestinal barrier, leading to increased permeability, commonly referred to as “leaky gut"16,17, which allows pro-inflammatory microbial products, such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS), and cytokines to enter systemic circulation and induce widespread inflammatory responses18. In this context, LPS can activate toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), which is expressed in both the gut and brain regions such as the substantia nigra, leading to activation of local immune responses19,20. The precise mechanisms by which the microbiota-gut-brain axis contributes to SAH pathophysiology remain unclear, and further investigation may reveal novel therapeutic targets.

TLR4 is highly expressed in the brain, and its activation is triggered by the presence of blood components and damaged brain tissue following aneurysmal rupture21. When TLR4 is activated, the MyD88-dependent TLR4 pathway initiates nuclear translocation of NF-κB, thereby promoting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-12, IL-8, IL-6, and IL-1β22. Sterol 14α-demethylase (CYP51), a member of the cytochrome P450 enzyme family, is involved in the demethylation of sterol precursors23 and plays an essential role in sterol biosynthesis across diverse biological systems, including fungi, bacteria, plants, and mammals24. CYP51 promotes the demethylation of berberine, enhancing its intestinal absorption24; it can also metabolize FLZ and facilitate its conversion and absorption in the gut microbiota25; CYP51 activity is involved in lipid metabolism in diet-induced obese mice26; increased intake of apple peel leads to reduced body weight and adipose tissue mass, accompanied by downregulated CYP51 expression27. We have reason to believe that CYP51 not only participates in obesity but also contributes to drug conversion and absorption through its activity in the gut microbiota. Modulating CYP51 in the gut microbiota may influence the CYP51 and TLR4/NF-κB pathway, thereby alleviating brain injury in rats after SAH.

To improve cognitive function following SAH, it is important to investigate the relationship among Tet, gut microbiota, CYP51, the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway, and SAH-associated cognitive impairment. Tet may alleviate gut microbiota dysbiosis and suppress activation of the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, thereby contributing to the regulation of neuroinflammation. Modulation of the gut-brain axis through inhibition of the TLR4/NF-κB pathway may play a beneficial role in ameliorating cognitive dysfunction associated with SAH. Further investigation into the regulatory effects of Tet on CYP51 and the TLR4/NF-κB pathway via gut microbiota may enhance our understanding of the underlying mechanisms contributing to brain injury in SAH. These findings could offer new perspectives and potential targets for developing effective therapeutic strategies. A graphical abstract is provided to illustrate the proposed mechanism by which Tet modulates the gut-brain axis to improve cognitive impairment induced by SAH.

Materials and methods

Animal experiments and grouping

Adult male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (SPF grade), purchased from Hunan Silaike Jingda Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (Changsha, China), weighing between 220 and 250 g, were housed under controlled environmental conditions, including a temperature of 20–25 °C, relative humidity of approximately 50%, and ambient noise levels below 85 dB. All animals were fasted overnight before the surgery. After successful model establishment, the animals were returned to their cages and maintained under standard conditions until the designated time for experimental procedures.

The rat model of SAH was established using the endovascular perforation method. Briefly, the rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 3% pentobarbital sodium (30 mg/kg). Once adequate anesthesia was confirmed, the animals were secured in a supine position on the surgical platform. The left internal carotid artery was exposed, and a 3 cm length of 4 − 0 monofilament nylon suture was gently advanced until resistance was encountered at the bifurcation. The suture thread was then advanced an additional 2 mm to induce arterial perforation and bleeding, and immediately thereafter, the filament was withdrawn, and the surgical incision was closed using standard suturing techniques. Throughout the procedure, aseptic conditions were strictly maintained28. After model establishment, the rats were randomly assigned into four groups (n = 6 per group): sham + vehicle, SAH + vehicle, SAH + Tet, and SAH + Tet + ABX. Rats in the SAH + Tet group received intragastric administration of Tet (C₃₈H₄₂N₂O₆, HPLC ≥ 98%, Aladdin, Cat# 877811) at a dose of 20 mg/kg/day, starting 2 h after SAH induction and continuing daily until the endpoint29. The SAH + Tet + ABX group was pretreated with an antibiotic cocktail for 5 days prior to modeling. The ABX solution, composed of ampicillin (1 g/L), neomycin sulfate (1 g/L), metronidazole (1 g/L), and kanamycin hydrochloride (0.5 g/L), was administered via drinking water. In the sham + vehicle and SAH + vehicle groups, rats received daily gavage with an equal volume of normal saline. Tet treatment in the designated groups continued for 28 days post-modeling. After completion of cognitive function assessments30, all rats were euthanized. At the end of the experiments, the rats were euthanized via an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (250 mg/kg). Death was confirmed by the absence of spontaneous breathing, cardiac arrest, and unresponsiveness to noxious stimuli. Subsequently, peripheral blood, hippocampal tissue, ileal tissue, and fecal samples were collected for further analyses, including assessments of inflammation, tissue injury, and gut microbiota composition. The animal experiments was approved by Animal Ethics Committee, Affiliated Nanhua Hospital, University of South China (No. 2023-ky-201). All experiments were conducted in accordance with the South China University Guidelines for Animal Experiments and reported following the ARRIVE guidelines.

Neurological deficit evaluation (mNSS)

Neurological function was assessed using mNSS, a series of tests evaluating motor function, abnormal movements, sensory function (including vision, tactile response, and proprioception), and reflex activity. A score of 1 was assigned for each failed task or absent reflex, and higher scores indicated more severe neurological impairment, with a normal score of 0 and a maximum score of 18. The degree of brain injury was determined based on the overall mNSS score.

SAH scoring

The severity of SAH was assessed using the Fisher and Hunt-Hess grading scales. Fisher grade classification was based on computed tomography (CT) findings: Grade 1 indicated no visible blood; Grade 2 indicated mild hemorrhage; Grade 3 indicated moderate hemorrhage; and Grade 4 indicated extensive bleeding on CT. Hunt-Hess grading was based on clinical presentation: Grade 1 represented asymptomatic status or only mild headache and neck stiffness; Grade 2 indicated moderate headache and alert consciousness with possible mild neurological deficits; Grade 3 denoted severe headache with altered consciousness and more pronounced deficits; Grade 4 indicated coma or stupor with severe neurological impairment; and Grade 5 represented a moribund state or shock.

Morris water maze experiment

The Morris water maze consisted of a circular pool measuring 130 cm in diameter and 50 cm in height, with a water depth of 30 cm. The pool was divided into four equal quadrants (I–IV), and a transparent plexiglass platform (11 cm in diameter and 29 cm in height) was placed in quadrant IV. The pool was enclosed by blue curtains and surrounded by fixed visual cues to assist spatial orientation. A camera was mounted above the pool and connected to a computer-based tracking system to record the swimming trajectories of the rats. All rats underwent place navigation training for 4 consecutive days. During each trial, the rat was randomly introduced into one of the quadrants, and the latency to locate the submerged platform, along with the swimming path, was recorded.

Open field experiment

Rats were individually placed into an open-field arena equipped with video cameras or motion-tracking systems to monitor and record their movement trajectories. The tracking system was calibrated to ensure precise detection of their position and locomotor activity. Their average movement speed was calculated based on the total distance traveled over time. A central zone was defined within the open-field apparatus, and each rat’s time in this area was recorded in seconds. These data were used to evaluate exploratory behavior and anxiety-like responses.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

ELISA kits were used to detect the levels of TNF-α (CSB-E11987r), IL-1β (CSB-E08055r), IL-6 (CSB-E04640r), and LPS (CSB-E14247r), all purchased from Elabscience (Wuhan Huamei Biotech Co., Ltd.), and CYP51 (m1928540V) from Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotech Co., Ltd. All reagents were equilibrated to room temperature (18–25 °C) for at least 30 min before use according to the manufacturer’s protocols. After the reaction, the wells were emptied and blotted dry without washing. Stop solution (50 µL per well) was added to terminate the reaction. The optical density (OD) of each well was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader (MB-530, HEALES, China) within 5 min of adding the stop solution.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from tissue samples using the RIPA lysis buffer (r0010, Solarbio, China). Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA method and quantified accordingly. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes via electrophoretic transfer. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against TLR4 (rabbit, 1:1000, 30400-1-AP, Proteintech), phosphorylated NF-κB (p-NF-κB, rabbit, 1:1000, ab76302, Abcam), and total NF-κB (rabbit, 1:1000, 10745-1-AP, Proteintech). After washing with Tris-buffered saline containing Tween-20, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:5000, AWS00001-1, Abiowell), and signals were visualized using Supernal Plus chemiluminescence substrate (K-12045-D50, Advansta, USA). β-actin was used as a loading control.

Nile red staining

The samples were first baked at 60 °C for 12 h. For deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were immersed in xylene (10023418, Guoyao, Shanghai) for 10 min twice, followed by rinsing with distilled water for 5 min. The sections were stained with Nile red staining solution for 0.5–1 min, adjusted according to staining intensity, then washed to remove excess dye. Differentiation was performed using 1% acetic acid until the background appeared colorless under the microscope, with cells and Nile bodies exhibiting distinct outlines. Sections were mounted with buffered glycerol and examined under a microscope (BA210T, Motic, China).

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining

The tissue sections were rinsed with distilled water for 5 min, then stained with hematoxylin (AWI0009a, Abiowell, China) for 1–10 min, rinsed again, and subjected to bluing using PBS. Eosin staining (AWI0029a, Abiowell, China) was subsequently performed for 1–5 min, followed by rinsing with distilled water. Dehydration was achieved using a graded alcohol series (95–100%), with each step lasting 5 min. The sections were then immersed in xylene for 10 min twice, mounted with neutral resin, and observed under a microscope.

Immunofluorescence

The tissue sections were subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval by boiling for 20 min, followed by cooling to room temperature for an additional 20 min, then incubated with diluted primary antibodies, including NeuN (1:50, AWA10318, Abiowell), ZO-1 (1:50, 21773-1-AP, Proteintech), Occludin (1:100, 27260-1-AP, Proteintech), and Claudin-1 (1:100, 13050-1-AP, Proteintech), targeting proteins in intestinal tissues. After incubation with the primary antibodies, fluorescently labeled anti-rabbitIgG secondary antibodies (AWS0005a, Abiowell) were applied, and the sections were incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. DAPI working solution (AWC0293a, Abiowell) was subsequently added for nuclear counterstaining at 37 °C for 10 min. Fluorescence signals were visualized under a fluorescence microscope.

16 S rRNA

Microbial DNA was extracted from fecal samples, and the 16 S rRNA gene region was amplified using specific primers. The amplified DNA fragments were used to construct sequencing libraries. High-throughput sequencing, typically performed using the Illumina platform, was conducted to generate sequence data. The raw data were processed through a series of bioinformatics analyses, including operational taxonomic unit (OTU) clustering, species annotation, and microbial diversity assessment. Predicted functional profiles of the microbial communities were inferred from 16 S rRNA data using PICRUSt2. The analysis was conducted under license KEGG_PERMISSION_252662.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s posthoc test was used to assess differences among multiple groups. Pearson correlation and distance correlation analyses were employed to evaluate correlation coefficients between variables. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Tet improved learning, memory abilities and cognitive functions in SAH rats by modulating the gut microbiota

A rat model of SAH was initially established to evaluate the therapeutic effects of Tet on SAH-related pathology. Throughout the three-week observation period, rats in the SAH + vehicle group demonstrated significantly higher mNSS scores compared to those in the Sham + vehicle group, indicating sustained neurological deficits (Figure S1). By the fourth week, both mNSS and SAH scores remained elevated in the SAH + vehicle group relative to the Sham + vehicle group. Notably, the SAH + Tet group exhibited significantly reduced scores compared to the SAH + vehicle group, whereas the SAH + Tet + ABX group showed a reversal of this improvement, with increased scores comparable to those of untreated SAH rats (Fig. 1A and B). To further evaluate behavioral performance, the Morris water maze and open field tests were conducted. Rats in the SAH + vehicle group showed impaired cognitive and locomotor abilities, characterized by shorter escape latency, reduced movement speed, and diminished total distance moved relative to the Sham + vehicle group. In contrast, the SAH + Tet group demonstrated improved performance, with prolonged escape latency, increased movement speed, and extended travel distance compared to the SAH + vehicle group (Fig. 1C). Collectively, these results indicate that Tet significantly enhances learning, memory, and cognitive function in SAH rats.

Tet alleviates cognitive impairment in SAH rats. (A) Modified neurological severity score (mNSS). (B) Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) score. (C) Behavioral performance in the Morris water maze and open field tests. *P < 0.05 vs. Sham + vehicle group; #P < 0.05 vs. SAH + vehicle group; &P < 0.05 vs. SAH + Tet group; n = 6 per group.

Tet inhibited TLR4/NF-κB pathway activation in SAH rats’ brain tissues by modulating the gut microbiota

Tet was found to alleviate cognitive impairment in SAH rats. To further explore the underlying mechanism, we investigated whether Tet exerted its effects through modulation of the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway. ELISA results demonstrated that the levels of inflammatory factors, including LPS, TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6, were significantly elevated in both the serum and hippocampal tissues of SAH rats. These increases were effectively suppressed by Tet treatment. However, co-administration of antibiotics (ABX) reversed the anti-inflammatory effects of Tet, suggesting that its therapeutic action may depend on gut microbiota integrity (Fig. 2A). The results showed that the expression levels of TLR4 and the p-NF-κB/NF-κB ratio were significantly upregulated in SAH rats. Tet treatment markedly reduced both TLR4 expression and NF-κB phosphorylation, whereas these suppressive effects were attenuated in the presence of ABX (Fig. 2B). Histological examination of the hippocampal CA1 region further revealed that Tet treatment alleviated neuronal injury, reduced the number of Nissl bodies, and enhanced NeuN fluorescence intensity compared to the SAH + vehicle group. In contrast, the SAH + Tet + ABX group exhibited more severe neuronal damage, increased Nissl body accumulation, and decreased NeuN expression (Fig. 2C–E). These findings indicate that Tet inhibits activation of the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway and reduces neuroinflammation in SAH rats, and that these effects are mediated, at least in part, by modulation of the gut microbiota.

Tet inhibits activation of the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway in the brain tissues of SAH rats. (A) Levels of LPS, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in serum and hippocampal tissues measured by ELISA. (B) Expression of TLR4, total NF-κB, and phosphorylated NF-κB (p-NF-κB) in hippocampal tissues analyzed by western blot. (C) Neuronal damage in the CA1 region of the hippocampus assessed by hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining. (D) Neuronal morphology in the CA1 region evaluated by Nissl staining. (E) NeuN expression in hippocampal neurons detected by immunofluorescence. *P < 0.05 vs. Sham + vehicle group; #P < 0.05 vs. SAH + vehicle group; &P < 0.05 vs. SAH + Tet group; n = 6 per group.

Tet restored gut microbiota imbalance in SAH rats

Considering that Tet improved cognitive function in SAH rats, potentially involving the gut microbiota, fecal samples were collected to determine differences in gut microbiota composition among the experimental groups. The rank abundance curve showed that microbial species were most abundant in the Sham + vehicle group, while the SAH + vehicle group exhibited the lowest microbial abundance. In the SAH + Tet group, microbial abundance was slightly increased compared to the SAH + vehicle group (Fig. 3A). Alpha diversity analysis indicated that microbial diversity was decreased in the SAH + vehicle group compared to the Sham + vehicle group. In contrast, the SAH + Tet group showed an increase in microbial diversity compared to the SAH + vehicle group (Fig. 3B). Beta diversity analysis demonstrated clear inter-group differences, with longer distances and larger variations among the groups (Fig. 3C). At the phylum level, microbial composition in the SAH + vehicle group was significantly less diverse than in the Sham + vehicle group. Tet treatment led to increased microbial diversity relative to the SAH + vehicle group (Fig. 3D). A similar trend was observed at the genus level, where the SAH + vehicle group exhibited reduced diversity, while the SAH + Tet group showed increased diversity (Fig. 3E). In summary, Tet was able to reverse the gut microbiota dysbiosis in SAH rats.

Tet inhibits gut microbiota dysbiosis in SAH rats. (A) Rank abundance curve illustrating microbial species richness across groups. (B) Alpha diversity analysis indicating within-group microbial diversity. (C) Beta diversity analysis evaluating inter-group differences in microbial community structure. (D) LEfSe analysis showing species-level differences at the phylum level. (E) LEfSe analysis showing species-level differences at the genus level.

Tet improved gut barrier function in SAH rats via modulation of the gut microbiota

Tet was found to inhibit gut microbiota dysbiosis in SAH rats, prompting further investigation into whether gut barrier function was also preserved. Intestinal tissues were collected and subjected to hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining. In the Sham + vehicle group, intestinal morphology appeared normal, with pink-stained nuclei, blue-stained cytoplasm, orderly cell arrangement, regular morphology, round or oval nuclei, and evenly distributed chromatin. In contrast, the SAH + vehicle group exhibited visible structural damage and infiltration of inflammatory cells. Notably, the SAH + Tet group showed no significant histological abnormalities, indicating improved intestinal tissue integrity compared to the SAH + vehicle group (Fig. 4A). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining was used to assess the expression of tight junction proteins ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin − 1 in intestinal tissues. The SAH + vehicle group demonstrated markedly reduced fluorescence intensity for all three proteins compared to the Sham + vehicle group. In contrast, the SAH + Tet group exhibited increased fluorescence intensity of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1, indicating restoration of tight junction protein expression relative to the SAH + vehicle group (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that Tet effectively improves gut barrier function in SAH rats, likely through modulation of the gut microbiota.

(B) Expression of tight junction proteins ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin − 1 in intestinal tissues detected by immunofluorescence staining. *P < 0.05 vs. Sham + vehicle group; #P < 0.05 vs. SAH + vehicle group; &P < 0.05 vs. SAH + Tet group; n = 6 per group.

Tet regulated CYP51 and the TLR4/NF-κB pathway via the gut microbiota to alleviate brain damage in SAH rats

CYP51, a cytochrome P450 enzyme expressed across fungi, bacteria, lower eukaryotes, higher plants, and mammals, plays an important role in xenobiotic metabolism24.To further investigate the potential mechanisms linking gut microbiota to brain injury improvement in SAH rats, CYP51 expression and related microbial changes were evaluated. Compared to the Sham + vehicle group, the CYP51 content was elevated in the SAH + vehicle group. Tet treatment significantly reduced CYP51 levels relative to the SAH + vehicle group, whereas CYP51 expression increased again in the SAH + Tet + ABX group (Fig. 5A). At the species level, microbial abundance increased significantly in the SAH + vehicle group compared to the Sham + vehicle group and was reduced in the SAH + Tet group compared to the SAH + vehicle group (Fig. 5B). Functional enrichment analysis showed that CYP51-associated pathways included D-glutamine and D-glutamate metabolism, ribosome biogenesis, mismatch repair, and peptidoglycan biosynthesis, among others (Fig. 5C).

Tet regulates CYP51 and the TLR4/NF-κB pathways via gut microbiota. (A) KEGG31 enrichment analysis of predicted metabolic pathways associated with CYP51. (B) LEfSe analysis of species-level differences in gut microbiota. (C) CYP51 enzyme activity in rat fecal samples measured by ELISA. (D) Correlation analysis of species-level microbial abundance with CYP51, LPS, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels. *P < 0.05 vs. Sham + vehicle group; #P < 0.05 vs. SAH + vehicle group; &P < 0.05 vs. SAH + Tet group; n = 6 per group.

Correlation analysis revealed that the expression of CYP51, along with LPS, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, was positively associated with certain bacterial species such as s_Burkholderiales_bacterium, s_Bacteroides_eggerthii, s_Collinsella_provencensis, and s_Anaerofustis_sp. Conversely, it was negatively correlated with species including s_uncultured_Clostridiales, s_Alistipes_obesi, s_Lactobacillus_faecis, s_unidentified_rumen, and s_uncultured_Desulfovibrionales (Fig. 5D). Overall, Tet was found to regulate CYP51 and the TLR4/NF-κB pathway via the gut microbiota, thereby alleviating brain injury and improving cognitive function in SAH rats.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the therapeutic potential of Tet in SAH, highlighting its ability to improve cognitive function in SAH rats. Mechanistically, Tet restored gut microbiota balance and suppressed activation of the CYP51 and TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathways, thereby reducing the levels of inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and LPS. These findings suggest that Tet may alleviate SAH-induced brain injury through the gut-brain axis.

Tet is a natural alkaloid extracted from Chinese medicinal plants and has demonstrated a range of pharmacological properties, including notable anti-cancer effects, particularly against gliomas and colorectal cancer32. Previous studies suggest that Tet exerts anti-inflammatory activity by reducing ROS production during cerebral I/R injury, as well as by inhibiting neutrophil and platelet aggregation, thereby providing neuroprotection in ischemic stroke models33. Early evidence has shown that Tet can reduce neurological deficits, infarct volume, and cerebral edema in ischemic mice34. In the context of SAH, current findings indicate that Tet is capable of restoring gut microbiota balance, alleviating neurological impairments, and potentially mitigating neuronal injury induced by SAH. The observed improvements in cognitive function may be attributed to Tet’s combined effects on suppressing systemic inflammation and modulating gut-brain axis communication.

Recent studies have demonstrated that fecal microbiota transplantation exerts protective effects in mouse models of Alzheimer’s disease35, and also provides neuroprotective benefits in patients with Parkinson’s disease36. However, investigations into the mechanisms underlying the effects of FMT in PD remain limited. Given the established role of gut dysbiosis in the pathogenesis of neurological disorders, the present study employed Tet as a microbiota-modulating agent in an SAH rat model to evaluate its therapeutic effects and underlying mechanisms. The results showed that Tet significantly improved cognitive performance, alleviated intestinal dysfunction and motor deficits, and reduced gut inflammation, epithelial damage, and pathological changes in SAH rats. Collectively, these findings indicate that Tet provide neuroprotection in SAH via modulation of gut microbiota, supporting its potential as an effective therapeutic strategy. Further studies in clinical populations are warranted to explore the relevance of these microbial associations in human SAH. Notably, Tet’s anti-inflammatory properties appear to extend beyond direct neuronal effects; by altering gut microbiota composition, Tet may also modulate peripheral immune responses, thereby reducing systemic inflammation and its impact on the brain.

The correlation between CYP51 and specific bacterial taxa (e.g., s_Burkholderiales_bacterium, s_Lactobacillus_faecis) suggests that microbial metabolites may influence sterol metabolism, which could in turn affect blood-brain barrier integrity and neuroinflammation. Further studies are needed to determine whether Tet regulates CYP51 through direct enzymatic inhibition or indirectly via microbiota shifts. In this study, CYP51 was positively correlated with bacteria such as s_Burkholderiales_bacterium, s_Bacteroides_eggerthii, s_Collinsella_provencensis, and s_Anaerofustis_sp, and negatively correlated with s_uncultured_Clostridiales, s_Lactobacillus_faecis, s_unidentified_rumen, and s_uncultured _Desulfovibrionales. Thus, our study demonstrated that Tet exerts a protective effect in the SAH rat model by regulating gut microbiota, indicating its potential as an effective treatment for SAH. Further investigation into these bacteria and their relevance in clinical SAH cases is warranted. Given the limited treatment options for SAH-induced cognitive impairment, Tet’s multi-target actions on microbiota, CYP51, and TLR4/NF-κB signaling support its promise as a candidate for clinical translation. However, caution is necessary when extrapolating findings from rodents to humans, due to interspecies differences in gut microbiota composition and drug metabolism.

Additionally, TLR4 plays a key role in initiating signaling pathways that lead to the activation of NF-κB and the release of pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-8, IL-1β, and IL-637. TLR4 can recruit adaptor molecules, including TRIF and MyD88, thereby activating downstream signaling cascades such as NF-κB38. It has also been reported that TLR4, through interaction with MyD88 and TRIF, directly activates the NF-κB pathway39. In our study, Tet intervention in the SAH rat model alleviated neuronal deficits, inhibited the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway, and reduced the levels of inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and LPS. The suppression of TLR4/NF-κB signaling by Tet may also involve interaction with other pathways, such as Nrf2/HO-1, which are known to counteract oxidative stress in SAH. Future studies are warranted to determine whether Tet’s anti-inflammatory effects are exclusively TLR4-dependent or involve broader regulatory networks.

In conclusion, SAH led to impaired learning, memory, and cognitive function in rats. Tet intervention suppressed CYP51 enzyme activity, inhibited TLR4/NF-κB signaling, and restored gut microbial balance, collectively resulting in improved cognitive outcomes. These findings support the potential application of Tet in the treatment of SAH-related cognitive impairment.
