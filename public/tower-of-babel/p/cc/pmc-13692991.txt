Expectorated bronchial casts revealing lymphatic plastic bronchitis due to thoracic duct agenesis: case report
Authors: Nela Stastna, Pavlina Lisa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlastic bronchitis is a rare disorder most often linked to aberrant lymphatic drainage or congenital and acquired cardiac anomalies. In patients with lymphatic flow abnormalities, leakage or anomalous lymphatic–bronchial communications can allow lymphatic fluid to enter the airways, where it solidifies into gelatinous or rubbery bronchial casts that may obstruct airflow and are typically expectorated.Case presentationA 49-year-old man presented with chronic, recurrent expectoration of pale bronchial casts accompanied by episodic dyspnea and intermittent fever. A chest CT demonstrated right-lower-lobe ground-glass opacities, interstitial changes with a lymphatic distribution pattern, and mediastinal lymphadenopathy. Bronchoscopy was unremarkable, and sputum microbiology was negative. Pulmonary function testing showed a mild obstruction. Cytologic evaluation of an expelled cast revealed mucus with fibrin, erythrocytes, macrophages, lymphocytes, and neutrophils. MR lymphangiography identified a congenital absence of the thoracic duct. Abdominal lymph drained through two markedly dilated lymphatic vessels converging in the retromediastinum and supplying predominantly right-sided pulmonary lymphatics, with a normal left-sided system. While bronchial casts are typically identified and removed bronchoscopically—often preserving the morphology of the bronchial tree—in this patient the casts were not amenable to bronchoscopic removal, and were instead repeatedly expectorated as cohesive clots. Background

Plastic bronchitis is a rare disorder characterized by the formation of bronchial casts within the tracheobronchial tree. Casts may be identified during bronchoscopy, in surgical specimens, or may be spontaneously expectorated, and they vary in size, shape, and anatomical distribution [1]. These casts can cause partial or complete airway obstruction, leading to symptoms such as chronic productive cough, dyspnea, fever, and chest pain; in severe cases, mechanical obstruction of major airways may result in acute respiratory failure [2]. The aetiology of plastic bronchitis is highly heterogeneous, and although the exact pathophysiology remains incompletely understood, two major types of bronchial casts have been described. Type I casts are associated with inflammatory airway diseases and are composed predominantly of inflammatory cells, including neutrophils and eosinophils, as well as Charcot–Leyden crystals. In contrast, type II casts are typically observed following surgical procedures and are largely acellular, consisting mainly of mucin. Radiological findings are often nonspecific, and atelectasis may be observed. Treatment is primarily directed at the underlying cause and includes interventional approaches such as lymphatic embolization, thoracic duct ligation, or endovascular stent grafting, particularly in patients with lymphatic abnormalities.

Case presentation

A 49-year-old Caucasian man was first evaluated at the age of 35 years (2011) for a 3-month history of chronic cough and night sweats. At that time, his hobby was parrot breeding and was an active smoker. He had no previous documented serious health history. His medical history was notable just for allergy to iodine-based contrast agents. Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed mediastinal lymphadenopathy and interstitial changes in the lung parenchyma (Fig. 1). He underwent bronchoscopy, with endobronchial ultrasound–guided sampling of the lymph node, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL; 95% macrophages, 4% lymphocytes, 1% neutrophils), and transbronchial lung biopsy. Sarcoidosis, lymphoma, systemic connective tissue disease, acute infection and tuberculosis were excluded. Based on parrot exposure, hypersensitivity pneumonitis was considered the most likely diagnosis. Pulmonary function tests showed preserved lung volumes (FEV₁ 93% predicted, FEV₁/FVC 81%, TLC 86%) with mildly reduced diffusing capacity (DLCO 71%). No treatment was initiated, and the patient was followed conservatively. Over subsequent years, lung function remained largely stable; he discontinued parrot breeding and quit smoking.

In 2015, he came in with chest pain and mild haemoptysis. A chest CT demonstrated stable interstitial changes with lymphatic distribution and persistent enlargement of mediastinal and bilateral hilar lymphatic vessels without progression. A follow-up CT in 2019 showed similar findings, with only minimal enlargement of lymph nodes (Figs. 2 and 3).

From 2022, the patient reported mild haemoptysis occurring approximately twice weekly. CT imaging in 2023 revealed increased ground-glass opacities predominantly in the right lung, while other findings remained unchanged (Figs. 4 and 5). The radiologic pattern was reconsidered as atypical for hypersensitivity pneumonitis, with differential diagnoses including sarcoidosis and lymphoid interstitial pneumonia. A CT scan after six months demonstrated partial resolution of the ground-glass opacities. Bronchoscopy showed normal endobronchial anatomy. EBUS-guided sampling of subcarinal lymph nodes revealed benign histology with lymphocyte predominance, and microbiological cultures were negative. Systemic corticosteroid therapy (prednisone 30 mg/day with gradual taper) was initiated, resulting in reduced frequency of haemoptysis, which stabilized on a maintenance dose of 5 mg/day until 2024.

At that time, the patient first reported expectoration of unusual pale long threads occurring approximately once weekly, accompanied by intermittent dyspnea and paroxysmal cough (Fig. 6). The expectorated material was submitted for histopathological analysis and measured 25 × 12 × 7 mm. Microscopy demonstrated mucus and fibrin containing erythrocytes, macrophages, lymphocytes, and rare neutrophils, without eosinophils or malignant cells.

These findings supported the diagnosis of plastic bronchitis. Subsequent bronchoscopy revealed hyperemia and oedema of the bronchial mucosa; no obstruction of the bronchus by a cast was detected. Because most of the plastic bronchitis is caused by lymphatic vessels pathology, dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI lymphangiography was performed, with gadolinium injected into bilateral inguinal lymph nodes under ultrasound guidance (Fig. 7). Imaging demonstrated absence of the thoracic duct, suggestive of a congenital abnormality. Lymph from the abdominal region drained via two enlarged retroperitoneal lymphatic vessels converging in the posterior mediastinum at approximately the Th8 level, forming a common trunk that deviated sharply toward the right lower lobe bronchus and subsequently filled lymphatic vessels of both lungs. Concurrently, mediastinal lymphatic drainage occurred via multiple branches into the region of the left brachiocephalic vein. Consultation with an interventional radiologist recommended lymphatic embolization of infradiaphragmatic lymphatic connections; however, due to the high risk of complications and relatively infrequent symptoms, a shared decision was made not to pursue invasive treatment. Thoracic surgical consultation did not offer alternative options. Corticosteroids were gradually discontinued.

Pulmonary function tests remained stable (2025: FEV₁ 100% predicted, DLCO 77%). The frequency of bronchial cast expectoration and mild haemoptysis persisted at approximately once weekly.

Discussion and conclusion

Plastic bronchitis is a rare disorder. Also referred to as cast, fibrinous, pseudomembranous, or Hoffmann bronchitis, it is characterized by a broad spectrum of aetiologies, variable clinical manifestations and often nonspecific or inconclusive radiological findings. Although the majority of cases are diagnosed in childhood, plastic bronchitis should also be considered in adults, particularly in individuals with a history of conditions that may predispose to cast formation. Aetiology is heterogenous and includes anatomical abnormalities (particularly involving the lymphatic system), postoperative states (e.g., following the Fontan procedure, coronary artery bypass grafting, or lung transplantation), infectious causes (predominantly viral), asthma, cystic fibrosis, cardiac disease, acute chest syndrome, idiopathic cases, and other less common conditions [2–4].

The patient in this case is unique by expectorating the casts, whereas most of the cases describe the casts removed by invasive methods. In the available literature, the casts are mostly distributed in the form of a bronchial tree, but in the case of expectorating, the patient coughs up a clump of pinkish-white casts, and at first glance, it is difficult to identify the bronchial casts. Lymphatic aetiology is mostly frequent. The white colour of the bronchial casts might be instructive, but correlation of the form and aetiology is not known. In our case, the cytology differentiation between mentioned type I and II cast is not clear because these casts are formed mostly by mucin and erythrocytes, macrophages, and lymphocytes. This is strengthen by the fact, that the pathophysiology is not fully clear and typology might be inconsistent. Several cases of lymphatic plastic bronchitis reveal similar histopathological analysis like neutrophil-predominant mucus plugs and lipid-laden macrophages without evidence of infective agents [5].

In patients with lymphatic flow abnormalities, leakage or anomalous lymphatic–bronchial communications can allow lymphatic fluid to enter the airways, where it solidifies into gelatinous or rubbery bronchial casts that may obstruct airflow and might be spontaneously expectorated [6–8]. Those that cannot be expectorated are most commonly identified during bronchoscopy in the available literature and removed using endobronchial techniques. Entering the lymphatic fluid into the airways might be partially visible on chest CT as thickening of interlobular septa in the lower lobes with lymphatic distribution in this case.

Because most reported cases are associated with congenital heart disease or lymphatic abnormalities, management primarily focuses on treatment of the underlying condition by surgical intervention. Other aetiologies have been described mainly in isolated case reports and their treatment management differs significantly. Therapeutic strategies depend on the underlying cause, and thus standardized treatment guidelines cannot be established due to the rarity of the disease and heterogeneity of reported approaches. Emerging bronchoscopic techniques and respiratory physiotherapy may facilitate cast removal and improve airway clearance [9, 10]. Some authors described such methods as fibrinolytics agents, mucolytics, corticosteroids or anticoagulants, but currently there is no specific pharmacological therapy proven to prevent cast formation or to reliably accelerate cast dissolution [11, 12]. In case of lymphatic plastic bronchitis, percutaneous lymphatic embolization (using an endovascular glue or coils) is a suitable method for complete resolution of lymphatic reflux or communication with of abnormal lymphatic channels with airways, but our patients refuse the extensive lymphatic intervention due to possible complications and little impact on his health and lifestyle, so his symptoms persist, and we cannot observe the impact of appropriate interventions [7]. Further systematic data collection is needed to improve understanding of the pathophysiology and optimal management of this rare condition.
