Thrombin is increased in diabetic retinal pathology in the STZ mice model, and its attenuation by a specific inhibitor, PARIN5, is associated with preserved function
Authors: Zehavit Goldberg, Valery Golderman, Ettel Bubis, Tal Shadi, Ofira Zloto, Efrat Shavit-Stein, Joab Chapman, Ifat Sher, Ygal Rotenstreich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetic retinopathy (DR) is a leading cause of vision loss in working-age adults. Retinal neurodegeneration precedes vascular pathology, highlighting the need to identify early molecular biomarkers as a target for intervention. The thrombin receptor, protease-activated receptor-1 (PAR1), a G-protein-coupled receptor involved in coagulation and neuroinflammatory signaling, is expressed in the retina and has been implicated in retinal barrier dysfunction and angiogenesis in late DR. We presently addressed its role in early diabetic neuroretinal pathology. We investigated the thrombin/PAR1 pathway one month after induction of diabetes with streptozotocin (STZ) in mice. Retinal thrombin activity and coagulation-related gene expression were quantified, PAR1 localization was examined by immunofluorescence and cytosolic/nuclear fractionated Western blotting, and retinal function was assessed by electroretinography (ERG). Additionally, we assessed the therapeutic potential of PARIN5, a selective thrombin–PAR1 modulator, across these outcome measures. Diabetic retinas showed increased thrombin activity (2.496 vs. 1.00 mU/ml, p = 0.01) and PAR1 mRNA expression (1.31 ± 0.11 vs. 1.00 ± 0.08, p = 0.028), along with decreased prothrombin and factor X mRNA (0.64 ± 0.07 vs. 1.00 ± 0.05, p = 0.0025; 0.75 ± 0.03 vs. 1.00 ± 0.09, p = 0.042; respectively). Immunofluorescence confirmed increased PAR1 staining, including nuclear localization, confirmed by fractionated Western blotting. PARIN5 treatment reduced thrombin activity (p = 0.0091), attenuated PAR1 staining, and preserved both dark-adapted (ratio post treatment/pre-diabetes induction: maximal a-wave 1.23 ± 0.13 vs. 0.39 ± 0.07 for carrier-treated mice, p = 0.0002; maximal b-wave 1.15 ± 0.14 vs. 0.49 ± 0.08, p = 0.0083) and light-adapted (maximal b-wave 1.21 ± 0.14 vs. 0.51 ± 0.05, p = 0.0024) ERG responses. Importantly, these molecular and functional changes occurred without morphological alterations in the retina. In conclusion, we identify thrombin/PAR1 increase and nuclear translocation as a novel early event in diabetic retinopathy and demonstrate that PARIN5 treatment preserves retinal function before structural pathology emerges. Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a common complication of diabetes mellitus and a leading cause of visual loss in working-age adults1. The global prevalence of diabetes is estimated to reach 700 million patients by 2045, and over 20% of them will suffer from DR2. Early stages of DR are typically asymptomatic, but once vision declines, retinal damage may be irreversible. Thus, identifying upstream molecular events that precede the structural hallmarks of DR is critical for developing early interventions. Recent quantitative optical coherence tomography (OCT) studies revealed rod photoreceptor abnormalities in diabetic patients without DR and in those with only mild non-proliferative DR (non-PDR), suggesting that rod neurodegeneration is among the earliest retinal events triggered by diabetes3. These observations suggest the presence of a central molecular pathway in the early process of neurodegeneration, preceding the onset of visible retinal pathology.

One such pathway may involve the protease-activated receptor 1 (PAR1), a seven-transmembrane G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) activated by serine proteases4,5. Thrombin is the most potent activator of PAR1, although other proteases, such as FXa, granzyme A, metalloprotease-1 (MMP-1), and activated protein C (aPC), also cleave the receptor6,7. Following cleavage, the tethered ligand activates PAR1-dependent intracellular signaling8. Beyond its classical role in coagulation, PAR1 is expressed in neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and other cell types, where it mediates both neurodegenerative and neuroprotective functions. The balance between these outcomes depends on the nature of the activating agonist and the subsequent downstream signaling cascades9–13. In diabetes, thrombin activity is intrinsically elevated in peripheral nerves, contributing to conduction block and structural damage at the node of Ranvier (NOR)14,15. Inhibition of thrombin prevents these deficits, indicating that the thrombin/PAR1 pathway represents a pivotal pathological process in diabetes-induced neurodegeneration.

The need for a specific thrombin-PAR1 modulator that lacks significant anticoagulant properties and does not block PAR1 directly, thereby enabling alternative protective activation, has prompted us to develop a class of inhibitory compounds targeting the thrombin binding site on PAR1. PARIN5 is one of these inhibitory compounds. It includes a five amino-acids backbone, blocked by a Tosyl group on the N-terminus, which protects against degradation by amino peptidases. Another similar compound, composed of six rather than five amino acids (SIXAC), was previously found to inhibit thrombin-induced PAR1 activation, as demonstrated in vitro by preventing thrombin-induced molecular and cellular downstream effects such as ERK phosphorylation. These small inhibitory peptides, designed to interfere with thrombin’s binding to and activating PAR1, protect against diabetes-induced peripheral nerve dysfunction in experimental models without exerting anticoagulant effects16,17. Such compounds illustrate that selective modulation of this pathway can redirect the course of disease progression.

Evidence also implicates thrombin/PAR1 in retinal pathology. Elevated vitreous thrombin levels have been reported in patients with proliferative DR (PDR)18, and thrombin exposure disrupts retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) barrier function, induces VEGF production, and promotes angiogenesis and inflammatory cytokine release in vitro and in vivo18–26. Moreover, PAR1 is abundantly expressed in the neuroretina, including rod photoreceptors, where thrombin activity increases upon depolarization27. Collectively, these findings suggest that the thrombin/PAR1 pathway may serve as an early mediator and a potential biomarker in diabetic retinal degeneration positioned upstream of morphological changes and that its modulation may offer a novel strategy to delay or prevent DR.

Here, we investigated the involvement of the thrombin/PAR1 pathway in early diabetic neuroretinal degeneration.

Results

Thrombin activity is increased in the retina of the STZ-diabetic mice

To examine whether thrombin activity is altered under diabetic conditions, we measured enzymatic thrombin activity in isolated retinas from STZ-induced diabetic mice (n = 19) and age-matched controls (n = 23). Thrombin activity was calculated relative to control mice and was significantly elevated in diabetic mice compared to controls (2.496 ± 0.447 vs. 1.00 ± 0.123, p = 0.01; Fig. 1A). Thrombin activity did not show a direct correlation with the severity of hyperglycemia or the degree of weight loss. Specifically, markedly elevated thrombin levels were observed across a broad range of blood glucose concentrations and body weights (Supplementary Table 1).

Data are presented relative to the mean of the non-diabetic control mice. Data are presented as individual values with mean ± SEM; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01.

Altered expression of coagulation-related transcripts in the retina of the STZ-diabetic mice

We next evaluated the mRNA expression of PAR1, PT, and FX in retinal tissue (control, n = 5; STZ-induced diabetic mice, n = 6). STZ-diabetic mice showed significantly increased PAR1 expression relative to controls (1.31 ± 0.11 vs. 1.00 ± 0.08, p = 0.028). In contrast, FX and PT mRNA expressions were significantly reduced in diabetic retinas compared to controls (0.64 ± 0.07 vs. 1.00 ± 0.05, p = 0.0025; 0.75 ± 0.03 vs. 1.00 ± 0.09, p = 0.042; respectively; Fig. 1B).

Together, these results indicate that the thrombin/PAR1 pathway is significantly altered at both enzymatic and transcriptional levels in the diabetic retina, identifying this pathway as a mediator and potential early biomarker of diabetes-associated neuroretinal degeneration. Given this early activation, we next evaluated whether targeted modulation of thrombin/PAR1 signaling using the specific inhibitor PARIN5 could attenuate thrombin activity under diabetic conditions.

PARIN5 treatment reduces thrombin activity in the STZ-diabetic mice retina

To determine whether thrombin activity can be modulated under diabetic conditions by inhibiting the thrombin/PAR1 pathway, STZ-diabetic mice were treated with PARIN5. Initially, the thrombin activity measures were compared between the groups treated with PARIN5 by eye drops vs. eye drops and intraperitoneal injection. No significant difference was observed between treatment groups (p = 0.946). Hence, the data from the two PARIN5 treatment groups were pooled and presented relative to vehicle-treated STZ group. The analysis indicated that thrombin enzymatic activity was significantly reduced in PARIN5-treated (n = 17) compared with vehicle-treated (n = 10) STZ mice (0.73 ± 0.06 vs. 1.00 ± 0.08, p = 0.0091; Fig. 2). This reduction suggests that PARIN5 effectively attenuates diabetes-associated thrombin activation in the retina.

Immunofluorescence reveals an increase and nuclear translocation of PAR1 in the STZ-diabetic mice retina

Immunofluorescence staining showed low basal PAR1 expression in control retinas (n = 11; Fig. 3A, B). STZ-diabetic retinas, vehicle-treated, displayed not only increased PAR1 staining overall, but also strong staining in the cell soma, which may suggest nuclear localization of the receptor within cell nuclei across multiple retinal layers (n = 12; Fig. 3C, D). This striking nuclear staining pattern suggests a translocation of PAR1 from its classical membrane-associated localization into the nucleus under diabetic conditions. PARIN5 treatment markedly reduced both the overall PAR1 signal and the apparent nuclear localization (n = 4; p = 0.0144; Fig. 3E, F). Blinded semi-quantitative scoring by four independent observers confirmed higher staining scores in diabetic mice compared with controls (p = 0.0026, Fig. 3D). No significant difference was observed between control and PARIN5-treated diabetic mice (p = 0.9442). These results indicate that diabetes induces an increase and nuclear translocation of PAR1 in the retina, which is attenuated by PARIN5 treatment.

To further examine the immunofluorescence observation that PAR1 appears to localize to the nucleus in diabetic retinas, we performed Western blot analysis of nuclear and whole cell ratio protein fractions from isolated retinas. PAR1 levels were measured in control (n = 3), vehicle-treated (n = 3), and PARIN5-treated STZ-diabetic mice (n = 6) retinas (Supplementary Fig. 1). We calculated, for each retina, the ratio of nuclear PAR1 relative to the corresponding whole-cell fraction (predominantly cytoplasmic). This analysis confirmed enrichment of PAR1 in the nuclear compartment of vehicle-treated STZ-diabetic retinas compared with controls, and this enrichment was attenuated by PARIN5 treatment (Fig. 3H). To ensure the nuclear fraction was not contaminated by cytoplasmic proteins, we verified that tubulin was absent from the nuclear extracts. Although these differences did not reach statistical significance due to the small sample size, the pattern strongly supports the immunofluorescence results, suggesting that diabetes promotes nuclear accumulation of PAR1 in the retina and that PARIN5 treatment attenuates this effect.

Topical PARIN5 treatment rescues retinal function in diabetic mice

Electroretinography (ERG) was used to assess retinal function in control mice (n = 20), vehicle-treated STZ-diabetic mice (n = 8), and STZ-diabetic mice treated with PARIN5 eye drops (n = 19). Both a- and b-wave responses were significantly reduced in vehicle-treated STZ-diabetic mice compared with controls, under dark- and light-adaptation conditions, indicating impaired photoreceptor and inner retinal function.

For the dark-adapted ERG, the ratio of maximal a-wave amplitudes measured before diabetic indication and at the end of treatment (T1/T0) were significantly reduced in vehicle treated STZ-diabetic mice compared with non-diabetic controls (0.39 ± 0.07 vs. 0.95 ± 0.13, p = 0.0096), while PARIN5-treated mice displayed improved responses compared to vehicle-treated STZ mice (T1/T0 ratio: 1.23 ± 0.13 vs. 0.39 ± 0.07, p = 0.0002; Fig. 4A). The ratio of maximal dark-adapted b-wave amplitudes measured before diabetic indication and at the end of treatment showed a similar reduction in STZ-diabetic mice compared with non-diabetic controls mice (0.49 ± 0.08 vs. 1.03 ± 0.14, p = 0.0282) and were significantly improved in PARIN5-treated mice (1.15 ± 0.14 vs. 0.49 ± 0.08, p = 0.0083; Fig. 4B).

Analysis of the light-adapted ERG further confirmed this protective effect. Diabetic vehicle-treated STZ mice showed reduced light-adapted b-wave amplitude ratios compared with controls (0.51 ± 0.05 vs. 1.26 ± 0.20, p = 0.0046), whereas PARIN5-treated mice exhibited preservation of retinal function (b-wave ratio of 1.21 ± 0.14), with a significant difference compared to vehicle-treated STZ-diabetic mice (p = 0.0024; Fig. 4D). The light-adapted a-wave ratio showed no significant change, with comparable ratios observed in STZ-diabetic mice compared to controls (1.08 ± 0.11 vs. 1.34 ± 0.12) (0.94 ± 0.09; Fig. 4C), indicating no effect of diabetes induction or PARIN5 treatment on the light-adapted a-wave. Representative dark-adapted ERG traces from control, vehicle-treated STZ-diabetic mice, and PARIN5-treated mice are shown in Fig. 4E. Light adapted ERG traces are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Together, these data show that topical PARIN5 eye drops protect against diabetes-induced functional decline in both scotopic and photopic ERG responses. These functional improvements are consistent with the molecular and histological findings, supporting the notion that the thrombin/PAR1 pathway is an important mediator in diabetic retinal pathology and that modulation of this pathway may preserve retinal function under diabetic conditions/in DR.

No significant histological or vascular pathology was found in the early diabetic retina

Histological examination of retinal sections confirmed preserved morphology across all groups, with no gross differences between control, vehicle-treated, and PARIN5-treated STZ-diabetic mice retinas (Fig. 5). This indicates that the thrombin/PAR1 activation, PAR1 nuclear translocation, and functional ERG deficits described above arise at an early stage, before the development of the morphological changes that characterize diabetic retinopathy.

Discussion

This study provides new insights into the expression pattern and functional role of the thrombin/PAR1 pathway in the diabetic mouse neuroretina. It identifies this pathway as a central early biomarker and a potential mediator of the pathological cascade leading to DR. Our data show that thrombin activity and PAR1 expression are upregulated in the diabetic retina at both enzymatic and transcriptional levels, even before the appearance of the morphological and vascular alterations that define DR.

Interestingly, we observed that the degree of retinal thrombin activation did not strictly correlate with the severity of systemic markers (weight and blood glucose levels). Specifically, some mice with extreme hyperglycemia exhibited lower thrombin activity compared to those with moderate glucose levels, suggesting that thrombin induction in the diabetic retina is driven by complex, multi-factorial signaling pathways rather than a simple direct consequence of glucose concentration. Importantly, modulation of the thrombin/PAR1 pathway with the specific inhibitor PARIN5 attenuates both molecular and functional deficits, suggesting the thrombin/PAR1 pathway as a diagnostic and therapeutic target pathway in early disease.

Consistent with our previous findings28, the widespread distribution of PAR1 suggests that it is a broadly expressed player in neuroretinal cells. Here, we expand on this by demonstrating that PAR1 is markedly elevated in both the inner and outer retinal layers of diabetic mice. One of the most striking and unexpected findings was the nuclear localization of PAR1 observed by immunofluorescence and supported by fractionated Western blot analysis. PAR1 is classically described as a membrane receptor mediating thrombin signaling through G protein–coupled mechanisms. Its appearance in the nucleus of retinal cells suggests a previously unrecognized role, potentially linking thrombin signaling to transcriptional or chromatin-associated processes. Importantly, immunofluorescence performed on retinas obtained after perfusion yielded comparable PAR1 distribution, indicating that the staining pattern is unlikely to arise from residual intravascular blood. However, mRNA expression analysis was performed on non-perfused neuroretinas. Given that endothelial cells comprise only ~ 0.6% of retinal cells29, that the choroid was removed prior to tissue lysis, and considering that mouse platelets lack PAR1 expression30, the contribution of blood-derived transcripts is assumed to be negligible relative to the total neuroretinal signal. The consistent trends across immunofluorescence, mRNA, and protein fractionation strongly support this observation. However, larger studies and higher-resolution validation, including co-localization with nuclear markers, will be required to define its mechanistic basis.

Antibody specificity was validated in our previous study28, in which Western blot analysis using the same PAR1 antibody (NBP-71770, Novus) detected a ~ 52 kDa band in retinal lysates from wild-type C57BL/6 J mice but not in PAR1-knockout (PAR1⁻/⁻) mice or in mouse platelets, which lack PAR1 expression. These controls confirmed the antibody’s selectivity and support the specificity of the current immunofluorescence and subcellular fractionation findings. Our results indicate increased thrombin enzymatic activity and elevated retinal PAR1 mRNA, consistent with early involvement of the thrombin/PAR1 axis in the diabetic retina. Although these transcript measurements, normalized to a single reference gene, cannot resolve cell-type-specific contributions, future studies will help define the cellular sources of PAR1 upregulation. Interestingly, PARIN5 treatment prevented the increase in thrombin enzymatic activity and reduced nuclear accumulation of PAR1 but did not normalize PAR1 or prothrombin mRNA expression (supplementary Fig. 3). This dissociation suggests that PARIN5 primarily modulates thrombin-driven PAR1 cleavage and signaling, rather than upstream transcriptional regulation. Possible explanations include feedback loops within the coagulation system or temporal differences between RNA and protein-level changes. Future studies should examine longitudinal changes to clarify these dynamics.

The functional consequences of early PAR1 activation were evident in our ERG results. Diabetic mice exhibited reduced dark- and light-adapted a- and b-wave amplitudes, consistent with early neuroretinal dysfunction. Remarkably, PARIN5 preserved retinal function in both conditions, confirming the protective effect of thrombin/PAR1 modulation. The molecular and functional data support retinal thrombin/PAR1 modulation by PARIN5; however, ocular pharmacokinetics and target engagement were not directly assessed. Our ERG findings support the reports of early rod dysfunction in diabetic patients3,31 and reinforce the idea that targeting early neuronal changes may delay or prevent progression to irreversible vision loss. In our previous study, we have indeed demonstrated that PAR1 is enriched in rods rather than cones in the retina28. This may be linked to the increased susceptibility of rods to early diabetes-associated changes.

Histological examination further underscored the novelty of our findings. Despite clear evidence of thrombin/PAR1 pathway activation and functional decline, retinal gross morphology remained preserved in diabetic mice. This is consistent with previous reports in STZ-induced diabetic mice: at ~ 5 weeks post-diabetes induction, mice show little to no apparent structural histological alterations in the retina despite functional deficits and inflammatory changes, indicating that morphological DR pathology has not yet developed at these early stages32,33. At early stages of diabetes, structural retinal changes are often subtle and not consistently detected across studies. While some reports describe mild thinning at early time points, more robust and consistent changes in retinal thickness and ONL are typically observed at later stages (e.g., ≥ 8–12 weeks)34,35. Moreover, early alterations are frequently minimal and may not be readily detected by standard histological assessment.

This indicates that the molecular, cellular, and functional alterations we observed represent early events in DR pathogenesis, occurring before the vascular and structural changes that classically define the disease. The recognition of such an early biomarker, the point where the pathological self-perpetuating cycle begins, may provide a unique therapeutic window for intervention.

It should be noted that our experiments were restricted to early stages of experimental diabetes, and the long-term trajectory of PAR1 nuclear localization and its long-term modulation by PARIN5 remain unknown.

Our data also highlights the therapeutic potential of PARIN5. Systemic administration previously showed protective effects in peripheral nerve models36,37. Topical delivery via eye drops may be a particularly promising route in DR, as it avoids potential systemic complications while achieving local modulation of the thrombin/PAR1 pathway. The preservation of retinal function with PARIN5 treatement supports its translational potential as a novel approach for early DR therapy. Beyond DR, the identification of thrombin/PAR1 nuclear translocation suggests broader implications. Thrombin has been implicated in a range of neurodegenerative and inflammatory diseases, including ALS, GBM, and diabetic neuropathy14,36,38. The parallels between thrombin-driven neuroinflammation in the brain and its activity in the retina raise the possibility that PARIN5 or related compounds could be beneficial in other retinal pathologies characterized by inflammation and neurodegeneration, such as retinitis pigmentosa39.

While this study provides important insights into early diabetic retinal changes, several limitations should be considered as directions for future research. The current work focuses on an early time point following STZ-induced diabetes, thereby establishing a foundation for future longitudinal studies to evaluate the temporal dynamics of the thrombin/PAR1 pathway activation over time. The sample size, consistent with exploratory preclinical studies, will be expanded in future work to enable more refined statistical assessment. In addition, mRNA analyses were performed on whole neuroretinal tissue, and future studies will be aimed at resolving cell-type–specific contributions. Importantly, further studies are needed to assess the pharmacokinetics of PARIN5 and its ocular bioavailability, including the retina and vitreous, in order to support advancement toward clinical translation. In addition, while our findings suggest a potential nuclear localization of PAR1, further studies are required to directly validate its nuclear translocation and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and functional significance of this process.

In conclusion, our findings uncover the thrombin/PAR1 pathway as an early biomarker and potential contributor in the diabetic retina, including excess thrombin activity and PAR1 expression. These early molecular and functional changes precede gross vascular and morphological alterations, positioning thrombin/PAR1 as an early biomarker in DR pathogenesis. By demonstrating that PARIN5 can attenuate these changes and preserve retinal function, we provide a strong rationale for targeting this pathway as an early intervention strategy to prevent the progression of diabetic retinopathy.

Materials and methods

Animals

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6JOlaHsd mice (purchased from Envigo Laboratories, Israel), were housed at the Sheba Medical Center animal facility. All animal procedures and experiments were approved by the Sheba Medical Center Institutional Animal Care Committee (approval 1210/19-SMC-ANIM) and conformed to recommendations of the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and according to the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines.

Diabetes induction

Diabetes was induced by a single (150 mg/kg) intraperitoneal (IP) injection of streptozotocin (STZ, S0130, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, 0.1 M sodium citrate buffer, pH 4.5). Control mice were injected with the same volume of 0.1 M sodium citrate buffer, pH 4.5. Glucose levels were measured with a commercial glucometer (Xpress-i, Nova Biomedical) on three occasions within one week of STZ injection, at the same time of day, with no prior fasting. Mice were considered hyperglycemic when glucose levels were above 200 mg/dL.

Treatment protocol

The PAR1 activation inhibitor drug PARIN5 was synthesized by the American Peptide Company (Sunnyvale, CA, USA). The PARIN5 molecule includes a five-amino acid backbone and was designed based on the thrombin recognition site sequence of the PAR1 N-terminal (37TLDPR41)37. The length of the backbone amino acid sequence was tested in a preliminary study and selected based on its high efficacy and safety profile36. The molecule is protected by a tosyl group at the N-terminus and conjugated to a chloromethyl ketone (CMK) at the C-terminus, which serves as a serine active-site blocker. The purity grade was over 96%, as verified by HPLC.

Diabetes was induced via a single STZ injection at eight weeks of age. Blood glucose levels were monitored up to three times during the following week. Mice were classified as diabetic once blood glucose levels exceeded 200 mg/dL. Treatment commenced immediately upon confirmation of diabetes, falling within a window of 2–7 days post-STZ injection. Diabetic mice were then treated daily with PARIN5 for five weeks. Except for the electrophysiological (ERG) experiments, which were performed using topical treatment alone, all other analyses, including thrombin activity, immunofluorescence, and gene expression, were conducted using mice from both the combined-treatment and topical-only treatment groups. Treatment was administered either by topical ocular delivery (eye drops, 10 µL/eye, 100 nM, n = 6) alone or by a combination of topical ocular delivery (10 µL/eye, 100 nM) and intraperitoneal injection (375 ng/kg, n = 11). Vehicle-treated diabetic mice received either topical ocular delivery or a combination of topical ocular delivery and intraperitoneal injection.

The combined treatment approach was initially conducted as a pilot study to assess potential systemic effects of the compound on diabetes-associated neuropathic damage. This cohort was designed to reduce the number of animals used due to ethical considerations.

When analyzed separately, both treatment regimens (topical alone and combined topical + IP) showed a statistically significant effect compared to untreated STZ mice. However, no statistically significant difference was observed between the two treatment groups. Therefore, for the primary analysis, the treatment groups were pooled into a single “PARIN5-treated” group (n = 17) to simplify data presentation and increase statistical power. In the following experiments, focusing on retinal function (ERG), mice were treated with PARIN5 via eye drops in order to specifically evaluate the efficacy of the topical route. All experiments were conducted across six independent batches following a consistent timeline. Mice were euthanized at 13 weeks of age (five weeks post diabetes induction), and the eyes were subsequently used for various analyses, including histology, thrombin activity assays, and nuclear protein extraction. In total, across all experimental batches, 27 vehicle-treated STZ mice, 17 PARIN5-treated mice, and 32 untreated non-diabetic control mice were included.

Immunofluorescence staining

Mice were sacrificed at age 13 weeks, and their eyes were fixed in 4% formaldehyde as previously described40,41. Retinal paraffin sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Following epitope retrieval using citrate buffer (pH 6.0, Zytomed Systems GMBH, Germany), retinal sections were blocked with 10% donkey serum in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100, followed by incubation with primary antibodies.

The primary antibody (Novus Biologicals, NBP1-71,770) was diluted 1:50 in PBS supplemented with 0.1% Triton X-100 and 1% donkey serum and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Next, sections were extensively washed in PBS and incubated with fluorescently labelled secondary antibodies (Anti-mouse CY3-conjugated donkey, Millipore, AP192C, diluted 1:400 in PBS) for 1 h in room temperature. Sections were mounted with an aqueous mounting medium containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI Fluoromount-G, Emsidiasum) and viewed with a confocal microscope (Confocal Microscope ZEISS, LSM 700).

The semi-quantitative scoring was performed using a predefined scoring scale, applied by four masked evaluators to ensure a consistent and reproducible visual assessment.

Control non-diabetic mice (n = 11), vehicle-treated STZ-diabetic mice (n = 12), and PARIN5-treated mice (n = 4) were included in the analysis. For each retina, three sections were collected on a single slide, and evaluation was performed on one representative section per retina. All images were assessed under standardized conditions using Fiji/ImageJ, with identical display settings and fixed magnification across all samples. Semi-quantitative scoring was performed independently by four evaluators, who were masked to group allocation, to ensure consistency and reduce observer bias.

Neuroretina isolation

Mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital, 100 µl (Pentobarbital 20%, CTS, Israel). Eyes were removed and placed in ice-cold PBS. Corneas were removed by punching a hole in the limbus using a 25-G needle, followed by an incision around the periphery of the cornea. The lens was removed, and the neuroretina was separated from the RPE and the sclera using two pairs of blunt-tip tweezers. The procedure was performed on ice under a stereo microscope (SMZ745T, Nikon, Japan).

Thrombin activity measurement

Thrombin activity was measured in an isolated neuroretina as previously described28. Each retina was placed into a single well in 96-well black microplates (Nunc, Roskilde, Denmark) containing a thrombin substrate buffer (in mM: 150 NaCl, 1 CaCl2, 50 Tris–HCl: pH 8.0), bovine serum albumin (BSA, 0.1%, 9048-46-8, Amresco, Ohio), bestatin (0.1 mg/ml, 70,520, Cayman-chemical company, Michigan) and prolylendopeptidase inhibitor (0.2 mM, 537,011, Calbiochem, San Diego). Thrombin enzymatic activity was measured using a fluorometric assay, based on the cleavage rate of the synthetic thrombin fluorogenic substrate Boc-Asp (OBzl)-Pro-Arg-AMC (14 µM, I-1560, Bachem, Switzerland) as previously described16. Briefly, the cleavage rate of the substrate was defined by the linear slope of the fluorescence intensity versus time42–44. The fluorescence signal was measured by a microplate reader (Infinite 2000; Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland) with excitation and emission filters of 360 ± 35 nm and 460 ± 35 nm, respectively. A calibration curve was used in each experiment with 0.00078–0.05 u/ml bovine thrombin (T-4648, Sigma-Aldrich). Mice from six cohorts were used for Thrombin activity assay. Control non-diabetic mice (n = 23), STZ-diabetic mice (n = 19). Ten STZ-diabetic mice were treated with vehicle. Five mice received vehicle treatment via eye drops, and five mice received the vehicle by combination of topical ocular delivery and intraperitoneal injection. Seventeen STZ-diabetic mice were treated with PARIN5. Six mice received PARIN5 treatment via eye drops, while 11 mice received PARIN5 by combination of topical ocular delivery and intraperitoneal injection.

Preparation of whole-cell and nuclear fractions of neuroretinal lysates

Nuclear and whole-cell fractions were obtained using a Nuclear Extract Kit following the manufacturer’s instructions (Active Motif, CA, USA, catalog numbers 40010 and 40,410). Briefly, after tissue homogenization, the cells were collected in ice-cold PBS containing phosphatase inhibitors. Then, the cells were resuspended in hypotonic buffer and detergent, and the nuclear fraction was separated. Next, nuclear proteins were solubilized in a detergent-free lysis buffer in the presence of protease inhibitors (P-2714, Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA).

Western blot analysis

Protein samples (20 µL) from isolated neuroretinas were separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies in TBST (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20), followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature. Signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and imaged with an iBright CL750 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

For fractionation validation, membranes were first probed for PAR1 and subsequently, following extensive washing, re-probed with tubulin or GAPDH. This sequential probing approach was used to verify cytoplasmic contamination and to ensure accurate detection while minimizing potential cross-reactivity between targets of similar molecular weight.

Electroretinogram (ERG) recording

Mice were dark-adapted, by housing in total darkness for 16 h before testing. Animals were anesthetized with IP injection of 75 mg/kg Ketamine and 10 mg/kg Xylazine, pupils were dilated with topical 1% tropicamide, and the corneas were kept moist with 2.5% hydroxypropyl methylcellulose. ERGs were recorded from both eyes simultaneously using silver wire loops on the corneas as previously described40. ERG recordings were performed using five increasing scotopic flash intensities (0.2, 0.24, 2.45, 7.80, and 23.5 cd·s/m2). Light-adapted recordings were performed at intensities of 0.25, 1.0, 2.45, 7.8, and 23.5 cd·s/m2. The maximal retinal response was used for analysis. At each light intensity, responses were averaged from multiple flashes, decreasing with increasing intensity (20, 10, 5, 3, and 3 flashes, respectively).

The interval increased with scotopic flash intensity (3, 10, 20, 30, and 30 s, respectively). Two second intervals were used in all five photopic intensities. Responses from both eyes of each mouse were recorded, averaged, and reported as a single data point. Mice from four cohorts were used for ERG measurement. Control non-diabetic mice (n = 20), STZ-diabetic mice (n = 9), and STZ-diabetic mice treated with PARIN5 (n = 19).

Results are reported as fold changes relative to control neuroretina of non-diabetic animals and reported as mean ± SEM. The primers used in the qRT-PCR analysis are listed in Table 1. Statistics

Data was analyzed using GraphPad Prism software [GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 (244), https://www.graphpad.com/]. Normality of each dataset was assessed. For normally distributed data, comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, while multiple group comparisons were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s or Tukey’s multiple-comparison post hoc tests, as appropriate. For experiments involving two independent variables, a two-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s post hoc test was used. For non-normally distributed data, comparisons were performed using Mann–Whitney or Kruskal–Wallis tests followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparison test.

For repeated-measures datasets with occasional missing values (e.g., longitudinal ERG amplitudes), a mixed-effects model (REML) was applied, followed by Sidak’s post hoc test when applicable. All results are expressed as mean ± SEM, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
