Effect of the extubation predictive score (ExPreS) on reintubation in the ICU: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Julia Almeida Oliveira, Fernanda Klein, Natália Godoy Guzatti, Ana Paula Fernandes Kojoroski, Herika de Vargas Ciello, David Junior da Silva, Jéssica Locatelli, Letícia Fiorelli da Silva, Marcella Parise, Arthur Vacchi Spörr, Wan Cley Rabuske, Gustavo Bruno Rático, Silvano Altair do Nascimento, Diego de Carvalho, João Rogério Nunes Filho, Antuani Rafael Baptistella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtubation failure occurs in 10–30% of critically ill patients who successfully complete a spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) and is associated with worse clinical outcomes. The Extubation Predictive Score (ExPreS) was developed to assist clinicians in identifying patients at risk for extubation failure; however, its effectiveness as a decision-support tool has not yet been evaluated in a randomized controlled trial. We aimed to determine whether the use of ExPreS reduces reintubation within 48 h in mechanically ventilated adults.MethodsWe conducted a single-center, parallel-group, prospective, randomized, controlled superiority trial in two ICUs of a Brazilian university hospital (November 2021–December 2024). Adults ventilated ≥ 24 h who passed an SBT were randomized (1:1) to extubation guided by a standard protocol or by the ExPreS algorithm (score range 0–100; eight clinical variables). The primary outcome was extubation failure, defined as reintubation within 48 h. Secondary outcomes included duration of mechanical ventilation, ICU length of stay, and ICU mortality.ResultsA total of 540 patients were randomized (267 control, 273 ExPreS). In the intention-to-treat analysis, extubation failure within 48 h occurred in 13.0% of patients in the control group and 8.0% in the ExPreS group (absolute risk reduction 5.0%; relative reduction 38%; p = 0.070). In the per-protocol analysis, extubation failure occurred in 12.9% of controls and 7.3% of ExPreS patients (absolute risk reduction 5.6%; relative reduction 43%; p = 0.042). In a post-hoc analysis excluding airway obstruction, failure was further reduced with ExPreS (3.4% vs. 9.1%; p = 0.011). No differences were observed in duration of mechanical ventilation, ICU length of stay, or ICU mortality.ConclusionIn this randomized controlled trial, use of the Extubation Predictive Score (ExPreS) was associated with numerically lower rates of reintubation within 48 h compared with standard care, although the primary intention-to-treat analysis did not reach statistical significance. Secondary and exploratory analyses showed a consistent direction of effect. These findings should be interpreted cautiously and warrant confirmation in larger multicenter studies.Trial registrationregistered in ClinicalTrials.gov on October 07, 2021(NCT05085457).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-026-05861-w.Keywords: Mechanical ventilation, Weaning, Extubation, Predictors of extubation outcome, Randomized controlled trial, Extubation predictive score (ExPreS)

Introduction

The discontinuation of invasive mechanical ventilation, a process called weaning, is a critical step for most critically ill patients during the ICU stay [1, 2]. Delayed weaning and prolonged duration of mechanical ventilation (MV) are associated with clinical complications and worse outcomes. Weaning failures, particularly those related to extubation failure, are associated with longer ICU and hospital stays and a higher risk of mortality [3]. It is likely that extubation failure has an independent negative impact on patient outcome and can also be considered a marker of more severe disease [4, 5]. Therefore, finding the optimal time to extubate these patients can significantly impact their recovery in the ICU.

Once the patient meets the predefined weaning criteria, a spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) is performed to assess readiness for liberation from mechanical ventilation. The patient’s tolerance to the SBT is a key determinant in the decision to proceed with extubation [1, 6]. Even with well-established protocols, approximately 10–30% of patients who pass an SBT experience extubation failure [5, 7]. Extubation failure is associated with higher ICU mortality, corresponding to a relative increase of approximately 25–50%, and an absolute risk difference of about 8–10% compared with successfully extubated patients [4, 5, 8–10].

Several predictors of weaning and extubation outcomes have been proposed over the past three decades. Most studies have focused either on identifying predictors of successful completion of a spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) or on identifying predictors of extubation success or failure among patients who have already passed an SBT [7, 11]. However, despite this extensive body of literature, uncertainty remains regarding how best to integrate predictors of extubation failure after a successful SBT into clinical decision-making.

The Extubation Predictive Score (ExPreS), published in 2021 [12], is a decision-support tool developed using a multivariate regression model to estimate the probability of extubation success in mechanically ventilated patients. The score incorporates eight clinical and physiological parameters reflecting different organ systems, generating a composite score ranging from 0 to 100. In its derivation and validation cohorts, higher ExPreS values were associated with a lower risk of extubation failure, with scores ≥ 59 corresponding to a high predicted probability of extubation success. ExPreS was designed to assist clinicians in identifying patients at increased risk of extubation failure after successful completion of a spontaneous breathing trial.

Based on this rationale, we aimed to determine, in a randomized controlled trial, whether the use of ExPreS as a decision-support tool reduces the reintubation rate in adult ICU patients undergoing weaning from mechanical ventilation.

Method

Trial design

The study was a single-centre, parallel, two-arm, prospective, randomized, controlled superiority clinical trial conducted in two mixed medical-surgical ICUs of a Brazilian university hospital from November 2021 to December 2024. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee (Comitê de Ética em Pesquisa Unoesc/Hust, nº 5.106.578 - CAAE: 39925920.0.0000.5367) and registered in ClinicalTrials.gov on October 07, 2021 (NCT05085457) before enrolment of the first patient. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their authorized representatives.

Patients

Adult (≥ 18 years), non–COVID-19 patients receiving invasive mechanical ventilation for at least 24 h who fulfilled predefined criteria for initiation of a spontaneous breathing trial (SBT). Readiness for SBT was assessed using standardized physiological and clinical criteria, including: (1) improvement in the condition that caused respiratory failure; (2) adequate oxygenation defined as PaO₂ >60 mmHg with FiO₂ ≤0.40 and PEEP ≤ 5 cmH₂O; (3) hemodynamic stability, defined as absence of decompensated coronary insufficiency or arrhythmias with hemodynamic repercussion and no or low-dose vasopressor support; (4) ability to initiate an inspiratory effort; (5) absence of significant acid–base imbalance; and (6) tolerance of pressure support ventilation with PEEP 3–5 cmH₂O and pressure support of 7 cmH₂O, achieving tidal volume approximately 6 mL/kg and respiratory rate < 35 breaths/min [1, 13]. Patients who underwent tracheostomy, were transferred, experienced unplanned extubation (self-extubation or accidental extubation), or were extubated outside the protocol before meeting criteria for SBT were not included in the study. Patients who were randomized but never extubated were not included in the primary analysis because the primary outcome—extubation failure—can only occur after extubation. As these patients could not be classified as having experienced extubation success or failure, they were not eligible for outcome assessment. No other post-randomization exclusions were applied in the primary analysis. (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Randomization

Randomization occurred when patients met the criteria to perform the SBT. Participants were assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the control group or the ExPreS group. The allocation sequence was computer-generated at random.org in blocks of 10 and concealed in sealed, opaque envelopes by a research assistant who was not part of the ICU care team and did not assess patient eligibility for the study (Supplementary Fig. 1).

To reduce the risk of bias, the investigators responsible for endpoint collection were not involved in clinical decisions, and the database was managed by third parties who were not directly involved in the study and had no vested interest in the outcome. Data analysis was performed according to the protocol established prior to the start of the study.

Procedure

In both groups, extubation decisions followed a standardized protocol based on current clinical practice guidelines, including assessment of readiness for SBT, execution of a 30-minute SBT, predefined criteria for SBT success, and evaluation of the risk of post-extubation upper airway obstruction using a cuff leak test. In the control group, RSBI was calculated as an adjunctive parameter after successful SBT, whereas in the intervention group, ExPreS was calculated as an adjunctive decision-support tool. Neither RSBI nor ExPreS replaced the standard extubation protocol.

After criteria for SBT were met, informed consent was obtained, randomization was performed, and patients underwent 30 min of SBT in the T-tube. Any sign of intolerance [13] (Supplementary Fig. 1) during the 30 min was considered SBT failure. The patient who failed SBT was reconnected to the ventilator with the previous respiratory parameters and maintained in MV for at least 24 h to evaluate the possibility of performing a new SBT.

In the control group, the Rapid Shallow Breathing Index (RSBI) was calculated as the respiratory rate in SBT/tidal volume (L), measured by a ventilometer connected to the endotracheal tube, for those patients who passed SBT. If the RSBI was ≤ 104 breaths/min and the cuff leak test (CLT) showed an expiratory tidal volume 10% lower than the inspiratory tidal volume, the patient was immediately extubated. Patients with RSBI > 104 were reconnected to the MV for at least 24 h.

In the ExPreS group, the Extubation Predictive Score (ExPreS) was calculated using the ExPreS application (Supplementary Fig. 2) after the patient successfully completed the SBT, as previously described [12]. ExPreS is a composite score derived from eight clinical and physiological parameters reflecting multiple organ systems, including rapid shallow breathing index, duration of mechanical ventilation, lung compliance, hematocrit, serum creatinine, level of consciousness, peripheral muscle strength, and the presence of neurological comorbidity. Each variable is assigned a predefined weight, generating a total score ranging from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating a higher predicted probability of extubation success. The complete scoring system and calculation method are detailed in Supplementary Appendix 2. Patients who scored ≥ 59 and passed the CLT as described above were immediately extubated. Patients who scored between 45 and 58 and had no risk factors for extubation failure (obesity, cardiopathy and COPD) and passed the CLT were also immediately extubated. Patients scoring between 45 and 58 with risk factors for extubation failure and those scoring ≤ 44 were re-connected to the ventilator for at least 24 h. The study team performed the RSBI and ExPreS calculations. Then, the primary clinician, who was blinded to the randomization, was informed if the patient met the criteria for extubation.

After successful completion of the spontaneous breathing trial, patients were briefly reconnected to mechanical ventilation solely for the purpose of performing the cuff leak test (CLT). This reconnection was short and standardized, and extubation was performed immediately thereafter if criteria were met. The CLT was performed under controlled ventilator settings, with the patient in volume-controlled ventilation and a stable tidal volume. The difference between inspiratory and expiratory tidal volumes was measured with the endotracheal tube cuff inflated and then deflated. The cuff leak was expressed as the percentage difference between inspiratory and expiratory tidal volumes. A leak ≥ 10% was considered indicative of a low risk of post-extubation upper airway obstruction, in accordance with Brazilian Mechanical Ventilation Guidelines [13]. The CLT was used as a screening tool to assess the risk of post-extubation stridor and did not replace clinical judgment. The same CLT protocol was applied consistently in both study groups.

Noninvasive ventilation (NIV) after extubation was categorized into three predefined and mutually exclusive clinical scenarios: Prophylactic NIV was defined as NIV initiated immediately after extubation in patients considered at high risk for extubation failure, in the absence of clinical, gas exchange, or respiratory deterioration, was used according to Brazilian guidelines [13], based on Statement of the Sixth International Consensus Conference on Intensive Care Medicine and was not considered extubation failure. Rescue NIV was defined as NIV initiated after extubation in response to objective evidence of post-extubation respiratory failure, including one or more of the following: respiratory acidosis, worsening hypoxemia, tachypnea, increased work of breathing, or signs of respiratory muscle fatigue. Use of rescue NIV was prespecified as extubation failure. And NIV dependence was defined as the inability to discontinue NIV within 48 h after extubation due to persistent respiratory failure. NIV dependence was also prespecified as extubation failure.

Transitions from prophylactic NIV to rescue NIV or NIV dependence required fulfillment of predefined objective criteria and were explicitly documented. NIV-related decisions were made by the treating clinicians using standardized protocols and were applied uniformly across both study arms.

Blinded

Due to the nature of the intervention, complete blinding of all study procedures was not feasible. However, several measures were implemented to minimize bias. RSBI and ExPreS assessments were performed by members of the research team who were not involved in patient care, extubation decisions, or outcome assessment. Treating clinicians had full access to the patient and to all standard clinical information, including criteria for readiness to perform a spontaneous breathing trial (SBT), patient tolerance and performance during the SBT, and results of the cuff leak test. After completion of the SBT and the research assessments, treating clinicians were informed only whether the predefined criterion for extubation had been met, without being informed which decision-support tool (RSBI or ExPreS) had been used. Therefore, treating clinicians were blinded to group allocation at the time of the extubation decision.

Clinicians responsible for assessing post-extubation outcomes and reintubation criteria were not part of the research team and were blinded to randomization group. Data analysts were blinded to group assignment during the primary analyses.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was extubation failure within 48 h. The reintubation criteria were the presence of any of the following: respiratory acidosis (pH < 7.35 with PaCO2 > 45 mm Hg), SpO2 less than 90% (in COPD less than 88%) or PaO2 less than 60 mm Hg at FiO2 greater than 0. 4 or oxygen flow ≥ 10 L/min, respiratory rate > 35/min, decreased level of consciousness (GCS decrease > 2 points), agitation, inability to remove secretions, or clinical signs suggestive of respiratory muscle fatigue or increased work of breathing (use of accessory respiratory muscles, paradoxical abdominal movement, or retraction of the intercostal spaces) [14]. The clinicians responsible for assessing reintubation criteria was not part of the study team and was blinded to the randomization group. Extubation failure was defined as reintubation, death, NIV dependence, or the need for post-extubation rescue NIV for respiratory failure within 48 h.

Secondary endpoints were MV duration, ICU length of stay and ICU mortality.

Data collection

Baseline data were collected at enrolment. After randomization, SBT data were collected. In case of extubation, SBT, RSBI, ExPreS and CLT results were collected, as well as post-extubation care and reintubation episodes. Patients were followed throughout their ICU stay and outcomes were recorded.

Statistical analysis

The expected reintubation rate in the control group is 15%, and based on the results of the validation cohort of the ExPreS study [12], we hypothesized a 50% reduction in the extubation failure rate in the ExPreS group (7.5%). With an alpha error of 5% and a power (1 - β) of 80%, this hypothesis required a sample of 269 patients in each group (538 total).

Categorical variables were presented as absolute numbers (n) and relative frequencies (%), and chi-squared test was used to compare these variables. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the distribution of continuous variables. Normally distributed variables were summarized as means (± standard deviation) and non-normally distributed variables were summarized as medians (with interquartile range). The independent samples Student’s t-test was used to compare differences between groups for normally distributed variables, and the independent samples Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare differences between groups for non-normally distributed variables.

For graphical presentation of the primary outcome at 48 h, absolute risk differences and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were displayed using a forest plot. This approach was used to visually summarize the primary intention-to-treat analysis, with per-protocol and post hoc exploratory analyses presented for comparison.

Time-to-event outcome (risk of extubation failure at 48 h) was analyzed with Kaplan-Meier curves and compared by log-rank test (Mantel-Haenszel). Following the hypothesis that ExPreS can predict the risk of extubation failure only due to upper airway non-related causes, a post hoc analysis was performed using Forest Plot and Kaplan-Meier curves and compared by log-rank test, excluding patients who failed due to upper airway obstruction/stridor, to evaluate the effect of ExPreS use in reducing reintubation non-related to upper airway obstruction/stridor. Causes of extubation failure were adjudicated using predefined clinical criteria, consistent with previously published definitions of upper airway obstruction. These criteria included clinically documented stridor, clinician assessment of upper airway obstruction, and the requirement for reintubation primarily due to airway compromise, as described in prior studies [15, 16]. When available, additional supporting information such as direct airway visualization or documentation of emergency airway interventions was used to reinforce classification. These analyses should be interpreted as exploratory. Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using a univariable Cox proportional regression model in a semi-parametric survival analysis to estimate the effect size of the ExPreS group. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using the Schoenfeld residual plots and found no evidence of violation. The Forest Plot was performed using fixed effect model, with inverse variance and presenting the results as risk ratio with 95% confidence intervals [17]. As a complementary analysis, a logistic regression model was used to estimate the odds of extubation failure within 48 h according to study group assignment. Patients with missing primary outcome data were excluded from the primary analysis.

The primary analysis was conducted according to the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle and included all randomized patients who were extubated and therefore became at risk for the primary outcome. Because extubation failure can only occur after extubation, patients who were randomized but never extubated could not be classified as having experienced extubation success or failure and were not included in the ITT analysis.

A per-protocol (PP) analysis was prespecified as a secondary, sensitivity analysis and included patients from the ITT population who were extubated according to the study protocol. This analysis was performed to explore the consistency of findings among protocol-adherent patients and is acknowledged to be subject to post-randomization selection bias.

For the primary outcome, absolute risk differences were calculated as the difference in event proportions between groups, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. Relative risk reduction was derived from the relative risk estimate (1 − relative risk).

Because the primary outcome was defined as reintubation within 48 h, all deaths occurring during the outcome window were preceded by reintubation and therefore fulfilled the definition of extubation failure. No patient died without prior reintubation, and no patient was transferred or discharged from the ICU within the 48-hour follow-up period. Consequently, no censoring or competing risk assumptions were required for the analysis of the primary outcome.

Weaning classification was defined as short, difficult and prolonged weaning, based on WIND study [18].

Significance was set at 0.05 and statistical analyses were performed using JASP 0.19.3 software (Amsterdam, The Netherlands). JASP was also used to generate graphs.

Results

Patients

Between November 1, 2021, and December 31, 2024, a total of 997 adult non–COVID-19 patients received invasive mechanical ventilation for more than 24 h, of whom 457 did not meet the inclusion criteria. The remaining 540 patients were randomized, with 267 assigned to the control group and 273 to the ExPreS group. Baseline characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1, including age, sex, APACHE II score, admission diagnosis, and weaning classification. After randomization, 37 patients were never extubated and therefore never became at risk for the primary outcome. Consequently, the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis included 503 extubated patients, comprising 254 patients in the control group and 249 in the ExPreS group (Fig. 1). Eighteen patients were excluded from the per-protocol analysis because of protocol deviations (auto-extubation or extubation outside protocol), resulting in 240 patients in the control group and 245 in the ExPreS group included in the per-protocol analysis.

Flowchart of the study

Fifty-three patients were reintubated within 48 h. The median time to reintubation was 17 (6–31) hours in the control group and 7 (2–18) hours in the ExPreS group (p = 0.101). The mean duration of mechanical ventilation after randomization was 1.19 (SD = 0.472) hours in control group and 1.28 (SD = 0.562) hours in ExPreS group (p = 0.069). The causes of extubation failure were upper airway obstruction/stridor (37.7%), acute respiratory failure (26.4%), decreased level of consciousness (17.0%), and hemodynamic instability/shock (13.2%), and cardiopulmonary arrest (5.7%), with no difference between groups (p = 0.216). The use of prophylactic NIV during the first 48 h after extubation did not differ between groups (21.7% in the control group vs. 23.7% in the ExPreS group; p = 0.597). (eTable 1 in Supplement 1).

Primary outcome

The primary effect estimates for extubation failure within 48 h are presented as absolute risk differences in a forest plot (Fig. 2A). In the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis, extubation failure occurred in 13.0% of patients in the control group (33/254) and in 8.0% of patients in the ExPreS group (20/249), resulting in a risk difference of 5.0% (95% CI, − 0.45 to − 10.41) and a relative risk reduction of 38% (p = 0.070) (Table 2). In a complementary logistic regression analysis, use of ExPreS was associated with lower odds of extubation failure within 48 h (odds ratio, 0.585; 95% CI, 0.326–1.050; p = 0.073), consistent with the effect estimates observed in the primary analysis and summarized in the forest plot (Fig. 2A).

In the per-protocol analysis, extubation failure occurred in 12.9% of patients in the control group (31 patients) and in 7.3% of patients in the ExPreS group (18 patients), corresponding to a risk difference of 5.6% (95% CI, − 0.17 to − 11.08) and a relative risk reduction of 43%, representing a statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.042) (Table 2).

Kaplan–Meier curves were used as a descriptive tool to illustrate the timing of reintubation events within the predefined 48-hour window. In the ITT analysis, the hazard ratio for reintubation was 0.612 (95% CI, 0.351–1.067; log-rank p = 0.080) (Fig. 2B). In the per-protocol analysis, ExPreS use was associated with a significantly lower risk of reintubation within 48 h (hazard ratio, 0.561; 95% CI, 0.314–1.003; log-rank p = 0.048) (Fig. 2C).

All extubation failure events observed in the study were due to reintubation. No patient received rescue noninvasive ventilation or remained dependent on noninvasive ventilation without subsequent reintubation, and all deaths occurring within the outcome window were preceded by reintubation. Therefore, although a composite outcome was prespecified, the observed primary outcome in this trial effectively reflected reintubation alone.

Secondary outcomes

The median duration of mechanical ventilation to extubation was 5 (3–8) days in both groups (p = 0.996). The median ICU length of stay was 8 (6–14) days in the control group and 9 (6–14) days in the ExPreS group (p = 0.804), and the ICU outcome did not differ between the control and ExPreS groups (0.243) (Table 2).

Exploratory outcomes

A sensitivity analysis using reintubation within 7 days as an alternative definition of extubation failure yielded results consistent with the primary analysis. Extubation failure occurred in 18.5% of patients in the control group (47/254) and in 12.5% of patients in the ExPreS group (31/249), resulting in a risk difference of 6.0% (95% CI: −1.8 to −10.0%; p = 0.061) (eTable 1 in Supplement 1).

Because ExPreS was designed to predict non-airway related failure and does not evaluate factors associated with airway related failure, a post-hoc analysis was performed to evaluate the effect of ExPreS on extubation outcome at 48 h, excluding the 20 patients who failed due to upper airway obstruction (10 in each group). In the control group, 9.1% of patients failed due to non-airway related causes compared to 3.4% in the ExPreS group (p = 0.011) showing that the reduction in extubation failure associated with ExPreS was more pronounced, as illustrated in the forest plot (Fig. 2A). Kaplan-Meier curves showed a hazard ratio of 0.362 for patients in the ExPreS group to be reintubated within 48 h (95% CI, 0.160–0.817) (Fig. 2D).

eTable 3 in Supplement 1 shows that the groups did not differ in alertness and agitation, based on the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS), heart rate, SpaO2, blood gas analysis (pH, PaCO2, PaO2, HCO3) and FiO2, but the respiratory rate at the end of the SBT was higher in the control group (median; 20; IQR; 7) compared to the ExPreS group (median; 18; IQR; 6; p = 0.007). According to Fig. 3, results from a post hoc analysis showed that the effect of ExPreS on extubation outcomes did not differ among subgroups of patients with specific diagnoses, weaning classifications, or post-extubation non-invasive ventilation (NIV) requirements. The median ExPreS was 70.0 (62–79) for patients in the ExPreS group who successfully completed extubation and 64.5 (54–77) for those who failed (p = 0.113) (eTable 4 in Supplement 1).

Extubation failure was associated with mortality, as the mortality rate was 3.8% in patients with successful extubation and 34.0% in those with extubation failure (p < 0.001) (eTable 5 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

In this randomized controlled trial, patients managed with the Extubation Predictive Score (ExPreS) had lower observed rates of reintubation within 48 h compared with those receiving standard care. However, the primary intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis did not reach statistical significance, although secondary analyses showed a consistent direction of effect. All extubation failure events observed in the study were due to reintubation, and no differences were observed in other secondary clinical outcomes.

Extubation failure has been consistently associated with worse outcomes, including prolonged mechanical ventilation, longer ICU stay, increased healthcare costs, and higher mortality [4, 8, 19]. Previous studies have shown that 10–30% of patients who tolerate a spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) subsequently require reintubation, highlighting the limitations of SBT alone in predicting successful extubation [5, 7].

In the present study, the reintubation rate in the control group (12.9%) was similar to that reported in the WEAN SAFE study, in which 13.7% of extubated patients were reintubated within seven days [20]. These findings suggest that the observed event rate in the control arm reflects contemporary ICU practice.

The mechanisms of extubation failure are heterogeneous and are commonly categorized as airway-related and non–airway-related causes, each accounting for approximately half of early reintubations [16]. ExPreS integrates parameters across multiple physiological domains, including respiratory, neurological, renal, and musculoskeletal systems, and may therefore capture vulnerabilities not fully identified by SBT alone. The lower observed reintubation rate in the ExPreS group may reflect improved identification of patients at higher risk of extubation failure due to non–airway-related mechanisms.

Although ExPreS includes parameters related to consciousness and neurological status, it does not directly assess the risk of post-extubation upper airway obstruction related to laryngeal edema. Consistent with prior observational data, airway-related mechanisms accounted for a substantial proportion of early extubation failures in our cohort [16]. Pre-extubation assessments such as cuff leak testing have only moderate sensitivity for predicting post-extubation airway compromise, which may explain the persistence of airway-related failures despite systematic evaluation [21].

A post hoc exploratory analysis excluding patients with airway-related extubation failure showed a greater relative reduction in reintubation with ExPreS compared with standard care. This finding should be interpreted cautiously but suggests that the potential benefit of ExPreS may be more pronounced for non–airway-related mechanisms of extubation failure.

Strengths of the study

It was conducted as a randomized controlled trial in a real-world ICU setting, integrating the intervention into routine clinical practice through a pragmatic, guideline-based extubation protocol. The primary outcome, reintubation within 48 h, was objective, clinically meaningful, and consistently adjudicated using predefined criteria. In addition, the direction of effect was consistent across multiple analytic approaches, supporting the internal coherence of the findings.

Limitations of the study

This study also has limitations. First, although conducted in two independent ICUs with different clinical teams, it remains a single-center trial, which may limit generalizability. Second, the sample size calculation assumed a 50% relative reduction in reintubation, whereas the observed reduction was smaller, which may have limited statistical power to detect modest but clinically relevant differences, as reflected in the borderline statistical significance of the ITT analysis. Third, the pragmatic nature of the intervention means that the observed effect may reflect not only the ExPreS score itself but also a more holistic clinical assessment prompted by its use. Fourth, protocol deviations occurred in both groups; however, patients extubated outside protocol were retained in the ITT analysis because the primary outcome could be ascertained. Fifth, although 540 patients were randomized, 37 were never extubated and therefore never became at risk for the primary outcome, which by definition can only occur after extubation. Consequently, the primary ITT analysis included all randomized patients who were extubated. This limitation is inherent to the outcome definition and may have reduced statistical power to detect modest differences. Finally, the number of patients was insufficient to draw firm conclusions regarding specific subgroups, such as cardiac or neurological patients, or to evaluate the impact of ExPreS on ICU and hospital mortality.

In this single-center randomized controlled trial, use of the Extubation Predictive Score (ExPreS) was associated with lower observed rates of reintubation within 48 h compared with standard care, although the primary intention-to-treat analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference. Secondary and exploratory analyses showed a consistent direction of effect. These findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating, underscoring the need for confirmation in larger, multicenter randomized trials.
