Sustainable electrochemical detection of benoxinate using MnO₂/MWCNT carbon paste electrode
Authors: Heba M. Hashem, Heba Elmansi, Rasha Abo Shabana, Heba Samir Elama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A green electrochemical sensor utilizing pomegranate-peel-derived δ-MnO₂ nanoparticles combined with multiwalled carbon nanotubes was created as a carbon paste electrode (δ-MnO₂/MWCNTs/CPE) for the precise detection of benoxinate (BNX) in ophthalmic formulations. Cyclic voltammetry demonstrated that the modified electrode displayed the greatest electroactive surface area among the evaluated compositions, resulting in a significant improvement in BNX oxidation. An improved electrode formulation including 1.5% (w/w) MWCNTs and 1.5% (w/w) birnessite (δ-MnO₂) attained peak current responsiveness and analytical sensitivity. Under optimal conditions, the anodic peak current exhibited remarkable linearity with BNX content (r² = 0.999), whereas kinetic analyses indicated a diffusion-controlled electro-oxidation process. The sensor demonstrated remarkable selectivity, with signal changes of less than ± 2% in the presence of typical tear electrolytes, thus proving minimal interference. The suggested approach has elevated sensitivity and precision, featuring a low detection limit of 2.1 × 10⁻⁸ M and a quantification limit of 6.3 × 10⁻⁸ M. Due to its eco-friendly synthesis, durability, and analytical efficacy, the δ-MnO₂/MWCNTs/CPE platform is very appropriate for the trace-level measurement of BNX in standard pharmaceutical analysis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-026-01840-y.

Introduction

In the field of pharmaceutical analysis, there is a demand for reliable, cost-effective, and efficient analytical methods for drug quality control. Current techniques often suffer from lengthy procedures, poor selectivity, or high equipment costs. Thus, developing simple, rapid, and accurate methods for active ingredient analysis remains a priority.

Benoxinate or oxybuprocaine hydrochloride (BNX), an ester-class local anesthetic, is commonly employed in eye, ear, nose, and throat medicine. Its chemical name is (4-amino-3-butoxybenzoic acid 2-(diethylamino) ethyl ester, monohydrochloride. It is especially useful for brief eye-related procedures such as minor surgeries, intraocular pressure measurement, contact lens adjustments, and topical pain management for ocular trauma [1]. It is clinically administered as a 0.4% ophthalmic solution for short-duration procedures [1]. With a high purity standard of 99.80% ± 0.6%, it is recognized as an official pharmacopoeial drug in the U.S., Europe, and Japan for topical conjunctival anesthesia [2, 3]. Different analytical methods have been reported for BNX determination, including spectrophotometric [4, 5], spectrofluorimetric [6, 7], chromatographic [8, 9], and electrochemical methods [10].

The sensitivity, selectivity, and affordability of electrochemical sensors have drawn a lot of interest in their development for pharmacological analysis. Manganese dioxide (MnO₂) is an attractive charge storage material for supercapacitor positive electrodes due to its high theoretical specific capacitance (1370 F g⁻¹) and near-ideal rectangular cyclic voltammetry (CV) profiles within a ~ 0.9 V potential window in mild aqueous electrolytes. These exceptional properties have driven significant research interest in MnO₂-based supercapacitors [11–13]. Novel electrode design strategies have emerged to optimize ion transport pathways in MnO₂-based energy storage systems [14]. With improvements made possible by nanomaterials such as multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) and manganese dioxide (MnO₂) nanoparticles, modified carbon paste electrodes (CPEs) have become increasingly promising platforms [15–18]. These substances enhance surface area, electron transfer kinetics, and electrocatalytic activity.

Carbon-based nanomaterials have attracted much attention due to their diverse structural features and distinctive physicochemical attributes [19–22]. Over decades of research, these materials have discovered varied applications across diverse fields. However, their most critical role in the context of this study lies in electrochemistry and (bio) sensor development [23–25]. Among these materials, graphene stands out as a preferred choice due to its exceptionally large surface area, which profoundly enhances its functional properties. For example, carbonaceous-modified surfaces such as single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs), multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) and graphene offer a perfect substrate for biomolecule immobilization and retention of biological behavior. The use of these nanomaterials drastically reduces electron transfer electrical resistance between the active sites of the biomolecules and the outer layer of the electrode, consequently increasing the performance of sensors [26, 27].

The imperative for environmentally sustainable methodologies has led to the emergence of green synthesis, a paradigm that develops chemical and physical techniques that are ecologically benign, cost-effective, and scalable without requiring extreme pressure, energy, temperature, or toxic reagents. A keystone of green synthesis is the plant-mediated synthesis, which stands out as particularly promising due to its rapid nanoparticle formation and enhanced stability [28, 29]. The development of bio-inspired nanoparticles has attracted significant attention, alongside advances in precise size control and morphology tuning.

This work presents the first electrochemical assessment of BNX using green-synthesized δ-MnO₂ nanoparticles derived from pomegranate peel waste combined with MWCNTs. The originality lies in the eco-friendly, cost-effective approach: highly capacitive δ-MnO₂ nanoparticles are produced from biomass waste, while the disposable carbon paste/syringe-based platform repurposes waste as the electrode. The synergistic interplay between catalytic MnO₂ and highly conductive MWCNTs enhances double-layer capacitance, reduces charge-transfer resistance (Rct), and accelerates electron-transfer kinetics, resulting in outstanding analytical performance. Furthermore, this sustainable and miniaturizable sensor platform offers broad applicability for detecting diverse electroactive substances in ocular formulations, including anesthetics, antibiotics, and anti-inflammatory drugs, making it a practical tool for routine pharmaceutical quality control and on-site analysis. Different approaches were utilized to rigorously evaluate the environmental impact of the proposed green synthesis approach. Standardized new tools such as Click Analytical Chemistry Index (CACI) [30] and Analytical Green Star Area (AGSA) [31] are employed to confirm that the proposed plant-mediated synthesis can be systematically validated for its reduced ecological footprint, minimal use of hazardous chemicals, and energy efficiency, reinforcing its alignment with the principles of green chemistry and sustainable nanotechnology. Hereby, A green δ-MnO₂/MWCNT-modified CPE was developed for sensitive and selective BNX detection, showing high electroactive surface area, low detection limit, minimal interference, and excellent linearity. Its eco-friendly synthesis, durability, and analytical performance make it suitable for trace-level pharmaceutical analysis and broader ocular drug applications.

Experimental

Materials and reagents

Benox (0.4% Eye Drops; EIPICO, Egypt) served as the source of BNX. A stock solution of BNX (0.1 mM) was created by diluting 378 µL of the eye drop formulation in high purity methanol (HPLC grade, Fisher Scientific, Toronto, Canada) in a volumetric flask (50 mL) with ultrasonic agitation. The produced solution was stored at 4 °C, shielded from light. Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany) provided powdered graphite with particle size < 20 μm and MWCNTs. Acros Organics company (New Jersey, USA) supplied potassium permanganate (KMnO₄), and liquid paraffin was acquired from Merck company (Darmstadt, Germany). A 0.04 M Britton–Robinson buffer was created by mixing acetic, phosphoric, and boric acids with 0.4 M NaOH to alter pH (2–8).

Instrumentation

Electrochemical studies were performed on a Metrohm 884 Professional VA electro-analyzer (Utrecht, Netherlands) with a three-electrode system that included CPE/MnO₂/MWCNTs, Ag/AgCl (3 M KCl), and a platinum wire as working, reference, and auxiliary electrodes, respectively. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) pictures and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) investigations were taken at 30 kV with a JEOL JSM-6510LV (Tokyo, Japan). The Attenuated Total Reflection-Fourier Transform Infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectra were acquired with a Bruker Alpha II ATR spectrometer (Germany). Additionally, a pH meter (Jenway 3510, UK) and an ultrasonic cleanser (Eumax SS 101 H 230, USA) were utilized. The patterns of X-ray diffraction (XRD) were obtained from the Egypt Nanotechnology Center using a Bruker D8 Discover diffractometer (35 kV, 30 mA) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å), step size 0.02°, scan speed 0.016° s⁻¹, across 2θ = 5–50° for 2 h. The impedance (EIS) study was performed in 1.0 mM BNX in BRB buffer (pH 6) at room temperature. The setup, software, and all experimental parameters are described in our previous work [32].

Preparation of working electrode

Carbon paste electrode (CPE)

To make a homogeneous paste, 0.5 g of powdered graphite and 0.2 g of liquid paraffin were combined in a mortar for 15 min. A piece of the paste was placed into an insulin injection with a copper filament connected to the device. The electrode tip was cleaned on porous cellulose sheet to achieve a gleaming surface. Following each determination, the electrode surface is meticulously replaced.

Modified electrodes

To improve CPE, MWCNTs were used, and δ-MnO₂ was manufactured as shown in Section S1 (Supplementary Material), and a composite of MWCNTs and δ-MnO₂. The MWCNTs/CPE and δ-MnO₂/CPE were individually synthesized by combining 3% (w/w) of each modifier with graphite powder in a mortar. Subsequent to the addition of 1 mL of diethyl ether for uniform dispersion and its subsequent evaporation, paraffin oil was integrated to create a smooth paste, adhering to the identical process as the bare CPE. A composite paste of 1.5% (w/w) MWCNTs and 1.5% (w/w) δ-MnO₂ was created for the MWCNTs/ δ-MnO₂/CPE, thereafter packed into the electrode cavity and polished to a smooth finish prior to utilization.

Optimization of electrode composition

The effect of each modifier was initially assessed separately using carbon paste electrodes with 1%, 3%, and 5% (w/w) of either δ-MnO₂ or MWCNTs. Composite electrodes containing both δ-MnO₂ and MWCNTs were then fabricated at varying ratios, maintaining a constant total modifier content of 3% (w/w). The examined ratios of MnO₂ to MWCNTs were 0:3, 0.5:2.5, 1:2, 1.5:1.5, 2:1, 2.5:0.5, and 3:0 ratios. All electrodes were manufactured in accordance with the entire procedure outlined in Sect. 2.3.2 (Modified Electrodes).

General procedure

Construction of calibration curve

Aliquots of BNX standard solutions were dispensed into a succession of 10 mL volumetric flasks to achieve final concentrations between 4.9 × 10⁻⁸ and 3.24 × 10⁻⁶ M. The quantities were calibrated to the mark utilizing Britton–Robinson (BRB) buffer at pH 6. After that, a small-electrolysis unit was used to transfer each solution. The solution was stirred for one minute at 1200 rpm and then let to rest for ten seconds before to measurement. Differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) was carried out over a potential window of + 0.6 to + 1.2 V, employing a scan speed of 50 mV·s⁻¹, a pulse width of 0.05 s, and a pulse height of 50 mV. To derive the regression equation, the concentration (µM) of BNX was plotted against the anodic peak current (Ip) to construct the calibration plot.

The 3% (w/w) concentration of each individual modifier yielded the optimal electrochemical response for BNX as shown in Figure S2a for δ-MnO2, and Figure S2b for MWCNTs. Among the composite electrodes, the 1.5% (w/w) δ-MnO₂ and 1.5% (w/w) MWCNTs electrode demonstrated the highest anodic peak current, excellent sensitivity, and minimal %RSD, identifying it as the optimum formulation for BNX detection. Electrochemical behavior of BNX

BNX electrochemical behavior was studied using CV in 1.0 mM K4Fe(CN)6 in 0.1 M KCl at a scan speed of 50 mV/s throughout a potential range from − 0.4 to + 0.8 V at several electrode surfaces, including bare CPE, MWCNTs/CPE, δ-MnO₂/CPE, and δ-MnO₂/MWCNTs/CPE. The observed voltammograms of BNX revealed a single, clearly identifiable anodic (oxidation) peak with no corresponding cathodic (reduction) peak on the opposite scan, confirming that the electrochemical oxidation of BNX is an irreversible mechanism. A comparison of the electrochemical responses of BNX at various electrode surfaces, including naked CPE, MWCNTs/CPE, δ-MnO₂/CPE, and δ-MnO₂/MWCNTs/CPE, is illustrated in Fig. 3a. The peak current intensities vary significantly. The unmodified CPE has the lowest redox peak currents, signifying restricted electron transfer capacity attributed to its relatively inert surface and poor conductivity. Following the modification with MWCNTs, there is a significant rise in both anodic and cathodic currents. This improvement is due to the superior electrical conductivity and extensive surface area of carbon nanotubes, which promote electron mobility and offer additional active sites for electrochemical processes. The δ-MnO₂/CPE electrode exhibits enhanced redox current responsiveness relative to the bare CPE, indicating the redox-active characteristics of δ-MnO₂ that promote charge transfer via surface redox transitions between Mn⁴⁺/Mn³⁺. The δ-MnO₂/MWCNTs/CPE composite electrode demonstrates the largest anodic and cathodic peak currents, indicating a significant synergistic interaction between δ-MnO₂ nanoparticles and MWCNTs. The nanotubes establish a conductive network that improves electron transport, whilst δ-MnO₂ offers redox activity and catalytic sites. The increase in peak current and the diminished peak-to-peak separation (ΔEp) at δ-MnO₂/MWCNTs/CPE signify enhanced reversibility and accelerated electron transfer kinetics, establishing this composite electrode as the most electroactive surface among all evaluated configurations.

The electrodes’ interfacial charge-transfer characteristics were assessed using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) as shown in Fig. 3c. In Nyquist plots, a semicircular zone at high frequencies correlates to charge transfer resistance (Rct), whereas a linear tail at low frequencies corresponds to diffusion-controlled processes. The equivalent circuit model (inset of Fig. 3c). Bare CPE has the greatest semicircle diameter, highest Rct (≈ 4.1 KΩ), and slowest electron-transfer kinetics. The semicircle diameter decreases significantly after MWCNT modification, indicating increased conductivity and electron transport due to the highly conductive carbon network (Rct ≈ 3.3 KΩ). The synergistic effect between MWCNTs’ large surface area and conductivity and MnO₂’s catalytic active sites enhances the hybrid electrode’s interfacial electron-transfer capabilities.

Influence of pH

The initial cyclic-voltammetry (CV) investigations of BNX (1.33 × 10⁻6 M) in a Britton–Robinson buffer (BRB) over the pH range of 2–8 (scan speed 50 mV s⁻¹) revealed a strong pH dependence of the oxidation process (refer to Fig. 4). Proton participation in the oxidation mechanism is indicated by the anodic peak potential (Eₚ) shifting to less positive values as pH increases, as illustrated in Fig. 4a. As evidenced by the variation of peak current (Iₚ) as a function of pH (Fig. 4b). The peak current is at its highest around pH 6–7, and the current is diminished at both lower and higher pH values. Based on these findings, pH 6 was determined to be the most suitable medium for the subsequent electrochemical investigation of BNX. Although the oxidation peak potential of BNX shows clear pH dependence (slope ≈ 25 mV pH⁻¹), the deviation from the theoretical 59 mV pH⁻¹ (expected for a 1 H⁺/1e⁻ process) indicates that the reaction does not involve equal proton–electron transfer. The relatively small slope suggests that the rate-determining step is primarily electron-transfer controlled, with more electrons than protons involved at this stage; while proton transfer occurs in a separate, faster step [35]. This lesser slope suggests that the proton transfer step is slower than electron removal or that the rate-determining phase entails a single-electron transfer with partial proton participation. While deprotonation at higher pH levels promotes the formation of the amino radical cation (Ar–NH₂•⁺), which oxidizes further to form the quinone-imine intermediate (Ar = NH) through a two-electron, two-proton overall process as shown in Figure S3, protonation of the amino group (–NH₂) on the aromatic ring at lower pH values prevents its oxidation [36–38]. Based on these findings, pH 6 was determined to be the most suitable medium for the subsequent electrochemical investigation of BNX.

This behavior has been reported for several electroactive compounds, such as quinone derivatives and aromatic amines, where the protonation equilibrium of functional groups influences the oxidation potential and results in slopes between 20 and 40 mV/ pH [39].

Validation of the method

The International Council of Harmonization (ICH) requirements were followed in the validation of the established approach [42]. To ascertain the linear range, quantification and detection limits, accuracy, and precision, three replicates of each concentration were measured.

Sensitivity and linearity

The suggested BNX electroanalytical method’s linear response was evaluated using differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) under optimal experimental circumstances. Figures 6a demonstrate that the oxidation peak current of BNX increased proportionally with its concentration within the range of 0.049–3.24 µM, indicating exceptional linearity as shown in Fig. 6b. Accuracy and precision

The accuracy of the hypothesized approach was assessed by analyzing triplicates at three different concentrations in the linear range and the data including % recoveries, relative standard deviations and percentage errors are generated and reported in Table 2. The method used was validated by comparison of the findings obtained with the proposed electrochemical approach with the results obtained by the known spectrofluorimetric method [7], utilizing the t-distribution test and Fisher’s F-test [43], revealing no significant difference in accuracy and precision between the two approaches. The described spectrofluorimetric technique relies on the derivatization of BNX into a fluorescent product using fluorescamine [7].

To examine precision, three distinct concentrations were measured in duplicate over three days to investigate inter-day precision. However, three concentrations were evaluated in triplicate on the same day to determine intra-day precision. The comparative standard deviation values were < 2%, indicating that the approach has adequate precision.

Reproducibility, reusability, and stability of the modified electrode

The stability and lifetime of the fabricated electrode (δ-MnO₂/MWCNTs/CPE) were systematically evaluated through repeatability, reproducibility, and long-term storage studies (Figure S4). The reproducibility test (Figure S4a), performed using five independently prepared electrodes, showed nearly overlapping voltammetric responses with negligible variation in peak current, indicating excellent fabrication consistency. The degree of repeatability was evaluated by 5 subsequent measurements with the same electrode (Figure S4b), where the peak currents remained almost unchanged, confirming stable surface performance without significant fouling. Long-term stability (Figure S4c) was examined by storing the electrode under ambient conditions and measuring its response over several days (exceeds 10 days); the peak current exhibited a minor decrease less than 5%, demonstrating good operational durability. The results verify high stability, reproducibility and long lifetime of the modified electrode in solution.

Comparison between the proposed method and the electrochemical reported methods for the determination of BNX

The proposed δ-MnO₂/MWCNT/CPE exhibits superior performance for BNX determination compared to previous methods as illustrated in Table S1. Unlike the PVC-based ion-selective electrodes of Elzanfaly et al. [44] which rely on nonspecific ion-pair recognition and suffer from limited sensitivity, narrow linear range, and poor stability, and the electrochemical sensor of Attia et al. [10], which lacks sufficient capacitive components and selectivity, the proposed electrode offers high sensitivity, low charge-transfer resistance, high capacitance, wide linear range, and a low detection limit. It also demonstrates excellent stability across different electrodes, multiple runs, and over 10 days, while being cost-effective and sustainably produced using pomegranate-peel-derived MnO₂, combining both environmental and analytical advantages over conventional approaches.

Practicality and greenness of the method

Click Analytical Chemistry Index (CACI) is an effective and easy to use device to evaluate the feasibility of analytical processes [30]. This index provides a standardized framework to evaluate and compare analytical methods, promoting the choice of cost-effective, practicable and environmentally friendly methodologies. Our voltametric approach provides a CACI score of 75, confirming its feasibility (Fig. 7). Additionally, adherence to the 12 Green Analytical Chemistry (GAC) principles (maximum score = 36) is illustrated through a star plot of Analytical Green Star Area (AGSA) [31], where higher scores indicate greener methodologies. The proposed method attains a total score of 80.56%, classifying it as “Excellent Green.” The use of modified carbon paste electrodes (CPEs) with multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) and MnO₂ nanoparticles reduces material expenses while enhancing performance. The electrode modification process is straightforward, eliminating the need for complex instrumentation. Additionally, the improved electron transfer kinetics and electrocatalytic activity of MnO₂-based systems enable rapid and reliable measurements.

Conclusion

MnO₂ nanoparticles are widely acknowledged for their superior catalytic and redox characteristics. This study utilized a green synthesis method, employing concentrated pomegranate peel extract as a natural reducing agent, thereby circumventing traditional chemical reagents. The resultant birnessite/δ-MnO₂ nanoparticles were effectively integrated with MWCNTs to construct a modified carbon paste electrode (CPE/ δ-MnO₂/MWCNT). Comparative analyses including bare CPE, CPE/ δ-MnO₂, and CPE/MWCNT revealed that this hybrid alteration yielded the most electroactive surface area (0.164 cm²), resulting in enhanced sensitivity and selectivity for the detection of BNX. The sensor exhibited negligible interference from prevalent tear electrolytes, such as Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻, and HCO₃⁻, with signal fluctuation confined to ± 2%. Mechanistic investigations established that the electro-oxidation of BNX at the electrode was controlled by diffusion, whereas the electrochemical response demonstrated a strong linear correlation throughout an extensive concentration range. Moreover, green analytical chemistry measures validated the method’s sustainability, yielding CACI and AGSA scores of 75 and 80.56, respectively. This study demonstrates the efficacy of integrating green-synthesized δ-MnO₂ nanoparticles with MWCNTs to provide a robust, highly sensitive, and selective platform for BNX detection, illustrating the promise of sustainable nanomaterials in pharmaceutical sensing applications.
