Clinical study on the 3D morphology and mechanical factors of lumbar facet joints in patients with degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis
Authors: Xiao Xiong, Yunsheng Wang, Da Shi, Tong Tong, Baoyue Zhang, Yilai Li, Linfeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsTo construct a mechanical model using a spatial Cartesian coordinate system and vectors, and to explore the role of facet joint morphology and mechanical parameters in degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis (DLS).MethodsA retrospective imaging study was conducted to collect the demographic data and imaging parameters of patients with and without DLS. The 3D structure and mechanical parameters of facet joint were constructed for comparative analysis. Receiver operating characteristic and regression analysis explore predictors for DLS. A total of 149 patients (67.8% female) were included in the study, with a median age of 63 years (IQR 58.5 to 68.0).ResultsCompared with non-DLS patients, there were significant differences in the facet joint angle (FJA), facet degeneration of L4-5, the sagittal joint angle (SJA), and intervertebral angles of L3-5 in DLS. Regardless of correction, the L4-5 posterior shear force of DLS patients is smaller than non-DLS. Posterior shear force has a high area under the curve and is significantly different from FJA, medial shear force, and vertical stress. High SJA and low posterior shear force are independent predictors for the progression of lumbar spondylolisthesis.ConclusionIn addition to the FJA, the larger SJA and lower posterior shear forces are also a good index to predict the progress of DLS. The posterior shear force is a more valuable indicator for predicting DLS progression, and may be a direct factor in the anterior displacement. When the posterior shear force is less than 0.537, the possibility of lumbar spondylolisthesis greatly increases. At this time, even in cases where patients do not experience spondylolisthesis, muscle exercise or intraoperative fixation are recommended to maintain lumbar stability.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(7):880–890.

Introduction

Recently, degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis (DLS) has emerged as a prevalent disease in the elderly population (> 60 years old), and the incidence of L4-5 level is the most significant.1 The symptoms of anterolisthesis are more common than retrolisthesis because it may lead to degeneration of lumbar intervertebral discs and facet joints, lumbar instability, traction of nerve roots, and other related injuries.2

The facet joints participate in regulating segmental movement and restricting movement, playing a key role in maintaining spinal stability and in the process of transmission and movement. Although facet joints can only perform small-amplitude movements, the superposition and coupled movements of multisegment facet joints amplify the amplitude of movements like flexion and extension.3 Furthermore, the facet joints bear approximately 15% to 20% of the total compressive loads together with the lumbar intervertebral discs.4

The morphology of the facet joint is closely related to its load transmission, showing significant anatomical changes from L1-2 to L5-S1 segments. They are arranged in a coronal position on the articular surface of the upper lumbar segments, allowing a wider range of flexion and extension movements. With the gradual transition downward to the sagittal plane, the articular surface is also gradually enlarged to bear more compression load and shear stress.5,6 Excessive sagittal orientation or tropism may increase the load on the facet joints due to stress imbalance, thereby causing degeneration of the facet joints and even posing a risk of spondylolisthesis.

Current studies are predominantly limited to 2D analyses in either the coronal or sagittal plane, without considering that the force of facet joints is comprehensively obtained from multiple angles. Moreover, large-sample 3D analyses remain scarce, and finite element models often rely on single specimens that lack population representativeness. Based on extensive clinical imaging data, this study found a novel 3D mechanical analysis model to estimate the magnitude of the shear force on the facet joint surface, and analyze the relationship between the facet joint stress and lumbar spondylolisthesis more deeply.

Methods

In this study, we sought out patients with lumbar degenerative spondylolisthesis and non-degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis (NDLS) in our hospital, and searched their demographic data such as age, BMI, and sex assigned at birth, as well as imaging data such as radiographs, CT, and MRI. Then we corrected the data and constructed the mechanical structure according to the space rectangular coordinate system, before performing statistical analysis. This study has been approved by our ethics committee.

A total of 357 patients diagnosed with L4-5 lumbar spinal stenosis or L4 spondylolisthesis in our hospital from January 2023 to July 2024 were included. Excluded patients with missing imaging data, combined fractures, scoliosis, tumours, and other spinal disorders, as well as those with multiple segmental lesions.

Radiological parameters

We collected standing lateral radiographs from patients with vertebral displacement greater than 3 mm, further classified into grade I and grade II according to MEYERDING grading.7 Patients without slippage served as the control group. Sagittal CT was selected to gather the sagittal joint angles (SJAs) of the upper and lower facet joints of the L4 vertebra, the inclination angles of the L4 and L5, the intervertebral disc heights, and the intervertebral angles of L3-4 and L4-5 of all patients. The facet joint angles (FJAs) of the upper and lower facet joints, the degree of facet joint degeneration of the L4 vertebra, and the rotation angles of the L4 and L5 vertebrae were collected in the CT axial position. Sagittal T2W1 phase MRI was selected to collect the degree of intervertebral disc degeneration of L3-4 and L4-5 according to the Pfirrmann grades.

Among them, the FJA is defined as the angle between the line connecting the anterior inner edge and the posterior outer edge of the facet joint, and the posterior margin of the vertebral body in the axial position. The SJA is identified as the angle composed of the anterior upper edge and posterior lower edge of the facet joint, and the posterior margin of the vertebra in the sagittal position (Figure 1). The position of the measurement image is determined by comparing the midpoint of the facet joint line between the axial and sagittal images. This method measures the spatial position of the facet relative to the vertebral body, facilitating the study of facet joint loading and vertebral morphology. Furthermore, FJA was adjusted using the vertebral rotation angle (between the spinous process–vertebral centre line and the sagittal reference line in the axial plane), and SJA was corrected using the vertebral inclination angle (between the posterior vertebral margin and a vertical reference line in the sagittal plane) for studying joint forces relative to the horizontal plane during upright walking.

Mechanical construction of facet joint surface

After obtaining the FJA and SJA, a 3D Cartesian coordinate system O-xyz is constructed. The axial image overlaps with the plane xOy, and the sagittal image overlaps with the plane yOz. Similarly, the posterior edge of the vertebral body in the axial and sagittal planes is parallel to the x-axis and z-axis, respectively. Within this framework, OA and OB represent the facet joint edges in the axial and sagittal planes. Their combination defines the 3D facet joint plane (AOB). The direction of the support force F on the facet joint is the direction of the normal vector perpendicular to plane AOB (Figure 1). For convenient calculation, OA→ and OB→ is set as the unit vector here.

Considering that the support force of the facet joint must be perpendicular to it, the direction of the normal vector in the 3D Cartesian coordinate system is the direction of the support force. Of course, although the facet joint surfaces have diverse shapes, the angular directions are all posterior, medial, and superior. Therefore, this study will assume that the directions are consistent and will not consider their directionality.

Calculation of normal vector: Taking the left articular surface as an example, obtain the expressions of vectors OA→ and OB→ in the spatial Cartesian coordinate system.

OA→=(cos⁡α, sin⁡α,0) ; OB→=(0,−sin⁡β, cos⁡β)

the normal vector Fn→=OA→×OB→=(sin⁡αcos⁡β, −cos⁡αcos⁡β, −cos⁡αsin⁡β)=(x,y,z)=

(−sin⁡α cos⁡β, cos⁡αcos⁡β, cos⁡αsin⁡β)

At this moment, the medial shear force that represents the decomposition of joint support force to the x-axis direction is Fnx=sin⁡αcos⁡β, the posterior shear force that decomposes to the y-axis direction is Fny=cos⁡αcos⁡β, and the vertical stress that decomposes to the superior z-axis direction is Fnz=cos⁡αsin⁡β. From this, the decomposition force situation of joint surfaces with different shapes can be obtained. The physical meaning is the movement tendency of the superior articular process given by the inferior articular surface. For example, the greater the posterior shear force, the greater the ability of the previous vertebral body to resist forward sliding.

The above is a mechanical construction of facet joint surface for the vertebral body level. This study also adjusted the tilt and rotation angles of different vertebral bodies to simulate the position of the facet joints relative to the horizonal plane during walking upright.

Descriptive statistics

A total of 357 patients were recruited from January 2023 to July 2024, excluding 23 patients with severe deformities and other lesions, 167 patients with severe multilevel lesions, and 18 patients without complete imaging data. Ultimately, 149 patients were included in the study, comprising 69 non-DLS (NDLS) patients and 80 DLS patients (Figure 2). There were 32.2% males in the total sample, with a median age of 63 years (58.5 to 68.0) and a mean BMI of 26.23 kg/m2 (SD 3.24). Reliability analysis found that all ICC values were greater than 0.7, indicating good interobserver and intraobserver consistency (Table I).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics (v. 26.0; IBM, USA). A two-way random effects model was used to calculate the interclass correlation coefficient (ICC) to evaluate intra- and inter-rater agreements in 30 patients randomly, with repeated measurements separated by > two weeks. The subjects were divided into the control group (NDLS) and the case group (DLS). Normality was tested for all continuous variables; independent-samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests were applied accordingly, while chi-squared test was used for categorical variables. To avoid excessive variables and collinearity, we averaged the parameters of the left and right facet joints and included them in the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis to determine the discriminative power of each indicator. Perform univariate regression analysis on valuable variables, adjust for confounding factors, and conduct stepwise regression to remove collinearity before incorporating them into multivariate ordered regression analysis. p < 0.05 represents statistical significance.

Discussion

Current studies indicated that axial facet joint parameters such as facet tropism (FT), facet orientation (FO), and FJA influence facet joint mechanics, vertebral morphology, paravertebral tissue, and treatment prognosis. Several researchers found that the coronal tropism shows higher contact force in extension, and that the sagittal facet joint will increase the corresponding intervertebral disc stress.8,9 The same applies to double-level DLS.10 High FO has been proposed as an imaging predictor of adverse clinical outcomes after interbody fusion of DLS.11,12 Shi et al13 noted that FT increased with the degree of facet degeneration, while other researchers associated larger FT with higher facet joint osteoarthritis, vertebral rotation grade, and concave muscle degeneration.14 A larger proximal FJA is associated with an increase in proximal adjacent segment degeneration, which can easily lead to a poor prognosis after fusion.15,16 However, some scholars have found no clear correlation between FT, FO, and the stability of spondylolisthesis.17 These inconsistent findings may stem from oversimplifying the facet joint as a 2D structure.

This study found that the inferior SJA in DLS patients was greater than NDLS patients. The same is true after correction. Leng et al18 found that an increasing sacral slope is associated with more horizontally oriented facet joints and a higher slip percentage. A more horizontal SJA may reduce the lumbar spine’s resistance to anterior stress, predisposing the joint to forward displacement. On the other hand, this trend of forward displacement may accelerate the degeneration of facet joints, thereby affecting spinal stability and increasing spondylolisthesis progression. In different segments, this study found that the L4-5 FJA was higher than L3-4 in DLS patients, in contrast to NDLS patients, and the same was true for SJA. A reasonable explanation is that the FJA in DLS tends to be sagittal, with the FJA of the DLS segment (L4-5) being higher than that of the NDLS segment (L3-4). In addition, the load borne by the L4-5 facet joints is greater than L3-4, as is the range of motion. Under the combined action of abnormal stresses such as greater medial shear force and higher forward displacement trend, the facet joints with larger angles undergo osteophyte hyperplasia and joint surface reshaping.10 This secondary remodelling further reduces the ability of the facet joints to resist slippage, as evidenced by the higher degree of degeneration of the lower facet joints in DLS patients.

Currently, there is scant research on the SJA. Some scholars have defined the angle formed by the facet joint edges and the transverse bisector of the corresponding intervertebral disc in the CT sagittal view as the SJA.19 However, intervertebral disc displacement may affect the measurement results. The most similar method to that used in this study is the pedicle facet angle (PFA), which measures the angle between the pedicle and inferior facet joint. However, the measurement error increases due to the variation of the pedicle, and this angle poorly reflects the facet-vertebral relationship. The SJA used in this study can better reflect the facet joint position relative to the vertebral body, which is similar to some researchers’ measurements on the cervical spine.20 He et al21 found that the FJA of DLS patients was smaller and the PFA was the largest, which was consistent with this study, however their study only examined separate correlations without integrating both angles.

The upper intervertebral angle and vertebral inclination angle of DLS were greater than NDLS, but there was no significant difference in the upper intervertebral disc height. Inose et al22 reported that the increase of intervertebral angle and translation was associated with poor prognosis. The increased upper intervertebral angle tilts the upper vertebra toward the inferior vertebral arch, indirectly raising the force transmitted from the pedicle to the lower facet joint, and then promoting the degeneration of the lower facet joint. This is similar to the biomechanical mechanism that the increase of lumbar lordosis leads to the augment of force on the posterior facet joint.23 The rotation of facet joint due to the increase of vertebral inclination angle leads to the relative increase of sagittal angle. As mentioned earlier, the trend of forward displacement further increases the possibility of DLS. Simultaneously, more curved lumbar and highly inclined vertebrae may cause the posterior column structure to bear higher shear load, and thus cause facet joint degeneration and spondylolisthesis.24

Many studies have found that DLS is associated with facet joints and intervertebral discs. Shi et al13 found that intervertebral height index (IHI) was an important influencing factor of facet joint degeneration. Facet joint degeneration will lead to capsule ligament relaxation, increase the range of spinal mobility, and then induce lumbar spondylolisthesis.25 Some scholars also believe that mechanical stress will cause more severe degeneration of nucleus pulposus and annulus fibrosus, resulting in discogenic pain.26,27 This study found a significant difference in joint stress between patients with and without DLS, while the intervertebral disc height and degree of degeneration were not statistically significant, which may indicate that the compression load on the intervertebral disc was not the main determinant of spondylolisthesis, corresponding with a 2020 biomechanical study.28 Of course, further and more rigorous prospective analysis is needed to verify this.

In this study, the force decomposition revealed that the medial shear force of the inferior articular surface was greater in DLS patients, while the posterior shear force was lower. After correction, only the difference in L4-5 posterior shear force remained significant, indicating its greater contribution to lumbar spondylolisthesis during daily activities. BMI, vertebral shape, and other factors will affect the force on the facet joint. For instance, each segment of the lumbar will bear approximately 5 N compression load per kg of weight increase.29 Therefore, this study balanced various confounding factors to reduce their impact. Cai et al30 reported increased axial facet forces at adjacent segments in degenerative cervical spine models, which was consistent with the longitudinal load enhancement of the left upper facet joint found in this study.

ROC analysis indicated that the posterior shear force can replace the FJA as a more effective index to predict lumbar spondylolisthesis. Based on the cutoff values, to improve specificity, we consider that when the FJA exceeds 56.125° and the SJA exceeds 17.475°, the prevalence of spondylolisthesis significantly increases. Additionally, the posterior shear force below 0.537 corresponds to the highest likelihood of DLS. This means that even if one angle is below its threshold, if the gap of another angle is too large to increase the posterior shear force, we also believe that the probability of DLS increases. Logistic regression also found that the L4-5 SJA and posterior shear forces were independent predictors of DLS progression, while the corrected indicators and vertical stress of L3-4, although significant before adjustment, were weakened by confounding factors. This shows that L4 spondylolisthesis is mainly caused by the facet joints of L4-5 segment, while L3-4 influences appear indirect.

Based on the above results, we believe that paying attention to the FJA, SJA, and the direct indicator of forward shear force of L4-5 in NDLS patients can help to determine whether there is a trend of forward slippage. When formulating surgical plans, fusion may be used for patients whose specific indicators exceed the range to prevent postoperative lumbar instability. However, the strategy of combining fusion and decompression is still up for debate. Several studies have suggested that decompression fusion does not show better results in terms of cost and clinical outcomes than decompression alone.31,32 However, for these patients, preoperative and postoperative nutritional support and functional exercise (such as progressive resistance training) may increase the stability of paraspinal muscles to the vertebral body and counteract forward shear forces.33

Based on imaging data, this study constructed the morphology of the 3D facet joint surface through mathematical analysis, realized the quantitative evaluation of the 3D mechanical characteristics of the lumbar facet joint at the clinical research level for the first time, and established the relationship between DLS and the shear force of facet joint. Unlike traditional 2D imaging measurement, this study analyzes the stress of the facet joint from the 3D perspective, and overcomes the mechanical evaluation deviation caused by a single plane measurement. The imaging parameters are converted into mechanical data by mathematical method which provides a new framework for the structural analysis of lumbar facet joints in clinical research. Compared with finite element analysis, there is no need to establish a complex material structure model; combined with the direct use of imaging data, this offers a novel pathway for clinical biomechanical investigation.

This study also has its limitations. Although the total sample size meets the statistical requirements, subgroup sizes remain slightly insufficient. Measurement errors are inevitable, but reliability analysis demonstrated good consistency. All the patients selected in this study were hospitalized patients who strictly followed exclusion criteria, introducing selection bias by excluding individuals who were healthy, or had comorbidities such as scoliosis. In the future, we will further explore the scoliosis population and study the differences between these individuals and the normal population. Vertebral rotation quantification is limited on radiograph, therefore vertebral tilt and rotation were measured on CT to improve correlation with FJA. However, the vertebral body position of patients in upright and supine positions will have certain differences, which will affect the accuracy of the experiment.34 Future work could develop an approach similar to the Perdriolle method to quantify the vertebral tilt and rotation degree of standing radiographs.35 The mechanical analysis level of this study is not as accurate as finite element and cadaveric testing. In the future, finite element analysis needs to be performed to improve biomechanical accuracy.

In conclusion, significant changes were observed in L4-5 FJA, SJA, facet joint degeneration degree, L3-4 SJA, and intervertebral angle in DLS patients. Relative to the vertebral body, DLS patients have higher medial stress, and lower posterior stress of L4-5 and vertical stress of L3-4. In terms of walking upright, DLS patients have less resistance to forward joint stress. The posterior shear force, as a direct factor in preventing slippage, has good discriminatory ability for lumbar spondylolisthesis. Larger SJA and smaller posterior shear forces are independent risk factors for the progression of lumbar spondylolisthesis. It is considered that when the posterior shear force is less than 0.537, the possibility of lumbar spondylolisthesis greatly increases. At this time, even if patients do not experience spondylolisthesis, muscle exercise or intraoperative fixation are recommended to maintain lumbar stability. Further prospective studies and finite element analysis are needed to establish these findings.
