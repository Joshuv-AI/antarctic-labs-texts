Advances in cataract surgery: is a new era on the horizon?
Authors: Xiaobo Zhang, Yibo Yu, Ke Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose of reviewTo summarize recent technological, procedural and material advances that are reshaping cataract surgery and to appraise their implications for visual outcomes, safety and global accessibility.Recent findingsPhacoemulsification remains the gold-standard technique, yet refinements in fluidics, ultrasound modulation and heads-up 3-D visualization continue to enhance efficiency and surgeon ergonomics. Femtosecond laser assistance yields measurable benefits in dense or unstable lenses but offers comparable long-term vision to modern phaco in routine cases. Parallel efforts to expand workforce capacity, reduce costs and minimize waste are essential to ensure these innovations benefit patients worldwide.

INTRODUCTION

Cataract surgery is one of the most commonly performed and cost-effective interventions in medicine [1,2]. Approximately 26 million cataract procedures are done worldwide each year, restoring sight and productivity [3,4]. Cataract remains the leading cause of reversible blindness globally, affecting an estimated 95 million people worldwide and responsible for roughly 17.0 million cases of blindness and 83.5 million cases of moderate-to-severe visual impairment [5▪,6,7]. Modern cataract surgery outcomes have improved substantially: recent series from diverse settings report that, with no significant ocular comorbidity, well over 95% of eyes achieve a postoperative uncorrected visual acuity of 20/40 or better [8,9▪▪]. As patient expectations rise - viewing cataract surgery not only as sight-restoring but also as a refractive procedure to reduce dependence on glasses – there is continual pressure to refine surgical methods and technologies. However, with ageing populations, the volume of cataract surgery continues to rise – particularly in low- and middle-income countries where unmet need remains high; therefore, while ongoing innovation in surgical techniques is essential, it is equally imperative to expand the accessibility of cataract surgery in resource-limited settings [10,11▪,12▪].

Over the past half-century, cataract surgery has undergone remarkable evolution. The evolution from large-incision intracapsular cataract extraction (ICCE) to small-incision extracapsular surgery and ultimately phacoemulsification has dramatically reduced surgical invasiveness and complications. Sir Harold Ridley's first intraocular lens implantation in 1949 pioneered the concept of restoring the eye's refractive power after cataract removal [13]. Charles Kelman's introduction of phacoemulsification in 1967 revolutionized cataract extraction by using an ultrasonic probe to emulsify the lens through a 3 mm incision [14]. Today's standard phacoemulsification techniques permit sutureless surgery with incisions as small as 2 mm, promoting faster recovery and minimal astigmatism. In parallel, ongoing improvements in surgical technology, from advanced fluidics and ultrasound modulation to femtosecond lasers and image guidance, aim to further enhance the safety, efficiency, and refractive accuracy of cataract surgery.

Equally important are advances in intraocular lens (IOL) technology. Earlier-generation monofocal IOLs restored vision but left patients dependent on glasses for near tasks. Recent years have seen the maturation of presbyopiacorrecting IOLs, including multifocal, trifocal, and EDOF designs, which can reduce or eliminate the need for spectacles at multiple distances [15,16]. New materials and surface modifications have improved IOL biocompatibility and reduced issues like posterior capsule opacification (PCO) and glistenings [17▪▪,18]. Innovative IOLs that can be adjusted postoperatively or actively change focus are now emerging from clinical trials [19▪].

This review discusses these major developments across surgical techniques, IOL innovations, intraoperative guidance, artificial intelligence applications, and surgical training, as well as their impact on visual quality and patient outcomes. By evaluating the prospect of an impending paradigm shift in cataract surgery, we integrate the latest evidence to show how emerging technologies and evolving surgical techniques are working in concert to make cataract surgery safer, more effective, and accessible to a broader patient population.

EVOLUTION OF SURGICAL TECHNIQUES

The surgical removal of the opaque natural lens has evolved tremendously, yielding progressively smaller incisions, faster recovery, and better visual outcomes. This section outlines the current state and recent advances of major cataract surgery techniques, from phacoemulsification to femtosecond laser-assisted and manual alternatives, as well as the advent of robotic assistance.

Phacoemulsification

Phacoemulsification (phaco) is the dominant cataract extraction technique worldwide, representing an evolutionary leap from earlier manual methods. Introduced by Kelman in 1967, phacoemulsification uses a vibrating ultrasonic tip to emulsify the lens nucleus, allowing removal through incisions as small as 2–3 mm [14]. By preserving the posterior capsule and dramatically reducing incision size, phacoemulsification accelerated visual recovery and cut complication rates compared to ECCE [20,21]. Early adoption was challenging due to primitive equipment and high complication rates, but subsequent innovations in phaco technology cemented its status as the gold standard [22,23]. Over decades, surgeons and manufacturers refined phaco fluidics, ultrasound modulation, and tip design to improve efficiency and safety [24,25]. For example, the advent of torsional and pulse phaco modes reduced ultrasound energy and corneal endothelial damage in dense cataracts [26]. Contemporary phacoemulsification platforms integrate active fluidics that dynamically stabilize intraocular pressure and chamber depth, thereby reducing postocclusion surge and enabling higher vacuum settings for faster lens extraction [27]. Such advances have shortened phaco time and further enhanced safety, even for dense nuclei. Techniques like divide-and-conquer and phaco chop have also evolved to manage hard nuclei more safely. Continuous curvilinear capsulorrhexis (CCC), described in the 1990s, improved capsular bag integrity and facilitated safe phaco and IOL implantation [28]. Today, phacoemulsification yields excellent outcomes in the vast majority of cases and is considered one of the safest and most routine surgeries performed [29]. It has also become more cost-effective over time, though expense and reliance on technology remain considerations in low resource regions.

Despite its success, phacoemulsification continues to be refined. Recent studies have focused on reducing total ultrasound energy to protect the cornea, using adjuncts like intraocular lens fragmentation devices, such as femtosecond lasers or the miLOOP, and improved ophthalmic viscosurgical devices (OVDs) [30,31▪].

Additionally, emphasis on surgical ergonomics and microscope visualization, such as heads-up 3D display systems, aim to further improve surgeon performance and outcomes [32,33]. Thus, while phacoemulsification is a mature technique, incremental innovations in equipment and technique persistently advance its capabilities.

Manual small incision cataract surgery

In parallel to high-tech phacoemulsification, manual small incision cataract surgery (MCIS) was developed to serve high-volume needs in resource-limited settings. MSICS removes the lens nucleus through a self–sealing scleral tunnel (~6 mm), eliminating the need for phacoemulsification equipment and thereby reducing reliance on expensive machinery. Refined primarily in the 1990s, MSICS can produce visual outcomes comparable to phacoemulsification in many cases [34,35]. A 2013 meta–analysis of six randomized controlled trials (1315 eyes) showed that phacoemulsification provides better uncorrected visual acuity and induces less surgically induced astigmatism than MSICS, while the two techniques are comparable in visual rehabilitation, endothelial–cell loss, and overall complication rates [36].

Although MSICS induces slightly greater surgically, induced astigmatism than phacoemulsification, its markedly lower cost and rapid operative workflow make it the procedure of choice for high-volume charitable hospitals; consequently, programs across India, Nepal and sub-Saharan Africa, exemplified by the Aravind Eye Hospital model where surgeons complete dozens of cases daily, achieve visual results equivalent to those of phacoemulsification [37–39]. MSICS remains indispensable for reducing cataract-related blindness in regions where phacoemulsification technology is not universally accessible.

Femtosecond laser-assisted cataract surgery

Femtosecond laser-assisted cataract surgery (FLACS) emerged in the past decade as a high-technology adjunct intended to improve the precision, safety and reproducibility of key steps of cataract surgery. FLACS emerged in the past decade as a high-technology adjunct intended to improve the precision and reproducibility of key steps of cataract surgery [40–42,43▪▪]. The laser can produce an accurately centered and perfectly circular capsulotomy with a diameter precision often unattainable manually, which may lead to more consistent effective lens position and refractive outcomes [44,45▪]. Several studies demonstrate that, in hard nuclear cataracts, FLACS markedly decreases effective phacoemulsification time and cumulative ultrasound energy, thereby reducing corneal endothelial cell loss and promoting faster visual recovery compared with conventional phacoemulsification [46–49]. Furthermore, FLACS can perform precise arcuate keratotomy for astigmatism with image-guided planning, potentially improving uncorrected outcomes in mild astigmatic cases [50▪▪,51].

Recent longitudinal evidence shows that, in routine cataract cases, FLACS delivers long-term visual results that are statistically indistinguishable from those achieved with contemporary phacoemulsification techniques [52,53]. Furthermore, FLACS increases costs and operative time, so its benefits are most fully realized in high-volume, well-resourced surgical centers [54]. Consequently, FLACS has not replaced phacoemulsification as initially expected, and its adoption remains modest. Although its cost-effectiveness is limited in routine surgery, FLACS may still offer distinctive advantages in eyes with very dense nuclei or weakened zonules, as laser-mediated lens fragmentation and a precisely centered capsulotomy can lessen zonular stress and enable safer extraction [44,46,55,56]. Additionally, ongoing improvements, such as smaller, mobile femtosecond systems, and better integration with surgical planning may yet enhance its utility. Overall, FLACS represents an impressive technological leap, and with continued refinements and rising patient expectations for premium postoperative visual quality, its advantages are poised to become even more pronounced.

Robotic and semi-automated systems

Robotic platforms have revolutionized many surgical fields, and ophthalmology is beginning to explore robotic and computer-assisted technologies for cataract surgery. Cataract surgery presents unique opportunities and challenges for robotics.

Prototypes such as the Intraocular Robotic Interventional Surgical System (IRISS), developed at UCLA, have demonstrated the feasibility of robot-assisted cataract procedures in laboratory and animal settings [57]. IRISS, a tele-operated microrobotic system, automates continuous capsulorhexis and cortical aspiration under surgeon supervision; when augmented with real-time optical coherence tomography, it generated uniform, centrated capsulotomies and complete cortical removal in porcine eyes [58,59]. In addition to fully robotic systems, certain laser platforms and hybrid devices offer semi-automation. The Keranova system, an image-guided, high-frequency femtosecond laser platform, achieves “photo-emulsification” of the crystalline lens, fragmenting even grade 4–5 nuclei into sub-200 μm cubes that can be aspirated without ultrasound; early clinical data show a marked reduction, and often complete elimination, of effective phaco time compared with conventional phacoemulsification, positioning the device as a computer-controlled alternative that could one day obviate phaco handpieces [60]. Such advances hint at the possibility of laser- or robot-assisted “no-phaco” cataract extraction. Potentially, robotic systems might enable surgeons to perform multiple cases simultaneously or perform cataract surgery remotely in the future.

As of 2025, although no fully robotic platform for cataract extraction is yet in clinical use, experience from vitreoretinal robotics indicates what is technically achievable: the CE-marked Preceyes Surgical System has already performed human retinal procedures, including sub-retinal drug delivery, with tool-tip accuracies on the order of ~10 μm, thereby illustrating the precision that future cataract robots may be able to replicate. Despite its promise, the introduction of robotics into cataract surgery confronts several substantial obstacles. Development and capital costs are high, and widespread adoption will require clear, demonstrable gains in safety or operative efficiency. Ongoing maintenance and technical support for delicate robotic platforms could be difficult, especially in resource-limited settings. Most critically, any robotic system must equal the versatility of a skilled surgeon, safely managing the full gamut of cataract scenarios, from soft lenses and brunescent, rock-hard nuclei to small pupils and cases complicated by zonular weakness or dialysis. In summary, while phacoemulsification by human hands remains the gold standard at present, robotic and computer-assisted cataract surgery represents a frontier with enormous potential.

ADVANCES IN INTRAOCULAR LENS TECHNOLOGIES

Intraocular lens technology has seen dramatic advancements, fundamentally expanding the goals of cataract surgery from just restoring transparency to actively enhancing visual performance. In the past five years especially, there has been a proliferation of new IOL designs and materials, many targeting presbyopia correction and other unmet needs. This section reviews major categories of advanced IOLs and highlights key innovations in each.

Multifocal and trifocal intraocular lenses

The past 15 years have seen great strides in intraocular lens technology. Standard monofocal IOLs provide clear vision at one distance. To reduce dependence on glasses, multifocal IOLs were developed, incorporating multiple optical zones or diffractive steps to focus light at distance and near. Early multifocal lenses improved near vision but at the expense of intermediate vision and contrast sensitivity. The introduction of trifocal IOLs, adding a third focal point for intermediate distance, has largely closed the gap, providing patients with a full range of functional vision [61]. Accumulating comparative evidence shows that implantation of trifocal intraocular lenses yields a statistically superior improvement in uncorrected intermediate visual acuity compared with bifocal designs [62,63▪]. As a result, spectacle independence for intermediate tasks, like computer use or dashboard reading, is substantially higher with trifocals. For instance, many patients with bifocals still needed glasses for computer work, whereas trifocal recipients often do not. Recent studies indicate that modern trifocal IOLs, such as AcrySof IQ PanOptix, Zeiss AT Lisa Tri and FineVision, achieve spectacle-independence rates of approximately 89–94.8% [64–66]. However, multifocal and trifocal IOLs are not appropriate for every patient, because their light-splitting optics inherently lower contrast and can provoke positive dysphotopsias, including halos, starbursts, and night-time glare around point light sources [67,68]. A recent meta-analysis of the PanOptix trifocal across 580 patients reported halos in 43.9% and glare in 33.6% of patients at 1–12 months postop, but only ~5% described these phenomena as severe [69▪]. Despite these drawbacks, multifocal and trifocal IOLs represent a major advance, enabling most patients to achieve functional, spectacle-independent vision across near, intermediate and distance tasks. New trifocal designs have mitigated some issues by improving light distribution and adding an intermediate focal point, resulting in better intermediate vision and slightly fewer complaints of glare compared to older bifocals [15,70▪]. Taken together, these lenses have ushered in the era of “refractive cataract surgery”, where restoring refractive function is a primary goal.

Extended depth-of-focus and light-adjustable intraocular lenses

A newer approach to presbyopia correction is the extended depth-of-focus (EDOF) intraocular lens. EDOF IOLs, such as the Tecnis Symfony, employ diffractive echelette optics, or comparable designs, to elongate the eye's focal corridor, thereby delivering a continuous range of clear vision instead of two or three discrete focal points [15,71]. The result is typically excellent distance and intermediate vision, with functional near vision at about 40–60 cm, albeit not as strong at very close distances as a trifocal lens [16,72]. A key advantage of EDOF IOLs is their lower rate of dysphotopsias because, by avoiding multiple discrete focal points, they produce fewer halos than conventional multifocal lenses [73,74]. Studies have shown EDOF IOL recipients have better contrast sensitivity and night vision performance compared to multifocal recipients [8]. On the other hand, since EDOF IOLs lengthen the eye's focal range rather than adding substantial near power, many recipients still need low-power reading glasses for very fine print, and their uncorrected near acuity remains lower than that achieved with trifocal implants [16,75▪].

Some newer EDOF designs attempt to bridge this gap by combining mechanisms; for instance, the Synergy lens (Johnson & Johnson) is a hybrid diffractive design that provides an extended range and a near focus [76]. One of the most remarkable recent innovations in IOL technology is the Light-Adjustable Lens (LAL). The LAL (RxSight Inc.) is a photosensitive silicone IOL that can be adjusted postoperatively using targeted UV light to alter the lens power. After cataract surgery with the LAL, the patient's refractive outcome is measured; then, one or more noninvasive light treatments are applied to “lock in” a desired refraction by polymerizing certain areas of the lens [19▪,77]. This technology directly addresses one of cataract surgery's remaining challenges: achieving zero residual refractive error. The LAL allows a bespoke finetuning of sphere and up to ~2 D of cylinder after surgery, and in the pivotal FDA phase 3 trial eyes implanted with this lens were roughly twice as likely to achieve 20/20 uncorrected distance visual acuity as those with a standard monofocal IOL [77]. Approximately 79% of LAL eyes attained 20/20 or better uncorrected distance vision, versus 33% of control eyes with a monofocal, given that the trial included patients with astigmatis [78]. Moreover, 96% of LAL eyes were within ±0.50 D of the target refraction after final adjustment, a level of accuracy far beyond typical cataract outcomes [79]. There are some practical considerations with the LAL. After implantation, patients must wear UV-blocking glasses for ~2–4 weeks until all adjustments and the final lock-in are completed [80]. This is to prevent ambient UV from unintentionally changing the lens. Additionally, the adjustment process requires extra office visits and meticulous refractions. Despite these, patient satisfaction is high because residual refractive errors can be eliminated.

In summary, EDOF IOLs and the Light-Adjustable Lens highlight two different but complementary advances. EDOF lenses enhance the range of vision with minimal side effects, appealing to patients who want freedom from glasses for most tasks but without night vision trade-offs. The LAL introduces a paradigm shift by allowing postoperative customization of refraction, effectively making cataract surgery outcomes less dependent on preoperative prediction accuracy. Surgeons now have a wider armamentarium, from trifocals to EDOFs to LALs, to help patients achieve their desired visual goals, whether it be spectacle independence, razor-sharp clarity, or both.

Accommodative and novel intraocular lens designs

Despite the success of multifocal and EDOF IOLs, the “holy grail” of cataract surgery remains an IOL that can truly accommodate, changing focus like the young crystalline lens. Over the past two decades, a wide array of accommodative intra-ocular lens (AIOL) designs has been explored in both laboratory research and human trials. The archetypal single-optic model attempts to harness ciliary-muscle contraction to shift the optic anteriorly within the capsular bag and thus provide additional near power [81]. However, imaging and biometric studies have shown that postoperative capsular fibrosis limits forward movement to roughly 0.1–0.3 mm, yielding less than 1D of true accommodative amplitude in most eyes.

Newer approaches to accommodative IOLs are considerably more ambitious. Recent accommodative intraocular lens research centers on fluid based or deformable optics that actively reshape to generate dioptric change; the modular two component Juvene lens from LensGen moves a fluid filled optic forward within its capsular base to add power [82], whereas the FluidVision accommodating lens developed by PowerVision now Alcon shifts silicone oil between haptic reservoirs and the central optic, steepening curvature and delivering about two diopters of objective accommodation in early clinical studies [83,84]. Another design, dual optic accommodating lenses such as Lumina and OmniVu employ ciliary driven anterior translation of the front optic, and multicenter studies in Europe report about two to two point five diopters of objective accommodation with these implants, confirming their potential to restore functional near vision beyond that achieved by earlier single optic designs [85,86▪▪]. These new accommodative IOLs are in early-stage clinical evaluation, but they demonstrate that meaningful accommodation might be achievable. If an IOL can reliably provide 3 or more diopters of true accommodation, it would allow patients to seamlessly focus from distance to near without splitting light or causing halos, essentially emulating a youthful crystalline lens. Although no fully accommodating IOL is commercially available yet, the progress in prototypes suggests that within the next decade, we may see an approved IOL that provides genuine dynamic focusing ability.

Intra-ocular lens materials and drug-eluting platforms

In tandem with optical design advances, IOL material science has improved substantially. Most modern IOLs are made from biocompatible acrylics with high refractive index, allowing thin profiles and small incisions.

A long-standing material issue, glistenings, has been largely solved by newer formulations. Studies show that next-generation hydrophobic acrylics like the Alcon Clareon and the Hoya Vivinex exhibit essentially zero glistenings even years after implantation [87,88▪,89]. These material improvements maintain optimal contrast sensitivity by eliminating light scatter from glistenings. IOL edge designs have also been optimized – most lenses now have a squared posterior edge to reduce posterior capsule opacification (PCO) by preventing lens epithelial cell migration [90]. This has successfully lowered PCO rates, though at the cost of occasionally causing negative dysphotopsia in a small subset of patients [91]. Some manufacturers have introduced slightly rounded or frosted edges to mitigate dysphotopsia while still inhibiting PCO [92]. Additionally, aspheric IOL optics are now standard in many implants, improving image sharpness and mesopic contrast sensitivity compared to older spherical lenses [93,94]. Blue-light filtering chromophores are another material feature incorporated into many IOLs to more closely mimic the natural lens’ light transmission and potentially protect the retina from high-energy violet light [95].

One of the most exciting material frontiers is drug-eluting IOLs. Researchers are experimenting with IOLs that slowly release medications intraocularly to improve postoperative outcomes. Recent study demonstrated an drug-eluting IOL impregnated with the NSAID significantly inhibited PCO in rabbit eyes and suppressed inflammatory markers, compared to control lenses [96,97]. Another study using an agarose hydrogel coating on IOLs to release doxorubicin showed significantly reduced lens cell migration and capsular opacification in an animal model [98]. So far, no drug-eluting IOL has reached the market, but early results are encouraging.

Another novel platform, the SpyGlass Pharma device, in trials is a combined glaucoma + cataract IOL: an IOL with extended-release bimatoprost embedded in its haptics. Early first-in-human data show that the SpyGlass drug-eluting intraocular lens, which releases bimatoprost from its haptics, achieved a mean intraocular- pressure reduction of roughly 43.7% at 18 months; moreover, 96% of treated eyes maintained pressures at or below 18 mmHg without topical medication, underscoring the promise of combining cataract extraction with durable glaucoma therapy [99]. Even beyond drugs, surface modifications like nanopatterning are being explored to make IOLs inherently antifibrotic and antimicrobial [100,101]. Taken together, the humble artificial lens is becoming a smart, active device. These enhancements, alongside the sophisticated optics described earlier, are transforming the cataract operation into an opportunity for ocular rehabilitation, correcting refractive error, treating comorbidities, and preventing complications in one session. It truly underscores how cataract surgery has advanced from a mere sight-restoring procedure to a comprehensive vision-enhancing and disease-managing intervention.

INTRAOPERATIVE IMAGING AND BIOMETRIC NAVIGATION

Modern cataract surgery has embraced intraoperative imaging and guidance systems to enhance precision. With the refractive outcomes of surgery becoming ever more important, surgeons now rely on digital tools to guide incisions, align toric IOLs, and confirm IOL power choices in real time. Additionally, microscope-integrated imaging like intraoperative OCT (optical coherence tomography) provides unprecedented views of ocular structures during surgery, assisting in complex cases.

One widely adopted technology is digital image-guided surgery systems. These systems, such as Zeiss CALLISTO and Alcon VERION, use preoperatively acquired corneal images and biometry to project guides onto the surgeon's microscope view [102,103]. These guides help the surgeon accurately center and orient corneal incisions and IOL alignment without manual ink marks. Several studies indicate that image-guided registration systems raise the proportion of eyes achieving residual astigmatism below 0.50 D by roughly 10–15% relative to ink marks [104,105]; however, other studies have found manual and digital marking to be equivalently effective when manual marks are done carefully [106,107]. In practice, many surgeons find the convenience and real-time feedback of digital alignment beneficial, particularly for high toric powers [108▪]. The technology continues to improve, with better tracking of ocular motion and integration with femtosecond laser platforms. Microscope-integrated intraoperative OCT (iOCT) is another innovation enhancing cataract surgery, especially in complex cases. Real-time OCT during surgery allows visualization of structures that are otherwise hidden; for instance, posterior capsule integrity in dense cataracts, presence of retained lens fragments, or the positioning of a toric IOL's haptics in the capsular bag [109,110,111▪].

While not yet ubiquitous in cataract ORs, iOCT is increasingly common in combined surgeries and in teaching hospitals. Studies have shown that iOCT feedback can improve performance of novices by confirming, for example, a well centered capsulorhexis or complete cortex removal [58,112]. In complex cases such as intumescent cataracts, iOCT helps ensure the capsulorhexis is complete and not running out, by providing a cross-sectional view of the capsule edge [112,113]. Ongoing gains in scanning speed and field width are expected to broaden its value in cataract surgery by guiding safe removal of residual lens fragments and ensuring precise in-bag positioning of toric intraocular lenses [114▪].

Finally, a notable shift in visualization is the adoption of heads-up 3D display systems. Digital heads-up three-dimensional visualization platforms such as NGENUITY and CALLISTO enable surgeons to operate while viewing a high resolution stereoscopic screen rather than microscope oculars [32,115]. This approach offers ergonomic benefits and facilitates teaching because the whole operating room can observe the identical surgical field [33]. Studies have found that heads-up surgery is as safe and effective as traditional microscopes for cataracts, with no difference in complication rates or visual outcomes [116]. Moreover, surgeon surveys and cross-sectional studies demonstrate that heads up three-dimensional visualization lowers cervical and lumbar musculoskeletal strain during cataract surgery [117▪,118▪], and early comparative data suggest that the high-magnification stereoscopic view can also improve the precision of continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis [119,120].

As more surgeons become comfortable with heads-up surgery, this trend may continue, potentially integrating with augmented reality overlays. In summary, advances in intraoperative imaging and guidance, including digital overlay alignment systems, microscope integrated optical coherence tomography, and heads up three dimensional visualization, enable cataract surgeons to operate with greater precision and to manage complex eyes more confidently, which in turn improves refractive predictability and overall surgical safety.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE IN CATARACT SURGERY

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning are increasingly impacting all areas of ophthalmology, and cataract surgery is no exception. AI has been applied along the entire cataract surgery pathway, from improving preoperative IOL power calculations to intraoperative decision support and error detection, to enhancing surgical training and postoperative care. In cataract surgery's new era, AI serves as a powerful tool to augment the surgeon's expertise, reduce errors, and personalize patient care.

Intraocular lens power prediction

One of the earliest and most impactful uses of AI in cataract surgery has been improving IOL power prediction. Traditional formulae (for example SRK/T, Hoffer Q) are based on analytic or regression models. In recent years, data-driven approaches have outperformed these, particularly in eyes with unusual anatomy [121]. Modern intraocular lens power formulas such as Barrett Universal II and Olsen apply data driven modelling and advanced optimization, delivering significantly higher predictive accuracy in eyes of typical axial length than earlier generation equations [122,123▪]. Furthermore, explicit machine-learning algorithms have been developed. Hill RBF was one of the first artificial-intelligence intraocular-lens power calculators: it predicts lens power through pattern recognition trained on large clinical datasets and relies on data interpolation rather than an explicit optical model [124▪]. This data-driven philosophy, now also embodied by newer machine-learning formulas such as Holladay AI, shows that some contemporary “formulas” are essentially trained models rather than traditional analytic equations [125▪].

Another is the Kane formula, which, while partly theoretical, also incorporates AI optimization and was developed on an extensive dataset of 30 000 eyes [125▪]. The Kane formula has consistently ranked among the most accurate across multiple studies, demonstrating the benefit of hybrid AI approaches [126]. Research confirms that these AI or “modern” formulas have reduced the refractive error after cataract surgery. A network meta-analysis in 2025 compared 21 formulas in short eyes and found that the top-ranked formula was an AI-based one (PEARL-DGS), outperforming all conventional vergence formulas [127▪▪]. The Pearl-DGS formula uses a machine learning model to predict an effective lens position and has shown excellent results in short eyes where older formulas tended to leave hyperopic surprises [128]. In that meta-analysis, Pearl-DGS had the highest percentage of eyes within ±0.50 D of target in short eyes, and overall AI formulas performed better in these difficult cases [127▪▪].

AI has also enabled the creation of novel formulas that combine strengths. The Ladas Superformula project, for example, initially aimed to blend existing formulas, but later iterations incorporated machine learning (ML) to refine predictions further [121,129]. The latest version, Ladas 2.0, uses support vector regression and other ML techniques to improve upon the baseline theoretical model [125▪].

The continued integration of AI into biometry, such as new optical biometers with built-in AI calculations, promises an era of “refractive cataract surgery” where outcomes are akin to those of laser refractive surgery. Ongoing research even explores adaptive AI formulas that learn from a surgeon's own outcomes to further personalize IOL constants [130]. In summary, AI has already firmly established itself in IOL power calculation, demonstrably improving refractive outcomes and reducing outliers. Future algorithms, trained on millions of cases and perhaps incorporating factors like effective lens position measured by intraoperative imaging, may virtually eliminate systematic refractive surprises.

Surgical error detection and planning

Beyond preoperative planning, AI is increasingly applied to cataract surgical performance itself. Using computer vision techniques, researchers have developed AI models that can analyze surgical video in real time, identifying which phase of surgery is occurring, tracking instrument movements, and even detecting deviations that might indicate a complication [131]. Deep-learning systems trained on large, annotated cataract-surgery video datasets now identify key operative phases, such as capsulorhexis, hydrodissection and phacoemulsification, with frame-level accuracies above 90% [132,133], and companion algorithms can locate surgical instruments in each frame and quantify their path length and motion smoothness; these capabilities enable objective skill assessment for trainees and provide the positional feedback needed for future robot-assisted cataract procedures [134,135]. There is also interest in using AI to predict, early during surgery, the likelihood of complications, for example by recognizing signs of zonular weakness or a small pupil that might foreshadow vitreous loss, thereby enabling proactive adjustments [136]. Early studies have trained neural networks on thousands of case videos to predict posterior capsule rupture; some success has been reported, although these models are not yet in clinical use [137,138▪▪]. Though these remain goals for the coming era, rapid progress is being made.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

The trajectory of cataract surgery advancements suggests several exciting future directions. On the surgical front, truly accommodating IOLs are arguably the most anticipated development. As detailed earlier, multiple accommodative IOL designs are in the pipeline and at least one may achieve regulatory approval within the next decade if trials are successful. A lens that effectively mimics the natural accommodation process would eliminate the need for multifocal optics and likely avoid dysphotopsias, providing a significant quality of vision improvement. Another major future direction is the increasing automation and digitalization of surgery. The concept of a femtosecond laser “factory line” for cataracts, where multiple laser stations prepare eyes for a surgeon to quickly remove lenses, has been floated to increase efficiency [54,139]. While FLACS did not fully realize this due to cost and space constraints, automation could still enhance efficiency in high-volume settings.

In terms of global impact, the future will hopefully bring cataract surgical services to all who need them. Millions still suffer blindness because they lack access to surgery. Innovations in portable low-cost surgical equipment such as inexpensive phaco machines and high quality MSICS instruments, together with expanded training of cataract surgeons and telemedicine support, are expected to keep improving global cataract surgical coverage [140,141]. Another focus is sustainability. Cataract surgery as performed in high income countries can be wasteful because each procedure often generates a large amount of medical waste [142]. There is growing recognition of this environmental impact and calls to adopt “greener” practices, such as reusing instruments and drapes when possible, reducing single-use plastic, and exploring steam sterilization of certain consumables. Some recent initiatives, like a fully recyclable cataract surgery pack and efforts to eliminate unnecessary packaging, are steps in the right direction [143,144]. The ophthalmic community is increasingly aware of this issue, and we expect guidelines and innovations aimed at sustainable cataract surgery to become more prominent. Finally, the future of cataract surgery concerns patients as much as technology. Patient expectations will keep rising, so we must pair improved tools with personalized care by choosing IOLs that match each lifestyle, using intraoperative technology to fine tune outcomes, and providing thorough counseling. As AI and big data advance we may see individualized IOL recommendations that predict which lens will best suit each patient's anatomy and preferences. Extended follow up and enhancement pathways such as femtosecond laser adjustments or novel IOLs will likely become routine in refractive cataract workflows to ensure patients achieve their desired vision.

In summary, cataract surgery is poised to become safer, more personalized, and more comprehensive. The procedure will not only clear a cloudy lens but also restore vision by correcting refractive errors, treating concurrent ocular disease through drug eluting lenses or combined minimally invasive glaucoma surgery, and optimizing postoperative visual quality. Advances in accommodating and adjustable IOLs, intraoperative AI guidance, and improved training methods will drive this progress. Reducing global disparities in surgical access and enhancing environmental sustainability will be equally essential for delivering the full benefits of modern cataract care.

CONCLUSION

Cataract surgery has advanced from a high-risk procedure of last resort into a safe, routine surgery with extraordinary vision outcomes. Innovations across multiple domains – surgical technique, IOL technology, intraoperative imaging, digital integration, and training – have converged to make modern cataract surgery one of the most refined operations in medicine. Patients today can expect not only restoration of sight but often spectacle independence and excellent visual quality, a concept unthinkable just a few decades ago. The developments reviewed here, such as femtosecond laser assistance, EDOF and multifocal IOLs, light-adjustable lenses, AI-powered planning, and VR training, illustrate the dynamic progress in the field. While some, like FLACS, have not yet realized a transformative impact, others (for example advanced IOLs and improved formulas) have already shifted practice paradigms. As we look to the future, further refinements, such as accommodating IOLs achieving true dynamic focus, greater automation via robotics and AI, and pharmacological cataract treatments, are on the horizon and hold the promise of an even more remarkable era. In conclusion, cataract surgery is indeed entering a new era – one marked by enhanced vision restoration capabilities and broader reach. By continuing to integrate technological innovation with surgical expertise and global health strategies, ophthalmology is poised to effectively eliminate cataract blindness and significantly elevate the visual function and quality of life of our patients. The future of cataract surgery is bright, and as ongoing research and development address current limitations, it stands to become not only the most common surgery, but also one of the most perfected.
