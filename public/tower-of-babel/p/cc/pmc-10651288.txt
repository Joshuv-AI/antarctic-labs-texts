A commentary on ‘Detection of cancer cells and tumor margins during colorectal cancer surgery by intraoperative flow cytometry’
Authors: Yuan Chen, Chengcheng Wang, Yupei Zhao
Published: 2023

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest and pleasure the article entitled ‘Detection of cancer cells and tumor margins during colorectal cancer surgery by intraoperative flow cytometry’ published in the International Journal of Surgery 1, which demonstrated the effectiveness of flow cytometry in determining the surgical resection margin status of colorectal cancer. However, despite the exciting result, we still have some concerns about this study from the following three perspectives.

First, this study demonstrated that intraoperative flow cytometry was able to identify the presence of cancer cells at the surgical resection margin by labeling the number of aneuploid cells and cell cycle status. However, it is worth noting that the cell types in the resection margin tissues are diverse, including cancer cells, normal epithelial cells, immune cells, and so on. Therefore, flow cytometry analysis of single-cell suspensions of resection margin tissue yields results that are not limited to cancer cells and normal epithelial cells but also introduce the influence of a list of intermingled cells. Meanwhile, since cancer development is often accompanied by inflammation2, the regeneration process of normal epithelial cells and the activation of immune cells will show a state of overall vigorous proliferation to varying degrees. All of these factors tend to make it difficult to identify cancer cells by intraoperative flow cytometry, which does not seem to be well addressed in the manuscript.

Second, the DNA-Index in the study was defined as a proportion of the modal DNA values of the tumor G0 and G1 cells (peak channel) to the DNA content of the diploid standard, which was unacceptable in the calculation validity to some extent. Since the authors emphasized the presence of aneuploidy as a feature of cancer samples, why was the presence of aneuploidy not detected in almost half of the cancer samples? This point needs to be further clarified. Additionally, this study indicated that the detection limit of the assay for pathological cells was 0.4–0.8%. However, due to the low fraction of cancer cells in the resection margin itself, the limit of detection is likely to be reached in practice, and how to optimize this result is the key to the large-scale application of this technique.

Third, this study focused on the identification of cancer cells only from their proliferation-related properties, which was quite limited. Cancer cells are highly heterogeneous, and not all cancer cells exhibit extremely high proliferation-related properties. And studies have even reported the presence of dormant cancer cells3, which are temporarily quiescent but exhibit a high potential for recurrence and metastasis. Moreover, even in terms of proliferative properties, cancer cells are somewhat heterogeneous. Their proliferation status is not only dependent on their own gene expression but also influenced by other external factors4, such as chemotherapy, inflammation, and underlying disease status. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop more comprehensive cancer-related features that can be identified by flow cytometry so as to precisely identify cancer cells and determine the resection margin status.

Overall, we highly appreciate the authors for their invaluable work in advancing flow cytometry to accurately determine the surgical margins of colorectal cancer. However, how to develop a precise cancer cell recognition framework based on flow cytometry needs to be further explored, and its efficacy needs to be evaluated in larger cohorts, especially in comparison with traditional pathological criteria.
