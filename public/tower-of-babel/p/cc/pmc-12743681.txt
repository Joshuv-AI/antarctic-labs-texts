Optimal capacity planning for long-term care facilities considering patients’ gender, language, and age group
Authors: Ghazal Khalili, Mohsen Zargoush, Kai Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Long-term care facility networks in Canada face significant challenges in balancing demand and capacity, a problem exacerbated by rising demand. In other words, the growing elderly population is escalating the need for long-term care resources. To address this issue, this study proposes a Mixed-Integer Linear Programming model based on the current standing of the long-term care system in Ontario, a representative case for considering varied patient supports. The proposed model simultaneously optimizes the timing and location of constructing new long-term care facilities while dynamically adjusting each facility’s capacity, including human resources and beds. Moreover, patient assignments are optimized based on their demand region, gender, language, and age group over a finite time horizon. The model incorporates multiple constraints to accommodate patients’ gender and language, addressing language barriers, alleviating feelings of loneliness, and aligning with Canada’s commitment to inclusive care. Additionally, it considers patient journeys by incorporating age groups and assigning patients from different demand regions in an equitable manner through the geographical equity constraint. To validate our proposed model, we conduct a case study on the existing network in Hamilton, Ontario. An extensive set of numerical analyses is executed to provide insights into the problem. Most importantly, the results demonstrate that the model effectively optimizes facility placement and patient allocation while significantly reducing un-assignment and misassignment rates. Specifically, the results indicate that over 88% of patient demand can be accommodated annually throughout a five-year planning horizon. In addition, patients can be assigned based on language and gender with marginal additional costs. Lastly, operational costs constitute the largest share of total expenditures, whereas misassignment costs account for the smallest proportion.

Introduction

Long-term care (LTC) refers to various medical and non-medical services designed to support individuals, particularly older adults with disabilities or chronic diseases. LTC homes, also known as nursing homes or LTC facilities, provide residents with housing, nursing care, and assistance with daily activities, catering to those individuals who need 24-hour care and cannot live independently. Because of the increase in the elderly population, the spread of chronic illnesses, and injury incidence, the need for LTC services is increasing sharply across the world [1].

LTC facility networks in Canada and many other countries face shortage issues, as the existing LTC facilities do not have enough capacity to meet the growing demand. Between 2017 and 2035, Canada is projected to require an additional 199,000 LTC beds to satisfy the rising demand [2]. Notably, in Canada, around 15% to 20% of the excess demand stems from Alternate Level of Care (ALC) patients, who do not need to remain in acute care services but await discharge to more appropriate settings, such as LTC homes [3]. The capacity shortage in LTC homes is the principal reason for the backlog of ALC patients in hospitals [4]. Therefore, efficient capacity planning within LTC networks not only can alleviate the capacity issue in LTC homes but also has a considerable impact on solving the congestion crisis in acute care settings, improving the overall healthcare system’s efficiency.

Canada is known as a multicultural country, home to residents from over 200 countries and speakers of approximately 130 different languages [5]. While English and French are the official languages, there is a shortage of French-speaking human resources in LTC facilities (except for Quebec) and a lack of optimal distribution of patients across facilities based on language needs. Therefore, patients may experience depression in healthcare environments, such as LTC homes, where they cannot communicate with staff or other patients. According to a report by The Globe and Mail [6], there is a vital need to provide LTC access for patients in their primary language, especially for those struggling with mental health conditions (such as dementia), diseases in need of constant screening (such as cancer), and chronic illnesses (such as asthma or diabetes). This language support is essential for patients’ safety, health, and quality of life. Studies indicate that individuals with limited language proficiency may lose their communication ability due to stress, extreme pain, and drug side effects, leading to misdiagnosis, assessment errors, delay in treatment services, inequity experiencing, and feeling underprivileged [7]. Language barriers also impact caregivers; for example, nurses face challenges arranging appointments and explaining regimens to patients with limited language efficiency [8]. Some studies have found that, on average, non-English-speaking patients spend 0.09 hours more in the emergency department compared to English-speaking patients [9], while other research has reported that patients with limited language proficiency had a 0.36-day shorter inpatient length of stay (LOS) [10].

Considering gender in LTC facilities has gained increased attention in recent decades [11]. For instance, research suggests that promoting gender-sensitive practices in LTC facilities can help remove care obstacles and foster a more inclusive and equitable system, especially for female residents [12]. Additionally, studies indicate that men and women often experience different trajectories in LTC facilities, particularly in discharge patterns [13]. Therefore, incorporating gender into models of patient journeys and capacity/resource allocation is essential for addressing these differences effectively.

There are few quantitative studies that focus on optimizing capacity planning in LTC and its ramifications. This highlights the need for further studies that incorporate local insights into optimal capacity planning of LTC networks, considering the specific needs of local communities as a critical matter. To fill these gaps, this study presents a dynamic Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) model based on the current LTC system in Canada. This model determines the timing and location of constructing new LTC facilities, allocates the capacity of each facility (including human resources and beds) over time, and assigns patients to facilities based on their demand region, gender, language, and age group within a discrete and finite time horizon. The principal objectives of this optimization model revolve around two key principles. First, the model aims to minimize the expected total planning costs. Secondly, the model incorporates explicit constraints that ensure patients receive support tailored to their specific characteristics, including gender and language.

Ontario is the largest province in Canada with more than 38% of the Canadian population [14]. In Ontario, as in the rest of Canada, there is a high demand for LTC. For example, in 2020, almost 38,000 Ontarians were on a waiting list for an LTC bed [15]. Additionally, demographic projections indicate that Ontario will encounter a surge in the older adult population over the next two decades, with the proportion of individuals aged 65 or older increasing from 18.1% in 2021 to 22.4% in 2041 [14]. This growth will result in the senior population aged 65 or older increasing by 1.5 times its current size and doubling the number of seniors aged 85 or older [16]. Consequently, there will be a growing demand for LTC beds, projected to increase by 38% over the next decade [17]. Home and Community Care Support Services (HCCSS), formerly known as Local Health Integration Networks (LHINs), are the government associations in Ontario responsible for determining whether applicants are eligible for admission to an LTC home [18]. Generally, individuals qualify for LTC homes when they have serious physical or mental problems and cannot live independently in their own homes or retirement homes. Since 2010, admission criteria have become stricter, requiring patients to be in need of high or very high levels of physical or mental support to be admitted to an LTC home [19]. This may lead patients to stay in LTC homes until the end of their life.

In this study, we propose a novel mathematical programming model to optimize location-allocation and capacity planning in LTC facility networks simultaneously. The model incorporates age groups to better capture the dynamics of patient journeys over time. Additionally, we introduce patient support constraints, taking into account patients’ gender and language. This approach may be beneficial in reducing feelings of loneliness among residents. To this end, we specify the need for different types of human resources, particularly language-based staff, to help decision-makers predict the human resource capacity (e.g., English and French-speaking staff requirement for Ontario’s designated healthcare providers [20]). In addition, the model aims to support equitable patient assignments by considering geographical equity. Finally, the performance of the proposed model is assessed through a case study, i.e., the city of Hamilton, ON, Canada. It is worth noting that in Canada, encompassing varied patient support and addressing language barriers have received growing attention in recent decades [7, 21, 22]. Thus, this study holds great significance for the policymakers in Hamilton, helping them to allocate LTC beds more effectively and efficiently.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents a brief literature review. The problem and its formulation are defined as a MILP model in Section 3. Computational results and numerical analysis are presented in Section 4. Finally, Section 5 concludes the study and discusses future works.

Literature review

Healthcare facility location problems aim to optimize the locations of healthcare facilities. On the other hand, allocation problems deal with the optimal assignments of patients and resources to various healthcare services.

Mitropoulos et al. [23] present a mathematical programming model with two objectives to determine the locations of hospitals and primary healthcare centers. The first objective is to minimize the distance between patients and facilities, while the second objective is to distribute the facilities among people in an equitable manner. In another study, Shroff et al. [24] use Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) to determine the optimal potential spots for constructing an LTC facility in Northern Virginia by considering criteria pertaining to the characteristics of healthcare services. Mousavi et al. [25] employ a multi-objective approach, aiming to find the optimal locations of healthcare facilities. Their study involves minimizing the total cost of establishment, the total transfer time for patients, and the total waiting time inside the facilities. For the third objective function, they use an artificial neural network (ANN) in combination with a simulation to estimate the waiting time. Optimizing patient and resource assignments to hospitals during an influenza outbreak is examined by Sun et al. [26]. In this study, they minimize costs associated with patients’ access to healthcare facilities. Also, their model can estimate the shortages of medical resources at specific times and for hospitals during periods of increased demand.

According to Li et al. [1], capacity planning problems generally deal with balancing supply and demand for an organization, and in healthcare, it can be applied to determine the required number of human resources for home care services, number of beds, diagnostic equipment, and operating rooms in hospitals, or number of hospitals needed in a specific region. As the first study on capacity planning in LTC networks, they model transfers between nursing homes and home and community-based services (HCBS) using an open migration network. They employ a newsvendor-type model to specify the optimal capacity of LTC service networks. Their findings suggest that under financial restriction, decreasing the capacity of nursing homes rather than HCBS yields better outcome in total network profit.

Several studies use simulation techniques for capacity planning in LTC networks [3, 4, 27, 28]. In a seminal work, Patrick [3] presents a Markov decision process (MDP) to determine the LTC capacity needed to ensure the census of hospital ALC patients remain under a given threshold. The author also uses simulation to estimate how considering the decisions from the MDP model can affect LTC wait times and help decision-makers plan future LTC capacity. Zhang and Puterman [4] determine LTC capacity for each year over a planning horizon using simulation optimization techniques. As demand and LOS in LTC may differ for individuals of various genders and age groups, they categorize clients by these two criteria to achieve more accurate results.

There are numerous studies in the literature that investigate capacity planning, facility location, and allocation problems concurrently [29–36]. Although there are many studies on facility location problems in healthcare, the number of papers on location-allocation and capacity planning in LTC, especially those using optimization techniques, is not considerable. To the best of our knowledge, Song et al. [36], Intrevado et al. [29], and a series of studies by Cardoso et al. ([30–33]) are the only studies that determine the optimal location-allocation and capacity of LTC services simultaneously. In this domain, Song et al. [36] propose two mathematical models with different objective functions and sets of constraints. They mainly focus on the travelling distance and construction (opening) costs. Cardoso et al. [30] develop a two-stage stochastic MILP model to schedule the delivery of LTC services within a nationalized healthcare system (such as Portugal). They minimize expected costs in the objective function while considering a variety of equity criteria (namely geographical equity, socioeconomic equity, equity of access, and equity of utilization) as constraints. Their multi-period and multi-service two-stage stochastic programming model considers demand and LOS uncertainties. In the first stage, a finite number of facilities and their capacity decisions are determined, while assignment decisions are made in the second stage. In another study, Cardoso et al. [31] consider maximizing health gains in the objective function as well as minimizing expected costs. Following the framework developed in their previous works, Cardoso et al. [30] and Cardoso et al. [31], in their other study, Cardoso et al. [32] develop a multi-objective and multi-period mathematical programming model in order to determine the location and capacity of LTC services for the medium term in a nationalized healthcare system. Unlike their other works, they treat budget limitation as a constraint while considering different equity-related policies in the objective function. More recently, Cardoso et al. [33] have expanded their model by adding two new policy objectives, Health and Wellbeing benefits. They also use scenario-reduction methods to reduce complexity. Moreover, Intrevado et al. [29] introduce an innovative approach to designing LTC networks based on the patient-centric principle. They develop two deterministic dynamic MILP models to help decision-makers optimize facility locations, capacity of LTC services, and patient allocation while emphasizing the quality-of-life concept, which we also consider in our optimization model. Treating facility location variables as integer variables is another new feature of their model. They also explore the potential of home care as a substitute for LTC facilities, mentioning that it could decrease LTC costs under special circumstances. However, none of these studies explicitly incorporate important factors such as patient support based on gender and language, changes in patients’ LOS or discharge rate as a consequence of aging, and the need for human resources based on language; all of which are taken into account in this paper, as discussed in the following sections.

Modeling framework

This section defines the problem by discussing the planning timeline, location selection for new facilities, capacity planning, patient assignments, admission process, and other relevant policies. Then, the MILP model is developed to address the problem.

Problem setting

We base our model on the LTC system operating in Ontario. In Ontario, LTC facilities provide nursing and support for individuals unable to live without outside help and who need 24-hour care for daily living [37]. There are different types of LTC facilities in Ontario, including for-profit, not-for-profit/charitable, municipal-run homes, and others owned by individuals or companies. These facilities receive funding from the provincial government, except for patients’ accommodation fees [19, 38]. It is important to note that owners of for-profit facilities may allocate a portion of these funds as profit for business purposes, while not-for-profit facilities should spend all funds only on issues related to the facility [39]. Our study focuses on not-for-profit LTC facilities. The dynamic MILP model is developed based on the following characteristics.

Planning timeline

Our model is dynamic, meaning that we consider a discrete set of periods during which various events may happen, altering the features of the LTC facilities network over time. We denote the planning horizon of the problem by T.

Location selection and capacity planning

Building LTC facilities, operating beds, and expanding capacity at each facility are costly endeavours. In our optimization model, we look for optimal strategies to determine the locations and timing for establishing new facilities within a predefined set of discrete periods and sites, where each site can accommodate at most one not-for-profit facility. We also designate the optimal policy for adding new beds to each facility over time and evaluate how the number/work hours of each type of human resource should be adjusted in each facility and period.

Patient assignments

One significant application of our model is to enhance the assignment process. The model assigns patients to the facilities in each period based on their location, gender, language, and age group of patients. Patient demand is influenced by these same factors. Our model aims to allocate patients to the same service points as their demand points. In other words, the model tries to assign patients to the same region where they apply for LTC service. In cases where this is impossible, the model aims to assign patients to the nearest facilities outside their demand region. This allocation approach help patients’ families and friends take a shorter journey to visit patients. In situations where a patient is assigned to a service point outside their demand region, a misassignment penalty, which can also be defined as a geographically undesirable assignment penalty, is applied based on the distance (a function of travel time) between demand and service points. Additionally, by considering gender, the model ensures a minimum proportion of each gender at each facility. Moreover, since the rate of leaving LTC facilities (discharge rate) vary depending on the patient’s age, we categorize patients by their age group to have a more accurate formulation.

Language considerations in planning the LTC facilities system in Ontario are critical. First, by defining a minimum number of patients at each facility, the model can alleviate the patients’ loneliness problem (due to language restrictions), which, based on interviews with several healthcare managers in LTC institutions, is a significant concern in Ontario, particularly in multilingual regions such as the Greater Toronto Area. This issue mainly arises from the shortage of caregivers speaking the same language as the patients. Second, we specify the need for various types of human resources who can communicate in specific languages to assist decision-makers in addressing the challenge of lacking particular types of human resources (especially French-speaking human resources in Ontario).

Finally, geographical equity has been incorporated into the constraints to ensure that a specified percentage of the population in each demand region receives LTC services. In other words, the inclusion of geographical equity constraints guarantees that the model allocates a minimum proportion of patients from each demand region, thereby promoting accessibility across different geographic areas.

Admission process

In our model, we assume a central authority manages the application and admission processes, which is also consistent with our case study. Specifically, Canada follows a structured process for admission to LTC homes, overseen and organized by the HCCSS. First, the patient is assessed by a care coordinator upon request. If the coordinator determines that LTC is the best option for the patient, the patient can go through the application process. The patient has the option to select up to five preferred LTC homes. When a spot becomes available, the patient receives an offer. The patient can accept or reject the offer while remaining on the waiting list for their other top priorities [18].

To clarify, in the current practice in Canada, the central authority does not consider gender and language when assigning patients to available beds in LTC homes. Instead, patients are allowed to choose up to five homes and are offered a place as soon as one becomes available. When we consider genders and languages in capacity planning and admission procedures, the quality of care can potentially be improved. In practice, the managing organization could establish multiple waiting lists for patients based on these factors. Consequently, when a bed becomes available, the patient is more likely to be assigned to a suitable nursing home that aligns with their preferences and specific requirements. For example, a French-speaking female patient would be assigned to a facility of her choice when a bed is available in a female room with French-speaking human resources.

Other criteria

In our model, we account for an un-assignment penalty for patients not placed in any not-for-profit LTC facility. We also assume that unassigned patients will be retained in acute care hospitals or allocated to alternative types of LTC facilities, such as for-profit and municipal facilities. To open new facilities and assign patients to a facility, the cost of the un-assignment penalty should be higher than other costs [1]. At the end of each period, some patients depart the system from every age group (purple arrows), while the remaining patients either stay in their current age group (dark red arrows) or transition to an older age group (blue arrows). Additionally, at the beginning of each period, there are patient assignments from different classifications (location, gender, language, and age group) to all age groups (black arrows).

The objective function in Eq. 1 minimizes the total cost, comprising the following cost components over T years: Total costs of adding beds and operational costs (first term): Equation 2 formulates the costs of adding beds at each time step by multiplying the number of additional beds in each facility by the cost of adding a single bed. Using (5), we ascertain the number of new patients assigned to any facility. Equation 6 defines the total demand at each period. It is noteworthy that due to the end-of-horizon effect in finite time problems [40], the model may assign a higher percentage of patients in the final periods to minimize the total costs under certain conditions, mostly when the un-assignment penalties remain nearly consistent across all periods. This factor can also aid in overcoming practical constraints and facilitate the implementation of additional policies.Misassignment penalties (last term): These penalties are calculated by multiplying the total number of misassigned patients by the corresponding misassignment penalty. Misassignment penalties depend on the distance between the patient’s demand region and the assigned facility’s location. These penalties are designed to reflect the undesirability of assigning a patient who lives in one region to a facility located in another region. The penalty increases as the distance between the assigned location and the patient’s demand region increases. So, the model tends to assign patients to facilities in their home region, reducing transportation cost. However, if it is not possible, the model tries to minimize the costs by assigning the patient to the nearest available facility while also considering capacity and other constraints.

Fixed cost of opening an LTC facility

This cost includes expenses regarding construction, such as land purchase and infrastructure development. To the best of our knowledge, no documents present this value for Hamilton, so we adapt a formula presented by Intrevado et al. [29], which calculates the fixed cost of opening an LTC facility in the city of Montréal, located in the province of Quebec. Using statistics from reports on construction costs of different building types in both Montréal (as representative of Quebec) and Ontario [46, 47], and by comparing the prices of basic rooms in publicly funded LTC homes in both locations [48, 49], we determined that, on average, constructing a building costs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$24\%$$\end{document} less in Ontario, and an LTC room costs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$4\%$$\end{document} less than in Montréal. As a result, we have adjusted the formula presented by Intrevado et al. [29] by reducing the cost estimates by 14% (the average of 4% and 24%). The modified formula for estimating the fixed cost of opening an LTC facility in Hamilton is as follows:29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_{jt} = & \$2,503,772 + \$153,231 \times \text {number of beds at facility}\, j \\& \forall j \in J, t \in T \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where the first term represents the fixed costs for land acquisition and infrastructure, while the second term accounts for the variable costs based on the number of beds the facility in region j can accommodate.

Single period patient Un-assignment penalty

There is no existing data or formulation to specify this value, as it depends on the jurisdictions and their policies governing the distribution of patients across different types of LTC homes (such as for-profit, not-for-profit, and municipal facilities) as well as what percentage of clients can be supported in LTC homes based on the available budget and other operational constraints. For this study, we assume that if a patient cannot secure placement in an LTC home, they receive care from acute care hospitals. This care is significantly more expensive for the government, costing approximately 6.7 times more to support ALC patients in hospitals compared to LTC homes [2]. According to Gibbard [2], in Ontario, the additional cost of keeping ALC patients in hospitals was estimated at $421,700 per patient per year in 2017 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\$495,900 - \$74,200$$\end{document}). Adjusting for the average inflation rate of 2.2% over the past decade, we estimate the cost in 2023 at $480,517 per patient per year (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\$421,700 \times 1.022^6$$\end{document}) [50]. For the case study, we use the estimations of un-assignment penalty for the period 2023-2027.

Misassignment penalty

The model prioritizes assigning patients to the nearest facility whenever possible. This approach is more convenient for family members, as it allows them to visit patients more easily when the care facility is closer to their home region. For our case study, we calculate this penalty based on travel duration via private vehicles or public transportation. Providing services to patients as close to their homes as possible is a reasonable policy. We use the following equation to estimate the misassignment penalty:30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {MP}_{ij}&= \frac{{TB}_{ij} + {TC}_{ij}}{2} \times DP&\forall i \in I, j \in J \end{aligned}$$\end{document}In Eq. 30, the first term denotes the average travel time (in minutes) between demand region i and service region j via public transportation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${TB}_{ij}$$\end{document}) and private vehicles (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${TC}_{ij}$$\end{document}). We consider buses in our computational results as they are the main public transportation form in Hamilton. The second term (DP) represents the penalty per minute of travel time, used to convert travel distance (in minutes) into a cost unit (in dollars). In our sensitivity analysis section, we justify the choice of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$DP= \$1000$$\end{document} for the case study. Additionally, for demand and service regions lacking public transportation, we apply a higher penalty by multiplying DP by 10. It is noteworthy that these estimated values are not fixed and may vary slightly from real-world values.

Conclusions and future works

This study introduces a mathematical programming model to simultaneously optimize location-allocation and capacity planning in the LTC facility network. The proposed dynamic MILP model determines the optimal timing and locations for constructing new LTC facilities, while also optimizing facility capacity (human resources and beds) over time. Moreover, it facilitates patient assignments based on various criteria such as their demand region, gender, language, and age group within a specified finite time horizon.

We have demonstrated that our model can assist authorities across Canada, particularly in Hamilton, Ontario, in addressing critical challenges within the LTC network, including excessive demand, the anticipated surge in demand in forthcoming years, language considerations, and the lack of efficient capacity planning. Additionally, it provides valuable guidance for enhancing patients’ experiences in LTC facilities by assigning them to the nearest appropriate facilities and mitigating gender and language isolation. This is achieved by ensuring a minimum number of patients from each gender and language in every facility and period. Our computational results indicate that by tolerating a slight increase in total costs, we can move toward a more equitable LTC network that not only accommodates patients’ gender- and language-specific needs but also fosters greater inclusivity in care allocation. Moreover, increasing the un-assignment and misassignment penalties leads to fewer patient un-assignments and misassignments, respectively. Furthermore, variations in these penalties and other parameters impact the proportion of different cost components, including misassignments, bed additions, bed operations, facility openings, and un-assignments, as well as the level of satisfied demand, and the percentage of misassigned patients. In our base case benchmark, operating bed costs constitute the largest expense, while misassignment costs account for the smallest share of the total cost. Ultimately, policymakers must determine whether these changes in satisfied demand and associated costs are significant. Although the model is developed based on the LTC network in the province of Ontario, it can be generalized and adapted to LTC networks in other Canadian provinces or countries with similar characteristics and criteria.

To enhance the model’s practicality, two sets of constraints (32-35) can be applied, which enable policymakers to implement stricter policies. For example, decision-makers may opt to develop the LTC network gradually over the planning horizon due to limitations such as budget and workforce. Constraints (32) and (33) allow decision-makers to set a maximum limit on the number of new beds added to the LTC system. Similarly, constraints (34) and (35) can be applied to restrict the number of new facilities opened in each period. Although the number of new facilities can be regulated by adjusting the Pol factor in the objective function, these four additional constraints provide greater flexibility. We acknowledge that segmenting patients based on specific attributes may not fully capture the breadth of diversity. While some may argue that such segmentation could discourage diversity and inclusivity, we emphasize that the purpose of grouping patients in this context is not to limit or discourage diversity but rather to address particular needs identified through interviews with healthcare professionals and supported by existing literature.

Several avenues can be explored to extend the current work. First, the model could be expanded to incorporate all forms of elder care, including seniors’ housing, LTC, home care, and community services. Second, the inherent uncertainty in patient demand, discharge rates, and transition rates across different genders, languages, and age groups presents an opportunity to develop a stochastic version of the model. Third, additional equity measures, such as socioeconomic equity, could be integrated into the constraints. Fourth, further research could examine a broader range of diversity factors to further enhance the model’s inclusivity. Lastly, a simulation study could provide deeper insights into the influence of various sources of uncertainty on the model’s performance, complementing traditional sensitivity analysis.
