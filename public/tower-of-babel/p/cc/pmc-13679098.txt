Intracluster correlation coefficients for neonatal trials: an analysis of national population level clinical data
Authors: S Jawad, A T Prevost, C Gale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClustering of outcomes occurs naturally in neonatal data due to multiple births clustered by mother and infants clustered within the neonatal unit administering their care. Estimating these cluster effects is important for neonatal study design and impacts upon sample sizes for neonatal trials.MethodsWe analysed retrospective data from all neonatal admissions in England and Wales between January 2016 and January 2020 held in the National Neonatal Research Database. Intracluster correlation coefficients (ICCs) and their 95% confidence intervals were calculated for core neonatal outcomes and those commonly used in trials, at the level of both the neonatal unit and mother. Results were stratified by gestational age and neonatal unit level of birth. To illustrate the impact of clustering, the design effect was estimated for a theoretical cluster trial.ResultsIntracluster correlation coefficients varied between outcomes and gestational age groups. Neonatal unit level intracluster correlation coefficients were low for mortality (0.0054, 95% CI 0.0039, 0.0068) and other core outcomes (severe necrotising enterocolitis 0.0042, 95% CI 0.0020, 0.0063) and were higher for outcomes related to care delivery (duration of intensive care 0.0237, 95% CI 0.0177, 0.0298; duration receiving parenteral nutrition 0.0265, 95% CI 0.0197, 0.0332). Gestation at birth was inversely correlated with neonatal unit ICC estimates. At the level of the mother, ICC estimates were generally larger, especially for preterm infants.ConclusionsWe have estimated ICCs for key neonatal outcomes at the level of both neonatal unit and mother with high precision using national, population-level routinely recorded data. These ICC estimates can be used to inform future neonatal studies and sample size calculations for neonatal trials.Keywords: Intracluster correlation coefficient, Neonatal trial design, Design effect, Cluster, Randomised controlled trial, Preterm, Neonatal

Step-by-step guide on how to use these ICCs1) Identify the gestational age stratum which is closest to the planned trial population2) Specify whether neonatal unit or mother clustering is of interest3) Identify and match the outcome for which the ICC is needed (usually the primary outcome)4) Choose the corresponding ICC estimate and consider using the upper confidence interval estimate for a more conservative approach5) Inflate the sample size using the ICC and if unequal sample sizes are expected

Background

Cluster trials are the optimal design to address certain clinical research questions, and in some situations, may be the only feasible approach—for example where an intervention can only be administered at the level of the neonatal unit [1], or for individual level interventions when contamination between trial participants risks adherence to the randomised treatment: for example a novel mobile phone app allocated to parents as an intervention but which may also be used by parents allocated to the standard care arm.

Cluster effect estimates such as intracluster correlation coefficients (ICC) are critical to accurately calculate sample size and to aid in choosing an appropriate analysis but are lacking in neonatal care [2]. Where they have been reported, cluster effect estimates are commonly obtained from previous trials or observational studies; however, this approach has limitations: the population, outcomes and treatment arms may not match that of the proposed trial and sample sizes are commonly low, leading to inaccuracies and imprecision [3]. This can lead to mis-specified cluster effects, inaccurate sample size estimates, loss of statistical power in the subsequent trial, and ultimately to research waste [4].

All neonatal trials (both cluster and individually randomised) are further complicated by the presence of clustering at multiple additional levels. Neonatal units may be nested within regional neonatal networks or federated health systems, while infants belonging to multiple births are clustered at the level of the mother. Multiple births comprise a considerable proportion (up to 30%) of participants in preterm trials [5], and unblinded individually randomised neonatal trials commonly randomise infants from multiple births together recognising the preference of parents and patients for this approach [6]. Consequently, many individually randomised neonatal trials require cluster estimates at the level of mothers to compute accurate sample sizes and plan appropriate analyses.

The aim of this study is to calculate cluster estimates with high precision at the level of both the neonatal unit and the mother using routinely collected, population-level, observational data from the UK. This population-level analysis has been conducted in other areas such as primary care [7] but not, to the best of our knowledge, in neonatal care.

Methods

We used the National Neonatal Research Database (NNRD) as the sole source of data. The NNRD [8] holds data from infants admitted to all National Health Service (NHS) neonatal units in England, Scotland and Wales (approximately 90,000 infants annually). The included population comprised babies admitted for neonatal care at a contributing neonatal unit without restriction by gestational age at birth. Data are extracted from neonatal electronic health records completed by health professionals during routine clinical care. A defined dataset, the Neonatal Dataset [9] of approximately 450 data items, is extracted quarterly, cleaned, and duplicates and queries about discrepant and implausible data are fed back to health professionals and rectified. Completeness and accuracy of NNRD data have been validated [8] in comparison to independent trial case report forms. Deidentified data used in this study were taken from all neonatal admissions, including any transfers to other neonatal units, between January 2016 and January 2020 inclusive. The neonatal data analysis unit (NDAU) holds UK Research Ethics Committee approval (21/LO/0024) for the NNRD.

Neonatal units in the UK are organised in regional networks of non-tertiary and tertiary neonatal units. The intention is for infants requiring high level care to receive it in a tertiary neonatal unit, with step-down transfer to a non-tertiary neonatal unit within the same network when appropriate. Neonatal units in English hospitals are classified as special care baby units (SCBU), local neonatal units (LNU) and tertiary level neonatal intensive care units (NICU) [10]. These levels and designations are analogous with but differ slightly from those of the American Academy of Paediatrics [11]. Neonatal intensive care units provide ongoing intensive care for babies of all gestations and may offer surgical and other specialist care such as cardiac surgery; local neonatal units are generally expected to be able to care for uncomplicated, singleton infants from 27 weeks’ gestation onwards and twins or higher order multiples from 28 weeks’ gestation onwards. Special care baby units are expected to provide care for babies from 32 weeks’ gestation and provided birthweight is above 1000 g but are not expected to provide intensive care apart from initial stabilisation for transfer.

Intracluster correlation coefficients and asymptotic 95% confidence intervals were estimated from unadjusted one-way analysis of variance models using STATA’s (Stata Corp) “loneway” command for the neonatal unit clusters and multiple births clusters separately. Negative lower bound confidence intervals and negative ICC estimates were truncated to zero as the ICC is a nonnegative value. The ICC estimates for binary outcomes are specifically observed-scale estimates as opposed to latent-scale logistic ICC estimates. The ICCs for multiple births were estimated using the entire dataset including both singletons and multiples. A combination of gestational age and levels of care were used as stratification factors. These were chosen based on typical trial populations so that the cluster estimates reflect these populations; stratification groups are not mutually exclusive. ICCs for binary and continuous outcomes were calculated in the same way but are presented separately for the reader’s benefit. Descriptive statistics such as means, medians, 25th and 75th centiles were presented for all outcomes [18]. The ICC analyses were conducted using cases with non-missing outcome data. The NEC diagnoses were grouped into three categories: (1) confirmed NEC, (2) no NEC (survival without NEC or no NEC but died prior to discharge home), (3) missing NEC data (died prior to discharge home but NEC diagnosis data missing or NEC diagnosis data missing). Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) missing values were defined as indeterminable BPD. Receipt of respiratory support at time of discharge was used to determine BPD for infants discharged before 36 weeks postmenstrual age.

A trial scenario was created to illustrate the design effect of different ICCs for individual and cluster randomised trials. Design effects (also known as variance inflation factors) are defined as Deff=1+(n-1)ρ, where n is the mean cluster size and ρ the ICC [19]. The design effect and the sample size are multiplied in order to inflate the sample size to be appropriately powered for a cluster design. The design effect was firstly calculated at the level of the mother, assuming a study with both singletons and multiples would have an approximate average cluster size of 1.1 infants. This average cluster size was calculated from the data. A second design effect was estimated for the neonatal unit level. Here, the average cluster size was assumed to be 40 trial participants per neonatal unit, estimated from the typical number of neonatal admissions in the UK. A final design effect for neonatal unit cluster randomised trials was produced as a product of the mother and neonatal unit level design effects [20]. The stated design effect is a simple formula for parallel cluster and equal cluster sizes and requires further modification when used with alternative cluster designs such as stepped-wedge, cluster-crossover and repeated-period designs.

Results

Routinely recorded data from babies born between January 2016 and January 2020 from 204 neonatal units (some neonatal units were restructured over the study period) and 417,675 babies were included in this analysis.

Cluster estimates at the level of the neonatal unit for both continuous and binary outcomes are reported in Table 1 for dichotomous outcomes and Table 2 for continuous outcomes. Cluster estimates vary between outcomes: dichotomous outcomes corresponding to the neonatal core outcome set have lower ICC values (such as survival for babies of all gestations 0.0054 95% CI (0.0039, 0.0068)) (Table 1) than continuous outcomes describing delivery or care (such as days on neonatal unit (0.0450 95% CI (0.0338, 0.0562)) (Table 2). The ICC varied within each outcome between the gestational age and level of care groups. The ICC for term infants tended to be smaller than the ICC for more preterm infants. The ICC for the combined outcome of BPD or death had the reverse relationship: ICC increased with gestational age (Table 1). Cluster estimates calculated at the level of the mother are reported in Supplemental Tables 1 and 2; these were higher than neonatal unit level ICC.

Discussion

We report marginal, unadjusted intracluster correlation coefficients for core and common neonatal outcomes with high precision and in subgroups relevant to neonatal trials. As expected, cluster estimates in neonatal care vary between outcomes and by population characteristics such as gestational age and the level of the neonatal unit delivering care; these result in a range of design effects and consequent inflation of sample size to account for clustering in neonatal trials. This study is unique in using national data recorded as part of routine care to estimate ICC for a wide range of outcomes; this large dataset has resulted in precise estimates with narrow confidence intervals for key outcomes based on empirical data and robustly defined core outcome sets [12, 13]. Intracluster correlation coefficients were estimated for common gestational age and neonatal unit level of care groups, making them appropriate for use in a range of neonatal trial situations. These data will inform and improve the design of individually and cluster randomised neonatal trials.

There is a paucity of published data describing neonatal cluster estimates. Rysavy et al. [21] presented neonatal unit cluster estimates ranged from 0.02 to 0.13 among preterm infants admitted to units in the Institute of Child Health and Human Development Neonatal Research Network. Although these are estimated from a different population to the NNRD, the estimates are in the same ranges. Additionally, and consistent with our results, Rysavy et al. [21] also found an inverse relationship in the magnitude of ICC estimates with gestational age.

Yelland et al. [22] estimated ICCs for multiple births from four completed perinatal trials: the DINO trial (n = 657), the BCD trial (n = 319), the TTB trial (n = 470) and the N3RO trial (n = 1273). The ICCs were estimated on a range of outcomes including death or bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) and necrotising enterocolitis (NEC) and identified large differences between the ICCs for different outcomes. Intracluster correlation coefficients for death/BPD were similar although not identical to those we report: 0.33 95% CI (0.17, 0.49) in a trial with infants < 29 weeks gestational age compared to our finding of 0.42 95% CI (0.37, 0.48) for infants < 28 weeks gestational age. Necrotising enterocolitis was defined as ‘proven NEC’ in the trials analysed by Yelland et al., with the ICC reported as 0.17 95% CI (−0.04, 0.95) compared to a more precisely estimated 0.21 95% CI (0.17, 0.24) in our study. NEC for very preterm infants was comparable between Yelland et al., 0.08 (−0.07, 0.42) for infants < 29 weeks gestational age and 0.09 95% CI (0, 0.19) for infants < 28 weeks gestational age in this study. The wide confidence intervals reported by Yelland et al. highlight the limitations of using relatively small datasets and relatively few clusters from clinical trials; we build on this previous work by reporting similar ICC with much greater precision. A separate study by Yelland et al. presents the ICC estimates for multiple births from combining the infant level data from 12 preterm trials. They report some ICCs to be much larger than our study found, particularly for mortality (0.66 95% CI (versus 0.2264 in this study) and brain injuries (median ICC for intraventricular haemorrhage of 0.36 versus 0.1647 in this study). Other variations between estimates were seen for bronchopulmonary dysplasia (0.51 versus 0.4222 in this study) and necrotising enterocolitis (0.13 versus 0.09144 in this study). The estimates from Yelland et al. [23] differ from this study as they have been produced using adjusted log binomial models, which account for the effects of both trial treatment group and for known confounders. This model was not chosen for our analysis since the focus of the study was unadjusted estimates which are easily computed using ANOVA for both continuous and binary outcomes.

Large ICCs are due to larger between-cluster variations when compared to within-cluster variations. We find that the ICC for length of neonatal unit stay and other outcomes that are more closely related to unit practice are high for all stratified groups, indicating this outcome is more variable between units, potentially because practice within units may be guided by unit or regional policy. Other disease outcomes, such as mortality and brain injury, have much smaller ICCs implying that variations between clusters are small, consistent with a pattern well described in other clinical fields. ICCs are larger for mother-level estimates compared to neonatal unit-level estimates which is also to be expected, since multiples share genetics, their intrauterine environment as well as many other factors associated with neonatal outcomes such as socioeconomic deprivation. Interestingly, the ICC estimates for death or BPD had the inverse relationship with gestational age compared to other outcomes such that ICCs for both neonatal unit and mother levels were both smaller for more preterm infants. This effect may be explained by the high prevalence of death or BPD at the lower gestational age groups which would translate into less variation between clusters. In addition, since BPD is defined by receipt of treatment (respiratory support at 36 corrected gestational weeks), this outcome may be more prone to neonatal unit cluster variation than other clinical outcomes.

To illustrate the impact of different ICCs on sample size, we present the design effect for an example cluster trial; both the ICC and average cluster size are needed to calculate the design effect, and it is important to note that a small ICC will still have a substantial design effect with larger average cluster sizes. Cluster-appropriate analysis is recommended whenever clusters are present in the data; multiple births are common in preterm trials and can constitute up to 30% or more of the total trial population. In such trials the average cluster sizes and design effects are small when estimating the ICC on the level of mothers, but the benefit of exercising good practice and ensuring that the trial is suitably powered outweighs any additional challenges relating to the marginal increase in sample size.

Cluster estimates on the level of the mother are intended to be interpreted as the effects when both singletons and multiple births are present in trial datasets, rather than solely the effect of multiple births. It was chosen this way in order to estimate population-level ICCs which would be directly applicable to neonatal trial design.

Strengths of this study include use of a large, real-world, routinely recorded dataset resulting in ICCs estimated with high precision for multiple core and common neonatal outcomes, with analyses stratified by populations and cluster levels. Furthermore, we illustrate the impact of different ICCs on sample size through the design effect; a limitation of this aspect is that the design effects assume equal cluster sizes which do not hold for every trial design, especially when there is missing outcome data or when centres recruit more participants than others. In these instances, the design effect needs to be calculated with care as unequal cluster sizes require a larger sample size in order to preserve power [24, 25]. We suggest future planned neonatal cluster analyses use the data we present, rather than data from individual studies, given the magnitude of this dataset. Limitations of this work include the assignment of neonatal unit level as that of the first provider; this may have resulted in incorrect outcomes assignment in the case of infants transferred soon after birth, and further work could evaluate this against assigning the neonatal unit cluster to the longest provider; however, in practice, infants are commonly recruited to a study shortly after birth which is most likely to occur at the first provider of care. First provider may not be the neonatal unit of randomisation for trials with late interventions, and as such, these ICC estimates may be less applicable. Cluster estimates for the neonatal network level were not calculated as trials seldom, if not never, conduct cluster trials where entire neonatal networks are randomised.

A further limitation to the ICC estimates is the data accuracy and completeness of data in the NNRD; however, these should be mitigated due to the previously validated nature of this dataset for the outcomes that we focused on [8]. In addition, the inability to distinguish between missing data and non-cases in outcomes in the NNRD potentially allows for under-reporting which may have deflated the ICC estimates and is a main limitation of using routinely collected data. The estimates are unadjusted estimates and are not estimates of unit practice variation; therefore, care should be taken to avoid using the estimates as crude neonatal unit level practice variation. Care should be taken when using the brain injury outcome as it is a very broad definition that may not be appropriate to use in all trials. Similarly, length of stay is defined among survivors only, as it eliminates a main intercurrent event; however, this definition may not be useful to trialists interested in integrating death as a composite outcome.

Conclusions

Neonatal studies should account for clustering at both the design and analysis stages. We present population-level estimates of ICC for commonly reported neonatal outcomes and trial populations to enable improved neonatal trial design and reporting.
