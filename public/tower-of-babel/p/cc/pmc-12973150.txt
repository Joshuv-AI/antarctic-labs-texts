Sex and life experience shape locus coeruleus pretangle tau pathology
Authors: Zia Hasan, Sarah E. Torraville, Tamunotonye Omoluabi, Aida Maziar, Onyedikachi N. Belolise, Lauren A. MacGowan, Cassandra M. Flynn, Camila Reinhardt, Susan G. Walling, Touati Benoukraf, Qi Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAlzheimer's disease features early a pathology in the locus coeruleus (LC), yet how sex and life experience shape LC vulnerability remains poorly understood.METHODSWe expressed pseudophosphorylated human tau (htauE14) in LC neurons of TH‐Cre rats and exposed both sexes to early‐ or late‐life enrichment or stress. Behavioral, histological, protein, and hippocampal single‐nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA‐seq) analyses were performed.RESULTSLC‐targeted htauE14 impaired learning and increased anxiety‐like behavior. Early enrichment reduced htauE14 spread and LC microglia activation, elevated hippocampal brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), and improved olfactory learning in males. Late enrichment alleviated anxiety and enhanced spatial memory, whereas late stress exacerbated LC degeneration. Hippocampal snRNA‐seq revealed sex‐ and cell type–specific transcriptional responses, with htauE14 preferentially engaging metabolic and synaptic pathways in females, effects amplified by early stress but stabilized by early enrichment. Late‐life experiences primarily recruited homeostatic regulatory programs.DISCUSSIONSex and developmental history critically shape early LC tau‐related vulnerability.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) develops insidiously over decades, yet the earliest biological events that confer vulnerability, and the modifiable factors that influence them, remain poorly defined. One of the first brain regions to exhibit pathology is the locus coeruleus (LC), 1 , 2 , 3 the brain's primary source of forebrain norepinephrine (NE). Hyperphosphorylated pretangle tau accumulates within LC neurons decades before clinical symptoms emerge, initiating tau spread along noradrenergic and limbic circuits. 1 , 2 , 3 These pretangle tau species, particularly in oligomeric form, are neurotoxic, disrupting synaptic function before neurofibrillary tangle (NFT) formation and driving the Braak staging cascade. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 LC neuronal and integrity loss closely track cognitive decline and disease progression, 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 positioning the LC as both an early biomarker and a potentially modifiable therapeutic target in prodromal AD.

Biological sex and environmental exposures may independently and interactively influence AD risk and trajectory through the LC‐NE system. Women are disproportionately affected, exhibiting greater tau burden, faster cognitive decline, and more severe symptoms than men. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 A leading hypothesis 16 implicates estrogen‐dependent regulation of the LC‐NE system: the LC is rich in estrogen receptors, and estrogens enhance NE synthesis and modulate stress via corticotrophin‐releasing hormone signaling. During perimenopause, declining estrogen may destabilize this system, initiating a cascade of reduced NE, neuroinflammation, blood–brain barrier breakdown, and increased tau vulnerability, as proposed by Luckey et al. 16 This risk is compounded by intrinsic LC sexual dimorphism: rodent studies show that females possess greater LC volume, neuronal density, and dendritic complexity, traits that enhance responsiveness but heighten metabolic burden and susceptibility. 17 , 18 , 19 These features are consistent with human multiomics and imaging data, 20 , 21 suggesting that hormonal decline during midlife interacts with structural and molecular sex differences to amplify AD vulnerability in women.

Environmental factors also play a critical role in shaping cognitive resilience or vulnerability in AD. Lifelong enrichment, through education, physical activity, or social engagement, delays disease onset, 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 whereas early adversity and chronic stress accelerate pathology. 26 The LC is a key mediator of these experience‐dependent effects: in humans, novelty‐related tasks that engage phasic LC activity are linked to reduced tau deposition, enhanced LC‐medial temporal lobe connectivity, and slower cognitive decline. 27 , 28 , 29 In rodents, phasic LC stimulation promotes resilience to pretangle tau pathology, whereas stress‐associated tonic activation impairs neuronal health and behavior. 30 Although growing evidence highlights these modulatory influences, the interplay between sex and life experience in shaping LC vulnerability in AD, and the consequent impact on hippocampal memory circuits, remains poorly understood. Moreover, most AD models focus on amyloid beta (Aβ) or late‐stage tau pathology, overlooking early LC involvement and often failing to include sex as a biological variable or to consider the timing of environmental exposures, limitations that obscure critical windows for prevention and mechanistic insight.

To address these gaps, we developed a rat model in which pseudophosphorylated human tau (htauE14) is selectively expressed in LC neurons using Cre recombinase‐dependent viral delivery in tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)‐Cre rats. 30 , 31 This model captures early‐stage pretangle tau pathology without NFT formation and with limited cortical involvement. 30 , 31 Another defining feature of this model is robust tau phosphorylation at s262 and s356, 32 , 33 key sites associated with soluble pretangle tau assemblies in AD. 34 We examined how environmental enrichment or stress, delivered during early life (prior to htauE14 expression) or adulthood (after htauE14 induction), modulates tau propagation, LC inflammation, hippocampal function, and behavior in mid‐age rats. Behavioral assays assessed anxiety and cognition; tau pathology and inflammatory markers were quantified via immunohistochemistry and western blotting. Finally, we used single‐nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA‐seq) to characterize transcriptional responses across hippocampal cell types, a major LC projection target.

This study provides a mechanistic framework for how sex and life experience converge on the LC and its projecting targets to shape early AD vulnerability. Our findings position the LC as a dynamic, sex‐sensitive hub for tau pathology and the hippocampus as an environmentally responsive site of disease progression, highlighting the lasting impact of life history, particularly early experiences, in modulating AD risk.

METHODS

Subjects and ethics statement

TH‐Cre rats of both sexes, bred by crossing homozygous TH‐Cre males (SAGE Labs) with Sprague Dawley females (Charles River Laboratories), were used in this study. Offspring were assigned randomly to experimental groups. All animals were housed individually with ad libitum access to food and water under a 12‐h light–dark cycle. The housing and experimental protocols were approved by Memorial University of Newfoundland's Institutional Animal Care Committee (certificate number: 18‐01‐QY) and complied with Canadian Council on Animal Care guidelines.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: The locus coeruleus (LC) is the earliest site of tau pathology in Alzheimer's disease (AD), yet few studies have examined how modifiable environmental factors shape this process in a sex‐specific manner. Prior work has emphasized LC degeneration but rarely addressed mechanistic links to life experiences or sex during early disease stages. Interpretation: Our study shows that LC‐targeted pretangle tau is sufficient to impair cognition and that environmental exposures modulate these outcomes in a sex‐dependent manner. Early enrichment limited tau spread, reduced LC microglia, and preserved hippocampal transcriptomic stability, whereas early stress amplified female‐biased disruption of metabolic and synaptic pathways. Late enrichment also improved behavioral outcomes. These findings extend current knowledge by establishing sex and developmental history as critical determinants of LC vulnerability. Future directions: Future studies should test sex‐specific enrichment and stress‐reduction strategies across the lifespan to enhance resilience and delay AD progression.

Experimental design

The experimental flow is illustrated in Figure 1A. At 3 months of age, rats received bilateral infusions of adeno‐associated virus (AAV) carrying either a pseudophosphorylated human tau gene (AAV9‐rEF1a‐DIO‐EGFP‐htauE14) or a control GFP gene (AAV9‐rEF1a‐DIO‐EGFP) into the LC. The Cre‐dependent AAV design ensured selective expression in noradrenergic LC neurons. To model environmental influences, subsets of animals were exposed to early maternal enrichment (eER) or early stress (eSTR) during postnatal Days 2–10, or to late enrichment (lER) or late stress (lSTR) in adulthood (5–7 months). Additional control groups included cage‐housed htauE14 (cE14)‐ and GFP (cGFP)‐infused rats. Behavioral assessments were performed beginning at 11 months of age (8 months post‐infusion). Subsets of animals were processed for immunohistochemistry and western blot analyses, whereas a separate, behavior‐naïve cohort was used for snRNA‐seq at 8 months post‐infusion.

AAV infusion surgeries

Rats were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane and secured in a stereotaxic apparatus. Body temperature was maintained with a heating pad. Preoperative analgesia was provided by subcutaneous administration of Meloxicam Slow Release (10 mg/mL), and Sensorcaine Epinephrine (0.25%, 0.25 mL) was injected along the incision line. The coordinates used were 11.8–12.2 mm posterior, 1.2–1.4 mm lateral to bregma, and 6.3 mm ventral to the brain surface. A Hamilton syringe attached to an infusion pump delivered 1 µL of AAV mixed with 0.4 µL of blue fluorescent beads at two sites (1.2‐ and 1.4‐mm lateral) within the LC on each side, angled 20° caudally to the coronal plane. Following surgery, animals were placed in a clean cage on a heating pad for recovery.

Behavioral experiments

Rats were assigned to 1 of 10 experimental groups: eSTR, eER, lSTR, lER, or cage control, each receiving either htauE14 or GFP AAV infusion. Behavioral experiments were performed by experimenters blinded to AAV infusion type and enrichment or stress treatment.

Environmental manipulation

Early stress and early maternal enrichment

eSTR and eER were induced through maternal separation from postnatal Days 2–10. For eSTR, pups were separated from the dam and other pups for 5 h daily and kept warm with a heating pad. They were reintroduced to the dam after each separation, and maternal behavior was recorded during the first 15 min. Early‐life maternal enrichment involved brief (15‐min) maternal separation, which is known to enhance maternal care upon reunion, offering a form of enrichment. 35 Rat pups do not open their eyes or ear canals until ≈2 weeks after birth 36 ; during this period, olfactory cues and somatosensory interactions with the dam and littermates serve as the primary sources of environmental input.

Late stress and enrichment

lSTR was implemented using a Chronic Unpredictable Stress Protocol (CUSP) for 6 weeks, applied daily for 2 h. Stressors included restraint, wet bedding, tilted cages, and irregular light cycles. Late enrichment involved placing 4–5 rats in a 60 × 60 × 50 cm artificial glass play arena with toys, chews, and exercise equipment for 2 h daily over 6 weeks.

Behavioral tests

Open field test

Rats were placed in a 60 × 60 × 50 cm open field arena for a 10‐min trial to assess general locomotion and anxiety‐like behavior. The distance traveled was recorded using ANYMaze software, whereas rearing behavior (lifting both front paws off the ground) was recorded manually.

Marble burying test

Rats were placed in a cage with 16 marbles arranged in a grid on a bedding layer. Over a 30‐min session, the number of marbles buried by at least 75% in bedding was recorded as an index of anxiety‐like and compulsive behavior.

Elevated plus maze (EPM)

Rats were placed on an elevated plus maze for a 5‐min trial. The maze consisted of two open arms and two closed arms. The amount of time spent in the closed arms (an index of anxiety) and the number of head dips (an exploratory behavior where rats peer over the edges of the open arms) were recorded.

Y‐maze

The Y‐maze test consisted of a training phase and a testing phase, separated by 4 h. In the training phase, rats explored two of three arms of the Y‐maze. The inaccessible arm was counterbalanced between all animals and became the novel arm during the testing phase. In the testing phase, all three arms were accessible. The percentage of entries into the novel was recorded as an index of spatial memory.

Spontaneous location recognition (SLR)

In the SLR task, designed to assess long‐term spatial memory, rats were first allowed to explore an open arena for 10 min, where three identical objects (Objects 1, 2, and 3) were placed, with Objects 2 and 3 positioned 20 cm apart. Twenty‐four hours later, during the test phase, rats were reintroduced to the arena, now containing only two objects: one in the original location of Object 1 (familiar) and the other placed midway between the previous positions of Objects 2 and 3 (novel). Spatial memory performance was quantified using a discrimination ratio, calculated as the difference in time spent exploring the novel versus familiar location, divided by the total exploration time at both locations (tnovel‐tfamilial/ttotal).

Odor discrimination

Rats were presented sequentially with three odors: mineral oil (three trials), 0.001% heptanol in mineral oil (O1; three trials), and a mixture of 0.001% heptanol and octanol in mineral oil (O2; one trial). The amount of time spent sniffing the odor was recorded. The discrimination index was the ratio of the sniffing time difference between the O2 and the third presentation of O1 to the total sniffing time (tO2−tO1‐3)/(tO2+tO1‐3).

Go/no‐go olfactory discrimination learning

Rats were trained to discriminate between two odors in a go/no‐go task using a Knosys olfactometer. Training began with simple odor discrimination (positive odor: 1% orange, negative odor: 1% peppermint), progressing to a more difficult discrimination odor pair (positive odor: 0.001% heptanol, negative odor: 0.001% heptanol+octanol). The blocks to criterion (≥70% correct responses in three consecutive blocks) during the stage of difficult discrimination were recorded to assess olfactory discrimination and learning capabilities. Correct response time courses were tracked and compared across experimental groups.

Immunohistochemistry and analysis

After behavioral testing, rats were anesthetized using isoflurane and decapitated to retrieve their brains. Hind brains containing the brain stem were post‐fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and then transferred to 20% sucrose for cryoprotection. Brains were sectioned at 25 µm on chrome‐gelatin coated slides or at 50 µm as free‐floating sections. Immunohistochemical staining was performed using primary antibodies against Iba‐1 (microglial marker; 1:2000; Wako, 019‐19741) and GFP (1:2000; Invitrogen, A11122), with appropriate secondary antibodies, and developed using 3, 3′‐diaminobenzidine. Nissl staining was performed following Iba‐1 staining to visualize general cellular architecture. Slides were coverslipped using Permount following staining. Active caspase 3 (AC3; 1:2000; BD Pharmingen, 559656) and DBH (1:2000; EMD Millipore, MAB508) immunofluorescence labeling was performed, followed by secondary antibody incubation, and slides were coverslipped with Fluorshield Mounting Medium containing 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindoleDAP (ThermoFisher). DBH immunolabeling was used to delineate the LC boundary for AC3 quantification.

Sections were analyzed using ImageJ software by experimenters that were blind to the treatment groups. GFP‐positive cells were quantified across an equal number of brainstem sections spanning the anterior to posterior axis (AP −7.64 to −10.52 relative to bregma) and categorized according to their position relative to the LC. Cell counts were ranked using the following scoring system: 1 (1–10 cells), 2 (11–20 cells), 3 (21–30 cells), 4 (31–40 cells), and 5 (>40 cells). Iba‐1+ and AC3+ cells in the LC were also quantified, with counts averaged from three sections from a similar rostral to caudal range per animal.

Western blotting

Hippocampal tissue was dissected following brain extraction and was homogenized in RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay, and equal protein amounts were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were stained with Ponceau S, blocked, and incubated with primary antibodies against brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) (1:2000; Abcam, ab108319), glucocorticoid receptor (GR; 1:2000; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA1‐510), Triggering Receptor Expressed on Myeloid Cells 2 (TREM2; 1:5000; R&D Systems; AF1729), TH (1:5000; Millipore Sigma, MAB318), tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα; 1:2000; Abcam, ab6671), DBH (1:2000; EMD Millipore, MAB508), s262 ptau (1:2000; Abcam, ab92627), T22 (1:2000; Millipore Sigma, ABN454) and Tau5 (1:3000, Millipore, 577801) followed by HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using chemiluminescence and quantified with ImageJ.

Single‐nucleus RNA sequencing

Nuclei isolation and library preparation

Nuclei were isolated from frozen hippocampal tissue (right hemisphere), counted, and assessed for quality using fluorescence microscopy, following the detailed protocol available (https://www.protocols.io/private/BE210BE858B311ED9CED0A58A9FEAC02). Nuclei concentrations were optimized to 700–1200 nuclei/µL. snRNA‐seq libraries were prepared using Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3ʹ Reagent Kits v3.1 (10x Genomics) as per our previously described protocol. 32 Paired‐end sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Génome Québec, Canada).

Preprocessing, integration, and clustering of snRNA‐seq data

Demultiplexed FASTQ files from 36 samples (N = 3 rats per sex per group, across six groups: cGFP, cE14, E14.eSTR, E14.eER, E14.lSTR, and E14.lER) were aligned to the Rattus norvegicus genome (GRCr8, Ensembl) using Cell Ranger v9.0.1 (https://www.10xgenomics.com/support/software/cell‐ranger/latest/release‐notes/cr‐release‐notes#v9‐0‐1) with default parameters (–include‐introns), which handled alignment, filtering, and unique molecular identifier (UMI) counting. Filtered count matrices underwent quality control using the Trailmaker platform hosted by Parse Biosciences (https://www.parsebiosciences.com/data‐analysis/). Nuclei with high mitochondrial content or doublet probability were excluded using scDblFinder. 37

Normalized data were processed with Seurat v4.4.0 (https://github.com/satijalab/seurat), selecting 2000 highly variable genes (HVGs), scaling, and integrating them for clustering using Harmony. 38 Dimensionality reduction was performed using 30 principal components, followed by Louvain clustering (resolution = 0.5) and UMAP embedding (minimum distance = 0.3, Euclidean metric), resulting in 25 pre‐clusters.

Cell‐type identification

To identify cell type–specific marker genes for each pre‐cluster, we performed a Wilcoxon rank‐sum test using Scanpy v1.10 (https://scanpy.readthedocs.io/), selecting the top 25 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) per cluster. Differential expression analysis was also conducted with the Presto package in R, which integrates the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve analysis. Cell types were annotated by comparing DEGs to established cell type–specific markers from published datasets. Pre‐clusters corresponding to the same cell type were merged to define final cell‐type clusters.

Sub‐clustering of excitatory and inhibitory neuronal cell types

Excitatory and inhibitory neuronal populations were further subclustered. Excitatory clusters were defined using region‐specific markers, and inhibitory clusters were classified according to canonical molecular markers. Data were normalized, and HVGs selected, scaled, and integrated using Harmony. Clustering and dimensional reduction were performed with 30 principal components using Louvain clustering (resolution = 0.1) and visualized via UMAP. Final analyses identified 10 excitatory (120,911 nuclei) and 9 inhibitory (18,323 nuclei) biologically distinct subclusters.

Differential gene expression analysis

To identify DEGs by comparing the groups, we applied the pseudobulk limma‐voom package, 39 with genes considered differentially expressed if they had an adjusted p‐value (Adj‐p‐value) < 0.05 and an absolute log2fold change >0.50. The data are visualized via Volcano plots, generated by EnhancedVolcano’ v1.22.0 package (doi: 10.18129/B9.bioc.EnhancedVolcano).

Gene ontology analysis

Gene ontology (GO) analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler v4.12.6. 40 DEGs were separated into up‐ and down‐regulated sets. GO enrichment was performed in parallel across groups using compareCluster with enrichGO against Rattus norvegicus (org.Rn.eg.db), restricted to the biological process (BP) ontology, with ENSEMBL IDs (keyType = “ENSEMBL”) and readable = TRUE. The following parameters were used: pvalueCutoff = 0.05, pAdjustMethod = “BH,” qvalueCutoff = 0.20, minGSSize = 10, maxGSSize = 500, and pool = FALSE. For figure panels, enriched terms were further filtered for display (gene count ≥3; Adj‐p‐value < 0.05). For the main figure, a curated subset of GO terms was plotted to highlight processes of interest.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were conducted using OriginPro 2022b software. Three‐way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) followed by post hoc Tukey tests were used to compare the effects of AAV, treatment, and sex on behavioral measures. For the olfactory learning time course, a separate three‐way ANOVA assessed the effects of treatment, training block, and sex. Two‐way ANOVAs were used for group and sex comparisons in the cellular and molecular measures. Sample sizes were based on previous studies for detecting significant effects. Outliers (≥ mean ± 2 SD) were excluded from the analysis. Normality was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk tests applied to the residuals of the two‐way or three‐way ANOVA models. For the majority of variables (15/17), the residuals met normality assumptions (p > 0.05). For variables showing mild deviations (e.g., slight tail departures on Q‐Q plots), data were analyzed using three‐way permutation‐based ANOVA, followed by permutation‐based pairwise comparisons for significant main effects or interactions. Sex comparisons in S262 ptau expression were performed using two‐tailed Welch's t‐tests, which do not assume equal variances and are robust to unequal sample sizes. Effect sizes are reported as Hedges’ g with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

RESULTS

Sex‐ and experience‐dependent behavioral outcomes in pretangle tau and control rats

To examine how environmental factors influence pretangle tau pathology, we used TH‐CRE rats with targeted expression of htauE14 or control GFP in LC neurons at 3 months of age. Previous findings indicate minimal behavioral and mitochondrial deficits associated with wild‐type htau, 32 suggesting that neurotoxicity arises primarily from tau hyperphosphorylation rather than the presence of htau alone. Following bilateral LC infusions of htauE14 or GFP, rats were randomly assigned to cage control (cE14, cGFP), early or late enrichment (eER, lER), or early or late stress (eSTR, lSTR) conditions to assess effects on tau propagation, neuroinflammation, and behavior (Figure 1A). eER promoted maternal care compared to eSTR (Figure S1). Behavioral testing was conducted at 11 months across 10 experimental groups (2 AAVs × 5 treatments, both sexes), including open field test for general activity, marble burying and EPM for anxiety, and olfactory and spatial discrimination learning tests. Efficient targeting of htauE14 to LC noradrenergic neurons was confirmed, with high transduction efficiency (84.5 ± 1.9%; n = 9) (Figure 1B,C), consistent with previous studies. 30 , 31 LC tissue from htauE14 animals also contained oligomeric tau species (Figure S2).

In the open field test, htauE14 rats exhibited reduced locomotion compared to GFP controls (AAV: F 1,95 = 12.226, p < 0.001, η2 p = 0.114, 95% CI 0.022–0.238), while female rats were generally more active than males (sex: F 1,95 = 25.240, p < 0.001, η2 p = 0.210, 95% CI 0.081–0.342) (Figure 1D). There was a significant main effect of treatment (F 4,95 = 7.517, p < 0.001, η2 p = 0.240, 95% CI 0.082–0.351), with lER rats exhibiting the greatest locomotion relative to other groups (p < 0.01). Rearing behavior revealed a significant AAV x treatment interaction (F 4,93 = 5.427, p < 0.001, η2 p = 0.189, 95% CI 0.042–0.294). In GFP‐expressing rats, rearing was increased by both late treatments (lER and lSTR) as well as eER. Among htauE14 rats, lER significantly increased rearing relative to lSTR (p < 0.001) (Figure 1E). However, htauE14 rats in both late treatment groups reared significantly less than their GFP counterparts (p < 0.001).

In the marble burying test, htauE14 rats exhibited increased marble burying behavior (F 1,100 = 40.556, p < 0.001, η2 p = 0.289, 95% CI 0.154–0.428). A significant AAV × treatment interaction was observed (F 4,100 = 8.269, p < 0.001, η2 p = 0.249, 95% CI 0.096–0.369). Early treatments (eER and eSTR) reduced marble burying in GFP rats (p < 0.01), whereas eSTR increased burying in htauE14 rats (p < 0.05) (Figure 1F). The effects of htauE14 on anxiety‐related behavior was further supported by the EPM, where htauE14 rats spent more time in the closed arms. A three‐way permutation ANOVA revealed significant main effects of sex (F 1,93 = 8.31, pperm = 0.020, η2 p = 0.082, 95% CI 0.009–0.190), and AAV (F 1,93 = 27.88, pperm = 0.005, η2 p = 0.231, 95% CI 0.094–0.359). lER broadly reduced this anxiety‐related behavior (treatment: F 4,93 = 8.24, pperm = 0.005, η2 p = 0.262, 95% CI 0.095–0.368) (Figure 1G). Moreover, htauE14 rats showed reduced head‐dipping, a measure of exploratory behavior, which was reversed by lER (AAV x treatment: F 4,90 = 5.088, p < 0.01, η2 p = 0.184, 95% CI 0.036–0.283) (Figure 1H).

In the Y‐maze test assessing short‐term spatial memory (Figure 1I), htauE14 rats made significantly fewer entries into the novel arm (AAV: F 1,92 = 5.407, p = 0.022, η2 p = 0.056, 95% CI 0.000–0.161), indicating impaired spatial learning. A significant AAV x treatment x sex interaction was observed (F 4,92 = 3.106, p = 0.019, η2 p = 0.119, 95% CI 0.003–0.212). eSTR enhanced spatial memory in male GFP rats (p < 0.05 or 0.01), but this effect was absent in htauE14 rats. In the SLR test, htauE14 rats again exhibited deficits in spatial memory. A three‐way permutation ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of AAV (F 1.88 = 13.22, pperm = 0.005, η2 p = 0.131, 95% CI 0.026–0.248) (Figure 1J). Even though learning in the cGFP rats was limited, a significant treatment effect was observed (F 4,88 = 3.788, pperm = 0.010, η2 p = 0.147, 95% CI 0.013–0.239), with lER improving performance (Figure S3).

In the similar odor discrimination test, a sensitive measure for pretangle tau pathology, 30 htauE14 rats exhibited a significant overall deficit (AAV: F 1,94 = 9.223, p = 0.003, η2 p = 0.089, 95% CI 0.011–0.207) (Figure 1K). In the go/no‐go odor discrimination task, htauE14 rats required significantly more training blocks to reach the learning criterion (AAV: F 1,64 = 4.164, p = 0.045, η2 p = 0.041, 95% CI 0.000–0.119) (Figure 1L). Further analysis of learning time courses within each AAV cohort revealed distinct treatment and sex effects (Figure 1M and Figure S4). In GFP rats, lSTR accelerated learning (group: F 4,287 = 4.287, p = 0.002, η2 p = 0.056, 95% CI 0.008–0.104). In the htauE14 cohort, there was a significant group x sex interaction (F 4,224 = 4.312, p = 0.002, η2 p = 0.072, 95% CI 0.022–0.258), with males in the eER and eSTR groups outperforming female counterparts and cE14 males.

To identify coordinated patterns across behavioral measures, we performed a principal component analysis (PCA; Figure S5). PC1 captured the dominant source of variance and was strongly structured by AAV genotype and environmental treatment, reflecting a behavioral activation‐spatial engagement dimension that integrated locomotor activity, exploratory behavior, spatial performance, and avoidance‐related measures. PC2 accounted for substantially less variance and, although reflecting variation across specific behavioral domains including olfactory performance and marble burying, was not systematically structured by AAV genotype or environmental treatment. These results indicate that genotype and environmental experience primarily reshape a common behavioral state spanning activation and spatial engagement, even when effects on individual behavioral assays are modest.

Early enrichment mitigates, whereas late stress exacerbates, pretangle tau pathology

Tau spread in the brainstem of htauE14 rats was observed consistently across groups in regions including the subcoeruleus nucleus, dorsal and median/paramedian raphe, and pontine tegmental nuclei (Figure 2A–C). eER significantly reduced pretangle tau propagation compared to cE14, lSTR, and lER groups (treatment: F 4,112 = 3.746, p = 0.007, η2 p = 0.118, 95% CI 0.011–0.209) (Figure 2D). Of note, initial transduction efficiency in the LC did not differ significantly between groups (Figure S6), indicating that differences in tau spread were not due to variability in viral expression or tau clearance in the LC. Phosphorylation of tau at s262 did not differ across htauE14 groups with or without early‐life treatments (Figure 2E,F). A one‐way ANOVA confirmed the absence of group effects (F 2,8 = 0.26, p = 0.776, η2 p = 0.062, 95% CI 0.00–0.33) (Figure 2F). In contrast, s262 phosphorylated tau was significantly higher in the LC of females compared with males (Welch's t‐test: p = 0.012; Hedges’ g = 1.67; 95% CI 0.14–3.20) (Figure 2G).

snRNA‐seq identifies major hippocampal cell types and regionally distinct neuronal subpopulations

Given that the hippocampus is a primary target of dense LC innervation, 41 plays an essential role in learning and memory, and is highly sensitive to environmental modulation, 42 , 43 it represents a key region in which LC tau pathology may exert profound effects. To investigate how htauE14 expression and environmental factors influence hippocampal cell populations, we performed snRNA‐seq. Quality‐control filtering removed low‐quality nuclei, yielding 243,103 high‐quality nuclei from 36 samples across six experimental groups, both sexes, and three biological replicates per sex. Integration and clustering of the dataset revealed seven major cell types, visualized on a UMAP plot (Figure 4A; Figures S9–S10), with minimal batch effects (Figure S11). The identified clusters included excitatory neurons (Slc17a7+ ), 44 , 45 inhibitory neurons (Gad1+ ), 44 , 45 oligodendrocytes (Mbp+ ), 46 oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (Pcdh15+, Pdgfra+, Vcan+ ), 47 , 48 microglia (Arhgap15+, Inpp5d+, Ctss+ ), 44 , 48 , 49 astrocytes (Slc1a2+, Slc1a3+ ), 44 , 48 , 50 and endothelial cells (Ebf1+ ). 44 , 48 Clusters expressing heterogeneous or ambiguous marker profiles were categorized as low‐quality cells (LQCs). Although Cluster 25 (Unassigned) expressed oligodendrocyte markers, it was excluded from downstream analyses because it was detected in only a single biological replicate. Excitatory neurons represented the largest cell population, followed by oligodendrocytes and inhibitory neurons (Figure 4B), a distribution consistent with transcriptomic profiles reported in AD. 51 Notably, cell‐type proportions were stable across both sexes and treatment conditions (Figure 4C).

Sub‐clustering of excitatory neurons revealed 10 distinct clusters corresponding to various hippocampal subregions, identified using region‐specific gene markers (Figure 4D; Figure S10). 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 Cluster 0 corresponded to the dentate gyrus (DG), defined by expression of Dock10, Prox1, and Sema5a. Cluster 1 represented dorsal/intermediate CA1 (d/iCA1), characterized by Gpc5, Ndst3, Arhgap12, Mpped1, Wfs1, and Fibcd1. Cluster 2 was annotated as CA3 based on expression of Rerg, Nectin3, Cpne4, Grik4, and Mgat4c. Cluster 3 corresponded to the subiculum, marked by Tshz2 and Tox, whereas Cluster 4 exhibited a deep subiculum‐like identity defined by Tle4 and Cntnap4. Cluster 5 displayed a CA1‐like transcriptional profile, expressing Camk2d, Arhgap6, Hcn1, and Pex5l. Cluster 6 could not be annotated confidently due to insufficient marker specificity. Cluster 7 was classified as a ventral CA1‐like population based on selective expression of Ndst4, whereas Cluster 8 represented ventral CA1 (vCA1) neurons characterized by Htr2c expression. Finally, Cluster 9 was identified as mossy cells, marked by Calb2.

Inhibitory neurons were resolved into nine clusters based on established interneuron subtype‐specific markers (Figure 4E; Figure S10). 44 , 54 , 56 , 57 , 58 Cluster 0 was enriched for Meis2 and was annotated as a Meis2‐expressing interneuron population. Cluster 1 expressed Pvalb and was classified as parvalbumin (Pvalb) interneurons, whereas Cluster 2 was defined by Sst expression and annotated as somatostatin (SST) interneurons. Cluster 3 exhibited Cck expression and was classified as Cholecystokinin (CCK) interneurons. Cluster 4 expressed Lamp5 and Nos1, consistent with a Lamp5/Nos1 interneuron identity, whereas Cluster 5 expressed Vip together with Calb2, indicative of a VIP/Calb2 interneuron subtype. Cluster 7 was characterized by Lamp5 and Reln expression, consistent with a Reln‐expressing Lamp5 interneuron population. In contrast, Clusters 6 and 8 showed mixed or ambiguous marker profiles, suggestive of doublets or transcriptional heterogeneity, and were therefore designated as unannotated inhibitory clusters.

Sex‐ and experience‐dependent transcriptomic remodeling of hippocampal neurons and glia induced by LC pretangle tau

To determine how LC‐targeted htauE14 expression and environmental experience shape hippocampal neuronal and glia transcriptomes, we analyzed DEGs and GO BP enrichment relative to cGFP controls across hippocampal subregions and cell types. Major observations are summarized in Table S1.

Excitatory neurons

In excitatory neurons (Figure 5; Figures S12–S13), overall, transcriptional responses to htauE14 were strongly sex‐ and experience dependent. Females exhibited markedly greater transcriptional sensitivity than males, with the most extensive changes observed following eSTR, whereas eER conferred relative transcriptional stability.

In females, htauE14 alone induced robust gene expression changes across clusters C0, C1, and C2, corresponding to the DG, d/iCA1, and CA3, respectively (Figure 5A), with minimal effects in other hippocampal regions. In contrast, cE14 males showed few transcriptional alterations across subregions, diverging from prior reports of male‐biased LC transcriptional responses to htauE14. 32 These earlier findings, including upregulation of Dbh and Th in the male LC, 32 may reflect compensatory increases in noradrenergic synthesis that mitigate downstream hippocampal transcriptional effects.

Early‐life stress markedly amplified htauE14‐associated transcriptional remodeling in both sexes, particularly in DG, d/iCA1, CA3, subiculum (C3‐4), and vCA1 (C7) clusters. However, the magnitude and direction of these changes remained sex biased, with females consistently exhibiting broader DEG profiles. In contrast, eER induced minimal transcriptional alterations, particularly in females, suggesting a protective or buffering effect against tau‐associated dysregulation. Late‐life treatment elicited moderate transcriptomic activation. lSTR produced more modest DEG signatures than eSTR, whereas lER elicited moderate but comparable transcriptional responses across sexes (Figure 5A; Figure S12).

Overlap analyses revealed the greatest DEG convergence between cE14 and eSTR females in DG and CA1, and between eSTR males and females across multiple hippocampal clusters (Figure S13). To further examine regional and temporal structure, we visualized the top DEGs from DG (C0), d/iCA1 (C1), and CA3 (C2) using heatmaps (Figure 5B). These analyses revealed pronounced sex‐ and experience‐dependent transcriptional patterns, particularly under eSTR conditions.

In DG, cE14 and eSTR females shared upregulation of genes associated with synaptic remodeling and translational demand, including C1ql3 and Fau, consistent with coordinated synaptic and proteostatic engagement. In contrast, DG neurons in males remained largely transcriptionally stable under cE14. eSTR elicited largely non‐overlapping DEG sets between sexes, whereas eER females showed minimal transcriptional deviation. Under lSTR, both sexes converged on a small set of commonly regulated genes linked to mitochondrial function (Pacs2, Ankzf1), synaptic organization (Cntnap5a), and RNA processing (Rbm10, Nop56), suggesting engagement of shared homeostatic programs in response to late stress.

A similar pattern emerged in CA1. Late‐life interventions produced overlapping DEG profiles across sexes, with lSTR associated with coordinated regulation of genes involved in mitochondrial metabolism, protein synthesis, and transcriptional control (Nop56, Pacs2, Rbm10, Tcerg1), and lER associated with upregulation of genes linked to mitochondrial function and translational regulation (Htra2, Rps6kb2, Nek5, and Ybx2). In contrast, eSTR induced strongly divergent transcriptional responses between sexes. Females exhibited downregulation of genes involved in neuronal structural integrity (Itabl1, Tm4sf4, and Palld), whereas males showed robust upregulation of genes related to cytoskeletal remodeling (Tuba1b, Tubb4b, Tagln3), synaptic function (Scg5, Nts), mitochondrial metabolism (Slc25a4, Slc25a5, Pgk1), and protein processing (Ubb, Tmtc4, Apt6v0c), suggesting sex‐specific strategies for responding to early‐life challenge.

CA3 largely mirrored DG and CA1, with early‐life experiences driving divergent, sex‐specific transcriptional responses and late‐life interventions yielding more convergent patterns. Notably, a core set of genes (Nop56, Tcerg1, Pacs2, Rbm10, Ankzf1) was consistently regulated across hippocampal subregions under lSTR, defining a region‐invariant transcriptional signature associated with late‐life adaptation. In contrast, eSTR‐induced changes partially overlapped with CA1 signatures, with greater overlap observed in females.

GO enrichment analyses across DG, CA1, and CA3 reinforced these patterns (Figure 5C). In females, htauE14 alone enriched pathways related to mitochondrial energy metabolism, RNA and protein regulation, cellular stress responses, and neurodevelopmental programs. eSTR expanded these effects across sexes and additionally engaged synaptic signaling, plasticity, and learning‐related pathways. In contrast, eER preserved transcriptomic stability and yielded no significant GO enrichment. lSTR in females selectively modulated pathways related to myelination and axon ensheathment, whereas lER produced no detectable pathway enrichment across regions.

Together, these findings demonstrate that LC‐derived pretangle tau and environmental experience exert region‐, sex‐, and timing‐dependent effects on hippocampal excitatory neurons. Early‐life stress selectively engages extensive, sex‐biased transcriptional programs, particularly in females; whereas late‐life interventions preferentially recruit convergent, subtle transcriptional responses consistent with homeostatic regulation across hippocampal subregions.

Inhibitory neurons

Inhibitory neurons exhibited more limited, but still experience‐ and sex‐dependent transcriptional responses to htauE14 and environmental manipulations (Figure 6A,B; Figures S14–S15). Compared with excitatory populations, htauE14 alone produced minimal transcriptional changes in inhibitory neuron clusters, with the exception of modest, female‐specific alterations in Pvalb + (C1), Sst + (C2), and Cck + (C3) interneurons. In these clusters, DEGs were predominantly enriched for mitochondrial energy metabolism, including consistent upregulation of electron transport chain components (Nd4, Cox1, Cox2, and Cox3).

eSTR induced broader transcriptional remodeling across inhibitory subtypes, affecting multiple interneuron classes in both sexes (C1–4), with additional male‐specific effects in Vip + (C5) neurons. Beyond mitochondrial metabolism, eSTR‐associated DEGs were enriched for synaptic regulation, calcium signaling, and protein homeostasis pathways. Autophagy‐ and chaperone‐related genes (Hsp90ab1, Hspa8, Hsp90aa1, Syt11, Slc25a4) were consistently upregulated across inhibitory clusters, indicating coordinated engagement of metabolic and proteostatic mechanisms.

In contrast, eER and late‐life interventions produced minimal transcriptional changes in inhibitory populations. Modest effects were observed in select clusters, primarily in males under lSTR and lER. Notably, several genes regulated in excitatory neurons under lSTR, including those involved in RNA processing (Nop56, Tcerg1, Rbm10), mitochondrial function (Pacs2, Apoo), and calcium‐dependent synaptic signaling (Cpne8), were similarly regulated in inhibitory neurons.

Overall, paralleling excitatory neurons, htauE14 induced mitochondrial‐related alterations in females, which were extended to both sexes by eSTR and accompanied by engagement of additional pathways, including synaptic signaling and protein homeostasis. Inhibitory neurons recruited partially overlapping transcriptional programs with excitatory neurons in response to lSTR but displayed more constrained, cell type–specific responses with a male bias. Consistent with excitatory neuron findings, GO analyses revealed few enriched pathways under late‐life conditions, suggesting limited transcriptional impact at this stage.

Non‐neuronal cell types

Transcriptomic patterns in non‐neuronal populations partially paralleled those observed in neuronal clusters (Figure 7A–C; Figure S16). LC‐targeted htauE14 expression preferentially affected glial cells in females, with pronounced changes in oligodendrocytes and astrocytes and more modest effects in microglia. In females, cE14 enriched GO pathways related to mitochondrial energy metabolism and RNA/protein regulation in both oligodendrocytes and astrocytes.

eSTR induced robust transcriptional responses across microglia, oligodendrocytes, and astrocytes in both sexes and expanded the affected gene sets to include pathways related to synaptic signaling and neurite structural remodeling. In microglia, cE14 and eSTR females shared a small set of DEGs characterized by reduced expression of inflammatory lipid signaling (Ltc4s) and translational genes (Fau1) and increased expression of cytoskeletal regulators (Map1b), consistent with enhanced surveillance and structural engagement rather than overt inflammatory activation.

In oligodendrocytes, eSTR was associated with consistent downregulation of genes involved in membrane‐cytoskeletal stabilization (Sgcd) across sexes, suggesting increased myelin vulnerability under stress. Astrocytes similarly exhibited early stress‐associated structural remodeling (Basp1) accompanied by reduced expression of genes involved in ionic homeostasis (Atp1b1).

In contrast, eER elicited minimal glial transcriptional responses. Despite limited DEG numbers, oligodendrocytes in both sexes showed increased expression of genes associated with myelin remodeling and membrane maintenance (Bcas1, Sh3glb2, Jhy), consistent with adaptive structural support superimposed on htauE14 expression. GO analysis revealed no significant enrichment following eER or late‐life interventions.

Late‐life interventions produced modest transcriptional effects in oligodendrocytes and astrocytes, with more evident changes in males. lSTR was associated with upregulation of genes involved in fatty acid oxidation (Acadvl), translational engagement (Nop56), and ER‐mitochondrial coordination (Pacs2) in oligodendrocytes, indicating a metabolically demanding, stress‐responsive state. In contrast, lER promoted expression of cytoskeletal (Sorbs2) and transcriptional regulatory genes (Son) in oligodendrocytes and astrocytes, respectively, in both sexes, consistent with enhanced structural stability and supportive glial function.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that pretangle tau pathology confined to the LC is sufficient to drive widespread behavioral and molecular alterations through sex‐ and experience‐dependent mechanisms (Figure 8), even in the absence of amyloid pathology or cortical tau tangles. By selectively expressing pseudophosphorylated human tau (htauE14) in LC neurons, we model an early, preclinical stage of pathology that precedes classical neurodegenerative hallmarks. This targeted manipulation impaired spatial learning, disrupted olfactory discrimination, and increased anxiety‐like behavior, phenotypes characteristics of prodromal AD. 59 , 60 , 61 , 62 These behavioral effects are accompanied by LC fiber degeneration in projection targets such as the hippocampus and piriform cortex, along with evidence of peripheral and neuroinflammation. 30 , 31 , 63 Transcriptomic analysis revealed pronounced sex divergence: males exhibited greater regulation of synaptic and ion homeostasis pathways in the LC, 32 whereas females showed heightened transcriptional vulnerability in the hippocampus, involving metabolic and mitochondrial pathways. Collectively, these findings reinforce the view that LC dysfunction is a critical upstream event in AD pathogenesis, capable of reshaping distributed brain circuits long before overt cortical tau deposition.

Life experience strongly and differentially modulated the impact of LC pretangle tau pathology. eER broadly reduced tau propagation and LC neuroinflammation across sexes and conferred molecular and behavioral benefits, including increased hippocampal BDNF expression and enhanced olfactory learning specifically in males. These findings are consistent with prior evidence that enrichment slows tau progression and supports cognition, 64 and suggest that early enrichment promotes resilience through partially sex‐divergent mechanisms. In contrast, eSTR sensitized animals to dysfunction, exacerbating hippocampal transcriptional instability and increasing anxiety‐like behavior. This pattern parallels human studies linking early adversity to long‐lasting alterations in stress regulation, neuroimmune signaling, and hippocampal structure. 65 , 66 Notably, eSTR also enhanced olfactory learning in males, indicating that although stress heightens vulnerability in hippocampal circuits, it may in parallel induce compensatory, sex‐dependent reprogramming within sensory pathways.

Late‐life experiences exerted more modest and distinct effects. Adult enrichment reduced anxiety, enhanced exploratory behavior in htauE14 rats, and improved spatial learning in general, but engaged genes largely distinct from those recruited by eER. Conversely, adult stress worsened LC neuronal health yet produced limited behavioral consequences relative to caged htauE14 controls. Limited enrichment in GO analyses indicated that late‐life interventions primarily activated subtle, compensatory transcriptional programs rather than broad pathway reorganization, suggesting that shared homeostatic responses can be re‐engaged post‐developmentally in a largely sex‐independent manner.

A central insight of this study is the pronounced sex dependence of hippocampal molecular responses to LC pretangle tau. Despite broadly similar behavioral impairments in males and females, hippocampal transcriptional alterations were strongly female biased, particularly within excitatory neurons and glia. Females showed enhanced regulation of metabolic, mitochondrial, and RNA/protein homeostasis pathways, whereas males exhibited comparatively restrained hippocampal responses. This dissociation between behavioral and molecular outcomes suggests that sex differences in neuromodulatory tone and compensatory capacity shape early disease trajectories without necessarily producing immediate behavioral divergence. In males, previously reported upregulation of LC noradrenergic biosynthetic enzymes 32 may help stabilize hippocampal circuit function despite limited transcriptional engagement, effectively buffering behavior in the face of emerging pathology. In contrast, females exhibit more extensive hippocampal transcriptional reprogramming, particularly in metabolic and stress‐related pathways, potentially reflecting a greater reliance on molecular compensation to maintain comparable behavioral output. Thus, similar behavioral performance across sexes may arise from distinct, sex‐specific adaptive strategies rather than equivalent underlying circuit states. These findings align with epidemiological evidence of greater AD risk and faster progression in women and support models in which sex‐specific metabolic and stress responses confer differential preclinical vulnerability. 13 , 16 , 67

The molecular–behavioral disconnect likely reflects several converging factors. Transcriptional alterations may precede overt behavioral impairment, compensatory mechanisms may transiently preserve performance despite accumulating molecular stress, and standard behavioral assays may lack the resolution to detect subtle or circuit‐specific dysfunctions. Consistent with this, PCA revealed that genotype and environmental experience primarily reshaped a distributed behavioral activation–spatial engagement state rather than producing large, isolated effects in individual tasks. An important limitation is that moderate sample sizes in some experimental groups reduced power to detect small behavioral effects. Together, these considerations highlight the need for longitudinal studies and more sensitive circuit‐level and behavioral approaches to capture early, sex‐dependent trajectories of dysfunction.

Transcriptomic profiling of the hippocampus provides critical insight into how sex and life history interact to shape the molecular architecture underlying memory encoding. Life experience emerged as a major modifier of LC tau‐driven effects, with early‐life exposures exerting particularly durable influence. Early enrichment largely stabilized transcriptional states across hippocampal cell types, attenuating htauE14‐associated alterations and limiting pathway‐level engagement. This stabilization occurred without strong induction of new molecular programs, suggesting preservation of cellular homeostasis rather than compensatory overactivation. Such findings are consistent with the concept of cognitive reserve and with experimental evidence that enrichment during development confers long‐lasting resilience to neurodegenerative insults. 64 In contrast, early‐life stress did not simply exacerbate pathology but selectively reprogrammed transcriptional responsiveness in a sex‐dependent manner, most prominently in females. Rather than inducing indiscriminate dysfunction, early stress engaged coordinated metabolic, synaptic, and regulatory pathways, indicative of heightened molecular plasticity. This pattern is consistent with developmental programming models in which early adversity establishes long‐term biases in cellular reactivity that may initially support adaptation but increase susceptibility to later neuromodulatory decline. 65 , 66 Furthermore, early‐life adversity in the first 3 years of human life has been linked to reduced hippocampal volume, including DG, CA1, and CA3. 68 These structural vulnerabilities may amplify the impact of later insults and increase AD risk.

Late‐life interventions produced a markedly different molecular signature. Across neuronal and glial populations, late stress and late enrichment were associated with modest, more convergent transcriptional changes across sexes and regions, accompanied by minimal GO pathway enrichment. This pattern suggests that late‐life experiences primarily engage fine‐tuned, homeostatic regulatory mechanisms rather than large‐scale transcriptional reorganization. Clinically, this distinction is important: it implies that although late‐life interventions may not reverse early developmental programming, they can still stabilize cellular metabolism, protein homeostasis, and organelle coordination, thereby supporting resilience. These findings parallel clinical trial data showing modest but reproducible cognitive benefits of lifestyle interventions in mid‐ to late life, despite limited effects on established AD biomarkers. 69 , 70

Cell‐type–specific analyses further clarify how LC dysfunction and experience interact to shape hippocampal vulnerability. Consistent with findings from early‐stage AD, 70 excitatory neurons emerged as the most transcriptionally responsive population, particularly under early stress, consistent with their high metabolic demand and central role in plasticity and memory encoding. Inhibitory interneurons were comparatively spared, supporting models in which inhibitory dysfunction arises later in AD progression rather than during preclinical stages. 71 Glial populations, however, showed experience‐sensitive transcriptional responses that complement neuronal findings. Astrocytes and oligodendrocytes exhibited alterations consistent with shifts in metabolic support, structural maintenance, and myelin integrity in mediating AD vulnerability, 21 , 72 whereas microglial responses favored cytoskeletal remodeling and surveillance rather than overt inflammatory activation. These patterns suggest that early LC dysfunction reshapes neuron–glia interactions in the hippocampus in ways that may destabilize circuits, a state increasingly recognized as relevant to early AD. 73

Of note, these transcriptional alterations occurred in the absence of detectable transneuronal tau spread to hippocampal or piriform cortex neurons, despite clear LC axonal degeneration in these regions. 30 , 31 This indicates that disrupted neuromodulatory signaling, rather than direct tau toxicity, drives much of the downstream dysfunction. This interpretation is supported by prior work showing that restoration of LC activity preserves axonal integrity and reverses behavioral deficits in this model. 30 The LC exerts widespread control over cortical and limbic circuits through norepinephrine and co‐transmitters that regulate synaptic plasticity, metabolic homeostasis, and stress responsivity. 74 , 75 Early compromise of this system may therefore represent a unifying mechanism linking stress, sex differences, and early AD vulnerability.

From a translational perspective, these findings emphasize the importance of life‐course approaches to AD risk. Early experiences appear to exert disproportionate influence on long‐term molecular resilience, with enrichment conferring protection and stress biasing circuits toward heightened reactivity. These enduring effects likely involve epigenetic mechanisms that shape transcriptional potential across cell types and persist into adulthood. 73 At the same time, the modest but convergent effects of late‐life interventions suggest that opportunities for promoting resilience remain even after early insults, particularly through stabilization of metabolic and proteostatic processes.

In summary, this study identifies the LC as a critical upstream node in early AD pathogenesis and demonstrates that pretangle tau‐induced neuromodulatory dysfunction interacts with sex and life experience to shape hippocampal vulnerability across neurons and glia. By linking early‐life adversity, sex‐dependent molecular responsiveness, and late‐life homeostatic regulation within a single experimental framework, these findings provide a mechanistic bridge between developmental risk factors and later‐life neurodegenerative disease. Integrating neuromodulatory biology with life‐history variables may therefore be essential for understanding individual differences in AD susceptibility and for designing effective, stage‐specific intervention strategies.
