Structural dynamics of the π-stacking network governing cofactor-substrate cooperativity of SbSOMT methyltransferase
Authors: Kah Chee Pow, Nan Zhang, Ming Yan, Xiaorong Wang, Andy C. W. Lui, Clive Lo, Quan Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SAM-dependent methyltransferases are enzymes that catalyze the transfer of a methyl group from a cofactor to a substrate through an ordered or random sequential bi-bi mechanism. However, the structural dynamics governing binding cooperativity between the cofactor and substrate remain understudied. In this study, we demonstrate that SbSOMT, a plant O-methyltransferase, exhibits bilateral positive cooperativity between the cofactor and substrate, except the unproductive SbSOMT-SAM-pterostilbene complex. Furthermore, SbSOMT displayed substrate-binding kinetics that shift in response to the nature of bound-cofactor. Sinefungin-bound SbSOMT exhibited positive cooperativity primarily attributed to an increased substrate association rate constant (kon), whereas SAH-bound SbSOMT displayed positive cooperativity driven primarily by a decreased dissociation rate constant (koff). Structural analysis implies that these cooperativity switch and divergent binding kinetics stem from the interactions between the cofactor and substrate at the methyl binding site. Integrating structural insights reveals that a dynamic W279 π-stacking network governs this cooperativity. Upon binding of the first ligand, H196, W279, and H282 rearrange to form a π-stacking network, in which W279 serves as the essential central plane that also stacks with the substrate. Accordingly, W279A mutagenesis substantially impaired the substrate affinity, cooperativity and enzymatic activity.

Introduction

SAM-dependent methyltransferases are ubiquitous enzymes that catalyze methyl group transfer through an ordered or random sequential bi-bi mechanism1,2. In a random sequential bi-bi mechanism, the methyltransferase forms a productive ternary complex by recruiting the methyl-donating cofactor, S-adenosyl methionine (SAM), and the methyl-accepting substrate in random binding order. Cofactor-substrate cooperativity arises when the binding of the first ligand alters the conformational landscape of the enzyme and thereby modifying the binding kinetics of the subsequent ligand.

In several methyltransferases, this cooperativity has been exploited for drug discovery, specifically in designing cofactor-dependent cooperative inhibitors with amplified affinity or selectivity under specific microenvironments3–7. As a fundamental property, cooperativity can also be harnessed to modulate enzyme performance. Methyltransferases are attractive biocatalysts for late-stage modifications of fine chemicals due to their diverse substrate spectra, high chemo-selectivity, and cofactor expandability8,9. The majority of methyltransferases are classified under Class I SAM-dependent methyltransferase superfamily which conserve a seven-β-strand Rossmann fold as the SAM binding domain10–12. Notably, the homologs under plant O-methyltransferase subgroup have revealed unprecedented catalytic capabilities, including pericyclic, redox, and diazo insertion reactions13–17. Despite their physiological importance and commercial value, the underlying mechanism of cofactor-substrate cooperativity in methyltransferases is not profoundly studied.

Our previous study established the structural basis of substrate regioselectivity in Sorghum bicolor stilbene O-methyltransferase (SbSOMT)18. In this study, we further employed binding kinetics analysis and X-ray crystallography to decipher the structure-function relationship of cofactor-substrate cooperativity in SbSOMT.

Results

SbSOMT exhibits bilateral and positive cofactor-substrate cooperativity in general

To elucidate the cofactor-substrate cooperativity, we employed isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) to profile the binding affinities of substrate and cofactor analogs and to calculate the cooperativity (σ). Note that SbSOMT catalyzes the sequential methylation of resveratrol (RSV) to produce monomethylated pinostilbene (PNS) and dimethylated pterostilbene (PTR), converting one molecule of SAM to S-adenosyl homocysteine (SAH) per transfer (Fig. S1a)18. The affinity profile of these ligands and the inhibitor sinefungin (SFG) was probed to capture the cooperativity at various ligand combinations. SFG is utilized as a SAM analog because the binding properties and cooperativity effects of SFG and SAM are more similar than those of SAM and SAH. Unlike SAH, SFG contains a protonatable amino group in place of the transferable methyl group of SAM (Fig. S1b), allowing it to trigger transition-state-like properties when bound to the methyltransferase19–21.

The binding affinity profile towards RSV (Table 1 and Fig. 1a) has indeed revealed distinct differences between SFG-saturating and SAH-saturating conditions. In the SAH-saturating condition, the titration was monophasic and fitted with affinity (KD) of 197 nM; yielding positive cooperativity (σ) of 12.03. Conversely, the thermogram of the SFG-saturating condition (Fig. 1b) exhibited biphasic binding kinetics. The titration curve was successfully fit using the “two sets of sites” model, with the stoichiometry (N) of the two sites converging at 0.39. The shift in stoichiometry implies that SFG-bound SbSOMT exists as a dimer, and the biphasic thermogram implies that the protomers contain independent binding sites with different binding kinetics. These two sites demonstrated positive cooperativity at different scales and exhibited divergent binding enthalpy. In one protomer, the binding was endothermic (∆H of 2.63 kcal/mol) with RSV affinity strengthened approximately 3-fold to 818 nM. Conversely, the exothermic binding (∆H of -5.48 kcal/mol) was fitted with a KD of 24.2 nM and σ of 97.93; yielding the strongest affinity and cooperativity observed. The binding affinity profile for SAH also displayed a positive cooperativity trend upon saturation with RSV or PTR (Fig. 1c). These profiles imply that SbSOMT operates via a random sequential bi-bi mechanism, in which either the cofactor or the substrate can bind first to trigger positive cooperativity for the binding of the second ligand. Notably, occupancy of the methyl binding site—the cavity that accommodates the transferable methyl moiety of SAM—further enhances the degree of positive cooperativity, as suggested by the results for the SbSOMT-SFG-RSV and SbSOMT-SAH-PTR complexes. In these cases, the amino moiety of SFG and the methyl moiety of PTR are the structural equivalents to the transferable methyl moiety of SAM, suggesting the binding kinetics of SbSOMT-SAM-RSV complex may also exhibit a strong and bilateral positive cooperativity.

Unproductive combination of SAM and PTR leads to negative cooperativity

Conversely, attempting to overcrowd the methyl binding site with two methyl groups negatively affects the cofactor-substrate cooperativity. The cooperativity of SbSOMT switched to negative when the binding combinations of SAM-PTR and SFG-PTR were examined (Table 1 and Fig. 1d–f). This negative cooperativity is relatively weak in the case of SAM binding in PTR-saturating condition (Fig. 1f), where the affinity was weakened to 139 μM (σ of 0.81). Others showed negative cooperativity ranging from 0.40 to 0.45. Notably, the binding of PTR towards SFG-bound SbSOMT yielded a halved stoichiometry (N of 0.45) and monophasic thermogram (Fig. 1b). This implies that SFG-bound SbSOMT also exhibits asymmetric dimeric characteristics regarding PTR binding. The result implies that the SFG-bound SbSOMT dimer only binds to one PTR molecule, while the second substrate binding pocket remains available for reactive substrates like RSV and PNS. Conversely, SAM-bound SbSOMT showed a stoichiometry of 0.91, indicating an absence of dimer asymmetry. We speculate that the observed asymmetry may represent a transition-state characteristic of SbSOMT triggered by SFG. Given that the cofactor and substrate binding sites of two protomers are distantly located (Fig. S2), this dimer asymmetry is likely propagated through the conformational shifts in the polypeptide backbone.

In short, SbSOMT cooperativity is selective and discriminates against unproductive combinations of SAM and PTR, likely via steric hindrance at the methyl binding site. This selectivity may also facilitate the discrimination between the unproductive 3-methoxy group and productive 5-hydroxy group of PNS, thereby promoting the efficient methylation of PNS in SbSOMT.

Adenosine and 5-methylthioadenosine trigger cofactor-dependent positive cooperativity

The cooperativity effects of cofactor fragments were also examined to identify the specific functional groups capable of triggering positive cooperativity. The affinity of RSV was measured via ITC in the presence of 1 mM of each cofactor fragment: adenine (ADE), adenosine (ADN), 5-methylthioadenosine (MTA) or methionine (MET). While these high concentrations do not necessarily ensure complete saturation, they are expected to skew the equilibrium towards the fragment-bound state.

In the presence of ADN and MET, SbSOMT showed positive cooperativity of 3.46 and 16.81, respectively, whereas other conditions resulted in negative cooperativity (Table 1 and Fig. 1a). These findings identify ADN as the minimal functional group for triggering positive cooperativity in SbSOMT. Additionally, these results suggest that these ubiquitous metabolites may play a physiological role in modulating the in vivo activity and cooperativity of methyltransferases, pondering the existence of a broader protein-metabolite regulatory network.

Insights into the conformational entropy correlated with SbSOMT cofactor-substrate cooperativity

The global thermodynamics recorded by ITC can provide insights to the cooperativity-associated conformational entropy (∆Sconf). Although the global thermodynamics is influenced by multiple factors (e.g., protein-ligand interaction, dilution of titrant, solvation), studies have shown that the extracted entropic contribution (-T∆S) can be a reliable reference to conformational entropy22,23.

As shown in Table 1, the ITC titrations of SbSOMT with SFG and RSV showed interesting thermodynamic behavior, providing insights to the thermodynamics of the formation of productive SbSOMT-SAM-RSV complex. The binding of RSV to apo SbSOMT was entropically unfavorable (-T∆S of 3.98 kcal/mol) while the binding of RSV to SbSOMT-SFG complex was entropically favorable. The endothermic and exothermic binding of RSV to the two dimer monomers showed -T∆S of -10.90 kcal/mol and -4.92 kcal/mol, respectively. Surprisingly, the binding of RSV to the SbSOMT-SAH complex revealed an even more unfavorable entropy (-T∆S of 5.26 kcal/mol) than its binding to apo SbSOMT. The other way around, the binding of SFG to apo SbSOMT was also entropically unfavorable (-T∆S of 5.43 kcal/mol) while the entropy of the binding of SFG to SbSOMT-PTR complex was increased (-T∆S of 2.74 kcal/mol). Importantly, the entropy of the binding of SFG to SbSOMT-RSV complex was increased and switched to favorable entropy (-T∆S of -5.41 kcal/mol).

These data reveal that the binding of the first ligand, either RSV or SFG, pays the entropic penalty for ligand and polypeptide rigidification, while the binding of the second ligand is entropically favorable. The nature of cofactor and substrate analogs was also shown to strongly affect the thermodynamic quantities.

SFG and SAH drive positive cooperativity via divergent binding kinetics

Biolayer interferometry (BLI) was employed as an orthogonal method to investigate ligand-binding kinetics by determining the association (kon) and dissociation (koff) rate constants. This analysis successfully resolved the binding kinetics of substrate analogs and the resultant cofactor-dependent cooperativity. Although the binding affinities measured by BLI were shifted proportionally into the nanomolar range, the cooperativity effects were consistently recapitulated (Fig. 1g–n).

Strikingly, SFG and SAH induced positive cooperativity through divergent RSV-binding kinetics. Under SFG-saturating condition, the kon was increased greatly (8.40-fold) to 5.95 × 106 M-1s-1, and the koff was decreased (2.22-fold) to 1.31 × 10-2 s-1(Fig. 1g) relative to the apo condition. This suggests that the SAM-bound SbSOMT can rapidly form a productive ternary complex. In contrast, under SAH-saturating condition, the kon increased by only 1.15-fold to 8.13 × 105 M-1s-1, while the koff decreased substantially (7.26-fold) to 4.01 × 10-3 s-1. This trend remains consistent when comparing SAH-bound SbSOMT to the apo form, where the decrease in koff (12.82-fold) predominated over the increase of kon (1.76-fold). Interestingly, the koff of SAM-bound SbSOMT for PTR was 2.38-fold greater than that of the apo enzyme, implying that SAM binding promotes the release of the PTR product.

This BLI analysis further elicits how SbSOMT modulates kon and koff to enhance its catalytic efficiency. While adenosine binding is sufficient to trigger positive cooperativity, molecular interplay at the methyl binding site allows SbSOMT to discriminate between various cofactor-substrate combinations, resulting in a cooperativity switch and the modulation of ligand-binding kinetics. These findings have important implications for drug design, as the increased kon and decreased koff can lead to distinct pharmacological profiles and residency times.

Crystal structures of SbSOMT at different conformational states

Previously, our group solved the crystal structures of SbSOMT in complex with various substrate analogs to decipher the structural basis of substrate recognition18. Here, we solved the apo and SAH-bound SbSOMT crystal structures and compared them with the RSV-bound structure (PDB: 7VB8) to elucidate the dynamic transitions of the enzyme conformation across different states and to uncover the underlying mechanism of positive cooperativity.

The apo SbSOMT was solved at 2.04 Å resolution, with a large asymmetric unit containing three SbSOMT dimers (Fig. S3a). All six chains exhibit an open conformation of the SAM binding domain, resulting in increased solvent accessibility of the substrate binding pocket (Fig. 2a, b and Fig. S3b). Notably, the SAM binding domains of Chains E and F lack crystal contacts (Fig. 2c), which explains the wider opening of these domains (Fig. S3b) as well as the relatively high average B factor and relatively poor electron density (Fig. 2d and Fig. S3c). The crystal packing also suggests two potential higher-order oligomeric states of apo SbSOMT. A symmetrical dimer-of-dimers tetramer is observed, in which two dimers reciprocally clamp the N-terminal loop (residues 69–76) of the opposing dimer via the opening of the SAM binding domain (Fig. S3d). Additionally, a partial inter-dimer β-sheet is formed between the sixth β-strand of the SAM binding domain’s Rossmann fold core (residues 361–367) and the transient β-hairpin (residues 96–104) within the N-terminal region (Fig. S3e).

On the other hand, the SAH-bound SbSOMT binary complex (solved at 2.90 Å) adopts a closed conformation (Fig. 2b and Fig S4a, b). Upon SAH binding, SbSOMT exhibits a tighter closure of the SAM binding domain compared to the previously reported RSV-bound structure (Fig. 2b). SAH is bound to both chains and occludes the substrate pocket, albeit with subtle variations (Fig. 2e and Fig. S4c). In both chains, the adenine moiety is sandwiched between L245 and M265 while forming a π-π interaction with F176; the ribose moiety forms the conserved bidentate bond with D244 and an additional hydrogen bond with H190. In Chain A, the distance between the SAH sulfur center and D283 is measured at 3.172 Å—approximately 0.9 Å closer than in Chain B. Instead, the SAH molecule in Chain B is stretched by the hydrogen bonds at the amide end, specifically between the carboxyl group and the side chains of S197 and K278.

Notably, the SAH-bound SbSOMT reveals a region of weak electron density in the vicinity of the bound cofactor, involving a hydrophobic network composed of seven residues (M143, F149, Y171, F176, Y178, L179 and L185) that adopt flexible conformations (Fig. 2f and Fig. S4c). Conversely, these residues exhibit stable conformations and well-resolved density in apo and RSV-bound SbSOMT structures (Fig. 2f). Insights from the homologs suggest that the π-π interaction between F176 and the adenine moiety of SAH may contribute to the increased flexibility of the region. The F176 is a conserved residue essential for enzymatic activity of plant O-methyltransferase24. Structurally, the π-π interaction between adenine and phenylalanine is shown to occur at shorter distances than those observed in SAH-bound SbSOMT25,26 (Fig. 2e and Fig. S5a, b). Meanwhile, the crystal structures of other homologs exhibit unmodeled regions corresponding to this hydrophobic network, effectively expanding the accessibility of the substrate binding pocket that sits behind this hydrophobic network (Fig. S5c, d)27,28. Hence, in SAH-bound SbSOMT, the flexibility of the hydrophobic network likely facilitates maintaining the accessibility of the substrate binding pocket and mitigating the steric occlusion imposed by the bound cofactor, thereby allowing substrate binding towards cofactor-bound SbSOMT and supporting the random sequential bi-bi mechanism.

Computational model of SbSOMT ternary complex

To investigate the cofactor-substrate interactions at the methyl binding site, we computed a ternary complex model via superimposition. Using the SAH-bound SbSOMT as a template, whole-chain superimposition was performed with RSV-bound SbSOMT (PDB: 7VB8; RMSD: 0.756 Å) and PTR-bound SbSOMT (PDB: 7WAS; RMSD: 0.775 Å). Additionally, a partial superimposition of the seven-β-strand motif within SAM binding domain was conducted with the SAM-bound PaOMT9 ternary complex (PDB: 8ZN0; RMSD: 0.534 Å)29.

The model reveals that the methyl binding site is the only contact point between the cofactor and substrate, and is circumscribed by F176, F189, M193, W279, H282 and D283 (Fig. 3a). Within this site, the cofactor-substrate interactions appear feasible and likely contribute to the observed cooperativity effects. The modeled ligands exhibit no major clashes to the template polypeptide except a clash between I144 and substrate analogs (Fig. 3b). As for the cofactor-substrate interactions, the binding conformations of SAM and PTR result in a clashing distance of 1.70 Å between their respective methyl groups, indicating significant steric hindrance in this orientation. The steric conflict is relieved when SAM is replaced by SAH, which aligns with the observed switch in cooperativity. Together, these findings strongly suggest that steric hindrance drives the decreased affinity of SAM for PTR- or SFG-bound SbSOMT, and vice versa. Conversely, the transferable methyl moiety of SAM and the 3-OH moiety of RSV are positioned within 2.50 Å, a distance conducive to catalysis (Fig. 3b). Overall, these structural insights highlight that the cofactor-substrate interactions at the methyl binding site play a crucial role in modulating the cooperativity switch and ligand-binding kinetics of SbSOMT.

W279 π-stacking network governs cofactor-substrate cooperativity of SbSOMT

To decipher the structure-function relationship between conformational dynamics and cooperativity, we compared SbSOMT structures across different conformational states. In the apo state, the SAM binding domain adopts an open conformation in which H196 and Y311 sequester W279 from the binding pocket and form a transient hydrogen bond, as observed in Chain F (Fig. 4c). Conversely, in both SAH-bound and RSV-bound structures, the SAM binding domain exhibits a closed conformation, in which W279 becomes exposed to form a coordinated π-stacking network (Fig. 4a, d). The exposed W279 indole ring serves as a central plane for four π-stacking interactions. As exemplified by the RSV-bound SbSOMT (Fig. 4d), at the exposed façade, the pyrrole ring stacks with catalytic H282, while the benzene ring stacks with the substrate’s A ring at the C2 position; this arrangement facilitates the interaction between the Nε2 of H282 and the 3-OH of RSV. On the rear face, the benzene ring stacks with H196. Furthermore, in an alternative RSV-bound structure (PDB: 7WAQ), the flexible K278 forms a transient cation-π stacking interaction with the rear face of W279 (Fig. 4e).

This π-stacking network is linked, both directly and indirectly, to the closure of SAM binding domain. The H196-W279 interaction directly bridges the N-terminal core with the SAM binding domain. Indirectly, the bound substrate facilitates domain closure by interacting with the N-terminal binding pocket as well as residues H282 and W279 within the SAM binding domain. We hypothesize that the conformational transition mediated by the W279 π-stacking network, along with the transient H196-Y311 hydrogen bond, creates the energy barrier that differentiates the conformational landscapes for first and second ligand-binding events.

W279A mutation impairs cooperativity and enzymatic activity of SbSOMT

To validate the functional role of the W279 π-stacking network, we substituted W279 with alanine and assessed the resulting binding kinetics and enzymatic activities. The binding affinity profile of the W279A mutant revealed a complete loss of positive cooperativity and a significant impairment of RSV affinity (Fig. 4f and Table S1). Under SFG-saturating condition, the RSV binding exhibited a monophasic thermogram and a KD (27.6 μM) comparable to that of apo condition (33.1 μM) (Table 1). In contrast, the SFG affinity of W279A was stronger in the apo condition (0.88 μM) and comparable to that of RSV-bound wild-type SbSOMT. This impaired RSV affinity indicates that the W279-mediated π-stacking interactions are critical for substrate binding. As expected, the enzymatic activity of W279A mutant was also significantly impaired (Fig. 4g). The W279A reaction required a 10-fold higher enzyme concentration and a 32-fold longer incubation period to achieve a yield comparable to that of the wild type. Together, the binding and enzymatic profiles of the W279A mutant confirm that the W279 π-stacking network governs the positive cooperativity of SbSOMT.

Discussion

In this study, we integrated biochemical and structural analyses to elucidate the intrinsic mechanism by which SbSOMT governs cofactor-substrate cooperativity. We identified that SbSOMT exhibits bilateral and selective positive cooperativity that is driven by the dynamic W279 π-stacking network. Furthermore, the molecular interplay at the methyl binding site substantially modulates both cooperativity switch and ligand-binding kinetics of SbSOMT.

Based on our findings, we propose a structure-function relationship between the dynamics of W279 π-stacking network and the cofactor-substrate cooperativity of SbSOMT. In the apo state, the SAM binding domain of SbSOMT adopts an open conformation, in which W279 is sequestered by H196 and Y311. The binding of cofactor—specifically the π-π interaction between F176 and the adenine moiety—triggers the closure of the SAM binding domain and the rearrangement of H196, W279, and H282 into a functional π-stacking network. The F176-adenine interaction also disturbs the hydrophobic network (comprising M143, F149, Y171, F176, Y178, L179 and L185), destabilizing the local secondary structure. This conformational shift exposes the substrate binding pocket and W279, priming the enzyme for subsequent substrate association. Similarly, substrate binding triggers domain closure and π-stacking network formation through direct interaction with W279. Closure of the SAM binding domain reorients cofactor-interacting residues across the domains, optimizing the binding geometry for subsequent binding of cofactor. As a result, the conformational landscape of first-ligand-bound states exhibits a stronger affinity for the subsequent cognate ligand compared to the apo enzyme. Collectively, our biochemical and structural data strongly suggest that SbSOMT operates via a random sequential bi-bi mechanism.

Among SbSOMT homologs, we identified four plant O-methyltransferases (InterPro: IPR016461) within PDB—specifically LpOMT130, FaOMT25, S9OMT31 and PSMT126 —that conserve the W279 residue of SbSOMT (Fig. S7). Notably, W266 in FaOMT and W251 in S9OMT undergo rearrangement upon cofactor binding, suggesting the potential for tryptophan-mediated cooperativity (Fig. S7b, c). However, the specific π-stacking network motif of SbSOMT is fully conserved only in FaOMT (via residues H183, W266 and H269). In PSMT1, the corresponding W293 maintains similar conformations across the apo, cofactor-bound, and ternary complex states (Fig. S7d). The inconsistencies imply that the W279 π-stacking network of SbSOMT is not a conserved motif among plant O-methyltransferases. In other words, the π-stacking network motif could be harnessed to exploit the cooperativity effects for tuning and enhancing the enzymatic performance of other homologs. Furthermore, these variations suggest the existence of cofactor-substrate cooperativity governed by alternative interaction networks and divergent residue combinations.

The cofactor-substrate cooperativity in SbSOMT showcases a bilateral and positive cooperativity that is driven by a dynamic π-stacking network and modulated by molecular interplay at the methyl binding site. Our findings provide structural principles for harnessing cooperativity for enzyme engineering and drug design.

Methods

Bacterial strain and culture conditions

Escherichia coli strains were cultured in 2xYT medium (16 g/L tryptone, 10 g/L yeast extract, and 5 g/L NaCl) supplemented with appropriate antibiotics in a 37 °C orbital shaker, in general. E. coli DH5α was used for molecular cloning and sub-cloning purpose while E. coli BL21-CodonPlus (DE3)-RIL was used for the purpose of recombinant protein overexpression.

Molecular cloning, recombinant protein overexpression, and purification

Plasmid construct, pH6SbSOMT, was constructed in previous study18, comprising pET-N-His-TEV vector backbone and SbSOMT(2-377). Mutagenesis of SbSOMT, namely W279A mutant, was constructed via ClonExpress MultiS One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme) using a single PCR amplicon from designated primers (W279A Forward Primer:5’- GCAGTTCTGCTCAAGGCGATTCT TCATGATTGGGACG -3’; W279A Reverse Primer:5’-CTTGAGCAGAACTGCATTTC-3’).

Overexpression of recombinant wild-type SbSOMT or W279A mutant was conducted with slight modifications to a previously reported protocol18. Briefly, the expression strain was freshly prepared from chemically competent cells. Transformed cells were subcultured into 2xYT medium supplemented with kanamycin and chloramphenicol, and recovered via overnight shaking at 37 °C. Fresh 2xYT medium was then inoculated with the overnight culture and shaken at 37 °C until the OD600nm reached 0.6–0.8. Protein expression was induced with 0.1 mM isopropyl-β-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at 16 °C, overnight.

Harvested cells were resuspended in Lysis Buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM glutamate, 50 mM arginine, 10% glycerol, 10 mM TCEP, pH 7.9) and lysed by high-pressure cell disruptor. The cell lysate was centrifuged, and the supernatant was clarified through a 0.45 μm filter. The clarified supernatant was loaded onto a HisTrap HP column (Cytvia), followed by a wash with 10 column volumes of Wash Buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 25 mM imidazole, 5 mM TCEP, pH 7.9). Bound protein was eluted using an imidazole gradient (up to 500 mM) in Elution Buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, and 5 mM TCEP, pH 7.9). Fractions containing the desired protein were pooled and mixed with TEV protease. The mixture was concentrated and buffer-exchanged to Low Salt Buffer (20 mM HEPES, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM TCEP, pH 7.9) by ultrafiltration (10 000 MWCO), followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C. The incubated sample was clarified and passed through a HisTrap column prior to loading into HiTrap Q HP column (Cytvia) for anion exchange chromatography. The loaded column was washed with Low Salt Buffer until the baseline stabilized, and bound protein was eluted using a salt gradient (up to 1.0 M NaCl). Desired fractions were pooled, concentrated, and equilibrated into Storage Buffer (10 mM HEPES, 100 mM NaCl, pH 7.9). The purified protein was flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for long-term storage.

Isothermal Titration Calorimetry (ITC)

ITC binding experiments were conducted using a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC instrument (Malvern Panalytical). The running buffer consisted of 100 mM HEPES (pH 7.9) and 0–5.0 v/v% PEG 400 (as specified in Table S2). The concentrations of protein (in cell), ligand (in syringe) and saturating ligand (constant, in both cell and syringe) that were used in each run are listed in Table S2. The stock proteins were stored in Storage Buffer at concentrations at least 10-fold higher than the final experimental concentration (in cell). The stocks of substrate analogs were dissolved in 50 v/v% PEG 400 aqueous solution. Except for SAH (50 mM stock dissolved in 50 mM HCl), SFG and SAM stocks were prepared in deionized water.

All ITC runs were conducted at 25 °C, 750 rpm stirring speed, and with reference power of 10.0 μcal/s. Each run was initiated with 60 s delay, followed by a 0.5 μL first injection and 19 consecutive injections of 2.0 μL. The intervals between injections were 180 s. The thermograms were analyzed using MicroCal PEAQ-ITC Analysis Software (Malvern Panalytical). Except for one exception, all results were calculated by integrating the injections from 2nd to 20th injection, using the “One Set of Sites” fitting model at default settings. For the ITC run of RSV binding in SFG-saturating condition, the results were calculated by integrating the 18 injections and excluding 1st and 15th injection, using a “Two Set of Sites” fitting model at default settings. The individual thermograms and integrated plots are available in Figs. S8 and S9.

Biolayer Interferometry (BLI)

BLI binding experiments were conducted using Gator Prime instrument (Gator Bio) and SA XT biosensors (Gator Bio) at 30 °C. Briefly, SbSOMT was biotinylated by EZ-Link NHS-LC-LC-biotin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and subsequently equilibrated in Loading Buffer (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4). Biosensors were pre-wetted in Loading Buffer and the biotinylated SbSOMT was immobilized onto the biosensors via hour-long incubation until surface saturation was achieved. The loaded biosensors were washed in fresh Loading Buffer until a stable baseline was observed. The prepped biosensors were then processed for BLI assay.

Each kinetic cycle consisted of three primary steps – baseline (60 s), association (120 or 150 s) and dissociation (180 s). The basal assay buffer comprised 20 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl and 1% DMSO, at pH 7.4. In SFG or SAH-saturating conditions, the buffer was topped with 50 μM of the respective cofactor. Lastly, the buffer composition in the association step comprised additional ligand of interest (RSV or PTR) with a concentration gradient (listed in the figure legend) across different wells. Data were acquired and analyzed using the GatorOne software (Gator Bio). Analyzed sensograms generated by GatorOne are available in Figure S10.

Protein crystallization

Initial crystallization screenings of apo SbSOMT (10 mg/mL SbSOMT, 10 mM HEPES, 100 mM NaCl, pH 7.9) and SAH-bound SbSOMT (10 mg/mL SbSOMT, 2 mM SAH, 10 mM HEPES, 100 mM NaCl, pH 7.9) were first conducted using commercial kits (Hampton Research and QIAGEN). The screenings were conducted in sitting drop vapor diffusion fashion where 100 nL protein sample and 100 nL crystallization reagent were added together by a Mosquito liquid handler (SPT LabTech) and incubated at 18°C.

Crystallization of apo SbSOMT (20 mg/mL SbSOMT, 10 mM HEPES, 100 mM NaCl, pH 7.9) was optimized in crystallization reagent comprising 0.2 M sodium malonate (pH 7.0), 20% PEG 3350, and 10% glycerol. Equal volume (1.0 μL) of protein and crystallization reagent was mixed together and sealed with 100 μL reservoir. The crystals were obtained after >4 weeks incubation at 18 °C. Co-crystallization of SAH-bound SbSOMT (30 mg/mL SbSOMT, 20 mM HEPES, 100 mM NaCl, pH 7.9) was optimized in crystallization reagent comprising 0.04 M citric acid/0.06 M bis-tris propane (pH 6.4), 20% PEG 3350; and 5% glycerol. The drop was automatedly dispensed by the sequential addition of 500 nL protein, 250 nL ligand (50 mM SAH in 50 mM HCl), and 500 nL crystallization reagent. Crystals were obtained after ~1 week incubation at 18 °C. Crystals with suitable size and quality were harvested and cryoprotected by 20% glycerol. The cryoprotected crystals were mounted on a loop, followed by cryo-cooling with liquid nitrogen. The vitrified crystals were stored in a puck under cryo-condition until data collection.

X-ray crystallography

Single crystal cryo-crystallography was employed to obtain X-ray diffraction datasets of the crystals. The datasets were collected from beamline BL02U132 of Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF), Shanghai, China. The raw data were processed by XDS33 using the autoproc automated pipeline34. CCP4 suite35 was used in the following steps. The unmerged data was reduced by Aimless36. Molecular replacement was conducted via PHASER37 to solve both apo and SAH-bound SbSOMT, where the template was retrieved from a previously published structure (PDB: 7VB818). Refinement of the structures was performed automatically via REFMAC538 and PDB-REDO39,40, and manually via the COOT interface41. Compilation of structure factor and coordinate for final deposition, and generation of data collection statistics were facilitated by OneDep42. The statistics and metrics of reported structures are compiled in Table S3. Figures featuring protein structures were prepared using UCSF ChimeraX43.

In vitro enzymatic assay

Methylation activity of purified wildtype SbSOMT and W279A mutant were examined with varied final enzyme concentrations (0.1 and/or 1.0 μM) and varied incubation durations (10, 30, 60 and/or 960 min) with 0.5 mL final reaction mix comprising 0.1 mM HEPES (pH 7.9), 0.5 mM SAM, 0.5 mM PNS, and 10% methanol. Reaction was initiated by incubation at 30 °C and quenched by acetonitrile (equal volume) and an ice bath.

The enzymatic activity was determined by quantifying the PTR product via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). The HPLC system was equipped with Diode Array Detector (Agilent) and C18 column (YMC-Pack ODS A, YMC). Briefly, the quenched mixture was first filtered through a 0.22 μm PVDF filter prior to loading. The analyte (10 μL) was loaded into a pre-equilibrated column with a mobile phase of 70% water and 30% methanol. After loading, the mobile phase was gradually increased to 100% methanol over a course of 6 min at 0.5 mL/min flow rate, followed by 3 min of isocratic mobile phase (100% methanol) at the same flow rate. PTR was detected based on UV absorption at 334 nm. Three technical replicates were performed for each enzymatic reaction. The chromatograms (Figs. S11 and S12) were processed via Agilent OpenLab software. Quantification of PTR was conducted using a calibration curve of PTR standards (Fig. S13).
