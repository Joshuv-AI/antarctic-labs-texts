A study on the optimal timing for Rib fracture surgery: A report from a single center is earlier surgery always better?
Authors: Kaile Jiang, Jiaming Zhang, Weiwei He, Xiao Xie, Weiming Wu, Yonghong Zhao, Yi Yang, Lei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgical stabilization of rib fractures (SSRF) is increasingly employed in the management of rib fractures. Current guidelines recommend performing SSRF within 48 to 72 h post-injury. This study aimed to validate this recommendation using data from a single center in China.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective analysis to investigate the effects of different surgical timings on the length of hospital stay, ICU stay, incidence of postoperative pulmonary infection, postoperative respiratory failure, pain scores at 1 week after surgery, and pain and quality of life scores at 1 and 6 months post-discharge in patients with rib fractures.ResultsA total of 3899 patients were analyzed. The early surgery group (≤ 72 h) had significantly shorter hospital and ICU stays compared to the delayed surgery group (> 72 h) (P ≤ 0.001). There were no significant differences in postoperative pulmonary infection, respiratory failure, or pain scores at 1 week, 1 month, and 6 months post-discharge between the two groups. However, quality of life scores at 1 and 6 months post-surgery were significantly higher in the early surgery group (P < 0.001). Additionally, within the early surgery group, those who underwent surgery within 48 h of injury had a shorter hospital stay (P = 0.003).ConclusionsSSRF performed early after injury can significantly shorten hospital and ICU stays and improve patients’ quality of life for six months. SSRF performed within 48 h post-injury is recommended to achieve better outcomes.Keywords: Surgical stabilization of rib fractures, SSRF, Rib fracture, Rib fixation, Timing of surgery

Background

Trauma is an unavoidable condition affecting individuals of all ages worldwide, with chest trauma accounting for approximately one-quarter of all trauma cases.Rib fractures are the most common injuries resulting from chest trauma. In recent years, SSRF has been increasingly used to treat multiple rib fractures. Compared with conservative treatment, surgical treatment can significantly shorten the length of hospital stay, ICU stay and duration of mechanical ventilation, and reduce the risk of complications such as pneumonia and chest wall deformity, thereby achieving faster recovery, lower complication rate and better prognosis [1]. Position papers from both the World Society of Emergency Surgery (WSES) and the Chest Wall Injury Society (CWIS) recommend that early surgical intervention may benefit patients[2].This study aims to verify whether early implementation of SSRF can improve patient outcomes through a large sample of single-center data. The method is to retrospectively analyze the data of 3899 patients who underwent SSRF surgery and evaluate the impact of different surgical timing on the risk of postoperative complications, pain improvement and quality of life.

Methods

Study design

This study was a retrospective cohort study based on a self-designed chest trauma database. Since 2021, this database has been collecting chest trauma data and covers clinical data of patients admitted to the Department of Thoracic Surgery at Shanghai Sixth People's Hospital for rib fractures since December 1, 2013. It is continuously updated. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Shanghai Sixth People's Hospital. Since all personally identifiable information was anonymized, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Study population

We reviewed the clinical data of patients with rib fractures admitted to the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Shanghai Sixth People's Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, between December 1,2013 and December 1, 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) confirmed rib fractures by chest CT and three-dimensional reconstruction, with ≥ 3 fractures (defined as fracture end dislocation > 50%); (3) SSRF performed in our department. The exclusion criteria included: (1) patients who had undergone rib fracture surgery before admission or whose medical records were incomplete; (2) patients who had organ injuries requiring emergency surgery (e.g., splenic rupture) at admission; (3) patients with severe spinal fractures and paraplegia; (4) patients with preoperative lung infection; (5) patients who were in a coma at admission; and (6) patients with a history of severe cardiopulmonary disease (e.g., chronic heart failure, COPD).

Data collection and outcome measures

The following variables were extracted from the database: patient demographic information (age, sex, BMI), presence of diabetes, injury severity score (AIS), fracture location (unilateral or bilateral), total number of rib fractures, and the number of fracture ends.

The study evaluated multiple clinical and patient-reported outcomes, including length of hospitalization and six-month postoperative follow-up. Specific endpoints assessed were: length of total hospital stay; duration of ICU admission; incidence of postoperative pulmonary infection; occurrence of respiratory failure; pain scores at one week postoperatively; and pain scores and quality-of-life assessments at one month and six months after hospital discharge.

The pulmonary complications comprised pulmonary infection and respiratory failure. Imaging findings alone have limited capacity to distinguish consolidation attributable to inflammatory processes (i.e., pneumonia) from that resulting from blunt thoracic trauma; in clinical practice, these two etiologies frequently coexist. A diagnosis of pulmonary infection was established when the patient exhibited at least one of the following criteria: (I) fever ≥ 38 °C; (II) new or worsening respiratory symptoms—including cough, sputum production, or exacerbation of preexisting respiratory complaints; (III) auscultatory evidence of moist rales on pulmonary examination; and (IV) leukocytosis (white blood cell count ≥ 10 × 10⁹/L) or leukopenia (white blood cell count < 4 × 10⁹/L) on routine complete blood count, or identification of pathogenic bacteria in sputum culture. Respiratory failure was diagnosed based on arterial blood gas analysis—defined as either a PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio ≤ 300 mmHg or a PaO₂ < 60 mmHg.In cases where arterial blood gas analysis was unavailable or yielded inconclusive results, respiratory failure was diagnosed when tracheal intubation or tracheostomy was performed and mechanical ventilation was required for more than 24 h following surgical intervention.[3].

For quality-of-life assessment, we utilize the Barthel Index—a standardized, validated instrument with well-defined scoring criteria. Within our department, a dedicated clinical nurse is assigned to conduct structured telephone follow-ups with patients and administer the Barthel Index scoring accordingly.The aforementioned outcome indicators are likewise stored in the database.

Statistical analysis

The analysis of the study was conducted in two distinct parts. The first part compared outcomes for patients who underwent surgery within 72 h of injury to those who had surgery more than 72 h post-injury. The second part focused on comparing outcomes for patients who received surgical intervention within 48 h of injury against those whose surgeries occurred between 48 and 72 h post-injury.

To ensure balance among covariates across the different cohorts, propensity score matching was employed. The covariates considered included sex, age, body mass index (BMI), diabetes status, Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) score, bilaterality of fractures, total number of rib fractures, and the number of fracture fragments. A logistic regression model was utilized to calculate the propensity scores while incorporating all the aforementioned relevant covariates.

Nearest neighbor matching was performed at a 1:1 ratio with a caliper width set at 0.05. To assess the balance of covariates between the comparison groups before and after matching, the standardized mean difference (SMD) was calculated; an SMD value less than 0.1 was regarded as indicating satisfactory balance.

A total of 3,899 patients met the inclusion and exclusion criteria outlined in the database. Among these, 2,766 patients underwent surgery more than 72 h post-injury, while 1,133 patients received surgical intervention within 72 h. Significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding baseline characteristics, AIS chest score, unilateral or bilateral fracture status, total number of fractures, and the number of fracture fragments (p < 0.001) (Table 1). Following a 1:1 matching process, no significant differences were detected between the two groups in terms of baseline characteristics (Table 2). The comparison between the two groups revealed that the duration of hospital stay and ICU admission was significantly shorter for patients who underwent surgery within 72 hours, in contrast to those who had surgery after 72 hours (p ≤ 0.001). Additionally, pain scores at one and six months following discharge did not differ significantly between the groups. Discussion

Multiple rib fractures are common in trauma patients[4], 10% of all injured patients and 55% of patients with blunt chest trauma experience rib fractures[5].In the United States, it is estimated that between 22,000 and 45,000 rib fractures occur annually [6].

In 2025, China admitted over 500,000 patients with rib fractures.

Studies have demonstrated that surgical fixation of rib fractures (SSRF) in patients with flail chest can significantly reduce the rates of unplanned intubation, shorten the duration of mechanical ventilation, decrease ICU and total hospital stays, and lower the incidence of deep vein thrombosis and ventilator-associated pneumonia [7–9]. Currently, surgical intervention for patients with flail chest has become widely accepted.

The number and severity of fracture displacements have been identified as independent predictors of patient mortality and pulmonary complications,such as pneumonia and acute respiratory distress syndrome,regardless of whether flail chest is present [10, 11]. Many studies have confirmed that SSRF significantly alleviates pain in patients with fractures involving three or more ribs-particularly when pharmacological management is ineffective-while also reducing the duration of mechanical ventilation, lowering the incidence of pneumonia, shortening both ICU and total hospital length of stay, and mitigating chest wall–related discomfort[1, 12, 13].

Currently, the application of SSRF for specific patient populations (particularly those with three or more displaced rib fractures) to enhance prognosis has gained widespread recognition among scholars [2].All patients included in this study fulfilled the criterion of having "three or more displaced fractures."

It is widely acknowledged that the timing of surgery plays a crucial role in the success of rib fracture repair. Current consensus indicates that SSRF should be performed within 48 to 72 h following a traumatic injury[2]. For patients with severe rib fractures (AIS ≥ 3), conducting SSRF within 48 h post-injury significantly decreases both the length of stay in the ICU and the overall duration of hospitalization, as well as reducing mechanical ventilation time[14].Haines et al. reported that each day of delay in performing SSRF markedly elevates the risk of ventilator-associated pneumonia, endotracheal intubation, and ARDS[15].SSRF performed within 48 h not only shortens the ICU stay, the total hospitalization period, and the days on mechanical ventilation but also reduces hospital costs. Additionally, early fixation through SSRF decreases levels of inflammatory cytokines and infection markers, thereby preventing excessive inflammation and improving the infectious profile of patients with multiple rib fractures[16]. Early SSRF, performed within 48–72 h of admission, is linked to shorter hospital and ICU stays, fewer days on mechanical ventilation, lower rates of pneumonia, lower rates of tracheotomy, and reduced hospital costs. In contrast, late-stage SSRF, performed more than 3 days after admission, is associated with outcomes that are similar to or even worse than those of nonoperative management[17].

However, there are perspectives suggesting that revision surgery should be deferred to allow for an extended period of medical treatment. It is argued that surgical stabilization of rib fractures (SSRF) should only be performed on patients whose conditions are unstable or who do not respond adequately to initial treatment, thereby mitigating unnecessary surgical risks for certain individuals[18]. While a considerable number of studies advocate the numerous benefits associated with early surgery, it is crucial to interpret these conclusions with caution. Most relevant studies are retrospective in nature and may be susceptible to selection bias and attrition bias. For example, patients undergoing late surgery might require prolonged mechanical ventilation, potentially due to receiving SSRF after endotracheal intubation[19]. Furthermore, variations in diagnostic and treatment standards across different medical centers may influence surgical outcomes[20].Some studies have small sample sizes and limited statistical power[21].

All patients in this study were recruited from the largest rib fracture surgical center in China. The sample size was substantial, and the data were matched according to systemic and rib injury severity, resulting in a high degree of homogeneity. This enhances the reliability of evidence supporting the timing of SSRF. The results indicated that compared to surgeries performed more than three days post-injury, SSRF conducted within 72 h was not significantly associated with postoperative pneumonia, respiratory failure, or pain scores at one and six months following surgery. However, quality of life scores showed significant improvement at both one and six months post-surgery. Rib fractures, even when isolated injuries occur, are linked to a decreased quality of life [22]. While SSRF can effectively alleviate pain associated with rib fractures [23], this study demonstrated no significant impact of surgical timing on pain scores. Fredric M. Pieracci et al. reported that early surgery significantly reduced pain levels; however, this assessment occurred within two weeks post-surgery [11]. Another investigation revealed no notable difference in pain after one year among patients with flail chest regardless of whether they underwent surgery [24]. Therefore, we conclude that while SSRF markedly improves short-term pain (within six months), earlier surgical intervention does not lead to a significant reduction in pain scores. This study confirms that early SSRF improves quality of life, consistent with previous studies[25].

Studies have shown that earlier surgery can lead to better prognosis[26]. This study further divided patients who underwent surgery within 72 h into two groups: one group underwent SSRF within 48 h, and the other group underwent surgery between 48 and 72 h. The study found that SSRF within 48 h only reduced hospital stay; it did not significantly reduce the incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications or improve short-term quality of life. It should be noted that once inflammation and callus formation begin, it may become more difficult to reposition the rib fracture. Over time, the fracture may further displace, complicating anatomical reduction[27]. Therefore, early surgery is more conducive to surgical operation.

This study has several limitations.Firstly, due to its retrospective design, there may be selection and indication bias in the collection and review of patient data. Certain information could be obscured, and data might be lost during follow-up periods. Furthermore, the extended duration of this study may have introduced variability in prognostic outcomes owing to incremental refinements in the treatment regimen.Secondly, pain scales and quality of life assessment tools are subjective methods for evaluating clinical outcomes; thus, the lack of objective evaluations may introduce additional bias. Thirdly, although the clinical data were sourced from a single center, the 10-year time span suggests that advancements in treatment modalities could have influenced patient outcomes to some extent. Fourthly, our quality of life assessment tool is not specifically designed for patients with thoracic trauma and may not accurately reflect the efficacy of surgical interventions. Expanding the sample size and conducting prospective studies across high-level multicenter settings would provide more robust conclusions.

Conclusions

SSRF can effectively alleviate pain; however, no definitive association has been established between surgical timing and clinical outcomes. Early surgical intervention reduces both overall hospitalization duration and ICU length of stay, while improving quality of life during the first six months postoperatively. Surgery performed within 48 h after injury significantly shortens total hospital stay compared with surgery conducted at 72 h post-injury. Earlier surgical intervention may offer clinical benefits to patients.
