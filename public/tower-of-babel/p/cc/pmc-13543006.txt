Primary Bronchiectasis and Chronic Sinus Disease in Retinitis Pigmentosa
Authors: Yanisa Kluanwan, Chi Wan Koo, Teng Moua
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Retinitis pigmentosa (RP) is a rare degenerative retinal disorder characterized by ciliary dysfunction and photoreceptor cell death, leading to progressive vision loss and blindness.1 Clinical syndromes and genetic abnormalities associated with RP include Bardet-Biedl syndrome, McKusick-Kaufman syndrome, and RPGR gene mutations.2 Reports suggest primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD), a sinopulmonary disorder characterized by ciliary dysfunction in the respiratory tract, may share an X-linked inheritance pattern mediated by the RPGR gene.3, 4, 5 This study aims to systematically assess the background clinical and radiologic prevalence of primary bronchiectasis and chronic sinus disease in patients with RP.

Methods

A computer-assisted search of the electronic medical record was performed to delineate adult patients with RP (using International Classification of Diseases [ICD] 9/10 codes 3532.74 and H35.52) and chest CT scan seen in the Mayo Clinic Health System from January 1, 2000, to December 31, 2022. Patients without confirmed RP (lack of definitive testing with dark adaptometry, full-field electroretinography, multifocal electroretinography, or genetic testing) and those with known or suspected secondary causes of bronchiectasis (asthma or COPD, cystic fibrosis, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, recurrent aspiration, systemic autoimmune rheumatic disease, common variable or other acquired immunodeficiency, nontuberculous mycobacterium infection, and drug or radiation-induced injury) were excluded. Radiologic bronchiectasis was defined as any 1 of the following6: (1) bronchoarterial ratio > 1 to 1.5 (internal airway lumen and adjacent pulmonary artery), (2) lack of bronchial tapering before the next branch point, and (3) airway visibility within 1 cm of the costal pleural surface or mediastinal pleura. Those with available sinus CT scans were assessed for acute or chronic sinus disease defined as focal or diffuse mucosal thickening, air-fluid level or opacification of any sinus space, and nasal thickening or obstruction. Baseline characteristics including age at CT findings, sex, smoking history, pulmonary function testing, genetic consultation, and treatments were collated for all patients, with bronchiectasis or sinusitis-associated clinical or radiologic findings assessed in only those with radiologic abnormalities.

Results

A total of 1,249 patients were screened, with 186 patients with RP completing chest CT scan for any indication included in the final cohort. The median age was 73 years, with a male-to-female ratio of 55.4% to 44.6%. Radiologic bronchiectasis was observed in 27 of 186 patients (14.5%), and clinical or radiologic sinusitis was found in 31 patients (16.7%) (Fig 1, Table 1). Of the 27 patients with radiologic bronchiectasis, 8 had coexisting clinical (n = 1) or radiologic sinus disease (n = 7). Clinical or radiologic sinusitis was seen in 23 patients (14%) without radiologic bronchiectasis. Bronchiectasis was bilaterally distributed in most patients (81%), with single and multilobar findings in 15% and 3.7%, respectively. All lobes were affected (middle: 78%, lower: 74%, and upper lobes: 59%), with cylindrical morphology being the most prevalent (92%). Saccular, cystic, and mixed morphologies were each seen in 7.4%, respectively. Additional CT findings assessed in those with bronchiectasis included bronchial wall thickening (67%), mucous plugging (29%), and focal nodular densities (22%). Radiologic findings associated with sinus disease included mucosal thickening (95%) and air-fluid levels (55%) in 22 patients with abnormal sinus CT scans (7 with bronchiectasis and 15 without) (Fig 1). Pulmonary function testing was similar between those with and without radiologic bronchiectasis. Chronic or recurrent respiratory symptoms were seen in 78% of patients with radiologic bronchiectasis and 84% of those with clinical or radiologic sinusitis. Staphylococcus aureus was the most frequently identified organism on microbiologic testing. Nonpharmacologic therapy with mechanical airway clearance was pursued in 54% of those with bronchiectasis, whereas sinus surgery was performed in 71% of those with recurrent or chronic sinus disease. Genetic testing was performed in 11 patients, with RPGR gene mutations found in 2 of 4 patients with bronchiectasis and 1 of 7 without. RPGR mutations in the 2 patients with bronchiectasis included a nonsense mutation at exon 12 and missense mutation at exon 6, respectively, whereas a novel nonsense mutation at exon 12 was seen in the 1 patient without bronchiectasis. Other detected genetic abnormalities included autosomal recessive inheritance of SCLT1 in a 42-year-old man with Bardet-Biedl syndrome, and 2 cases of CEP250 mutations associated with Usher-like syndrome (progressive vision and hearing loss). Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to systematically assess primary clinical and radiologic bronchiectasis and sinusitis in patients with RP, excluding known or suspected secondary causes. Bronchiectasis and chronic sinusitis were observed in 14.5% and 16.7% of patients, respectively, with 4.3% having both conditions—levels higher than that reported in age-adjusted healthy background populations. In patients with RP, particularly those with an X-linked inherited RPGR variant, ciliopathy may be a likely mechanism for bronchiectasis, contributing to impaired mucociliary clearance, recurrent infections, and subsequent permanent airway damage.4 Prior reports have demonstrated the potential shared pathophysiology of RP and PCD, with affected organs having similar mechanisms of ciliary dysfunction.3,7,8 Using transmission electron microscopy, Kuroda et al3 described ciliary defects as disruptions of the 9 + 2 microtubule array and absence of outer and inner dynein arms in a 32-year-old man with bronchiectasis and childhood diagnosis of RP. These ciliary abnormalities were categorized as class 1 defects according to international consensus guidelines9and often associated with PCD. Subsequent genetic testing in patients with RP and bronchiectasis and transmission electron microscopy-confirmed ciliary microstructural abnormalities led to the identification of an X-linked mutation on the RP3 locus of the RPGR gene,4,10 which is involved in proper cilia orientation in both the retina and respiratory system.4,8 Although phenotypic characteristics of bronchiectasis in patients with RP and PCD overlap, bronchiectasis was found more commonly in men in this cohort, perhaps because of the X-linked transmission of RPGR-associated disease in RP. Prevalence of bronchiectasis and sinusitis also occurred in only 14.5% and 16.7% of this cohort, whereas bronchiectasis and sinusitis are reported in 80% and nearly 100% of those with PCD, respectively.11 Differences in penetrance again may be explained by X-linked inheritance patterns or unknown mutations beyond or involving other regions of the RPGR gene. Only 11 patients were assessed for genetic abnormalities in this cohort, with RPGR mutations appearing to occur more frequently in those with bronchiectasis.

In summary, increased prevalence of bronchiectasis and chronic sinusitis appears to be associated with RP and may reflect a shared disease mechanism of ciliopathy. Additional research confirming genetic abnormalities in patients with RP may lead to better understanding of clinical penetrance and risk of bronchiectasis and sinus disease development and progression.
