A Letter to the Editor about “Endometrial Cancer: A Daily Clinical Practice Guide for Radiologists” Published Online: August 20, 2025
Authors: Yumiko Oishi Tanaka
Published: 2026

Dear Editor

I read the above-entitled review article1 with great concern. The authors explained both paradigm shifts, from morphology to molecular-based classification2 and staging, using imaging modalities. I appreciate the tremendous educational value for diagnostic radiologists, particularly for those in Japan.

As they mentioned, myometrial invasion is one of the most important prognostic factors that can be diagnosed with MRI. In the early era of diagnostic MRI for gynecological diseases, the junctional zone is recognized as a marker for the depth of invasion.3,4 Additionally, there are 2 types of growth patterns for endometrial cancer: exophytic and endophytic. Nowadays, especially after molecular classification was applied to FIGO staging 2023,2 Bokhman’s famous subtypes are not commonly used. Bokhman’s type I (most are low-risk histology) tends to show exophytic growth, and type II (most are high-risk histology) tends to show endophytic growth.5 The junctional zone is a crucial indicator for the myometrial invasion in both exophytic and endophytic tumors. Destruction of the myometrial zonal anatomy, with an indistinct border of the junctional zone and the outer myometrium, is a sign of endophytic tumor growth. On the other hand, the junctional zone remains a reliable marker of the depth of the myometrial invasion in exophytic tumors, as it expands the myometrium around the tumor, even with minimal or no myometrial invasion. In such a case, myometrial invasion measured by the method shown in Fig. 4(2)b and Fig. 5(1)b in the present article1 results in overdiagnosis, as this method does not consider the expansion of the myometrium without tumor invasion.

Therefore, the authors should emphasize the importance of observing the thinning and disruption of the junctional zone to prevent misleading young diagnostic radiologists and avoid overdiagnosis.

Best Regards,

Yumiko Oishi Tanaka, MD, PhD.

Dear Dr. Yumiko Oishi Tanaka, MD, PhD,

We appreciate your insightful comments on our manuscript1.

As pointed out by Dr. Tanaka, Fig. 4(2) shows preservation of the junctional zone (JZ) on the T2-weighted imaging (T2-WI); therefore, diagnosing myometrial invasion based on this finding was considered to be an overdiagnosis. Accordingly, we would like to revise the description in the figure legend as outlined below.

However, although not shown here, contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imagings suggested possible myometrial invasion, and we would like to add a statement to address this finding as well.

The figure legends for Fig.4(2) should read as following:

In Fig. 4,

(b) Axial T2WI showed exophytic tumors and suggested a preserved junctional zone circumferentially around the tumor, indicating minimal or no myometrial invasion.

(b) Axial T2WI showed exophytic tumors and suggested a preserved junctional zone circumferentially around the tumor, indicating minimal or no myometrial invasion.

(c) However, axial diffusion-weighted image (b = 1000 mm/sec) demonstrated an irregular tumor–myometrial interface along the anterior wall, more conspicuous on post-contrast sequences (not shown here). This was suggestive of possible focal myometrial invasion at this site (the oblique horizontal line indicates myometrial thickness; the long line depicts the estimated depth of tumor invasion).

(c) However, axial diffusion-weighted image (b = 1000 mm/sec) demonstrated an irregular tumor–myometrial interface along the anterior wall, more conspicuous on post-contrast sequences (not shown here). This was suggestive of possible focal myometrial invasion at this site (the oblique horizontal line indicates myometrial thickness; the long line depicts the estimated depth of tumor invasion).

As for Fig. 5, Fig. 5(1) was selected as a representative case with myometrial invasion based on confirmation of the pathological findings.

However, although disruption of the JZ was suspected on the sagittal images, the JZ was identifiable on the oblique coronal T2-WIs, which may have presented potentially confusing imaging findings to the readers.

Therefore, we propose to remove the case shown in Fig. 5(1).
