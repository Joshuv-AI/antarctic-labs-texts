An uncommon complication of dental bridge ingestion: Dual obstructive uropathy and bowel obstruction – Case report

Introduction

Denture ingestion constitutes an uncommon yet clinically significant event, occurring predominantly in vulnerable populations such as young children, elderly patients, and individuals with neurocognitive disorders or altered consciousness (e.g., due to alcoholism or substance use) [1]. While most ingested foreign bodies pass spontaneously through the gastrointestinal tract, 15 % of cases require endoscopic extraction, and fewer than 1 % necessitate surgical management [9].

The esophagus is the most common site of retention for these foreign bodies, small bowel impaction is rare and may lead to major complications such as obstruction or perforation [1,3]. This case presents a particularly rare occurrence of simultaneous intestinal obstruction and anuria in a male patient with a history of Bricker urinary diversion. The Bricker conduit is a form of incontinent urinary diversion created after radical cystectomy or cystoprostatectomy, typically for bladder cancer. It involves using an isolated ileal segment (neobladder) to channel urine from the ureters to an abdominal wall stoma, requiring an external collection device [13].

A detailed clinical history, especially in edentulous elderly patients, is essential for diagnosing foreign body ingestion. Computed tomography (CT) plays a critical role in identifying complications, such as obstruction or perforation, locating the foreign body, and guiding appropriate management strategies [2,9]. The morbidity and mortality associated with foreign body ingestion depend on several factors, including the nature of the object, the patient's overall health, the presence of complications, and the timeliness and adequacy of treatment [9].

This case report emphasizes the importance of early diagnosis and effective management in such scenarios.

Case presentation

A 52-year-old edentulous male with a history of bipolar disorder and a cystoprostatectomy with Bricker-type urinary diversion (performed 15 years ago for bladder cancer) presented to the emergency department with a one-week history of subumbilical abdominal pain, vomiting, abdominal bloating and anuria. Physical examination revealed fever (38.5 °C), abdominal distension, localized guarding in the right iliac fossa, and a non-functional urostomy. Laboratory investigations demonstrated significant inflammatory markers with leukocytosis (16 × 109/L) and elevated C-reactive protein (120 mg/L), accompanied by acute renal insufficiency (serum creatinine 126 μmol/L). Abdominal computed tomography (CT) imaging identified a mechanical small bowel obstruction caused by impacted dense fecal material, with proximal bowel dilation leading to extrinsic compression of the Bricker conduit, which showed wall thickening (Fig. 1). This case involves acute mechanical intestinal obstruction with severe clinical signs including fever, right iliac fossa guarding, and anuria, alongside marked biological inflammation and acute kidney injury. Thus, surgical intervention was indicated. A laparoscopic approach was excluded due to both extensive abdominal adhesions from prior cystoprostatectomy with Bricker urinary diversion and the presence of clinico-biological signs of severity. Additionally, attempts to catheterize the Bricker conduit were unsuccessful owing to the patient's acute pain and distress.

CT scan showing small bowel obstruction caused by impacted dense fecal material (yellow arrow), with proximal bowel dilation leading to extrinsic compression of the Bricker conduit, which showed wall thickening (white arrow).

Following brief resuscitation, the patient underwent exploratory laparotomy. Intraoperative findings revealed ileal distension upstream of a stricture zone located 60 cm from the cecum, which was adherent to the distended Bricker loop. The Bricker loop was catheterized using a Foley catheter, enabling the evacuation of retained urine. This maneuver facilitated the release of the ileum, and palpation of the penultimate loop identified an intraluminal foreign body. An ileal enterotomy was performed, and a dental bridge was successfully extracted (Fig. 2). The foreign body, combined with impacted digestive content, had formed a composite mass causing ileal obstruction near the Bricker assembly. The Bricker loop was severely compressed by the adjacent dilated bowel loop, accounting for the observed anuria.

Extracted dental prosthesis (black arrow).

Postoperative course was unremarkable. Both intestinal transit and urinary flow were successfully restored. Oral fluids were reintroduced on the second postoperative day, and the patient was discharged on the fifth postoperative day without complications. Following hospital discharge, care coordination included psychiatric follow-up for bipolar disorder maintenance therapy and dental referral for complete oral prosthetic rehabilitation given his edentulous state.

Discussion

The inhalation or ingestion of dental prostheses represents a complex, multidisciplinary challenge involving emergency medicine, surgery, dentistry, endoscopy, otorhinolaryngology, anesthesiology, psychiatry and neurology [1]. Foreign body ingestion is more common in children, but adults also experience it, with dentures being an uncommon yet significant cause. Dentures account for 4–18 % of swallowed foreign bodies in adults, often due to accidental ingestion [2]. The high prevalence of denture ingestion is unsurprising, given that the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (USA) reports 20 % of adults aged 18–74 use dentures [2].

Risk factors include old age, ill-fitting or broken dentures, facial trauma, psychoneurological disorders, alcohol intoxication, drug overdose, and general anesthesia [2,10,11]. Diabetic patients are at heightened risk due to diminished oral sensitivity and impaired pharyngolaryngeal control [1].

While approximately 80 % of ingested foreign bodies pass spontaneously through the gastrointestinal tract, the remaining 20 % risk impaction at narrowed sites, including the pylorus, ileocecal valve or rectosigmoid colon [1,4]. The bridge's size and rigidity likely contributed to its entrapment at the ileal conduit, exacerbating obstruction risks.

Clinically, patients typically present with a history of accidental ingestion, though diagnosis can be challenging in cases of unwitnessed aspiration (e.g., during alcohol intoxication, sleep, seizures, or trauma) or among mentally impaired individuals (e.g., dementia, delirium) [3]. Symptoms are nonspecific and depend on the site of impaction and subsequent complications: in the esophagus may cause various symptoms including dysphagia, airway obstruction, or even perforation. Conversely, those located in the digestive tract can lead to less specific symptoms such as abdominal pain, melena, or perforation [4,6,7].Oral exam is especially crucial for patients with psycho-neurological deficits and as a part of perioperative anesthesiology assessment.

The ingestion of dental prostheses can lead to severe complications, particularly in elderly patients with comorbidities. These include bowel obstruction (14 % of complications), fistula formation (22 %), and hollow viscus perforation (50 %, most frequently occurring in the esophagus). Additional risks include hemorrhage, abscess formation, and in rare cases (2.4 %), mortality secondary to aspiration or sepsis [12]. In our case, the impacted dental bridge within the small bowel led to mechanical obstruction, with secondary compression of the Bricker's loop, resulting in both intestinal and urinary outflow obstruction—a rare but clinically critical scenario.

Medical imaging plays a pivotal role in diagnosis. While plain abdominal radiography may reveal suggestive signs (e.g., hydro-aeric levels), its utility is limited by the radiolucency of many prosthetic materials. Thus, CT should be the first-line imaging modality, as it can reliably identify dentures—typically appearing as hyperdense curvilinear structures with focal protrusions corresponding to prosthetic teeth. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), though capable of detecting acrylic resin, offers no significant diagnostic advantage and is less accessible in emergency settings [1,2]. In our case, the dental prosthesis was not visible on CT scan because it was completely coated with fecal material (appearing as dense intraluminal material obstructing the bowel loop).

The management of ingested dental prostheses may be conservative, involving clinical and radiological monitoring when no warning signs (e.g., perforation, intestinal obstruction, or bleeding) are present. For prostheses lodged in the upper gastrointestinal tract, endoscopic removal remains the first-line treatment in uncomplicated cases. However, endoscopic management of small-bowel foreign bodies poses significant technical challenges due to the limited reach of conventional endoscopes to distal impaction sites. Although double-balloon enteroscopy enables both diagnosis and retrieval, its restricted availability outside tertiary centers often delays intervention [1,2,7]. In our clinical scenario, these constraints - combined with the patient's emergent presentation and complex surgical anatomy (Bricker diversion) - mandated an exploratory laparotomy. This case highlights the critical importance of: institutional resource evaluation when selecting management strategies, early surgical consultation for proximal small bowel obstructions, and the development of regional referral networks for complex endoscopic retrieval in anatomically challenging cases.

Surgical intervention is warranted when endoscopic management fails or when concerning features (e.g., perforation, obstruction) emerge. Notably, only 12–20 % of partial small-bowel obstructions require surgery, as most resolve spontaneously within 48 h [5]. Critical to decision-making process are several factors: the size and configuration of the prosthesis, the presence of bowel wall compromise, the patient's surgical history and comorbidities, as well as the available institutional resources. This tailored approach emphasizes the importance of balancing conservative management when appropriate against timely surgical intervention in high-risk scenarios, particularly for vulnerable elderly patients with multiple comorbidities who may poorly tolerate complications [5,8].

Preventing denture ingestion requires a collaborative approach primarily involving dental professionals, anesthesiologists, and surgical teams, with particular emphasis on pre-procedural risk assessment. Dentists play a pivotal role in ensuring proper prosthesis fit and educating vulnerable patients, while anesthesiologists must maintain heightened vigilance during airway management, especially in elderly or cognitively impaired individuals. Emergency physicians serve as critical first responders in identifying unwitnessed ingestions. Although psychiatrists and otolaryngologists have more limited involvement, establishing standardized interdisciplinary protocols could significantly reduce incidence rates and improve outcomes. Such cooperation should focus on: routine denture integrity checks during dental visits, preoperative removal protocols for anesthesia cases, and emergency department screening tools for high-risk populations.

Conclusion

This case underscores the critical importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for denture ingestion in high-risk populations, particularly edentulous patients with abdominal symptoms and complex surgical histories. Early cross-sectional imaging and multidisciplinary collaboration are essential for timely diagnosis and management. While endoscopic retrieval remains first-line for uncomplicated cases, surgical intervention should not be delayed in the presence of bowel obstruction or anatomical complexity, as seen with urinary diversions. Preventive strategies must emphasize denture fit assessments and peri-procedural vigilance. Ultimately, tailored decision-making—balancing conservative and surgical approaches—is key to optimizing outcomes in these clinically challenging scenarios.
