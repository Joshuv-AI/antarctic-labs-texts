Parsing Heterogeneity: Sex Differences in Youth Antisocial Behavior
Authors: Maya A Irvin‐Vitela, Monica A Marsee, Tess M S Neal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined sources of heterogeneity in antisocial behaviors in a large‐scale and nationally representative sample of n = 7655 youth from the Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development Study. Separate latent profile analyses were conducted for males and females using indicators of physical aggression, relational aggression, callous‐unemotional (CU) traits, and conduct symptom severity. Distinct profiles emerged within each sex. Among females, profiles were primarily differentiated by levels of CU traits, whereas among males, physical aggression played a more central role in distinguishing groups. In both sexes, a higher‐risk profile characterized by elevated aggression and/or CU traits was identified alongside a lower‐severity marked by minimal conduct problems and aggression. Profiles also differed significantly in overall distress, with the higher‐risk male profile demonstrating the greatest distress and the lower‐risk female profile the least. Findings highlight meaningful sex‐specific configurations of aggression and CU traits and underscore the importance of person‐centered, sex‐informed approaches to developmental models, assessment, and intervention in youth with antisocial behavior.Keywords: aggression, antisocial behaviors, callous‐unemotional traits, conduct disorder

Background

Many youths may display behavioral problems such as aggression or bullying, but relatively few portray those behaviors to a serious degree (Erskine et al. 2014). When the behaviors are severe, persistent, and cause significant distress or dysfunction, clinical assessment and diagnosis may be appropriate. Effective intervention programs intended to target disruptive behaviors, especially those involving aggression in youth, require the accurate identification of individuals who could benefit from the intervention. Diagnostic criteria provide a limited understanding of the presentation of aggressive behaviors, and rather than including empirically‐supported forms and functions of aggression (e.g., Frick and Nigg 2012; Salekin 2016; Frick and Kemp 2021), they focus entirely on physical aggression (American Psychiatric Association 2022). Current diagnostic criteria are limited in that they are not capturing the full array of aggressive behaviors that may indicate the presence of psychopathology and distress, especially in girls, who have been shown to demonstrate different profiles of aggressive behavior than those of boys with conduct problems (Berkout et al. 2011; Konrad et al. 2022; Brennan and Shaw 2013).

Due to the heterogenous nature of disruptive behavior disorders, interventions to target behaviors must address the different presentations of symptoms. An increased understanding of how youth experience distress related to these disorders will improve current diagnostic, intervention, and treatment strategies, which all serve to support the experiences of youth and families who need resources and care. Thus, this study explored the relationship between sources of heterogeneity in a large‐scale and nationally‐representative sample of youth.

Aggresion

The display of aggressive behaviors is a core trait in disruptive behavior disorders. Aggression refers to behaviors that are intended to cause physical or mental harm and is more likely to lead to intervention from mental health professionals or law enforcement than other types of antisocial behaviors (Anderson and Bushman 2002; Kerr and Capaldi 2011). Extant research outlines forms, or methods, of aggression (i.e., sexual, emotional, physical, or relational) and associated outcomes differ based on the type of aggression people display (e.g., Evans et al. 2024; Perry and Ostrov 2023).

The distinction between forms and functions of aggression are particularly relevant for understanding sex differences. Relationally aggressive behaviors —those that intend to cause harm to relationships or social status, such as spreading malicious rumors or intentionally excluding others from social activities— are sometimes referred to as a “female type” of aggression (see Berkout et al. 2011 for review on gender differences in conduct disorder; Crick and Grotpeter 1995; Crick et al. 2007). This conceptualization does not fully capture the reality of social relationships and reinforces essentialized views of sex and gender differences. However, it does demonstrate an attempt to understand and explain the differences in the ways youth engage in aggressive behavior.

Although both males and females engage in relationally aggressive behaviors (e.g., Ellis et al. 2009; Zimmer‐Gembeck et al. 2013; Nesdale et al. 2013), some research finds that females are more likely to engage in relational aggression than males (e.g., Magram et al. 2025). Metanalytic results generally support these findings, but some researchers point out that these gender differences in relational aggression are negligible (Casper and Card 2017; Card et al. 2008). Other research suggests that relational aggression has been found to be a better predictor of social‐psychological maladjustment than physical aggression for females (Kamper‐DeMarco and Ostrov 2017; Crick and Grotpeter 1995) and that females tend to find relational aggression more distressing than males do (Spieker et al. 2012; Casper and Card 2017).

Although findings on sex and gender differences in aggression are inconclusive, it is important to consider measurement and informant biases, as commonly used instruments and diagnostic criteria have historically emphasized overt and physically aggressive behaviors, potentially leading to the under‐identification of relational or covert forms of aggression across genders (Archer 2004; Crick and Grotpeter 1995; De Los Reyes et al. 2013; Moffitt and Caspi 2001).

Conduct Disorder

Conduct Disorder (CD) is an emotional and behavioral disorder that describes antisocial patterns of disruptive behavior in children and adolescents under the age of 18. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM‐5‐TR; American Psychiatric Association 2022) includes four categories of criteria in the diagnosis of CD: aggression to people and animals, destruction of property, deceitfulness or theft, and serious violation of the rules. Within those categories, there are fifteen symptoms, three of which must have been present within the past twelve months and one of which must have been present within the past 6 months – and those behaviors must cause clinically significant impairment in social, academic, or occupational functioning for an individual to qualify for diagnosis (American Psychiatric Association 2022). Yet, people may face significant impairment without meeting current diagnostic criteria. CD conceptualizations are not immune from the challenges associated with the categorical approach to understanding and treating psychopathology (see Lilienfeld and Treadway 2016; Salekin 2016). Subclinical levels of CD still have the potential to adversely affect the lives of individuals who experience them.

CD is diagnosed at a significantly higher rate in boys than girls (Brennan and Shaw 2013; see Fairchild et al. 2019, for review), which has led to disparities in the amount of research pertaining to antisocial and conduct behaviors in girls. Previous research has proposed that the discrepancy is due to lower levels of antisocial behaviors in girls (see Moffitt 2018 for review); however, others have posited that the discrepancy may be due to a difference in the way that antisocial behaviors are measured and understood (Silverthorn and Frick 1999; Keenan et al. 2010). Furthermore, CD is highly heterogenous and there are developmental differences that have predictive utility in understanding the presentations and effects of CD across the lifespan (Nock et al. 2006; Barker et al. 2007). Extensive research has attempted to predict the course and severity of conduct problems by defining subgroups of children and adolescents with antisocial traits based on risk factors, types of behavior, and potential future impairment (see Fairchild et al. 2019).

Developmental Pathways in Conduct Disorder

Developmental pathways are one of the ways that prior research has attempted to predict CD‐related outcomes. The primary developmental and categorical model in the research and treatment of CD is age of onset, which distinguishes two groups of youth, each with unique outcomes based on age when antisocial symptoms begin (Moffitt et al. 2006). The first subtype is defined by the onset of symptoms prior to adolescence, which is known as the childhood‐onset type. Children in the childhood‐onset group, who may begin demonstrating conduct problems as early as preschool, typically demonstrate behavioral problems that escalate in frequency and severity with age (Frick and Ellis 1999; see Fairchild et al. 2019 for review). The childhood‐onset type is associated with higher levels of aggression, more significant neuropsychological and cognitive deficits, more challenges with impulsivity and emotional dysregulation, and an increased likelihood of demonstrating persistent antisocial behaviors in adulthood (Moffitt et al. 2002; Frick and Viding 2009). The adolescent‐onset type of CD, however, is thought to be more transient and normative as a result of environmental considerations, with symptoms limited to adolescence (Price et al. 2019). In the adolescent‐onset type, youth are more likely to “grow out” of their antisocial behaviors, unlike the life‐course persistent behaviors seen in the childhood‐onset type (Moffitt 2018).

Research suggests the possibility of an additional pathway to antisocial behavior separate from the childhood‐ and adolescent‐onset types. Neither the childhood‐ nor adolescent‐onset type appears to fully describe the trajectory of antisocial traits in girls (Silverthorn and Frick 1999; Keenan et al. 2010). Girls with antisocial traits share many similarities with the childhood‐onset pathway present in boys, including parental antisocial behavior, high rates of comorbid psychopathology, and functional impairment in adulthood (Frick and Viding 2009; Konrad et al. 2022). However, although girls with antisocial traits share those correlates with childhood‐onset boys, they are more likely to begin displaying symptoms during adolescence (e.g., Brennan and Shaw 2013; Frick and Viding 2009; Price et al. 2019). Because of this, researchers have advocated for the inclusion of a delayed‐onset path to describe the development, presentation, and persistence of antisocial traits in girls (Silverthorn and Frick 1999). The inclusion of a developmental pathway that describes the presentation of symptoms as they appear in girls, rather than trying to force girls into a category that was not constructed to account for their distinct experiences, has set an important precedent for including girls in the study of antisocial and conduct behaviors.

Defining Subgroups of Youth Antisocial Behavior

Frameworks such as developmental taxonomy highlight that antisocial behavior is not a unitary construct, but rather reflects multiple developmental processes that may vary in expression across individuals and contexts (Frick and Viding 2009). As a result, reliance on single indicators may obscure meaningful differences in the severity and form of antisocial behaviors.

Callous/unemotional (CU) traits have been proposed as a marker of a particularly severe and persistent developmental pathway (Eisenbarth et al. 2016; Frick et al. 2014; Moffitt 2018). CU traits (i.e., callousness, lack of empathy, lack of guilt) may facilitate instrumental and goal‐directed forms of harm and have strong utility in distinguishing between youth with antisocial behaviors (e.g., Frick et al. 2014; Frick and Viding 2009; Fanti 2013). It is possible that relational aggression represents a developmentally salient expression of antisocial behavior that aligns closely with the interpersonal style characteristic of CU traits. Developmental taxonomy models, distinctions between forms and functions of aggression, and research on CU traits converge on the need to examine configurations of antisocial behavior rather than isolated dimensions (Fairchild et al. 2019).

In recent years, person‐centered analytic approaches have increasingly been used to characterize heterogeneity in antisocial behavior and related traits, particularly CU traits and aggression (e.g., Bradshaw et al. 2013; Ettekal and Ladd 2017; Fairchild et al. 2019; Fanti and Kimonis 2017; Waller et al. 2015; Williford et al. 2011). Latent profile and latent class analyses have identified distinct subgroups of youth differing in the severity and configuration of antisocial behaviors and CU traits, with profiles demonstrating differential associations with developmental trajectories, risk factors, and functional outcomes (e.g., Fairchild et al. 2019; Fanti and Kimonis 2017; Waller et al. 2015). These approaches align with developmental theories emphasizing multifinality, as they allow for the identification of subgroups that may not be well captured by variable‐centered methods. Collectively, this research suggests that examining patterns of co‐occurring antisocial behavior, aggression, and CU traits using person‐centered methods may provide important insights beyond those afforded by traditional dimensional approaches.

Current Study

Current diagnostic criteria provide a limited understanding of the presentation of aggressive and antisocial behaviors. Seven of the fifteen diagnostic criteria for CD in the DSM‐5‐TR are related to aggression towards people and animals, which emphasizes the importance of the consideration of aggression in the diagnosis of the disorder (American Psychiatric Association 2022). However, as it stands, current assessment of CD primarily considers the presence of physical aggression or threats of physical aggression. Youth who engage in relational rather than physical aggression may still experience clinically significant levels of distress without meeting the diagnostic threshold (Perry and Ostrov 2023). Additionally, due to observed gender differences in aggressive behaviors, it is possible that gender may affect the relationship between aggression and impaired functioning (Brennan and Shaw 2013; Keenan et al. 2010). However, much of the existing literature relies on variable‐centered approaches, which limits insight into how forms of aggression, affective traits, and symptom severity co‐occur within and across youths. This is especially true when considering whether the configuration of these behaviors and traits differs by sex. As a result, it remains unclear whether aggression, affective traits, and symptom severity cluster differently for males and females in ways that may have distinct implications for research and practice.

The present study aimed to identify distinct subgroups of youth based on behaviors and traits associated with conduct problems and to examine whether these subgroups differed by sex. Prior research has emphasized the need for research on relational aggression in conduct behaviors and potential associated features. As such, while we thought there was potential for relational aggression to cluster with other conduct disorder features and with gender, we used an exploratory analytic approach through latent profile modeling to investigate this potential while also being open to discovering other patterns in the data.

Methods

Participants

Participants were 11,873 youth from the Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study®, a National Institutes of Health (NIH)‐funded longitudinal developmental study of brain development, physical and mental health, and behavior in the United States (see Volkow et al. 2018). Participants were recruited from 21 research sites across the country between the ages of 9–10 and, along with their parents/caregivers, completed an extensive protocol of assessments and physiological indicators related to genetics, culture, cognition, physical and mental health, family history, and social and behavioral functioning. According to study coordinators, the ABCD Study aims to track biological and behavioral development from childhood and adolescence into young adulthood to understand the interaction between childhood experiences, brain development, and outcomes related to social, behavioral, physical, and academic health (Jernigan et al. 2018).

Participants completed full assessments every other year and shorter assessments on alternating years. They also completed virtual follow‐ups every 3–6 months for the duration of their participation in the study. Participants and their families received monetary compensation for their participation. The data for the current study were accessed from the 10.15154/1523041 data release, which included baseline and follow‐up data for the 6‐month, 1 year, and 18‐month visits on the full participant cohort, as well as interim data for the 2‐year, 30‐month, 3‐year, and 42‐month visits (ABCD Study). To have the most complete recent data available, all analyses utilized data from 2‐year follow‐up visits. Accessed data had already been checked for thoughtless responding and feigning prior to download (Saragosa‐Harris et al. 2022).

All participants were required to have complete data on the measures of interest from the 2‐year follow‐up visit. Of the 11,873 participants, 1,459 participants were removed because they did not have any data available for the 2‐year follow‐up visit and an additional 2,329 participants were removed due to missing data on the outcome measure (Child Behavior Checklist [CBCL; Achenbach and Rescorla 2001], see below for detailed description). Finally, 430 participants were removed due to missing data on one or more of the grouping variable measures (Brief CU measure [Hawes et al. 2020], Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for the DSM‐5 [KSADS‐COMP; Townsend et al. 2020], Peer Experiences Questionnaire [RPEQ; De Los Reyes and Prinstein 2004], see below for detailed description of each measure).

Following these exclusions, n = 7,655 participants were retained for further analyses. All participants were between the ages of 10 and 14 (M = 11.96, SD = 0.65) at the time of data collection. Participants' biological sex at birth was either male (n = 4,032, 52.7%) or female (n = 3,623, 47.3%). Participants self‐selected racial identities, and most selected multiple identities from the list (n = 7,242; 94.6%). As such, racial identity categories overlapped, resulting in percentages that do not total to 100%. Participants self‐reported racial identities of American Indian/Alaska Native/Native American (n = 275, 3.6%), Asian/Asian American (n = 466, 6.1%), African American/Black (n = 1,359, 17.8%), Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander (n = 50, 0.7%), Caucasian/White (n = 1,504, 19.6%), and “other” (n = 485, 6.3%). Additionally, some individuals either did not know their racial identity (n = 65, 0.8%) or refused to answer (n = 34, 0.4%).

Although the ABCD Study was designed to be nationally representative at baseline, the analytic sample reflects ~54% of the original cohort. As a result, the final analytic sample of n = 7,655 participants may not fully reflect the representativeness of the baseline cohort. Attrition and missingness in longitudinal studies may be associated with sociodemographic characteristics or levels of behavioral risk, which could limit generalizability (e.g., Young et al. 2006).

Measures

Measures were drawn from multiple informants to capture complementary perspectives on youth behavior and psychopathology (Achenbach et al. 1987; De Los Reyes and Kazdin 2005). Aggression was assessed via youth self‐report to capture subjective experiences of aggressive behavior, CU traits were assessed via parent‐report given caregivers' vantage point on affective and interpersonal characteristics, and CD symptoms were assessed using a structured, computer‐assisted diagnostic interview administered to youth, consistent with diagnostic assessment standards (Shaffer et al. 2000).

Grouping Variables

Aggression

Perpetration of peer aggression was measured via the revised version of the Peer Experiences Questionnaire (RPEQ; De Los Reyes and Prinstein 2004). The RPEQ consists of 18 child‐report questions scored on a 5‐point Likert scale to assess frequency of peer experiences (1 = Never, 2 = Once or twice, 3 = A few times, 4 = About once a week, 5 = A few times a week). Half of the questions in the RPEQ are related to perpetration and the other half are related to victimization; however, only the perpetration items were used in current analyses. The RPEQ has two perpetration factors—physical aggression perpetration and relational aggression perpetration. Factor scores have demonstrated acceptable to good internal consistency in previous research (α = 0.68–0.84; De Los Reyes and Prinstein 2004). In this sample, both relational and physical aggression perpetration demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.75 and α = 0.70 respectively).

Callous‐Unemotional Traits

CU traits were not originally assessed as part of the ABCD Study assessment protocol. To rectify that issue, researchers developed a brief 4‐item CU measure using parent‐report ABCD data and assessments (Hawes et al. 2020). Higher scores on the four items (Not considerate of others' feelings; Not helpful if someone is hurt or upset; Does not offer to help others; Lack of guilt after misbehaving) indicate higher levels of CU traits, each of which is scored dichotomously (i.e., 0= does not endorse the item, 1 = does endorse the item). The measure demonstrated strong predictive and construct validity compared to other brief CU measures and showed acceptable internal consistency when tested during its development (α = 0.73; Hawes et al. 2020). Within the current sample, acceptable internal consistency was also demonstrated (α = 0.76). Although these items capture core affective and interpersonal features associated with CU traits, they do not provide the breadth of a comprehensive inventory. Findings should therefore by interpreted with consideration of the measure's constrained scope.

Conduct Disorder Symptoms

Symptoms associated with conduct disorder were assessed via the computerized version of the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for the DSM‐5 (KSADS‐COMP; Townsend et al. 2020). The conduct behaviors section of the KSADS‐COMP includes groups of questions related to presence and frequency of past and current behaviors. One group of questions asks youth whether they have ever engaged in a list of behaviors (e.g., Was there ever a time that you often told lies?) and asks them to answer yes or no (0 = No; 1 = Yes). Questions related to the frequency of behaviors were not included in analyses. Symptom counts were calculated by summing dichotomous (yes/no) items, and in accordance with prior research using ABCD Study data, were then used as a continuous measure of conduct severity (Tillem et al. 2022).

Outcome Variable

Overall Distress

Overall distress was assessed via the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL; Achenbach and Rescorla 2001), a parent‐report measure designed to assess behavioral and emotional problems in children and adolescents aged 6‐18. The CBCL contains 113 items about problems related to anxiety, depression, somatic complaints, social problems, thought problems, attention problems, rule‐breaking behavior, and aggressive behavior. Responses are formatted as a 3‐point Likert scale (0 = Not True [as far as you know], 1 = Somewhat or Sometimes True, 2 = Very True or Often True). Items are summed to compute a Total Problems score, such that a higher score corresponds with more extensive emotional and behavioral issues. Achenbach and Rescorla (2001) reported that the total problems score demonstrates strong internal consistency (α = 0.97). In the current sample, the total problems score also demonstrated good internal consistency (α = 0.90).

Latent Profile Analyses

Latent profile analyses (LPA) were conducted in R using the tidyLPA package (Rosenberg et al. 2018) to identify mutually‐exclusive latent subgroups of youth with antisocial behaviors. Unlike factor and regression analyses, which are primarily variable‐centered, LPA is a person‐centered analytic approach. Its primary assumption relies on the idea that an unobserved, or latent, variable may have explanatory utility in interpreting observed behaviors or phenomena (Williams and Kibowski 2016). As a result, LPA is especially suited to studying heterogenous populations and for exploratory analyses.

We used relational aggression, physical aggression, CU traits, and conduct symptom severity as profile indicators. We ran two LPAs – one with male participants only and one with female participants only – to allow profile structure to emerge independently within male and female participants. Due to the exploratory nature of this study, we used a step‐wise approach (Brislin et al. 2021) and relied on empirical fit indices and class interpretability to inform the selection of the best fitting model (Brislin et al. 2021; Collins and Lanza 2009).

Different patterns of aggression, callousness, and symptom severity were examined to account for heterogeneity in the sample. Model fit and entropy statistics for all tested models are presented in Table 3. In both the male and female analyses, a two‐profile solution provided the highest certainty of correct classification via each measure of model fit. Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) were acceptably low for the two‐profile model and entropy was 0.98 in the female sample and 0.87 in the male sample. The Bootstrapped Likelihood Ration Test (BLRT) provided evidence that there was a significant improvement (p = 0.01) when moving from a one‐ to a two‐profile model. Although the BLRT did indicate the potential of statistical improvement in models with more profiles, the associated decrease in other fit indices made this possibility less statistically appealing. Due to a preponderance of the empirical evidence, as well as parsimony and theoretical interpretability, the two‐profile solutions were retained for further analyses.

Group Comparison Analyses

Following group identification, the groups were compared via a one‐way ANOVA with overall distress as the dependent variable. The one‐way ANOVA revealed a significant difference in overall distress between the groups, F(3, 7651) = 63.08, p < 0.001, η² = 0.02, suggesting a small effect size. Although statistically significant, the overall ANOVA effect size was small (η²p = 0.02), indicating that differences, while reliable, may have modest practical or clinical impact.

Results were further analyzed through Games‐Howell post‐hoc comparison to investigate how groups differed on CBCL Total Problems scores as well as to appropriately account for unequal variance among groups. Given the number of analyses conducted, as well as the large sample size, the statistical significance threshold was adjusted via a Bonferroni correction to p < 0.005 to minimize risk of Type I error. Post‐hoc comparison indicated that the High Aggression Group‐Male had significantly higher CBCL Total Problems scores than did the Profile 1‐Female (M = 4.44, SE = 0.97, d = 0.36), Profile 2‐Female (M = 8.69, SE = 0.88, d = 0.75), and Profile 2‐Male groups (M = 6.85, SE = 0.88, d = 0.58), p < 0.001. The Profile 1‐Female Group had significantly higher scores on the CBCL Total Problems scale than Profile 2‐Female (M = 4.25, SE = 0.50, d = 0.37) and Profile 1‐Male (M = 2.41, SE = 0.50, d = 0.19), p < 0.001 (see Table 5).

Discussion

Prior research has established the need to better understand the heterogeneity of conduct problems, given the implications for assessment, intervention, and outcomes (Keenan et al. 2008). The current study identified latent profiles based on aggression, conduct symptom severity, and CU traits, each of which has been linked to impairment and distress (Nock et al. 2006; Barker et al. 2007). Rather than reflecting consistently high or low scores across indicators, the LPA profiles revealed a more nuanced pattern of differences.

For female participants, CU traits appeared to be the primary distinguishing trait, whereas for male participants, physical aggression was more influential in group formation. These differences may reflect legitimate sex differences in the presentation of symptoms, problem severity, and referral rates (see Fairchild et al. 2019), although it is also possible that they are indicative of a gendered socialization process in which boys are more likely to express antisocial traits via physical aggression and girls are more likely to demonstrate similar traits relationally or affectively (Card et al. 2008). Furthermore, the contribution of measurement factors must be considered. For example, relational aggression may be more easily and reliably observed in girls, even if boys and girls are engaging in relationally aggressive behaviors similarly.

The formed groups also shed light on the interplay between CU traits, aggression, and symptom severity in predicting psychosocial distress. The Profile 1‐Female group, which had the highest mean scores on CU traits, was not the group with the highest level of overall distress. Although the Profile 1‐Male group had significantly higher levels of overall distress than all other formed groups, they had lower mean levels of CU traits than did the Profile 1‐Female group. This pattern aligns with prior research suggesting that CU traits and aggressive behavior can independently lead to disruptions in psychosocial functioning, but their interaction predicts psychosocial impairment above and beyond their independent effects (Fairchild et al. 2019; Frick et al. 2014). From a developmental perspective, these findings are consistent with Moffitt's taxonomy, which posits that life course‐persistent and adolescent‐limited pathways may manifest differently across sexes and that CU traits may moderate the impact of aggression on functioning and distress (Moffitt and Caspi 2001).

In addition to clear theoretical implications, these findings have practical significance for the assessment of and intervention with youth exhibiting antisocial behaviors. Assessments should be multidimensional to capture the possible range of behavioral indicators and affective traits characteristic of youth with antisocial traits. Assessments that focus solely on overt aggressive behaviors may overlook important traits indicative of significant distress and social difficulties. These findings may be especially useful to inform screening practices as a way to prioritize intervention targets, especially in schools and clinics, and to select appropriate strategies. Interventions tailored towards building skills that are most implicated in the expression of antisocial behaviors are likely to be especially beneficial. For example, girls endorsing high levels of CU traits will likely benefit most from interventions that focus on building affective and social‐cognitive skills, such as empathy training, perspective‐taking, and emotional awareness (see for review Magram et al. 2025). In contrast, boys demonstrating high levels of both physical and relational aggression will likely benefit most from combined approaches, such as interventions that specifically target the development of skills related to social problem‐solving and behavior regulation.

Limitations and Future Directions

While this study addressed gaps in the literature and contributed to the knowledge base related to relational aggression and conduct problems, several limitations should be noted. The age range (10.5–14 years of age) limited exploration of age‐of‐onset effects on group formation. Additionally, although the ABCD Study provided a wealth of data and resources, this study is limited to the measures available through the study, which was not originally intended to study CU traits or delinquent behaviors. As such, it was necessary to use a brief CU measure created by summing items in extant ABCD Study measures (Hawes et al. 2020). The brief measure has demonstrated promising psychometric properties; however, it is possible that the use of a more robust measure of CU traits may have been more effective in measuring the construct (e.g., Inventory of Callous Unemotional Traits; Frick 2004). Furthermore, it is possible that the measure is capturing the absence of prosocial skills rather than the presence of antisocial traits, thereby failing to fully capture the CU construct. The lack of prosocial skills may be associated with any number of social, emotional, and developmental disorders, including autism spectrum disorder or ADHD (American Psychiatric Association 2022), neither of which are synonymous with antisocial behaviors. Future research may consider the inclusion of additional measures to the ABCD Study protocol or other new studies designed more specifically to understand disruptive behavior disorders, both of which would aid in further investigating how to best identify youth and families who need targeted intervention.

Grouping variables were derived from different informants, which may introduce informant‐specific variance. Although the use of multiple informants is a strength and captures best practices in youth psychopathology research, the use of different informants across measures could potentially influence group classification and observed associations. Results should therefore be interpreted with this consideration in mind.

Furthermore, although sex‐specific latent profile analyses allowed profile structures to emerge independently within males and females, this approach did not formally test profile equivalence across sex. Multigroup latent profile analysis or models including sex as a predictor of class membership would permit direct evaluation of structural invariance and prevalence differences (Morin et al. 2016). Future research should employ such approaches to determine whether the configurations observed here reflect truly distinct sex‐specific patterns or partially overlapping profiles that differ in distribution.

Low base rates of CU traits scores in the sample may limit the generalizability of findings to youth experiencing clinical levels of symptoms. Although the ABCD Study was designed to be both diverse and representative, the prevalence of CU traits is relatively low in the general population (Fairchild et al. 2019). This was demonstrated in this sample as well (Range = 0–4, M = 0.94, SD = 1.09), which presents the possibility that data from alternative samples, such as juvenile detention centers or emotional and behavioral treatment facilities, where base rates of CU traits are higher, may result in different or alternative groups based on youth performance on profile indicators.

Conclusion

Overall, these findings underscore the importance of parsing heterogeneity in youth antisocial behavior. Distinct combinations of aggression, CU traits, and conduct symptoms were associated with different levels of psychosocial distress, with sex‐specific patterns likely influenced by a combination of gendered socialization, behavioral expression, and measurement factors. Understanding these profiles has the potential to directly inform the precision of assessment and intervention, allowing researchers and practitioners alike to better address the diverse needs of youth exhibiting antisocial behavior.
