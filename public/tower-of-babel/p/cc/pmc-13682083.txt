Skeletal muscle metastasis from gastric carcinoma: a systematic review of published case reports and case series over six decades
Authors: Srikanth Thiyagarajan, Mahalakshmi N, Abhishek Reji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSkeletal muscle metastasis (SMM) from gastric carcinoma is exceptionally rare despite the extensive vascularity and mass of skeletal muscles. The true prevalence remains unknown, and existing knowledge is limited to isolated case reports.ObjectiveTo systematically review all reported cases of SMM arising from gastric carcinoma, summarize clinical features, diagnostic approaches, imaging characteristics, treatments, and outcomes, and highlight gaps in existing literature.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Google Scholar from inception to January 2025, following PRISMA guidelines. All case reports or case series describing biopsy-confirmed skeletal muscle metastasis from gastric carcinoma were included.ResultsA total of 31 fully documented cases were identified from 30 published studies spanning 1960–2025. Patients ranged in age from 30 to 80 years, with a male predominance. Skeletal muscle metastases most commonly involved the thigh, gluteal, paraspinal, and abdominal wall muscles. Patients typically presented with painful, enlarging intramuscular masses that were frequently misdiagnosed as abscesses or soft-tissue sarcomas. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) were the most sensitive imaging modalities, while histopathological examination remained the gold standard for diagnosis. Most lesions were associated with poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma or signet-ring cell carcinoma. Survival data were available for 18 of the 31 patients; among these, the mean survival following the diagnosis of skeletal muscle metastasis was 7.7 months, with a median survival of 4.0 months (range, 0.6–30 months), indicating a poor overall prognosis.ConclusionSMM from gastric carcinoma is extremely rare but Despite its rarity, skeletal muscle metastasis should be considered in patients with gastric carcinoma presenting with new painful intramuscular masses, particularly during postoperative surveillance. Early recognition using MRI or PET-CT followed by histopathological confirmation may facilitate timely multidisciplinary management, although the prognosis remains poor.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05119-3.

Introduction

Gastric carcinoma remains a major global health burden, ranking fifth in incidence and fourth in cancer-related mortality. Metastatic spread commonly involves the liver, lymph nodes, peritoneum, lung, and bone. However, skeletal muscle metastasis (SMM) is exceedingly rare, reported in less than 1% of all metastatic cancers and even rarer in gastric cancer.

Skeletal muscles constitute nearly 40% of total body mass and receive abundant blood supply, yet metastasis to these tissues is remarkably uncommon. Proposed protective mechanisms include contractile activity, high tissue pressure, lactic acid accumulation, and an active immune environment.

Since the first recorded case in the 1960s, only isolated case reports have been published. A systematic understanding of SMM from gastric carcinoma is lacking.

This review consolidates all available evidence over six decades to define clinical patterns, diagnostic considerations, treatment outcomes, and areas for future research.

Methods

This review followed the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines.

Data sources

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement. A comprehensive literature search was performed across PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Google Scholar from database inception to January 2025.In studies reporting more than one patient, each case was extracted and analyzed individually.

Search terms

The search strategy included combinations of keywords and Boolean operators such as: (“gastric cancer” OR “gastric carcinoma”) AND (“skeletal muscle metastasis” OR “intramuscular metastasis” OR “muscle metastasis”).

Two independent reviewers screened titles, abstracts, and full texts. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Inclusion criteria

Studies were considered eligible for inclusion if they reported histologically confirmed gastric carcinoma with radiologically and/or biopsy-confirmed skeletal muscle metastasis. Case reports, case series, and observational studies describing such presentations were included in the review.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they described metastases involving non-skeletal muscle structures such as the myocardium or diaphragm, reported cases with an unknown primary tumor, or involved metastases arising from esophageal or gastroesophageal junction malignancies unless the primary tumor was explicitly confirmed to be gastric in origin.

Data extraction

From all eligible reports, relevant clinical and demographic data were extracted when available. The extracted variables included patient age and sex, the specific skeletal muscle involved, presence of metastases at other sites, treatment modalities administered for the primary gastric tumor, treatment directed toward the skeletal muscle lesion, and survival duration following the diagnosis of skeletal muscle metastasis.For descriptive analyses, summary statistics were calculated using only cases with available data for the relevant variable. Cases with missing information were excluded from the denominator for that specific analysis. A total of 30 studies describing 31 patients were included in the final analysis. PRISMA flow summary

The systematic selection process is summarized in a flow diagram detailing the progression from initial database identification to final inclusion. A total of 487 records were identified through electronic database searches of PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Google Scholar. After removal of 165 duplicate records, 322 unique articles remained and were screened based on titles and abstracts. Following this initial screening, 33 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Of these, 30 reports met the eligibility requirements and were included in the qualitative synthesis. These reports collectively described 31 individual patients, which constituted the final study population for this systematic review.One included study (Touheti et al., 2004) reported two individual patients.

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes of interest included the clinical presentation patterns, diagnostic characteristics, treatment strategies employed, and survival outcomes following the detection of skeletal muscle metastasis.

Each study was evaluated across eight domains, including patient demographics, clinical history, diagnostic clarity, intervention details, outcomes, and follow-up.

Methods justification

Given the rarity of skeletal muscle metastasis from gastric carcinoma and the absence of large-scale observational studies or randomized controlled trials, a systematic review of published case reports and case series was considered the most appropriate methodological approach. This design enables comprehensive aggregation of dispersed evidence to identify clinical patterns, diagnostic features, treatment strategies, and outcomes associated with this uncommon metastatic presentation.

Results

Overview

The study selection process is summarized in Fig. 1. Thirty studies describing 31 patients were included in the final qualitative synthesis.

Patient characteristics and histology

The 31 reported patients with skeletal muscle metastasis (SMM) from gastric carcinoma had a mean age of approximately 58 years (range, 30–80 years), with a clear male predominance. Histopathological information was available for the majority of reported cases. Poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma and signet-ring cell carcinoma were the predominant histological subtypes, indicating that skeletal muscle metastasis is more frequently associated with biologically aggressive gastric cancers. Histological subtype was unavailable in several earlier case reports, reflecting variations in reporting practices over the study period.

Timing of metastasis

Based on the interval between the diagnosis of the primary gastric carcinoma and the detection of skeletal muscle metastasis, two distinct patterns were observed. Metachronous metastases were more frequently reported, among patients with metachronous disease, the interval between the diagnosis (or curative treatment) of the primary gastric carcinoma and the development of skeletal muscle metastasis ranged from several months to 84 months, demonstrating that skeletal muscle recurrence may occur long after apparently successful treatment, whereas synchronous metastases were identified at the time of the initial diagnosis. This variability highlights the need for continued clinical vigilance for skeletal muscle metastasis throughout the disease course, particularly in patients presenting with new painful intramuscular masses.

Common muscle sites

Skeletal muscle metastasis occurred at a wide range of anatomical sites. The most frequently involved locations were the lower limb muscles, including the calf, soleus, gastrocnemius, quadriceps, vastus lateralis, psoas, iliacus, sartorius, and gluteal muscles. Metastatic involvement was also reported in upper limb and trunk muscles such as the deltoid, trapezius, latissimus dorsi, and abdominal wall musculature. In addition, several cases demonstrated involvement of extraocular muscles, including the medial and lateral rectus and superior oblique muscles, as well as paraspinal and lumbar muscles. This broad anatomical distribution underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of clinical suspicion when evaluating patients with unexplained skeletal muscle swelling or pain.

Clinical presentation

Patients most commonly presented with a painful or firm enlarging skeletal muscle mass. In some cases, the presentation mimicked an acute inflammatory process, resembling conditions such as abscess or myositis. When extraocular muscles were involved, neurological manifestations such as diplopia were observed. In rare instances, skeletal muscle metastasis represented the initial manifestation of gastric carcinoma, as reported in several cases. Owing to these nonspecific clinical features, misdiagnosis was frequent, with lesions often initially interpreted as abscesses, soft-tissue sarcomas, or hematomas.

Associated metastasis

Many patients exhibited synchronous systemic dissemination at the time of diagnosis of skeletal muscle metastasis, most commonly involving the peritoneum, lungs, liver, brain, and regional or distant lymph nodes. However, several reports described isolated or metachronous skeletal muscle metastasis without evidence of concurrent visceral involvement, including cases affecting the calf or thigh musculature. These presentations suggest that skeletal muscle metastasis may occasionally occur as a solitary or delayed metastatic manifestation.

Diagnostic workup

Magnetic resonance imaging was the most sensitive diagnostic modality, typically demonstrating heterogeneous intramuscular masses with peripheral rim enhancement and surrounding edema. Positron emission tomography–computed tomography was highly effective in identifying multifocal metastatic disease owing to intense fluorodeoxyglucose uptake within metastatic lesions. Definitive diagnosis was established by tissue biopsy, which commonly revealed immunohistochemical positivity for cytokeratin 7 and CDX2 in intestinal-type gastric adenocarcinoma. In addition, atypical radiologic features such as heterotopic ossification within skeletal muscle metastases were described in some reports.

Treatment approaches

Given the frequently disseminated nature of disease at presentation, systemic chemotherapy—most commonly platinum-based regimens combined with fluoropyrimidines—was the primary treatment modality in the majority of reported cases. Palliative radiotherapy was employed for local symptom control, particularly for pain relief. Surgical excision of skeletal muscle lesions was undertaken in selected patients, either for diagnostic confirmation or for palliative purposes. In more recent reports, immunotherapeutic approaches, including HER2-targeted therapy and programmed death ligand-1 inhibitors, were utilized in selected cases based on tumor molecular characteristics.

Prognosis

Survival outcomes following the diagnosis of skeletal muscle metastasis suggest that this manifestation generally represents a terminal stage of disease, although occasional outliers have been reported. Among patients with available follow-up (n = 18), the mean survival after diagnosis of skeletal muscle metastasis was 7.7 months, while the median survival was 4.0 months (range 0.6–30 months). The longest reported survival was 30 months, as described by Gogou et al. These findings highlight the importance of early recognition—particularly through sensitive imaging modalities such as magnetic resonance imaging or positron emission tomography–computed tomography—to facilitate timely transition to appropriate palliative care strategies.

Discussion

Interpretation

Skeletal muscle metastasis from gastric adenocarcinoma is a rare but important clinical entity. The large number of muscles reported—extraocular to gluteal—highlights that metastasis can occur in any skeletal muscle group. The heterogeneity in presentation often leads to misdiagnosis as abscess, soft-tissue sarcoma, thrombophlebitis, or benign inflammatory conditions.

The rarity of skeletal muscle metastasis, despite high vascularity, remains a paradox in oncology. This review consolidates over six decades of evidence and highlights that such metastases may not be as incidental as previously thought but rather underreported due to diagnostic challenges.

The predominance of metastases involving the thigh and gluteal muscles observed in this review may reflect their relatively large muscle mass and abundant vascular perfusion, increasing the likelihood of tumour cell entrapment during haematogenous dissemination. Nevertheless, no specific skeletal muscle group appears to be selectively predisposed, and metastases have been reported in diverse anatomical locations, emphasizing the need for clinicians to maintain a high index of suspicion when evaluating new painful intramuscular lesions in patients with current or previous gastric carcinoma.

Pathophysiology of resistance

Several physiological mechanisms have been proposed to explain the relative resistance of skeletal muscle to metastatic involvement. The unique metabolic environment of skeletal muscle, characterized by high concentrations of lactic acid and fluctuating oxygen levels, may result in a locally acidic milieu that inhibits tumor cell proliferation or induces apoptosis. In addition, constant muscle contractility and elevated intramuscular pressure generate mechanical forces that create an unfavorable environment for stable implantation and growth of metastatic cells. Furthermore, skeletal muscle exhibits active immune surveillance, with local production of anti-tumor cytokines and immune mediators that may further suppress metastatic colonization.

The predominance of metachronous skeletal muscle metastasis highlights the importance of continued oncological surveillance even after apparently curative treatment of the primary gastric tumour. In several cases, skeletal muscle involvement developed months or years after gastrectomy, suggesting that dormant micrometastatic disease may persist despite initial disease control. Consequently, new painful or enlarging intramuscular masses in patients with a history of gastric carcinoma should prompt consideration of metastatic disease, regardless of the interval since primary treatment.

Diagnostic challenges

Although MRI provides excellent soft-tissue characterization and PET-CT facilitates the detection of metabolically active lesions throughout the body, imaging findings alone are frequently insufficient to distinguish skeletal muscle metastases from abscesses, primary soft-tissue sarcomas, or inflammatory myopathies. Therefore, histopathological confirmation through image-guided core needle biopsy or excisional biopsy remains essential for establishing the diagnosis and guiding subsequent management.

Therapeutic implications

At present, there is no standardized treatment protocol for skeletal muscle metastasis from gastric adenocarcinoma. Systemic chemotherapy, most commonly platinum-based regimens combined with fluoropyrimidines, remains the cornerstone of management due to the frequent presence of disseminated disease. Palliative modalities, including radiotherapy for pain control and surgical excision for isolated or symptomatic lesions, play an important supportive role. In selected contemporary cases, targeted and immunotherapeutic strategies—such as HER2-directed therapy and programmed death ligand-1 inhibitors—have been employed based on tumor molecular profiling.

Prognosis

The poor survival observed in this review likely reflects the presence of advanced systemic disease rather than the skeletal muscle metastasis itself. Skeletal muscle involvement is commonly accompanied by widespread metastatic dissemination, limiting the effectiveness of local therapies. The longest interval to skeletal muscle metastasis identified in this review was seven years, illustrating the unpredictable biological behaviour of gastric carcinoma and reinforcing the need for continued vigilance beyond conventional surveillance periods.Consequently, treatment is generally individualized, with systemic chemotherapy forming the cornerstone of management, while surgery or radiotherapy may be considered for selected patients with isolated symptomatic lesions or for palliative purposes.

Methodology defence

The present study adopts a systematic review approach focusing on case reports and small case series, which represents the highest level of available evidence for rare clinical entities such as skeletal muscle metastasis from gastric carcinoma. Although such evidence is inherently descriptive, it provides valuable insights into clinical presentation patterns, diagnostic challenges, and therapeutic strategies that cannot be captured through conventional large-scale studies due to the extremely low incidence of this condition.

Despite inherent limitations, systematic aggregation of rare case-based evidence remains essential for improving clinical awareness and guiding decision-making in uncommon oncological presentations.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the available evidence consists exclusively of case reports and small case series, making the findings susceptible to publication and reporting bias. Second, the rarity of skeletal muscle metastasis from gastric carcinoma resulted in a limited sample size, precluding quantitative meta-analysis and restricting the statistical power of subgroup analyses. Third, several reports lacked complete information regarding histopathological subtype, immunohistochemical findings, interval to metastasis, treatment details, and follow-up duration, which limited the precision of pooled estimates. Additionally, variations in diagnostic techniques, therapeutic strategies, and reporting standards over the six-decade study period introduced considerable clinical heterogeneity. Finally, survival analyses were based only on patients with available follow-up data, and several cases reported ongoing treatment or incomplete follow-up, potentially influencing the calculated survival estimates.

Future studies should focus on the establishment of multicentre registries and collaborative databases to facilitate the systematic collection of clinicopathological characteristics, molecular profiles, treatment strategies, and long-term outcomes of patients with skeletal muscle metastasis from gastric carcinoma. Standardized reporting of rare metastatic presentations will improve understanding of disease biology, enable more robust prognostic analyses, and help develop evidence-based recommendations for diagnosis and management.

Conclusion

Skeletal muscle metastasis from gastric carcinoma represents a rare but diagnostically challenging manifestation of advanced disease. Early recognition using high-resolution imaging and timely biopsy is essential to avoid misdiagnosis. Future multicenter registries and molecular studies are required to elucidate mechanisms of muscle tropism and improve therapeutic strategies.

While most cases represent advanced, disseminated disease with a poor prognosis, early recognition is vital for optimizing palliative strategies and improving the patient’s quality of life. Future research utilizing international registries and molecular profiling is needed to better understand the mechanisms of muscle tropism and evaluate the efficacy of modern immunotherapies.
