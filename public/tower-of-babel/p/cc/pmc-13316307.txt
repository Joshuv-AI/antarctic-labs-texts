Monoclonal Antibodies in Pediatric Atopic Dermatitis; Efficacy and Safety
Authors: Niloofar Masoumi, Taha Monadi, Zahra Aminzade, Afsaneh Soltani, Hesam Noqani, Seyed Ehsan Enderami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtopic dermatitis (AD), which causes eczema, dryness, and itching, is one of the most prevalent skin diseases in children. Along with topical corticosteroids and the liberal application of emollients, monoclonal antibodies have recently been used for systemic treatment in moderate to severe cases. This study aims to review the current literature on the application of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) in treating children with AD.MethodsTo conduct a narrative review of the literature, we searched for the keywords (monoclonal antibodies OR biologics) AND (pediatric OR children) AND (atopic dermatitis OR eczema) in PubMed and Google Scholar.ResultsThe pathophysiology of atopic dermatitis still requires better understanding (or, remains incompletely understood). The most well-known factors contributing to the pathophysiology of AD include filaggrin gene mutations and abnormalities in Th1 and Th2 cell regulation. Cytokines, such as IL-4, IL-13, IL-17, IL-22, IL-31, and IL-33, are crucial mediators that lead to IgE antibody production and result in skin barrier degradation. Monoclonal antibodies have several advantages over other systemic therapies. Among these, dupilumab, tralokinumab, omalizumab, nemolizumab, lebrikizumab, and benralizumab have shown favorable results.ConclusionA systemic immunomodulatory medication (monoclonal antibodies) is frequently required for moderate to severe AD as supplementary treatment. Dupilumab has received FDA approval for pediatric AD. Further clinical trials are needed for other mAbs before they are used in children.Key words: Atopic dermatitis, Biologics, Cytokines, Dupilumab, Monoclonal antibodies, Pediatrics

Introduction

According to an estimate by the International Study of Asthma and Allergies in Childhood (ISAAC), AD affects about 15% to 20% of children globally.1 Even though the pathogenesis of AD is still incompletely understood, recent research has revealed a complex interaction between genetic predisposition, environmental and nutritional factors, and immunological dysregulation.2 A genetic mutation in the Filaggrin (FLG) gene causes dryness and irritation, the introduction of allergens and microbes, and the activation and inflammation of the immune system.3

According to existing evidence, sensitization and atopy in AD are primarily driven by skin barrier defects.4 However, immune dysregulation due to T helper cell type 1 (Th1) and T helper cell type 2 (Th2) also plays an essential role in AD pathogenesis. The skin of AD patients has an unbalanced T helper cell population, particularly Th1, Th2, Th17, and Th22. Moreover, acute and chronic stages of AD are marked by elevated Th2 cells, whereas the chronic phase of the disease is characterized by elevated Th1 cells.5, 6, 7

Clinically, AD manifests as eczematous lesions, xerosis, and pruritus. Infants and young children typically have more severe involvement in some areas, such as the face, scalp, and extensors.2 Patients with mild to moderate disease are initially treated with emollients, topical corticosteroids, and calcineurin inhibitors. However, moderate to severe cases also need systemic therapy to achieve adequate control. Systemic medications include cyclosporine, azathioprine, methotrexate, and mycophenolate mofetil.8,9 However, some concerns about the efficacy and safety of these systemic agents may restrict their use.9 Advances in our understanding of the etiopathogenesis of AD could help us identify additional targets for biological treatments as an alternative to conventional systemic therapy.10 A few novel medications have been released recently, with biologics dominating the market. Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have several benefits over other systemic treatments, including enhanced selectivity and targeted therapy, longer half-life in lower doses, and less indirect toxicity.8,9 In this article, we review the efficacy and safety of various mAbs for treating AD in children, as well as pathophysiological mechanisms and biological therapies that are currently available.

Historically, moderate-to-severe pediatric atopic dermatitis (AD) was managed with broad-spectrum systemic immunosuppressants such as cyclosporine, azathioprine, and methotrexate.8 Advances in understanding the immunopathogenesis of AD enabled the development of targeted cytokine-based therapies, culminating in the emergence of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs).8,11,12 Recognition of AD as a heterogeneous, endotype-driven disease has facilitated the development of pathway-specific biologics tailored to distinct immunologic profiles.12

Although atopic dermatitis (AD) has been extensively reviewed, pediatric-focused reviews remain scarce, particularly regarding novel biologics and emerging monoclonal antibodies.11,12 The recent emergence and regulatory approval of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) such as dupilumab and tralokinumab, targeting key cytokines including IL-4, IL-13, and IL-31, highlight unique therapeutic and safety considerations in children.8,11,12

This review aims to integrate current insights into immunopathogenesis with clinical pharmacotherapy, emphasizing recent regulatory developments and providing evidence-based guidance for pediatric AD management within the paradigm of precision medicine and targeted immunotherapy.

Method

A literature search was conducted using keywords such as monoclonal antibodies, biologics, pediatric, children, atopic dermatitis, and eczema, both individually and in combination, within the electronic databases of PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science, and ScienceDirect. Articles published up to October 2025 were considered in this study. In addition to peer-reviewed research articles, relevant review articles, clinical guidelines, and conference abstracts were examined to provide a comprehensive overview of the topic. Studies were screened based on title and abstract, and full-text articles were assessed for eligibility according to predefined inclusion criteria (clinical studies, pediatric population, outcomes related to efficacy or safety) and exclusion criteria (non-English language, studies focused solely on adults). While a formal risk-of-bias assessment was not conducted, study quality and level of evidence were considered when interpreting results.11,12

Results

Pathophysiology of atopic dermatitis: insights into the stratum corneum dysfunction, immune response, and cytokine regulation

The stratum corneum and immune system dysfunction in atopic dermatitis

Atopic dermatitis pathogenesis is complex, involving a multifaceted interplay between the stratum corneum's abnormalities, immune system dysfunction, and behaviors associated with pruritus, such as scratching-induced skin damage and exacerbation of inflammation-related reactions.13 The stratum corneum serves as an essential physical barrier, significantly contributing to epidermal function, with its clinical degradation being the most evident feature of atopic dermatitis (AD).14 Essential proteins like Involucrin (IVL), Loricrin (LOR), Filaggrin (FLG), transglutaminases, keratins, and intracellular proteins work in harmony to maintain a healthy stratum corneum.15,16 However, the additional strain of immunological activation exacerbates the weakening of the stratum corneum, leading to increased inflammation and heightened allergic sensitivity to allergens.17

The immune etiology of AD remains a subject of debate, as interactions among numerous factors contribute to its development, including but not limited to the inside-out and outside-in theories. The former emphasizes immune dysregulation as the primary driver of AD, with allergen exposure weakening the stratum corneum. Conversely, the latter hypothesis posits that a damaged dermal barrier represents the preliminary phase in the evolution of AD, paving the way for immune dysregulation to occur.18 The congenital immune response comprises several key elements, including cytokines, immune system cells, pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), skin microbes, antimicrobial peptides, and the epidermal barrier.19 Receptors for pattern recognition on both immune and nonimmune cells can identify pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) common to fungus, bacteria, viruses, and protozoa. The innate immune system utilizes PRRs to differentiate between the body's cells and foreign microorganisms. These receptors can be divided into 4 notable families: RIG-I-like, NOD-like, Toll-like, and C-type lectin receptors.20 These are various receptor types that can be found on different immune cells. Toll-like receptor's primary function is to recognize dangerous pathogens,21 whereas RLRs can precisely identify viral RNA and induce an immunological response in the body.22 By detecting bacterial products, nucleotide oligomerization domain (NOD)-like receptors are essential for innate immunity.23 C-type lectin receptors, on the other hand, are in charge of identifying carbohydrate structures found on pathogens.24 Activation of these types of receptors induces the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, facilitating the progression of AD.25

T-helper cells and cytokine alterations in AD

The adaptive immune response is initiated upon capturing allergens, antigens, and superantigens by antigen-presenting cells, all of which are then presented to specific CD4 (Cluster of Differentiation 4) memory T-cells. The imbalance in the reaction of Th lymphocytes 1 and 2 is considered a hallmark characteristic of AD, with a predominance of Th2 responses.26,27

Acute stage

During acute phase, there is a rise in the release of cytokines from Th2 and Th22 cells, as well as Th17 cells, to a lesser extent. Th2 cytokines, including IL-4 and IL-13, are critical mediators in AD development and are secreted by multiple cellular phenotypes like basophils, eosinophils, mast cells, keratinocytes, and ILC2.28 These cytokines induce the formation of IgE antibodies and the recruitment of eosinophils, contributing to the pathogenesis of AD.29

Chronic stage

As the lesions progress, there is an escalation in Th1 response markers, leading to a further strengthening of Th2 and Th22 responses.30 During the chronic phase of AD, Th1 lymphocyte chemotaxis and cytokine secretion, including IL-2, IL-12, TNFα (Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha), and IFN (Interferon), are increased. Th22 and Th17 cytokines, including IL-17, IL-19, and IL-22, also promote the pathogenesis of AD.31 IL-36, a pro-inflammatory cytokine categorized under the IL-1 clan, has been implicated in a pivotal function in advancing AD.32

Immune signaling pathways in AD

Alongside Th2 cytokines, other immune regulatory pathways have been associated with the pathogenesis of atopic dermatitis in particular populations.33

IL-31 mediated signaling pathway

IL-31, primarily secreted by activated Th2 cells and requiring IL-4 signaling for transcription, induces pruritus in AD patients by stimulating the secretion of natriuretic peptides from the brain and promoting the cytokines and chemokines expression by skin cells. IL-31 also promotes the enlargement and branching of sensory nerves, leading to heightened sensitivity to stimuli.34,35

Thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) mediated signaling pathway

TSLP, released in human epithelial tissues such as the skin, triggers the activation of tissue-resident dendritic cells (DCs), which stimulate naïve CD4+ T cells and spread to the nearby lymph node, producing Th2. As a result, TSLP contributes to the development of memory T cells and potentially to the onset, growth, and advancement of atopy. TSLP is the primary pruritogen that triggers an interconnected signaling pathway between the epidermis, immune, and nervous systems, contributing to inflammation and itch in individuals with AD. Moreover, TSLP indirectly induces pruritus by stimulating specific immune system cells to secrete substances that can trigger irritation, such as IL-4 and IL-13.36, 37, 38, 39, 40

IL-33 signaling pathway

IL-33 triggers the NF-kB (Nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells) and MAP (Mitogen-activated protein) pathways through its receptor in the skin, activating an immune response involving ILC2 and the release of cytokines like IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13.41 In AD, infiltration of ILC2 cells in damaged skin has been detected, and multiple receptors, including IL-25, IL-33 ST2, PGD2 CRTH2, and TSLP, were reported to be upregulated on these cells.33,42 IL-25 also triggers the production of several chemokines, like eotaxin, TARC (CCL17), and MDC (Macrophage-Derived Chemokine).43

Interaction between TSLP and IL-33 signaling pathways

TSLP strongly activates immature dendritic cells, which results in increased chemokine production while simultaneously inhibiting IL-12 secretion. This process promotes the recruitment and chemotaxis of eosinophils, as well as the development of antigen-presenting cells. Furthermore, TSLP amplifies the expression of cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 in IL-33-activated human ILC2 cells, cementing its role as an essential mediator in multiple facets of the immune response.44,45

Cytokine exposure and downregulation of key barrier proteins

AD is connected to malfunctioning of the skin barrier, which increases allergen sensitization and elevated IgE levels.46 Encountering several cytokines, such as IL-4, IL-13, IL-17, IL-22, IL-31, IL-33, and high-mobility group box 1, can lead to a decrease in the expression of crucial skin barrier components, including FLG, keratins, LOR, and cell adhesion molecules.26,33,42,47 Furthermore, pruritus-induced scratching might weaken the skin's protective layer and encourage Staphylococcus aureus colonization, which prolongs the Th2 immune reaction and exacerbates the overexpression of IL-4, IL-13, and IL-22, leading to the aggravation of AD symptoms.26

Biologic pharmacotherapy in pediatric atopic dermatitis

Biologic drugs are a pharmaceutical subset designed to target specific inflammatory mediators. Recent advancements in biological therapies have drawn attention to them in recent years. However, it took until 2017 for the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to approve them for treating AD. Until now, dupilumab and tralokinumab have received FDA approval as biological treatments for AD (dupilumab from 6 months and tralokinumab from 12 years old). Both belong to the category of biologics known as mAbs. Other mAbs are being investigated and may receive FDA approval. In the following, we will introduce and review the pharmacotherapy of biological drugs in pediatric AD.48,49

Dupilumab

Dupilumab, a fully humanized mAb, targets interleukin-4 (IL-4) receptor alpha subunit (IL-4Rα). As a result, IL-4 and IL-13 intracellular signaling will be blocked. Additionally, it has the potential to lower serum levels of IgE.50 Dupilumab (DUPI) is used to treat different allergic diseases, including allergic dermatitis, eczema, asthma, chronic spontaneous urticaria, and eosinophilic esophagitis. It also has been applied in nasal polyps, alopecia areata, and prurigo nodularis.51,52

Dupilumab was the first biological drug approved for children and adolescents in 2019; the FDA and European Medicines Agency approved it for adolescents aged 12 and up in 2020. In 2022, the approval was issued for children aged 6 years and older. When topical treatments are insufficiently effective for children and adolescents with moderate to severe AD or when alternative therapies are not advised, this medicine is suggested.53,54

A Real-Life Study confirmed the effectiveness of DUPI in children with moderate to severe AD aged 6 to 11 years old. It also showed a positive effect on the amount and severity of symptoms and generally affected these individuals' quality of life.55 DUPI has been shown to enhance skin barrier function. Also, a study demonstrated that children and adolescents using DUPI are at a lower risk of infection than placebo.56

Dupilumab, marketed under the name Dupixent, is given subcutaneously (SC) into the thigh, belly, or upper arm. It comes in a single-dose prefilled syringe. For moderate to severe AD, patients older than 6 and adolescents under 17 need an initial dose of DUPI based on age and weight. The initial amount for 6 to 17-year-old children, whose weight range is from 15 to 30 kg, is 600 mg then, followed by 300 mg every 2 weeks. Also, children with a 30 to 60 kg weight range need a 400 mg initial dose, followed by 200 mg every 2 weeks. Besides, children above 60 kg need 600 mg for the initial dose, followed by 300 mg every 4 weeks. Infants older than 6 months to 5 years do not need an initial amount. The dose selection for this age, for 5 to 15 kg weight, is 200 mg every 4 weeks, and for 15 to 30 kg is 300 mg every 4 weeks (see Table 1).57, 58, 59

Conjunctivitis, injection site response, temporary eosinophilia, and headache are the most common adverse effects of dupilumab, which has been reported to be well tolerated.60,61 Dupilumab, the first biologic approved for use in AD, has allowed other targeted therapies to be developed for AD.48

The approval of DUPI in pediatrics with AD was based on data reported from several clinical trials. Paller et al. performed a randomized, double-blinded, placebo-controlled phase III trial (NCT03345914) to examine the efficacy and safety of DUPI administration with simultaneous treatment with topical corticosteroids (TCS) in children aged from 6 to 11 years suffering from severe AD. The patients were randomized 1:1:1 to DUPI SC injection with concomitant medium-potency TCS in a 16-week treatment period. The first group was treated with 300 mg of DUPI injection every 4 weeks, the second group received a weight-based regimen every 2 weeks, and the third was treated with a placebo. Both DUPI regimens were well tolerated in children. When compared to patients who received the placebo, patients' symptoms, indicators, and quality of life showed a statistically significant improvement.62

In a retrospective, observational study, monotherapy with DUPI was assessed in children aged from 6 to 11 years. During a 16-week course of treatment, the extent and severity of signs and symptoms and the patient’s quality of life were positively affected.61 Another phase III randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in children older than 6 months and younger than 6 years with uncontrolled moderate-to-severe AD by topical treatments (NCT03346434) evaluated DUPI effectiveness and safety while concurrently administering low-potency TCS. Patients were treated with either a placebo or DUPI (200 or 300 mg based on the weight) administered every 4 weeks plus hydrocortisone acetate 1% cream over 16 weeks, compared to the placebo group. The arm's skin of patients who received DUPI appeared almost translucent and more remarkably improved in itching. Study results also indicated more reduced severity from the baseline achieved by DUPI injections. The safety profile in both 16- and long-term (52 weeks) treatment was similar to that in older patient trials.63 The findings of a phase IIa open-label trial followed by a phase III open-label extension study in children aged ≥6 to <12 years with severe AD support the sustained safety and efficacy of continued treatment with DUPI for up to 52 weeks (1 year).64 A 5-year study has been ongoing since this study was reported (Table 2).

Favorable outcomes and tolerability were also observed in adolescents treated with DUPI. The findings of a randomized, double-blind, parallel-group, phase III clinical trial (NCT03054428) showed 16-week treatment with DUPI (200 or 300 mg based on the patient’s weight) in adolescents (aged ≥12 to <18 years) with moderate to severe inadequately controlled AD was followed by major improvement in signs and symptoms, including itching and insomnia, reduced severity of the disease, and acceptable safety profiles. In general, the effectiveness of the every-2-week regimen was higher than that of the every-4-week strategy. They also observed that it improved comorbid conditions such as rhinitis and asthma in patients treated with DUPI. Moreover, nonherpetic infection rates on the skin decreased.65 Findings of a 52-week phase III open-label clinical trial (III) in 294 subjects (aged ≥12 to <18 years) showed response to every 2-week regimen in patients with inadequate response to every 4-week regimen, and suggest keeping the maintenance dose to prevent disease relapse or re-initiating of the treatment in case symptoms recur after complete recovery.66 The same results were also reported in other trials and case series.67, 68, 69, 70 Kamphius et al. evaluated the effectiveness and safety of DUPI usage in more severe AD (as they were on immunosuppressive drugs during DUPI initiation) pediatric patients in daily practice. After 28 weeks of treatment, the level of biomarkers associated with disease severity was decreased, and therapy with DUPI was accompanied by significant improvement in symptoms and severity of the disease.71 A pooled analysis of trial data indicated that AD treatment with DUPI in children aged 6 to 11 years and adolescents was followed by statistically significant lower rates of skin infection (both herpetic and nonherpetic) compared with placebo.72

Even though a few studies documented alopecia areata worsening or developing in adult AD patients using DUPI,73, 74, 75 some studies suggested DUPI as a therapeutic option in pediatric patients with alopecia areata and concomitant AD.76,77 As was previously mentioned, the most frequent side effects noted in pediatric DUPI trials include conjunctivitis and injection site responses. Also, keratitis, blepharitis, dry eye, eye pruritus, and herpes simplex virus infections have been associated with DUPI administration in adults and pediatric AD patients.78,79 Head and neck dermatitis, more frequent in adults, has been reported in adolescents who received DUPI for AD.80 One of the most crucial elements before beginning a new treatment option in children is its interference with vaccination. Based on the available data, vaccination during treatment is no risk factor for AD exacerbation. Fortunately, there is no need to interrupt the administration of inactivated vaccines. However, live attenuated vaccines must be avoided during DUPI treatment, and vaccination should be performed at least 4 weeks before initiation of DUPI.81

Tralokinumab

Tralokinumab, or Adtralza, is a high-affinity, fully human IgG4 mAb designed to neutralize IL-13. It inhibits the IL-13 cytokine by attaching to a heterodimeric receptor consisting of IL-4Rα, IL-13Rα1, and the IL-13Rα2 decoy receptor, hence reducing cytokine levels.82 Tralokinumab is the first and only biologic drug targeting IL-13 that has FDA approval (December 2021) for treating moderate to severe AD in teenagers and adults who are candidates for systemic treatment.83,84 The European Commission also approved the drug for adolescents aged 12 to 17 years with moderate to severe AD and insufficient response to topical therapy.85 This biological medication is administered SC in increments of 300 mg every 2 weeks after an initial dose of 600 mg.86 The approval was based on the results of the ECZTRA 6 trial conducted by Paller et al.87

In 52-week, international, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled studies, ECZTRA 1 (NCT03131648) and ECZTRA 2 (NCT03160885) (86) were also developed, with respective populations of 802 and 794. By dividing 3:1, patients received a loading dose of 600 mg on day 0 and 300 mg of the tralokinumab or placebo SC, every 2 weeks for 16 weeks as the primary endpoint. As signs and symptoms revealed, tralokinumab therapy is superior for moderate to severe AD over placebo. In 2:2:1, the drug was continued every 2 weeks, every 4 weeks, and placebo, respectively, until 36 additional weeks. The initial measurements showed early improvements in pruritus, sleep disruption, the Dermatology Life Quality Index, and AD scoring.

More than 50% of individuals administered tralokinumab biweekly at week 16 and exhibiting clinical improvements did so without requiring rescue medication through week 52, while 39% to 51% of patients who received tralokinumab every 4 weeks did the same. Unexpectedly, when tralokinumab responders at week 16 were rerandomized to placebo, 21% to 47% still had responses at week 52.88

ECZTRA 6 is a phase III, multinational, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group clinical trial (NCT03526861), which assessed the efficacy and safety of tralokinumab in 289 pediatric patients (age 12 to <18 years) with moderate to severe AD. Patients were randomly divided into 1:1:1 groups and received 150 or 300 mg tralokinumab or placebo SC injection every 2 weeks for 16 weeks as the primary endpoint. Patients who achieved the primary endpoint were randomly treated with Adtralza every 2 or 4 weeks for an additional 36 weeks. The results of the 52-week trial showed that tralokinumab was well tolerated in this age group. The treatment was accompanied by reduced disease severity and a significant improvement from baseline. The incidence of adverse effects, including conjunctivitis, was similar between Adtralza and the placebo group, and the safety profile was identical to trials in adult patients.89 More investigations are needed to investigate the efficacy, safety, effective dose, and side effects of tralokinumab in children under 12 years of age and to compare the choice of this drug with other available treatment options. Tralokinumab adverse events reports are viral upper RTI, conjunctivitis, and injection site pain and responses.90,91

Omalizumab

Omalizumab, sold under the brand name Xolair, is a recombinant humanized IgG1 antibody with a 95% human genome sequence against human IgE that has FDA approval for the treatment of moderate to severe persistent allergic asthma in patients aged 6 years or older92 and chronic idiopathic/spontaneous urticaria (CIU) in adults and adolescents over 12.93

The AD Anti-IgE Pediatric Trial (ADAPT) is a single-center, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (NCT02300701) that evaluated the safety and efficacy of SC omalizumab after 24 weeks of treatment in 62 children aged 4–19 years with severe AD who have failed optimal topical or systemic therapy.94 Patients were randomly allocated to the omalizumab and placebo groups, with the drug dosage established according to the total IgE level and body weight at the time of randomization. Unlike the results of the 2 previous RCTs conducted in adults, this study was associated with favorable clinical outcomes and acceptable safety profiles in children. This seems to be due to using a higher dose of omalizumab (more than twice) in this study. Patients tolerated treatment with omalizumab well and showed significantly improved quality of life. Decreased positive skin prick tests were observed in the omalizumab arm compared to the placebo group, which suggests improvement in associated atopic conditions. Omalizumab significantly ameliorated the severity and intensity of AD signs and symptoms despite highly elevated total IgE levels at baseline and improved the affected children's overall well-being. However, patients with lower baseline IgE levels showed a better response.94,95

Iyengar et al. evaluated the immunologic effects of omalizumab in a clinical randomized, placebo-controlled clinical study (NCT01678092) of 8 subjects with a mean age of 11.6 years suffering from severe refractory AD, unresponsive to standard therapy. Study results demonstrated meaningful changes in the patient's biomarker immunologic molecule with a known key role in AD pathogenesis. Free IgE levels significantly decreased in patients treated with omalizumab. Furthermore, omalizumab successfully decreased cytokines associated with AD pathogenesis. However, IL-10, a regulatory cytokine involved in the inflammation process, was surprisingly increased in the population treated with omalizumab, which supports omalizumab as a promising therapeutic option in children with severe unresponsive AD.96

A case study was reported from Italy, with 2 adolescent patients suffering from AD and asthma treated with omalizumab. Patients received SC injections of omalizumab every other week (1 patient 600 mg and the additional 450 mg). After a few months of single therapy with omalizumab, patients showed satisfying therapeutic responses with no adverse effects for AD and asthma.97 In another case study, 7 patients aged 6 to 19 with severe AD were treated with an adjusted dosage of SC omalizumab based on total mass and serum IgE level (never exceeded 375 mg). Significant clinical improvement, reduced serum IgE level, decreased use of topical steroids. No need for other systemic treatment was observed during the therapy period, and no side effects were reported after 30 months of follow-up.98

Based on studies, although omalizumab seems to be a prominent candidate in treating resistant AD in children, more extensive investigations are needed to determine efficacy, safety, and appropriate dosage. In addition, finally, this issue should be investigated whether omalizumab is a cost-effective choice considering the other available options.99

Nemolizumab

Nemolizumab is a first-class humanized mAb that targets IL-31Rα, a recognized mediator in AD that causes itch, inflammation, keratinocyte differentiation, and fibroblast activation.100 Nemolizumab showed effectiveness in early-phase clinical trials, including for individuals suffering from moderate-to-severe AD, in decreasing pruritus and skin symptoms.101, 102, 103 In 2022, this drug was approved for treating the itching associated with AD in adults and children 13 years and older. They conducted a 16-week, double-blinded, parallel-group, randomized phase III trial of monotherapy with nemolizumab in 215 subjects over 13 years with AD whose disease had no adequate response to topical agents. The treatment regimen consisted of 134 patients receiving 60 mg SC injections of nemolizumab every 4 weeks for 16 weeks (others received a placebo). Patients who achieved primary and secondary endpoint could enter a long-term follow-up trial for 52 weeks. The results showed a more significant decrease in pruritus in the nemolizumab-treated group than placebo through 16 weeks of treatment. They also observed improved quality of life, reduced sleep loss, and decreased AD severity followed by nemolizumab treatment. The most common adverse effect was injection site reaction, which was more common in the nemolizumab. Despite the apparent reduction in pruritus, some patients experienced worsening AD as an adverse effect.104

In an open-label, phase II study, Sidbury et al. enrolled 20 patients aged 12 to 17 with uncontrolled moderate to severe AD and associated pruritus and investigated pharmacokinetics, biomarker profiles, safety, and efficacy during 16-week nemolizumab treatment. Eighteen patients met the inclusion criteria and received a 60 mg SC injection of nemolizumab as the loading dose, followed by a 30 mg SC injection as a maintenance dose every 4 weeks for up to twelve weeks with background TCS treatment. Results demonstrated pharmacokinetics similar to the studies in adults with the same condition. Nemolizumab improved signs and symptoms and downregulated AD-related pro-inflammatory biomarkers, which are indicators of disease severity. Two subjects showed severe adverse reactions during treatment, including eczema, peripheral edema, and staphylococcal skin infection, leading to study discontinuation.105 Although there are ongoing trials, more data must be needed to draw definite conclusions.

Future biologics in pediatric atopic dermatitis

Lebrikizumab

Lebrikizumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that binds to soluble IL-13 at an epitope that overlaps with the IL-4Rα binding site.82 In 2019, it received a grant from the FDA, and trial investigations have produced promising results for adolescents and adults with moderate to severe AD.106,107 It was used in 8 phase III and 1 phase II clinical trials in July 2022 to treat moderate to severe AD.108 Treatment with this mAb is linked to a quick onset of clinical response in patients with a notable change in pruritus, skin clearance, and quality of life, which was enhanced by concurrent administration of TCA, according to the results of 2-phase III trials (ADvocate and ADhere), which included adolescents over the age of 12 years old. Adequate therapeutic response, the need for less frequent dosing regimens for maintenance, and a slow loss of clinical response due to lebrikizumab withdrawal make this selective IL-13 inhibitor a potential candidate for AD treatment in pediatrics.107,109 It is anticipated that the European Union and the United States will approve this for treating AD shortly. This biological therapy is well tolerated, and due to the suppression of IL-13, the most prevalent adverse events are conjunctivitis, respiratory tract infection (RTI), nasopharyngitis, headache, discomfort, and an injection site reaction.90,91

Benralizumab

Benralizumab is an afucosylated humanized mAb that binds to IL-5R α chain, inhibiting IL-5 and its receptor and, thus, could decrease IgE so that it can be used to treat hypereosinophilic-syndrome-associated AD in the future. However, trials are still in the early stages.110 Spontaneous resolution of AD symptoms was previously documented in an adolescent patient in the phase 3 CALIMA pivotal trial, which was treated for severe, uncontrolled, eosinophilic asthma.111 However, in patients with moderate to severe AD (including adolescents) who had not responded well to prior topical therapy, the phase II randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled HILLIER trial found no reaction to benralizumab and no discernible improvement in the patients' symptoms.112 These results indicate that targeting eosinophils alone may not treat this heterogeneous disease. However, Additional research is required to confirm the exact effect of benralizumab and the effective dose in AD patients.

Tepezelumab

Other treatments that target TSLP and OX40, like Tepezelumab, and GBR830/IBS830, have shown effective results in adult trials that could be investigated in the pediatric population.113 Tezepelumab (AMG 157/MEDI9929) is a fully human immunoglobulin G2λ mAb that blocks thymic stromal lymphopoietin and inhibits T2 inflammatory cascade. The FDA-approved medication, marketed under the brand name Tezspire, is used as an adjuvant maintenance treatment to treat severe asthma in adults and children 12 years of age and above.114 In individuals with moderate-to-severe AD aged 18 to 75 years, ALLEVIAD, a phase IIa randomized, double-blind, and placebo-controlled study, assessed the safety and effectiveness of subcutaneous injection of 280 mg of Tezepelumab or placebo every 2 weeks in addition to class 3 TCS. Although no statistically significant clinical outcome was obtained, numerical improvements were demonstrated in the Tepezelumab arm over the placebo in 12 weeks, with a further response at 16 weeks, suggesting potential effects of Tezepelumab in AD, which requires additional clinical studies with more extended treatment periods.115 GBR 830, another humanized IgG1 anti-OX40 antibody, has been associated with improved clinical scores and decreased mRNA expression in skin lesions in patients with AD reported in an exploratory phase IIa study.116

BMS-981164

BMS-981164 is an anti-IL-31 mAb that specifically targets circulating IL-31. Between 2012 and 2015, a phase I clinical trial was conducted to examine BMS-981164′s pharmacokinetic and safety profile. The study was randomized, double-blind, and placebo-controlled, and the drug was given through SC and IV (Intravenous). The dosage range for BMS-981164 in the survey was 0.01 to 3 mg/kg, and both AD-affected adults and healthy participants were included in the trial. However, as of now, results from this study have yet to be released or made publicly available.117,118

Ligelizumab

A new humanized mAb called ligelizumab, targeting IgE, has been created. A phase II clinical trial that followed a randomized, controlled, double-blind approach assessed its effectiveness and safety in individuals with moderate to severe AD. Despite the completion of the study, findings or outcomes have yet to be made available.5 In a recently released study, 22 individuals with moderate-to-severe AD participated in a randomized, multicenter, double-blind clinical trial to assess the safety and effectiveness of ligelizumab 280 mg SC every other week. The safety and efficacy of the active (cyclosporine A) control group were also investigated. They discovered that ligelizumab decreased serum IgE and allergic skin prick testing. The Eczema Area and Severity Index 50 response, itching, and sleep disruption were not substantially alleviated by ligelizumab compared to cyclosporine A. Interestingly, individuals with higher baseline IgE had a somewhat, though not substantially, more robust response to treatment than those with lower baseline IgE.119

Mepolizumab

Mepolizumab, a humanized mAb belonging to the IgG1/k class, functions by blocking the activity of circulating IL-5. IL-5 is known to be involved in the development, maturation, and survival of eosinophils, which may play a crucial role in AD pathogenesis. To assess its efficacy, a study was conducted using a randomized, placebo-controlled parallel group design, where individuals with moderate to severe AD received 2 separate doses of 750 mg mepolizumab, administered 1 week apart. Administering 2 single doses of 750 mg mepolizumab did not alleviate AD symptoms, even though it greatly decreased peripheral blood eosinophils.120

Fezakinumab

Fezakinumab is a fully human IgG1-lambda type mAb against IL-22. IL-22 is a known inflammatory cytokine that promotes epidermal thickening and causes skin barrier defects. Research has demonstrated that IL-22 is significantly elevated in AD patients and is closely linked to the disease's severity.121,122 Fezakinumab was initially designed to treat psoriasis and rheumatoid arthritis. Still, following further observation of the pathological role of IL-22 in AD, assumptions were directed toward the therapeutic effect of this novel mAb in atopic dermatitis. About 60 adult patients with moderate-to-severe AD participated in a small phase II, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicenter clinical trial to examine the safety and effectiveness of fezkinumab in AD. At 12 and 20 weeks, patients who received Fezakinumab (600 mg loading dose, 300 mg every 2 weeks) showed a statistically significant improvement in clinical outcomes compared to those who received a placebo. Patients experienced an improvement in the severity of AD accompanied by a favorable safety profile.123 Examining the lesional and nonlesional of patients' skin biopsy samples treated with Fezakinumab compared to placebo in another study showed that the inhibition of IL-22 is associated with the reversal of the AD pathological features in the skin, and this effect was found more prominent in patients with a higher initial level of IL-22, indicating that IL-22 blockade could be a potential therapeutic approach in both adult and pediatric patients which suggests further investigations.124

Rituximab

Rituximab, an anti-CD20 chimeric monoclonal antibody, is currently used to manage certain types of autoimmune disorders and lymphoproliferative conditions.125 In an open-label pilot study in 2008, 6 adults with severe AD and unsatisfactory response to TCS and systemic therapy received 1000 mg of IV Rituximab 2 weeks apart. Rituximab was well tolerated and improved skin symptoms followed by a significant decrease in severity index were observed in all 6 patients.126 Additionally, Sánchez-Ramón et al. observed a significant clinical improvement in 4 adult patients with severe AD who were not responding to conventional therapy after receiving Omalizumab and Rituximab in sequential combination treatment.127 A 7-year-old male patient with a severe type of AD experienced a long-lasting remission, according to Duarte et al., despite not responding well to topical and systemic steroids and systemic immunomodulation therapy. The patient received 375 mg/mL once weekly for 4 weeks. The third treatment improved dramatically, and the patient's skin was almost clear after the fourth infusion.128 Although some case studies indicated that Rituximab may benefit AD patients, further investigations are required to demonstrate the efficacy and safety of this biological drug in AD, including the pediatric population.

Conclusion

AD significantly affects the quality of life of pediatric patients, making effective management of moderate-to-severe disease essential. While topical therapies and emollients remain the cornerstone of treatment, many patients require systemic immunomodulatory agents. Among biologics, Dupilumab and Tralokinumab have received regulatory approval, with Dupilumab approved for children older than 6 months, whereas agents such as Omalizumab, Nemolizumab, Lebrikizumab, and Benralizumab have demonstrated promising results in pediatric AD. However, further clinical trials are needed to establish the role of emerging biologics, including Tezepelumab, Ligelizumab, Mepolizumab, Fezakinumab, and Rituximab. Future advances in AD management will likely depend on precision medicine approaches, biomarker-guided patient stratification, and identification of predictors of treatment response, alongside evaluation of long-term safety, durability, and comparative effectiveness of biologics and combination therapies, particularly in refractory cases.11,12,129
