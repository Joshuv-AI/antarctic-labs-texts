Evaluation of the Physical Properties of Different Modifications of Glass Ionomer Dental Restorative Materials in Primary Dentition: A Comparative Ex Vivo Study
Authors: Vivek Mehta, Nikhil Srivastava, Saif Ali Chaudhry, Vivek Rana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate and compare the physical properties of different modifications of glass ionomer cement, namely giomer, zirconomer, and type IX glass ionomer cement (GIC) (pediatric), with resin-modified glass ionomer cement (RMGIC) in the primary dentition in terms of their compressive strength (CS), flexural strength (FS), wear resistance, and microleakage.Materials and methodsA total of 224 extracted human primary molars were selected and allocated into three interventions and one control group (n =14); group I: RMGIC (control group), group II: giomer, group III: zirconomer, and group IV: type IX GIC (pediatric). The samples (n = 14) were restored after making standardized class II cavities and then evaluated for CS using a universal testing machine. The evaluation for FS was done using a three-point bending test on bar-shaped tooth samples (n = 14) after restoration. To assess wear resistance, restored tooth samples (n = 14) were evaluated after being subjected to a two-body wear test. For microleakage tests, restored tooth samples (n = 14) were prepared and subjected to thermocycling after storage in distilled water for 24 hours. After applying two coats of nail varnish 1 mm short of the restorative margins, samples were sectioned buccolingually and immersed in methylene blue dye for 24 hours. The evaluation of the cut sections was done using a stereomicroscope. The data obtained were subjected to statistical analysis.ResultsType IX GIC (pediatric) exhibited significantly greater CS, FS, and wear resistance in primary teeth as compared to RMGIC, giomer, and zirconomer in primary teeth. However, there was no significant difference in microleakage among the tested restorative materials.ConclusionPrimary teeth restored with type IX GIC (pediatric) exhibited superior physical properties compared to other materials tested. However, further in vitro and clinical trials with large sample sizes are recommended to validate these results.Clinical relevanceAlthough GIC is the most frequently used dental material for restorative purposes in pediatric dentistry, the long-term success of conventional GIC is questionable due to suboptimal physical properties. The results of the study will help restore primary teeth with improved physical properties and long-term success.How to cite this articleMehta V, Srivastava N, Chaudhry SA, et al. Introduction

Early childhood caries is a prevalent chronic childhood disease, and if untreated, it can cause the loss of teeth at an early age.1 It can cause pain in children, further resulting in sleep disturbances, lack of concentration, and a negative impact on the quality of life of both children and their parents.2,3 As dental caries develop rapidly among children shortly after tooth eruption, early prevention is necessary, and appropriate oral health care should be delivered in a timely manner to reduce the deleterious effects. The restoration of a carious lesion becomes compulsory once the dental caries progresses. Since their development in the early 1970s, glass ionomers have been an essential part of dentists' armamentarium for providing restorative and preventive treatment while maintaining esthetics.

Glass ionomer cements (GIC) are beneficial as pediatric restorative materials due to their numerous attractive characteristics, such as the ability to form a chemical bond with tooth structure, sustained release of fluoride, and reduced moisture sensitivity.4 Despite being the most common and the standard restorative material for the primary teeth in child patients globally, conventional GIC has been associated with disadvantages, such as reduced mechanical and esthetic properties.5 To overcome these limitations, subsequent research has been done, and a variety of modifications have been introduced with different compositions to enhance the durability, dimensional stability, and marginal seal. Some important modifications, such as giomer, zirconomer, type IX GIC (pediatric), and resin-modified glass ionomer cement (RMGIC).

Resin-modified glass ionomer cement was introduced as an alternative to conventional glass ionomers to improve its physical properties. The setting reaction consists of a combined acid-base reaction and photochemical polymerization and has enhanced fracture toughness and tensile strength with better initial esthetics.6 Whereas zirconomer or zirconia-reinforced glass ionomer cement was developed to improve the mechanical properties of GIC by incorporating nanozirconia fillers, thereby increasing the CS and durability of the restoration.7 Another type of glass ionomer, giomer, is a direct restorative dental material, combining the functional and esthetic properties of glass ionomers and dental composites. It has incorporated mechanical qualities, esthetics, surface finishing properties of dental composites, and the protective properties of fluoride from glass ionomers.8 The type IX GIC (pediatric) is a radiopaque restorative cement commonly used by dental professionals for posteriors in child patients and the atraumatic restorative technique (ART). It has an enhanced viscosity and improved condensability with an esthetic appeal.9

The success of restorative treatment depends on the knowledge of the mechanical properties of the dental hard tissues and restorative materials.10 The knowledge of the physical properties of restorative materials helps in the decision-making process regarding the best material option for a particular clinical condition and consequently reduces the probability of retreatment. Henceforth, the clinician's decision to select an appropriate restorative material for primary dentition should rely on the property comparisons of the materials in the best interest of the child's oral health.

A review of the literature suggested that a lot of research studies have been done previously to determine the physical properties of pediatric restorative materials, but to date, no investigation has compared the physical properties of commonly used modifications of glass ionomer cement using primary teeth. Thus, the purpose of this study was to investigate and compare the physical properties of the RMGIC with three other glass-ionomer-based dental restorative materials in primary teeth. Findings from this study will assist pediatric dentists in choosing an appropriate restorative material for primary dentition in terms of strength, durability, and marginal integrity.

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was estimated using an online tool available at powerandsamplesize.com from the data obtained from a previous study conducted by Bhatia et al.11 Considering the means of the two out of four groups to be 151.47 and 160.91, a pooled standard deviation of 8.23, and several pairwise comparisons to be 6, a minimum of 14 samples per group were required at an alpha error of 5% and a power of 95%.

Specimen Preparation

The primary molars were visually inspected macroscopically under magnification for the presence of cracks and fracture lines. Extracted human primary molars initially stored in 0.1% thymol solution were included in the current ex vivo study. Informed consent was obtained from the patients and parents for the use of extracted teeth for the current research study. A contra-angle handpiece was used for the preparation of class II cavities in extracted primary molars, only after cleaning the teeth. The periodontal probe was used to verify the dimensions of the cavities as follows: Width 3 mm, depth 1.5 mm, and cervical margins 1 mm beneath the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) in dentine.12 The extracted molars were continuously moistened in water before and after the restorative and test procedures. CS Test

For assessing the CS, 56 specimens were prepared (n = 14) by using extracted primary molars (length = 12 mm, width = 3 mm, thickness = 2 mm), including both tooth and restoration from each of the restorative materials used in the present study. Four groups were employed in CS tests. The specimens were kept in distilled water at 37°C for 24 hours, and the dimensions were measured to a precision of 0.001 mm. The specimens were placed between plates of a computerized software-based universal testing machine (ACME Engineers, India, and Model No. UNITEST-10), and a compressive load was applied along the long axis at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/minute (Fig. 2). For assessing the FS, again 56 specimens were prepared (n = 14) by using extracted primary molars (length = 10 mm, width = 3 mm, thickness = 2 mm), including both tooth and restoration from each of the restorative materials used in the current study. Wear Resistance Test

In a similar way, for assessing the wear resistance, 56 specimens were prepared again (n = 14) by using extracted primary molars. As in the above cases, the specimens were stored in deionized water at a temperature of 37°C for 24 hours. The wear testing was done with the two-body wear tester (Fig. 4). The specimens were mounted on a fixture using a primary tooth as an antagonist at 350 rpm, and a normal load of 20 N. Each masticatory cycle was repeated 5,000 times. Water at 37°C was used for the simulation of the oral environment and to remove worn particles from the surface of the material during the experimental in vitro mastication process. Microleakage Test

Once again, for assessing microleakage, in a similar way, 56 extracted primary molars were restored after preparing standard class II cavities in the teeth and were stored in distilled water at 37°C with 100% humidity, and the teeth were then allocated into four groups. A double coating of nail varnish was applied to the outer surface of the tooth structure and was kept 1 mm short of the restorative margins. The teeth were then immersed in 1% methylene blue dye for 24 hours and then washed properly under running water to remove the dye superficially attached to the teeth (Fig. 5). The tooth samples were then sectioned buccolingually into two equal parts through the center of the restoration, using a diamond disc under water spray.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was performed utilizing SPSS software for Windows (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, version 23.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, United States). The significance level was kept at 5%. The data was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The results revealed that the data of CS and FS followed a normal distribution, whereas the data of wear (%) and microleakage did not follow a normal distribution. Therefore, the comparison of CS and FS among the primary teeth restored with four restorative materials was performed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test followed by the post hoc Tukey test for pairwise comparisons. The comparison of wear (%) and microleakage among the primary teeth restored with four restorative materials was performed using a Kruskal–Wallis test followed by a post hoc Mann–Whitney test for the pairwise comparisons.

Discussion

Glass ionomer cement is a clinically active restorative material composed of an ion-leachable fluoroaluminosilicate glass and copolymers of polyacrylic acid, most commonly used for pediatric patients due to its unique advantages. However, it has some disadvantages, including inferior physical properties, that is, low CS, low wear resistance, and brittleness,14 thereby increasing its failure rates. Hence, an ideal restorative material was desirable for primary dentition with improved physical properties. Subsequently, modifications were developed to overcome the prevailing limitations. The physical properties of various restorative materials should be given important consideration for long-term clinical success. Henceforth, the present ex vivo study was planned to find if any differences in physical properties in various modifications of GIC existed when used in primary teeth. Limited research is available in the literature using extracted primary teeth.

Maintenance of the primary dentition until physiological exfoliation is important for mastication, esthetics, and phonation, prevents deleterious habits in youngsters, and avoids various orthodontic problems in the future.15 The restoration of teeth becomes necessary once dental caries arises.16 The resin-based composites are not as successful as pediatric restorative materials due to lower inorganic content in primary teeth, decreased patient cooperation, and difficulty in moisture control. Hence, glass ionomers became the material of choice due to fluoride release and ease of use. Due to the above considerations, glass ionomer-based restorative materials, along with the primary teeth, were selected for the current study.

The modifications of GIC selected for this study were giomer, zirconomer, type IX GIC (pediatric), and RMGIC, as they are frequently used for pediatric patients in routine dental practice. The RMGIC was used as a control group in the present study due to its superior physical properties when compared to traditional GIC, that is, increased working time, reduced moisture sensitivity during setting, and more user-friendly.17,18

In the present study, CS, FS, wear resistance, and microleakage of the primary teeth restored with modifications of GIC were analyzed. The results showed that the primary teeth restored with type IX GIC (pediatric) had the highest mean value of CS, FS, and wear resistance.

Compressive strength is a commonly used physical property and is a measure of the amount of force a material can support in a single impact before breaking.19 It is easiest to measure, gives an indication of the material's resistance to plasticity and creep, and is occasionally used as a screening test while developing novel materials.19 The results also revealed a significant increase in the CS of primary teeth restored with type IX GIC (pediatric) in comparison with primary teeth restored with RMGIC, zirconomer, and giomer. This is attributable to the highly reactive and ultrafine glass particles distributed evenly through the structure. However, no difference was found in the CS values of primary teeth restored with RMGIC and zirconomer. The lowest CS values were observed in primary teeth restored with giomer.

Flexural strength, an important mechanical property of restorative materials, is the ability of a material to bend before it undergoes breakage.20 In the present study, FS of primary teeth restored with RMGIC, giomer, zirconomer, and type IX GIC (pediatric) were analyzed. A significant increase was seen in the FS of primary teeth restored with type IX GIC (pediatric) in comparison with primary teeth restored with RMGIC, giomer, and zirconomer. In the present study, the mean value of FS of primary teeth restored with GIC type IX (pediatric) was greater than that of primary teeth restored with zirconomer, which was not according to the study done by Patil et al.21

Wear resistance is another important property of a dental restorative material used for posterior restorations.22 The primary teeth restored with type IX GIC (pediatric) restorative material showed the highest wear resistance (least wear%), and the least wear resistance (highest wear%) was seen in the primary teeth restored with RMGIC material. A pairwise comparison of wear resistance amongst the four groups showed that the wear resistance of primary teeth restored with type IX GIC (pediatric) material was significantly greater than that of the primary teeth restored with RMGIC and giomer material. The wear resistance of group III was significantly greater than that of the primary teeth restored with groups I and II and similar to that of group IV. The results obtained from the present ex vivo study revealed a statistically significant increase in the wear resistance of type IX GIC (pediatric) in comparison with RMGIC, zirconomer, and giomer. This may be due to the composition of type IX GIC (pediatric), which is characterized by small-sized glass particles and a higher powder-to-liquid ratio, resulting in greater wear resistance.18

Microleakage is defined as the “clinically undetectable passage of bacteria, fluids, molecules, or ions between a cavity wall and the restorative materials applied to it.”23 Microleakage is an essential characteristic used in the evaluation of dental materials used for restoration.24 The difference in the microleakage of the primary teeth restored with four restorative materials was statistically nonsignificant, but it was observed that primary teeth in groups I and II had comparatively lesser microleakage, and the possible explanation is that the use of dentine bonding agent and light cure might have resulted in greater bonding with the tooth material.

The results of the present ex vivo study imply that type IX GIC (pediatric) represents a well-characterized GIC material with significant improvements in physical properties, namely compressive strength, flexural strength, and wear resistance, compared to the RMGIC, zirconomer, and giomer for primary dentition, and a promising dental material that may be commonly used in pediatric restorative dentistry. They can be recommended for restorations of posterior teeth in primary dentition. The present results can serve as a reference dataset for the selection of pediatric restorative materials to material scientists at both national and international levels.
