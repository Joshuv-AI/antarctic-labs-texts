RESCUER mobile app to support pediatric resuscitation: Study protocol for a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Samantha A Lee, Diego Trujillo, Garth D Meckler, Carl Eriksson, Trang Huynh, Nathan Bahr, Jyotsna Sanjeevi, Matt Hansen, Jeanne-Marie Guise
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPediatric out-of-hospital cardiac arrest is a leading cause of death in children. This paper describes the study protocol for the randomized control trial to test a linear cognitive aid app (RESCUER) designed to support Emergency Medical Services (EMS) clinicians in responding to neonatal and pediatric out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (POHCA).ObjectiveThis randomized controlled trial (RCT) will investigate the effects of the RESCUER app compared with existing EMS practices and tools during simulated POHCAs.Study designThis RCT will be conducted with EMS first responders from rural and urban EMS agencies in the United States (US). EMS teams will be randomized to respond to simulated neonatal and POHCAs using either (1) the RESCUER app or (2) their current standard of care and tools. In addition to randomized assignment to intervention and control, we also will randomize the order of simulation scenarios.Main outcome measuresPrimary outcomes include time to complete American Heart Association (AHA) recommended steps for NRP and PALS. This study will examine whether an app used by EMS teams in responding to simulated neonatal and pediatric OHCAs will improve resuscitation performance and decrease cognitive load.Trial Registration NumberNCT06768099 (ClinicalTrials.gov).Keywords: Mobile applications, Pediatrics, Emergency medical services, Cardiopulmonary resuscitation, Patient safety, Randomized controlled trial (RCT)

Introduction

Cardiorespiratory arrest is one of the leading causes of death in children [3,4] Although survival from adult out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) has improved over the last two decades, pediatrics survival has not [5,6].

To standardize and improve outcomes for pediatrics cardiac arrest, the American Heart Association (AHA) introduced evidence-based Neonatal Resuscitation Program (NRP) and Pediatric Advance Life Support (PALS) guidelines in 1987 and 1988 respectively [7]. Adherence to these AHA resuscitation guidelines has been associated with improved pediatrics survival from in-hospital pediatrics cardiac arrest [8]. However, these resuscitation guideline recommendations are only followed in 22 % of pediatrics OHCAs [9].

Cognitive aids have been created to address pediatrics AHA guideline adherence discrepancy during life-threatening emergencies. In contrast to adult care, pediatrics emergency care requires EMS teams to follow a unique action sequence adjusted to a patient's weight and size. Existing cognitive aids have been developed to provide clinicians guidance throughout complex clinical responses during stressful situations. According to a survey conducted in EMS agencies, many EMS teams carry two to five different cognitive aids to help with providing pediatrics care, including the Handtevy™ system, protocol books, and length-based aides [10]. Current cognitive aids typically separate care protocols by equipment sizing and medication dosing and utilize branching logic to guide critical interventions.

Studies suggest linear cognitive aids may be more effective than those using branching logic [11,12]. A study using a branching logic mobile application for pediatrics resuscitation failed to show improvements over standard care. In contrast, another study comparing seven cognitive aids for anaphylaxis management found improvements in performance using linear compared to branching logic cognitive aids [13]. As these interventions are implemented, it is critical to ensure they fit into and support existing EMS team practices.

In-situ simulations have historically been used in many clinical domains to understand interventions’ effectiveness and replicate uncommon scenarios, especially rare emergency events [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. Additionally, simulations are a common training approach used by EMS teams to improve EMS care of high-acuity, low-frequency events where real-world opportunities for practice are limited, like pediatric resuscitation [18,19]. Simulations are endorsed by the AHA for training and continuous quality improvement and have demonstrated improved adherence to care guidelines and team performance for months after training [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. To fit the common training approaches of many EMS teams, this randomized control trial will test using a linear cognitive aid mobile app during in-situ simulations of pediatric OHCA.

We present the protocol of a randomized control trial of a linear cognitive aid mobile app versus standard of care for neonatal and pediatrics OHCA.

Methods

Objectives

The objective of this study is to test a linear cognitive aid mobile app (RESCUER) intervention to improve resuscitative performance in simulated neonatal and pediatrics cardiac arrest emergencies among EMS teams.

Study design

This study is a multi-site randomized control trial (RCT). We will perform high-fidelity in-situ simulations of two time-sensitive neonatal and pediatrics OHCA emergencies. Teams will be randomized to: (1) perform both resuscitations using their existing cognitive aids or (2) perform both resuscitations using our new linear cognitive aid app. We elected to not randomize the use of the app vs. usual care within the same team since we wanted teams to use the app or usual care for both scenarios so that the feedback we receive after the scenario could be attributed to the intervention vs. control and so that teams would be more comfortable with the app by the second simulation. The order of the scenarios will also be randomized to account for any learning effect of the simulation itself.

Teams randomized to perform both resuscitations using our new linear cognitive aid app will receive a brief overview of the app prior to participating in the in-situ simulations. This overview will orient EMS teams to the app's interface, like how to open the app, how to type into user input fields, how to mute the voice prompts, etc. This overview will not advance farther than the first page to protect the novelty of the in-situ simulation scenario for the entire EMS team.

Study population

We will identify 911 response fire and transport EMS agencies serving urban or rural communities. Eligible EMS agencies will include active public, private, volunteer, hospital-based, and third-party EMS providers delivering care to pediatrics and adult patients. EMS personnel who are 18 years of age or older are eligible to participate. EMS teams will work in their typical team structure of about three to six people, including at least one paramedic to ensure the crews are able to perform advanced life support interventions.

Randomization

Teams will be randomized in a 1:1 ratio of intervention versus “usual care.” Randomization will be conducted at the agency level. For agencies with an even number of teams an equal split will be assigned and for those with an odd number of teams, the allocation will follow a randomized 2:1 ratio. Each team assigned to a group, either control or test, for a given scenario will remain in the same group for the subsequent scenario. However, the order in which scenarios are presented will be randomized within each group. As agencies continue to be recruited, randomization will be adjusted dynamically to achieve an overall balance of 37 teams in each group by study completion. Randomization will be performed with SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute) for Windows. Due to the nature of the study, blinding of participants and research staff involved in the simulations will not be possible.

Intervention design

The RESCUER mobile app combines AHA step-by-step algorithmic guidance with age and weight-based pediatrics dosing and sizing of medications and equipment. The app provides time-based information on specific steps in care with minimal user input. It is “linear” in that the information provided is sequential and does not require the user to navigate or consider branch-points.

The tool requires minimal user input. The user only needs to enter either patient age, weight, or length (Fig. 1A). The app will take any one of these data points and calculate the other values based on provided inputs, which the app uses as a basis for medication dose calculations and equipment size determination (Fig. 1B). From there, the user only needs to input items a computerized monitor would not know, such as a physical examination findings or presence or absence of a pulse. The tool simulates automated cardiac rhythm interpretation after appropriate monitoring equipment is in place and uses these inputs to guide the user through each step of care. After the initial inputs and automated rhythm interpretation, a set of instructions based on NRP or PALS guidelines appear as a dynamically generated linear sequence, instructing the EMS team to perform actions (e.g. “check pulse”, “perform compressions and ventilations 15:2”) (Fig. 1C–D). The specific instructions, the time when they are given, and the doses for medication administrations are automatically calculated using the age, weight, or length indicated by the user. As shown in Fig. 2, this linear approach was chosen because preliminary studies have shown linear aids to work better than those with branching logic [24]. Importantly, these instructions are presented visually in the app interface and are also read out loud through voice prompts which can be muted. The voice prompts mimic current automated external defibrillator (AED) instructions which have been effective in guiding lay provider CPR and defibrillation [25,26].

The RESCUER app has been developed using the Ionic framework, which enables the application to be compiled as an Android or iOS application, as well as being a desktop browser app. The app uses a client-server architecture with a front-end and back-end running concurrently. The front-end application uses the React framework in the TypeScript language to handle the user interface, data model logic, and requests to the back-end server. This back-end server handles these requests with a Flask API in Python and also provides access for administrators to manually override details about the simulation. This enables administrators to simulate the behavior of a responsive defibrillator that can react to physiologic data input automatically, minimizing user input to the app. Together these elements allow maximum flexibility and utility for the diverse needs of EMS teams during emergency pediatrics resuscitations.

Variables and assessment methods

Outcomes

The primary outcomes are times for measurable AHA NRP and PALS metrics, such as medication administration, effective ventilation, and drying and stimulating an infant, which have been associated with improved survival and improved return of spontaneous circulation and survival in pediatrics cardiac arrest [[27], [28], [29]].

Secondary outcomes will include teamwork quality and cognitive load. Teamwork performance will be evaluated using the Clinical Teamwork Scale (CTS™), a validated tool assessing quality communication, situational awareness, decision-making and role clarity [1]. We expect a mean CTS™ score of 6.04 (SD = 2.10) based on preliminary data collected from EMS settings similar to our planned simulated scenarios [30]. Frequency of errors will be recorded such as omissions or incorrect actions during the scenarios. Cognitive load will be self-assessed by participants after each scenario using the NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) tool, which evaluates six dimensions: mental, physical and temporal demand, overall performance, effort, and frustration level [2].

Usability of and satisfaction with the app will also be evaluated in survey and interview formats.

Data collection

The study team will use a concurrent, embedded mixed methods design to gather data using the following methods.

Prior to simulations, participants will complete a survey requesting their demographic information such as total time as an EMs clinician, time with their agency, experience with children's cases, and recent educational trainings.

During each simulation, data will be collected through video recordings and real-time observations. Video recordings will allow for detailed post-hoc analysis of team performance, intervention timing, and identification of errors during resuscitation steps. Real-time observations will be conducted by trained assessors, with two clinicians independently evaluating teamwork using the CTS™. One of the clinicians will also document technical performance on a standardized electronic data capture tool, focusing on the timing and accuracy of critical resuscitation tasks, such as airway management, chest compressions, defibrillation, medication administration, and adherence to protocols. Discrepancies in assessments will be resolved collaboratively to ensure data accuracy. Each clinician observing and recording data will be trained and has performed similar duties in previously published work [31].

Following each simulation, participants will complete a cognitive aid tool in use survey to evaluate the utilized tools’ perceived utility, efficiency, and ease of use. Participants will also complete the NASA-TLX to assess cognitive load. If participants were randomized to use the RESCUER app, they will also complete an app functional performance survey after both simulated scenarios have been completed.

Following both simulations, all participants will also engage in a verbal, structured, group debriefing to provide qualitative feedback on their simulation experience and cognitive aid utilization. All participants will have the opportunity to suggest improvements to existing cognitive aids and share insights into their utility during pediatrics resuscitations. Group debrief sessions will be audio recorded and debrief leaders will take live field notes for later qualitative analysis.

All surveys will be collected and managed using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap).

Sample size calculation

The sample size estimation is based on two primary outcomes: (1) the proportion of cases completing dry and stimulation within 10 min for childbirth and (2) the time to epinephrine administration in Pediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS) scenarios.

For the childbirth scenario, preliminary data indicate that 17.7 % of cases complete dry and stimulation within 10 min under usual care [32]. We hypothesize that the cognitive aid will increase this proportion by 30 %, yielding a moderate effect size of 0.66. To achieve 80 % power with a two-sided alpha of 0.05, we require 37 simulations per group in a 1:1 allocation.

For the PALS scenario, the mean time to epinephrine administration is estimated at 5.3 min with a standard deviation of 1.2 min [22]. A 15 % improvement with the cognitive aid corresponds to an effect size of 0.66. Using these estimates, 37 simulations per group will be required to detect a statistically significant difference with 80 % power at a significance level of 0.05.

Data analysis

Quantitative analysis

The proportional differences between intervention and control groups will be analyzed using the Wilson test for proportions. If assumptions for approximate methods are violated (e.g., small sample size or extreme proportions), an exact binomial test will be used. Additionally, time-to-event outcomes (e.g., time to medication administration), teamwork performance, and error rates, will be assessed using Welch's t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, depending on data distribution.

Qualitative analysis

The group debrief sessions audio recordings, debrief leaders' associated field notes, and the app functional performance survey qualitative components will be analyzed using MAXQDA software using a grounded theory approach coupled with an immersion-crystallization process. The identified patterns in the qualitative data will be reported in the study's published results as high-yield and practical feedback on the prototype cognitive aid that can be deployed in a future study.

The qualitative analysis will be used to enhance quantitative results reported to align with the concurrent, embedded mixed methods research design.

Discussion

Our study aims to support EMS clinicians as they respond to low-frequency, high-stakes out-of-hospital emergency pediatrics resuscitations. Specifically, we will evaluate whether a novel linear cognitive aide app for newborn and pediatrics resuscitation improves efficiency, guideline concordance, and technical and nontechnical team performance among EMS teams responding to simulated neonatal and pediatrics OHCAs. We will employ high fidelity simulation and a pragmatic approach in which EMS teams respond in their usual teams to simulated neonatal and pediatrics OHCA emergencies which will allow generalization to real-world practice. The robust assessment measures, including AHA guideline milestones, teamwork, and cognitive load, in addition to structured interviews will provide important insights into the app's efficacy as well as pediatrics safety in EMS practice.

Ethics and dissemination

The Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Centre Institutional Review Board approved this study (2023P000386). All proposed research does not collect any personal or identifiable information from participating EMS providers or agency staff. The overall risk to participating EMS agencies is minimal due to only anonymous data being collected and retained.

To ensure the impact of our work, we will disseminate our findings through several key avenues. Research findings will be published in scientific journals and presented at relevant national research meetings. Additionally, when possible, our findings will also be shared with participating EMS agencies, EMS leaders, and medical directors via their preferred modes of communication with themselves and their associated local and national organizations.
