Lumbar extradural extraosseous notochordal lesion resulting in radiculopathy: ecchordosis physaliphora versus chordoma. Illustrative case

While the vast majority of ecchordosis physaliphoras (EPs), a benign extraosseous lesion of fetal notochordal cell origin, described in the literature involve the clivus, only 2 cases of symptomatic EP have been reported in the mobile spine. These consist of a cystic thoracic spine lesion1 and a cervical spine lesion.2 However, the latter example showed histological features more consistent with a chondroid nodule rather than EP.3 Extraosseous chordomas involving the mobile spine are also rare, but they have been described more frequently in the literature than spinal EP. These tumors are typically localized within the intradural, extradural, and extra-axial regions, with most cases presenting with radicular symptoms.4 The histopathological features associated with EP and chordoma overlap, a feature that can make their distinction challenging. We report the case of a woman who presented with low back pain and radiating left lower extremity pain associated with an intraforaminal extraosseous mass at the origin of the left lumbosacral L5–S1 neural foramen. Based on the size, histopathological features, and retrospective imaging evaluation, the lesion was felt to be consistent with EP, and 39-month follow-up imaging revealed no evidence of recurrence. The patient has remained symptom free postoperatively in terms of her left lumbar L5 radiculopathy.

Illustrative Case

Clinical and Imaging Features

A 46-year-old right-hand-dominant woman presented for an outpatient neurosurgical evaluation with a history of chronic low back pain responsive to conservative interventions. She described the onset of progressively worsening low back pain in conjunction with radiating left lower extremity pain and hypesthesia corresponding to a left lumbar L5 dermatomal distribution. Trials of lifestyle modifications, chiropractic interventions, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medications, and two previous diagnostic/therapeutic left lumbar L5 selective nerve root blocks all failed to result in satisfactory sustained symptom relief. Preoperative radiographs of the lumbar spine revealed mild degenerative arthritis (not shown). Preoperative lumbar spine MRI without contrast enhancement showed a well-circumscribed mass near the origin of the left L5–S1 neural foramen that was associated with mass effect on the traversing L5 nerve root (Fig. 1). The mass had nonspecific signal characteristics with low signal on T1-weighted and high signal on T2-weighted sequences and measured approximately 10 mm × 6 mm. The imaging findings were interpreted as being most consistent with herniated disc material. Clinical findings consistent with a left L5 radiculopathy were identified on preoperative neurosurgical evaluation, and preoperative dynamic lateral lumbosacral radiographs revealed no segmental spinal instability. Surgery to decompress the left L5 nerve root was offered as a means of definitive management.

Preoperative unenhanced sagittal (A) and axial (B) T2-weighted (left) and T1-weighted (right) MR images revealing a small focal extradural T2-hyperintense mass (arrow) located near the origin of the left L5–S1 nerve root foramen that extends cephalad along the vertebral body and is associated with compression of the exiting traversing left L5 nerve root.

The patient wished to proceed with surgery, and 3 weeks later she underwent an uneventful left L5–S1 microdecompression during which time a gelatinous, light gray mass was removed in its entirety from the left L5–S1 foraminal region. The mass appeared to emanate from the disc space of L5–S1 on the left side at the origin of the left L5–S1 foramen with lateral recess extension, and following complete microsurgical resection, the descending traversing exiting neural elements appeared to be completely decompressed. The tissue was sent for permanent section histopathological evaluation, as it was presumed preoperatively that the mass most likely represented herniated disc material.

Histopathological Analysis

Microscopically, the tumor showed cohesive sheets of epithelioid cells with abundant eosinophilic to clear multivacuolated cytoplasm, extracellular basophilic myxoid matrix, and a few aggregates of small lymphocytes. The cells lacked significant nuclear atypia, mitotic activity, and necrosis. In immunohistochemistical analysis, the tumor cells were positive for brachyury and cytokeratin. Based on the combined histological, immunohistochemical, and imaging findings, the mass was diagnosed as “Notochordal lesion showing features consistent with ecchordosis physaliphora” (Fig. 2).

Postoperative Course

The patient was discharged from the ambulatory surgery center the same day of surgery, and other than expected progressively resolving incisional discomfort, the patient has experienced no radicular pain since surgery. Thirty-nine-month (Fig. 3) postoperative lumbar MRI studies with and without contrast revealed no residual mass within the left L5–S1 nerve root foramen, and no focal neural compression. At the last clinical follow-up, the patient reported minimal low back pain and no radiating radicular pain.

T2-weighted (left), noncontrast T1-weighted (center), and postcontrast T1-weighted (right) images reveal interval resection of the mass shown in Figure 1 with decompression of the adjacent left L5 nerve root and expected postoperative change. There is no evidence of a residual or recurrent mass in the region of the lateral recess or nerve root foramen, satisfactory decompression of the exiting traversing left L5 nerve root, and interval progressive consolidation of expected epidural enhancement consistent with fibrosis within the hemilaminotomy defect.

Discussion

Lesions of notochordal cell origin include EP, a remnant of the fetal notochord; benign notochordal cell tumor (BNCT), a benign tumor; and three malignant tumors that include conventional chordoma, dedifferentiated chordoma, and poorly differentiated chordoma. EP is an extraosseous lesion typically located along the midline of the craniospinal axis from the dorsum sellae to sacrococcygeal regions. As a result of its most common location within the cranial base, EP most often presents with neurological symptoms reflective of its typical localization, including cranial nerve palsies, transclival pseudomeningocele formation, hemisensory abnormalities, hemiparesis, headache, CSF fistula, tension pneumocephalus and pneumoventricle, and even subarachnoid hemorrhage and death.5–8 BNCT and the vast majority of chordomas arise as primary bone tumors within the axial skeleton. Only rarely do chordomas arise as extraosseous spinal tumors or in extra-axial sites such as somatic soft tissue, viscera, nasopharynx, joint spaces, and the appendicular skeleton.4,9,10

Studies examining the imaging features of EP have primarily centered on those arising in the clivus, the most common site of origin. Although imaging findings can aid in the differential diagnosis of EP and chordoma involving the skull, inconsistencies may arise, leading to diagnostic uncertainty.8,11 The single reported case of an EP lesion located in the mobile spine1 was localized to the thoracic spine and described as cystic, consisting of “mucoid fluid” and a cyst wall. Based primarily on autopsy studies, EP lesions are said to typically range in size from a few millimeters to 1–2 cm.12,13 The size of the EP lesion in the single case of Rengachary et al.1 was not documented, although the cyst volume of 0.5 mL suggests the lesion was small, and no obvious extrinsic bony scalloping within the foramen was identified in the published images. The combination of a notable absence of any associated osteolytic or osteoblastic changes suggestive of bone destruction, along with the absence of recurrence at the 3-year follow-up, would support the benign diagnosis of EP in this case.

From an imaging perspective, spinal intraosseous chordomas typically present as lytic destructive lesions of variable size and are frequently associated with a soft tissue mass. In contrast, intradural or extradural extraosseous chordomas present as soft tissue masses of variable size and often demonstrate nonspecific MRI signal characteristics, including low to intermediate signal intensity on T1-weighted images and hyperintense signal on fluid-sensitive sequences; as a result, they may mimic a variety of benign and malignant tumors. Although these soft tissue masses vary in size, the literature commonly describes them as measuring greater than 2.0 cm.14–17 They often resemble benign neurogenic tumors, sometimes demonstrating a plexiform appearance18 or dumbbell morphology, with the largest portions of the tumor being extracanalicular, with or without prominent vertebral bone scalloping and/or bony foraminal expansion.4,9,16,19–24

Regarding the natural history of spinal EP and extraosseous spinal chordoma, the postoperative recurrence rate provides some insight. No recurrence at 3 years was documented in our case and in the single previously reported case of spinal EP,1 which suggests a more indolent course for spinal EP. Interestingly, the absence of recurrence at follow-up ranging from 6 months to 7 years after lesionectomy alone in nearly all the published cases of extraosseous spinal chordomas, presumed to be benign tumors on preoperative imaging, suggests a more indolent natural history when compared to osseous spinal chordoma.4,13–15,23 Only a single case of en bloc bony and vascular resection followed by spinal reconstruction has been described for the management of extraosseous spinal chordoma. It involved a tumor in the cervical region diagnosed as chordoma by a preoperative needle biopsy and showed no recurrence at the 12-month follow-up.4 Nevertheless, the biological behavior of these extraosseous spinal tumors—a clinical metric that would aid in diagnostic clarity—is not entirely understood due to the relatively small number of patients, varied treatment approaches, and lack of long-term follow-up in published reports.

From a surgical management perspective, the clinical distinction between EP and intradural or extradural extraosseous spinal chordomas appears arbitrary given their similar histopathological appearances. However, even the mention of chordoma on intraoperative and/or final histopathological analysis introduces uncertainty regarding lesionectomy versus en bloc resection, a dilemma also encountered in the skull base.8 To this end, Alsavaf and Prevedello25 have recently suggested the use of a paradigm that consolidates EP and extraosseous spinal chordoma “as part of the spectrum of the same disease, chordoma, [with classification] based on severity according to the radiological findings for size, location, and bone involvement.” Similarly, Stevens et al.26 suggested the use of a more contemporary classification of clival notochordal lesions with descriptive terminology that reflects a spectrum of disease, ranging from benign to malignant, rather than exclusively categorizing them as EP or chordoma. Whether such a paradigm as this is broadly applicable has yet to be determined. Nevertheless, it is important for surgeons to be aware of this current diagnostic dilemma when planning surgical management, particularly because tumors diagnosed as extraosseous spinal chordoma appear to have a better prognosis than intraosseous chordoma. Hopefully, elucidation of tumor molecular genetics combined with long-term follow-up involving a larger cohort will provide clarity regarding disease classification and the surgical decision-making process.15

Observations

To our knowledge, this is the first case documenting lumbar radiculopathy associated with focal nerve root compression from a notochordal lesion consistent with EP. An isolated case involving extraosseous neural compression within the thoracic spine1 has been reported in association with radiculopathy. Our case is unique in that the EP lesion had a preoperative imaging appearance that included the differential diagnosis of acute focal disc herniation.27 However, the gross appearance of the lesion at the time of lumbar surgery clearly was inconsistent with herniated disc material. Ultimately, the histopathological findings, size, imaging features, and origin within the nerve root foramen were felt to be consistent with the current clinical paradigm of EP rather than extraosseous chordoma or intervertebral disc material. In particular, the small size (10 mm × 6 mm) aligns with the range historically used in defining EP, a diagnosis that also minimizes the risk of overly aggressive treatment. Nevertheless, given the paucity of clinical studies examining the definition of spinal EP, follow-up imaging was recommended for the patient in this report.

Lessons

The extraosseous presence of a focal notochordal lesion, diagnosed as consistent with EP within the lumbosacral junction and resulting in symptomatic lumbar radiculopathy, has not been reported in the neurosurgical or spine literature. We report this case of EP within the origin of the left L5–S1 nerve root foramen compressing the exiting traversing left L5 nerve root and resulting in clinically symptomatic left L5 radiculopathy. The patient described in this case report underwent successful gross-total microsurgical resection of the focal notochordal lesion, which resulted in complete resolution of her left L5 radiculopathy. Thirty-nine-month follow-up imaging revealed no residual or recurrent tumor. The classification of extraosseous notochordal lesions along the craniospinal axis will likely evolve as more long-term clinical follow-up and molecular genetic analysis become available.
