Identification of myocardial contractility-related genes as regenerative targets and diagnostic biomarkers in coronary artery disease
Authors: Fangzhou Qu, Xifeng An, Yaqi Lei, Chong Lu, Hanxiu Liu, Huayan Ma, Yongle Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary artery disease (CAD) causes irreversible myocardial dysfunction and progressive heart failure due to loss of contractile function and limited endogenous regenerative capacity. Current therapeutic strategies fail to restore lost myocardium or regenerate damaged cardiac tissue. Identifying dysregulated contractility-related genes may reveal actionable targets for stem cell engineering, iPSC-derived cardiomyocyte design, and tissue regeneration aimed at restoring myocardial contractility and function.MethodsTranscriptomic data from the GSE20680 dataset (195 total samples; 87 CAD cases versus 52 healthy controls, N = 139; 56 intermediate stenosis subjects used for sensitivity analyses) were analyzed. Functional enrichment was performed with clusterProfiler, and protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed with STRING/Cytoscape. Protein validation was conducted in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from 64 angiographically confirmed CAD patients and 55 matched healthy controls by Western blotting, with diagnostic accuracy assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Mechanistic studies employed human coronary artery smooth muscle cells (HCASMCs) transduced with lentiviral overexpression vectors or transfected with siRNA constructs targeting selected DEGs; proliferation (colony formation assay) and apoptosis (Annexin V/PI flow cytometry) were evaluated to establish regenerative targeting rationale.ResultsDifferential expression analysis identified 319 DEGs (226 downregulated, 93 upregulated). Nine myocardial contractility-related genes were prioritized: UQCRQ, COX7C, COX6C, SLC8A1, COX7B, COX7A2, TNNT2, CACNB2, and CACNB1. Protein expression changes in PBMC lysates were directionally consistent with mRNA dysregulation: eight genes were significantly downregulated at the protein level, while COX7B was upregulated (P < 0.05). ROC analysis demonstrated robust diagnostic performance (AUC 0.799-0.900). In HCASMCs, correcting dysregulation of the eight downregulated genes (by lentiviral overexpression) suppressed proliferation and enhanced apoptosis, suggesting restoration of contractile phenotype. COX7B exhibited the reverse pattern, supporting its distinct detrimental role. These expression patterns suggest that gene correction in engineered cardiomyocytes could restore contractile function and improve cell survival post-transplantation.ConclusionThis nine-gene contractility-related signature represents a set of CAD-associated biomarkers with plausible links to myocardial excitation-contraction pathways. Our integrated PBMC and HCASMC data suggest that these genes may influence vascular remodeling, and they highlight candidate targets for future regenerative studies, but direct validation in human myocardial tissue and cardiomyocytes will be required before firm conclusions about myocardial contractility or regenerative efficacy can be drawn.Keywords: Coronary artery disease, Myocardial contraction, Bioinformatics analysis, Coronary smooth muscle cells

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is characterized by the gradual narrowing of coronary arteries due to atherosclerotic plaque accumulation. This process leads to myocardial ischemia, hypoxia, and necrosis, which contribute to progressive cardiac dysfunction [1,2]. CAD represents a significant global burden, with nearly 300 million cases estimated in 2022 alone [3,4]. Its risk increases with age, metabolic disturbances, and other comorbidities and more than 80% of individuals above 60 years develop some degree of CAD, with almost one-third progressing to symptomatic coronary heart disease [5,6]. The etiology of CAD is multifactorial. Approximately half of CAD cases are attributed to genetic predisposition, while environmental factors also play key roles in disease onset and progression [7]. Multiple differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including IL1B, ICAM1, CCL2, EGR, and IL8, have been implicated in CAD pathogenesis [8,9]. Despite advances in pharmacological therapy and revascularization strategies, CAD continues to pose serious clinical challenges, highlighting the need for deeper mechanistic insights and the development of personalized treatment approaches [[10], [11], [12]].

Myocardial contractility is a fundamental determinant of cardiac performance, defined as the intrinsic ability of cardiomyocytes to regulate the strength and velocity of contraction independent of preload and afterload [13,14]. In CAD, compensatory increases in contractility may initially support cardiac output but eventually exacerbate oxygen demand, leading to ischemic injury and functional decline [15,16]. Regulation of contractility may therefore represent a novel therapeutic avenue. Recent developments in myocardial regenerative medicine have further emphasized the potential of targeting contractility-related pathways to improve outcomes in CAD and heart failure [[17], [18], [19]].

Previous transcriptomic studies have examined datasets such as GSE12288, GSE28858, GSE23766, GSE19339, and GSE56885 to identify DEGs and microRNAs involved in CAD progression [8,[20], [21], [22]]. Notably, transcriptomic analysis of GSE20680 has also been applied in recent studies and offers well-characterized angiographic phenotyping, making it suitable for identifying CAD-associated gene expression signatures when analyzed with strict case-control stratification. Additional studies demonstrated development of 10-gene immune-related prognostic model for CAD, highlighting the role of immune dysregulation in disease progression [[23], [24], [25]]. Additionally, Chang et al. identified hub biomarkers linked to inflammatory pathogenesis using machine learning and bioinformatic analyses, emphasizing the contributions of immune responses and immune cell infiltration to CAD [26].

In contrast, growing evidence indicates that impaired myocardial contractility and dysregulation of vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) phenotype are critical but understudied contributors to CAD pathogenesis. Notably, dysregulation of VSMC proliferation and reduced apoptosis are hallmarks of arterial remodeling and atherosclerosis progression, processes directly linking cardiac dysfunction to CAD [27]. Remarkably, despite recognized links between contractility-related gene dysregulation and CAD pathogenesis, no investigation has systematically explored the coordinated dysregulation of myocardial contractility-related genes in CAD by combining transcriptomic analysis (GSE20680) with protein-level validation in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from CAD patients and functional assessment in human coronary artery smooth muscle cells (HCASMCs). PBMC were chosen as the sample source for transcriptomic and protein validation for several reasons: (i) PBMCs are the predominant cellular component of the GSE20680 whole-blood samples, ensuring tissue consistency between the discovery dataset and our validation cohort; (ii) Peripheral blood gene expression signatures have been established as surrogate biomarkers for coronary artery disease, and, given that PBMCs are the major cellular component of whole blood, PBMC transcriptomic changes are expected to reflect disease-related cardiovascular and myocardial pathophysiology [23], and (iii) peripheral blood sampling is minimally invasive and clinically feasible, facilitating future translational applications. HCASMCs were selected for functional experiments because phenotypic switching of vascular smooth muscle cells (aberrant proliferation and apoptosis resistance) is a central pathological process in CAD-related arterial remodeling and plaque formation [28]. Dysregulation of myocardial contraction-related genes may therefore indirectly influence myocardial blood supply and contractile performance by modulating VSMC function, a mechanism that is consistent with recent work showing that VSMC contractile dysfunction and vascular toxicity contribute to subsequent cardiac complications [29].

Impaired contractility in CAD reflects dysregulation of genes governing excitation-contraction coupling and cardiomyocyte survival, which simultaneously compromises endogenous regenerative capacity and limits the heart's ability to repair ischemic damage. Despite advances in acute coronary management, current therapies fail to restore lost contractile function or regenerate myocardium representing a critical gap that regenerative medicine strategies targeting contractility-related genes could address. Understanding the molecular basis of contractility dysfunction may unlock opportunities for stem cell engineering and tissue regeneration aimed at restoring myocardial function in CAD patients.

Recent transcriptomic studies using GSE20680 and related CAD blood datasets have identified immune-related diagnostic markers [25,30]. Similarly, rRNA-depleted RNA-seq analysis of large CAD cohorts has revealed disease-associated molecular networks [31]. However, these studies have primarily focused on immune dysregulation and pan-disease mechanisms, without systematically examining the coordinated dysregulation of myocardial contractility-related genes in CAD or linking such genes to VSMC phenotypic switching and regenerative therapeutic targets which is the central innovations of the present work.

This study addresses this critical gap by systematically investigating nine myocardial contractility-associated genes (UQCRQ, COX7C, COX6C, SLC8A1, COX7B, COX7A2, TNNT2, CACNB2, and CACNB1) found dysregulated in CAD. Through integrated transcriptomic analysis and functional validation in PBMCs and HCASMCs, this nine-gene panel exhibits strong diagnostic performance in peripheral blood and mechanistically regulates HCASMC proliferation and apoptosis, key processes in coronary artery smooth muscle cell phenotypic switching and vascular remodeling.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and processing

The gene expression dataset GSE20680 was downloaded from the GEO database (GEO Accession: GSE20680; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE20680). This series profiles whole blood from 195 total subjects on the Agilent-014850 Whole Human Genome Microarray 444K G4112F, platform GPL4133. Subjects were phenotypically categorized based on coronary angiography findings as follows: (1) healthy controls (n = 52; <25% luminal stenosis in all arteries); (2) intermediate stenosis subjects (n = 56; 25-50% stenosis in any artery); and (3) CAD cases (n = 87; ≥70% stenosis in 1 major vessel OR >50% stenosis in 2 or more arteries). For the primary differential expression analysis, we performed a binary comparison between CAD cases (n = 87) and healthy controls (n = 52), excluding the 56 intermediate stenosis subjects (N = 139 for primary analysis). Intermediate stenosis subjects were retained and analyzed separately in sensitivity analyses to assess robustness of findings and validate the absence of confounding from this heterogeneous group.

Differential expression analysis

Differential expression between CAD cases and controls was assessed with limma linear models and empirical Bayes moderation. The design matrix included disease status. Covariates such as age or sex, if available in sample metadata, were considered in sensitivity analyses. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method. Genes with adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed [32]. Volcano plots and heatmaps were generated to visualize DEGs.

Functional and pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs

To focus on DEGs directly related to myocardial contraction, we applied a multi-step filtering strategy. First, GO term selection was performed using the Gene Ontology database (http://www.geneontology.org/), restricting the analysis to biological process (BP) terms that are directly linked to contractile function: (i) “muscle contraction” (GO:0006936, BP); (ii) “cardiac muscle contraction” (GO:0060047, BP); and (iii) “calcium signaling” (GO:0007202, BP). Because “calcium signaling” is a broad term, we further intersected this term with the KEGG myocardial contraction pathway (hsa04260), retaining only genes that also appeared in hsa04260 to ensure myocardial specificity. Second, screening criteria were applied as follows: genes were required to (i) map to at least one of the above GO terms or the KEGG myocardial contraction pathway; (ii) rank among the top 20% of hub genes in the PPI network according to cytoHubba's MCC algorithm; and (iii) be present in at least one GO term plus one pathway, or in two or more of the selected GO terms. Third, manual curation was performed to exclude genes that were clearly unrelated to myocardial contraction, such as immune cell-specific calcium signaling genes (e.g., IKBKG) or vascular endothelium-specific genes (e.g., EDN1). Where indicated, we also conducted GSEA on ranked gene lists to capture coordinated pathway-level shifts [21].

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction

To focus on genes most relevant to contractile function, we filtered DEGs using GO terms related to “muscle contraction,” “cardiac muscle contraction,” and calcium signaling. These myocardial contractility-related DEGs were uploaded to the STRING v12 database, with interactions restricted to the human interactome and a medium confidence cutoff (≥0.40). Networks were visualized in Cytoscape v3.8.2. Hub genes were ranked using cytoHubba (MCC algorithm), and dense submodules were detected with MCODE to aid interpretation [23].

Study subjects

A total of 64 patients with angiographically confirmed CAD and 55 age and sex-matched healthy controls were recruited from Shaanxi Provincial People's Hospital between April 2018 and December 2023. Sample size was estimated using G-Power software (version 3.1) for an independent-samples t-test comparing CAD patients versus healthy controls (two-tailed, α = 0.05). For a medium effect size (d = 0.5) and desired power of 0.80, the calculation indicated 64 subjects per group (128 total). We recruited 64 angiographically confirmed CAD patients and 55 age- and sex-matched healthy controls (N = 119 total). All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, following STROBE and STARD guidelines, with approval from the Human Ethics Committee of our Hospital (Approval #2018065). Patients were eligible if they were diagnosed with CAD by coronary angiography, were fully conscious, and had intact communication skills. Individuals with vital organ dysfunction, coagulation abnormalities, or a history of other cardio-cerebrovascular diseases (such as stroke, cerebral hemorrhage, or myocardial infarction) were excluded. PBMC isolation protocol

Whole blood (5-10 mL per subject) was collected in EDTA tubes and processed within 2 h. Blood was diluted 1:1 with PBS and carefully layered over Ficoll-Paque PLUS (GE Healthcare) in a 15 mL conical tube. After centrifugation at 400g for 30 min, the buffy coat containing PBMCs was collected, washed twice with PBS, and cell pellets were resuspended in RIPA lysis buffer. Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min with periodic vortexing, then centrifuged at 12,000g for 15 min to collect the supernatant.

Western blotting analysis

The protein concentration of the PBMC lysates was estimated using a BCA protein assay kit (Cat #23225, ThermoFisher Scientific). An equal amount of total protein from each sample was then mixed with SDS-PAGE sample loading buffer, denatured by boiling at 95-100 °C for 10 min, and subjected to electrophoretic separation and PVDF blotting. After blocking with 5% skimmed milk for 1 h, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies at 1:1000 dilution: anti-UQCRQ (Cat# ab224180, Abcam), anti-COX7C (Cat# 11411-2-AP, Proteintech), anti-COX6C (Cat# ab150422, Abcam), anti-SLC8A1 (Cat# A03876, BosterBio), anti-COX7A2 (Cat# ab248815, Abcam), anti-TNNT2 (Cat# MA5-12960, ThermoFisher Scientific), anti-CACNB2 (Cat# ab93606, Abcam), anti-CACNB1 (Cat# ab230019, Abcam), and β-actin (Cat# 4967, CST). After rinsing with TBST, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies at 1:2000 dilution: anti-rabbit (Cat# 7074, CST) or anti-mouse (Cat# 7076, CST). Bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (ECL, ThermoFisher Scientific) and quantified with ImageJ software (NIH, USA). Original western blots are provided as supplementary file, S1.

Cell culture and transfection

Human HCASMCs were obtained from BeNa Culture Collection (China) and maintained in 10% high-glucose DMEM supplemented in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were used for experiments. When cultures reached 70-80% confluence in 12-well plates, cells were transduced with lentiviral overexpression vectors or transfected with small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), along with their corresponding negative controls (nc), targeting UQCRQ, COX7C, COX6C, SLC8A1, COX7B, COX7A2, TNNT2, CACNB2, and CACNB1. All recombinant vectors were synthesized by Obio Technology (Shanghai, China). For lentiviral overexpression, HCASMCs were transduced with lentiviral particles at multiplicity of infection (MOI) 10 in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h, after which the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. Lentiviral transduction resulted in stable genomic integration, establishing polyclonal populations with constitutive target gene expression without antibiotic selection. For siRNA-mediated knockdown, cells were transfected with 50 nM siRNA using Lipofectamine® 2000 (Invitrogen, USA). Functional assays were performed 48 h post-transduction (for lentivirus) or 48 h post-transfection (for siRNA).

Cloning of HCASMCs

For lentiviral overexpression experiments, 48 h post-transduction, HCASMCs were trypsinized, counted, and reseeded at 1 × 102 cells per 60 mm dish for clonogenic assays. Cells were cultured for two weeks, with medium refreshed every three days. During the two-week culture period, the integrated lentiviral transgene ensured sustained and stable protein overexpression, independent of continued selection, enabling the assessment of long-term proliferative effects. Colonies were washed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and air-dried. Colonies containing ≥50 cells were counted under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan) to evaluate clonogenic capacity.

Flow cytometry

Apoptosis in transfected HCASMCs was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC/Propidium Iodide (PI) apoptosis detection kit (Cat# 88-8007-72, ThermoFisher Scientific). After 48 h of transduction (for lentiviral overexpression) or transfection (for siRNA knockdown), cells were washed twice with PBS and digested with EDTA-free trypsin for 2 min. For lentiviral transduction experiments, the 48-h timepoint was selected to assess acute changes in apoptosis while maintaining stable transgene expression; for siRNA transfection, this timepoint represents peak knockdown efficiency. Trypsinization was terminated by adding complete medium, and cells were resuspended at 1 × 106/mL in pre-cooled PBS. A total of 500 μL of binding buffer was mixed with 5 μL Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL PI, followed by incubation for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Samples were analyzed on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA), and data were processed with FlowJo software (Tree Star, USA). Early apoptotic cells (Annexin V-FITC positive/PI negative) and late apoptotic cells (Annexin V-FITC positive/PI positive) were quantified, and the total apoptosis rate was calculated as their sum.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., USA). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For cell-based assays, results represent the average of at least n = 3 independent experiments. Independent-samples t-tests were applied for comparisons between two groups, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni post hoc testing was used for multiple-group comparisons (nc, si, ov). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of hub genes. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

DEG screening results and enrichment analysis

To investigate the genetic mechanisms linking myocardial contractility to CAD, we first performed transcriptomic analysis of the publicly available GSE20680 dataset. This dataset comprises 195 whole blood samples, of which 139 were included in the primary analysis (87 CAD cases vs. 52 healthy controls, excluding 56 intermediate stenosis subjects). This dataset was selected because it includes well-annotated, angiographically confirmed CAD and control samples and has been widely used in prior CAD bioinformatics studies, providing a credible foundation for reliable DEG discovery. Exclusion of intermediate subjects in the primary analysis ensured clear phenotypic separation and reduced potential confounding from angiographically undefined disease states. Differential expression analysis of the GSE20680 dataset identified a total of 319 DEGs, including 226 downregulated and 93 upregulated genes (Fig. 1A). The expression patterns of the top 50 DEGs are shown in the heatmap (Fig. 1B). GO enrichment revealed that these DEGs were mainly involved in biological processes such as localization and transport, and were enriched in cellular components including the cytosol, endomembrane system, vesicles, and nucleoplasm (Fig. 1C). KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated significant enrichment in metabolic pathways, pathways in cancer, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), oxidative phosphorylation, and Parkinson disease, among others (Fig. 1D). After applying the GO- and pathway-based filtering strategy described in Section 2.3 (GO:0006936, GO:0060047, GO:0007202 plus KEGG/Reactome/HALLMARK cross-validation and manual curation), nine core genes directly related to myocardial contraction were identified from the 319 DEGs, among which UQCRQ, COX7C, COX6C, COX7B, COX7A2 formed strong functional clusters in the PPI network (Fig. 1E). In summary, screening of GSE20680 revealed 319 DEGs, including nine linked to myocardial contractility, forming the basis for validation and functional studies. Analysis of clinical expression of DEGs

To validate the clinical relevance of the nine identified DEGs from GSE20680, we measured their protein expression levels in PBMC lysates from 64 CAD patients and 55 healthy controls by Western blotting. Consistent with the transcriptomic findings, protein levels of eight genes (UQCRQ, COX7C, COX6C, SLC8A1, COX7A2, TNNT2, CACNB2, and CACNB1) were reduced in CAD patients compared with controls (Fig. 2A–J). However, COX7B protein was significantly upregulated in CAD patients in contrast to the downregulation observed for the other genes. ROC analysis (Table 2) confirmed strong diagnostic performance (AUC 0.863, 95% CI 0.800-0.926). Collectively, the concordance between transcriptomic dysregulation (GSE20680) and protein expression changes (validation cohort) in the same biological compartment (peripheral blood cells) supports the biological validity and clinical translatability of the identified gene signature.

Effects of DEGs on HCASMC proliferation

To investigate the functional impact of the identified DEGs on CAD pathogenesis, we examined their effects on HCASMC proliferation using colony formation assays. Cells were transduced with lentiviral overexpression vectors (ov) or transfected with siRNA (si) for each target gene, with negative control (nc) used as baseline. For lentiviral overexpression groups, stable genomic integration ensured sustained protein expression throughout the two-week colony formation period. Overexpression of UQCRQ, COX7C, COX6C, SLC8A1, COX7A2, TNNT2, CACNB2, and CACNB1 significantly reduced the clonogenic capacity of HCASMCs, whereas silencing of these genes promoted proliferation (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3A–I). These results indicate that these myocardial contractility-related DEGs act as potential suppressors of vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation, a key process in atherosclerotic plaque formation and stability. In contrast, lentiviral-mediated overexpression of COX7B exhibited a pro-proliferative and anti-apoptotic phenotype, significantly enhancing HCASMC proliferation and reducing apoptosis. siRNA-mediated silencing of COX7B produced the opposite effect, reducing proliferation and enhancing apoptosis. Collectively, these findings suggested that the identified DEGs influenced vascular remodeling processes centrally to CAD progression.

Influence of DEGs on HCASMC apoptosis

To further evaluate the role of the identified DEGs in CAD, we examined their effects on apoptosis in HCASMCs at 48 h post-transduction or post-transfection using flow cytometry. Consistent with the proliferation results, lentiviral-mediated overexpression of UQCRQ, COX7C, COX6C, SLC8A1, COX7A2, TNNT2, CACNB2, and CACNB1 significantly increased apoptosis rates, whereas siRNA-mediated silencing reduced apoptosis compared with the nc group (P < 0.05). By contrast, COX7B demonstrated the opposite pattern: lentiviral-mediated overexpression reduced apoptosis rates, whereas siRNA-mediated silencing enhanced apoptosis compared with the negative control (P < 0.05, Fig. 4A–I). These findings suggested that dysregulation of myocardial contractility-related DEGs contributed to CAD by disturbing the balance between proliferation and apoptosis in HCASMCs.

Discussion

CAD remains a major global health burden, with acute complications such as myocardial infarction and sudden cardiac failure underscoring the urgent need for early detection and personalized therapeutic approaches [33]. Systemic inflammatory markers such as CRP are widely used, but their lack of specificity being elevated in diverse conditions including renal failure and other inflammatory states limits their diagnostic accuracy for CAD [34,35]. Advances in sequencing technology and the integration of bioinformatics with experimental validation now provide a robust framework to identify novel, disease-specific biomarkers and therapeutic targets [36]. In this study, we identified and validated nine myocardial contractility related genes, UQCRQ, COX7C, COX6C, SLC8A1, COX7B, COX7A2, TNNT2, CACNB2, and CACNB1 that are dysregulated in CAD, exhibit considerable diagnostic potential, and functionally modulate key vascular cell processes.

Recent transcriptomic studies using GSE20680 and related CAD blood datasets have primarily focused on immune dysregulation and inflammatory pathways. For example, Feng et al. integrated multiple whole-blood microarray datasets (including GSE20680, GSE20681, and GSE42148) to construct a 14-gene diagnostic model based on differentially expressed immune-related genes and to characterize the peripheral immune landscape and IL13RA1-centred inflammatory mechanisms in CAD [37]. Li et al. focused specifically on the immune microenvironment, using CIBERSORT and WGCNA on GSE20680 and GSE42148 to identify M2 macrophage, associated modules and to derive an eight-gene M2 macrophage-related diagnostic signature (C1orf105, CCL22, CRYGB, FRK, GAP43, REG1P, CALB1, PTPN21) [38]. Zhang et al. analyzed cuproptosis-related genes in GSE20680 and developed a five-gene cuproptosis-based biomarker panel (F5, MT4, RNF7, S100A12, SORD), which they linked to alterations in the peripheral immune microenvironment [30]. In parallel, McCaffrey et al. applied rRNA-depleted whole-blood RNA-seq in angiographically characterized CAD cohorts to define a global transcriptomic signature dominated by widespread downregulation of regulatory T-cell associated transcripts and to propose a blood-based diagnostic test for CAD, without specifically interrogating myocardial contractility-related pathways [39]. These microarray- and RNA-seq-based studies collectively highlight the central role of immune and inflammatory mechanisms in CAD, but they did not systematically interrogate myocardial contractility-related genes or link such genes to VSMC phenotype switching and regenerative targets. In contrast, the present work focused on a nine-gene contractility-related panel, validates its dysregulation at the protein level in PBMCs, and demonstrates direct functional effects on HCASMC proliferation and apoptosis, thereby complementing the existing immune-focused literature with a mechanistic chain from contractile gene dysregulation to vascular remodeling and potential regenerative interventions.

Our bioinformatic analysis of the GSE20680 dataset confirmed the established role of immune and inflammatory pathways in CAD but also revealed marked dysregulation in genes critical for myocardial contractility [40,41]. This finding is clinically relevant, as impaired contractility is a hallmark of CAD progression to heart failure [42]. Importantly, the pharmacological basis of mainstay therapies such as β-blockers involves reducing myocardial oxygen demand by modulating contractility [43]. Thus, our focus on contractility-related genes aligns with established therapeutic concepts while introducing novel molecular targets. To systematically identify dysregulated contractility genes in CAD, we leveraged the GSE20680 transcriptomic dataset, a comprehensive whole blood expression study with detailed angiographic phenotyping (N = 195: 87 CAD cases versus 52 healthy controls, with 56 intermediate stenosis subjects excluded from primary analysis). This stringent binary case-control stratification enabled robust identification of CAD-associated contractility gene dysregulation. This phenotypic stratification based on objectively quantified coronary artery stenosis severity ensures high confidence in both case and control definitions, reducing the risk of misclassification bias. The exclusion of intermediate subjects in the primary analysis, while retaining them for sensitivity checks, strengthens the validity of the identified DEGs by focusing on clearly differentiated disease states. This analytic approach is consistent with cardiovascular biomarker discovery standards and has been successfully employed in prior GSE20680-based studies.

The nine-gene signature forms the nucleus of our discovery. UQCRQ, COX7C, COX6C, COX7B, and COX7A2 formed strong functional clusters, while TNNT2, SLC8A1, CACNB2, and CACNB1 showed weaker or unidirectional associations. These results align with prior evidence, such as UQCRQ mutations being linked to mitochondrial defects in hypoplastic left heart syndrome [44]. Downregulation of mitochondrial complex genes (UQCRQ, COX7C, COX6C, COX7A2) suggests impaired energy production, while reduced SLC8A1, CACNB1, and CACNB2 indicates calcium-handling abnormalities, both of which are central to cardiac contraction and smooth muscle cell function. Decreased TNNT2 further reflects impairment of the contractile apparatus itself. Notably, COX7B exhibited a contrasting pattern, being upregulated in CAD patients and promoting proliferation and suppressing apoptosis when overexpressed in HCASMCs. This apparent mechanistic divergence from the other COX subunits (COX7C, COX6C, COX7A2) warrants mechanistic clarification. Although COX7B belongs to the same cytochrome c oxidase complex as the other subunits identified, its opposite expression trend likely reflects tissue-specific isoform switching rather than functional contradiction. COX7B is part of a family of tissue-specific isoforms of complex IV subunit VIIa, which includes the heart-specific isoform COX7A1 and the liver-type isoforms COX7A2/COX7B. Recent single-cell and in vivo studies have demonstrated that COX7B is upregulated as an early-stage compensatory mechanism in cardiac stress and hypertrophy, where it substitutes for the predominant heart-type isoform COX7A1 [45]. In our CAD cohort, elevated COX7B expression likely reflects a compensatory response to the energy deficits caused by downregulation of the catalytic core subunits (COX1-3) and other structural subunits (COX7C, COX6C, COX7A2). However, this compensatory upregulation of the liver-type isoform COX7B, while initially maintaining ATP synthesis, may eventually become maladaptive. Prolonged substitution of COX7B for COX7A1 promotes a metabolic shift toward increased proliferation and reduced apoptosis in vascular smooth muscle cells, a pro-atherosclerotic phenotype that accelerates CAD progression. This isoform-switching phenomenon is supported by recent evidence showing that COX7B overexpression enhances cellular proliferation and reduces apoptosis rates, whereas COX7A1 is critical for maintaining normal cardiac contractility and preventing progression to heart failure. Thus, the upregulation of COX7B in CAD may represent an initially protective but ultimately detrimental adaptation that contributes to vascular dysfunction and plaque progression.

We validated GSE20680 dataset findings at the functional protein level using PBMC lysates. This approach is justified by several considerations: (i) PBMCs are the major cellular component of GSE20680 whole blood samples, providing direct tissue correspondence between bioinformatic discovery and protein validation; (ii) PBMC gene expression signatures have been extensively validated as surrogate biomarkers in multiple cardiovascular diseases; (iii) peripheral blood transcriptomic profiling is clinically translatable for identifying mechanistic pathways and therapeutic targets in cardiovascular conditions; and (iv) PBMC-based gene expression analysis is clinically viable, requiring only a simple venipuncture, and has demonstrated reproducibility in multi-center cardiovascular studies. Additionally, ROC analysis confirmed the strong diagnostic performance of this nine-gene panel. In contrast to traditional inflammatory markers such as CRP, which demonstrate limited diagnostic specificity for CAD (AUC 0.565-0.735) and are frequently elevated in diverse pathological conditions including renal failure and systemic infections, the nine-gene contractility signature exhibits superior discriminatory performance (AUC 0.799-0.900) with substantially higher specificity, positioning it as a superior biomarker for CAD diagnosis and patient stratification. This finding is supported by prior studies demonstrating the utility of peripheral blood biomarkers in cardiovascular disease diagnosis [[46], [47], [48]]. Public human cardiac single-cell atlases demonstrate that cardiomyocytes express classical sarcomeric and calcium-handling genes, including TNNT2 and SLC8A1 [49], which are components of our nine-gene panel, supporting their direct involvement in excitation-contraction coupling. These evidences support an association between this gene signature and myocardial contractile function that extends beyond PBMCs and VSMCs, although direct functional validation in cardiomyocytes will require future experimental studies.

We also assessed the functional roles of these DEGs in HCASMCs using lentiviral-mediated stable overexpression and siRNA-mediated transient knockdown. Lentiviral transduction enabled sustained transgene expression over the two-week colony formation assay period due to stable genomic integration, while siRNA provided acute knockdown for short-term functional assays. Overexpression of UQCRQ, COX7C, COX6C, SLC8A1, COX7A2, TNNT2, CACNB2, and CACNB1 suppressed proliferation and enhanced apoptosis, while silencing produced the opposite effect. This cellular behavior hyperproliferation coupled with apoptosis resistance has been shown to drive CAD progression [27]. Thus, the loss of these protective genes may contribute directly to disease pathology. In contrast, COX7B promoted proliferation and reduced apoptosis, underscoring it as a potential risk factor and unique therapeutic target. Our results also align with reports identifying TNNT2 and CACNB1 as markers in acute ischemic heart failure [50,51].

Beyond diagnostic utility, our identified contractility genes represent actionable targets for regenerative medicine strategies. The dysregulated genes modulating proliferation and apoptosis in HCASMCs could be leveraged in multiple regenerative contexts: (1) engineering iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes with corrected gene expression profiles to restore contractile function, (2) designing cardiac tissue patches that mimic normal contractility gene regulation, (3) developing gene therapy approaches delivering corrected contractility genes to infarcted myocardium, and (4) identifying small-molecule modulators of these pathways to enhance stem cell survival and contractile differentiation. For instance, genes suppressing apoptosis could be upregulated in transplanted stem cells to improve engraftment and long-term function, while genes enhancing contractile protein expression could be amplified to accelerate functional recovery.

Among our nine candidate genes, TNNT2 (cardiac troponin T), UQCRQ (ubiquinol-cytochrome c reductase core protein), and CACNB1 (calcium channel beta-1 subunit) represent particularly promising regenerative targets due to their direct roles in excitation-contraction coupling and established druggability. TNNT2 mutations are amenable to gene correction via CRISPR-Cas9 or antisense approaches; UQCRQ dysregulation could be addressed through mitochondrial-targeted therapies enhancing oxidative phosphorylation; and CACNB1 modulation via calcium channel modulators could restore intracellular calcium signaling essential for contractility. Future studies should prioritize validation of these genes in iPSC-derived cardiomyocyte models and in vivo cardiac regeneration assays to establish causality and efficacy of regenerative interventions.

This study also have other limitations. First, the transcriptomic discovery phase relied on a single whole-blood microarray dataset (GSE20680), and we did not perform a formal replication of the nine-gene contractility-related signature in independent whole-blood RNA-seq cohorts such as those reported by McCaffrey et al. [31]. Second, our external comparison to these RNA-seq studies is therefore qualitative rather than based on re-analysis of raw sequencing data. Future work should validate this nine-gene panel across additional RNA-seq datasets and multi-center cohorts, ideally integrating multi-omics and longitudinal clinical outcomes, to firmly establish its generalizability and prognostic utility.

Taken together, our findings identify a nine-gene, contractility-related signature that is dysregulated in CAD blood, shows promising diagnostic performance, and modulates VSMC proliferation and apoptosis, thereby nominating these genes as candidate biomarkers and putative regenerative targets whose direct roles in myocardial contractility will require further in vivo and cardiomyocyte-specific studies.
