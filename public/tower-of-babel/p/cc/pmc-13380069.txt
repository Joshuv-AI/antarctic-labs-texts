Lessons from the Bundibugyo outbreak for the Americas
Authors: Denise O. Garrett, Rebecca M. Martin
Published: 2026

The Bundibugyo virus disease (BVD) outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), with cross-border spread into Uganda, may seem geographically distant from the Americas. Yet, there is an important lesson for the region: outbreaks are contained not only by disease-specific tools, but by preparedness systems built long before a crisis becomes visible. For countries in the Americas facing recurrent and emerging threats, including dengue, yellow fever, Zika, Oropouche, and other zoonotic and vector-borne diseases, the outbreak underscores the need for sustained investment in integrated, risk-informed preparedness at community, subnational, national, and regional levels.1, 2, 3, 4

The declaration of a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) for BVD by WHO has focused global attention on the urgent need to respond.5 The current outbreak has also exposed important preparedness gaps: no vaccine is currently licensed for the prevention of BVD,6 point-of-care diagnostic capacity remains limited because the GeneXpert platform cannot detect Bundibugyo virus and confirmation requires access to broader laboratory testing capacity, approved therapeutic options remain limited, and response efforts have been complicated by conflict, insecurity, population displacement, fragile health systems, and longstanding mistrust.5, 6, 7, 8 Beyond these immediate challenges, however, the broader lesson is that preparedness systems must be built before an outbreak begins.

By the time an outbreak pathogen is detected, many of the conditions that shape the response have already been set. Outbreaks do not reveal only the characteristics of a pathogen; they reveal the strength of the systems that were in place before the first case was recognized. Surveillance networks, laboratory capacity, specimen transport systems, trained response teams, emergency operations centres, logistics and supply chains, access to medical countermeasures, infection prevention and control capacity, adequate personal protective equipment, surge capacity to establish isolation or quarantine units, risk communication mechanisms, community engagement structures, and coordination between public health and health-care delivery systems determine whether outbreaks are detected early, contained quickly, and managed safely. When these systems function well, they are largely invisible. When they fail, they suddenly become the focus of international attention.

Much of the discussion surrounding the current BVD outbreak has focused on the absence of licensed Bundibugyo-specific vaccines, validated diagnostic pathways, and proven therapeutics.5, 6, 7, 8 But medical countermeasures alone do not stop outbreaks. Diagnostics do not help if available platforms cannot detect the outbreak pathogen, or if confirmation depends on transporting specimens over long distances to laboratories with broader testing capacity. Ebola treatment units cannot be rapidly established or sustained without trained personnel, reliable supply chains to deliver critical materials, and the operational surge capacity needed to respond at scale. Personal protective equipment does not protect health workers if stockpiles are non-existent, or have been depleted, and supply chains cannot be rapidly reactivated with the needed logistical support. Vaccines, even highly effective ones, cannot compensate for delayed detection, fragmented surveillance, or the absence of sufficient response capacity.

The success of outbreak control depends as much on social and operational infrastructure as on biomedical innovation. Affected communities must be partners in outbreak response. Trust between communities and health systems is built daily, not during an emergency. Contact tracing does not work without trust. Community trust requires more than technical expertise. Community engagement cannot begin when an outbreak is declared.5 Trust, local partnerships, and culturally appropriate communication must be established before a crisis occurs.

For the Americas, the lesson is not that countries should prepare for every pathogen, separately, but that preparedness should be integrated across the threats countries are most likely to face. Recent dramatic increases in dengue activity in the region, the geographic expansion and unusual clinical concerns associated with Oropouche virus, and the re-emergence of yellow fever in areas with no recent history of transmission all point to the same critical need: countries require preparedness systems that can detect, verify, and respond to evolving risks before they become larger emergencies.1, 2, 3 These diseases differ from Bundibugyo virus, but they test many of the same capacities and systems: early warning and event-based surveillance, laboratory confirmation, risk communication, community engagement, trained frontline workers, emergency coordination, cross-border information sharing, and the ability to maintain essential health services during a crisis. These capacities need to be embedded in risk-informed national and subnational plans, supported by One Health governance structures that connect human, animal, and environmental health systems, and operationalized at the lowest level where outbreaks are first detected and response begins.4

Recent disruptions in global health financing make this lesson more urgent.9 In many countries, preparedness capacities have been built through a combination of domestic investment and external technical and financial assistance. These investments have supported workforce development, surveillance systems, laboratory strengthening, emergency preparedness planning, outbreak response, and purchase of medical countermeasures. The weakening or loss of these external investments raises urgent questions about how core capacities for preparedness will be sustained. Countries should not depend solely on external support; however, preparedness systems built over decades cannot be allowed to erode without consequences. As the Global Preparedness Monitoring Board has noted, the cost of preparedness is measured in billions, whereas the cost of failure is measured in trillions.10 Sustained domestic financing, regional cooperation, and predictable international partnerships are all needed to maintain the systems that detect, verify, and respond to health threats before they become regional crises.

For the Americas, the question raised by the current outbreak is whether countries and regional institutions are prepared and can sustain the systems between health emergencies, when political attention fades and there are multiple health priorities. Health systems continue to fall into a cycle of panic and neglect, mobilizing resources during crises but failing to maintain preparedness once outbreaks recede.11 Preparedness cannot be built during a crisis. It is a long-term investment that must be financed, tested, strengthened, and maintained before it is needed. Waiting until the next outbreak to rebuild capacity is not preparedness. It is response. By then, transmission has already occurred, opportunities for containment may have been lost, and the cost of inaction is measured in lives rather than budgets.

The Bundibugyo outbreak will not be the last test of preparedness. The next test may come from a different pathogen, but the systems required to detect it early, respond effectively, and maintain public trust are shaped by decisions made now.
