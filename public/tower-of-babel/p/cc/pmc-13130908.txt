Clinical Strategy for Gerbode Defects
Authors: Lianglong Ma, Xiangming Fan, Zhuo Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: right-sided catheterization, tricuspid valve, valve repair, ventricular septal defect

Background

After reading the report by Jebain et al1 in this issue of JACC: Case Reports, we have 2 points that need discussion: first, acquired Gerbode defect (aGD) after tricuspid valve repair is not rare, but the long course of diagnosis is rare, and the difficulty of diagnosis calls for more focus on this condition; and second, there are multiple options for the management of aGD, with device occlusion being one of them.

Gerbode defect (GD) is named after Frank Gerbode, who successfully performed surgery on 5 patients with this anomaly in 1958.2 GD means that there is a left ventricular–to–right atrial (LV-RA) communication.

Depending on its pathology, GD can be classified into 2 kinds, direct and indirect. A direct defect is located in the membranous septum between the LV and RA, and an indirect defect presents with a perimembranous ventricular septal defect (VSD) associated with tricuspid regurgitation, which is very common in clinic work. Sakakibara and Konno3 classified GD into 3 types: supravalvular (type I), subvalvular (type II), and intermediate (type III; a combination of types I and II) based on the location of the defect with respect to the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve.

If classifying GD on its etiology, the defect can be either congenital or acquired. A congenital Gerbode defect (cGD) means the defect occurred during heart formulation before birth.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Acquired GD can be iatrogenic or noniatrogenic.4,10, 11, 12, 13, 14 Acquired iatrogenic GD occurs after cardiac surgeries close to the membranous septum, such as mitral/aortic valve replacement,11, 12, 13 tricuspid annuloplasty,1,14,15 repair of complete atrio-VSD, or percutaneous interventions such as transcatheter aortic valve replacement, atrioventricular node ablation, and endomyocardial biopsy. The conditions giving rise to acquired noniatrogenic GD include infective endocarditis,8,10,12 myocardial infarction in the right coronary artery distribution, and penetrating chest trauma.4

We have operated on a patient with obstruction of the left outlet after SWITCH operation because of transposition of the great arteries. There was fibrous fusion under his new right and left coronary sinus, when in a normal child the new right coronary sinus should be a noncoronary sinus. During our dissection of the fibrous tissue, a small, 4-mm hole was made; the right atrium was seen through it. It was repaired using self pericardium, and the patient recovered well postoperatively. If the hole had not been found during the operation, a true aGD would have been found during follow-up.

How to Diagnosis Gerbode Defect

The report by Jebain and colleagues1 reminds us of a clinically useful perspective, the challenge in diagnosing aGD. As they describe, it took more than 3 years before the diagnosis of aGD was made in their patient. They thought the reason was owing to mischaracterization on transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) as severe tricuspid regurgitation—a diagnostic pitfall. They suggest we maintain high clinical suspicion and use advanced imaging modalities for accurate diagnosis. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), especially with 3-dimensional color Doppler and spectral Doppler imaging, played a key role in identifying the location and shape of the defect. The value of real-time 3-dimensional TEE lies in accurately showing intracardiac shunts, guiding percutaneous interventions, and offering superior anatomical detail and procedural guidance as compared to conventional imaging. Comprehensive TEE enabled accurate diagnosis and anatomical localization. High clinical suspicion in at-risk patients and understanding of key echocardiographic features can lead to prompt diagnosis and treatment. Cardiac computed tomography angiography (CTA) provides more detailed anatomic information,15 and it can confirm GD diagnosis in addition to TTE.

Congenital GD is often clinically apparent during childhood. But in older children and adults, the rib cage and lung tissues could obscure the proper visualization of the defect. Hence, one should resort to other imaging modalities such as 2-dimensional TEE, real-time 3-dimensional echocardiography, CTA,15 and cardiac magnetic resonance.2

TTE remains the diagnostic method of choice of DG, for unlike TEE, it does not cause uncomfortable reactions such as nausea and vomiting. When interpreting the Doppler signals, clinicians must not inaccurately identify these high-velocity signals as tricuspid regurgitation. When more accurate visualization of the defect is needed, TEE should be used. Careful Doppler interrogation and echocardiographic image acquisitions using multiple views and catheterization data help to differentiate DG from membranous VSD with tricuspid regurgitation, endocardial cushion defect, or ruptured sinus of Valsalva. High-velocity jet in the RA could be secondary to tricuspid regurgitation and severe pulmonary arterial hypertension, but the presence of 2 separate jets, RA dilatation, lack of right ventricle hypertrophy, and shunting predominantly in systole and normal pulmonary diastolic pressure indicate the presence of double LV to RA shunt; all of these signals indicate GD.

Management

The outcomes after management of GD are very good. There are 3 choices for treatment of GD. Chronic, asymptomatic, or small defects can be managed conservatively,9 with no more effects on the patient, but surgical treatment is an option with excellent outcomes, especially in smaller patients.8

A percutaneous approach to GD repair may be preferable to surgical repair for patients at high risk of surgical morbidity and mortality.5,11,14,15 Complete atrioventricular block may occur, and a pacemaker may be implanted later.14

Facing a GD patient, when surgical repair or device occlusion is difficult to choose, careful multidisciplinary team evaluation of the situation is essential.
