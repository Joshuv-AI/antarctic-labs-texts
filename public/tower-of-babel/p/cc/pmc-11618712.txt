Lamellipodia‐Mediated Osteoblast Haptotaxis Guided by Fibronectin Ligand Concentrations on a Multiplex Chip
Authors: Chao Liu, Xiaotian Feng, Seoyoung Jeong, Melissa L Carr, Yiwen Gao, Radhika P Atit, Samuel E Senyo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSkull morphogenesis is a complex, dynamic process involving two different germ layers and progressing to the coordinated, directional growth of individual bones. The mechanisms underlying directional growth toward the apex are not completely understood. Here, a microfluidic chip‐based approach is utilized to test whether calvarial osteoblast progenitors undergo haptotaxis on a gradient of Fibronectin1 (FN1) via lamellipodia. Mimicking the embryonic cranial mesenchyme's FN1 pattern, FN1 gradients is established in the chip using computer modeling and fluorescent labeling. Primary mouse calvarial osteoblast progenitors are plated in the chip along an array of segmented gradients of adsorbed FN1. The study performs single‐cell tracking and measures protrusive activity. Haptotaxis is observed at an intermediate FN1 concentration, with an average directional migration index (yFMI) of 0.07, showing a significant increase compared to the control average yFMI of ‐0.01. A significant increase in protrusive activity is observed during haptotaxis. Haptotaxis is an Arp2/3‐dependent, lamellipodia‐mediated process. Calvarial osteoblast progenitors treated with the Arp2/3 (Actin Related Protein 2/3 complex) inhibitor CK666 show significantly diminished haptotaxis, with an average yFMI of 0.01. Together, these results demonstrate haptotaxis on an FN1 gradient as a new mechanism in the apical expansion of calvarial osteoblast progenitors during development and shed light on the etiology of calvarial defects.Keywords: fibronectin gradient, haptotaxis, lamellipodia, microfluidic, skull morphogenesis

Introduction

The roof of the skull, or calvaria, is formed by a set of paired primordia that grow directionally from the base above the eye to the apex and become bone through intramembranous ossification.[ 1 , 2 ] While the osteochondral differentiation program of the calvaria is well‐studied, the mechanisms underlying of directional growth of the calvaria toward the apex are not completely understood. Genetically indelibly marked or DiI (lipophilic membrane stain) ‐labeled calvarial osteoblast progenitors in the basal position above the eye were later found throughout the calvaria, intermixed with unlabeled cells. The distribution pattern suggests that active cell movement may contribute to the expansion of the calvaria, by a mechanism to be determined.[ 3 , 4 ] Emerging studies suggest that dysregulation of calvarial growth, in medio‐lateral and baso‐apical directions, occurs in genetic mutants associated with craniosynostosis,[ 5 ] a common congenital disorder characterized by premature cranial suture fusion, cranial deformities, and elevated intracranial pressure.[ 6 ] Thus, demonstrating directional cell migration by calvarial osteoblast progenitors and defining the underlying mechanism will provide new insight in calvarial growth and elucidate the etiology of calvarial defects.

Cell migration plays a crucial role in numerous physiological processes, including embryonic morphogenesis, tissue repair and regeneration, and immune surveillance.[ 7 ] Directed cell migration is regulated by various stimuli, encompassing soluble factors inducing chemical gradients (chemotaxis), insoluble proteins creating substrate gradients (haptotaxis), and gradients of stiffness (durotaxis).[ 8 ] Haptotaxis, specifically, involves migration along a gradient of substrate‐bound chemoattractant that polarizes cellular adhesion to the apical front of the cell relative to rear basal side. Extracellular matrix (ECM) comprises insoluble molecules such as fibronectin, laminin, and various collagens that facilitate cell adhesion and migration.[ 9 ] The dynamic expression patterns of interstitial ECM components during fetal development are critical to directional cell migration for morphogenesis.[ 10 ] Gene expression profiling at mouse embryonic day at 11.5–12.5 shows differential expression of adhesion interactions and Fibronectin1 (FN1) in the apical cranial mesenchyme preceding calvarial bone expansion, suggesting the role of adhesion and migration.[ 11 , 12 ] Recent work by Feng et al. has highlighted FN1 as a critical interstitial graded ECM protein that may serve as a directional substrate for calvarial osteoblast migration. They found other interstitial ECM proteins including collagen, laminin, and elastin show low and uniform expression in the early stage of calvarial development (E12.5‐E13.5).[ 13 ] Osteoblasts progenitors exhibit migratory behavior both as single cells and as a collective in vitro.[ 14 ] Additionally, Sotobori et al. demonstrated that immobilized fibronectin or collagen 1 prompt osteoblast migration in vitro compared to laminin.[ 15 ] Together, there is emerging evidence to test our hypothesis that calvarial osteoblast progenitors expansion and growth is linked to ECM‐mediated cell movement by haptotaxis.

Mesenchymal cells are generally small and extend their cell bodies to detect local variations in external stimuli. Haptotaxis involves distinct steps, including pronounced membrane protrusion toward increasing concentrations of insoluble molecules in the ECM, stable adhesion to anchor the protrusion, cell contraction toward the anchoring point, and translocation with adhesion release at the rear. Molecular machinery at the membrane and cytoskeleton orchestrates each step.[ 16 ] Lamellipodia at the leading edge extends dynamic membrane protrusions enriched with actin filaments, both in broad lamellipodia and spike‐like filopodia.[ 17 ] Actin filament polymerization is regulated by the Arp2/3 complex to form branched actin filaments, resulting in cell protrusion. Membrane‐bound integrin receptors bind to ECM proteins like fibronectin forming nascent adhesions.[ 18 ] A central signaling node, including Rho, matures focal adhesions that serve as traction points.[ 8 ] In the final step, adhesion release at the rear of the cells involves Rho and its effector, Rho kinase (ROCK), promoting tail retraction by altering integrin affinity.[ 19 , 20 ] The coordinated actions of lamellipodia through Rho/ROCK signaling pathways and Arp2/3 actin filament aggregation collectively drive directional cell migration in response to gradients of ECM components such as fibronectin.[ 21 , 22 ] Notably, these mechanisms have not been considered in cell‐autonomous calvarial osteoblast movement during skull morphogenesis.

There are inherent challenges in conducting in vivo cell migration studies, which can be circumvented with microfluidic‐based systems in vitro. The generation of substrate‐bounded ligand gradients in vitro using microfluidics has been extensively reviewed and compared with other approaches.[ 23 ] The utilization of microfluidic devices for tracking cell migration has garnered attention due to its advantages, including live microscopy, minimal liquid volumes, suppression of turbulent flow, surface functionalization, and repeatability of experimental conditions. However, current haptotaxis chip designs generally require iterative experiments to evaluate cell migration, often with limited control over the gradient profile. There is a need to develop a high‐throughput strategy combined with the benefits of microfluidic chips to assess haptotaxis across a wide range of ligand patterns (e.g., concentration, gradation, multiple ligands) that mimic the in vivo niche for studying haptotaxis. Our chip features an automated control system for precise fluid flow regulation via valve operation, enabling accurate spatial and temporal cell positioning along gradients and multiplexed culture conditions. Here, through the integration of mathematical modeling, we demonstrate the capacity to evaluate haptotaxis across diverse ligand patterns (e.g., concentration, gradation) that simulate the in vivo niche, providing insights into skull morphogenesis.

In this study, we aimed to investigate whether calvarial osteoblast progenitors exhibit lamellipodia‐dependent directional migration up a gradient of increasing FN1 concentration. We employed a multiplex microfluidic chip to generate an array of different FN1 gradients, termed a haptotaxis array. By challenging the system with small molecule inhibitors, we observed Arp2/3‐dependent directional migration by calvarial osteoblast progenitors on a defined range of fibronectin gradients, accompanied by an increase in protrusive activity. In regions with high FN1 concentrations, cells increased in size with diminished speed and polarity, whereas at lower concentrations, cells exhibited motility with random orientation. Through the multiplex microfluidic chip approach, we provide evidence that calvarial osteoblast progenitors undergo directional migration by haptotaxis on fibronectin, representing a new paradigm for apical expansion during calvarial growth.

Results

The Design of the Fibronectin1 Gradient in the Chip Matches the Histology

Recent studies have shown graded expression of FN1 protein in the mouse cranial mesenchyme preceding apical expansion of calvarial osteoblast progenitors and FN1 protein expression is dysregulated in calvarial defects.[ 13 , 24 , 25 ] This observation suggested that the FN1 gradient could potentially function as a directional cue, guiding the migration of calvarial osteoblast progenitors, by a mechanism yet to be determined. To test the role of graded FN1 in directional migration by calvarial osteoblast progenitors we first sought to recreate a similar gradient in vitro. FN1 protein expression in mouse E13.5 cranial mesenchyme was visualized by indirect immunofluorescence and quantified using fluorescence intensity line graph scans (Figure 1A). We found a remarkable two to threefold change in intensity was revealed, spanning from the basal to the apical direction of the skull (Figure 1B). To test the role of graded FN1 in directional movement of calvarial osteoblast progenitors, we employed a multiplex microfluidic chip previously developed in our laboratory.[ 26 ] Briefly, the two‐layer PDMS microfluidic chip consists of 32 individually addressable sets of cell culture units, each comprising four identical cell culture chambers and one waste channel (Figure 1C). The fibronectin source and media resupply were achieved through PDMS control valves, operated by a custom microfluidic control system and computer user interface, as previously described.[ 26 , 27 ] For this study, three cell chambers were designated as migration chambers, while one served as the source chamber to establish a diffusion‐based fibronectin haptotactic gradient. Utilizing this multiplex microfluidic chip, we systematically generate arrays of gradients with precise control over ligand deposition, with the initial pre‐selection facilitated through MATLAB.

MATLAB Model Simulation for Fibronectin Diffusion Gradient and Validation

The generation and validation of fibronectin gradients were conducted combining mathematical modeling and experimental verification to select an appropriate gradient slope and concentration. A diffusion‐based chemotactic gradient in flow‐free microfluidic chambers has been previously described.[ 28 , 29 ] Previous studies of fibronectin mediated haptotaxis have utilized concentrations ranging from 100 to 500 µg mL−1 over 200–1000 µm distance.[ 30 , 31 ] In this study, we employed a similar approach to generate a gradient to evaluate haptotaxis in a multiplex array, allowing for independent control over gradient steepness, concentration, and duration. Concentrated fibronectin (100 µg mL−1 shown as an example) was introduced into the source chamber, after which the gate between chambers was opened to facilitate the diffusion of fibronectin into adjacent chambers, and this cyclic process is illustrated in Figure 2A. To assess and determine suitable methods for gradient generation, a MATLAB model was employed, which takes into account parameters such as cell chamber geometry, fibronectin diffusion coefficient, initial fibronectin concentration, and resupply frequency. The model outputs a dynamic representation of fibronectin concentration over time and position within the chambers, presented as either a 2D map (x‐y coordinates at set time points) or a 3D map (concentration‐time‐y coordinates), with color indicating concentration changes (Figure S1A,B, Supporting Information). Along the y‐axis, the fibronectin concentration gradient profile was plotted to observe changes over time (Figure 2B). As time elapsed, fibronectin gradually diffused into neighboring chambers, resulting in a decreasing slope, while subsequent chambers experienced an increase in overall fibronectin levels (Figure 2B). Based on the locations along the gradient, we designated three migration chambers as upper, middle (mid), and lower gradient chambers, with the upper gradient chamber at the highest concentration closest to the source chamber (refer to Figure 2B). The corresponding locations of the fibronectin gradient chambers in the line profile are highlighted with gray shading and labeled below the graph. We tested various parameters affecting the fibronectin concentration profile, including initial concentrations and resupply frequencies to the source chamber (Figure S1C,D, Supporting Information). The simulations revealed that the initial source concentration had an impact on the overall fibronectin gradient profile, whereas alterations in resupply time (ranging from 30 to 120 min) had negligible effects on the profile. We decided to replenish fibronectin every 30 min to sustain a consistently high concentration in the source chamber.

To validate the MATLAB fibronectin diffusion model, we conducted experiments using FITC‐dextran with a molecular weight similar to fibronectin (FITC‐dextran: 500 kDa; fibronectin: 440–530 kDa).[ 32 ] Different FITC‐dextran concentrations (1, 10, 100, and 1000 µg mL−1) were tested for diffusion, and fluorescent timelapse images were recorded to analyze the fluorescent gradient profile (Figure S1E, Supporting Information). The FITC‐dextran concentrations of 1 and 10 µg mL−1 were not detectable in fluorescent imaging, and consequently, were excluded from consideration for future experiments. The 100 µg mL−1 FITC‐dextran diffusion profile at 24 h were only visible until the edge of upper chamber, while the lower and mid chambers were below limit of detection (Figure S1E, Supporting Information). For visual demonstration, we used the time‐lapse images of 1000 µg mL−1 FITC‐dextran diffusion over a period of 48 h to depict gradation of dextran fluorescent intensity (Figure 2C). The intensity changes over time were assessed by averaging line profiles from eight technical replicates across the y‐axis, spanning the entire chamber region with gray shading to distinguish individual chambers (lower, mid, and upper) (Figure 2D). We observed a consistent trend in the first five timepoints (0–8 h) in the fluorescent line profiles, aligning with the predictions of the fibronectin diffusion MATLAB model. However, the fluorescent intensity did not notably increase in the lower gradient chamber (Figure 2E). We attribute this discrepancy to the limitations of the fluorescent camera, as concentrations of 1 and 10 µg mL−1 were not distinguished from the background. Additionally, the fluorescent line profiles at different concentrations closely matched the model predictions (Figure S1F, Supporting Information), further confirming the accuracy of the MATLAB fibronectin diffusion model. The combination of this model with FITC‐dextran validation allows us to select the concentration diffusion model most likely to closely align with histological observations.

Fibronectin Gradient Formation at Different Starting Concentrations

The MATLAB model served as a valuable tool for pre‐screening parameters and conditions. Subsequently, we aimed to assess fibronectin diffusion and gradient formation using both unlabeled and FITC‐labeled human fibronectin, while also examining fibronectin adherence to the plastic surface of the microfluidic chip. Based on the literature and our modeling, we started with two concentrations (100 and 500 µg mL−1) for gradient generation in our study. We utilized 500 µg mL−1 FITC‐fibronectin for real‐time fluorescent imaging (Figure 3A). At the 24‐hour timepoint, FITC‐fibronectin gradient formation was observed in all diffusion chambers, as depicted by the averaged line profile with gray shading indicating chamber locations (Figure 3B). Additionally, we evaluated 100 µg mL−1 FITC‐fibronectin diffusion and a blank control (Figure S2A, Supporting Information). Notably, the concentration of FITC‐fibronectin reached the fluorescent detection limit at the mid‐gradient chamber with an initial concentration of 100 µg mL−1 (Figure S2B, Supporting Information). To assess fibronectin gradient adsorption to the surface, chambers were washed with 1x PBS and imaged for FITC fluorescence (Figure S2C, Supporting Information). The averaged fluorescent intensity line profile confirmed gradient formation from the upper to the lower gradient chamber in the 500 µg mL−1 concentration group. Furthermore, fibronectin gradients were visualized through protein staining using Coomassie blue staining (Figure S2E, Supporting Information). Both averaged fluorescent and monochromatic intensity line profiles demonstrated gradient formation (Figure S2B,F, Supporting Information), offering comprehensive insights into gradient formation under varying times and concentrations, each exhibiting distinct slopes.

Validation of gradient formation through FITC‐fibronectin diffusion. A) Fluorescent images of 500 µg mL−1 FITC‐fibronectin diffusion at 24 h compared with a blank control. Images were color‐coded as previously described. B) The line profiles of averaged fluorescent intensity (red: 500 µg mL−1 FITC‐fibronectin; black: Blank control) across the y‐axis were generated for the lower, mid, and upper gradient chambers (shaded in gray), derived from multiple line profiles and utilizing eight technical replicates.

To evaluate cell migration behavior across fibronectin concentrations (100 and 500 µg mL−1), we conducted a pilot experiment with calvarial pre‐osteoblasts. Notably, compared to a blank uncoated surface, we observed a significant reduction in cell migration velocity approaching being stationary across the upper and mid FN1 gradient chambers at 500 µg mL−1 (Data not shown). Consequently, we proceeded with the 100 µg mL−1 concentration. To validate fibronectin adsorption and enhance fluorescent signal, we used antibody staining (Figure S3A, Supporting Information). We observed a fibronectin gradient through immunostaining in both the upper and mid chambers of the fibronectin gradient with a low exposure time of 200 milliseconds. Increasing the exposure time to seconds revealed the presence of the fibronectin in the lower chamber distinct from the blank control (Figure S3C,D, Supporting Information), while the mid and upper chambers reached fluorescent signal saturation at this exposure. These findings confirm the establishment of fibronectin gradients in the upper, mid, and lower chambers by varying source concentration. Additionally, we compared the gradient slope between the mouse embryo cranial region and the antibody‐stained FN1 gradient in the chip by normalizing the minimum and maximum fluorescent intensities to a range of 0–1. (Figure S4A,B, Supporting Information). Subsequently, the slope at distinct ranges (lower, mid, and upper) was calculated. The slopes of the mid gradient (embryo: 1.0916; chip: 1.0807) and the upper gradient (embryo: 0.3537; chip: 0.3278) for FN1 are comparable between the embryo and chip. Based on the validated MATLAB model and the fibronectin pattern resembling histology, we decided to conduct the cell migration experiment using a fibronectin gradient generated from a 100 µg mL−1 source concentration.

Directional Migration of Calvarial Osteoblast Progenitors Occurs Specifically within the Intermediate Fibronectin Concentration Range

To evaluate the cell response to the fibronectin gradient, we isolated and expanded primary calvarial pre‐osteoblasts from E14.5 mouse embryos in vitro.[ 33 ] Cells in passages 2–3 were sparsely and uniformly seeded (≈15–25 cells per chamber) in individual chambers of the generated fibronectin gradient, along with uniform fibronectin and blank control groups. All cells were allowed to attach and were provided low‐serum media to prevent excessive proliferation. Time‐lapse imaging was conducted at 20‐minute intervals over 18–24 h. Tracking of individual cells within these chambers was conducted using FIJI/ImageJ plugin Manual Tracking.[ 34 ] An illustrative example of a tracking path overlaid on the time‐lapse video is presented in Video S1 (Supporting Information). Clustered, indistinguishable, or stationary cells and those in contact with the chamber wall were excluded from analysis due to potential tactile interference, known as contact inhibition. All other cells were tracked, and their trajectories were aggregated from common starting points and plotted to visualize migration patterns in a tracking plot. To visually represent the preferred direction of cell movement, rose plots were utilized to identify predominant migration patterns and directionalities (Figure S5, Supporting Information). We observed an increased migration of cells toward the increasing concentration direction (upward) in the mid FN1 gradient group (Figure 4A,C). The rose plot illustrates the directional migration of cells within the mid FN1 gradient group compared to other conditions, suggesting that calvarial osteoblast progenitors detect the fibronectin gradient in this concentration range and migrate toward regions with higher fibronectin concentrations (Figure 4A). Additionally, we conducted a morphological analysis using phalloidin staining to label actin filaments at the conclusion of the experiment (Figure 4B). We measured cell area across different experimental groups. Interestingly, the lower and mid FN1 gradient groups displayed smaller cell areas, resembling the cell areas observed in the blank control and uniform fibronectin groups (Figure 4B,D). Conversely, cells within upper FN1 gradient chamber exhibited enlarged sizes.

The tracking data were analyzed to assess the forward migration of cells (yFMI) in the gradient direction (Figure 5A; Figure S6A, Supporting Information), comparing segmented concentration gradients across three chambers within the fibronectin gradient groups and control groups. The mean values and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals are provided in Table S1 (Supporting Information). Analysis of the yFMI plot indicated haptotaxis in cells within the mid FN1 gradient (mean yFMI = 0.07, 95% CI [0.04, 0.10]), with directed movement toward higher concentration regions. Conversely, cells in the blank control (mean yFMI = −0.01, 95% CI [−0.04, 0.03]) and uniform fibronectin (mean yFMI = −0.03, 95% CI [−0.06, 0.01]) groups exhibited random motion without discernible trends. Cells exhibit a significant increase in forward migration (yFMI) toward higher concentrations in the mid FN1 gradient chamber compared to the blank control groups. However, when comparing across segmented fibronectin chambers, results showed higher yFMI values for the mid FN1 gradient chamber compared to lower (mean yFMI = 0.02, 95% CI [−0.01, 0.06]) and upper FN1 gradient chambers (mean yFMI = −0.03, 95% CI [−0.09, 0.02]) (Figure 5A; Figure S6A, Supporting Information). Additionally, analysis of average cell velocity demonstrated a significant reduction in migration speed in the upper FN1 gradient chamber, rendering cells less responsive to the gradient. Evaluation of Euclidean distance revealed that the upper FN1 gradient group exhibited significantly lower movement compared to other groups. Minimal differences were observed in cell velocity and Euclidean distance measurements when comparing across mid FN1 gradient and control groups (Figure 5B,C; Figure S6B,C, Supporting Information). In summary, assessment of distinct regions within the segmented 100 µg mL−1 fibronectin gradient in comparison with control groups highlighted clear haptotaxis behavior in chamber B, positioned at the gradient midpoint (Figure 5A).

To evaluate changes in structure and shape in response to fibronectin gradient stimuli, we performed a morphological analysis. In the presence of a mid FN1 gradient, we assessed cell and nuclei morphology, and quantified the number of protrusions. At the 9‐hour mark in the microfluidic chip experiment, the cell nuclei length‐to‐width (L/W) ratio remained comparable across the blank control, fibronectin gradient, and uniform fibronectin coating conditions (data not shown). However, only the mid FN1 gradient group showed a significantly greater number of protrusions compared to the blank control group (Figure 5D).

Inhibition of Arp2/3 Abolishes Fibronectin‐Induced Haptotaxis

To assess the molecular mechanisms underlying haptotaxis, we tested small molecule inhibitors that are key components of cell migration, including the Rho signaling ROCK inhibitor, Y27632 and Arp2/3‐dependent lamellipodia protrusions inhibitor, CK666. In scratch assays, we sought to determine effective inhibitor concentrations for inducing alterations in cell migration. The scratch assay examines the phenomenon of collective cell migration, defined by cells maintaining robust cell‐cell contacts and exerting forces on one another. We determined the testing range of CK666 and selected Y27632 concentrations based on published literature.[ 30 , 35 , 36 , 37 ] In a dose responsive curve of CK666 (20‐200 µm), we identified that CK666 at concentrations of 100 µm (or 200 µm) resulted in a reduction in cell migration velocity, whereas the ROCK inhibitor Y27632 at 50 µm increased migration velocity compared to the control group (Figure S5, Supporting Information). Next, we employed the microfluidic chip system to investigate the impact of small molecule inhibitors, CK666 and Y27632, on the individual cell's capacity to sense and respond to fibronectin gradients.

In the chip experiment, all units were coated with a 100 µg mL−1 fibronectin‐generated diffusion gradient for a 24‐hour period. E13.5 calvarial osteoblast progenitors were then seeded uniformly at a low density into individual chambers (≈15–25 cells per chamber) and allowed to adhere for 1 h. Subsequently, the effective concentrations determined from the scratch assay were applied in the media treatment. Four treatment groups were evaluated in parallel, which included a no‐treatment control, CK666 at low (20 µm) and high (100 µm) concentrations, and Y27632 at a concentration of 50 µm, each with four technical replicates. Control and treatment media were perfused into the cell chambers at 30‐minute intervals. All analyses were conducted specifically for the mid FN1 gradient chamber in the context of small molecule inhibitor experiments (Figure 6B). The same preprocessing steps and cell tracking techniques were employed to quantify changes in cell migration behavior.

The tracking plot and rose plot confirmed the persistence of haptotaxis behavior in the control mid‐FN1 gradient group without treatment, whereas cell cultures exposed to 100 µm CK666 and 50 µm Y27632 exhibited random cell migration patterns (Figure 6A). The percentage of cells up the FN1 gradient was quantified (Figure 6C), revealing a slight decrease upon treatment with 100 µm CK666 and 50 µm Y27632. Furthermore, the yFMI estimation plot revealed a significant reduction in yFMI for cells treated with 100 µm CK666 (mean yFMI = 0.01, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.05]) compared to the mid FN1 gradient (mean yFMI = 0.09, 95% CI [0.04, 0.13]) group; however, CK666 at 20 µm (mean yFMI = 0.05, 95% CI [0.01, 0.09]) was not significantly different from mid FN1 gradient control in yFMI (Figure 7A; Figure S6D, Table S2, Supporting Information). A comparison of yFMI values between the mid FN1 gradient control and Y27632‐treated groups (mean yFMI = 0.02, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.06]) on the mid FN1 gradient revealed a decreasing trend, accompanied by a significant reduction in velocity. Interestingly, while the small molecule CK666 did not appear to affect cell velocity significantly, Y27632 induced a substantial reduction in cell migration velocity, indicating limited mobility compared to other experimental groups (Figure 7B; Figure S6E, Supporting Information). Furthermore, analysis of the Euclidean distance plot demonstrated that increasing CK666 concentration led to a decrease in Euclidean distance, while velocity remained relatively constant (Figure 7C; Figure S6F, Supporting Information). This observation suggests that higher doses of CK666 resulted in cells losing haptotaxis and migrating non‐directionally in the presence of a fibronectin gradient.

We then analyzed the morphological changes across these groups. Compared to the cells on the mid FN1 gradient control, the number of protrusions was significantly lower in the presence of 20 and 100 µm CK666 inhibitor‐treated cells (Figure 7D). In line with findings in fibroblasts, we demonstrate that calvarial osteoblast progenitors on the fibronectin gradient exhibit Arp2/3 dependent protrusive activity, which is required for haptotaxis.[ 30 ] Additionally, quantification of cell area in phalloidin staining images reveals a significantly larger cell area in cells treated with 50 µm Y27632 (Figure 6D). Together, our results demonstrate that embryonic calvarial osteoblast progenitors use Arp2/3 lamellipodia dependent protrusions for haptotaxis on a mid FN1 gradient.

Discussion

Understanding directional cell migration in calvarial osteoblast progenitors is crucial for studying calvarial growth and elucidating the etiology of calvarial disorders. Recent studies have suggested that the distribution of extracellular matrix (ECM) protein deposition, particularly Fibronectin1 (FN1), serves as a directional guide for the migration of calvarial osteoblast progenitors.[ 13 , 15 ] Our investigation aims to determine whether calvarial osteoblast progenitors exhibit directional migration along a FN1 gradient indicative of haptotaxis using a microfluidic chip setup. By creating FN1 gradients resembling those found in embryonic cranial mesenchyme during calvaria expansion, we observed haptotaxis in mid FN1 concentrations. This migration is dependent on Arp2/3 and involves lamellipodia activity, as evidenced by increased protrusive activity. Inhibiting Arp2/3 resulted in diminished haptotaxis and reduced protrusive activity. These findings suggest haptotaxis as a novel mechanism for calvarial osteoblast migration and provide insights into the development of calvarial defects.

The directional cell movement in developmental contexts occur on graded FN1 substrate by haptotaxis (adhesion), durotaxis (stiffness), and in combination with chemotaxis.[ 38 , 39 , 40 ] During organ development, various types of cells, such as fibroblasts and vascular smooth muscle cells, directionally migrate toward the stiffer regions of polyacrylamide hydrogels coated with fibronectin in vitro.[ 41 , 42 ] King et al. suggested that the fibroblasts use lamellipodia, which are the Arp2/3 dependent cell protrusions, to move along the FN1 gradient by haptotaxis in vitro.[ 30 ] Furthermore, the necessity of the Cdc 42 and WASL/N‐WASP an upstream and downstream regulator of Arp2/3, respectively are required for efficient apical expansion of the calvarial bones in vivo, indicating that the lamellipodium‐mediated cell movement plays an essential role in calvarial morphogenesis.[ 13 , 43 , 44 ] While haptotaxis has been validated in other microfluidic platforms,[ 28 , 30 , 45 ] assessing additional factors influencing morphogenesis presents challenges. Our chip offers precise control over temporal and spatial variations in ECM patterns and enables multiplexed culture conditions. This capability sets the stage for future investigations into various aspects, including the integration of soluble ligands like Wnt, co‐culturing with haptotaxis, application of mechanical stimuli to cells, and exposure to small molecules. In addition to ECM‐based haptotaxis movements, our previous work has identified two distinct morphogenetic cell movements in vivo, along with associated cellular behaviors, as new players to calvarial bone morphogenesis between E10.5‐12.5 in mouse embryos. These processes are dependent on cytoskeletal dynamics and non‐canonical Wnt signaling.[ 46 ] The transition from basal cell rearrangements to polarized lamellipodia‐dependent directional movements apically demonstrates how different morphogenetic movements can influence tissue shape, with different intercellular strategies being employed in vivo.[ 46 ] We deduce that the cellular movement mechanisms regulating calvarial expansion evolve both temporally and spatially during calvarial development in vivo.

The emergence of motile cell shape and migration velocity is governed by the interaction between ECM ligands and cellular components such as the adhesion receptor integrin, actin network, non‐muscle myosin, and the cell membrane.[ 47 ] Cell‐substrate adhesion strength exhibits a diphasic effect on cellular motility: cell speed increases at intermediate adhesion strengths but decreases at both low and high adhesion strengths.[ 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 ] Our findings reveal a significant decrease in migration velocity and Euclidean distance within upper FN1 gradients compared to mid FN1 gradients. This distance reduction is accompanied by an increase in cell area, suggesting that calvarial osteoblast integrins are saturated with abundant FN1 ligands, resulting in high adhesion strengths. The enlarged cell area likely results from adhesion anchor points across the cell body, which contribute to the observed decrease in cell velocity within the upper FN1 gradient chamber. In contrast, calvarial osteoblast progenitors residing in mid FN1 gradient chambers experienced intermediate adhesion strength. The orientation of spindle‐shaped cells enables varying levels of adhesion strength along their cell bodies, contributing to migration toward regions of higher FN1 concentration without compromising speed, consistent with haptotaxis. Fibronectin is recognized to exhibit mechanosensitive changes to tertiary structure. Stiffness mediated elongation of fibronectin reveals cryptic binding sites that target multiple distinct growth factors and cytokines.[ 52 ] Future work will evaluate how the interplay of tissue stiffness and the conformational state of fibronectin impact cell behavior and overall skull morphogenesis.

Cell migration, even guided by insoluble ligand gradients on substrates as in haptotaxis, generally exhibits non‐linear trajectories, reflecting the intricate interplay between cellular adhesion dynamics and substrate‐bound molecular cues.[ 48 ] To properly quantify and compare across experimental conditions, the forward migration index (FMI) was employed to assess the efficiency of cell forward movement. Distinct cell types exhibit varied FMI ranges and motility in response to stimulus gradients. For example, immune cells like T cells, neutrophils, and dendritic cells typically display elevated FMI values (0.2–0.4) and migration speeds of 2–7.2 µm min−1.[ 53 ] Conversely, mesenchymal stem cells generally exhibit lower FMI values (0.08–0.11) and migrate at slower speeds (0.26‐0.32 µm min−1).[ 54 ] Mouse embryonic primary calvarial osteoblast progenitors in our study demonstrate a cell velocity of ≈0.35 µm min−1 and a yFMI of 0.07‐0.09 on the mid FN1 gradient, consistent with the reported mesenchymal stem cell range in the literature. Our findings unveiled a distinct directional cell migration exclusively within the mid FN1 gradient range. Notably, while cell migration speed and Euclidean distance showed no significant differences in the mid FN1 gradient compared to controls, the yFMI significantly increased, indicating movement toward the FN1 gradient direction. Furthermore, cells in the mid FN1 gradient exhibited an augmented number of protrusions compared to controls, serving as a morphological indication of the cells actively sensing and responding to the FN1 substrate gradient. Collectively, our data strongly support haptotaxis in the mid FN1 gradient without altering migration speed. Similar to other published studies,[ 30 ] we investigated cell migration at low density to minimize the effects of cellular interactions and paracrine signaling, focusing solely on the impact of fibronectin gradients. Future research evaluating the complexity of skull morphogenesis will consider the role of paracrine signaling and hence cell density in these processes.

The importance of Arp2/3 in haptotaxis is well‐documented, with previous studies demonstrating that depleting the Arp2/3 complex and subsequent loss of lamellipodia in fibroblasts completely abolishes haptotaxis in fibronectin, laminin, or vitronectin gradients.[ 22 , 30 ] Our study corroborates these findings, revealing that calvarial osteoblast progenitors exhibit haptotaxis migration in response to fibronectin gradients sensed by Arp2/3 dependent protrusive activity. One intriguing aspect of our findings lies in the differential responses observed with Arp2/3 complex and Rho kinase inhibition. Inhibition of the Arp2/3 complex led to a decrease in yFMI without affecting cell migration velocity, underscoring the importance of Arp2/3 in maintaining directional migration. Furthermore, morphological analysis indicated a reduction in protrusion numbers with CK666 treatment. Conversely, inhibition of Rho kinase resulted in a significant decrease in cell migration velocity alongside a reduction in yFMI and increased cell area. However, the inability to assess the role of Rho kinase in gradient sensing due to inhibited movement presents an interesting avenue for future exploration. The scratch assay was employed to determine effective concentrations of small molecules by analyzing migration velocity as a surrogate index of bioactivity. The ultimate goal of these drugs was to disrupt cell machinery to investigate the regulation of directed cell migration. For the Arp2/3 inhibitor, maintaining cell speed in the chip studies was advantageous to evaluate the role of cytoskeletal membrane protrusions in directionality independent of motility. Interestingly, we observed discrepancies between the scratch assay and the chip studies, particularly with Rho kinase inhibition. The two models of migration are quite distinct. In the scratch assay, collective cell migration is influenced by constrained spacing for migration, dense cellular interactions, paracrine signaling, and shared mechanical forces. This study was conducted on a blank substrate without ligand coating. Conversely, in the low‐density chip studies, single cell migration relies on individual cell‐matrix interactions and independent cytoskeletal dynamics.[ 55 ] In this study, the Rho kinase impairment of motility did not impact the overall interpretation of Arp2/3 activity. Future studies will consider Rho kinase regulation with a wider range of concentrations and alternative inhibitory tools.

During mouse embryonic calvarial expansion in vivo, the graded distribution of FN1 protein expression undergoes spatiotemporal and dynamic changes.[ 13 ] The graded expression of FN1 in Figure 1A depicts a snapshot of FN1 expression development in one cross‐section of 3D space. To deepen our understanding of directional calvarial progenitor expansion in development that is guided by haptotactic cues, future studies could utilize this platform to simulate fibronectin distribution at various developmental stages along with other ligands. Using a MATLAB model, we simulated diffusion‐based FN1 gradient generation, which significantly reduced the number of experimental groups needed for empirical testing. While the MATLAB model effectively refined experimental conditions, future improvements could integrate fibronectin adsorption kinetics using experimental data. We designed the chip parameters to achieve a gradation of FN1 comparable to that observed in histological samples from primary tissue. We demonstrate the gradation of FN1 across and within cell culture chambers of the chip. The selected parameters that closely matched the skull pattern were found in the mid and upper FN1 gradients with an initial concentration of 100 µg mL−1. However, detecting subtle changes in the lower FN1 gradient poses a challenge due to the limited sensitivity of immunostaining approaches. Similarly, the visibility of FITC‐fibronectin approached the detection limit at the selected initial concentration of 100 µg mL−1, while a more visible 500 µg mL−1 concentration caused cells to remain stationary across most of the FN1 gradient range, particularly in the mid and upper regions. Considering an intermediate fibronectin concentration would enhance gradient visibility while shifting the haptotaxis range toward the edge of the chip. Such a modification would result in the loss of valuable information regarding cellular behavior below the haptotaxis gradient range. The selected parameters optimized fibronectin gain and loss as evaluated in genetic animal studies[ 13 ] and can be adjusted in future studies. Enhancing the detection limit is crucial for evaluating physiologically relevant levels of fibronectin during early development and for modeling this patterning in the chip. Future studies would aim to improve the limit of detection and quantify fibronectin deposition using surface analysis approaches such as mass spectrometry techniques ToF‐SIMS.[ 56 , 57 ]

Moving forward, it is essential to refine our comprehension of the complex cellular processes that underlie haptotaxis and its implications in physiological phenomena such as skull morphogenesis. Despite the absence of collagen gradation in embryonic cranial mesenchyme before E13.5, the phenomenon of haptotaxis may be influenced by a more complex combination of ECM substrates.[ 31 ] Addition investigation of mixed ECM molecules with uniform collagen/laminin and graded fibronectin, may inform additional characteristics of haptotaxis for this specific population of cells. Further exploration of additional mechano‐transduction pathways, particularly those involving integrins, focal adhesion molecules, YAP/TAZ signaling, and Piezo1/2 factors, hold the potential to yield deeper insights into the mechanistic fundamentals of gradient sensing and directional cell migration. Additionally, investigating the differentiation and mineral deposition processes from calvarial osteoblast progenitors following haptotaxis is crucial for elucidating the connection between migration and bone formation. In humans and mice, heterozygosity at TWIST1[ 58 ] and EPHRINA4 loci is associated with reduced calvarial growth and craniosynostosis.[ 59 ] Twist1, EphA4, and Twist1/EphA4 compound mouse mutants reveal a loss of segregation between osteogenic and non‐osteogenic cells at the coronal suture boundary, likely due to inappropriate migration of osteogenic cells within the coronal suture.[ 60 ] The roles of the extracellular matrix, cell movement, and directional cell behaviors in congenital calvarial bone defects, such as craniosynostosis, and in craniofacial bone diseases, such as craniometaphyseal dysplasia and craniodiaphyseal dysplasia, remain to be elucidated. Future studies will explore the interplay between intracellular regulation of migration and extracellular fibronectin‐dependent regulation of haptotaxis. EphA4 signaling has been shown to regulate beta1‐integrin signaling via Cas and FAK pathways. Clarifying these signaling pathways in craniosynostosis patients has the potential to define new molecular targets for therapy.[ 61 ] Through the integration of microfluidic platforms and computational modeling, forthcoming research can assess cell haptotaxis across various stages of craniofacial development and disorders like craniosynostosis, thereby laying the groundwork for innovative therapeutic approaches targeting cell migration in both developmental and pathological contexts.

Conclusion

The microfluidic platform employed in this study facilitates high‐throughput screening of fibronectin ligand gradients, guiding primary calvarial osteoblast haptotaxis. The validated MATLAB model accurately simulates fibronectin diffusion in microfluidic chambers. Our findings present evidence that osteoblast progenitors exhibit hapatotactic migration within a specific fibronectin gradient range, with higher concentrations impeding cell migration velocity. Compared to uniform fibronectin and blank control conditions, hapatotactic osteoblast progenitors demonstrate an increased protrusion number. Our study elucidates that the calvarial osteoblast haptotaxis mechanism is propelled by Arp2/3‐dependent lamellipodia. Treatment with the Arp2/3 inhibitor significantly reduces hapatotactic migration and protrusions while maintaining migration speed. The haptotaxis of calvarial osteoblast progenitors on a fibronectin gradient unveils a novel mechanism contributing to apical expansion in skull bone morphogenesis and provides insights into the etiology of calvarial defects.

Experimental Section

Mouse Lines, Husbandry, and Calvarial Osteoblast Dissociation

Wildtype mice on a mixed genetic background were used to obtain embryos for calvarial osteoblast culture. The vaginal plug day was assigned as embryonic day 0.5 (E0.5). Embryos were collected at E13.5 for immunohistochemistry and E14.5 for calvarial osteoblast progenitor culture. Case Western Reserve University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approved all animal procedures in accordance with AVMA guidelines (Protocol 2013–0156, Animal Welfare Assurance No. A3145–01).

Immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence

E13.5 wild‐type embryo heads from mixed genetic backgrounds were collected in cold 1X Phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C for 35–50 min, sucrose dehydrated, embedded in O.C.T Compound (Tissue‐Tek Sakura, Sakura Finetek USA, Torrance, CA, USA) and cryopreserved at −80 °C.[ 33 ] The embryos were cryosectioned at 14 µm in the coronal plane. Immunofluorescence (IF) on cryosections was performed by drying slides at room temperature, washing in 1X PBS, and blocking in 5–10% donkey or goat serum. Sections were incubated with rabbit anti‐Fibronectin primary antibodies (1: 250; Abcam ab2413; RRID: AB_2262874) overnight at 4 °C, washed in 1X PBS, incubated with species‐specific secondary antibody (1: 800; Invitrogen; A‐11012) for 1 hour at room temperature, washed with DAPI (0.5 µg mL−1), and then mounted with Fluoroshield (Sigma F6182). Sections were imaged with the upright Olympus BX60 microscope with 10X objective (Olympus UPIanFI 4/0.13) using CellSens Entry Software. Images were processed using Adobe Photoshop or Affinity Designer.

Fibronectin Line Profile Analysis in Immunohistochemically Stained E13.5 Mouse Skull

The immunofluorescent image of Fibronectin 1 in the E13.5 wild‐type embryo head was analyzed using Fiji/ImageJ software (version 2.14.0/1.54f). Initially, a Gaussian average filter with a sigma (radius) of 20 µm was applied to the 16 bits gray scale image to reduce shot noise and random fluctuations in intensity. Subsequently, four region‐of‐interest (ROI) lines were drawn spanning from the basal to apical side of the skull. The resulting ROI line profiles were exported to CSV files. These CSV files were then imported into a custom MATLAB script (Supplementary File 2) to obtain the averaged fluorescent intensity profile of Fibronectin1 across the skull. The calculation of standard deviation was incorporated into the plot as a shaded area around the averaged intensity profile.

Cell Culture of Calvarial Osteoblast Progenitors

Embryos were collected at E14.5 and calvaria was manually isolated away from ectoderm and brain tissues as previously described.[ 2 , 33 ] The isolated tissue was enzymatically digested in 0.25% Trypsin (Thermofisher, 25200056) for 5 min at 37 °C followed by micropipetting to release the calvarial osteoblast progenitors. The dissociated primary calvarial osteoblast progenitors were plated in DMEM (Thermofisher, 11995065), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma‐Millipore), and Anti‐Anti (Thermofisher, 15240062) in 12‐well tissue culture plates. The cells were incubated in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. The cells were passaged 1–3 times with 0.05% Trypsin‐EDTA (Thermofisher, 25300054) in 6‐well plates when they achieved ≈80–95% confluence. The media was changed every other day. Experiments were repeated using 3–4 biological replicates. RNA was collected from P1 and P2 cells and validated for calvarial osteoblast progenitors identity with Runx2 and Msx2 expression and compared to endogenous E13.5 calvaria primordia by quantitative RT‐PCR as previously described (data not shown).[ 33 ]

Haptotaxis Microfluidic Chip Fabrication

Microfluidic chips with 32 parallel 4‐chamber units were cast using a 2‐layer soft lithography technique as described previously.[ 26 ] Briefly, Sylgard 184 base and curing agent (Karyden DC4019862) were mixed at a 5:1 ratio to produce the control layer chip, which was baked for 30 min at 80 °C. The control layer inlets were then punched using a 1 mm biopsy punch. For the flow layer, PDMS with a 20:1 (base: crosslinker) ratio was mixed and spin‐coated onto the flow layer wafer at 1000 rpm for 60 s, left to reflow for 20 min, and then partially cured for 6 min at 80 °C. The control layer chip was carefully aligned onto the flow layer under a stereo microscope and baked for an additional 30 min. The assembled chip was then removed from the wafer and the flow layer inlet holes were punched. Next, the assembled chip was treated with oxygen plasma (20 W per 30 s) and bonded onto functionalized plastic slides (Ibidi 10812). Slides were functionalized by oxygen plasma (30 W per 30 s) and treated with 1% v/v (3‐Aminopropyl)‐tri‐ethoxy‐silane (APTES) for 20 min. The chips were then post‐baked for another hour and kept at room temperature for at least 2 weeks before use to prevent permanent valve collapse. The 128 chamber array chips were mounted on an Olympus IX81 microscope with a motorized stage and an incubator housing equipped with controlled gas supply (CO2) for long‐term culture, enabling multi‐position time‐lapse imaging.

Fibronectin Diffusion Gradient Model

To model fibronectin gradients observed on embryonic skull histology, fibronectin diffusion was first simulated in the microfluidic chip using MATLAB (R2022b 9.13.0.2049777). MATLAB Live scripts with a detailed explanation are included in Supplementary File 1. The actual chip geometry for a single unit was used to build the 2D model. Each unit (a total of 32 units) in the chip contains four serial chambers (labeled D, C, B, A, Figure 1C) of equivalent size connected by sieve valves acting as gates that are toggled open and close by the pneumatic regulated control layer. A high concentration of fibronectin was perfused into the source chamber (chamber D) while closed and then the gate was opened to allow fibronectin diffusion to the remainder of the unit (open chambers, C → A) generating the concentration gradient. Fluorescent Dextran Validation

To validate the fibronectin gradient generation model, fluorescent 500 kDa FITC‐dextran (Sigma FD500S) is utilized, which closely approximates the molecular weight of fibronectin (440–530 kDa).[ 63 ] The gradient of FITC‐dextran was observed through fluorescent time‐lapse imaging and quantified using line scan analysis. FITC‐dextran solutions at concentrations of 1, 10, 100, and 1000 µg mL−1 in 1X PBS were intermittently perfused into the source chamber (every 30 min). Fluorescent microscopy captured images every 20 min over a period of 48 h. Subsequently, the fluorescent images captured at 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h were analyzed using FIJI/ImageJ software (version 2.14.0/1.54f). Three line profiles were drawn at three distinct locations for each set of chambers (total of eight technical replicates per timepoints), and the resulting line profiles were averaged and normalized to the maximum intensity. The averaged fluorescent intensity profiles were plotted using a custom MATLAB script (Supplementary File 2).

Fibronectin Concentration Gradient Generation and Validation

To demonstrate the formation of the fibronectin gradient, fluorescent FITC‐fibronectin (Sigma F2733) is utilized, immunostaining of human plasma fibronectin (Sigma FC010), and Coomassie/Brilliant blue staining (Thermofisher AC191480050). Fibronectin concentrations of 500, 100, and 10 µg mL−1 were diluted in 1X PBS. For the uniform fibronectin control group, 10 µg mL−1 fibronectin was flowed through all chambers every 30–40 min for 3 h. For the gradient groups (100 and 500 µg mL−1), fibronectin was flowed through the source chambers (chamber D) for 1 min, and the valves between the source chamber and the rest of the chambers were opened to allow fibronectin diffusion, repeating every 30–40 min for up to 24 h. In between repeating cycles, residual fibronectin in the waste channel was washed with 1X PBS to prevent undesired cell attachment outside of the cell chamber. FITC‐fibronectin diffusion was evaluated by time‐lapse imaging. After deposition, all chambers were washed with 1X PBS. Unlabeled fibronectin was examined through fluorescent antibody staining and Coomassie blue staining, while FITC‐fibronectin was visualized directly using fluorescent microscopy. The primary fibronectin antibody (abclonal A12932) and secondary rabbit AF568 antibody (Invitrogen A11011) were separately diluted in a 1:20 ratio in 0.1% TX100 in 1X PBS solution. After fibronectin ligand diffusion, coating, and washing, the primary antibody was slowly perfused into each chamber for 15 min, followed by 5 min of washing, and then another 15 min of secondary antibody perfusion. All chambers were washed and kept in 1X PBS before fluorescent microscopy imaging. The average line profiles for the FITC‐fibronectin gradient, Coomassie blue staining, and antibody staining of the fibronectin gradient were generated by averaging data from at least four technical replicates. The slopes of the gradient in selected regions were calculated using the polyfit function (first‐order polynomial) in MATLAB. All cell experiments utilized human plasma fibronectin.

Microfluidic Chip Cell Culture and Phalloidin Staining

The chip was filled, degassed with sterile 1X PBS, briefly sterilized with 70% ethanol, and rinsed with PBS. Pluronic F‐127 (Sigma P2443) at 0.2% w/v in 1X PBS was flowed through the rising channels for 30 min and washed with 1X PBS to prevent undesired cell adhesion outside chambers. Following fibronectin gradient generation and coating, the chip was kept at 37 °C/5% CO2 for a minimum of 2 h before cell loading.[ 26 ] Primary calvarial osteoblast progenitors at P2‐P3, prepared at 2–5 million cells per mL in 2% FBS DMEM supplemented with 16% v/v OptiPrep (Sigma D1556), were loaded into 24G PTFE tubing (Cole‐Parmer EW‐06417‐31 or EW‐06417‐21) and uniformly seeded into the microfluidic chip (in groups of eight chambers or single chambers) using an automated microfluidics control system.[ 27 ] At the beginning of the experiment, each chamber contains ≈15–25 primary calvarial osteoblast progenitors. Each chip experiment utilized cells from two biological replicates, with a total of four biological replicates (mouse embryos) employed in each experiment. Cells attached for the first 30–60 mins, and 2% FBS DMEM (culture media) was then supplied through the chambers at low flow rates, replacing media every 40 min. Cell migration in each chamber was recorded by 10X Phase‐contrast time‐lapse stitched images (Olympus IX81 microscope with a motorized stage) at a frequency of 20 min per slice for 18–24 h. For small molecule testing, all units were coated with a fibronectin gradient generated from 100 µg mL−1 Fibronectin1 source chamber over 24 h. Primary osteoblast progenitors were seeded as described above and treated with culture media or media containing different concentrations of Arp2/3 or ROCK inhibitor.

Following the microfluidic chip cell culture experiment, primary calvarial osteoblast progenitors were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min. Subsequently, they underwent permeabilization and blocking with a 5% goat serum in 0.1% TX100 PBS solution (blocking buffer) for 5 min, all conducted through slow perfusion within the chip. Actin filament phalloidin staining was achieved by perfusing highly concentrated phalloidin (20 µg mL−1) in blocking buffer for 10 min (Cayman Chemical 20552). Nuclear staining was carried out by perfusing 10 µg mL−1 Hoechst 33342 (ab228551) in 1X PBS for 10 min. Cells were imaged with an inverted Leica Stellaris 5 Confocal or Olympus IX81 microscope.

Scratch Assay for Dose Response of Small Molecules

The effects of Arp2/3 and ROCK inhibitor small molecules were assessed using both the scratch assay in 96‐well plates and the microfluidic chip. In the multiwell plates, primary osteoblast progenitors were seeded at a density of 30k cells per well and cultured until confluence was reached within 24–48 h. Following this, a straight‐line scratch was made in each well using a 10 µL pipette tip, and the cell monolayer was gently washed with 1X PBS to remove detached cells before replenishing with fresh media or media containing varying concentrations of CK666 (Arp2/3 inhibitor; Cayman Chemical 29038) and of Y27632 (ROCK inhibitor; Selleckchem S1049). Each experimental condition included at least three technical replicates. Time‐lapse imaging at 4X magnification was conducted over 24 h, capturing frames at 2‐hour intervals. Quantitative analysis involved assessing changes in migration area, mean migration distance, and migration velocity, computed from images taken at 0, 4, 8, and 24 h. The images were preprocessed with FIJI/ImageJ (version 2.14.0/1.54f) to enhance contrast and remove background. Gap area and average gap width were subsequently measured using the Wound Healing Size Tool plugin.

Migration Tracking and Haptotaxis Analysis

The timelapse images for different groups were pre‐processed using FIJI/ImageJ (version 2.9.0/1.53t) to crop the chamber‐only region and enhance cell contrast. Background subtraction was performed by subtracting the average image from each timelapse image to eliminate stationary background. After this preprocessing step, the images were divided into four sets based on the chamber contour. The Manual Tracking tool plugin was then employed to track individual cells within each image, excluding clustered, indistinguishable, or chamber wall‐contacting cells. Imaging sets were blinded before analysis by naïve observers. Dividing cells were tracked as long as they remained visually separable. The resulting cell tracking paths were superimposed onto the timelapse images, and corresponding tracking location data were exported to a text file. Chemotaxis and Migration tool plugin (ibidi 1.01) was used to analyze various parameters such as forward migration index (FMI), velocity, migration distance, and directionality. Summed tracking path plots, color‐coded in blue/red to distinguish cell migration toward or away from the fibronectin gradient, were exported. Rose plots of directional migration on normalized polar coordinates were generated using a custom MATLAB script.

Cell Shape Morphology Analysis

Protrusion Number and Cell Area: Moving cells across all frames were chosen for analysis by evaluating the sequence of images in FIJI/ImageJ software. Cells that formed clumps, grouped together, overlapped each other, anchored on the chip wall, or migrated out of view during the image sequence were excluded. The number of protrusions was counted manually at the 9‐hour time point, with only individual or forked/branched protrusions included. Groups of extensions with similar length resembling serrations or waves were excluded. The determining factor to include an extension as a protrusion was the relative length of the extension compared to its width at the base, where it was connected to the cell body. Extensions that were at least half as long as the width were included. The cell area of individual cells was measured from phalloidin and Hoechst stained images at the end of the experiment (18–24 h) using Cell Profiler (version 4.2.5).

Statistical Analysis

The data from morphology analysis (protrusion number and cell area analysis) and scratch assay were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (v8.0.1). Group comparisons were made using one‐way ANOVA with Dunnett's multiple comparison test (p < 0.05 significance threshold). Migration tracking analyses, including yFMI, velocity, and Euclidean distance, were presented as estimation plots.[ 64 ] The data were downsampled to match the lowest sample number in each condition group (n = 150 for Figures 4D and 5A–C; n = 28‐31 for Figure 5D; n = 80 for Figures 6D and 7A–C, n = 30‐32 for Figure 7D, respectively). In these plots, datapoints were visualized as a swarmplot, with effect size represented by bootstrap 95% confidence interval (95% CI) on a separate, aligned axis beneath the raw datapoints.[ 65 ] Estimation plots were generated using the dabestr package in Rstudio (version 4.3.1). The one‐way ANOVA with Dunnett's multiple comparison test (p < 0.05 significance threshold) was conducted in GraphPad Prism to assess the statistical significance of differences, with resulting p‐values presented in the figure.
