Predicting low oxygen in patients with acute COVID-19 infection isolating at home: a clinical prediction model
Authors: Robert Wu, Alex Mariakakis, Eyal de Lara, Joseph Munn, Daniyal Liaqat, Salaar Liaqat, Junlin Chen, Teresa To, Philip W Lam, Andrew Simor, Adrienne K Chan, Nisha Andany, Sameer Masood, Nick Daneman, Tiffany Chan, Christopher Graham, Vikram Comondore, Alice Y Tu, Andrea Gershon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationaleIdentifying patients who could be safely managed at home versus those needing hospitalisation was a particular concern during early COVID-19. Respiratory viruses remain a concern, including new COVID-19 variants, influenza and respiratory syncytial virus. We developed COVIDFree@Home, a mobile application and clinician dashboard for remote monitoring, to determine if remotely collected measures could predict low oxygen saturation in home-isolating patients with COVID-19.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study of patients newly diagnosed with COVID-19 from three Toronto hospitals between 2020 and 2022. Participants used the COVIDFree@Home app daily to enter symptoms, temperature, heart rate and oxygen saturation at home, which clinicians monitored via an online dashboard. We analysed baseline characteristics and remote monitoring variables to identify predictors of oxygen saturation ≤92%. A random forest classifier was trained to predict low oxygen saturation in the following 2 days. A secondary objective was to identify factors predicting hospitalisation.ResultsOf 431 participants, 376 (87.2%) entered at least one measure. Of the 376, 49 (13%) experienced low oxygen saturation, and 19 (5.1%) were hospitalised. Baseline factors associated with low oxygen saturation included older age, obesity, pre-existing pulmonary disease and Alpha/Beta variant. The classifier predicted future low oxygen saturation with an area under the curve of 0.68 (sensitivity 57%, specificity 72%, positive predictive value 3%, negative predictive value 99%). Key predictive factors included cough, lower baseline oxygen saturation, severe fatigue, higher temperature and higher heart rate. Factors associated with hospitalisation included dyspnoea, fever, Alpha/Beta variant and comorbidities of hypertension, mental illness and diabetes. Patients with a runny nose or sore throat were less likely to be hospitalised.ConclusionsDuring the COVID-19 pandemic, remote monitoring along with knowledge of baseline characteristics could predict low oxygen saturation in the next 2 days in people with COVID-19. This approach may help identify individuals needing medical attention during future pandemics, though further model improvement is necessary.Trial registration numberNCT04453774.

Background

Viral respiratory infections, including COVID-19, influenza and respiratory syncytial virus, account for 6 million hospitalisations and between 400 000 and 700 000 deaths each year globally.1 2 Healthcare providers put little weight on the presenting signs and symptoms of these illnesses when prognosticating about their course, likely because of the wide variation in presentations, the difficulty of obtaining accurate data and lack of evidence they accurately predict deterioration.3 4 Remote monitoring may change this, as detailed collection of daily data becomes possible to use in clinical care. Specifically, data may be able to predict acute deterioration and the need for hospitalisation, especially in vulnerable populations such as the immunosuppressed or older patients.

A greater understanding of the acute presentation of viral illnesses would help predict severity and inform care. The COVID-19 pandemic presented a unique opportunity to capture daily information on viral illnesses in real-world populations. Learning from this experience may inform how we should manage current and future viral diseases, particularly in vulnerable populations.

During the first years of the COVID-19 pandemic, there was uncertainty as to who could be safely cared for at home and who would require further care, especially with new variants emerging that varied in their severity. A minority of patients with mild COVID-19 isolating at home deteriorated and required hospitalisation. Because not all patients experienced dyspnoea when they became hypoxic, there were recommendations to supplement symptom monitoring with remote monitoring. For example, pulse oximeters were recommended at home to detect decreases in oxygen saturation.4 5 To facilitate this, hospitals around the world rapidly deployed home monitoring programmes for patients with COVID-19. These efforts included the use of apps, cloud-based electronic medical record software, phone calls and video conferencing.69 Unfortunately, few were studied. We rapidly developed and deployed COVIDFree@Home, a mobile application and clinician dashboard for remotely monitored patients with COVID-19 when they quarantined at home. This platform allowed us to understand how oxygen saturation and symptoms evolve in people with acute COVID-19 disease.

Methods

Study design, setting and objectives

We conducted a prospective cohort study of patients who tested positive for COVID-19 at three hospitals in the Greater Toronto Area, Canada—Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre, University Health Network and Trillium Hospital—between 2020 and 2022. Patients were monitored when they were isolating at home using the smartphone app, COVIDFree@Home. Our primary objective was to identify factors predicting low oxygen saturation.

This study was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04453774). A previous publication described the feasibility of implementing the remote monitoring programme based on the same study population.10

Study participants

We recruited people who tested positive for SARS-COV2 by PCR test and were followed up by hospital programmes at our three study sites. We included patients who were 18 years or older and English speaking, within 6 days of testing positive for COVID-19. Patients who had significant comorbidities that would confound symptoms or sensor readings, and patients who were considered palliative were excluded.

Patient and public involvement

A patient was invited to be part of the research team at the initiation phase and was involved as a research partner in all aspects of the study including the original research question, the design of the intervention, the analysis and the manuscript.

Study intervention

Patient app

Patients were instructed to use the COVIDFree@Home mobile app to enter their symptoms, heart rate, temperature and oxygen saturation two times per day for up to 10 days. They would start after the day their symptoms started or the date of their COVID-19 test if they had no symptoms (online supplemental figure S1). If participants did not have a smartphone, pulse oximeter or thermometer at home, these devices were provided to them.

Clinician dashboard

Patient data were uploaded to a dashboard where clinicians could monitor their data. The clinicians were instructed to review patients’ data daily and follow-up out of range values using their clinical judgement (online supplemental figure S2). This usually involved contacting the patient via phone to assess how they were doing.

Outcomes

Our primary outcome was low oxygen saturation defined as a saturation of less than or equal to 92%,11 reported via COVIDFree@Home. The secondary outcome was hospitalisations.

Data collection

Participants completed a symptom questionnaire and entered their temperature, oxygen saturation and heart rate directly in the COVIDFree@Home two times per day. Research staff administered questionnaires on demographics, comorbidities, medications and active symptoms at the start of the study. Chart review was used to supplement data collection. Participants were asked to complete questionnaires on health services use after they finished the study. For the disease variant, we assigned the variant based on the most prevalent at the date of infection since most patients were not tested for specific variants.

Analysis

We considered baseline information, monitored vitals and symptoms as variables for the prediction of low oxygen saturation. Tests for significance were done with χ² for categorical variables and t-test for continuous variables.

Baseline variables and current and past vitals and symptoms were used to predict low oxygen saturation by training and then evaluating a random forest classifier using fivefold subject-wise cross-validation. Variables with over 50% missing data or those which had occurred with very low frequency were dropped (online supplemental table 1). Categorical variables were converted into binary dummy variables, missing data were imputed according to k-nearest neighbours and input features were scaled to have a zero mean and unit variance. To determine variable importance, SHapley Additive exPlanation values were determined.12 All machine learning was performed using the Python sklearn library.

Secondary analysis

We determined factors predicting hospitalisation using tests for significance similar to our primary analysis.

Results

Participants

We recruited 431 people who tested positive for COVID-19 from October 2020 to June 2022 (table 1). The average age was 38.9 years, and 56.5% were female. Among the study cohort, 376 (87.2%) participants entered information in the app at least once; of these, 373 (99.2%) reported symptoms and 363 (96.5%) recorded oxygen saturation level. On average, participants submitted symptom entries 1.7 times per day for a median of 5 days (IQR 3–8) and oxygen saturation measurements 1.5 times per day for a median of 6 days (IQR 4–9). Patients were monitored from a median day 5 (IQR 3–6) to day 11 (IQR 8–14) of their infection. For details on the feasibility and engagement of this study cohort, please see our previous work.10

Predictors of low oxygen saturation

Among participants, median oxygen saturation declined over time to reach a nadir between 6 and 9 days after COVID infection (online supplemental figure S3). Most cases of oxygen saturation 92% or lower took place in the 2nd week of infection (online supplemental figure S4). Factors associated with low oxygen saturation included being older, the Alpha/Beta variant, having obesity, having pre-existing pulmonary disease and symptoms of dyspnoea and severe fatigue (tables1 2). Having a runny nose was associated with decreased risk of low oxygen saturation.

Prediction model of low oxygen saturation

The random forest classifier had an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.68, sensitivity of 57%, specificity of 72%, positive predictive value of 3% and negative predictive value of 99% (positive likelihood ratio=2.0, negative likelihood ratio=0.60) to predict oxygen saturation ≤92% in the following 2 days (figure 1). The top six features that increased the likelihood of low oxygen saturation included symptoms of cough and severe fatigue; preceding vital signs of lower oxygen saturation, increased temperature and increased heart rate; and male sex (figure 2).

Hospitalisations

There was a total of 19 hospitalisations. Factors associated with hospitalisation included baseline dyspnoea (Medical Research Council Dyspnoea Scale), mental illness, hypertension and diabetes (table 3). With respect to remote monitoring variables, the symptoms associated with hospitalisation included fever and shortness of breath. Sore throat and runny nose were associated with decreased risk of hospitalisation. As anticipated, low oxygen saturations of less than or equal to 92% were associated with hospitalisations.

Discussion

We conducted a prospective cohort study of remote clinical monitoring of people infected with COVID-19 isolating at home using an app and a dashboard that collected daily symptoms, oxygen saturation, temperature and heart rate. Our machine learning model had modest ability (AUC 0.68, sensitivity of 57%, specificity of 72%) to predict oxygen saturation ≤92% in the next 2 days. These results suggest that remote clinical monitoring can provide some information to help predict deterioration in people with acute respiratory viral infections; however, ongoing refinement of the algorithm model will be necessary to optimise its performance for clinical use.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies that found similar risk factors for deterioration in people with acute COVID-19, including certain variants, hypertension, diabetes and obesity.13 Previous studies also found that people with upper respiratory symptoms like a runny nose and sore throat were less likely to have deterioration.14

A strength of our study is the prospectively collected daily symptom data,13 which allowed us to track patient status in real time and may improve prediction accuracy. We also incorporated other symptoms such as fever to previously described symptom clusters that have been associated with worse outcomes.15 Other strengths of the study include the detailed daily vital signs and symptom information in a large population of people during acute COVID-19 infection.

We were able to train a machine learning model which had modest ability to predict future deterioration. Further work is needed to improve the sensitivity and specificity of the model before it is useful in clinical practice. With a current positive predictive value of 3%, this model’s high false alarms could increase patient anxiety and healthcare burden. One method of increasing the model may be using continuous passive data collection through wearables as opposed to just daily collection from surveys.16 Of note, the important features of this model did differ from those identified by univariate analysis. For example, cough was the most important feature in the random forest model, but dyspnoea was the most statistically significant in univariate analysis. This may be due to the model only having preceding data to predict low saturation as opposed to univariate analysis having all data, even after desaturation occurred.

There are limitations to the study. First, we studied a relatively young and healthy population. The findings may therefore primarily apply to adults with lower risks, and results should be confirmed in studies with larger and more diverse populations, including older individuals and those with significant comorbidities. Second, we were also missing a modest amount of demographic data, as some people did not want to answer questions related to their residency and ethnicity. Third, we assumed disease variant according to the date of infection since most patients were not tested for specific variants. This may have resulted in some misclassification of variants and potentially biased associations with low oxygen saturation, especially for the Alpha/Beta variant. Finally, data from this study are not available for sharing, as it was not specified in the informed consent. This limits transparency and external validation of the prediction model. Future work should plan for prospective consent for data sharing to support reproducibility, particularly in the context of machine learning.

In summary, we demonstrated that at the height of the COVID-19 pandemic, remote clinical monitoring along with knowledge of baseline characteristics could predict low oxygen saturation in the next 2 days in people with COVID-19 isolating at home with a specificity of 72%. This may help identify people in need of medical attention. Further work is needed to improve the performance characteristics of the model.
