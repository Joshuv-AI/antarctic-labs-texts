Dual-band high-dissymmetry circularly polarized luminescence from cholesteric liquid crystals overlaid a gold cluster film
Authors: Xichen Hao, Zhenda Chen, Huizhen Ma, Yuhan Wang, Yaxian Cao, Buyi Yao, Ruotong Wang, Rui Cao, Yin Xiao, Changlong Liu, Ying Liu, Shouzhong Feng, Haitao Dai, Xiao-Dong Zhang, Yan-Jun Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecently, near-infrared (NIR) circularly polarized luminescence (CPL) has emerged as a research hotspot owing to its unique application prospects. Atomic precision Au clusters (AuCs) have shown excellent biocompatibility and characteristic emission in the NIR region, and have found various applications in biosensing and imaging. Here, we achieve stable and highly dissymmetric CPL in both visible and NIR regions from cholesteric liquid crystals (CLCs) overlaid on a uniform AuCs film. The glum factors (Maximum 2) in the visible and the NIR regions reach 1.8 and 0.7, respectively. AuCs films are prepared by the anti-solvent precipitation method for the first time. By overlaying wide-band CLCs on the AuCs film, dual CPL is simultaneously realized in both visible and NIR regions. Based on the intrinsic properties of CLCs, electrically tunable, thermally reversible and optically tunable CPL from CLCs/AuCs composites are successfully demonstrated. These features of CLCs/AuCs composites offer possibilities for applications in cryptography and anti-counterfeiting.Subject terms: Liquid crystals, Self-assembly, Chemical bonding

Introduction

In recent years, near-infrared (NIR) circularly polarized luminescence (CPL) has attracted great interests due to its promising properties, such as large penetration depth and safety to the human eye1–6, as well as the ability of CPL to enhance signal propagation in turbid media7–11. NIR CPL has found massive applications in optical displays12, encrypted transmission12, information storage12, and sensing13, significantly extending the wavelength range and enhancing versatility and applicability. To date, many materials have been developed for NIR CPL, such as chiral rare-earth compounds14–16 and non-rare-earth chiral metal compounds12,17,18, chiral molecules15,19,20, and upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs)21. To extend the CPL application, how to achieve high quality CPL, particularly, the high glum factor, has attracted extensive attention. The glum factor is defined as 2|(IL – IR) |/(IL + IR), where IL and IR are intensity of left- and right-handed circularly polarized light, respectively. The maximum value of glum is 222–27, indicating complete right or left circular polarization28,29. Currently, there are two main strategies used to achieve CPL. One strategy involves introducing chiral groups into luminescent units to achieve intrinsic CPL-active materials. However, the resulted glum factor is typically small, ranging from 10-3 to 10-2 20,30–32. The other one depends on the filtering effect of chiral structures with NIR emitters. By tailoring the coupling between chiral structures and NIR emitters, a high glum factor (nearly approaching 233–36) can be achieved.

Au clusters (AuCs) and derivatives with atomic precision are novel photoluminescent materials with important potential applications in bioimaging, sensing and photonics due to their strong NIR photoluminescence (PL), superior structural stability, and excellent biocompatibility37,38. AuCs have precise atomic composition and spatial coordination structure, expressed as Aun(SR)m, where SR is the mercaptan ligand, m is the number of ligands, and n is the number of Au atoms. AuCs are mainly composed of two parts, the core structure composed of Au atoms and the outer stapler-like -Sr-[AU-SR]n protective unit composed of Au atoms and surface ligands, such as 3-mercaptopropionic acid (MPA) and glutathione, etc. To further enhance the PL performance of AuCs, one Au atom in AuCs can be replaced with other metal atom, such as Cd and Zn, etc. Among those AuCs, Au25(MPA)18 and metal atom doped derivatives have attracted great interest due to their advantages of easy preparation, high yield, excellent thermal stability, and easy surface functionalization39–41. We have reported the synthesis of Au24Cd1(MPA)18, which shows 56.6 times enhancement of PL intensity in comparison with that of pure Au25(MPA)1838. In this work, Au24Cd1(MPA)18 is exploited for the high-performance CPL.

As known, AuCs and derivatives are usually non-polarized NIR photoluminescent indicator in aqueous solutions. To achieve high-performance CPL, we exploit the filtering strategy by integrating AuCs and derivatives with the chiral mask. In such an approach, two major challenges are addressed. First, a uniform film with stable PL based on AuCs and derivatives has been successfully prepared using the anti-solvent precipitation method, which is the first report of AuCs-based film to our knowledge. Second, the essential prerequisite for the filtering strategy is to find the optimal match between the PL peaks of the AuCs-based film and the photonic bandgap (PBG) of the chiral mask, which further ensures the high selectively for CPL. Here, cholesteric liquid crystals (CLCs) are selected as the chiral mask to match AuCs-based film for high quality CPL in NIR. CLCs are typical self-assembled helical structure with stimuli-responsive properties42,43, serving as a versatile platform for constructing CPL-active material systems. In particular, by overlaying a CLCs layer on the Au24Cd1(MPA)18 film to form a composite, the CLCs/Au24Cd1(MPA)18 composite perfectly preserve the helical arrangement and stimulus-responsive properties of CLCs, which endows high glum factors and convenient CPL modulation. As a result, dual CPL emissions in visible and NIR regions are achieved with corresponding glum factors of 1.8 and 0.7, respectively. Furthermore, electrically tunable, thermally reversible and optically tunable CPL from this composite are demonstrated, creating new application scenarios for CPL display and anti-counterfeit encryption.

Results and discussions

Synthesis of atomic precision Au24Cd1(MPA)18

We use “atomic engineering” techniques to synthesize Au24Cd1(MPA)18 clusters with high stability and brightness by replacing an Au atom in the Au25(MPA)18 surface structure with a Cd atom (see Section “Supplementary Methods and Discussions” for detailed synthesis steps). The geometric structure of Au24Cd1(MPA)18 is shown in Fig. 1a. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to analyze the surface elemental composition and the oxidation states of Au and Cd in Au24Cd1(MPA)18. The full XPS spectrum of the Au24Cd1(MPA)18 revealed the presence of C, N, O, S, Au, and Cd elements, with the peaks of N and Cd elements being very close and difficult to distinguish (Supplementary Fig. 1). To determine the oxidation states and contents of Au and Cd elements in the Au24Cd1(MPA)18, the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV was used for energy calibration, followed by data fitting. From the analysis of the XPS fitting results, it was found that Au(0) is the main state in the Au24Cd1(MPA)18 clusters (Fig. 1b), with binding energies of Au 4f7/2 and Au 4f5/2 at 84 eV and 87.7 eV, respectively. The binding energy of Au 4f7/2 is slightly higher than that of bulk Au foil (83.8 eV), indicating the presence of partially oxidized Au(I) in the Au24Cd1(MPA)18. In addition, the energy spectrum of the Cd 3d levels in Au24Cd1 was also tested. As illustrated in Fig. 1c, the Cd element exhibits distinct energy level peaks, indicating the successful doping of Cd into the Au25(MPA)18 structure. The binding energy of Cd 3d5/2 is determined to be 404.9 eV, which is between 405 eV (zero-valent) and 404.6 eV (divalent), suggesting partial charge transfer from Au to Cd, consistent with previous reports44. To verify the purity of the synthesized Au nanoclusters, we tested the samples using Electrospray Ionization Mass Spectrometry (ESI-MS). ESI-MS shows a characteristic peak at m/z ~2243 for the Cd-doped Au clusters (Supplementary Fig. 2a), assigned to [Au24Cd1MPA18-3H]3−. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) confirms that the ratio of Cd to the total metal is 2%, further validating the successful incorporation of single atoms (Supplementary Fig. 2b).

Due to the unique interband transitions of Au25 clusters, there are two distinctive absorption peaks at 450 and 670 nm, which distinguish from other Au clusters with different number of Au atoms. As shown in Fig. 1d, when an Au atom in the Au25 cluster is replaced by a single Cd atom, the characteristic absorption peaks demonstrate slight shift (450–447 nm and 670–672 nm) compared to those of the Au25 cluster. The result is consistent with the characteristic absorption peak of the Au24Cd1(MPA)18 cluster reported in previous literature44. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images and size distribution histograms are illustrated in Fig. 1e f, respectively. The Au24Cd1(MPA)18 clusters exhibit good dispersion and an average core size of 1.71 ± 0.60 nm. The results confirm that we have successfully synthesized the Au24Cd1(MPA)18 with precise atomic structure. In the following, we refer to AuCs and AuCs films specifically as Au24Cd1(MPA)18.

To identify the precise spatial atomic configuration, X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectra at the Au L3-edge for Au25(MPA)18and Au24Cd1(MPA)18, and at the Cd K-edge for Au24Cd1(MPA)18 were recorded. For reference, the spectra of Au and Cd foil standards are also included in the figures. As shown in Fig. 1g, the X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectra at the Au L3-edge for both clusters differ markedly from that of the Au foil standard, indicating that Au atoms in the clusters are not purely in the zerovalent state. The clusters exhibit two distinct absorption peaks at approximately 11,923 and 11,930 eV, a feature similar to Au foil, and the overall spectral line shape resembles metallic characteristics, suggesting that MPA-capped Au clusters retain partial metallicity. The white-line is a strong absorption peak near the absorption edge. It corresponds to electronic transitions into unoccupied d or f orbitals, and its intensity is therefore directly related to the number of unoccupied states in the material. A stronger white-line indicates that the element is in a higher oxidation state, meaning fewer electrons and more empty states. The white-line intensity of Au in the clusters is higher than in Au foil, attributable to their larger specific surface area, higher oxidation state, and increased d-band vacancies. The characteristic absorption edge of Au25(MPA)18 at ~11,920 eV corresponds to the 2p → 5 d electronic transition, indicating reduced d-band vacancies. After single-atom Cd substitution, the increased white-line intensity in Au24Cd1(MPA)18 results from electron transfer from Cd atoms, which decreases the 5d-electron density of Au atoms. The Fourier-transformed extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) spectrum shows that the Au–S bond in AuCs is about 1.9 Å, which does not exist in Au foil (Supplementary Fig. 2c). After Cd substitution, the Au–S bond still remains, indicating that Cd is substituted at the surface of the Au₁₃ core or at oligomeric sites. The k-space oscillations of AuCs and Au foil are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2d. The k-space of Au foil exhibits a typical FCC oscillation pattern, which is clearly absent in all AuCs due to their small core size.

To precisely determine the doping site of Cd atoms and thereby elucidate the atomic spatial configuration of Au24Cd1(MPA)18, we analyzed the XAFS spectra of Cd. Figure 1h displays the XANES spectrum at the Cd K-edge for Au24Cd1(MPA)18, revealing the local coordination structure around Cd atoms and providing parameters such as atomic distances and coordination numbers. Figure 1i presents the FT-EXAFS spectrum of Au24Cd1(MPA)18 at the Cd K-edge, shows a single dominant peak in the 1.6–5.0 Å range, which corresponds to scattering paths of photoelectrons from Cd atoms to neighboring S atoms in different coordination shells. Fitting this peak using the IFEFFIT program yielded the EXAFS parameters summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The Cd-S coordination number is 1.5 ± 0.4 at ~2 Å, suggesting Cd substitution at the oligomeric sites of the Au25(MPA)18 structure. This aligns with prior literature reports44.

PL characterization of highly uniform AuCs films

As aforementioned, gold clusters are usually used as the aqueous solution. The poor wetting on the glass leads to nonuniform film with spin-coating and the coffee ring effect with dropping directly, both of which result in low PL efficiency, thereby greatly limiting their PL-based applications. In this work, we employed the anti-solvent precipitation approach to prepare the AuCs films for the first time, as illustrated in Fig. 2a. Specifically, ethanol is firstly dropped onto plasma-treated indium-tin-oxide (ITO)-coated glass substrates, followed immediately by AuCs aqueous solution. Due to immiscibility of AuCs and ethanol, the AuCs precipitates would come out in ethanol, which self-assemble into the film and uniformly cover the substrate. A detailed discussion of the differences between the films formed by the anti-solvent precipitation and direct drop-casting can be found in “Supplementary Methods and Discussions” Section 5. Figure 2b shows an optical microscope image of the AuCs film, illustrating a uniform morphology. The illustration shows a photograph of the AuCs film prepared by the anti-solvent deposition method captured with a camera. In addition, the atomic force microscope (AFM) image confirms that the AuCs film has the surface roughness of 6.27 nm on average (Fig. 2c).

Subsequently, the PL performance was characterized. As shown in Fig. 2d, e, one can see that the PL of the as-prepared AuCs film shows a significant blueshift compared to that of AuCs in water solution. Specifically, the central wavelengths of the two PL peaks blueshift from 850 to 760 nm, and from 1002 nm to 919 nm, respectively. In addition, time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) (Supplementary Fig. 6) and fitting results (Supplementary Table 2) showed that the lifetime of the AuCs solution in the visible and NIR regions are 234 ns and 6.7 ns, respectively. We have explained the reason for the long lifetime in detail through DFT calculations, and the detailed discussion can be found in “Supplementary Methods and Discussions” Section 6. The photoluminescent quantum yield (PLQY) of AuCs solutions are 16.27% and 3.51% in the visible and NIR regions, respectively. However, the lifetime of AuCs films is 21.8 μs and 21.6 μs (Supplementary Table 3), and the PLQY are 50.01% and 20.54% in the visible and NIR regions, respectively. The long-lived, high-quantum yield luminescence observed in AuCs films can be better attributed to ligand-to-metal-metal charge transfer (LMMCT) relaxation occurring in the metal-centered triplet of AuCs gold shells45–47. This luminescence caused by LMMCT has been observed in multiple gold complexes48,49 and has been attributed to gold-affinity interactions. The blue shift of fluorescence peak is due to the transition of luminescence from the lower energy T2 state to the ground state in the triplet to the higher energy T1 state to the ground state (Supplementary Fig. 7). The insets in Fig. 2d show PL photographs of the film and solution. It should be emphasized that the AuCs film can emit both the visible and NIR light, which lays the foundation for dual-emission CPL with AuCs film.

High-quality CPL from a CLCs/AuCs composite

To achieve NIR CPL with AuCs, the AuCs film is assembled with a CLCs cell to form the CLCs/AuCs composite. The configuration of the CLCs/AuCs composite is schematically illustrated in Fig. 3a. Supplementary Fig. 8 shows the image of nematic LC E7 and AuCs composite under the polarizing optical microscope (POM). The uniform black image indicates high-quality alignment of the LC layer on the AuCs film. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images confirm that the composite emitting film and the stimulus-responsive LC layer form a bilayer structure throughout the composite (Supplementary Fig. 9). To achieve the CPL from the CLCs/AuCs composite, the reflective band (i.e., PBG) of CLCs should match with the PL peaks of AuCs film. Therefore, the concentration of chiral dopant has to be adjusted to realize a suitable helical pitch p. As we know, the helix pitch p of CLC is determined by the helical twisting power (HTP) and the concentration c of the chiral dopant: p = 1/(HTP × c). Furthermore, the central wavelength λc of CLC reflective band can be calculated according to λc = n ∙ p, here n is the mean refractive index of CLCs. Considering the refractive index of LC E7 and HTP of chiral dopant, 2.1 wt% and 1.8 wt% R5011 or S5011 are added to the host E7, which correspond to CLC PBGs of 732–785 nm and 880–966 nm, respectively. The experimentally measured and simulated CLC PBGs are in good agreement (Supplementary Fig. 10). Within the reflection band, the higher the reflectance, the higher the glum factor. Additionally, a decrease in the regularity of CLC alignment can limit its circular polarization ability. Specifically, the deformation of the CLC’s helical arrangement leads to structural defects, which reduce the reflectance. As a result, both left-handed and right-handed CPL decrease, and the difference between them diminishes, causing a reduction in circular polarization performance and a lower glum factor. In the composite system we prepared, we used the anti-solvent precipitation method to fabricate a uniform Au cluster film morphology, ensuring highly ordered helical alignment of the CLC and a prominent photonic band gap, which results in a high glum factor. Therefore, a high reflectance is a key factor in ensuring the emission of high glum factor CPL. Two samples of CLCs/AuCs composites are prepared with their PBGs in the visible and NIR regions, respectively. The left panel of Fig. 3b shows the POM image of the CLCs/AuCs composite in the visible region (Vis-CLCs/AuCs), which presents a uniform red color caused by the high reflection of the CLCs layer. The inset of Fig. 3b shows the picture of the Vis-CLCs/AuCs under UV irradiation, uniform red fluorescence can be clearly observed. For the CLCs/AuCs composite in the NIR region (NIR-CLCs/AuCs), the POM image shows that the AuCs film presents uniform black (the left panel of Fig. 3c) due to the invisible nature of NIR light. The observed uniform colors for all the samples indicate the high-quality AuCs film and well-aligned CLCs layer. The large field-view POM images show CLCs planar-state texture in both visible and NIR regions (the right panel of Fig. 3b, c).

Circular dichroism (CD) reflects the ground-state chirality of a material. The CD properties of CLCs/AuCs composites measured by the JASCO-1700 spectrometer are shown in Supplementary Fig. 11. To rule out the influence of linear birefringence, we tested the CD of the square samples at different angles. The composites exhibited ultra-high CD signals in the visible region (Supplementary Fig. 12) and NIR region (Supplementary Fig. 13). The composites consistently exhibit an ultra-high CD signal within the reflection band, indicating that the composites possess a high ground-state chirality within the reflection band, which is essential for emitting CPL.

The CPL properties of CLCs/AuCs composites measured by the JASCO CPL-300 spectrometer are demonstrated in the Fig. 3d–i. As expected, AuCs films showed no CPL signal, as illustrated in Fig. 3d, f. With CLCs/AuCs composites, significant CPL performance can be observed with the recorded maximum glum factor of 1.8 at 749 nm (Fig. 3e). Additionally, the composite also exhibits good CPL performance in the NIR region (Fig. 3f), with the maximum glum factor of 0.7 across a broad wavelength range of 850–950 nm (Fig. 3g). It is worth noting that by changing the dopant with different chiralities (R-5011 or S-5011) in the CLCs/AuCs composites, either positive (left-handed CPL, L-CPL) or negative (right-handed CPL, R-CPL) CPL signals can be achieved. Moreover, the composites displayed good CPL performance at different angles in the visible region (Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15) and NIR region (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17), which rules out the influence of linear birefringence. In summary, with the prepared high-quality CLCs/AuCs composites, CPL with high glum factors in visible and NIR regions have been successfully demonstrated.

The emitted CPL from the CLCs/AuCs composite was further confirmed by recording the light intensity with a home-made optical setup (Supplementary Fig. 18) as ref. 35 for visible light and Supplementary Fig. 19 for NIR light. As reported35, with this setup, the emitted R-CPL or L-CPL intensity would present an M-shape distribution upon rotating the linear analyzer, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 20. It is worth emphasizing that the CPL performance of the composite remains stable after 6 months at room temperature (exposed to air without any protective measures), with the maximum glum factors in visible and NIR regions stabilizing around 1.5 and 0.65, respectively, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 21. The highly stable and repeatable emission makes the CLCs/AuCs composite promising for many potential applications.

We have so far achieved the high-quality single-band CPL with CLCs/AuCs composites in either the visible or NIR region by means of tuning the CLCs’ reflective band. Given the dual emission of the AuCs film in both visible and NIR regions, it will be more advantageous to achieve CPL generation simultaneously in both bands with the CLCs/AuCs composite. To reach this goal, a sufficiently wide reflection bandwidth covering both visible and NIR bands is the key. It is well known that the CLCs’ reflection bandwidth is determined by the helical pitch and the birefringence of the nematic LC used, as described by ∆λ = p ∙ ∆n, where ∆λ is reflection bandwidth, and ∆n is the birefringence of the nematic LC. Here, we replaced the E7 (∆n = 0.224) with another nematic LC NC-M-LDn03 (∆n = 0.394) in the CLCs/AuCs composites to achieve the wide reflection bandwidth of CLCs. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 22, we can achieve the CLCs’ reflection bandwidth of 142 nm with the LC NC-M-LDn03, which effectively covers the two PL bands of the AuCs film in both visible and NIR regions. As shown in Fig. 3h, dual CPL peaks from the composite with central wavelengths locating at 751 nm and 910 nm can be simultaneously achieved, corresponding to visible and NIR regions. Figure 3i shows measured glum factors to be 1.6 at 751 nm and 0.5 at 910 nm, which are slightly smaller than those of single-band CPLs from the composites. There are two main reasons for the decrease of glum factors of dual-band CPL. One reason is the use of an 850 nm long-pass filter for the glum factor measurement of NIR CPL, which leads to energy loss of NIR light due to the surface reflection of the filter. The other one is that the CLC’s reflection bandwidth from 751 to 893 nm does not fully cover the PL bands. Our experimental results confirm that dual band CPL from the CLCs/AuCs composites can be simultaneously achieved in both visible and NIR regions. The achieved glum values of dual band CPL are much higher than those of the molecular compounds (order of 10−3)50, which could inspire many innovative CPL-based applications.

Graphical mode used for anti-counterfeiting encryption

For the CLCs/AuCs composite, the AuCs film can be easily patterned using a pre-determined mask with the laser etching technique, serving as graphical mode displays, which could be potentially useful for cryptography and anti-counterfeiting applications. As illustrated in Fig. 4a shows the schematic illustration of patterning the AuCs film using the laser etching technique, creating a “TJU” emission pattern with clean edges. The graphically patterned AuCs film can be then assembled into an CLC/AuCs composite using the same procedure as Fig. 3a. It can serve as an advanced anti-counterfeiting label, as the graphical information can be displayed under ultraviolet irradiation (Fig. 4b, c). However, when covered with a suitable circular polarizer composed of a QWP and a linear polarizer, the emitted CPL could be blocked with the same handedness. As a result, the graphical pattern will disappear (Fig. 4d). When the linear polarizer is rotated, the pattern appears again (Fig. 4e), as illustrated in Supplementary Movie 1. Based on this strategy, an anti-counterfeiting mechanism in the NIR region can be designed by inserting an 850 nm long-pass filter, allowing only emitted NIR signals to pass through, which cannot be recognized by the naked eye. Therefore, the encrypted information can be only observed in the NIR imaging system, as shown in Fig. 4c, f, g and Supplementary Movie 2 (detailed schematic in Supplementary Fig. 23). The visible and NIR responses of the CLCs/AuCs composite provide multiple channels to identify the labels, which could be a distinctive advantage for anti-counterfeiting applications.

Electrically tunable CPL

Inherited from the CLCs that are electrically and thermally tunable, it is straightforward to make the CPL emission of the CLCs/AuCs composite tunable. In this section, we demonstrate the electrical tunability of CPL of the CLCs/AuCs composite (Fig. 4h). When there is no applied voltage, the CLCs layer in the composite for visible and NIR regions show uniform planar states (Supplementary Fig. 24a, e). As the applied voltage increases, for the Vis-CLCs/AuCs composite, the CPL intensity decreases significantly (Supplementary Fig. 25a). As a result, the peak of glum decreases from 1.8 to 0.1 (Fig. 4i) when the applied voltage increases from 0 to 70 V. This effect is mainly attributed to the gradual mismatch between the PL of the AuCs film and the PBG of CLCs with the increased voltage. As shown in Fig. 4k, the central wavelength of the PBG in the CLCs/AuCs composite blue-shifts from 761 to 660 nm with the applied voltages. Meanwhile, the maximum reflectivity decreases from 57 to 20%, which also leads to a low glum factor (Fig. 4i). The blue shift of the PBG of CLCs with the increased voltages can be explained by the relation λc = n ∙ p. The CLC helix gradually unwinds under the high voltage, leading to the decreased effective refractive index n and subsequently decreased λc. In addition, we notice that the alignment of CLC molecules changes from a planar state to a focal conic state under the increased voltage (Supplementary Fig. 24b, c). With the voltage further increasing up to 120 V, both the glum peak and CPL drop to zero (Fig. 4m) because of the zero reflectivity of the composites caused by the phase transition of CLCs from a focal conic state to a homeotropic state (Supplementary Fig. 24d). Similarly, for the NIR-CLC/AuCs, as the applied voltage increases from 0 to 40 V, the peak value of glum decreased from 0.7 to 0.01 (Fig. 4j, l and Supplementary Fig. 25b). Compared with the Vis-CLCs/AuCs, the NIR-CLCs/AuCs requires a smaller unwinding driving voltage due to the relative weak helical twisting power caused by the lower concentration of the chiral dopant (2.1 wt% for the visible and 1.8 wt% for the NIR. Moreover, the central wavelength of the reflective band blue-shifts from 923 to 801 nm with the reflectance decreased from 39% to 18% (Fig. 4l). Similarly, the alignment of CLC molecules also experience the transition from the planar state to the focal conic state (Supplementary Fig. 24f,g). As the applied voltage increases to 80 V, CLCs appear as the homeotropic state (Supplementary Fig. 24h), and the circular polarization of the emitting light disappears (Fig. 4m). It is apparent that the CLCs/AuCs composite inherits the electrically tunable properties of CLC layer, which could provide a variety of tunable functions for anti-counterfeiting encryption.

Thermally switchable CPL with high reversibility

CLCs are also highly sensitive to temperature changes. The helical pitch of CLCs increases with rising temperature, leading to a significant red shift of the PBG and correspondingly decreased reflectivity (as shown in Fig. 5a, b). In our composites, the central wavelengths of the CLCs’ reflective bands are 750 nm and 925 nm in the visible and NIR regions, respectively. As the temperature gradually increases to 60 °C, the central wavelength of the reflective bands undergoes red shift due to the increased helical pitch p caused by the increasing temperature according to the relation λc = n ∙ p. Meanwhile, the reflectance decreases from 40% (visible) and 32% (NIR) to 0. Furthermore, temperature-dependent CPL properties are shown in Fig. 5c. It can be seen that the maximum glum factors in the visible and NIR regions gradually decrease from 1.8 and 0.7 to 0.01 when the temperature increases from 25 °C to 59 °C, corresponding to significant drop of the CPL peaks (Supplementary Figs. 26 and 27). This can be attributed to the reduced overlap between the PL of the AuCs film and the PBG of CLC. Moreover, because the PBG of the soft helix can be reversibly modulated, original glum factors can be restored when the temperature decreases from 59 °C to 25 °C. We further measured the change in glum factors with temperature cycling. As shown in Fig. 5d, the CPL exhibits reversible changes in glum factors with temperature variation. Due to the dynamic equilibrium of LC alignment fluctuations, there is some fluctuation in its maximum value, but it tends to stabilize overall throughout the cycle. Our experimental results also show that there is almost no effect of temperature on the PL intensity of the CLC/AuCs composite (Supplementary Movies 3 and 4). Besides the electrical tunability, the temperature-reversible CPL properties of CLCs/AuCs composites provide an additional degree of freedom for cryptography and anti-counterfeiting applications.

With thermally switchable CPL characteristics of the CLCs/AuCs composites, we further upgraded the encryption system by combining aforementioned anti-counterfeiting efforts. As shown in Fig. 5e, taking the left-hand composite with the “TJU” pattern as an example, nothing is visible at the room temperature under UV light (365 nm) irradiation since the left CPL is blocked by the circular polarizer. As the temperature increases, the pattern gradually appears and becomes brighter due to the drop of the CPL emission. Subsequently, during the cooling process, the pattern fades and eventually disappears, confirming the thermal switching of the CPL active composite (Supplementary Movie 5). By changing the composite’s temperature, we can easily adjust the PBG of the CLCs, thereby controlling their matching or mismatching. Similarly, we can use a long-pass filter to filter out visible light so that it is invisible to the naked eye, and the encrypted information can only be recognized by infrared imaging systems (Supplementary Movie 6). The thermally switchable CPL composite provides an advanced anti-counterfeiting strategy by controlling the emission polarization state, differing from traditional anti-counterfeiting mechanisms, and enhancing concealment, making it difficult to identify with the naked eye.

Optically tunable CPL

The realization of optical tuning of the CLC helical structure is primarily achieved using azobenzene-based photosensitive compounds. Azobenzene compounds are a class of photochromic substances that contain a nitrogen-nitrogen double bond51. These substances undergo a conformational change upon absorbing visible light. Under normal conditions, azobenzene compounds exist in a low-energy trans configuration. When exposed to external stimuli, such as visible light, they gradually transform into a higher-energy cis configuration. However, the cis configuration is unstable, and the compound will revert back to the trans configuration upon further external stimulation, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 28. In CLC, azobenzene-based chiral molecules can undergo reversible cis-trans isomerization under specific wavelength light, leading to significant changes in the molecular configuration. Meanwhile, the helical twisting power of the different isomers varies, causing the CLC system to form different pitches.

In this study, we used a chiral azobenzene-based photosensitive chiral agent, ChAD. The trans-configuration ChAD has an absorption peak around 365 nm, and this trans-configuration is the stable state. The cis-configuration ChAD have an absorption peak around 475 nm, and the cis state is unstable. Typically, the ChAD exists in the lower-energy trans state. When illuminated with 365 nm light, the ChAD absorbs energy and transitions to the higher-energy cis state. During this process, their helical twisting power decreases, leading to a reduction in the pitch of the CLC system and a red shift in the reflection center wavelength (Fig. 6a). As shown in Fig. 6b, the reflection center wavelength of the CLC doped with the photosensitive chiral agent changes over time under 365 nm light. With increasing exposure time, during the initial 9 s, the reflection center wavelength gradually red-shifts, but without a significant decrease in reflectivity. When the exposure time reaches 18 s, the reflection center wavelength shifts from the initial 760 nm to beyond 1000 nm. The gradual decoupling between the reflection band and the fluorescence band leads to a gradual decrease in the glum value. Since 365 nm light is required to excite the sample during CPL measurements, the sample is continuously exposed to this light until the test is completed. However, the irradiation from the excitation light causes a shift in the reflection band wavelength of the photosensitive CLC. As a result, the reflection band red-shifts before the measurement is completed, leading to a significantly reduced glum value that does not accurately represent the original value prior to light exposure. Therefore, in this work, the variation of the glum value under light irradiation is not presented.

Optically tunable CPL.

a Schematic diagram of the light-induced increase in CLC pitch. b Variation in the reflection band of CLC in the CLC/AuCs composites as a function of the exposure time. Photographs of the CLC/AuCs composites loaded with the “TJU” as a function of the exposure time under UV light irradiation (365 nm) at (c) 0 S, (d) 6S, (e) 9 S. Scale bar = 5 mm.

For example, with the left-handed chiral composites with the “TJU” pattern, the reflection wavelength is initially adjusted to 730–790 nm, which completely covers the PL wavelength (760 nm) of the AuCs film. Initially, the composite emits CPL. Since the CPL is blocked by a circular polarizer, the pattern is not visible at first. However, with the 365 nm light exposure, the reflection center wavelength gradually red shifts and no longer couples with the AuCs PL wavelength, causing the “TJU” pattern to gradually appear (Fig. 6c–e and Supplementary Movie 7). Optically tunable CPL provides a clean, noninvasive, and remotely controlled method for anti-counterfeiting encryption, requiring only an ultraviolet light to determine the presence or absence of information.

In summary, we have successfully constructed an efficient material composite that combines CLCs with AuCs films. Highly stable CPL emission was achieved from the CLCs/AuCs composites with glum factors of up to 1.8 and 0.7 in the visible and NIR regions, respectively. To achieve high glum factors of CPL in visible and NIR regions, we reported firstly the preparation of the uniform AuCs film by anti-solvent precipitation, and realized the perfect coupling between the PL of the AuCs film and the PBG of the CLCs. Meanwhile, we achieved simultaneous dual emission of CPL in visible and NIR regions with CLCs having the broadband PBG. Due to adjustability of CLC’s multi-physical fields, the CLCs/AuCs composites also exhibit electrically and thermally switchable CPL properties. These unprecedented CPL properties of the composites provide new insights into the development of CPL-active materials and composites for CPL display, cryptography, and anti-counterfeiting applications. Meanwhile, NIR CPL is expected to lay the foundation for biomedical development.

Methods

DFT calculations

DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab-Initio Simulation Package with RPBE functional52. The exchange-correlation energy of Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) and the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) were used to describe the electron exchange and correlation potentials53,54,55, with a plane wave cutoff energy set to 400 eV. The Monkhorst–Pack method was employed to sample the Brillouin zone, with a k-point grid of 1 × 1 × 1, and structural optimization calculations were performed with energy and force convergence criteria of 10-4 eV and 0.05 eV/Å, respectively. The calculation files of Au25 and Au24Cd1(MPA)18 are provided in Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Data 2, respectively.

Synthesis of Au24Cd1(MPA)18 clusters

The Au24Cd1(MPA)18 clusters reported in this work were synthesized according to the method described in the ref. 44. Specifically, 0.24 mL of HAuCl4·3H2O (20 mM) aqueous solution and 0.01 mL of Cd(NO3)2 (20 mM) aqueous solution (molar ratio Au:Cd = 24:1) were added to 2 mL of 3-MPA, 5 mM aqueous solution in 2.35 mL of deionized water, and the mixture was slowly stirred at room temperature for 5 min. Subsequently, 0.3 mL of sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 1 M) aqueous solution was added to the reaction solution, followed by the immediate addition of 0.1 mL of NaBH4 aqueous solution (prepared immediately before use by adding 21.5 mg of NaBH4 powder to 5 mL of NaOH solution (0.2 M)), and the reaction was stirred in the dark at a constant speed for 3 h. Finally, the reaction solution was aged at 4 °C for 12 h. After collection of the crude product, further purification was carried out using ultrafiltration tubes with molecular weights of 10 k and 3 k at a centrifugal speed of 1369.6 × g to remove large clusters, impurities, and small organic ligands. The purified Au24Cd1(MPA)18 clusters were then freeze-dried for further testing and applications.

Preparation of the Au24Cd1(MPA)18 clusters film

The ITO substrates were sequentially cleaned by ultrasonication in deionized water, ethanol, and acetone for 30 min. Prior to use, the substrates were further cleaned by UV ozone treatment for 40 min. The freeze-dried clusters powder was dissolved in water. Subsequently, films were prepared using anti-solvent precipitation method. The ITO substrates were heated to 100 °C, and then 350 μL of ethanol was dropped onto the ITO substrate. Immediately afterwards, 70 μL of the Au24Cd1(MPA)18 solution was dropped onto the glass substrate and annealed for 10 min to disperse the Au clusters solution on the glass substrates to form a uniform film.

Fabrication of CLCs/AuCs composites

The typical preparation processes are as follows. The PAPI solution was spin-coated on ITO glass substrate with the Au24Cd1(MPA)18 film at room temperature at 300 rpm for 10 s, 2500 rpm for 30 s, 100 rpm for 3 s, and then annealed at 150 °C for 10 min. After that, the rubbing process was carried out for the alignment purpose. The above same processes were applied to another ITO substrate without the Au24Cd1(MPA)18 film deposition. Subsequently, 20 μm spacers are evenly dispersed on the substrate. The two substrates are assembled into an LC cell in anti-parallel according to the rubbing direction. The CLCs layer is formed by mixing the nematic LC (E7) and chiral dopant (R-5011 or S-5011). The R-/S-5011 represents the right or left handedness, and their structures of molecules are showed in Supplementary Fig. 29. Different amounts of chiral dopants S-5011 and R-5011 were added to E7 and stirred at 70 °C for 2 h (2.1 wt% in the visible region and 1.8 wt% in the NIR region). Finally, the heated mixture is injected into LC cells by capillary action to form the CLCs/AuCs composite.

Characterization

Weighing for the active mass was performed using a precision electronic analytical balance (Sartorius BSA224S, Germany). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging of the Au clusters was conducted using a JEOL JEM-2100F instrument from Japan. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was carried out using a K-Alpha+ instrument from Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK, with a 300 W AlKα X-ray source. The binding energies were calibrated using the C1s peak of adventitious carbon at 284.8 eV. UV-Visible absorption spectra of the Au24Cd1(MPA)18 solutions were recorded using a Shimadzu UV-3600 spectrophotometer from Japan. Electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) was performed on a Bruker microTOF-Q instrument. ICP-MS was conducted using an Agilent 7700× instrument. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were acquired by a RigakuD/MAX 2550 diffractometer with the operation parameters of 40 kV and 100 mA and Cu Ka radiation (l = 1.5406 Å). SEM and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was performed using an EDS detector on a Thermo Scientific Phenom Pharos G2 SEM. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) images were acquired using a CSPM5500 scanning probe microscope from Benyuan Nano Instruments. Au L3-edge and Cd K-edge of Au24Cd1(MPA)18 XAFS analyses were performed with Si (111) crystal monochromators at the BL14W Beam line at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) (Shanghai, China). Before the analysis at the beamline, samples were placed into aluminum sample holders and sealed using Kapton tape film. The XAFS spectra were recorded at room temperature using a 4-channel Silicon Drift Detector (SDD) Bruker 5040. The L-edge extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectrum of Au and the K-edge EXAFS spectrum of Cd were acquired in transmission and fluorescence detection modes, respectively. Negligible changes in the line shape and peak position of Fe K-edge XANES spectra were observed between two scans taken for a specific sample. The XAFS spectra of these standard samples were recorded in transmission mode. The spectra were processed and analyzed by the software code Athena. The XAFS spectra of Au25MPA18 Au L3-edge were collected in transmission mode on a commercial Laboratory-Based XAFS spectrometer (Table XAFS-500A, Specreation Instruments Co., Ltd). An X-ray tube was used to generate X-ray, and the voltage and current were set to 30 kV and 20 mA. The Si (773) spherically bent crystal analyzers with a radius of curvature of 500 mm and the R 250 mm Rowland circle were used to provide monochromatized X-ray beam. Steady-state PL spectra were recorded using a HORIBA Fluoromax four instrument, with excitation at 468 nm. Fluorescence lifetimes and photoluminescence quantum yields (PLQY) of the films were measured using an Edinburgh FLS980 instrument. The PLQY was determined using a 365 nm Xenon lamp as the excitation source within an integrating sphere, and all PLQY measurements were conducted in ambient air at room temperature. The reflectance was measured using an Ocean Optics Maya 2000 Pro spectrometer. NIR images were captured using a custom-built NIR imaging system (Princeton Instruments; NIRvana SI 640) and visible images were captured using a homemade optical system (all lenses: JCOPTIX) and a charge-coupled composite (CCD: Thorlabs; DCC1545M-GL). Polarized optical microscopy (POM) textures were captured using a microscope (Pride Instruments; MX-4RW). Circular dichroism (CD) spectra were recorded on a JASCO 1700 spectrophotometer. CPL spectra were recorded on a JASCO cpl-300 spectrophotometer.
