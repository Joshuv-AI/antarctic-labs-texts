Optimization of traction power conservation and energy efficiency in agricultural mobile robots using the TECS algorithm
Authors: Yavuz Bahadır Koca, Barış Gökçe, Yılmaz Aslan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study proposes a new control algorithm based on the optimization of the conservation of traction power and efficient use of energy, which is adaptable to changing the tillage conditions. The proposed control algorithm is called the Tillage and Energy Control System (TECS). In this study, a cultivator was taken as a tillage tool. The optimized control speed is assigned to the vehicle by observing the slip rate that may occur in the wheel during the plunging and traction of the cultivator, which is the most important parameter of TECS. The traction optimizer obtained the optimal traction models a function of the traction ratios related to maximum traction and energy efficiency using experimental data on the traction-terrain interaction at different soil conditions. The optimal traction models were used to determine the desired value of traction by observing changes such as traction and slippage in real agrobot robots a control input in the traction controller. The traditional PID controller and Fuzzy Inference System model are used for optimal traction control. The proposed traction controller has been developed by compensating for the change in soil spectrum, the impact of stones during tillage, and the noises in the controller. Finally, TECS was experimentally verified by controlling a four-wheel skid steer robot prototype.Keywords: Agricultural mobile robot, Control, Optimization, Robot operating system, Embedded systemsSubject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Mechanical engineering, Computational science, Computer science, Software

Introduction

The interconnected trends of industrialization, rural depopulation, and aging global demographics are creating significant challenges for the agricultural sector1. As an increasing proportion of the world’s population moves toward urban centers, the need to achieve greater efficiency in food production and harvesting has become more pressing. At the same time, the expansion of industrial areas is gradually reducing available farmland, while rising labor and energy costs are making efficient agricultural machinery more essential than ever2,3. In response to these developments, researchers have increasingly focused on the practical implementation of unmanned ground vehicles and autonomous wheeled machines in agriculture4,5. So, robots will play even more critical roles in tasks such as seeding, fertilization, and chemical application within the sector in the near future.

The integration of drones and robots into digital farms has led to major transformations in agricultural facilities. In particular, the ability to manage farming operations remotely via tablets or smartphones, using data collected from satellites, ground-based sensors, and robotic systems, is ushering in a new era in the sector. In this evolving landscape, unmanned tractors and autonomous sprayers enable the precise and timely application of resources such as water, seeds, and pesticides. Moreover, robotic harvesters have shown the potential to increase crop yields over time, especially in sectors like fruit production6. However, the advancement of agricultural technologies also presents significant technical challenges, particularly in planting and harvesting processes where high directional and timing accuracy are essential7. Additionally, optimizing the energy consumption under compacted soil conditions requires the development of specialized control strategies, emphasizing the need for careful evaluation of both the soil properties and machinery efficiency8.

The effective management of agricultural robots requires machines that can dynamically adapt to varying environmental conditions such as temperature and humidity. Traditional control approaches, which rely heavily on static parameters, often fall short under these changing circumstances. For instance, classical methods like PID controllers struggle to adapt quickly to environmental fluctuations, leading to degraded system performance. Moreover, agricultural vehicles often exhibit nonlinear behaviors, including time delays and dead zones, which further complicate the control process. The diversity of soil types and terrain structures also affects robot mobility, increasing the number of control challenges and reducing operational efficiency9. Although skid-steer drive systems provide valuable flexibility in agricultural applications, navigating challenging terrains requires autonomous control strategies10–13. To achieve consistent long-term performance, agricultural robots must be equipped with robust traction control systems and efficient energy management frameworks. Given the highly variable ground conditions encountered in agricultural environments, the ability of wheeled robots to move effectively across different types of terrain is considered essential.

The adoption rate of advanced agricultural technologies in the field has yet to reach the desired level14. In particular, the high costs of these systems and the infrastructure knowledge required for their operation remain major barriers preventing farmers from transitioning to new technologies. Nevertheless, the advantages offered by digital agricultural practices are becoming increasingly evident. According to Geng et al. (2024), each increase in the intensity of digital technology use contributes more than 30% to farmers’ economic gains15. These findings demonstrate that investments in digitalization and automation in agriculture offer substantial long-term economic return potential.

Along with the integration of robotic systems into agricultural operations, artificial intelligence techniques, such as deep learning, have been increasingly applied at various stages of agricultural production16. In particular, depth-sensing technologies, including stereo cameras, structured light sensors, and time-of-flight cameras, enable robots to detect accurate routes and navigate safely in unstructured environments, such as sloped and rugged terrains17–21. Additionally, human–robot collaboration approaches have been proposed as innovative solutions to enhance the efficiency of agricultural tasks22–24. The combined use of AI-supported ground and aerial robots has led to a high level of automation, offering significant potential to improve production efficiency and reduce environmental impacts14. Considering this development, most modern agricultural robots are designed with four-wheel drive (4WD) systems. These robots typically do not incorporate robotic arms; instead, they rely on sensors, such as RGB cameras, primarily for tasks like weed detection. Jin et al. (2021) classified agricultural robots based on the sector they served (e.g., crop production, livestock, aquaculture), the function they performed (e.g., monitoring, mapping, harvesting), their level of autonomy (remotely controlled, human–robot collaborative, fully autonomous), and their mobility (stationary, mobile; land or air)25. These classifications indicate that the agricultural robotics ecosystem is significantly more complex than traditional mechanized systems, and that various challenges remain to be addressed.

Although numerous automation solutions have been developed in the market, significant gaps remain between the current level of agricultural robot development and the actual needs of the sector. One of the major barriers to the widespread adoption of automation and robotics in agricultural enterprises is the high cost of system installation. The substantial investment required by farmers to develop advanced robotic systems creates a serious bottleneck in this sector. Moreover, the integration of robots into agriculture should not be considered from a technical perspective; political, social, cultural, and security dimensions must also be considered. This highlights the need for agricultural robots to be evaluated through a comprehensive approach that considers both social acceptance and regulatory frameworks26–29.

Energy efficiency is becoming increasingly important in modern agricultural technologies, especially as sustainability concerns grow. Recent research has focused on cutting fossil fuel use and lowering greenhouse gas emissions, mainly by promoting electric-powered machinery and the use of renewable energy sources30. Over the past few years, a number of autonomous agricultural vehicles and robot prototypes have been developed to run on electricity. These prototypes often feature control systems that help to manage energy use more effectively31. Tests with electric robots in real vineyard conditions have shown that these systems can work in practice and not just in theory. Overall, the shift toward electrification appears to offer real benefits for reducing costs and improving environmental outcomes in agriculture.

Advanced control systems and sensing technologies must ensure that autonomous agricultural machines can operate precisely and reliably under complex field conditions. To address this issue, a new control design based on a simple neural network has been introduced. It separates reference inputs from real-world outputs and has shown much better results than older control systems, even up to 20 times better in some cases32. This method seems to make agricultural robots tougher against disturbances they face while working. Also, the AroPWEC platform includes smart subsystems that help robots tell weeds apart by using automatic guidance and spectral analysis. By mixing deep learning with multi-objective control, this system tries to make precision farming tasks more efficient33. In addition to control improvements, thanks to image processing methods, robots can now move through crop rows by themselves and make smart decisions when they run into obstacles. For finding their way, they use an extended Boundary Node Method along with a modified Lookahead-based Line of Sight algorithm, which helps them stay on track even if there are unexpected objects34. These kinds of improvements are really important in precision farming, especially for tasks like monitoring fields and spraying crops. Also, combining information from odometers and IMU sensors with a Kalman filter makes it much easier for robots to draw accurate paths, which is key for careful navigation and task performance35.

Multi-robot systems, which coordinate the actions of several robots and maintain safe human–robot interaction, are becoming an important area of research in agricultural automation. In these smart farming setups, different robots are designed to work smoothly together while keeping in touch with human operators. Because of this, the integration of robotic systems, autonomous vehicles, energy-efficient designs, and smarter control techniques has become essential for improving crop yields, making better use of resources, and supporting sustainability.

This study aims to contribute to the development of agricultural technologies by addressing challenges related to agricultural robots, autonomous vehicles, energy efficiency, and control systems36,37. It focuses on enhancing wheel traction while reducing battery energy consumption. To achieve this, the Tillage and Energy Control System (TECS), is proposed, integrating a slip-based optimization algorithm supported by a hybrid PID and fuzzy inference system (FIS). The proposed system dynamically adapts to different soil conditions in real time, ensuring effective traction control and efficient energy management across various terrains.

Interaction of soil and agrobot-traction analysis

The central aim of tillage optimization is to maximize the Agrobot’s traction during soil cultivation with the cultivator, thereby enhancing traction efficiency for energy savings. To accomplish this, an initial step involves analyzing the interaction dynamics between the vehicle and the soil, the cultivator and the soil, and the cultivator and the vehicle under varying soil hardness and structural conditions. The insights gained from this analysis will be used to develop a robust model that allows robots to be utilized for soil identification and control.

Existing literature contains extensive research on traction control models for conventional electric vehicles. Approaches to understanding wheel-ground interaction are broadly classified as either analytical or empirical. The analytical method focuses on the mathematical analysis of the contact area between the wheel and the ground. In this study, the analytical approach considers the mathematical relationship between the Agrobot’s drawbar pull and the ground contact area, factoring in the cultivator’s tillage characteristics. A key focus within this analysis is the mathematical correlation between shear stress and normal stress. Within the analytical equations, variables are determined by considering the physical properties of soil and stone particles, as well as the degree of soil hardness.

The analytical method provides the benefit of enabling the prediction of a tool-ground interaction model. However, its practical implementation in traction control necessitates the real-time prediction or measurement of numerous variables to effectively regulate wheel torque. While the analytical approach offers an advantage in formulating a wheel-ground interaction model, it also requires the real-time measurement or estimation of several parameters for effective wheel torque control38. This real-time data acquisition, however, can be challenging to achieve. Furthermore, dynamic models are employed to manage wheel torque under diverse off-road conditions39.

The empirical method represents an alternative approach to modeling vehicle-ground interaction, commonly employed in studies involving mobile robots and agricultural applications. This method involves deriving an experimental curve model through the use of exponential equations. Despite its simplicity, the empirical approach provides highly accurate estimations of vehicle traction. Notably, the results obtained using the empirical method are consistent with the Brixius model, which is particularly well-suited for real-time operation of wheeled robots. The Brixius model offers an advantage over the analytical method due to its reliance on a smaller number of variables. Consequently, this study employs the Brixius model to develop an optimized, energy-efficient traction model. While the traditional Brixius model relies on wheel sinkage and soil type as key parameters for assessing traction performance, this study did not consider the analysis of soil structure. Instead, a parameter termed the "soil mobility index" was developed based on the specific soil types utilized in the research.

Energy efficiency is a critical concern in the field of mobile robotics. The substantial weight and cost of battery systems impose significant constraints on robot performance, making effective energy management essential for accurately predicting operational lifespan and range. Consequently, analyzing energy consumption to devise energy-saving strategies is paramount. In this context, mathematical modeling of mobile robot energy consumption offers a valuable theoretical framework40. Initially, robots can employ sensors and energy measurement systems to quantify the energy demands of tillage operations and the consumption of individual components. This data then enables dynamic adjustments to energy usage based on working conditions, facilitating energy conservation. Moreover, this information allows for estimations of the robot’s energy autonomy for upcoming tasks. Prior research on energy consumption has explored minimization strategies through mechanical design, trajectory planning, wheel traction control, and slip optimization. However, some studies investigating vehicle-soil interaction have overlooked the significant role of traction in energy efficiency41–44.

This study introduces an energy model for optimized vehicle traction, leveraging the Brixius model based on the soil mobility index (Bn). This approach aims to unlock potential energy savings and offer benefits such as enhanced tillage performance. The central objective is to minimize energy consumption while sustaining maximum traction power across diverse soil types. To achieve this, the research focused on developing a control methodology that circumvents the need for a t-immersion estimator and a dynamic parameter observer.

Official drawbar tests for tractor performance have traditionally been conducted on hard surfaces like concrete. While these tests offer a standardized basis for comparing tractors, they provide limited insight into real-world field performance. A key distinction between these controlled tests and actual field conditions lies in the behavior of the tires or other traction mechanisms. Therefore, accurately predicting field performance necessitates a thorough understanding of traction performance. When combined with fundamental data from official tests, traction equations offer a foundation for forecasting how a tractor will perform in the field. Computer models serve as valuable tools for researchers and designers, enabling them to investigate various aspects of tractor performance, refine tractor designs, optimize operational parameters, and improve the matching of tractors with implements. These models allow for the assessment of the relative importance of different factors affecting a tractor’s field performance without the need for costly and time-consuming field trials. Developed models enhance the ability to comprehend and compare the diverse parameters influencing tractor performance. Furthermore, these models can assist tractor operators in refining and optimizing their equipment settings based on specific operating conditions45.

Traction parameters

Traction performance is described using five dimensionless parameters: slip ratio, net traction ratio, traction efficiency, gross traction ratio, and motion resistance ratio46. To develop interactive models for robot traction and energy efficiency, an experimental vehicle prototype and a cultivator model were utilized, and the interaction between the vehicle and the ground was analyzed.

This study employed the traditional Brixius model (TBM) as its basis. The TBM was adapted into a function of wheel slip ratio (s) and soil mobility index (Bn) to derive simplified equations related to the cone index (CI) under varying soil hardness and structural conditions. Subsequently, an analysis was performed for the Agrobot using the aforementioned traction parameters. Control strategy

The proposed wheel control algorithm, the Tillage and Energy Control System (TECS), is structured into three main phases. Initially, the vehicle’s target speed is compared to the actual wheel speeds, and any discrepancy is fed into a PID controller to ensure a stable system startup and balanced vehicle speed. Subsequently, the soil mobility index, determined by the soil type, and an optimized slip value, calculated to balance traction and energy efficiency, are computed within the control system. This slip optimizer dynamically determines the ideal slip ratio based on the Agrobot’s changing speed, considering both energy conservation and traction requirements. The soil mobility index is numerically estimated in real-time through robot states and wheel-ground interaction models. This calculation is based on Brixius’s basic parameters. In addition, the values accepted as standard by the American Society of Agricultural and Biological Engineers (ASABE) were selected for the calculations made in the studies on the subject in the literature for three soil types. Secondly, the optimized desired slip ratio is used as the input of the slip controller based on the PID control method. The output of the PID controller controls the wheels of a robot to follow the desired slip rate. Finally, a fuzzy logic slip modulator was applied to the slip controller to resist the load distortions on the wheel caused by the change in the soil spectrum. The slippage regulator system automatically adjusts the PID gains of the controller so that the fuzzy logic slippage controller performs better to follow a desired value. TECS works simultaneously for the right and left wheels of the Agrobot. The system block diagram is presented in Fig. 4. Figure 4 illustrates the overall control architecture of TECS. The system consists of three main components: an initial speed stabilization module using a PID controller, a slip optimization module based on soil mobility index estimation, and a fuzzy logic-based slip modulation system for adaptive control. This structure ensures optimal traction and energy efficiency across varying soil conditions.

This study employs a Fuzzy Logic Controller within TECS to achieve a balance between traction efficiency and slip control, adapting to varying terrain conditions. Performance evaluations of tillage and wheel control were conducted across three distinct soil conditions: soft, medium-hard, and hard soil with gravel, as illustrated in Fig. 7. These experiments took place at a dedicated field established at Afyon Kocatepe University, Afyon Vocational High School. The soft soil area was prepared by tilling it ten times with a tiller and then mixing in additional soft soil to optimize it for testing agricultural equipment like harrows. Approximately 150–200 kg of additional soft soil was uniformly spread over the area and thoroughly blended with the existing soil using mechanical rotary tillage equipment. After each test session, the soil was re-leveled and reconditioned to maintain consistent testing conditions for subsequent experiments. The medium-hard soil area was created by hoeing the existing soil three times. For the hard soil condition, a 10 cm layer of pebbles was applied, and the soil was further compacted using log stones to achieve the desired hardness.

The prototype of the unmanned agricultural vehicle, Agrobot, was tested in the area shown in Fig. 8. A crucial parameter within TECS is the wheel slip rate, which is essential to estimate in real time by determining the difference between the vehicle’s linear speed and the wheels’ rotational speed. To achieve this, the rear wheels of the system are equipped with encoders, enabling accurate measurement of the system’s actual speed. The speed of each wheel was calculated using data from their respective motor encoders. Data acquisition and processing were performed using a Linux OS-based ROS, which manages the CAN Bus communication between the Agrobot, its sensors, and the wheel controller.

Agrobot test area.

The performance of the proposed control system on the unmanned agricultural vehicle, Agrobot, was analyzed separately across three distinct soil types. Data on speed, traction power, energy consumption, and acceleration were collected from the Agrobot system as the soil conditions varied. During the experimental tests, a target speed of 2 m/s was input into the controller system. This speed value was adjusted based on the Agrobot’s body speed, and wheel speed information was also used to calculate the slip rate. Velocity graphs present both the raw measured velocity data and the velocity data after the application of a Kalman filter. In these graphs, the raw measured values are displayed above, and the filtered data is shown below, with the Kalman filter’s Q value set to 0.005 and the R value set to 20. The Kalman filter parameters were empirically tuned through preliminary trials conducted under various soil conditions. Here, Q denotes the process noise covariance, capturing uncertainties in the system’s internal dynamics, while R represents the measurement noise covariance, reflecting inaccuracies in sensor readings. The primary objective was to attenuate high-frequency noise in encoder data without compromising the real-time responsiveness of the control algorithm. The selected Q and R values were found to provide a practical trade-off between signal smoothing and system agility.

Conclusion

This study introduced a novel Tillage and Energy Control System (TECS) designed to optimize traction power and improve energy efficiency in agricultural mobile robots. The developed algorithm dynamically adjusts vehicle speed by minimizing wheel slip based on changes in the soil spectrum, thereby optimizing energy consumption. Experimental results obtained from the Agrobot across various soil conditions demonstrated the significant benefits of the implemented hybrid PID-FIS controller in enhancing both traction efficiency and energy utilization. By effectively determining optimal speed and traction values based on soil hardness and slip ratios, TECS offers potential energy savings in extensive agricultural operations. Tests conducted on soft, medium-hard, and hard soil types showed the proposed system’s superior performance in speed regulation and energy efficiency compared to conventional PID control. Notably, while the PID controller struggled to consistently reach desired reference speeds, the BM PID controller successfully achieved target values, providing more stable control. The development of the Agrobot involved the integration of mechanical, electronic, and software components into a modular architecture. Overall, this research has demonstrated the effectiveness of the TECS algorithm in improving traction efficiency and energy conservation for agricultural robots operating on diverse soil types. The findings underscore the value of adaptive control strategies, particularly fuzzy logic-based controllers, in enhancing robot stability and reducing power consumption in agricultural settings.

While this study demonstrated the efficacy of the TECS algorithm, certain limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, the experimental validation, although covering three distinct soil types, did not fully encompass the complexity and dynamism of real-world farming environments. Future research should incorporate a broader spectrum of soil conditions, including variations in moisture content and irregular terrain, to further refine the control model for applications in agricultural lands. Secondly, the Agrobot’s current control system relies on predefined parameters for slip estimation. Integrating machine learning techniques could significantly enhance its adaptability to real-time soil variations encountered in the diverse agricultural landscapes.

Future research directions could focus on incorporating Real-Time Kinematic (RTK)/GPS systems for precise navigation and route planning, crucial for efficient farming practices in areas. Furthermore, exploring advanced optimization techniques could enhance the adaptability of the TECS algorithm across a wider range of agricultural tasks prevalent in Türkiye. Expanding the algorithm to incorporate cooperative robotic systems and swarm-based control strategies could also revolutionize large-scale farming automations.
