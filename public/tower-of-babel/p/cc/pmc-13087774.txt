A Time-Driven Activity-Based Costing Analysis of Minor Hand Procedure Room Utilization at a Single Military Institution
Authors: Karolina A. Serhan, George C. Balazs, Nicholas H. Lake, Andrew Henebry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeCreate a time-driven activity-based costing model to quantify the financial and time-savings impact of implementing a minor procedure room to perform four common hand surgeries that were previously performed with the same local-only technique in the operating room.MethodsThis is a retrospective cohort study. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained. Cases were identified using the Military Health System Data Repository via common procedure technology codes for carpal tunnel releases (CTR), deQuervian releases (dQR), trigger finger releases (TFR), and ganglion excisions (DWG). Cases were collected from 01 Jan 2022 to 31 Oct 2024. Personnel-associated costs and time analysis were determined by creating a time-driven activity-based costing model specific to our single military institution.ResultsSixty-nine cases performed in the operating room (OR) met inclusion. Of these, 34 were CTR, costing $182.61 per case, 8 were dQR, costing $145.52 per case, 9 were TFR, costing $219.92 per case, and 18 were DWG, costing $262.37 per case. In comparison, 77 cases performed in minor procedure room (MPR) met inclusion. Of these, 45 were CTR, costing $46.09 per case, 12 were TFR, costing $50.06 per case, 5 were dQR, costing $46.38 per case, and 10 were DWG, costing $60.04 per case. The personnel related cost per case in the MPR environment was significantly less when compared to its OR counterpart. The mean total time spent in the hospital on procedure day for MPR cohort was 69.22 minutes (±18.1), versus 189.52 (±62.69) for OR cohort. The mean patient waiting time for MPR group was 38.17 minutes (±16.89) versus 115.57 (±44.15) for OR group.ConclusionsTransition of local anesthesia hand cases from the OR to MPR at our military institution resulted in a 73.2% reduction in personnel-associated costs, a 63.5% reduction in the overall time patients spend in the hospital on their procedure day, and a 42.9% reduction in personnel required to support a case.Type of study/level of evidenceEconomic and decision analyses III.

The benefits of performing hand surgery in a clinic-based setting are numerous.1 In 2023, the Naval Medical Center Portsmouth hand surgery team transitioned several minor hand surgeries, including carpal tunnel releases (CTR), deQuervian releases (dQR), trigger finger releases (TFR), and dorsal ganglion excisions (DWG), with only local anesthesia to a minor procedure room (MPR) setting with a goal of creating more consistent access to care and a more efficient patient process. Previously, these cases were performed in the standard operating room (OR) with the same local anesthesia method.

Recently, health-care business experts have determined that use of a time-driven activity-based cost (TDABC) model can more accurately capture the essential costs associated with hospital-based procedures and highlight patient-flow efficiency.2 The TDABC approach factors the health care-worker's time spent with patients, multiplied by a ratio of the health-care workers salary, thereby generating highly granular data on personnel-associated costs of procedures, as well as outlining potential time inefficiencies in care.2 TDABC models have been utilized in the arthroplasty literature3,4 and more recently in upper-extremity trauma.5 Given this, we set out to identify the financial and time-savings impact of performing four hand surgeries, CTR, DWG, dQR, and TFR in the MPR versus OR by creating a TDABC model specific to our military institution. Prior studies analyzing the impact of clinic-based procedures within the military have performed so using a universal cost-reporting tool that provides only large-scale data.6,7 Conversely, in this study, by creating a TDABC model specific to MPR versus OR utilization, high-resolution data can reveal workflow inefficiencies, staff utilization patterns, and cost discrepancies.

Materials and Methods

This is a retrospective cohort study. Institutional Review Board approval was secured prior to data collection. Cases were identified using the Military Health System Data Repository database and searched via common procedure technology (CPT) codes for CTR, dQR, TFR, and DWG. OR cases were collected from the time frame between 01 Jan 2022 and 31 Oct 2022, and MPR cases were collected from 01 Jan 2024 to 31 Oct 2024. These timeframes differ as prior to 2024 the MPR was not fully implemented at our institution, and local anesthetic cases were performed almost exclusively in the OR. Following transition to a regular workflow in the office-based setting in early 2024, the majority of CTRs, DWGs, dQR, and TFR were performed in the MPR, with occasional cases still being performed in the OR. These two differing time periods were selected to reflect optimal workflow in each setting. Inclusion criteria were age greater than 18 years, cases performed at Naval Medical Center Portsmouth, primary-only cases, cases performed with local anesthesia only (no anesthesia staff involvement), and CPT codes 64721, 25111, 26055, 25000, and 25110/25112. Cost savings analysis was performed by creating a TDABC model specific to our military institution. The TDABC was based on the amount of time each patient spent in the various phases of care on procedure day and modeled after Kaplan and Anderson.2

Specifically, hospital staff for each phase of care was identified. For MPR cases, each case required one corpsman (HM), one clinic receptionist, one certified surgical technician, and one hand surgeon. The OR required one HM, one preoperative nurse, two OR nurses per case, one certified surgical technician, one postanesthesia care unit nurse, and one hand surgeon. The time the patient spent in each phase of care was determined by using the time stamps captured in their chart: check in time, time in the OR, procedure time out, procedure end, time out of the OR, time patient arrived in postanesthesia care unit (for OR cases only), and discharge time. A capacity-cost rate (monthly salary/average hours worked per month), for each personnel was then determined based on individual salaries. The capacity-cost rate was then multiplied by the amount of time patients spent in each phase of care to determine the total personnel-associated cost for each case (Fig. 1). The collected data were analyzed via descriptive and inferential statistics. Additional variables included demographic data: gender, race, age, and military rank. No imputation was conducted throughout the investigation and all patient records with missing data required for model development were excluded. All statistical analyses were conducted in Microsoft Excel using the Real-Statistics Add-ins package and Data-Analysis Toolpack Add-ins. Statistical tests employed were parametric tests. Unpaired, two-tailed t-tests with unequal variances were used to assess for statistical differences between costs based on environment, OR versus MPR, as well as total time in the hospital, total procedure time, and time patients spent waiting for their procedure. The threshold for statistical significance in all analyses was 0.05. A chi-square test was used to analyze the categorical data and again a P-value of .05 was used as a threshold for statistical significance.

Results

A total of 115 cases matching CPT codes as above were performed in the main OR from 01 Jan 2022 to 31 Oct 2022; 69 of these cases met inclusion criteria. Forty-six cases were excluded as chart review revealed these were volar wrist ganglion excisions or had anesthesia staff present for the case. Based on our TDABC model for the OR, 34 were CTR cases, totaling $6,208.68 ($182.61 per case), 8 were dQR cases totaling $1,163.36 ($145.52 per case), 9 were TFR cases, totaling $1,979.29 ($219.92 per case), and 18 were DWG, totaling $4,722.6 ($262.37 per case). Collectively, all 69 cases totaled $14,073.92, for an average of $202.61 per case. In comparison, from 01 Jan 2024 to 31 Oct 2024, a total of 77 cases were performed in the MPR setting. Based on our model for the MPR, 45 were CTR cases, totaling $2,074.02 ($46.09 per case), 12 were TFR, totaling $600.71 ($50.06 per case), 5 were dQR cases, totaling $231.88 ($46.38 per case), and 10 were DWG, totaling $600.37 ($60.04 per case). Collectively, all 77 cases totaled $3,774.21, for an average of $50.64 per case. The mean total time spent in the hospital on procedure day for the MPR cohort was 69.22 minutes (±18.1), compared to 189.52 minutes (±62.69) for the OR cohort, P < .0001. The mean time spent waiting for the procedure for the MPR group was 38.17 minutes (±16.89) compared to 115.57 minutes (±44.15) for the OR group, P < .0001. Moreover, the mean overall time spent in the OR was 54.93 minutes (±19.96) versus 27.83 minutes (±7.52) in the MPR, P < .0001 (Fig. 2). Use of the MPR required 42.9% fewer personnel than OR cases. The mean procedure time (from incision to closure) across all case types in the OR was 28.26 minutes (±9.28) versus 19.54 minutes (±4.30) for the MPR cohort, P = .159. Discussion

The transition of local anesthesia hand surgery cases from the OR to the MPR at our military institution has resulted in a 73.2% reduction in personnel-associated costs, a 63.5% reduction in the overall time patients spend in the hospital on their procedure day, and a 42.9% reduction in personnel required to support a case, all while maintaining low complication rates. Specifically, the TDABC model illustrates the notable reduction in the demand on clinical support by freeing up OR nursing and OR time for other surgical demands. The time and associated clinical personnel cost savings demonstrated by this TDABC analysis further support the benefits of wide-awake hand surgery in a clinic-based setting and provides highly granular data on the actual time and personnel costs required to complete each phase of care for patients.

Several studies have used TDABC models in orthopedics. The authors argue that these models can better capture health-care costs associated with specific orthopedic procedures, given the granular data provided.8 TDABC models determine costs by unit of time based on salaries of the health-care workers required to complete a procedure.2 In doing this, a TDABC model can, in theory, better capture care complexity and time spent giving direct patient care. This is in contrast to the traditional methods of health-care accounting, where costs are assigned a value based on surveys of estimated work intensity, such as in a relative value unit model or ratio of costs to charges model.8,9 In the military health-care system, neither relative value units nor ratio of costs to charges models are used. Rather an entirely different system is used to reimburse military treatment facilities (MTF) that are not specific to the individual patient as the Military Health System is largely a socialized system of medical care for active-duty members and their families.10,11 Thus, creating TDABC models at MTFs could better aid government officials when analyzing the health-care budget within the larger national defense budget. Moreover, health-care personnel shortages are a growing concern both in civilian medicine and military medicine alike.12 In the military health-care system, personnel shortages are common because of balancing needs of operational demands. Humanitarian or military missions often source medical personnel from individual MTFs, which can create months-long personnel shortages even in large commands. In doing so, OR utilization must decrease given the shortages in support staff, which directly impacts access to care. The granular results of this TDABC study show how personnel time can be saved and how varied clinical personnel can be used in different phases of care for common hand procedures, thus optimizing staff utilization increasing access to care.

The results of this study align with recent publications analyzing the financial impact of performing clinic-based hand procedures. Two other military-specific studies were published analyzing the cost effectiveness of MPR utilization for these four specific cases.6,7 However, in these reports the authors used the Medical Expense and Performance Reporting System (MEPRS) to calculate costs. MEPRS is a standardized cost-estimating protocol designed by the Military Health System that assigns costs based on CPT code and operating environment. Their value-based assessment also included facility costs, in addition to cost of the procedure. Moreover, MEPRS does not specifically account for each member of the health-care team required to complete a procedure, rather it uses assumptions to provide macrolevel cost data incorporating military-specific facility costs and costs commonly assigned in a civilian health-care system. For example, according to MEPRS, a CTR performed in the OR costs $7,386.15 per case, whereas a CTR performed in the MPR costs $1,111.09 per case.6,7 These values are significantly greater than those determined by our TDABC model, which highlights the numerous hidden costs within the Military Health System associated with these procedures. The MEPRS data, however, does not demonstrate workflow patterns nor does it allow for analysis of personnel time associated with completing procedures. Ultimately, these studies also support MPR utilization for minor hand surgeries given the significant reduction in costs. However, these assessments provide macrolevel data and focus on patient reported outcomes as they relate to costs without providing granular details on time and resource utilization, which are highlighted in our study.

TDABC models have been used extensively in the arthroplasty literature to help provide better assessments of costs and reimbursement measures in the ever-changing landscape of Medicare and Medicaid.13,14 Recently, a group from the University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, created an institution-specific TDABC model to assess costs differences and Medicare reimbursement rates associated with open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) of intra- articular and extra-articular distal radius fractures among patients aged >65 years.5 The authors found that three-part intra-articular distal radius fractures were significantly costlier compared to two-part and extra-articular fractures whereas reimbursement did not appropriately cover these costs, resulting in net-losses in performing ORIF for more complex intra-articular fractures.5 Studies such as this highlight the importance of the granular data a TDABC model captures and can provide objective data for surgeons when billing for certain procedures.

Our study does have limitations. Namely, it is retrospective in nature, lending it subject to potential selection bias and information bias, depending on time-records in patient charts. Moreover, the study is at risk of temporal bias as the timeframes with which data were collected for the OR group, all within the year of 2022, versus the MPR group, all within the year of 2024, differ. These timeframes differ as prior to 2024 the MPR was not fully implemented at our institution and local anesthetic cases were performed almost exclusively in the OR. Additionally, this TDABC model only accounts for the costs associated with individuals directly involved in patient care on the day of procedure. Thus, it provides only personnel-associated costs; indirect costs such as costs for the equipment, tray sterilization, OR hospitality staff, and facility overhead, were not factored, thereby underestimating total cost. As previous studies have demonstrated, the comprehensive cost savings of performing these surgeries in the MPR versus OR is far greater.6,7 Finally, this TDABC model is specific to a military institution, particularly with respect to the costs calculated. These are based on the salaries of military personnel which differ compared to the civilian health-care system. However, the methods used to create the TDABC and assign value to each unit of time can be replicated and adapted to reflect a civilian center.

Ultimately, this study quantifies the considerable personnel-associated cost-savings of transitioning local anesthesia hand surgery from the main OR to the MPR, while also maintaining low complication rates and significantly reducing the time patients spend in the hospital on the day of procedure. The novelty of this study however comes from the use and development of a TDABC model specific to hand surgery within a military health-care system. The TDABC model uniquely identifies the time-associated costs of these hand surgeries at a granular level and reveals inefficiencies in patient care in real-time by calculating costs based on time and personnel required to coordinate and perform the procedure.

Future areas of research can use a TDABC model to analyze the impact of performing surgeries in the ambulatory surgery setting versus a hospital-based OR. While cost assessments in the military vary, personnel shortages are universal and based on operational tempo. The value of TDABC is that it can better characterize the personnel-time savings, which are imperative to delivery of care in the military setting, and in that sense, are well suited for military based care. As health-care providers and surgeons strive to improve patient care, the TDABC model serves as a useful and powerful tool to track flow of care, and identify more accurate costs per unit of time for resources required, all while allowing for workflow inefficiencies to be exposed.
