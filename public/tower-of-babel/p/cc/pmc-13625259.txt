Traditional medicinal bath plants along the Beibu Gulf coast of Guangxi, China
Authors: Tingting Li, Qianyun Wang, Binsheng Luo, Dipak Khadka, Kedao Lai, Renchuan Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedicinal baths are a widely practiced traditional health maintenance method in southern China, yet systematic ethnobotanical studies from coastal regions remain limited. The Beibu Gulf coastal area of Guangxi is a biologically and culturally diverse region where such practices are actively maintained. This study aims to document the plant species used in traditional medicinal baths and to characterize their composition, usage patterns, and regional variation.MethodsEthnobotanical interviews were conducted at 20 sites across Beihai, Qinzhou, and Fangchenggang City, which comprise the Beibu Gulf. A total of 135 informants (62 males and 73 females) were interviewed in medicinal markets and traditional villages. Data on plant species, plant parts used, and medicinal applications were recorded. Quantitative analyses, including Jaccard Index (JI), cumulative contribution analysis, and Gini coefficient, were applied to assess species similarity and usage patterns.ResultsA total of 286 plant species used for medicinal baths were recorded. These plants belong to 92 families and 218 genera. Fabaceae, Lamiaceae, Rubiaceae, and Asteraceae were the dominant families, with monotypic genera prevalent. Herbs were the most common life form (99 species, 34.62%). Branches, leaves, and whole plants accounted for 71.66% of use. The main applications of these plants in medicinal bath practices were for traumatic injuries, rheumatic diseases, and skin conditions (58.50%). Species richness was highest in Fangchenggang (249 species), followed by Qinzhou (238) and Beihai (144), with 133 shared species. Higher similarity was observed between Fangchenggang and Qinzhou (JI = 0.72). The top 10, 20, 30, 50, and 100 species contributed 10.24%, 18.16%, 25.10%, 37.47%, and 62.25% of total usage frequency, respectively, with a Gini coefficient of 0.383.ConclusionsThe medicinal bath plant system in the Beibu Gulf coastal region is characterized by high species richness and a balanced structure of commonly used and supplementary species. While a shared core flora exists, local differences among cities in the Beibu Gulf are also evident. This study provides comprehensive ethnobotanical data from southern coastal China and offers a regional case for understanding plant selection and functional convergence in medicinal bath practices.

Introduction

Plants have long been used by humans for diverse livelihood purposes, including their role in traditional healthcare systems. In many regions, plants are integral to practices such as fumigation and medicinal bathing [1–3]. Compared with general external herbal applications, medicinal baths and related steam bath practices are not simply based on the use of a single plant or a single therapeutic purpose. Rather, they represent integrated practices that have developed within specific living environments and health needs, involving plant selection, compatibility, and empirical correspondences between these practices and bodily conditions [2, 3]. Existing studies indicate that such practices are widespread across many regions of the world and are commonly associated with postpartum recovery, rheumatic conditions, skin disorders, and routine health maintenance [4].

Medicinal bath practices have been documented across diverse cultural contexts worldwide. Ethnobotanical studies, particularly from Asia and the Americas, indicate that these practices are typically characterized by well-defined application contexts, relatively stable plant selections, and corresponding usage practices. For example, among the Yao ethnic group in China, 110 plant species have been recorded for medicinal bath use, covering applications such as rheumatism, skin diseases, injuries, and gynecological conditions, reflecting clearly defined therapeutic contexts [2]. In Suriname, van ‘t Klooster et al. documented 349 plant species used in different types of medicinal baths and noted that, despite variation in species composition among communities, these practices exhibit consistent typological and structural characteristics [5]. Studies from French Guiana further show that herbal baths remain widely practiced in contemporary society and are closely associated with local body care and folk medical systems, demonstrating the persistence of these practices across cultural settings [6]. Overall, these findings suggest that medicinal bath plants are not incidental elements within general medicinal plant use, but constitute a distinct category of plant-based practices with independent ethnobotanical significance.

In China, medicinal bathing is also closely related to external therapies in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) and local folk medicine. Chinese herbal bath therapy usually involves adding herbal decoctions or extracts to warm water for bathing, soaking, or washing, and has been used as an external treatment for conditions such as pruritus, skin disorders, rheumatic pain, and other chronic discomforts [7–9]. Historical and clinical literature indicates that herbal bath therapy has long been used in China and remains a commonly reported complementary external therapy in contemporary TCM practice [7–9].

In southern China, medicinal baths have long been a common daily health practice, often associated with specific seasonal festivals. According to our fieldwork over the past two years, the Hakka people of Fujian still practice bathing in water boiled with mugwort (Artemisia argyi) during the Dragon Boat Festival. Meanwhile, existing folk customs indicate that some areas in Guangdong commonly use water boiled with mugwort, calamus (Acorus calamus), and peach tree (Prunus persica) branches and leaves for bathing during the Dragon Boat Festival to ward off evil spirits and impurities, while also providing practical skin-care benefits. However, compared to this widespread folk practice, ethnobotanical case studies of medicinal bath plants in China remain relatively limited. Currently reported cases are mainly concentrated in areas inhabited by ethnic minorities, such as the Yao people in southern China [2, 10].

The Beibu Gulf coastal region of Guangxi is located in southern China and mainly includes Beihai, Qinzhou, and Fangchenggang [11]. Located in the southern coastal area of the country, this region has a warm and humid climate and relatively abundant plant resources, providing a natural foundation for the diverse plant utilization practices of the local community [11]. Existing research indicates that the Beibu Gulf coastal area and its surrounding regions preserve a relatively rich knowledge of traditional plant utilization. For example, ethnobotanical surveys of communities surrounding the Fangcheng Golden Camellia National Nature Reserve have recorded numerous medicinal plants and related utilization experiences, demonstrating that this coastal region still retains a relatively complete system of local medicinal plant knowledge [12]. Furthermore, previous research on traditional edible and medicinal plants in the Beibu Gulf region has also revealed that the utilization of local plants is not only reflected in the selection of specific species and their uses, but is also deeply embedded in daily diet, physical conditioning, and local cultural practice [13]. In addition to the use of plants, research on medicinal uses of marine animals among the Jing ethnic group further illustrates that traditional medicine knowledge in the Beibu Gulf region has distinct local characteristics and high research value [14]. However, despite some existing research on medicinal plants and their local uses in Guangxi, systematic ethnobotanical studies on traditional bathing plants in the Beibu Gulf coastal region remain relatively insufficient, and this study therefore conducted a systematic ethnobotanical investigation in the Beibu Gulf coastal region.

Based on this background, this study focuses on traditional medicinal bath plants along the Beibu Gulf coast of Guangxi. The objectives were to document the plant taxa and associated local knowledge used in medicinal bathing, analyze their taxonomic composition, resource sources, plant parts used, perceived functions, use-frequency patterns, and conservation status, and examine similarities and differences in plant composition among Fangchenggang, Qinzhou, and Beihai. By further comparing these findings with existing records of medicinal bath plants from other regions of China, this study seeks to provide a regional case for understanding how medicinal bath practices are shaped by local plant resources, everyday health needs, and different ecological and cultural settings.

Methods

Study area

Fieldwork was conducted in Fangchenggang, Qinzhou, and Beihai, three prefecture-level administrative cities located in the Beibu Gulf coastal region of Guangxi, China, where elevation is generally near sea level (Fig. 1). The specific study sites are listed in Table 1. The area lies in the southern subtropical coastal zone, with coastal wetlands, mangroves, agricultural landscapes, and villages, exhibiting significant characteristics of a coastal-agrarian composite ecosystem [15]. Among these, the mangroves along the Beibu Gulf coast are an important component of the regional ecosystem [15]. The populations of Fangchenggang, Qinzhou, and Beihai are approximately 1.03 million, 4.20 million, and 1.85 million, respectively [16–18]. Han people form the majority population in the study area, while Zhuang, Yao, Jing, and other ethnic groups are also present, with differences among the three cities. Fangchenggang has a relatively diverse ethnic composition, Qinzhou includes mainly Han, Zhuang, and Yao communities, and Beihai is predominantly Han [16–18]. These areas are predominantly Chinese-speaking, with Yue and Hakka as the main language varieties; in minority communities, Zhuang, Yao, and Jing languages are also spoken. The religious landscape of the region is constituted by institutionalized religions which include Buddhism, Taoism, and Catholicism, and a diverse folk belief, such as maritime folk beliefs centered on Mazu (Goddess of the Sea), General Fubo, and the Jing people’s sea gods are particularly prominent, widely distributed in coastal fishing villages and Jing communities, reflecting a cultural identity rooted in maritime livelihoods [19].

Ethnobotanical data collection

This study was conducted from January 2023 to December 2025. Fieldwork was carried out by the research team at the survey sites, with assistance from local guides. Before the surveys, the research objectives, data collection content, and intended use of the data were clearly explained to all respondents. This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangxi Institute of Chinese Medicine and Pharmaceutical Science. Interviews and sample recording were conducted only after obtaining oral informed consent from the respondents. During the surveys, the wishes of respondents were respected, and information involving personal privacy or deemed unsuitable for public disclosure was not recorded or reported. All data collection, processing, and use followed the general ethical principles of ethnobotanical research as outlined by the International Society of Ethnobiology [20]. A combination of semi-structured interviews, key informant interviews, market surveys, and participant observation was employed [21]. Respondents mainly included medicinal herb dealers, collectors of medicinal bath plants, local knowledge holders, and residents with long-term experience in their use [10]. Key informants were selected based on their familiarity with local medicinal bath plants and related practices, including medicinal herb dealers, collectors of medicinal bath plants, local knowledge holders, and residents with long-term experience in medicinal bath use. The interviews were then further expanded through on-site referrals and a snowball sampling approach. The interviews primarily focused on documenting local names, uses, plant parts used, preparation methods, compatibility, resource sources, and usage experiences of medicinal bath plants [10]. In general, the recorded medicinal bath plants were prepared by boiling fresh or dried plant materials in water, and the resulting decoction was used for external bathing, washing, or soaking. Market surveys recorded the commercial forms of plant materials, sales practices, and sellers’ instructions on usage. For species with complex information or inconsistent responses, data were cross-checked through repeated verification across different survey sites to improve reliability.

Furthermore, participant observation was used to document the handling and use of medicinal bath plants in real-life contexts, including the procurement of materials, processing of fresh or dried products, preparation of compound formulations, and usage scenarios. This approach helped to better interpret experiential knowledge described by respondents during interviews.

In total, 135 individuals were interviewed in the study area (62 males and 73 females). Among them, 55 were key informants, while the remaining 80 were general respondents selected through snowball sampling from markets and villages. The ages of respondents ranged from 21 to 82 years.

Plant collection and identification

During the investigation, medicinal plants mentioned by respondents and for which physical samples could be obtained were collected or photographed. For plant materials sold in the market, voucher specimens were collected with the consent of the vendors. For species where living plants could be found in the wild or around villages, on-site collection was conducted based on respondents’ identification. During collection, information such as habitat, resource source, and utilization status was recorded as much as possible. Collected samples were processed into voucher specimens or preserved as investigation materials, and identified based on morphological characteristics, relevant floras, and taxonomic literature. The voucher specimens were deposited in the Herbarium of the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region Institute of Traditional Chinese Medicine (GXMI).

Data analysis

To describe the distribution pattern of the frequency of use of traditional medicinal bath plants among different species in the Beibu Gulf coastal area of Guangxi, this study added a cumulative contribution analysis to the conventional frequency statistics. All medicinal bath plants were sorted from highest to lowest frequency of use, and the cumulative frequency and its proportion of the total frequency for each ranking position were calculated. Based on this, a cumulative contribution curve was plotted. This analysis draws on the basic idea of the Lorenz curve to determine whether the frequency of use is relatively evenly distributed among different species or relatively concentrated in a few high-frequency species; the greater the deviation of the curve from the uniform line, the more uneven the distribution [22–24]. The total number of medicinal bath plant species is denoted as n. The Gini coefficient is a commonly used general indicator of distribution concentration; its value typically ranges between 0 and 1. A value closer to 0 indicates a more even distribution, while a value closer to 1 indicates a more concentrated distribution [22–24].

Furthermore, to compare the similarity of traditional medicinal bath plant compositions in different coastal areas of the Beibu Gulf in Guangxi, this study calculated pairwise Jaccard similarity coefficients between regions based on the lists of medicinal bath plants for each region, and then created similarity heatmaps to demonstrate the degree of similarity in the composition of medicinal bath plants in different regions [25, 26].

The value of J remains between 0 and 1, with higher values indicating more similar plant composition between two regions [25, 26]. Ailment categories targeted by medicinal bath practices were classified into eleven groups, with minor modifications based on the local context [27]. This classification was used to calculate the number of plant species used in each category.

The conservation status of the recorded plant species was assessed using the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species (https://www.iucnredlist.org/). In addition, a conservation priority framework was developed for 20 vulnerable species and one near-threatened species. A threat score was constructed following [28], with modifications, based on seven variables, including habitat type, distribution range, life form, ailment categories, plant parts used, IUCN conservation status, and relative frequency of citation. The conservation threat index was calculated using the R program [29].

Results

Taxonomic composition of medicinal bath plants

A total of 286 species of traditional medicinal bath plants, belonging to 92 families and 218 genera, as well as their related knowledge, were recorded in the Beibu Gulf coastal area of Guangxi (Table 2). The taxonomic distribution is presented in Table 3. At the family level, Fabaceae contained the highest number of species (21 species), followed by Lamiaceae and Rubiaceae (17 species each), while Asteraceae and Euphorbiaceae included 16 and 10 species, respectively. At the genus level, Ardisia had the highest species richness (6 species), followed by Clerodendrum (5 species), while Heptapleurum, Melastoma, and Syzygium each comprised 4 species.

Life forms and conservation status

The traditional medicinal bath plants recorded are predominantly herbaceous, with 99 species (34.62%), followed by shrubs 76 (26.57%), trees 68 species (23.78%), and vines 43 species (15.03%).

Traditional medicinal bath plants in the Beibu Gulf coastal area of Guangxi are mainly wild resources, 246 (86.01%), with only 26 cultivated species (9.09%) and semidomesticated 14 (4.9%) of the total species recorded. This distribution indicates a clear predominance of wild-sourced plants within the recorded medicinal bath plants.

Parts of plants used in medicinal bath

The plant parts used in medicinal baths were dominated by foliage, accounting for 124 species (40.39%), followed by whole plants (96 species, 31.27%) (Fig. 2). Stems, including running stems, tubers, and rhizomes, were used in 27 species (8.80%), while leaves and roots were recorded in 24 (7.82%) and 23 species (7.49%), respectively. Other plant parts, including bark (inner and outer), root bark, pulp, flowers, fruits, vines, and branches, were less frequently used, accounting for 13 species (4.23%). These results indicate that medicinal bath practices in the study area predominantly rely on aerial plant parts, particularly foliage and whole plants.

Ailment categories treated by medicinal bath plants

The therapeutic uses of traditional medicinal bath plants in the Beibu Gulf coastal region of Guangxi are relatively concentrated, primarily involving injuries from falls and blows, rheumatic conditions, and skin diseases (Table 4). These three categories were reported 140, 133, and 109 times, respectively, accounting for 58.50% of the total frequency and forming the core of local medicinal bath practices. Sedative-related uses and treatments for the common cold were also frequently mentioned, with 79 and 63 occurrences, accounting for 12.10% and 9.65%, respectively. In contrast, categories such as pediatric conditions, gynecological problems, heat-clearing, inflammation, and vascular conditions were reported less frequently.

This distribution indicates that medicinal bath practices in the region are primarily oriented toward musculoskeletal discomfort, rheumatic symptoms, and skin-related conditions, reflecting their close relevance to common health concerns in daily life. In practice, the use of medicinal bath plants typically involves combinations of multiple species rather than reliance on single plants, with respondents emphasizing that different plants contribute distinct properties, aromas, and perceived effects. Formulations are also adjusted according to user groups, with differences observed between adults and children in terms of strength, dosage, and ingredient selection, and between men and women in relation to physical condition and common health concerns.

For example, in local bathing formulas commonly used to relieve fatigue and promote blood circulation, men often used plants such as Aporosa octandra, Psychotria asiatica, Clausena lansium, Ilex hainanensis, Lygodium japonicum, and Microcos paniculata, whereas women more frequently used Blumea balsamifera, Uncaria sinensis, Vitex negundo var. cannabifolia, Liquidambar formosana, Litsea cubeba, and Bischofia polycarpa. Although these gender-related formulations differed markedly in plant composition, they were locally perceived to serve similar functions, such as relieving fatigue, improving circulation, and alleviating bodily discomfort. These patterns suggest that local medicinal bath practices have developed flexible compatibility strategies tailored to different users and contexts through long-term use.

Cross-regional comparison of medicinal bath plants

A total of 133 medicinal bath plant species were shared across the three cities, including Fangchenggang, Qinzhou, and Beihai. The highest number of species was recorded in Fangchenggang (249 species), followed by Qinzhou (238 species) and Beihai (144 species). Pairwise comparison of species composition based on the Jaccard Index (JI) showed the highest similarity between Fangchenggang and Qinzhou (JI = 0.721), followed by Qinzhou and Beihai (JI = 0.572), while the lowest similarity was observed between Fangchenggang and Beihai (JI = 0.523) (Fig. 3).

Frequency distribution and core species of medicinal bath plants

Cumulative contribution analysis showed that the use frequency of traditional medicinal bath plants in the Beibu Gulf coastal region of Guangxi was not highly concentrated in a small number of species, but rather distributed across a wide range of plant resources. The top 10, 20, 30, 50, and 100 species contributed 10.24%, 18.16%, 25.10%, 37.47%, and 62.25% of the total usage frequency, respectively. Among these, Artemisia indica, Rhus chinensis var. roxburghii, Liquidambar formosana, Vitex negundo, Lygodium japonicum, Melicope pteleifolia, and Clerodendrum cyrtophyllum showed relatively high frequencies, forming a prominent group of commonly used species in local medicinal bath practices (Fig. 4).

Most of these high-frequency species are widely distributed and readily available in the Beibu Gulf coastal region, and are also frequently reported in medicinal bath practices or related traditional medicine studies in other regions, suggesting that their frequent use is associated with both accumulated empirical experience and material accessibility. In contrast, the high frequency of Rhus chinensis var. roxburghii reflects a stronger local adaptation. In local traditional medicine, Rhus chinensis var. chinensis is widely recognized for both bathing and other medicinal uses, but its resources are relatively limited in the coastal areas investigated in this study. As a result, local residents often use the closely related R. chinensis var. roxburghii as a substitute. This local substitution is based on their close taxonomic relationship and perceived similarity in efficacy, contributing to the relatively high frequency of R. chinensis var. roxburghii in medicinal bath formulations.

Conservation status

According to the IUCN Red List categories, among the traditional medicinal bath plants recorded in this study, 222 species were classified as Least Concern (LC), 20 as Vulnerable (VU), 3 as Data Deficient (DD), and 1 as Near Threatened (NT) (Table 5). The results show that most medicinal bath plants in the Beibu Gulf coastal region of Guangxi fall into the Least Concern category, indicating a generally low level of conservation risk for the majority of species recorded. Only a small number of species were classified as Vulnerable or Near Threatened, with Illigera aromatica being the only Near Threatened species identified in this study.

Discussions

Characteristics of the traditional medicinal bath plant system in the Beibu Gulf

This study recorded 286 medicinal bath plant species in the Beibu Gulf coastal region of Guangxi, indicating high species diversity together with a distinct structural pattern. This species richness is higher than that reported for medicinal bath plants among traditional Yao communities in China, where 110 species were documented [2]. The richness of recorded species in the Beibu Gulf reflects both the diversity of local plant resources and the long-term continuity of medicinal bathing as an external therapeutic practice, which has maintained a relatively comprehensive foundation of plant utilization. At the family level, a certain degree of concentration is evident, whereas at the genus level, a high proportion of genera are represented by a single species. This pattern suggests that the medicinal bath plant system is not based on a limited set of fixed taxa, but rather has developed through long-term selection from the regional flora. Although herbs predominate, shrubs and trees also account for a substantial proportion, indicating that the sources of medicinal bath plants are diverse and not restricted to a single life form.

The patterns of resource origin, plant parts used, and ailment categories further highlight the practical nature of this system. The predominance of wild resources indicates that traditional medicinal bathing remains closely linked to the surrounding natural environment. Plant use is mainly concentrated on vegetative parts, particularly branches, leaves, and whole plants, which aligns with the practical requirements of bathing applications and reflects a preference for easily accessible materials, suggesting a degree of sustainability in local practices. The ailment categories are largely concentrated on trauma, rheumatic conditions, and skin-related problems, indicating that medicinal bathing is primarily oriented toward addressing musculoskeletal discomfort and common skin conditions in daily life.

The structure revealed by the frequency distribution provides additional insight. Although several species show relatively high frequencies of use, cumulative contribution analysis and the Gini coefficient indicate that overall use is not concentrated in a very small number of species, but rather distributed across a broad range of plants. This pattern suggests that traditional medicinal bath knowledge in the Beibu Gulf coastal region includes both a set of relatively stable, commonly used species and a substantial number of medium- and low-frequency supplementary plants.

The high-frequency species identified in this study deserve further attention because they connect local medicinal bath practice with broader ethnomedicinal and TCM-related knowledge. Several of these plants, such as A. indica, L. formosana, V. negundo, L. japonicum, L. japonicus, and Senecio scandens, or their closely related medicinal materials, have been recorded in Chinese Materia Medica, regional folk medicine, or ethnopharmacological studies. Reported phytochemical studies of these taxa and related groups have identified constituents such as volatile oils, flavonoids, phenolic compounds, terpenoids, and alkaloids, many of which are associated with anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antimicrobial, or skin-related activities [30–34]. These properties correspond broadly with the main local uses recorded in this study, especially for trauma, rheumatic conditions, common cold, and skin-related problems. In addition, the high frequency of R. chinensis var. roxburghii illustrates the role of local substitution in plant selection. Although R. chinensis is widely recognized in traditional medicine, its availability is limited in the Beibu Gulf coastal region; local residents therefore use the closely related R. chinensis var. roxburghii as a substitute, based on perceived similarity in efficacy.

Overall, the medicinal bath plant system in this region represents not a simple assemblage of a few dominant species, but a complex and well-developed knowledge system.

Regional differences and their potential drivers

Although the three coastal cities of the Beibu Gulf share a considerable number of medicinal bath plant species, clear regional differences are evident. Fangchenggang and Qinzhou exhibit the highest similarity in species composition, whereas Beihai shows a more distinct pattern, indicating that medicinal bath plant use does not follow a uniform pattern across the region.

These differences are likely related to variation in plant availability. Fangchenggang and Qinzhou are geographically adjacent and closely connected to mountain systems such as the Shiwan and Liuwan Mountains. In particular, Fangchenggang includes protected areas such as the Shiwan Mountains National Nature Reserve and the Fangcheng Golden Camellia National Nature Reserve, which support relatively high levels of biodiversity. In contrast, Beihai is characterized mainly by coastal plains, plateaus, and low hills, with a comparatively limited mountain forest background and a higher proportion of plantation forests, resulting in lower overall plant diversity. Such ecological differences are likely to influence the availability of medicinal bath plants, contributing to the greater similarity between Fangchenggang and Qinzhou and the differentiation observed in Beihai.

Regional variation may also be shaped by local socio-cultural contexts. Fangchenggang combines coastal and border characteristics and is home to several ethnic groups, including Zhuang, Yao, Miao, and the Jing, the latter forming the only concentrated community of this group in China. In contrast, Beihai is more strongly oriented toward coastal tourism and related development. These differences in social context may further influence patterns of plant knowledge, selection, and use. Taken together, the observed similarity between Fangchenggang and Qinzhou and the greater differentiation in Beihai likely reflect the combined effects of ecological background and socio-cultural factors.

The significance of the Beibu Gulf case in regional comparison

Comparison of this study with existing research on Yao medicinal bath practices reveals a consistent pattern: although the species composition of medicinal bath plants varies considerably across regions, their primary functional orientations remain broadly similar. For example, a study of Yao medicinal baths in Jinping, Yunnan, recorded 110 plant species mainly used for rheumatic conditions, skin diseases, traumatic injuries, and gynecological problems [2]. Research on medicinal baths and Dragon Boat Festival medicinal markets among the Yao in Jianghua, Hunan, further indicates that medicinal bathing is a common therapeutic practice and plays an important role in the transmission of local medical knowledge, with many plant uses also associated with rheumatism and general body conditioning [10, 35]. In the present study, medicinal bath uses in the Beibu Gulf coastal region are likewise concentrated on trauma, rheumatic conditions, and skin-related problems. These parallels suggest that, despite substantial differences in ecological environments, plant availability, and knowledge traditions among the Beibu Gulf coastal region, the mountainous border areas of southern Yunnan, and the Yao regions of southern Hunan, the core health concerns addressed by medicinal bathing remain highly consistent. Regional variation is therefore more strongly reflected in plant selection, whereas the underlying health needs associated with medicinal bath practices appear relatively stable. In this context, the Beibu Gulf case provides not only a new regional example but also additional evidence for understanding functional convergence in medicinal bath plant use across different regions.

Compared with the Yao community-based study in Jinping and the market-focused research in Jianghua, the Beibu Gulf case differs in both scale and survey setting. The Jinping study primarily documents medicinal knowledge within a single community, while the Jianghua study emphasizes the role of markets in knowledge circulation and maintenance. In contrast, the present study covers a coastal region in which markets and villages coexist as interconnected contexts of plant use. As such, the Beibu Gulf material reflects not only a local knowledge base but also a broader pattern of medicinal plant utilization that combines shared foundations with regional variation. Incorporating this case into regional comparisons contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of how local substitution and functional convergence coexist in medicinal plant use.

At the national scale, this pattern also corresponds to the broader logic of TCM external therapies, in which herbal materials are applied to the body surface through bathing, washing, soaking, or fumigation to address musculoskeletal discomfort, skin problems, fatigue, and other common bodily conditions [7–9]. However, the Beibu Gulf case also differs from more standardized TCM-related bath therapies because it is more strongly shaped by coastal environments, wild plant availability, market circulation, and household-level practice. The distinctiveness of the Beibu Gulf medicinal bath tradition is reflected in its local plant repertoire and in the way a broader external-therapy logic is expressed within a coastal ecological and social context.

Plant-based bathing practices in a broader Asian context

In addition to Chinese medicinal bath studies, several Asian studies have recorded plant-based bathing practices. In Southeast Asia, several plant-based bathing practices have been documented that, while related, differ from the Beibu Gulf medicinal baths in preparation, cultural context, and primary users. For instance, Malay mandi serom in Kedah, Malaysia, is a traditional postpartum bath practiced by midwives using multiple plant materials [36]. This practice is closely tied to postpartum care, aiming to support maternal recovery, cleanse the body, restore strength, and provide culturally defined protection during the vulnerable postpartum period. Similar postpartum-oriented bathing and plant-washing practices have also been recorded in Laos [37, 38], emphasizing recovery and care for women in specific life stages. These cases illustrate that plant-based bathing traditions in Asia can serve multiple functions depending on cultural and physiological contexts.

Another related practice is steam-bathing, which highlights further formal distinctions. In North Sumatra, Indonesia, Batak traditional steam-bathing uses heated plant materials and steam, rather than decoction-based immersion [39]. In Minahasa, North Sulawesi, the bakera steam bath is similarly focused on postpartum recovery and involves multiple plant species [40]. Comparable postpartum steam sauna or plant-washing practices have been reported in northern Thailand [41]. In contrast to the Beibu Gulf medicinal baths, these practices emphasize heat and steam exposure rather than direct washing with a plant decoction. This demonstrates that plant-based bathing traditions across Asia encompass a range of techniques, each shaped by local medical concepts, household practices, and community norms. Nonetheless, these diverse practices share a general external therapeutic principle: water, heat, and plant materials interact with the body surface to relieve discomfort, support recovery, cleanse, or provide protection.

From this broader Asian perspective, plant-based bathing practices reveal both considerable diversity in form and consistent convergence in underlying therapeutic logic. Rather than being regarded simply as a catalogue of useful plant species, these practices represent a flexible mode of external therapy, shaped by local ecological conditions, the availability of plant resources, embodied experience, and local understandings of health maintenance [36–41].

Conservation implications

This study documents both wild and cultivated plant species used in medicinal bath practices in Guangxi, China, and identifies a subset of species of conservation concern based on IUCN Red List categories and provincial protection status. Although most recorded species are not currently under high conservation risk, several vulnerable and protected species require targeted attention. Their conservation can be supported through education and awareness programs that promote sustainable use. The main threats to these species are associated with limited distribution ranges and continued demand driven by their perceived efficacy. For example, Liquidambar chingii, an endemic species in China, is valued for its phenolic acids and flavanols with relevance to rheumatic conditions [42], which may increase harvesting pressure. In addition, the use of destructive plant parts, such as roots or whole plants, may further exacerbate resource decline. The conservation relevance of medicinal bath plants should also be evaluated together with the plant parts used. In this study, although foliage was the most frequently used part, whole plants and roots were also recorded for 96 and 23 species, respectively, and such practices may create additional pressure on wild populations even when the species are not currently listed as threatened by the IUCN.

To address these issues, local knowledge should be incorporated into conservation planning and community-level education, encouraging the use of renewable plant parts such as leaves and twigs while regulating the harvesting of roots and whole plants. At the same time, the cultivation of key species should be promoted, provided that it does not negatively affect wild populations or their regeneration.

Limitations and future prospects

This study focuses on the documentation and pattern analysis of traditional medicinal bath plants and associated knowledge in the Beibu Gulf coastal region of Guangxi, based mainly on key informant interviews, market surveys, participant observation, and snowball sampling. This approach is suitable for recording locally recognized plants and use practices, but it cannot fully reveal how medicinal bath knowledge is distributed among different age groups, occupations, or communities. Future studies could combine ethnobotanical documentation with more systematic household surveys to examine how this knowledge is learned, transmitted, and transformed under changing social and economic conditions.

The discussion of efficacy, indications, and use in this study is based primarily on interview data and field observations. These records reflect local experiential knowledge and should not be interpreted as pharmacological or clinical validation. Further phytochemical, pharmacological, dermatological, and safety-related studies are needed for selected high-frequency species and culturally important formulations. Such work would help clarify which local practices have broader application potential and which are better understood within household or community-based contexts.

The future development of medicinal bath plant resources should also be considered in relation to environmental change and the health of humans, plants, and local ecosystems. Most recorded species are wild resources, and some are vulnerable, narrowly distributed, or collected through destructive harvesting of roots or whole plants. In coastal regions such as the Beibu Gulf, habitat change, tourism development, market demand, and shifts in land use may influence both plant availability and the continuity of local knowledge. Medicinal bath plants therefore should not be viewed only as resources for human healthcare, but also as part of a wider relationship among people, useful plants, habitats, and local ecological management.

Future work should further explore how the recorded knowledge can support community-centered practice. Possible directions include community-based inventories of locally important bath plants, participatory mapping of collection areas, identification of species suitable for leaf- or twig-based harvesting, cultivation trials for frequently used or conservation-sensitive species, and locally understandable educational materials on sustainable use. Any development of medicinal bath products or related cultural tourism activities should involve local knowledge holders, respect their consent and preferences, and establish appropriate mechanisms for benefit sharing. In this way, the documentation of medicinal bath plants can contribute not only to academic research, but also to community education, biodiversity conservation, and locally grounded health practices.

Conclusion

This study shows that the Beibu Gulf coastal region of Guangxi harbors a rich diversity of medicinal bath plants, most of which are derived from wild resources. These plants are primarily used for conditions such as traumatic injuries, rheumatic problems, and skin-related disorders, reflecting a clear functional orientation of medicinal bathing as an external practice for addressing limb strain, joint discomfort, and related surface symptoms. Regional comparison indicates that the composition of medicinal bath plants is more similar between Fangchenggang and Qinzhou, while Beihai exhibits a certain degree of differentiation, suggesting that medicinal bath plant use in the Beibu Gulf coastal region is characterized by both a shared foundation and local variation. Frequency distribution analysis further demonstrates that this system is not dominated by a small number of highly used species, but instead consists of a group of commonly used core species together with a large number of medium- and low-frequency supplementary plants. Overall, the use of traditional medicinal bath plants in the Beibu Gulf coastal region of Guangxi reflects both regional continuity and local diversity, and provides additional evidence for understanding the formation and persistence of medicinal bath plant knowledge under different ecological and cultural contexts in southern China.
