Real-world Longitudinal Patterns of Benzodiazepine Use in Korean Patients with First-onset Panic Disorder: A Multicenter Retrospective Study
Authors: Young-Chan Kim, Sang-Woo Hahn, Jin Pyo Hong, Kyoung-Uk Lee, Se Joo Kim, Sang-Hyuk Lee, Jong-Chul Yang, Seung Jae Lee, Seon-Cheol Park, Min-Sook Gim, Jeong-Ho Chae, Ho Jun Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAlthough benzodiazepines (BZDs) are recommended only for short-term adjunctive use in panic disorder, long-term use remains common in clinical practice. This study investigated real-world patterns of BZD prescribing and identified clinical factors associated with BZD dose reduction or discontinuation among BZD-initiating patients with first-onset panic disorder.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective longitudinal study included adults with first-onset panic disorder who initiated BZD treatment at psychiatric outpatient clinics between 2011 and 2023. Changes in BZD prescriptions were assessed at baseline, 3 months, and 12 months. Mixed-effects models were used to evaluate longitudinal changes in BZD dose. Logistic regression analyses examined clinical and treatment-related factors associated with BZD dose reduction or discontinuation at 12 months.ResultsAmong 733 patients who initiated BZD treatment, 32.3% had reduced or discontinued BZDs at 3 months, and 45.0% had done so at 12 months, while only 16.0% achieved complete discontinuation. Mean BZD dose showed no significant change at 3 months but decreased modestly by 12 months. Initial BZD dose was not significantly associated with long-term discontinuation. Alprazolam use was associated with higher odds of dose reduction or discontinuation, whereas comorbid psychiatric disorders and concomitant buspirone use were associated with lower odds. Antidepressant use did not significantly predict BZD tapering outcomes.ConclusionLong-term BZD use frequently persisted among BZD-initiating patients with first-onset panic disorder despite guideline recommendations for short-term adjunctive use. A substantial proportion of patients continue BZDs beyond 12 months in real-world clinical practice, indicating a clear gap between treatment guidelines and actual prescribing patterns.

INTRODUCTION

Panic disorder is characterized by recurrent, unexpected panic attacks, which are sudden surges of intense fear that peak within minutes [1]. It is a common psychiatric condition, with an estimated lifetime prevalence of 1−5% in the general population and 7−10% in primary care settings [1].

Panic disorder is typically managed using a combination of psychological and pharmacological interventions, most commonly selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and benzodiazepines (BZD) [2]. Although SSRIs and serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors are established as standard treatments, their delayed onset of action has led clinicians to consider adjunctive BZD use during the early phase of treatment. One such strategy involves the temporary co-administration of a BZD during the initiation of antidepressant therapy, followed by gradual tapering once the antidepressant effect becomes evident [2,3].

The American Psychiatric Association guideline emphasizes that combining an SSRI with a BZD may accelerate the initial treatment response, while also cautioning against potential difficulties with discontinuation, especially with short-acting agents like alprazolam [4]. Consistent with these considerations, the Korean treatment guidelines recommend BZD treatment for approximately four weeks during the acute phase and advise discontinuation during the maintenance phase in patients with panic disorder without agoraphobia or other psychiatric comorbidities [5].

While BZDs offer rapid anxiolytic effects, their use is associated with adverse effects, including sedation, memory impairment, and an increased risk of falls [6]. Thus, the benefits of their rapid anxiolytic action should be carefully balanced against the risks, particularly the challenges in tapering and discontinuation [7]. Despite these well-documented risks, studies have shown that approximately 14.7−31.4% of BZD users in the United States remain long-term users [8]. Accordingly, developing effective strategies for discontinuing long-term BZD use has become an important issue in clinical practice [9].

Although several guidelines recommend the short-term use of BZD for the treatment of panic disorder, few studies have examined how these agents are prescribed in real-world clinical settings. A prospective longitudinal study conducted in the United States in 2003, which followed 443 patients with panic disorder, found that despite guidelines recommending SSRIs as first-line treatments, the use of SSRIs remained low. Instead, BZDs were the most commonly and persistently prescribed class of medications, highlighting a substantial gap between evidence-based treatment guidelines and real-world clinical practice [10]. This discrepancy appears to stem largely from the continuous and prevalent use of BZDs.

Therefore, this multicenter study aimed to investigate the clinical characteristics and prescribing patterns of Korean patients with first-onset panic disorder who initiated BZD treatment, focusing particularly on the relationship between clinical features and longitudinal changes in BZD use among BZD-initiating patients.

METHODS

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a retrospective longitudinal study with a one-year follow-up of patients with first-onset panic disorder treated between January 2011 and December 2023. Patients who visited psychiatric outpatient clinics and initiated treatment with BZDs were included.

Changes in BZD prescriptions were assessed at the initial visit, 3 months, and 12 months of follow-up.

A total of 12 university-affiliated hospitals participated in this study: Catholic University Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital; Catholic University St. Vincent’s Hospital; Catholic University Uijeongbu St. Mary’s Hospital; Kyungpook National University Hospital; Sungkyunkwan University Gangbuk Samsung Hospital; Sungkyunkwan University Samsung Medical Center; Soonchunhyang University Seoul Hospital; Yonsei University Severance Hospital; Inje University Sanggye Paik Hospital; Inje University Haeundae Paik Hospital; Chonbuk National University Hospital; and Bundang CHA Hospital of CHA University School of Medicine.

This study was approved by the institutional review board of each participating university and hospital (XC17REDI0056).

Patient Selection and Eligibility Criteria

All patients who met the following criteria were included: (1) an International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis assigned by clinical psychiatrists: F40.01 (agoraphobia with panic disorder) or F41.0 (panic disorder without agoraphobia); (2) first-ever presentation of panic symptoms at a psychiatric outpatient clinic; (3) no prior history of psychiatric illness or treatment; (4) panic disorder as the primary diagnosis when comorbid with other psychiatric conditions; and (5) age ≥ 18 years.

Patients were excluded if they had: (1) a comorbid Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition diagnosis of schizophrenia, other psychotic disorders, bipolar disorder, organic psychosis, or dementia; (2) a medical or neurological condition severe enough to interfere with clinical evaluations or study interviews; (3) pregnancy or breastfeeding; or (4) received any psychotherapy other than treatment as usual (e.g., cognitive behavioral therapy).

Data Extraction Procedure

Medical records identified through this search were screened by a researcher at each participating institution (13 psychiatry residents and one psychiatrist). In cases of uncertainty, the records were reviewed and discussed with the principal investigator (HJS). Each patient included in the study was assigned a unique de-identified code.

Sociodemographic and clinical variables were extracted, including age, sex, marital status, current occupation, years of formal education, stressful life events, sleep problems, alcohol use history around symptom onset, duration of illness, comorbid physical or psychiatric disorders, primary panic symptoms, and the type and dose of psychotropic medications. All sociodemographic and clinical variables were obtained through retrospective chart review of electronic medical records at each participating institution. Comorbid psychiatric illness was defined as any secondary psychiatric diagnosis documented by the treating clinician in the medical record, including depressive disorders, other anxiety disorders, and alcohol use disorders. Cases in which missing data were identified for key variables were excluded from the corresponding analyses.

During the study period, 10 different BZD compounds were prescribed to the participants: alprazolam, clonazepam, lorazepam, etizolam, diazepam, clobazam, bromazepam, flurazepam, tofisopam, and ethyl loflazepate.

BZD dose equivalence was calculated based on established clinical references and prior literature [11].

Remission was operationally defined as BZD discontinuation initiated by the treating clinician based on documented clinical judgment that panic symptoms had sufficiently resolved, as recorded in the medical chart. Specifically, cases were classified as “discontinuation due to remission” when the clinician’s progress notes explicitly indicated that BZD cessation was attributable to symptom improvement or resolution. Because no standardized symptom severity scale was routinely administered across all sites, remission status was determined based on the treating clinician’s documented assessment rather than a predefined cutoff score.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed for the demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants. A mixed-effects model for repeated measures (MMRM) with Bonferroni post hoc correction was used to evaluate changes in BZD dose over time, with time as a fixed effect and institution, sex, and age as covariates. This approach was chosen because it uses all available observations under a missing-at-random assumption, thereby providing more reliable longitudinal estimates in the presence of loss to follow-up. Pairwise logistic regression analyses were performed to examine the association between initial BZD dose categories (0 < BZD ≤ 1, 1 < BZD ≤ 2, 2 < BZD ≤ 3, and 3 < BZD) and the likelihood of remission or BZD discontinuation during follow-up. Multivariable logistic regression models were also used to assess the associations between BZD dose reduction and various clinical variables. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.0 (SAS Institute). A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 814 patients with first-onset panic disorder were identified. Among them, 81 patients initiated treatment with antidepressants only. Finally, 733 patients who began treatment with BZDs were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). The demographic and clinical characteristics of these 733 patients are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of the participants was 42.6 years (standard deviation [SD] = 12.5), and more than half were male (52.1%) and employed (55.7%). Most participants (86.6%) had an education level of high school or above. A total of 86 patients (11.7%) were diagnosed with comorbid psychiatric disorders, such as depression, other anxiety disorders, or alcohol use disorder. The mean duration from the onset of panic symptoms to the first outpatient visit was 19.3 weeks (SD = 23.8).

Initial Use of Benzodiazepines

Most participants (92.8%) initiated treatment with a single BZD. Alprazolam (66.3%) was the most frequently prescribed agent, followed by clonazepam (14.7%), lorazepam (5.6%), and etizolam (3.7%). The mean equivalent dose of the initial BZD prescription was 1.01 (SD = 0.63). Initial doses of alprazolam and clonazepam were relatively higher than those of other BZDs. When two or more BZDs were prescribed (7.2%), the total equivalent dose was higher than that for single-agent use. Except for three patients who received BZD monotherapy, most patients received concomitant antidepressants (Table 2).

Change in Benzodiazepine Use over the Treatment Period

By the 3-month follow-up, 47 patients had discontinued treatment before achieving remission (Fig. 1). Among the 686 patients who remained in follow-up at 3 months, 67.7% maintained or increased their BZD dose compared with the initial dose (Fig. 2). Only 8.5% discontinued BZDs, and 23.8% showed a dose reduction. Between 3 months and 12 months, an additional 210 patients were lost to follow-up (Fig. 1). Among the 476 patients who completed the 12-month follow-up, 81.3% continued BZD use at 12 months. Of these, 262 patients (55.0%) maintained or increased their dose compared with baseline, while 89 patients (18.7%) achieved remission or successfully discontinued BZD use during the study period (Fig. 2). Among the 89 patients who discontinued BZDs, 27 (30.3%) were classified as discontinuation due to documented remission, while 62 (69.7%) discontinued for other reasons (Fig. 1).

Benzodiazepine Dose Changes over the Treatment Period

The mean equivalent dose of BZDs (n = 733) gradually decreased over time (Table 3). Using mixed-effects regression models with time as a fixed effect, the mean doses at baseline, 3 months, and 12 months were 1.01 (standard error [SE] = 0.03), 0.98 (SE = 0.03), and 0.87 (SE = 0.03), respectively, showing a significant overall difference across time (p < 0.001). The Bonferroni post hoc test revealed no significant difference between baseline and 3 months (p = 0.965), whereas significant differences were observed between baseline and 12 months (p < 0.001) and between 3 months and 12 months (p = 0.001).

Association between Initial Benzodiazepine Dose and Discontinuation

Table 4 summarizes the association between the initial BZD dose and discontinuation during the follow-up period. Of the 733 patients who initiated BZD treatment, 117 (16.0%) discontinued the medication during follow-up. Discontinuation rates were 18.4% for patients with an initial equivalent dose of ≤ 1, 13.5% for 1−2, 17.4% for 2−3, and 20.0% for > 3. Logistic regression analysis showed no significant association between initial BZD dose and the likelihood of discontinuation (p = 0.082−0.903 across dose groups). These findings indicate that higher initial BZD doses were not significantly predictive of poorer discontinuation outcomes after 12 months.

Factors Associated with Benzodiazepine Discontinuation or Reduction

By 12 months, a total of 214 patients discontinued or reduced BZD use. Pairwise logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with BZD continuation or discontinuation at 12 months, including age, sex, marital status, employment, education level, comorbid psychiatric disorder, stressful events around onset, main somatic panic symptoms, duration of illness, type of BZD prescribed, combination with other psychotropic medications, and initial BZD dose. Variables with p < 0.1 were entered into a fully adjusted multivariable logistic regression model (Table 5). In the final model, comorbid psychiatric disorder (odds ratio [OR] = 0.53, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.29−0.95), non-alprazolam BZD use (OR = 0.63, 95% CI = 0.40−0.97), and buspirone use (OR = 0.45, 95% CI = 0.23−0.88) were significantly associated with lower odds of BZD discontinuation.

DISCUSSION

This study, which tracked real-world treatment courses over 12 months in BZD-initiating patients with first-onset panic disorder, shows that long-term BZD use commonly persists. Despite multiple treatment guidelines recommending SSRIs as first-line therapy and discouraging prolonged BZD use [5,12], only 32.3% of patients had reduced or discontinued BZDs by the 3-month mark, and this proportion was still below half (45.0%) at 12 months. Notably, just 16.0% achieved complete discontinuation at 12 months. The mean BZD dose also showed no significant reduction at 3 months relative to baseline and demonstrated only a modest decrease (< 20%) by 12 months. Taken together, these findings indicate a substantial gap between the guideline-recommended approach—short-term adjunctive use followed by tapering and discontinuation—and actual prescribing patterns in routine clinical practice.

Long-term use of BZDs is generally not recommended because of their potential adverse effects. BZDs are considered safe and effective when used short-term—typically for 2 to 4 weeks—to alleviate acute symptoms. However, prolonged use has been associated with tolerance, dependence, withdrawal symptoms, cognitive impairment, and an increased risk of falls and fractures, particularly in older adults [13-15]. While the combination of antidepressants and BZDs may be appropriate during the initial phase of treatment, maintenance therapy is intended to rely primarily on antidepressant monotherapy once symptoms are stabilized [4,5,12]. Nevertheless, as demonstrated in the present study, a substantial proportion of patients continued BZD use for up to one year without dose reduction, suggesting that clinicians may tend to prescribe BZDs longer than recommended, possibly due to difficulties in symptom control or concerns about relapse.

Interestingly, this study found no significant association between the initial BZD dose and BZD discontinuation after one year. This finding contrasts with previous studies conducted in broader anxiety disorder populations, which suggested that higher initial BZD doses predict greater difficulty in achieving long-term discontinuation [16,17]. Although the initial dose is likely to have been determined based on the clinical severity at presentation—such as the intensity or frequency of panic attacks—our findings indicate that these baseline factors may not have directly determined long-term discontinuation outcomes. Rather, clinical improvement during the course of treatment may be a more relevant factor in determining long-term BZD use outcomes, though this hypothesis warrants further investigation. Consistent with this interpretation, prior studies have also reported that the initial BZD dose may predict short-term discontinuation (within 3 months) but fails to predict long-term discontinuation beyond 12 months [17].

In this study, patients treated with alprazolam—a short half-life BZD—were more successful than expected in achieving long-term dose reduction or discontinuation. Although shorter half-life BZDs, including alprazolam, are generally associated with more severe withdrawal, rebound anxiety, and psychological dependence [18-20], several possible explanations may be considered for this unexpected finding. First, one possible explanation is that alprazolam’s established efficacy for acute panic symptoms may have contributed to earlier clinical improvement, potentially facilitating subsequent tapering; however, this hypothesis could not be directly tested in the present study owing to the absence of standardized symptom severity assessments. Alprazolam has been shown to provide rapid relief of core panic disorder symptoms, including prevention of panic attacks, reduction of anticipatory anxiety, and improvement in agoraphobic avoidance [21,22], and is one of only two BZDs approved by the U.S. FDA for the treatment of panic disorder. The APA guideline also cites alprazolam as having the strongest evidence base among BZDs for panic disorder [4], and in the present study it was the most frequently prescribed BZD at baseline, consistent with its established efficacy and widespread clinical use. In addition, the rapid onset of action of alprazolam may facilitate effective control of acute panic attacks with less next-day sedation or cognitive dulling and allow flexible dose adjustment [4,23]. Importantly, a longer half-life does not necessarily translate into easier tapering or discontinuation; rather, when BZDs are tapered gradually, the tapering strategy itself may be more important than pharmacokinetic differences such as half-life [9]. These factors may help explain both the high initial preference for alprazolam and its association with more successful long-term tapering and discontinuation.

Another possible explanation—though somewhat paradoxical—is that clinicians may have been more aware of the high dependence potential of alprazolam, leading them to pursue more active tapering strategies during the course of treatment. In other words, even when alprazolam is chosen initially for its rapid symptom relief, its well-recognized risk of dependence may prompt clinicians to reduce the dose or switch to another medication after a certain treatment period.

The presence of comorbid psychiatric disorders significantly influenced BZD tapering outcomes. Patients with comorbid psychiatric conditions showed lower rates of BZD dose reduction or discontinuation over 12 months, consistent with previous evidence that panic disorder commonly co-occurs with depressive and other anxiety disorders, alcohol use disorder, and personality disorders, and that such comorbidity is associated with greater symptom severity and poorer treatment response [24]. In the present study, 11.7% of patients had comorbid psychiatric disorders, which is lower than the 50−80% lifetime comorbidity rates typically reported in epidemiological studies of panic disorder [24], likely reflecting the restriction of this sample to patients with first-onset panic disorder.

Buspirone is a non-BZD anxiolytic commonly used for generalized anxiety disorder; however, its efficacy in panic disorder is limited [25]. Previous studies have demonstrated no significant benefit of buspirone over placebo and inferiority to imipramine [26], no added advantage when combined with cognitive behavioral therapy [27], and no effect on facilitating BZD tapering or reducing withdrawal severity [28]. Consistent with these findings, patients who received buspirone in the present study were more likely to maintain or increase their BZD dose at 12 months. This may indicate that buspirone was preferentially prescribed to patients with more severe or treatment-resistant symptoms; however, it also suggests that buspirone itself may not provide sufficient anxiolytic control in panic disorder, thereby failing to reduce reliance on BZDs.

Antidepressants are considered the cornerstone of pharmacological treatment for panic disorder [4,5,12], yet their clinical effectiveness and tolerability remain imperfect, and at least 12 weeks of continued treatment is generally required before an adequate treatment response can be evaluated [29]. The overall response rate to antidepressants in panic disorder is approximately 50−70%, with dropout rates of 10−20% [30]. and several meta-analyses have demonstrated no significant difference in efficacy between BZDs and antidepressants [31,32]. In line with these findings, a substantial proportion of patients in the present study received BZDs as part of their initial treatment, and antidepressant use did not significantly predict BZD dose reduction at 12 months. This suggests that many patients continued BZD use despite concurrent antidepressant therapy, possibly due to insufficient efficacy or poor tolerability. Antidepressants are frequently associated with early adverse effects, including transient anxiety exacerbation, insomnia, and gastrointestinal symptoms, which may lead to treatment discontinuation or the need for additional anxiolytic support [2]. A 2021 meta-analysis showed that sertraline and paroxetine were significantly less tolerable than BZDs in patients with panic disorder [32], whereas BZDs are generally well tolerated when used short-term, with few serious adverse effects and a rapid onset of action [6,33]. Similarly, a 2019 meta-analysis reported that SSRIs produced more adverse events than BZDs during the acute-phase treatment of panic disorder and that BZDs conferred a protective effect against SSRI-induced side effects such as sweating, fatigue, nausea, diarrhea, and insomnia [6]. In addition, a 3-year comparative study demonstrated that clonazepam was associated with greater improvement in clinical symptoms and a lower incidence of adverse events than paroxetine, with the mean clonazepam dose remaining stable over time [34]. These findings suggest that limitations in antidepressant tolerability—and possibly efficacy—in panic disorder may be underestimated, helping to explain the continued or extended use of BZDs in real-world clinical practice despite guideline recommendations.

The benefits and risks of long-term BZD use remain a subject of ongoing debate [35,36]. Several studies suggest that long-term BZD therapy does not necessarily lead to tolerance, dose escalation, or serious adverse outcomes and may, in some cases, help maintain symptom control in panic disorder [34,37-42]. In a randomized controlled trial of 106 patients with panic disorder, low-dose maintenance treatment with BZDs prevented relapse without evidence of tolerance or dose escalation (for alprazolam), and the mean BZD dose significantly decreased over the maintenance period [37,38]. Similarly, other studies have reported that no clear tolerance develops to the anxiolytic effects of BZDs in patients with panic disorder [34,39]. A two-year follow-up study found that clonazepam produced sustained clinical improvement comparable to antidepressants without any increase in dosage [40], and a database analysis of 2,440 Medicaid recipients who used BZDs showed no significant dose escalation over two years, with long-term symptom control maintained at stable low doses [41]. Furthermore, a large-scale 20-year follow-up study involving more than 950,000 BZD users reported that only a small minority maintained high-dose use and that cases exceeding recommended dosages were extremely rare [42]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the common belief that “BZDs inevitably lead to chronic use or progressive dose escalation” may not be fully supported by available evidence. Moreover, a 2007 study by Wagner et al. [43], a state policy in New York that reduced BZD prescriptions by approximately 60% among elderly patients did not result in a significant decline in hip fracture incidence compared with neighboring New Jersey, suggesting that the relationship between BZD use and fall risk may be more complex than previously assumed.

Consistent with previous literature, the present study found no evidence of mean dose escalation over one year. Given that BZDs have been available for decades and are no longer under patent, the relative lack of recent clinical research may have contributed to a disproportionate emphasis on their risks, and the stable dosing patterns observed in the present study suggest that the long-term risk profile of BZDs at maintained doses may warrant re-evaluation. That said, because adverse outcomes such as dependence, cognitive effects, fall risk, and withdrawal severity were beyond the scope of this analysis, the absence of dose escalation should not be interpreted as evidence that long-term BZD continuation is without risk.

This study has several limitations. First, the absence of an objective clinical severity scale prevented detailed evaluation of how symptom severity influenced medication use or dose-reduction patterns. Future studies incorporating standardized symptom severity scales and proxy severity indicators are warranted to clarify these mechanistic pathways. Second, substantial loss to follow-up occurred during the study period: 47 patients were lost to follow-up by 3 months and an additional 210 between 3 and 12 months, leaving 476 completers (64.9% of the initial BZD-treated cohort). Although the primary BZD dose change analysis used MMRM, which accounts for missing data under the missing-at-random assumption, the discontinuation proportions reported at 12 months are based on completers only. To evaluate the robustness of these estimates, a sensitivity analysis was conducted under three scenarios regarding the outcomes of non-completers: (a) best-case, assuming all non-completers discontinued BZDs; (b) worst-case, assuming all continued; and (c) same-rate, assuming non-completers had the same discontinuation rate as completers (Supplementary Table 1; available online). Third, because the sample was limited to patients treated at general hospitals, the findings may not be generalizable to community or primary care settings, and the participants likely represent a group with more severe symptoms or greater treatment complexity. Fourth, potential selection bias and residual confounding related to physicians’ prescribing decisions cannot be fully excluded. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable real-world data on long-term BZD use and tapering patterns over a 12-month period among patients with panic disorder.

In conclusion, Although BZDs are not recommended for long-term use in the treatment of panic disorder due to their potential risks, this study demonstrated that among BZD-initiating patients with first-onset panic disorder, many continued BZD therapy for more than 12 months in real-world clinical practice. Factors associated with successful dose reduction or discontinuation included the use of alprazolam and the absence of comorbid psychiatric disorders, whereas concomitant use of buspirone was associated with poorer tapering outcomes. In contrast, initial BZD dose and antidepressant use were not significant predictors of long-term discontinuation. These findings indicate a discrepancy between clinical guidelines and actual prescribing patterns among BZD-initiating patients with panic disorder.
