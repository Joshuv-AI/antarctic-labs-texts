Neuromotor rehabilitation in Parkinsonian syndromes: outcomes and disability after hip fracture
Authors: Pietro Giuseppe Scamarcia, Ferdinando Ambrosi, Alessandra Demontis, Emanuela Monica Huci, Sabrina Perego, Luca Sanavia, Laura Vulpio, Gianluca Concardi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with Parkinsonian syndromes, including Parkinson’s disease (PD) and atypical parkinsonism (AP), are at increased risk of disability following hip fracture. Limited data are available on their functional recovery after inpatient rehabilitation. This study aimed to compare rehabilitation outcomes in patients with parkinsonism versus non-parkinsonian controls after hip fracture surgery and to identify clinical predictors of worse recovery within the parkinsonian group.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted on 60 patients with parkinsonism (45 PD, 15 AP) and 60 age- and sex-matched non-parkinsonian controls undergoing inpatient rehabilitation after hip fracture surgery. Functional status was assessed at admission and discharge using the Modified Barthel Index (MBI) and modified Rankin Scale (mRS). Linear mixed-effects models were applied to explore associations between clinical variables (including diagnosis, dementia, dysphagia, postural instability) and outcomes.ResultsPatients with parkinsonism had significantly lower functional scores at both admission and discharge as compared to controls (p < 0.001), with AP patients performing worse than PD patients (p = 0.02). Among parkinsonian individuals, dementia, dysphagia, and postural instability were independently associated with worse outcomes (p < 0.001). Although MBI change scores (delta-MBI) did not differ significantly between groups, mRS change (delta-mRS) was greater in controls, suggesting that parkinsonian patients experienced a higher overall disability burden at both admission and discharge, despite a smaller relative improvement. These findings indicate that non-motor symptoms substantially influence rehabilitation trajectories and should be considered in planning care.ConclusionsParkinsonian patients, particularly those with atypical parkinsonism, experience worse functional outcomes after hip fracture rehabilitation when compared with non-parkinsonian individuals. The presence of dementia, dysphagia, and postural instability further impairs recovery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03294-9.

Background

Hip fractures are a significant cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly among elderly individuals, and pose substantial challenges for rehabilitation [1]. When coupled with the presence of parkinsonism, encompassing Parkinson’s disease (PD) and atypical parkinsonian syndromes (AP), the rehabilitation process becomes more complex due to both motor and non-motor symptoms that affect gait, balance, and adherence to therapy [2]. In addition to the typical motor and non-motor symptoms of parkinsonian syndromes, osteoporosis and sarcopenia play a crucial role in further increasing the risk of falls and fractures [3]. A recent cross-sectional study demonstrated that more than one third of patients with mild-to-moderate Parkinson’s disease present with osteoporosis, with reduced lean body mass emerging as an independent risk factor [3]. Similarly, longitudinal national cohort data showed that progressive weight and body mass index decline in patients with PD is associated with a significantly increased incidence of femur fractures, underscoring the importance of nutritional and body composition monitoring in the clinical management of these patients [4]. Parkinsonian patients exhibit a higher risk of falls and fractures compared to the general population [5, 6]. The recent epidemiological data further underline the high fracture burden in Parkinson’s disease. A nationwide registry-based cohort study showed that individuals with PD have a substantially increased incidence of osteoporotic fractures compared to age- and sex-matched controls, with hip fractures being particularly overrepresented [7]. These findings confirm that fracture risk in PD extends beyond the effects of motor impairment alone, highlighting the combined impact of skeletal fragility and systemic factors [7]. Several studies have attempted to explore the functional outcome of individuals with PD who suffer a hip fracture [8–10]. However, heterogeneity in study methods and clinical settings has limited the comparability of results, and there remains a paucity of data on rehabilitation-specific outcomes in PD and AP. Few investigations have specifically examined functional trajectories during an organized rehabilitation program after hip fracture [11–13]. In this framework, we aimed to analyze the impact of parkinsonism on disability after hip fracture surgery in a cohort of individuals undergoing inpatient rehabilitation, comparing them to a matched control group without parkinsonism. Secondary objectives included identifying clinical predictors (i.e. dementia, dysphagia, postural instability, disease severity) influencing functional recovery in the Parkinsonian group, to provide insights for optimizing rehabilitation strategies and global care in this complex population.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

A retrospective study was conducted on a cohort of 60 patients with clinically diagnosed parkinsonism (PD or AP) [14] admitted for inpatient rehabilitation after hip fracture surgery. In parallel, a control group of 60 age- and sex-matched patients without clinical signs of parkinsonism was recruited from the same rehabilitation facility during the same period (selection pathway is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1). Inclusion criteria for both groups were age ≥60 years, surgical repair of a recent hip fracture (within 2 weeks prior to admission), and eligibility for an intensive inpatient rehabilitation program. Exclusion criteria were the presence of other major neurological disorders that might confound rehabilitation outcomes, severe comorbid orthopedic conditions unrelated to the hip fracture, and inability to undergo at least minimal physical therapy due to unstable medical conditions. According to clinical diagnosis based on the Movement Disorders Society (MDS) Diagnostic Criteria [14], the Parkinsonism group (FrP) was divided into a PD subgroup (Fr-PD) and an AP subgroup (Fr-AP). Patients with multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy, corticobasal syndrome, and Lewy body dementia were grouped together under the umbrella term AP. All participants had a history of Parkinsonism confirmed by a neurologist prior to admission. All data presented in this study were extracted from medical records with patient identifiers removed. The retrospective nature of the study and anonymization of data did not require specific patient consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. No specific funding was received.

Clinical and rehabilitation management

All patients (parkinsonian and controls) were managed with a standardized inpatient rehabilitation protocol consisting of therapist-supervised sessions 45–60 min per day, 6 days per week, including gait re-education, balance training, progressive muscle strengthening, and transfer practice. Whenever feasible, sessions were scheduled during the patient’s clinical ON state. Nutritional care and speech-language therapy were provided according to our unit-wide institutional pathway. Full procedural details are provided in the Supplementary Materials. All patients were evaluated in the ON state, when parkinsonian patients were at their best motor response to medication. Although a standardized protocol for medication adjustments was not used in this study, PD medications were maintained stable. The Levodopa Equivalent Daily Dose (LEDD) was calculated as described by Jost et al. [15]. Non-motor symptoms were managed according to the best clinical practice, but detailed standardized scales were not systematically collected. Freezing of gait and frequent falls in the preceding months were noted if mentioned in the records but were not systematically assessed using a validated scale.

Clinical assessments

Baseline data included demographic variables (i.e. age, sex) and medical history (surgical procedure performed and comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, osteoporosis), history of fractures or falls, and dysphagia (defined as ASHA NOMS score equal or lower than 5) [16]. Dementia was recorded as a pre-existing clinical diagnosis (yes/no) documented in the medical record by the treating specialists or in prior discharge summaries; no new diagnoses were assigned during rehabilitation. The “dementia” covariate denotes a pre-existing clinical diagnosis and is conceptually distinct from LBD as a primary diagnosis. Neurological examination confirmed clinically defined parkinsonism (PD or AP); disease severity was estimated using the Hoehn & Yahr (H&Y) stage [17]. Medication details (type and dosage of antiparkinsonian therapy, LEDD, antihypertensive drugs) were recorded. Functional evaluation was performed at admission and discharge using the Modified Barthel Index (MBI) [18] and the Modified Rankin Scale (mRS) [19]. Premorbid mRS, referring to the period before the hip fracture, was also retrieved from medical records and structured interviews with patients and their relatives. The length of stay in our unit and any relevant complications were documented. Standardized PD motor and nonmotor scales (e.g., MDS-UPDRS, freezing-of-gait measures) were not systematically available and were not analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Demographic, clinical, and cognitive data were reported as means ± standard deviations for continuous variables or frequencies/percentages for categorical variables. Given the retrospective nature of the study, no prospective sample size calculation was performed. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Between-group comparisons (FrO vs. FrP, and Fr-PD vs. Fr-AP) used independent-sample t tests for normally distributed continuous variables, Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normal continuous variables, and chi-squared tests for categorical variables. To explore factors associated with functional outcomes in parkinsonian patients, we used linear mixed-effects models with a random intercept for each patient. These models were adjusted for age, sex, disease severity defined as H&Y stage and surgical procedure. Dependent variables were MBI and mRS at discharge.

Independent variables included diagnosis (AP vs. PD), dysphagia, dementia, postural instability, vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, ischemic heart disease, atrial fibrillation, dyslipidemia, smoking), osteoporosis (pre-existing diagnosis), perioperative systemic infections, delirium, blood transfusion and LEDD. As an exploratory analysis, we modeled the discharge category (full autonomy < dependent < institutionalized) using ordinal logistic regression (proportional odds, logit link) with diagnosis, age, sex, H&Y stage, and surgery type. Given the conceptual overlap among H&Y stage, disease duration, and LEDD, we screened for multicollinearity using pairwise correlations, variance inflation factors, and condition indices on centered predictors. No concerning collinearity was detected. Full procedures and diagnostics are reported in the Supplementary materials. We also analyzed the differences between admission and discharge in MBI and mRS (delta-MBI and delta-mRS) in similar models. Analyses were performed on a complete-case basis; no imputation was applied. Multiple comparisons were controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 after adjustment. All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.0.3).

FrO subjects compared to those in the FrP group showed significantly lower scores in MBI pre-hospitalization (p < 0.001), at admission (p < 0.001) and at discharge (p < 0.001). There were no significant differences in delta-MBI, while delta-mRS was significantly greater in FrO than in FrP group (p < 0.001). Association analysis

Among parkinsonian patients, association analyses showed that dysphagia, dementia, and postural instability were associated with worse discharge outcomes: lower MBI (β = −14.93, p = 0.02; β = −15.95, p = 0.002; β = –17.44, p < 0.001, respectively) and higher mRS (β = 0.81, p = 0.003; β = 0.54, p = 0.02; β = 0.75, p < 0.001, respectively). PD diagnosis, rather than AP, was associated with better functional status at discharge (MBI: β = +19.87, p < 0.001; mRS: β = −0.89, p < 0.001). H&Y stage was associated with worse outcomes (MBI: β = −2.51, p < 0.001; mRS: p < 0.001). According to premorbid functional status, MBI pre-hospitalization was associated with higher MBI at discharge (β = +0.49, p = 0.004), and higher mRS pre-hospitalization was associated with higher mRS at discharge (β = +0.38, p = 0.005). Vascular risk factors, osteoporosis, perioperative complications (infections, delirium, blood transfusion) and different surgical procedures showed no significant associations with disability outcomes (all p > 0.05). No clinical variable was significantly associated with change scores (delta-MBI or delta-mRS). The results are summarized in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 2. In the ordinal model, higher H&Y stage was independently associated with a worse discharge category (odds ratio per one-stage = 13.35; 95% CI 2.42–73.9; p = 0.003). Diagnosis, age, sex, and surgery type were not significantly associated. Discussion

Our study investigated the functional outcome of parkinsonian patients after hip fracture surgery and rehabilitation compared with patients with similar fractures without Parkinsonism. Groups were sex- and age-matched, without significant differences according to the presence of dysphagia and dementia. The presence of vascular risk factors was similar, with the only exception of hypertension that was more common in the FrO group.

The functional outcome, measured as MBI and mRS scores, was worse in FrP compared to the FrO group. In addition, the functional performance at admission was poorer in FrP patients. The functional outcome in patients with PD who experienced a hip fracture event was investigated in previous studies, showing a contested worse mid-term and long-term performance in patients with PD [8–13]. Many studies showed a clearly poorer outcome in PD patients after hip fracture surgery [8–12], with higher risk of institutionalization, longer hospitalization, worse mobility and higher 1-year mortality than in non-PD patients [8]. A more recent study demonstrated that outcomes between PD patients and non-PD patients were mostly equivalent, but more PD patients required discharge to a higher-level care environment compared to non-PD patients [13] Even without full consensus, those studies identified some degrees of negative influence of PD on functional outcome, but they did not consider a rehabilitation process. Those studies showed heterogeneity in the post-surgery continuation of care, making the results not easily comparable to our current standards, which now involve a rehabilitation program after hip fracture surgery. Moreover, as already highlighted by Clubb and colleagues in a review [20], many less recent studies showed results based on the regimens that are not common practice nowadays (i.e. selection of surgery approach or immobilization period after surgery), while other studies included a high proportion of patients from nursing care homes, even with an unfavorable prognosis from the time of the fracture [11, 12]. According to our results, as an exploratory endpoint, discharge category is aligned with disease stage, defined as H&Y score, but given the sample size and unmeasured social factors influencing disposition, results should be interpreted cautiously.

It’s interesting to underline that the comparison of delta-MBI and delta-mRS in FrO and FrP groups showed different results: delta-MBI was not significantly different, while delta-mRS was. This observation might be linked to the different nature of these scales. In fact, MBI is suited for evaluation of task-specific areas of patients’ function and it is less effective in capturing global disability or cognitive impacts that do not directly affect the activity of daily living [18, 21]. On the other hand, mRS is sensitive to broad levels of disability, providing an overarching sense of the patient’s independence [19, 22].

The comparison between the two main groups did not show differences in sociodemographic and anamnestic variables, with the only exception of arterial hypertension, which was more common in the FrO group compared to the FrP. This result is not surprising: in parkinsonian patients the autonomic nervous system is often compromised, leading to a reduced sympathetic response, resulting in lower average blood pressure levels [23]. Moreover, virtually every parkinsonian patient is treated with dopaminergic medications, which influence blood pressure and contribute to hypotension [24].

Among parkinsonian patients, the ones with PD compared to those with AP had better pre-hospitalization functional scores, as well as at admission and at discharge. The diagnosis of PD rather than AP was associated with better MBI and mRS scores at discharge. This observation is supported by the previous findings [25], confirming a worse prognosis and suggesting the more difficult recovery after a traumatic event in AP compared with PD, who generally follow a more predictable progression and respond better to dopaminergic treatments [26]. The lack of differences between parkinsonian patients in delta-MBI and delta-mRS indicates that the rate of functional progression was similar in these groups, suggesting that the functional impact of the fracture starts with the traumatic event and remains stable during rehabilitation.

Our results, from a pathophysiological point of view, might be explained by differences in mechanisms underlying PD and AP [27, 28]. While PD is characterized by a relatively well-defined progression of loss of dopaminergic neurons, AP encompasses a broader spectrum of neurodegenerative processes, often involving multiple neural pathways [29, 30] and leading to a more rapid decline. These differences could affect neuroplasticity and response to rehabilitation, with AP patients experiencing limitations in motor and cognitive recovery after acute events [28].

Overall, our findings suggest that patients with PD may maintain a more resilient baseline functional capacity compared to AP patients [31]. This resilience could indicate that therapeutic strategies and rehabilitation efforts might yield better functional outcomes in PD patients following hospitalization or traumatic events. In contrast, the lower functional scores observed in AP patients point to a more complex trajectory, potentially necessitating specialized interventions to address the slower recovery and worse prognosis [32].

According to our analysis, the presence of dysphagia, dementia or postural instability was independently associated with poorer functional outcomes. These elements are important milestones in the natural history of parkinsonian disorders affecting the disability and the need for additional care [33].

Dysphagia can lead to malnutrition and aspiration, which may hinder recovery by limiting energy availability and increasing the risk of complications [34]. Nutritional deficits, in addition, may delay wound healing or reduce muscle strength, impacting physical therapy and the overall functional recovery [35].

Cognitive impairment, another important cause of loss of autonomy and mortality, in patients affected by other neurological disorders, such as cerebrovascular events, influences not only a patient’s ability to follow rehabilitation instructions but also their overall motivation and engagement, which are crucial for successful recovery [36]. Even though in scientific literature this topic regarding parkinsonian syndromes has not been particularly investigated, the underlying mechanisms and observations might be shared with such other neurological disorders [36].

Postural instability might lead to a delayed and more challenging adherence to the rehabilitation program, due to difficulty in mobilization or use of walking aids [37]. In the specific case of PD, even though the symptoms are generally responsive to dopaminergic treatment, postural instability shows only a partial response to medication, highlighting the complexity in treating this condition [38]. Postural instability is also linked to the risk of falls. Patients with parkinsonism are generally at a greater risk of falls and fractures due to motor impairments, postural instability, and medication side effects that may impact balance and coordination [39]. Parkinsonian patients, and in particular those with PD, often have a higher prevalence of osteoporosis and reduced bone mineral density compared to age-matched controls without PD [40]. This predisposition to osteoporosis adds another layer of complexity to fracture risk management in these individuals, as it increases the likelihood of severe fractures even from minor trauma [40]. Given these factors, fracture prevention should be prioritized in this population, aiming to reduce both the occurrence of fractures and the time required for recovery. The complex needs of parkinsonian patients, particularly those undergoing rehabilitation following a hip fracture, call for a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach. Collaboration between orthopedic surgeons, rehabilitation specialists, neurologists, and other healthcare providers can significantly improve functional outcomes for these patients [41]. We suggest that, considering the differences in rehabilitation outcomes among parkinsonian patients discussed above, it is essential to develop a personalized and interdisciplinary rehabilitation plan for each patient.

Emerging preclinical evidence provides a mechanistic rationale for the clinical pattern we observed. In 6-OHDA experimental models, oxidative stress and dopaminergic neuron loss drive motor impairment and postural instability, plausibly linking severe motor and non-motor burden to falls, fractures, and poorer rehabilitation outcomes. Consistent with this, antioxidant/anti-inflammatory candidates (e.g., syringic acid) mitigate neurotoxic cascades in PD models [42], and melatonin attenuates 6-OHDA-induced oxidative stress in hemiparkinsonian rats [43]. These data are hypothesis-generating for adjunct or disease-modifying therapies that could ultimately improve functional endpoints, but require rigorous human trials (safety, dosing, target engagement, and functional outcomes) before any inference about rehabilitation benefit can be drawn.

Our study is not free of limitations. This single-center, retrospective design limits causal inference; the AP subgroup was small (n = 15), and pooling heterogeneous AP entities reduces generalizability, precluding reliable subtype-specific modeling. Future adequately powered studies should analyze AP subtypes separately. Regarding PD, we did not consider symptomatic scales, such as the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale. This scale, even though its role in evaluation of disability is debated [44], was not systematically performed as part of routine admission, so these data were unavailable for analysis. Potential confounders were not systematically collected in retrospective records, including standardized motor and non-motor scale, frailty, mood and pain measures, nutritional status, bone mineral density, and social determinants (caregiver support, home accessibility, socioeconomic resources). Although rehabilitation scheduling was standardized, adherence at the session level and OFF-state performance were not consistently documented. Likewise, detailed perioperative information such as time-to-surgery, anesthesia type, fracture classification, and graded complications was not systematically available in retrospective records. Their absence may have biased adjusted estimates. Premorbid mRS was derived from medical records and caregiver interviews, which makes it subject to recall and misclassification bias. Functional outcomes were limited to MBI and mRS, which may show ceiling/floor effects and limited cognitive sensitivity. Inter-rater reliability was not assessed in this retrospective cohort. The outcomes were limited to discharge measures. The data on 90-day or 1-year mortality, readmissions, and institutionalization were not available in this retrospective cohort. Collinearity diagnostics between LEDD and H&Y (variance inflation factors and condition indices) did not reveal critical issues. However, residual confounding related to dopaminergic treatment cannot be excluded. Our sample was relatively small. No a priori power calculation was performed. Missing data were handled with complete-case analysis, which may reduce precision compared with multiple imputation. Based on our data, a future prospective study powered to detect a clinically relevant 10-point difference in MBI at discharge (SD 18–20, α = 0.05, 80% power) would require approximately 50–65 patients per group; the AP subgroup in the present cohort (n = 15) is underpowered for smaller effects. Sensitivity analyses based on propensity score methods (matching or inverse probability weighting) were not performed. Given the small sample size, particularly in the AP subgroup, these approaches would have been statistically unstable and prone to bias. Such methods should be incorporated in future, adequately powered prospective studies. Further studies might confirm our observations with a larger sample of patients, a longer follow-up period and a prospective design.

Conclusions

Our study highlights that parkinsonian patients experience poorer functional outcomes following hip fracture surgery and subsequent rehabilitation as compared to non-parkinsonian patients, although PD patients demonstrate greater resilience than those with AP. Factors such as dysphagia, cognitive impairment, and postural instability, that represent important milestones in the natural history of parkinsonian disorders, significantly impact functional outcomes, suggesting the need for personalized rehabilitation programs for this group of patients. An interdisciplinary approach for parkinsonian patients is desirable to optimize recovery and minimize long-term disability, although future research is warranted for a deeper understanding of parkinsonian patients’ recovery patterns and refine therapeutic strategies.
