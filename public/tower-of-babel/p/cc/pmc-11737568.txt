In-house manufactured 3D guides for the localization and treatment of mandibular lesions. Workflow and case series
Authors: Juan P. Rodríguez-Arias, Marta M. P. Martínez, Carolina C. Urquía, José L. C. Carretero
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The anatomical location of certain lesions can be a difficulty when locating them intraoperatively. The use of surgical navigation allows anatomical structures to be located with great precision. However, there are technical difficulties with its use in mandibular surgery. Three-dimensional (3D) printing has established itself as a definitive tool in the generation of biomodels for diagnosis and treatment as well as implantable devices. Some applications, little explored today, have to do with the use of 3D-printed devices for the localization of hard-to-reach lesions. To determine the position of mandibular bone lesions, we propose using 3D printed localization guides manufactured “in-house,” using freely licensed design programs (software) to design them, and made of biocompatible resins. This improves surgical precision and reduces morbidity from the intervention. 3D planning models are shown and segmented using open-source software (3D Slicer) using imported conventional computed tomography data. Digital component modification is possible with free software (Autodesk Meshmixer) to arrange precise osteotomy cuts for lesion localization. With the help of customized cutting guides, intraoperative placement is precise. These are created utilizing a fused filament manufacturing 3D printer and polylactic acid. Three localization guides were successfully completed, resulting in improved surgical accuracy and reduced surgical morbidity. The use of 3D surgical guides in cases of mandibular lesions in difficult or delicate locations saves the need for navigation, requires less surgical time, does not require splints or reference stars.

INTRODUCTION

Intraoperative navigation has proven to be very useful in the localization of lesions and anatomical structures, especially in the midface and skull base. However, there are technical difficulties when navigating the mandible as it is a mobile element, involving a slow and complex registration procedure with a large learning curve.

An alternative to the navigator may be the use of surgical positioning guides, which provide information on the location of bony lesions. This can reduce surgical time and facilitate surgery compared to the conventional technique, especially in small lesions, by increasing the precision of the procedure.[1]

The use of three-dimensional (3D) technology for these procedures has been very restricted and has usually required the involvement of industry with a considerable increase in costs and waiting times.

This article describes the procedure for the generation and use of in-house 3D guides using freely available software for their design and printing in biocompatible material suitable for contact with the patient’s tissues. Along with the workflow outlining the technical and technological requirements for the design and manufacture of the guidewires, success stories with the use of such guidewires are added.

The localization of mandibular lesions requires the processing of the images using suitable software. Below, we detail the steps for processing and the software used to create and print the guides:

CASE REPORTS

Case 1

We present the case of a 60-year-old female patient with a history of unicystic mandibular ameloblastoma who was operated on for the first time five years earlier. In subsequent routine radiological examinations (orthopantomography (OPG)), a radiolucent lesion was observed in the right ascending mandibular branch. A CT scan [Figure 2] was requested for a better characterization of the lesion, showing a region of 5 mm in maximum diameter with sclerotic borders in the right mandibular ramus. On physical examination, the lesion was neither palpable nor easy to locate three-dimensionally in the thickness of the ramus, so it was decided to create a 3D guide as an intraoperative aid for localization of the lesion [Figure 3]. The guide rested on the anterior border of the mandibular ramus and had a hole with the approximate dimensions of the lesion that extended in the form of a tube to provide information on both horizontal and vertical positioning, as well as the direction of bone reaming to the lesion. To provide stability and avoid erroneous positioning, the guide was provided with a lateral arm and a medial arm horizontally embracing the branch, remaining fixed in a single position, thanks to the geometry of the external mandibular oblique line. Approved by the Hospital Ethics Committee of the Hospital la Paz. PI-5076.

Mandibular CT scan showing a radiolucent lesion in the right mandibular ramus with non-palpable sclerotic borders

A guide was designed over the mandibular ramus for enucleation and curettage of the lesion

Once the guide was printed and sterilized, under general anesthesia, the right mandibular ramus was approached through an intraoral incision with the help of a wedge [Figure 4]. The guide fits perfectly in the ramus, remaining immobile in the planned position and allowing the bone to be drilled in a targeted fashion up to the lesion [Figure 5]. The lesion was excised without complications.

Case 2

Our second case is a 43-year-old patient, born in Taiwan, who had undergone surgery 6 years earlier with a diagnosis of left hemifacial fibrous dysplasia, and bone remodeling was performed in this area. In subsequent clinical-radiological controls, the patient again reported greater deformity in the left mandibular area, and a lytic lesion in the left mandibular body was observed in the OPG [Figure 6]. A CT scan was requested to identify and locate the lesion. The CT scan described findings compatible with known craniofacial fibrous dysplasia, identifying a lytic and expansive area in the left mandibular body branch that thinned the bone walls, with an extensive solution of continuity of the medial cortex, compatible with cystic degeneration in fibrous dysplasia [Figure 7]. The CT scan was accompanied by a guided biopsy, the histological result of which was indeterminate due to the insufficient sample.

OPG. A radiolucent lesion in the left mandibular body branch, approx. 2.5 cm in diameter

Mandibular CT scan showing a lytic and expansive area in the left mandibular body branch thinning the bony walls, with an extensive solution of continuity of the medial cortex

Due to the findings, the location, and the extent of the lesion, it was decided to create a 3D guide for the location and resection of the lesion [Figure 8]. Using an extraoral approach, guide-guided drilling and excision of the lesion with subsequent placement of a reconstruction plate were performed.

Case 3

Finally, we present a 50-year-old female patient who had undergone surgery in another center for a mandibular keratocyst via an external cervical approach. At the time of consultation at our department, she presented a recurrence at the extraosseous level, adjacent to the left mandibular ramus. A CT scan was requested for the correct localization of the lesion [Figure 9]. We designed a localization guide that adapted to the soft tissue to determine the location of the lesion without the need for an intraoperative navigator. This guide fits over the facial contour, with a hole in the area of the lesion. The guide was designed with reference to and adaptation to the ipsilateral tragus to stabilize its position [Figure 10]. Using the previous extraoral approach, the lesion was excised without complications.

DISCUSSION

Intraoperative navigation allows surgeons to determine the location of any bony landmark with an accuracy of approximately 1–2 mm.[1] In mandibular navigation, it is crucial to create superimposable and replicable conditions for mandibular positioning during the preoperative planning stages.[2]

Three different alternatives have been proposed as options for mandibular navigation: the use of intermaxillary fixation, reproducing the mandibular position in centric with the use of splints, and the placement of the navigation star in the mandible itself, which allows it to be moved and operated on in the open mouth.[1]

There are few articles in the literature regarding the use of navigation in non-implant oral surgery. Hong and Kim[3] used the navigator for the excision of a lesion diagnosed as a keratocyst in the mandibular ramus, assisted by endoscopy. This method offers precise anatomical information allowing the localization of the area where the surgeon should work, minimizing bone drilling and limiting it to the area of interest. They report a registration error of less than 0.7 mm.

Emery et al.[4] used a navigator for complex third molar surgery, also navigating the instruments to minimize the risk of injury to anatomical structures. They emphasize that the use of this technology has the potential to improve visualization, as the surgeon can actively view the surgical instruments in real time.

Surgical navigation systems minimize dissection, reduce morbidity, and shorten postoperative recovery time.[5] However, one of the disadvantages of intraoperative navigation is the time required in the process, which lengthens the surgical time by about 30 minutes.[23]

The surgical guides proposed in this technical note are a novel alternative to the use of the navigator in surgery for the removal of mandibular lesions. Although 3D printed guides are already widely used in maxillofacial surgery, only a few report their use in cyst localization, in this case, to facilitate the application of the bone lid technique, restore the mandibular contour, and reduce the surgical defect.[5]

On the other hand, Lukas et al.[6] described their experience with the use of a surgical guide with occlusal support to remove a broken anesthetic needle in the pterygomandibular space. They used a CBCT and an intraoral scanner for this purpose and designed a 3D-printed guide with a pointer that localized the region where the needle was located. This method proved to be suitable for identifying the exact position of the needle, allowing minimal but sufficient surgical access.

The surgical guide provides 3D information regarding the location of lesions, increasing accuracy compared to the traditional method. Its use in cases of mandibular lesions of difficult or delicate locations (e.g. close to the inferior alveolar nerve), saves the need for the navigator, requiring less surgical time without requiring splints or reference stars. In addition, the guide minimizes the bone approach, which can reduce the morbidity of this type of procedure. The guide integrates the necessary information to avoid important anatomical structures since its design also includes virtual planning of the orientation and direction of access; the approach plan is transferred to the operating room in the form of a drill tube.

One of the disadvantages of using this type of instrument is the time required for its design and manufacture, although with the development of in-hospital laboratories, known as Point-of-Care Manufacturing, production and shipping time are reduced for external companies. Compared to the navigator, the disadvantage is that the guide designed in this case does not have information on the drilling depth and does not allow the procedure to be visualized in real-time.

CONCLUSION

3D-printed guides may be an alternative to the use of a navigator in the localization of bone lesions, allowing for increased accuracy and minimizing of the approach required. Further prospective and comparative studies are needed to define accuracy and error with this type of guidewire.
