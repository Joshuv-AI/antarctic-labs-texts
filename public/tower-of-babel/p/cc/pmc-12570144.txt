A Multiscale Simulation Approach to Compute Protein–Ligand Association Rate Constants by Combining Brownian Dynamics and Molecular Dynamics
Authors: Abraham Muñiz-Chicharro, Gaurav K. Ganotra, Rebecca C. Wade
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Drug–protein binding kinetic parameters are key indicators of drug efficacy, but their experimental determination is often costly and time-consuming. Computational approaches require optimization of the in silico approximations to achieve sufficient accuracy while remaining computationally feasible. Here, the combination of Brownian dynamics (BD) and molecular dynamics (MD) has been investigated for this purpose. BD is used for simulating long-range diffusion and diffusional encounter complex formation, whereas MD captures the subsequent formation of the bound complex, providing a detailed treatment of short-range interactions and molecular flexibility. While existing methods that employ this approach have successfully yielded estimated association rate constants (k on), they often require extensive computational resources. In this work, we developed a multiscale pipeline that achieves improved computational efficiency by optimizing the sampling by BD simulation to generate an ensemble of diffusional encounter complexes in which the ligand comes very close to its protein binding site and then uses these as starting structures for MD simulation. Due to the much lower computational cost of BD simulation and the reduced MD simulation time, the approach is computationally efficient while preserving accuracy. The pipeline has been validated for a diverse set of protein–ligand complexes, varying in size, flexibility, and binding properties, yielding k on values that align well with experimental measurements, as well as insights into the physical determinants of the association rate.

Introduction

Drug–protein binding kinetic parameters have been shown to correlate with drug efficacy in vivo. However, experimental assays are expensive and time-consuming, and they offer limited mechanistic insights. Therefore, there is a growing interest in developing in silico approaches to estimate binding kinetic parameters that offer an alternative to experiments and also provide mechanistic insights that can guide molecular design. Here, we focus on the computation of the bimolecular association rate constant, k on. While k on values can be computed by running long conventional MD simulations and counting the number of binding events, this approach is computationally intensive and therefore not feasible for many systems, or for high-throughput or optimization studies.

To overcome the computational limitations of MD simulations, approximations and tailored methods are required. Northrup, Allison and McCammon developed the NAM method for computing k on values for diffusional association using Brownian dynamics (BD) simulations. BD simulations reduce computational time compared to MD simulations through the use of an implicit solvent model and approximations like coarse-graining and rigid-body treatments, thereby allowing efficient simulation of molecular association to form diffusional encounter complexes. , In the NAM method, many BD trajectories are simulated with the binding partners initially placed at a defined separation at which their interaction forces are negligible or centrosymmetric. The trajectories are simulated until criteria for formation of encounter complexes are satisfied or the binding partners separate to a second higher separation distance. From the trajectories, the probability of forming an encounter complex is computed and this is used to calculate k on. This approach has been successfully applied to calculate the rates of binding of a wide-range of systems whose k on value is diffusion-controlled, e.g., substrates to enzymes ,− and the rates of formation of electrostatically steered protein complexes. ,− It has also been applied to drug–protein complexes such as inhibitors binding to HIV-1 protease. , However, these BD simulations cannot capture all the effects determining k on values in general as they neglect factors such as water-mediated interactions, water molecule displacement or conformational changes during binding. In contrast, MD simulations, which can be carried out with explicit solvent and with molecular representations ranging from coarse-grained to fully atomistic detail, are better suited for capturing the postdiffusional steps of the binding process, where induced conformational changes may have a critical effect on binding rates. Thus, one approach to compute k on values is to employ the combination of Brownian dynamics (BD) and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations.

The combination of these two simulation techniques in multiscale approaches has been shown to enable the accurate computation of k on values. This was first demonstrated by Luty et al. who simulated the binding of superoxide to superoxide dismutase, a diffusion-controlled enzyme. They modeled the association by connecting steps simulated with rigid-body BD to subsequent MD steps using Markov chain models to map the probabilities that the superoxide substrate will transition between steps. With this strategy, they calculated a k on value of (1.62 ± 0.86)×109 M–1 s–1, which is in the range of the experimental data, estimated to be 1 × 109 – 6 × 109 M–1 s–1 at zero ionic strength. Moreover, the transition of the protein channel to the binding site from open to closed conformations was captured in the MD simulations.

Extending the approach of Luty et al., SEEKR was developed as a tool that makes use of milestoning to divide the interface between BD and MD, as well as to divide the MD region into smaller ones that are sampled in multiple MD trajectories using surfaces called ”milestones”. The advantage of this strategy is that all MD milestones can be equally sampled, which facilitates the observation of rare events compared to conventional long MD simulations. Moreover, the computational approach can be parallelized, as multiple trajectories are run. However, the correct sampling of the milestones needs thousands of short MD trajectories, which is rather expensive. For instance, to calculate the k on and k off values of the trypsin-benzamidine complex, 2 million BD trajectories were run and MD simulations totaling 4.4 μs were simulated, requiring 352 GPU hours. The bimolecular association rate constant obtained was (8.6 ± 0.7) × 106 M–1 s–1, which is within a factor of 3 of the value determined experimentally: 2.9 × 107 M–1 s–1.

Another method that combines BD and MD was developed by Zeller et al. This method does not divide the MD trajectories with milestones, but instead makes use of long trajectories. Furthermore, the MD region is defined not as in SEEKR as a sphere but rather as a cone. It was applied to compute the k on values for the binding of a neuraminidase monomer with the inhibitors zanamivir and oseltamivir. The vertex of the cone was positioned at the center of mass (COM) of the protein, and it extended toward the entrance to the binding pocket with a radius of 32 Å, and included an encounter surface (ES) such that MD simulations were run when the ligand was within this surface. A total of 1 million trajectories were run with BD and 5000 of the encounter complexes from BD were used as initial configurations for MD simulations. With this method, Zeller et al. computed the k on values of oseltamivir and zanamivir as (25.5 ± 1.3) × 106 and (5.5 ± 0.3) × 106 M–1 s–1, respectively; both about 5 times higher than the experimental values. If just BD simulations were run, zanamivir was computed to have a higher k on value as it reached the ES faster than oseltamivir, whereas oseltamivir has a 10 times higher probability of reaching the bound state from the ES in MD simulations. Thus, when BD and MD simulations were combined, the relative result obtained was in good agreement with the experimental data.

Beyond these BD–MD hybrid strategies, Dibak et al. proposed another multiscale approach that combines particle-based reaction–diffusion simulations with MSMs to investigate ligand–protein binding. This work further highlights the importance of multiscale frameworks in studying complex biomolecular association processes.

Here, we describe our development of a multiscale pipeline that combines BD and MD simulations to accurately calculate association rate constants (k on) while accounting for induced fit during binding. The strategy followed is similar to that employed by Zeller et al., but the diffusional encounter complexes are recorded when the ligand is much closer to the binding site to reduce the MD simulation time and improve the mechanistic insights from the simulations. The pipeline is evaluated for a set of protein–ligand complexes that are diverse in terms of physicochemical properties (polar/nonpolar), size and flexibility, see Table S11.

Theory

Bimolecular Association

The binding kinetics of two molecules describe the rates of the association and dissociation processes given by the equations: 1 [P]+[L]→v1[P:D]; v1=kon×[P]×[L] 2 [P]+[L]←v2[P:L]; v2=koff×[P:L]

where [P] and [L] are the protein and ligand concentrations, respectively, k on is the bimolecular association rate constant and k off is the dissociation rate constant. Equation shows the forward reaction, when the complex is being formed, with the rate of formation being dependent on the association rate constant (k on), the concentration of the protein and the concentration of the ligand. Equation shows the reverse reaction, when the complex dissociates, with the dissociation rate dependent on the dissociation rate constant (k off) times the concentration of the complex. The k on defines the rate of complex formation per unit concentration and unit time (M–1 s–1) whereas the k off defines the rate of complex dissociation per unit time (s–1).

Northrup-Allison-McCammon Algorithm

k on is a parameter that measures the rate at which solutes associate over time and can be computed by counting how many times a complex is formed at a defined concentration in a given time interval. However, this approach is inefficient as it is usually necessary to increase ligand concentration or run very long trajectories in order to collect sufficient binding statistics. The Northrup-Allison-McCammon (NAM) algorithm proposed by Northrup et al. provides a means to overcome this problem. The algorithm makes use of center-to-center distances between the two solutes, generating an inner sphere surface (b-surface) and an outer sphere surface (q-surface). One of the solutes (normally the largest one) is placed at the center of the two spheres while the other is randomly placed on the b-surface and then allowed to move in the BD simulations. Thus, the relative translational and rotational diffusion of the two solutes is simulated. Eventually, the diffusing solute will either form an encounter complex, as defined by a set of intermolecular (atomic) contacts known as reaction criteria, or will escape to the q-surface. The distance at which the reaction criteria is met defines the so-called ”window distance” of the encounter complex. Once all the trajectories have been completed, the diffusional k on value can be computed as 3 kon=kD(b)β∞

where k D(b) is the association rate constant for the ligand reaching the b-surface at standard bulk concentration, and β∞ is the probability of, having reached the b-surface, forming an encounter complex rather than diffusing to infinite separation.

To correct for truncating the trajectories at the q-surface, β∞ can be calculated applying a correction factor Ω = k D(b)/k D(q): 4 β∞=β1−(1−β)Ω

where β is the probability of forming an encounter complex computed from the BD simulations when the trajectories are started at the b-surface.

k D(b) can be analytically computed using the Smoluchowski equation: 5 kD(b)=4πD∫b∞eE(r)/kBTr2dr

where D is the relative translational diffusion coefficient of the solutes, E(r) is the mean force potential at a distance between solutes of r, and b is the distance between solutes when the moving solute is on the b-surface.

If the separation of the solutes at the b-surface is sufficiently large, E(b) will tend to zero, and k D(b) is given by 6 kD(b)=4πDb

For a multiscale simulation (e.g., a combination of BD and MD), the space around the central solute needs to be divided into two regions (inner and outer) by an interface and consequently, the equation to calculate k on with the NAM algorithm has been extended as follows: 7 kon=kD(b)[β1−(1−β)Ω]α1−(1−α)(Δ+[β1−(1−β)Ω](1−Δ))

where α is the probability of a trajectory starting from the interface between inner and outer regions resulting in a bound state and Δ is the probability of re-entering the inner region from the b-surface (Figure ). In this ”two regions” model, β is the probability of reaching the inner region (the interface) from the b-surface (Figure ).

Induced Fit

The pipeline was developed to account for induced fit (IF) under the assumption that the association process can be considered as two consecutive steps (Figure ). In the first step, the ligand diffuses to the protein without inducing significant conformational changes, with the dominant forces being the intermolecular long-range electrostatic force and the random force produced by the collision of solutes with solvent molecules. When the ligand is close enough to the protein binding site, there is a second step, the postdiffusional step, in which the two molecules induce conformational changes in each other and form the bound complex through noncovalent interactions (mainly hydrogen bonds and van der Waals interactions). Another process that takes place at close distances is the displacement of water molecules through desolvation, which is energetically favorable if the water has to leave a hydrophobic region of the molecular surface and can be unfavorable if the water has to leave a polar region. These postdiffusional processes drive the system to the final bound state.

A schematic diagram of a two-step binding process for formation of a protein–ligand complex. The initial state of the system has a local free energy minimum when the protein and ligand are dissociated (A). During the binding process, an intermediate state is formed at a second local minimum energy (B) before reaching the global minimum in the bound state (C). Created with BioRender.com.

To simulate these two steps, the pipeline makes use of BD simulations for the first and MD simulations for the second. To separate the two steps, the space is discretized according to window distance to analyze the position of the ligand along the trajectories generated.

Molecular Dynamics Simulations

For calculating the probability of reacting during MD simulations of the second step of the binding process, the pipeline assigns a weight to each starting configuration based on its cluster population in the BD simulations. The α value is then calculated from the MD trajectories as shown in Figure .

Diagram showing how α is computed from the MD simulations. First, each MD trajectory j is weighted according to the cluster population (cl j pop) assigned from cluster analysis of the BD trajectories. Each MD trajectory is tracked to determine which window distances it visits and where it terminates. The accumulated weighted number of trajectories visiting a window distance is denoted as N i , while the accumulated weighted number of those terminating at a window distance is denoted as L i . Final ligand positions in MD trajectories are classified into three scenarios: (i) bound state, where the trajectory terminates by satisfying the reaction criterion distance of 4 Å; (ii) within BDrxn, where the accumulated weight N i of the terminating window is used to estimate the probability of reaching the bound state; and (iii) beyond BDrxn, where the number of BD trajectories that visited the terminating window is used to estimate the probability of reaching the bound state. Then, the α value is calculated by summing the number of weighted bound states and the probabilities of ligand binding within a certain window distance at the end of the MD trajectory, divided by the sum of the cluster populations of all the MD trajectories.

The structures of the apo-proteins (Table ) were prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard module of the Schrödinger suite (version 22, release 4). This included adding disulfide bonds, terminal oxygen (OXT) atoms, and completing missing loops, among others. The protein structures were then protonated at pH 7 using the PROPKA module of the PDB2PQR software. Additionally, the same software was utilized to assign partial atomic charges and radii based on the Amber force field and to generate PQR files.

The coordinates of the holo-proteins and ligands were extracted separately and the protonation states of the ligands were predicted using the Protein Preparation Wizard, with PROPKA at pH 7. The holo-proteins were also protonated with PDB2PQR employing the PROPKA module at pH 7. Following this, an energy minimization for the ligand hydrogens was conducted, utilizing the OPLS4 force field. Then, the protocol developed by Ganotra et al. was applied to determine the molecular electrostatic potential of ligands. For this purpose, the GAMESS software was utilized with the 6–31G basis set, except for iodo-tuberdicin, for which the aug-cc-pvtz HF level was applied to account for the polarizability of iodine. Lastly, partial atomic charges were assigned to the ligands by fitting the computed electrostatic potential using the RESP module of AMBER v.22.

Brownian Dynamics Simulations

The APBS software v.1.1.0 was applied to compute the electrostatic potential grids for both the apo-proteins and the ligands. A grid spacing of 1 Å was utilized, solvent and solute dielectric constants were set to 78 and 2, respectively, the molecular surface was used to define the dielectric boundary and an ionic radius of 1.5 Å was set. The linearized Poisson–Boltzmann equation was solved under the single Debye–Hückel dielectric boundary condition. Temperature and ionic strength were set to match experimental conditions. ,,,

The SDA7.3.4 software was used for BD simulations. The effective charge model (ECM) was employed to reduce the number of charges and fit them to reproduce the electrostatic potential previously generated with APBS in a uniform dielectric medium surrounding the molecule. To determine the effective charges of the proteins, the standard protocol implemented in SDA was followed. However, for the ligands, the protocol of Ganotra et al. was applied to assign test charges (Figure ). These test charges are positioned on the polar and halogen atoms of the ligand.

Scheme for the computation of test charges for small molecule ligands employed in SDA7. As SDA7 uses an implicit hydrogen model, the partial charges of the hydrogens are added to the polar atoms to which they are covalently bonded. To ensure that the net charge of the molecule is maintained, one of the steps involves calculating the charge difference between the net charge and the cumulative charges of these polar atoms. This difference is then distributed among the polar atoms of the ligand to generate the new test charges..

The electrostatic desolvation and nonpolar desolvation grids were generated with a grid spacing of 1 Å. To calculate the electrostatic desolvation grids, the ionic strength was set to zero and a factor of 0.36 was applied. For nonpolar desolvation, the hydrophobic factor was set to −0.013. However, a refined model was applied to generate the nonpolar desolvation grid by ignoring the atoms with nonzero formal charge (Figure ). The SASA value for these atoms was also set to 0 for computing the nonpolar desolvation force contribution.

Refined model for the nonpolar desolvation term used to compute intermolecular forces in the BD simulations of the association of a small molecule with a protein. Isocontours are shown for the nonpolar desolvation grid of benzamidine generated with SDA7 (A) and for the refined grid excluding the contribution of atoms with a nonzero formal charge (the amino groups in benzamidine) (B). The reduction of the nonpolar desolvation term with this refinement results in a weaker interaction of these ligand atoms with the protein and was found to be more consistent with experimental findings.

Both the solvent and the probe radii were set to 1.4 Å, while leaving all remaining parameters at their default values.

Hydropro was utilized to calculate the translational and rotational diffusion coefficients. The atomic element radius (AER) was set to 2.9 Å for proteins and 1.2 Å for ligands.

For defining reaction criteria for formation of diffusional encounter complexes, the holo-complexes were analyzed. Polar contacts were identified, which involve hydrogen bond acceptor–donor pairs, as well as halogen-aromatic, aromatic–aromatic π–π, methyl-aromatic and nonaromatic ring-π contacts in the holo-states. These nonaromatic rings were required not to contain any polar atom nor any substituent. The coordinates of these contacts were extracted from the bound state structure and added to the reaction list when they were within distances of 3.5 Å for polar hydrogen bonds and 4.5 Å for other contacts. Then, amino acid sequence alignments between the holo- and apo-proteins were applied to identify and replace the atoms of the holo-protein involved in the reaction criteria with the corresponding atoms in the apoprotein, for which the BD trajectories were run. The distance threshold that was set between pairs of contacts to be considered independent (SDA parameter: dind) was 2 Å. Methyl-aromatic and the nonaromatic ring contacts that were closer than this threshold to another atom involved in an aromatic–aromatic, halogen-aromatic or polar–polar interaction were removed from the reaction criteria list. The reason is that polar–polar, halogen-aromatic and aromatic–aromatic interactions are much more specific than the methyl-aromatic and nonaromatic ring interactions and therefore are better contacts to proper orient the ligand in the encounter complex pose.

250,000 BD simulation trajectories were run to calculate the probability of forming an encounter complex. These trajectories started from an initial center-to-center distance of 100 Å between the solutes (b surface) for all systems. These trajectories were terminated when the ligand reached a center-to-center distance of 200 Å (q surface) or when it satisfied the reaction criteria at the BDrxn window distance (Figure ). A linear variation in the time step was applied, ranging from 1 ps when the center-to-center separation was 60 Å to 20 ps at a separation of 100 Å. To define a set of encounter complexes for k on calculations, various numbers of contacts were explored, ranging from 1 to 10, along with their corresponding distances. These distances spanned from less than 4 to 21 Å, with window intervals of 0.5 Å, which makes a total of 35 window distances monitored (n win). Up to 5000 encounter complexes that formed all possible independent contacts within window distances shorter than or equal to BD rxn were selected for clustering analysis, ensuring at least one contact at the BDrxn window distance (Figure ). A total of 50 clusters were generated and the most representative encounter complex per cluster was used as an initial configuration in each of the 50 MD simulations. To identify the path followed by the ligand, the percentage of reactive trajectories visiting the protein’s residues was calculated. This information was then used to replace the B-factor values in the protein PDB file for visualization purposes.

Molecular Dynamics Simulations

Atomistic explicit solvent MD simulations to fully account for desolvation effects and explicit water molecule interactions with the active site and the ligand were conducted using the Amber version 22 software. The protein and ligand were parametrized with the AMBER ff14SB and GAFF force fields respectively. Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) electrostatics was employed and a cutoff of 10 Å was set for nonbonded interactions. A periodic box with the TIP3P water model was generated to solvate the system, maintaining a distance of 10 Å between the solutes and the box boundaries. Na+ and Cl- ions were introduced to neutralize the system’s net charge and mimic the ionic strength of the experimental conditions. ,,,

Association Rate Constant Calculation

Following the NAM algorithm, the k on value was calculated based on the information from the two molecular simulations. The α value is the reaction probability from the start of the MD region to the bound state in the binding site, the β value is the binding probability from the b-surface to the MD region (BDrxn) and the Δ value is the probability that the ligand rebinds to the MD region after leaving it (Figure ).

A bootstrapping analysis was conducted to calculate the standard deviation of the k on value with 1000 iterations, leaving a random number of data points out at each iteration.

The pipeline is implemented with the aid of the SDAMD workflow which contains a set of scripts whose functionality includes setting up and running the BD and MD simulations and analyzing them to compute k on values. The scripts of the SDAMD workflow are available in the SDA 7.4.0 release and input files are described in detail in Muñiz-Chicharro et al.

Statistical Analysis for Comparison of Computed and Experimental k on Values

To quantitatively evaluate the agreement between the calculated vs experimental k on values, a Spearman rank correlation analysis with the scipy.stats package (https://scipy.org/; v1.16.0) was conducted. This correlation measure was chosen as it does not assume linearity, but instead evaluates monotonic trends based on the rank order of the values, and therefore is less sensitive to zero values or transformations in the scale such as logarithmic conversions.

In addition, p-values were computed with the scipy.stats package to quantify the probability of obtaining the observed differences between the computed and the experimental k on values by using a two-tailed paired t test to evaluate whether they are consistent with the null hypothesis (H 0), for which the mean difference is zero. A p-value lower than 0.05 was used to reject the null hypothesis, indicating a significant difference between computed and experimental values.

Results

We developed a multiscale simulation pipeline that integrates BD and MD simulations to calculate the k on value for protein–ligand complexation. The pipeline includes reaction criteria that connect these two simulation methods, allowing for an efficient transition between them. To compute k on values, we employed the NAM algorithm. Moreover, we accounted for critical factors such as the protonation of proteins and ligands, ab initio calculation of partial charges of the ligand, and the use of the apo-structure of the protein to ensure that the pipeline allows the calculation of the k on value in cases where the bound complex has not been determined experimentally.

Reaction Criteria and Simulation Time

The reaction criteria are defined as a set of contacts for identifying the formation of encounter complexes during BD simulations and the bound states during MD simulations. Therefore, the accurate identification of these contacts is important to correctly compute the k on values for protein–ligand systems. During the development of the pipeline, we observed that the number of independent contacts required during a BD simulation significantly influences the orientation of the ligand in the encounter complex. A wrong orientation can result in fewer bound states in MD simulations, leading to underestimated k on values. To prevent this problem, we required all independent contacts in the list of reaction criteria computed from the holo-protein structure to be formed within the defined window distance, ensuring that the ligand adopts the correct orientation in the encounter complex (Figure ).

The crystallographic pose of rivaroxaban (PDB 2w26, with protein molecular solvent accessible surface in cyan) is displayed in magenta and the most populated cluster pose is displayed in gray. In pose (A), the reaction criteria were met with all seven independent contacts at 7.5 Å and the RMSD with the crystallographic structure was 3.693 Å, whereas in (B), only three independent contacts were fulfilled at 5.0 Å with an RMSD of 4.304 Å. In MD simulations, the encounter complex that results in a successful reaction is the pose (A) because the chlorine atom and the ring attached to it are already inside the binding pocket, whereas in pose (B), the ligand needs first to rotate and then enter the binding pocket.

The number of independent contacts that can be formed varies between systems and its precomputation is crucial to identify intermediate and bound states. In addition, it is necessary to record encounter complexes during BD simulations as close as possible to the active site at window distances satisfying reaction criteria that vary between systems (Table ). This approach to defining encounter complexes allows computational time to be saved during the MD part. The ligand is recorded at the shortest window distance where at least 50 BD trajectories out of the 250,000 generated result in formation of an encounter complex. A minimum of 50 encounter complexes is required in order to ensuring good sampling of the diffusional encounter complex ensemble. These encounter complexes are then clustered to select representative structures for beginning the 50 MD simulations. Once the initial configurational states have been generated, MD simulations are run until the ligand reaches the b-surface (100 Å) or the bound state, or the maximum simulation time of 10 ns is reached. Moreover, the comparison of MD simulation time (Table ) gives information about how fast or slow the postdiffusional binding step is, and indicates whether the binding event is mainly diffusional or postdiffusional.

The trypsin - benzamidine complex is relatively small and the ligand is rather rigid, which makes this system an ideal one to test new protocols due to its low complexity. Of all the systems tested, benzamidine is capable of reaching the shortest window distance (4.5 Å) (Table ) to the active site of its protein target during BD simulations. Therefore, the MD simulation time required for the postdiffusional part is the shortest, approximately 0.5 ns (Table ), as no major conformational changes are needed for this system to reach the bound state. These findings indicate that the process that governs the association rate constant for trypsin-benzamidine system is diffusion.

The binding processes of the haspin systems are different. The ligands are recorded at the greatest window distances compared to the other systems in the BD simulations and therefore the longest MD simulation times are needed (465.1–500.0 ns). This indicates that the process of binding for these compounds is one of the slowest among the systems studied. The longest MD simulation time is observed for tuberdicin without halogen substituents, since this ligand does not form any bound state with haspin in the simulations, consistent with it having the lowest measured k on value among the haspin ligands and among all the systems studied (Figure ).

Comparison of computed and measured k on values in M–1 s–1 for the evaluation set. Experimental data are shown in gray, the results of BD simulations in light green, and the results of the multiscale BD and MD simulations in orange. Standard deviations were calculated by bootstrapping.

The Factor Xa complexes also require some protein side-chain rearrangements for ligand binding, though to a lesser extent than the haspin complexes, necessitating only 27.5 and 58.2 ns of MD simulation time, respectively.

The neuraminidase complexes show interesting differences as regards the event that governs the binding process. Oseltamivir spends much less time in the postdiffusional step than zanamivir (Table ), indicating that the diffusional step governs its binding process. This observation is in agreement with Le et al., who found a significant role for the protein’s electrostatic funnel in the binding of oseltamivir. Another key factor is that the number of independent contacts to be formed by zanamivir with neuraminidase in the bound state is greater than for oseltamivir (Table ).

Computed Association Rate Constants

From the BD simulations, the ligand position is recorded as close as possible to the bound state (shortest window distance) while ensuring that the standard deviation of the k on value computed from the BD only is below 25%. This is accomplished by choosing the shortest window distance visited by at least 50 of the trajectories. After the BD simulations, a total of 50 MD replicas are prepared and simulated to evaluate whether the system reaches the bound state within 10 ns of simulated time.

It is important to note that the results from BD simulations are very similar for the different complexes, see Figure , indicating that diffusion is not the rate-limiting step for most of these complexes and that it is necessary to use MD simulations to account for induced fit effects. However, the values obtained after MD still have some discrepancies with respect to the experimental data. It is important to note that the multiscale approach was unable to calculate a value for the k on of haspin - tuberdicin since this system did not have enough time during MD simulations to reach the bound state, consistent with a very low binding rate. Therefore, although a value was not computed, the multiscale pipeline correctly computed this complex to have the lowest k on value among the complexes in the evaluation set. The trypsin - benzamidine, neuraminidase - oseltamivir and factor Xa - apixaban systems have slightly overestimated k on values compared to experiment, ,, while for haspin-bromotuberdicin and haspin-iodotuberdicin, the calculated values are slightly underestimated compared to experiment.

Another correlation to point out is the difference in the k on values between systems formed of the same protein and different ligands. For the haspin complexes, the experimental k on values are systematically lower when the halogen atom is smaller. However, this correlation is not reflected in the calculated k on values. The same issue arises in the factor Xa systems: the calculations do not correctly differentiate between the apixaban and rivaroxaban ligands. The experimental value for the factor Xa - rivaroxaban complex is approximately three times higher than for the factor Xa - apixaban complex, but the k on value calculated for these two systems is very similar. These discrepancies might suggest an issue related to halogen atoms and the importance of accounting for their polarizability or multipole moment in the force field.

In contrast with the halogen atom cases, the experimental trend is reproduced for the neuraminidase systems: the k on of oseltamivir is higher than of zanamivir, in good agreement with experimental data.

To assess the agreement between the calculated vs experimental k on values, a Spearman rank correlation analysis was conducted. When comparing the k on values obtained using the BD-only approach to experimental data, the Spearman coefficient is 0.24, indicating poor correlation. In contrast, and despite the differences already mentioned between the BD+MD approach and the experimental k on values, the Spearman rank correlation coefficient was 0.72, indicating a good correlation.

Additionally, two-tailed p-values were calculated for the BD and BD+MD data sets to quantify how well their k on values agree with experimental measurements. A t test was applied to test the null hypothesis (H 0) that the simulated and experimental data sets are similar, while the alternative hypothesis (H a) suggests a significant difference. For the comparison between BD+MD and experimental k on values, the p-value was 0.536. Considering a threshold confidence level of 0.05, we fail to reject the null hypothesis, suggesting no statistically significant difference between the BD+MD and experimental data sets. In contrast, the comparison between BD and experimental k on values yielded a p-value of 0.003. This value was also computed using the two-tailed test for consistency, although k on values calculated from BD-only simulations are expected to be overestimated. Since the p-value calculated is much lower than the 0.05 threshold, the null hypothesis is rejected, indicating a statistically significant difference between the BD and experimental data sets.

Ligand Binding Pathways

Analysis of the ligand binding pathways is important to reveal the binding mechanisms leading to protein–ligand complexation. To elucidate which binding pathways a ligand followed, we considered a residue to be visited when any of the ligand’s atoms came closer than 4 Å to any of the residue’s atoms. We then calculated the ratio of residues visited by dividing the number of trajectories that visited the residue by the total number of trajectories.

Trypsin-Benzamidine

It has been described in the literature that benzamidine rolls over the surface of trypsin before binding. However, this 2D diffusion was not detected in the trajectories recorded. Instead, the benzamidine compound shifts from one residue to another. This shifting happens mainly because we recorded one snapshot every 1000 BD steps and, although there may be some 2D diffusion in the simulations, it was not captured during this recording interval. In addition, the time step in BD simulations is 1 ps when the ligand is close to the protein, which is 500 times higher than in other studies using MD simulations with a 2 fs time step, leading to jumps in space that may hamper the observation of rolling. Despite these differences, there are similarities in the path that benzamidine follows to reach the active site of trypsin. In Figure , it can be seen that the binding path of benzamidine involves interactions with His57, Ser190 and sometimes Trp215 before entering the binding pocket, which is also described in Girame et al.

Haspin-Tuberdicin Halogen Derivatives

The binding of the tuberdicin halogen derivatives to the haspin protein follows a simpler path than the trypsin-benzamidine complex. These ligands bind directly from the bulk solvent to the hinge region of haspin in the BD simulations, and then they approach to the binding pocket (Figure ). Afterward, the ligands enter the binding pocket during the MD simulations.

Factor Xa - Apixaban/Rivaroxaban

The inhibitors rivaroxaban and apixaban follow a similar path when binding to factor Xa. They go into the S4 site. From there, the ligands explore different orientations until finding the most optimal one to enter the binding pocket in the MD simulations. The initial binding is in good agreement with other studies. ,, However, during the BD simulations, the ligands cannot fully bind to the S4 site due to the obstruction by Phe174 and Glu97 in the rigid-body model (Figure ). As a result, the ligands lie on top of these residues, and are subsequently able to bind to the S4 site upon induced fit during the MD simulations.

Zanamivir and Oseltamivir diffuse directly from the bulk solvent to interact with some residues forming the catalytic site (Arg118, Asp151, Arg152, Arg292 and Arg371) during BD simulations (Figure ). Conclusions

We describe a multiscale pipeline that combines BD and MD simulations to efficiently calculate k on values. The pipeline was evaluated on ten different protein–ligand systems that are diverse in binding rates, size and flexibility. After computing the k on values by combining BD and MD simulations, and comparing the results quantitatively with Spearman rank coefficient and p-value measures, we find that the combination of BD and MD simulations provides a more accurate description of the binding process to compute k on values than using BD-only simulations. This is expected, as the k on values from BD-only simulations tend to be overestimated due to their sole consideration of diffusion processes and neglect of conformational changes. Moreover, the combined approach makes it possible to derive insights into the factors determining association rates.

The treatment of induced fit effects in the combined BD and MD simulation approach is particularly useful when the binding events involve steric clashes that need to be resolved during the MD simulations. The binding poses identified are very similar to the experimentally determined bound states and the computed k on values are in good agreement with experimental values reported in the literature. Only for the haspin-tuberdicin complex, was no bound state observed, indicating that the on-rate was below the threshold for obtaining a computed value. Given that each MD replica simulation has a maximum duration of 10 ns, the absence of bound states in the haspin-tuberdicin case is consistent with the slow binding process characterized by an experimental k on value of 2.0 × 105 ± 1.6 × 104 M–1 s–1. Additionally, the k on values computed for compounds containing large halogen atoms, i.e.,: bromine and iodine, are lower compared to experimental values, possibly due to the use of a nonpolarizable force field.

The pipeline is able to provide insights into the pathway(s) that the ligand follows into the protein binding pocket. Moreover, the binding regime (diffusional or postdiffusional) can be studied by examining the duration of the MD trajectories. A short MD simulation time indicates that minimal conformational adjustments were required to achieve the bound state and that binding is governed by the diffusional step. In such cases, the efficiency of the protocol increases, allowing the calculation of k on values within hours.
