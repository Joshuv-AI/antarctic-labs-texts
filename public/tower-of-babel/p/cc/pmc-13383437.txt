Clinical profile and hepatocellular cancer treatment patterns in resource limited settings- a cross-sectional observational study at two tertiary referral teaching hospitals, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia
Authors: Tebarek Jemal Hassen, Shimelis Nigussie, Henok Teshome, Henok Seife
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) imposes a high global burden. A significant treatment gap exists in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where low rates of curative surgical intervention highlight profound healthcare inequities.ObjectiveTo evaluate the clinical profile and treatment patterns of HCC patients at TASH and SPHMMC Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.MethodsThis hospital-based cross-sectional observational study included 130 HCC patients. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and described using means and ± SD, while medians and IQR used for skewed data.ResultsThe mean age was 54.5 ± 16.5 years with a 63.1% male predominance. Although viral hepatitis was common (47.7%; HBV: 72.6%, HCV: 22.6%), a high proportion of patients (37.0%) presented with cryptogenic (unidentified) risk factors. Late-stage presentation was characteristic, evidenced by a mean tumor size of 10.0(± 4.6) cm and 63.8% of patients being BCLC stage C. 50% of cirrhotic patients were Child-Pugh B. These factors limited curative options, with resection performed in only 17.7% of the cohort, while 27.7% received TACE.ConclusionMost HCC patients in this setting present with advanced-stage disease and large tumor burdens, leading to low curative resection rates. Addressing deficiencies in specialized human resources, infrastructure, and policy gaps is critical to improving access to life-saving surgical and loco-regional interventions.

Introduction

Liver cancer represents a significant global health challenge, contributing substantially to cancer-related mortality, morbidity, disability, and economic burden [1–8]. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for the majority of liver cancer cases, comprising 75–85% [9, 10], while intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma constitutes the remaining 20–25%. The cellular origin of HCC can vary, potentially arising from liver stem cells, transit amplifying populations, or mature hepatocytes, all in response to various oncogenic insults. The pathophysiological development of HCC is a complex and multi-step process. HCC development is a complex, multistep process. More than 90% of HCC cases arise in the setting of chronic liver disease, with cirrhosis—regardless of etiology—being the strongest single risk factor. Early malignant transformation of hepatocytes involves proto-oncogene activation, loss of tumor suppressor function, impaired apoptosis, neoangiogenesis, and immune suppression; together these changes drive uncontrolled cellular proliferation and tumor formation [11]. A detailed understanding of the molecular drivers of HCC is essential to advance targeted therapies. Nearly half of HCC patients harbor at least one recurrent oncogenic mutation, with TP53, CTNNB1, and TERT among the most common. The dysregulated signaling in HCC broadly falls into two categories. The first comprises receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK)–driven pathways—including VEGFR, EGFR, c-Met, PDGFR, FGFR, IGFR, KIT, and RET—and their downstream effectors such as the MAPK, PI3K, and JAK–STAT pathways, which govern proliferation, survival, and angiogenesis. The second includes cell-differentiation and tissue-homeostasis pathways—Wnt/β-catenin, Hippo, Hedgehog, and Notch—which are critical for maintaining liver architecture and function. Etiologic factors induce these signals and mutations through distinct mechanisms [11]. Leading risk factors for HCC include chronic hepatitis B and C infection, alcohol use, dysbiosis of the gut microbiota, aflatoxin exposure, and the metabolic spectrum of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) [10, 12]. Radiologic diagnosis commonly relies on characteristic CT and MRI patterns; however, approximately 10% of tumors—especially lesions 1–2 cm in size—may lack the classic imaging hallmarks, presenting a diagnostic challenge. Staging is frequently performed with the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) system, which integrates tumor burden, liver function, and performance status to guide management. Curative options for early-stage HCC include surgical resection and liver transplantation. When surgery is not feasible, local ablative techniques—particularly radiofrequency ablation (RFA)—are the most widely used nonsurgical interventions. For intermediate-stage disease, transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) has been the standard of care for the past two decades. The advanced HCC will first receive systemic therapies [13–15].

Methodology

Study area and period

This study was conducted within the Departments of Surgery, Internal Medicine, and Oncology at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH) and St. Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College (SPHMMC).

TASH, established in 1974, is the main teaching hospital for the Schools of Medicine, Pharmacy, Public Health, and Allied Health Sciences. As the largest referral and teaching hospital in Addis Ababa, it provides comprehensive services to ~ 400,000 patients annually, employs over 1,500 clinical and academic staff, has more than 800 inpatient beds, and is the country’s leading high-volume center for hepatobiliary and pancreatic surgery.

SPHMMC is a tertiary teaching hospital with over 2,800 staff, more than 700 inpatient beds, and an average of 1,200 emergency and outpatient visits daily. It offers nationwide specialized services, undergraduate and postgraduate training, and conducts basic and applied research. The hospital includes a dedicated Hepato-Pancreato-Biliary (HPB) unit within General Surgery (staffed by fellows), a Gastroenterology unit in Internal Medicine (with fellows), endoscopy and interventional radiology services, and essential departments such as Pathology, Emergency Medicine, Anesthesiology, and ICUs. The study period was eight months: October 1, 2024 – May 30, 2025.

Study design

Hospital based cross-sectional observational study design was employed. The study was conducted at two tertiary referral hospitals in Ethiopia. Data were collected from enrolled patients at the time of their diagnosis and initial treatment decision-making. The study aimed to describe the clinical profile, identified risk factors, diagnostic workup, and the range of treatment options for HCC patients in these resource-limited settings.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

Given the descriptive nature of this analysis and small calculated sample size, all eligible patients presenting within the defined study period at two high-volume hepatobiliary and pancreatic centers were included in the analysis. All adult patients age above 18years with a diagnosis of HCC are included in the study. This strategy aimed to capture a broad representation of the patient population undergoing treatment for relevant conditions at these specialized centers, acknowledging that the findings are primarily descriptive and may not be generalizable beyond similar clinical settings.

According to a study done at TASH from July 2016 to July 2017, a total of 70 HCC patients were included. This study used 2/3 of the population size, i.e. 47 in 8 months study period.

Data collection procedures and analysis

A structured questionnaire utilized in the study was crafted and encompassed sections on sociodemographic data, clinical profiles, surgical intervention, radiological and pathological report, laboratory and surgical outcomes. Prior to the primary data collection, the questionnaire underwent a pretest with 5% of the sample size to ensure the clarity and relevance of the questions. Apart from the questionnaire, electronic medical records were employed to retrieve clinical, surgical, and medical history review. Data collection was done by trained two general practitioners and the principal investigator checked the data consistency and completeness.

Following data collection, each questionnaire was reviewed for completeness, referencing the codes assigned during the data collection process. The data were then entered into Epi-Info version 7 before being exported and analyzed using SPSS version 25. Data analysis was performed using SPSS software, version 25. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, and comparisons between groups were conducted using the chi-square test. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were described using means and standard deviations, while skewed data were characterized using medians and interquartile ranges.

Clinical profile

The predominant presenting complaint among the study participants was abdominal pain, reported by 112 out of 130 individuals (86.2%), with a mean duration of 14.9 ± 11.3 weeks. Significant weight loss was also a common symptom, observed in 80 participants (61.5%). Upper gastrointestinal (UGI) bleeding, however, was a less frequent presentation, occurring in only 5 patients (3.8%). Nearly half of the participants (62/130, 47.7%) had a history of hepatitis infection. Among those with hepatitis, the majority were infected with Hepatitis B Virus (HBV) (45/62, 72.6%), while a smaller proportion had Hepatitis C Virus (HCV) (14/62, 22.6%). Co-infection with both HBV and HCV was present in 3 participants (3/62, 4.8%). Chronic alcohol use was reported in 18 participants (13.8%). Significantly high proportion (48/130, 37%) of patients in our cohort have unidentified risk factor for the HCC development. The majority of participants had a relatively good Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status: 20 participants (15.4%) had an ECOG score of 0, and 75 participants (57.7%) had an ECOG score of 1. A substantial proportion of patients (81.5%) exhibited alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels above the 75th percentile. A detailed breakdown of the clinical presentations is provided in Table 2; Fig. 1.

Diagnosis and prognostic profiles in the study cohort

In nearly all cases, triphasic abdominal computed tomography (CT) scans served as the primary diagnostic modality, utilized in 112 out of 130 participants (86.2%) and for those with inconclusive imaging findings histopathology was done in 16 patients (12.3%). Image analysis revealed vascular invasion in more than half of the cases (74/130, 56.9%) out of which 59.5% were intrahepatic invasions and 40.5% involved extra-hepatic invasion, while distance metastasis was present in 30 participants (23.1%). The most common site of distant metastasis was lung (63.4%, 19/30). Furthermore, imaging features of advanced HCC, extra-hepatic vascular invasion and/or extra-hepatic distance metastasis were found in 36.9%, of our patients. Signs of portal hypertension were observed in 34/130 participants (26.2%), while 58/130 participants (44.6%) exhibited evidence of cirrhosis. The majority of HCC cases presented as either multinodular/multifocal (58/130, 44.6%) or single/solitary (57/130, 44.5%) lesions with mean size 10.0 ± 4.6 cm. Adequate future liver remnant (FLR) was observed in 58/130 participants (44.6%). In terms of tumor location, the right lobe of the liver was the most frequently affected (54/130, 41.5%) and bilobar involvement (55/130, 42.3%) (Table 3; Fig. 2).

Based on the Albumin-Bilirubin (ALBI) grade, the majority of patients presented with either ALBI grade 1 (30/130, 23.1%) or ALBI grade 2 (84/130, 64.6%). Among those who underwent liver resection, left hepatectomy (8/23, 34.8%) and right hepatectomy (5/23, 21.7%) were the most common types of resections performed (Table 4; Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study offers a detailed and comprehensive analysis of the demographic characteristics, diagnostic pathways, treatment strategies, and existing gaps in the management of Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC) within the context of two national high-volume hepatobiliary and pancreatic centers in Ethiopia. Our investigation revealed a significant male predominance in the study population, with HCC primarily occurring in individuals around 50 years of age. This sociodemographic profile aligns consistently with findings from previous global, regional, and local studies, suggesting a relatively stable pattern across different populations. For instance, a multinational study conducted across several African nations reported that the mean age at HCC diagnosis in Egypt was 58 years, whereas the corresponding figure for the remaining African countries was notably lower at 46 years [16]. Similarly, previous studies conducted within Ethiopia have reported a male predominance in HCC cases (approximately 67%) and a mean age of around 52.0 years [17], findings that are consistent with the results obtained in our current study. These observed similarities may be attributed to the distribution of known HCC risk factors, which often vary across different sexes and geographic locations.

A multitude of risk factors, both modifiable and non-modifiable, contribute to the complex pathophysiology of Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC). These factors can either directly exert oncogenic effects on liver cells or promote the development of cirrhosis, which then serves as a fertile ground for HCC development [18–21]. In our study, we observed that chronic hepatitis infections were associated with 47.7% of HCC cases, with HBV accounting for 34.6%, HCV for 10.8%, and co-infection of HBV and HCV for 2.3%. Currently, overall medical screening for HCC risk factors in Ethiopia is critically inadequate and fragmented. Screening for viral hepatitis, the most common risk factors in our study is sub-optimal and primarily opportunistic (blood donors, pre-surgery, antenatal care, presenting liver disease stigmata) rather than systematic population-wide screening. Alcohol consumption is another significant risk factor in our study (13.8%) despite the high likelihood of under reporting and inconsistent screening mechanism for alcoholic liver disease (ALD). Our study revealed that significant proportion (37%) of HCC patients have unidentified risk factors, the often missed risk factors among others are Aflatoxin and Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Fatty Liver Disease (MAFLD). Our traditional storage of staple foods (cereals) and the emerging epidemic of MAFLD from rapid urbanization, dietary shifts (increased processed foods, carbohydrates, unhealthy fats), and sedentary lifestyles are driving an unmeasured rise in MAFLD prevalence in Ethiopia. Both Aflatoxin and MAFLD screening are virtually non-existent at the population or primary care level. This systemic gap is primarily driven by absent clinical or public health screening for Aflatoxin biomarkers due to cost and technical complexity, shortages in diagnostic tools, and low public/provider cognition regarding these critical etiologies [22, 23].

Our findings otherwise are generally consistent with global, African, and previous Ethiopian studies, although the precise proportions of each etiological factor may vary depending on the specific population and geographic location. On a global scale, chronic HBV and HCV infections are recognized as the most significant etiological factors for HCC, accounting for 50–80% of all HCC cases [24]. Chronic HBV infection is particularly prevalent as a cause of HCC in Eastern Asian countries and the majority of African countries, with the notable exception of Northern Africa [24], where HCV prevalence is highest. A multi-national study encompassing 2566 patients across 21 tertiary referral centers in various African countries (including Egypt, Nigeria, Ghana, Ivory Coast, Cameroon, Sudan, Ethiopia, Tanzania, and Uganda) revealed that HBV was the leading cause of HCC in the majority of African countries (55%) [16]. However, in Egypt, HCV was identified as the predominant etiological factor, accounting for 84% of HCC cases [16]. This difference may be attributed to the high prevalence of HCV in Egypt compared to other African nations. It is worth noting that HCV infection is primarily associated with the development of cirrhosis, which subsequently increases the risk of HCC. On the other hand, HBV can exert both direct oncogenic effects on liver cells and contribute to the development of cirrhosis, thereby providing a “fertile ground” for HCC development. Nearly half of the patients in our study presented with advanced disease characteristics, including large tumor size, vascular invasion, and a multinodular or multifocal presentation, often against a backdrop of cirrhosis. Despite this advanced presentation, almost half of the patients also had adequate future liver remnant (FLR). Alarmingly, only 13% of the patients were diagnosed at BCLC stage A, leading to a situation where less than 18% ultimately received curative liver resection. This highlights a significant challenge in HCC management: the lack of early detection and screening, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), results in a disproportionate number of patients being diagnosed at advanced stages, limiting treatment options and contributing to poor survival rates in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [25, 26].

In our study, curative liver resection was performed in approximately 18% of cases, a figure that is significantly higher compared to a multi-center study in Ethiopia where resection was conducted in less than 8% of cases [17]. Additionally, Transarterial Chemoembolization (TACE) was performed in approximately 28% of our patients, a substantially higher rate than the 2.8% [17] reported in the aforementioned multi-center study. Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) was utilized in 3% of cases, a step forward considering that previous studies in Ethiopia reported zero RFA utilization [17]. The remaining majority patients primarily received systemic therapy and best supportive care. Hepatic resection is considered the gold standard curative treatment for early-stage HCC, particularly for those without cirrhosis who are classified as BCLC stage 0 or A.

The INSIGHT study, a multi-site longitudinal cohort study conducted in several Asia-Pacific countries, reported a liver resection rate of 34%, primarily for patients with BCLC stage A and B HCC [27]. Ablative therapy (RFA) was performed in 8% of patients, while locoregional therapy (TACE) was utilized in 23.5% of cases [27]. The BRIDGE Study reported varying resection rates across different regions: 18% in North America, 16% in Europe, and 33% in Asia [28]. TACE utilization was also variable, with rates of 33% in North America, 29% in Europe, and 35% in Asia [28]. In stark contrast, a multi-national study conducted in Africa involving 2566 patients across 21 tertiary referral centers observed resection rates of less than 1%, RFA rates of 0%, and TACE rates of 1% in most African countries [16]. Egypt, however, showed slightly better progress, with resection performed in 2% of cases, transplantation in 1%, RFA in 32%, and TACE in 36% [16]. Another systematic review from 15 sun-Saharan African countries showed curative treatment was 6% and 84% received best supportive care only [29]. These significant differences in treatment modalities likely stem from variations in infrastructure and human capacity, healthcare policies, and prioritization of resources across different geographic regions.

Ethiopia, with a population exceeding 130 million, has nearly one HPB surgeon per 6 million people and one interventional radiologist per 16 million, alongside a severe shortage of hepatologists and medical oncologists. Safe liver surgery is compromised by lack of specialized tools for preoperative optimization and resection. Access to locoregional therapies (TACE, RFA) and systemic medications is minimal and there is no national liver transplant program.

We found better treatment adherence to BCLC staging in BCLC A 82.5% and BCLC-D 77.3% while notable disparity in BCLC B(37.5%) and BCLC C(50.6%) stages likely due to limited access to alternative treatment options for those stages, and/or a trend towards personalized management to overcome some of the drawbacks of BCLC staging by navigating other more inclusive staging. Italian study on adherence of field practice to different BCLC stages [13, 30] was 81% in the BCLC A stage and dropped to 53% in the BCLC C stage patients: similar variation in treatment adherence and choice of initial modality noted in a larger INSIGHT Study [27] and BRIDGE Study [28].

The principal strength of this study is the generation of high-level evidence included a wide spectrum of clinical variables which enabled to generate better quality data regarding clinical characteristics and treatment landscape for HCC. As the study done in top tier referral centers the findings reflect the capabilities and challenges encountered at the highest echelon of care in Ethiopia, providing valuable insights into the systemic gaps in HCC management that are often amplified in less-resourced primary or secondary care settings. However, the study has important limitations. Our cohort were drawn from only two major referral centers, which may introduce selection bias and limit the broader applicability, inadequate screening of HCC patients for risk factors and limited availability of treatment options may affect selection of therapy, and lack of longitudinal data on treatment compliance or outcomes. Consequently, these findings characterize the diagnostic and management landscape rather than ultimate clinical success.

Future research should include prospective multicentre studies across various healthcare and geographic settings to evaluate treatment outcomes, including HCC recurrence and survival rates. They should also investigate the feasibility and effectiveness of different HCC screening strategies tailored to the Ethiopian population. Policy makers are urged to prioritize human resource capacity building, infrastructure expansion and modernization.

In conclusion, despite observed improvements in the surgical management of Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC), reflected in increased rates of curative resection and local ablative therapies and TACE, these interventions are often limited to patients with relatively good performance status and younger age profiles. The advanced stage at presentation, characterized by high rates of vascular invasion and large tumor size, contributes significantly to the challenges in achieving optimal outcomes and places Ethiopia behind high- and middle-income countries in HCC management. This disparity is further compounded by limitations in human resources, infrastructure, and advanced treatment options, most notably the absence of a liver transplantation center within the nation. The lack of transplantation options limits the curative potential for patients with end-stage liver disease and unresectable HCC.
