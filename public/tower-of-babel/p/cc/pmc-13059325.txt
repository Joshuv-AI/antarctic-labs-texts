Amplitude-integrated electroencephalography for early diagnosis and severity assessment of neonatal intracranial hemorrhage: a multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Yayong Liang, Ruonan Xiang, Yi Zhong, Jingwen Zhou, Weineng Lu, Sinian Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeonatal intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) poses substantial neurodevelopmental risks; however, existing neuroimaging modalities (ultrasound, CT, MRI) are limited by accessibility and safety concerns. This study aimed to evaluate the predictive value of amplitude-integrated electroencephalography (aEEG) parameters for the early diagnosis and severity assessment of neonatal ICH.MethodsIn this multicenter retrospective study, 383 neonates (253 with ICH, 130 without ICH) admitted between 2018 and 2023 were analyzed. aEEG parameters, including cerebral function monitoring (CFM) scores, amplitude limits, and sleep-wake cycling, were compared between ICH and non-ICH groups. ICH severity was classified according to the Papile criteria. Multivariable logistic regression identified independent predictors, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to assess diagnostic performance.ResultsCFM scores and lower margin of amplitude (LMA) were significantly reduced in neonates with ICH compared to controls, in both preterm and term subgroups (all P < 0.001). Multivariable logistic regression identified CFM score (OR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.21–0.68, P = 0.001) and lower limit of amplitude (OR = 0.01, 95% CI: 0.00–0.10, P < 0.001) as protective factors against ICH, while higher LMA was associated with increased risk (OR = 11.56, 95% CI: 3.09–43.20, P < 0.001). The diagnostic model demonstrated good discriminative ability, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.849 (95% CI: 0.819–0.879). Furthermore, lower CFM scores and LMA values correlated significantly with increasing ICH severity in term neonates (P < 0.05).ConclusionaEEG parameters, particularly CFM scores and LMA, serve as reliable indicators for the early diagnosis and severity assessment of neonatal ICH. As a non-invasive bedside tool, aEEG may complement traditional neuroimaging, facilitating earlier intervention and potentially improving neurodevelopmental outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13052-026-02217-6.

Introduction

Neonatal intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) remains one of the most common and serious neurological complications during the neonatal period, particularly among preterm and critically ill infants [1]. Despite significant advances in perinatal and neonatal care, including improved survival rates for extremely preterm neonates, the incidence of neurological sequelae associated with perinatal brain injury remains high [2]. Extremely preterm infants (< 28 weeks of gestational age) are at heightened risk of developing long-term neurodevelopmental impairments, including motor, cognitive, and learning disabilities [3–5]. Early detection, timely intervention, and continuous monitoring of neonatal brain injury are therefore critical to optimizing neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Among the various forms of neonatal brain injury, ICH—including subdural hemorrhage, subarachnoid hemorrhage, intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), and subependymal hemorrhage—is a major contributor to neonatal morbidity and mortality [6]. The immature cerebral vasculature of preterm infants, particularly the fragile germinal matrix, is highly vulnerable to fluctuations in cerebral blood flow, hypoxia, and other external stressors, thereby increasing the risk of hemorrhage [7, 8]. Additional risk factors such as low birth weight, perinatal asphyxia, and intrauterine distress have also been implicated in the development of ICH [9, 10].

Timely diagnosis of ICH is essential for initiating appropriate management and potentially mitigating long-term neurological damage. Currently, cranial ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are the primary neuroimaging modalities used for diagnosing neonatal ICH [11, 12]. However, each technique has inherent limitations. Although cranial ultrasound is convenient and widely accessible, it has limited sensitivity for detecting hemorrhages in peripheral brain regions or within the subdural and subarachnoid spaces due to acoustic window and angle constraints [13]. CT, while more sensitive, exposes neonates to ionizing radiation, making it unsuitable for repeated evaluations [14]. MRI offers superior spatial resolution but often necessitates transport and sedation, posing additional risks to unstable neonates [15]. These limitations highlight the urgent need for a safer, more accessible bedside modality for the early detection of ICH in neonates.

Amplitude-integrated electroencephalography (aEEG), a simplified form of continuous cerebral function monitoring (CFM), has emerged as a promising tool in this context. aEEG enables real-time, non-invasive, and continuous monitoring of cerebral background activity and seizure patterns, providing valuable insights into brain function and maturity [16]. In recent years, aEEG has gained increasing attention for its role in evaluating neonatal encephalopathy, hypoxic-ischemic brain injury, and predicting neurological outcomes in preterm infants [17, 18].

Although several studies have demonstrated the utility of aEEG in assessing neonatal neurological function and prognostication [16–18], its application in the early diagnosis of neonatal ICH remains inadequately explored. In particular, there is a lack of systematic research investigating which specific aEEG features—such as background patterns, sleep-wake cycling, voltage amplitudes, bandwidth, or CFM scores—can reliably differentiate neonates with and without ICH. Moreover, the diagnostic and prognostic performance of aEEG parameters may vary depending on gestational age, necessitating stratified analyses for preterm and term infants. Accordingly, the present study aimed to evaluate the predictive value of aEEG parameters for the diagnosis and severity assessment of neonatal intracranial hemorrhage. By analyzing a large multicenter cohort, this study sought to validate aEEG as a practical, bedside tool for the early detection of ICH and to facilitate timely clinical intervention.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study included neonates admitted to the neonatal intensive care units of Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center and the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University between January 2018 and July 2023. A total of 253 neonates diagnosed with ICH based on neuroimaging findings (cranial ultrasound, CT, or MRI) were enrolled in the ICH group, including 163 preterm and 90 term neonates. The non-ICH group comprised 130 neonates (53 preterm, 77 term) without evidence of ICH during the same period. All neonates underwent aEEG monitoring within 7 days of birth and cranial imaging examinations within 10 days of birth.

Inclusion criteria for neonatal ICH followed the standards described in Zhu Futang’s Practical Pediatrics (7th Edition) [19], which require both (i) high-risk clinical manifestations (e.g., prematurity, seizures, abnormal neurological excitability or suppression), and (ii) confirmation of intracranial hemorrhage on neuroimaging (cranial ultrasound, CT, or MRI). The Papile classification was used only for grading the severity of periventricular–intraventricular hemorrhage (PVH-IVH) in preterm infants after the diagnosis was established. Other hemorrhage types in term neonates (e.g., subdural or subarachnoid hemorrhage) were not graded using the Papile system. Exclusion criteria included intrauterine TORCH infections, sepsis, central nervous system infections, inherited metabolic diseases, chromosomal abnormalities, bilirubin encephalopathy, and hypoglycemic encephalopathy. This study was approved by the institutional review board of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (No. II2025-167-01). Written informed consent was obtained from the parents of the patients.

aEEG monitoring and parameter interpretation

aEEG monitoring was performed using the NicoletOne EEG monitor (Natus Neurology Incorporated, USA). All enrolled neonates underwent continuous aEEG monitoring within the first 7 days after birth. Each monitoring session lasted for 8 to 12 h. Cranial imaging was conducted using a Discovery MR750 3.0T MRI scanner (GE Healthcare, USA), a Sequoia 512 ultrasound system (Siemens, Germany), and a Prosound α5 ultrasound system (Aloka, Japan).

Electrode placement followed the international 10–20 system (Fig. 1) [20], with an 8-channel disc electrode setup: ground electrode at Fpz, reference electrodes at Fz and Cz, and recording electrodes at Fp1, Fp2, C3, C4, T3, T4, O1, and O2. The aEEG recordings represented multichannel background activity, and aEEG parameters were interpreted based on the overall multichannel patterns rather than being derived from a single electrode pair. The raw EEG signals were amplified, filtered (2–15 Hz), amplitude-compressed, and smoothed to generate a continuous compressed spectral display (CFM) at a recording speed of 6 cm/h [21]. Impedance was monitored throughout the recording to ensure signal quality. The obtained aEEG recordings were independently interpreted by at least two experienced neonatal neurologists.

The aEEG parameters evaluated in this study included amplitude, sleep-wake cycling (SWC), background activity, continuity, epileptiform activity, and bandwidth, based on the criteria described by Hellström-Westas et al. [22]. Amplitude was assessed by measuring the upper margin amplitude (UMA) and lower margin amplitude (LMA). Sleep-wake cycling was evaluated by observing the cyclic variations in the bandwidth, reflecting alternations between active and quiet sleep phases, as validated in developmental studies of neonates [23]. Background activity was classified into five categories: continuous normal voltage, discontinuous normal voltage, burst suppression, continuous low voltage, and flat trace. Continuity refers to the stability of EEG amplitude fluctuations over time, categorized as either continuous or discontinuous. Epileptiform activity was defined by abrupt, repetitive, or evolving patterns consistent with seizure discharges. Bandwidth, calculated as the difference between the upper and lower margins, was used to reflect the degree of cerebral maturation.

Brain functional maturity assessment

Brain functional maturity was evaluated using the Burdjalov scoring system [24], which provides a quantitative assessment based on aEEG characteristics. The scoring system integrates four key components: continuity of background activity, SWC, lower margin of amplitude, and the relationship between bandwidth and the lower margin of amplitude. Each component is assigned a score ranging from 0 to 5 according to specific aEEG patterns.

Continuity was assessed based on voltage stability, with higher scores indicating more continuous background activity and higher lower margins of amplitude. Sleep-wake cycling was evaluated by the presence, clarity, and regularity of cyclic changes; progressively higher scores corresponded to more mature and stable SWC patterns. The lower margin of amplitude was classified from severe suppression (< 3 µV) to no suppression (> 5 µV). The bandwidth and lower margin relationship considered both the voltage span and the level of the lower margin, with low-span and higher lower margin features indicating greater maturity.

A total maturity score was calculated by summing the subscores across the four domains, with higher total scores reflecting greater cerebral maturation. Reference scores by gestational age were used for validation, with typical scores ranging from 2 points at 24–25 weeks of gestation to 13 points at 36–37 weeks.

Neuroimaging assessment

All neonates underwent cranial ultrasound within 10 days of birth. Additional CT or MRI examinations were performed when necessary. The severity of ICH was graded according to the Papile grading system [24], distinguishing between mild (grades I–II, subdural or subarachnoid hemorrhage) and moderate-to-severe (grades III-IV, parenchymal or cerebellar hemorrhage) hemorrhage.

Clinical data collection

Comprehensive clinical data were systematically collected from medical records. Maternal data included perinatal conditions, the presence of gestational hypertension, and the mode of delivery. Neonatal data comprised sex, gestational age at birth, birth weight, birth length, head circumference, and Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min. Blood gas analysis results, including pH, partial pressure of oxygen (PO₂), partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO₂), lactate, bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻), and base excess (BE), were also recorded. In addition, aEEG recordings and cranial imaging findings were reviewed and analyzed to support diagnostic classification and further evaluation.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using independent-sample t-tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA). Non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) and compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess associations between gestational age and aEEG parameters in different subgroups (all neonates, preterm neonates, and term neonates, both with and without ICH). Group comparisons of aEEG parameters were conducted between ICH and non-ICH neonates in both preterm and term cohorts. Logistic regression analyses were used to identify independent predictors of ICH. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analyses were included in multivariate models using a stepwise selection method. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted to evaluate the predictive performance of aEEG parameters. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM, USA).

Predictive value of aEEG parameters for ICH

Because gestational age and growth parameters differed significantly between ICH and non-ICH neonates (Table 1), these variables were included as covariates in the multivariable logistic regression model to account for baseline imbalances and ensure an unbiased evaluation of the predictive contribution of aEEG parameters. In the adjusted model, multivariable logistic regression identified CFM score (OR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.21–0.68, P = 0.001) and lower margin of amplitude (OR = 0.01, 95% CI: 0.00–0.10, P < 0.001) as protective factors, while LMA was associated with increased ICH risk (OR = 11.56, 95% CI: 3.09–43.20, P < 0.001). The model demonstrated strong discriminative power (AUC = 0.849, 95% CI: 0.819–0.879; Fig. 2; Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the potential of aEEG as a bedside tool for the early diagnosis and severity assessment of ICH in neonates. By analyzing aEEG parameters in 253 neonates with ICH and 130 controls, we confirmed that CFM scores, LMA, and lower limit of amplitude were independent predictors of ICH. Among them, the CFM score exhibited the strongest negative correlation with ICH severity. These findings highlight the clinical utility of aEEG as a non-invasive, real-time monitoring technique, particularly when compared with conventional neuroimaging modalities such as CT and MRI, which are associated with risks including radiation exposure and the need for patient sedation [14, 15].

Our study found that neonates with ICH exhibited significantly lower CFM scores and LMA compared to non-ICH neonates, in both preterm and term groups. Specifically, in preterm neonates, the mean CFM score was markedly lower in the ICH group (P < 0.001), and the LMA was significantly reduced (P < 0.001). In term neonates, the CFM score and LMA were also significantly lower in the ICH group compared to controls (P < 0.001 and P = 0.023, respectively). Categorical aEEG parameters, including background activity, continuity, and sleep-wake cycling, differed significantly between groups, supporting the role of aEEG in distinguishing between neonates with and without ICH. Logistic regression analysis further confirmed that CFM score (OR = 0.38) and lower margin of amplitude (OR = 0.01) were protective factors, whereas LMA (OR = 11.56) was associated with an increased risk of ICH. Notably, the predictive model achieved an AUC of 0.849 (95% CI: 0.819–0.879), indicating clinically meaningful discriminative power for ICH risk stratification. This AUC value surpasses the threshold of 0.8 generally considered indicative of strong diagnostic utility [25], suggesting that aEEG parameters could serve as viable biomarkers for early hemorrhage detection.

The observed aEEG alterations may be attributable to hemorrhagic injury-induced disruption of cortical network dynamics. Emerging evidence suggests that these pathological changes may involve multiple interacting mechanisms: (1) hypoxia secondary to impaired cerebral perfusion [26], (2) mechanical distortion of neural architecture by hematoma expansion [27], and (3) neuroinflammatory cascades mediated by cytotoxic CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration [28]. Collectively, these processes could compromise neuronal synchronization, potentially explaining the characteristic aEEG patterns of diminished LMA and background discontinuity. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that aEEG alterations are sensitive markers of cerebral injury in neonates [17, 18].

aEEG for assessing ICH severity

We further explored the relationship between aEEG parameters and ICH severity based on the Papile grading system [24]. The results revealed that more severe ICH (grades III-IV) was associated with greater abnormalities in aEEG patterns. In term neonates, moderate-to-severe ICH was linked to significantly lower CFM scores, more discontinuous background activity, absent or immature sleep-wake cycling, suppressed lower margin of amplitude, reduced bandwidth spans, and the observation of seizure-like activities, although statistical comparisons for seizures were not performed. Continuous variable analysis confirmed a stepwise decline in CFM scores and LMA values with increasing hemorrhage severity. This progression aligns with experimental evidence showing that larger hemorrhages trigger more frequent cortical spreading depolarizations (CSDs), which propagate through neuroinflammatory cascades and microvascular dysfunction to induce widespread cortical injury [29]. Similarly, in preterm neonates, those with ICH showed more abnormal aEEG findings compared to their non-ICH counterparts, including lower CFM scores, less continuous background activity, and less mature sleep-wake cycles. Although stratification by hemorrhage severity was not performed in preterm infants, the overall trend was consistent with findings in term neonates. These observations support the use of aEEG not only for early detection of ICH but also for severity stratification, which is critical for risk assessment and early intervention planning. In addition to aEEG markers, Multivariable regression analysis identified metabolic indices, such as elevated lactate levels (OR = 3.36) and larger negative base excess values (OR = 6.10), as independent predictors of ICH severity. Given that hypoxic-ischemic processes are known contributors to neonatal brain injury [30], these findings suggest that similar mechanisms may contribute to neonatal ICH pathophysiology.

Our findings underscore the clinical value of aEEG as a bedside tool for the early identification and grading of ICH in neonates. In this cohort, neonates with ICH exhibited mean CFM scores below 10 (preterm: 9.18; term: 10.32) and LMA values less than 7 µV (preterm: 6.21; term: 7.07). While these observed values may serve as preliminary references, further studies are needed to establish validated cutoffs through ROC analysis, balancing sensitivity and specificity for clinical application. Continuous aEEG monitoring may assist in assessing dynamic changes in neurological status, with lower CFM scores associated with more severe hemorrhage. Additionally, the loss of sleep-wake cycling was significantly more frequent in neonates with ICH and may indicate more extensive brain injury. Given its portability and non-invasive nature, aEEG provides a practical tool for early screening and assessment of neonatal ICH, particularly in resource-limited settings.

The strengths of this study include a relatively large sample size (n = 383), the inclusion of both preterm and term neonates, and the integration of aEEG with metabolic parameters to construct a multidimensional predictive model. However, several limitations should be noted. Given the retrospective design, baseline imbalances such as gestational age and head circumference may introduce confounding. Nevertheless, we employed multivariable regression to adjust for these differences, and aEEG parameters persisted as independent predictors of ICH. aEEG monitoring and neuroimaging were not uniformly performed within the first 72 h of life, during which most IVH/ICH events occur in preterm infants. Consequently, some neonates might have undergone assessments after hemorrhage onset, reducing the strength of evidence supporting the ultra-early diagnostic use of aEEG. Our results therefore indicate that abnormal aEEG is associated with the presence of ICH; however, its true early-warning capability requires confirmation. Issues with data completeness necessitated multiple imputations, which may have introduced bias. Moreover, the absence of long-term neurodevelopmental follow-up limited the ability to correlate early aEEG findings with later outcomes. Future prospective studies should adopt standardized serial aEEG monitoring (e.g., 24, 48, and 72 h after birth) coupled with synchronized bedside neuroimaging to accurately establish the temporal relationship between aEEG abnormalities and ICH occurrence. Future studies should further incorporate long-term neurodevelopmental follow-up and utilize advanced missing-data handling methods (e.g., machine learning–based imputation) to improve data reliability. Expanding validation to include extremely preterm neonates (< 28 weeks’ gestation) may also enhance the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

aEEG offers a reliable, non-invasive method for the early diagnosis and severity assessment of ICH in neonates. CFM scores, lower limit of amplitude, and LMA were identified as independent predictors of both the presence and severity of ICH. Early bedside aEEG monitoring has the potential to optimize clinical decision-making, enable timely interventions, and ultimately improve neurodevelopmental outcomes in affected neonates.
