Effect of yoga therapy as an add-on to conventional medicine in improving cognitive functions and depressive symptoms in major depressive disorder: A case report
Authors: Ekta Mishra, Shivam Sharma, Kashinath G Metri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMajor depressive disorder is a common mental disorder with a lifetime prevalence of 4.4 % worldwide. Cognitive dysfunction is a consequence of MDD. A 27-year-old female with MDD since 2015 experienced an acute MDD episode with anxiety, extreme sadness, poor memory, and impaired attention in July 2023 after discontinuation of anti-depressants. She was on anti-depressants since 2023, yet symptoms persisted. She enrolled in yoga therapy in January 2024. Yoga therapy consisted of a 60-min yoga session every day of the week for 12 weeks. She was followed up twice, on the 15th and 90th day. The selective and sustained attention, working memory and depressive symptoms were assessed using the Attention Network Test, Colour Stroop test, Corsi Block test, and the Beck Depression Inventory-II, respectively. There was marked improvement in selected and sustained attention and depressive symptoms but no improvement in working memory on the 90th day compared to baseline and 15th day. This case study concludes that yoga therapy as an add-on to the conventional treatment of MDD may enhance cognition and reduce depressive symptoms in patients with MDD who have a poor response to conventional anti-depressant treatment. This case study provides insights into the possible role of yoga in MDD patients who poorly respond to conventional anti-depressant therapy.Keywords: Yoga, Major depressive disorder, Cognition, Mental health

Introduction

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is a severe and common psychiatric condition with a lifetime prevalence of 5–17 %, averaging around 12 % [1]. Approximately 10–15 % of MDD patients commit suicide [1]. In addition to affective symptoms, impairments in non-affective cognitive functioning, such as dysfunctions in attention, executive functions (EF), concentration, processing speed, learning and memory [2], are often reported in MDD. Longitudinal studies have demonstrated that poor concentration, poor memory, and difficulty making decisions are common symptoms in MDD patients [3]. Impaired cognitive functions, especially attention and memory, can significantly impact daily life, from work and academic performance to social interactions, daily tasks, and safety issues. Individuals may have problems with productivity, organisational, and maintaining relationships [4]. Additionally, cognitive impairment in depression hampers managing health care, financial management, and self-care, which becomes more difficult. Anti-depressant medications have a limited role in cognitive enhancement in MDD. Alternate therapies such as Tai Chi, Yoga therapy and CBT (Cognitive Behavioural Therapy) have been frequently used interventions in the management of MDD.

Yoga, an ancient discipline of mind-body practice of Indian origin. It has positive effects on physical, physiological and mental measures [5,6] and is effective in improving major psychopathologies such as stress, anxiety, and depression [7]. Further, it also improves cognitive functions such as attention, concentration, memory, processing speed and others [8]. Growing scientific evidence shows that yoga positively affects cognition and the processes underlying its regulation [9]. A recent study of yoga reported improved levels of BDNF (Brain-derived neurotrophic factor) in MDD [10]. BDNF is a member of the neurotrophin family and is essential for learning, memory, brain development, and synaptic plasticity. MDD patients with regular yoga training also experienced a slight improvement in depressive symptoms and mood compared to conventional treatment [11]. A recent systematic review also advocated that yoga is more effective than medicine alone in treating MDD [12]. The positive results above the placebo and equivalent effects contrast with science-based treatments. Yoga is a successful intervention that can be incorporated into any medical system and has the potential to play a significant role in integrative medicine in future [13].

Patient information

A 27-year-old female visited the Department of Yoga, Central University of Rajasthan, for the Yoga Therapy. She was found to be conscious and oriented to time and space. The patient is an unmarried female pursuing her Master's degree and belongs to a middle socio-economic background. The patient had no history of allergies, childhood diseases, accidents, or injury history.

Chief complaints: Overwhelmed feelings of sadness, lack of interest, worthlessness, anxiety, guilt, crying spells, poor memory, impaired attention, disturbed sleep, lethargy, unable to concentrate, restlessness, confusion, and irritation for the last seven months.

N/H/O: Hallucination, delusion, and previous suicidal attempts.

Bowel: Constipated, Appetite: normal, Bladder: Normal,

No family history of any psychiatric diagnosis. Genetically, there is no reported hereditary medical issue.

Clinical findings & diagnostic assessments

The patient's disease history was noted. She has been diagnosed with Depressive Disorder by a psychiatrist (based on DSM-5 criteria for depression) in 2015. Vitals were found normal. Her BMI was 28.5 kg/m2. A body mass composition study conducted using MA601 body composition analyser and found 12 % visceral fat and 40.6 % body fat [14].

The Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-II) was administered by a psychologist to assess the severity of depression and found it to be severe depression.

Timeline

History

The patient was apparently healthy till 2015. In April 2015, she attempted a competitive exam and did not succeed in it. Since then, she started experiencing depressive symptoms like low mood, sadness, feelings of guilt, and worthlessness. She consulted local physicians for these issues and took medication for two years, but had no relief (the details of those medications are not available to the patient). In 2017, she consulted a psychiatrist in Agra, India, and took anti-depressants for 4 years, but found no satisfactory improvement in the symptoms. Then, in 2019, the patient visited another psychiatrist in New Delhi, where anti-depressants were changed to fluvoxin 100 mg, Daxid 100 mg, and Olimet 2.5 mg; this time, she felt significant improvement in her symptoms. She continued these medications till July 2023; then, she stopped medications as she was feeling completely normal. But after a week, her depression symptoms got aggravated. In November 2023, the patient started the same medication after consulting a psychiatrist, but did not experience improvement in symptoms at all. In January 2024, patients were enrolled on yoga therapy. Her current medication was Fluvoxin 100 mg, Daxid 100 mg, and Olimet 2.5 mg (See Table 1).

Materials and methods

Therapeutic intervention

Informed consent was signed by the patient prior to joining Yoga Therapy sessions. A counselling session was conducted to explain the diet modification, yoga therapy plan and timeline. She was advised not to discontinue the medication. The patient also consented to publish this case report in the journal.

Yoga intervention

A tailored yoga module was developed by a yoga therapy consultant. Daily two supervised yoga therapy sessions, 6 days a week, were administered by a certified yoga therapist under the Supervision of a yoga therapy consultant. The yoga therapy session lasted for 60 minutes.

Selected practices focused on reducing mental and physical fatigue, improving body awareness, and enhancing attention. Pranayama and relaxation techniques help in emotional regulation and cognitive clarity. Each practice was selected based on the physical and psychological benefits and easy-to-moderate difficulty level, which we believe were best for the patient.

The module consisted of Shukshma vyayama, Suryanamskar, Asana, Kriya, Pranayama, and Relaxation techniques. Shukshma vyayama (gentle joint movement) is incorporated to enhance peripheral blood circulation, reduce physical rigidity, and prepare the body for postural practice. Surya namaskara (sun salutation) is a dynamic sequence that helps release endorphins and uplift mood. Selected asanas (postures) like Ustrasana (camel pose), Bhujangasana (cobra pose), and Matyasana (fish pose) help to reduce muscle tension, enhance mood, and improve mind-body connection. Kriya (cleansing practice) like kapalabhati (illuminating skull breathing) helps to calm the mind and improve brain function. Pranayama (breathing techniques) like bhastrika (bellows breath) and bhramari (bee breath) help by influencing the nervous system and improving brain function. Relaxation techniques like quick relaxation techniques (QRT) and deep relaxation techniques (DRT) were integrated to facilitate parasympathetic activation, reduce stress, promote emotional regulation, and consolidate the benefits of the preceding practices. Assessments

To evaluate the patient's cognitive capacities and signs of depression, we employed four standardised tests:

The Attention Network Test (ANT) is a tool used to gauge how well an attention network is working. This computer-based test assesses various facets of attention, including conflict, alerting, and orienting, and is administered with the aid of PEBL software. Reaction time and accuracy are assessed under a variety of cued and uncued scenarios while the participant reacts to a target stimulus (such as an arrow).

Colour Stroop Test: The Stroop test gauges cognitive flexibility and selective attention. It is administered using PEBL software. In this task, participants were presented with colour words displayed; there were three types of responses: congruent word meaning and the ink was the same (e.g., RED and the ink were also red), incongruent word meaning and ink colour was not the same (e.g., RED filled with ink green) and neutral words like bus, car, tree, etc were displayed, in all the three response participant was asked to identify the ink colour rather than the word itself. The reaction time and accuracy in determining the correct colour reflect the participant's ability to manage interference, an essential component of executive function.

Corsi Block Test - Visual-spatial working memory is measured using the Corsi Block Test. It entails repeating a sequence of places (blocks) displayed in a specific order after remembering them. A person's visual memory and memory capacity are evaluated based on the length and precision of the sequence they can recall. The PEBL software is used to measure it.

The BDI-II – It evaluates the degree of depression symptoms using a self-report questionnaire. It consists of 21 questions that assess physical, mental, and emotional symptoms. Higher scores indicate more severe symptoms of depression, which is reflected in the scores.

All these tests were administered by a cognitive psychology student. Data extraction and scoring were performed manually. The patient was asked not to consume coffee, cigarettes, or alcohol at least 24 hours before the cognitive tests. The patient was advised to have a good night's sleep before tests. Proper orientation about the tests was given before the cognitive assessments were conducted.

Follow-up and outcomes

The patient was followed twice: first follow-up (15th day) and second follow-up (90th day). Initially, in the first week, the patient had difficulty performing yoga sessions as she had laziness, daytime drowsiness, and low interest in participating. Later, the patient started feeling improvement in the symptoms, and the attention network test and Beck depression inventory also showed improvement. The Corsi block test showed no improvement in memory. Details of the assessment are shown in Table 3. No adverse event was reported during the intervention.

Discussion

The case study is of a 27-year-old female patient who was suffering from severe depression. There was no improvement in the depressive symptoms despite resuming the medication after discontinuation. However, after the 3-month integrated yoga sessions as an add-on to conventional medicine, there was a significant decrease in depressive symptoms, accuracy, response time, alerting, conflict resolution, orienting, and selective attention of the patient. This case study demonstrated the potential role of yoga therapy, along with modern medicine, in reducing major depressive disorder.

These results align with existing research supporting yoga's positive impact on depressive symptoms in MDD patients. A previous randomised controlled trial [15] demonstrated a reduction in depressive symptoms among MDD patients practising yoga, consistent with the improvements observed in our case. A study combining Sahaja Yoga practice with anti-depressant treatment in patients with major depressive disorder also demonstrated improvements in attention [16]. A systematic review of RCTs from 2000 to 2021 has shown that yoga intervention improves cognitive functions like attention, memory and executive functions in mental health disorders [17]. A study on 83 participants (66 female and 17 male) with major depressive disorder, aged 49.65 ± 9.83, showed that a 3-month yoga intervention helped to decrease depressive symptoms and uplift mood. This study also showed that yoga rapidly improved symptoms, especially at the beginning of the intervention, which can also be seen in our study [11]. In this study, while response time and executive function measures showed improvement, visual-spatial memory showed no changes. This aligns with the existing literature suggesting that attention is more sensitive to short-term behavioural interventions like yoga therapy and may require longer intervention periods for memory [8].

As there is limited research specifically examining yoga's effects on cognitive functions in MDD patients, this study offers a unique contribution. High-reliability questionnaires and computer-based tests were utilised to assess these cognitive and emotional improvements.

The yoga protocol includes a set of practices - Suksma Vyayama exercises, Surya Namaskar, Kriyas, Asanas, Pranayamas, and relaxation techniques designed to address the symptoms reported by the patient, including prolonged sadness, disinterest, worry, guilt, poor memory and focus, sleep disturbances, and physical lethargy. For example, in sukshma Okayama, gentle micro-moments were used to improve flexibility and increase circulation, while Surya namaskar increases energy, clears mental blockage, and enhances mood. Kriya, like kapalbhati, helps to improve concentration and remove toxins from the body, while pranayamas like bhastrika and bhramari help to calm the mind. Quick relaxation techniques (QRT) and deep relaxation techniques (DRT) are two relaxation techniques that might help you feel better and balance your mind and body.

The possible explanation can be found in the holistic effect of yoga, which is a combination of physical exercise, cleansing, breathing practice, and meditation. In major depression, the telomere length is reduced in oligodendrocytes [18]. Several studies have shown that short telomere length is associated with cognitive impairment and dementia [19]. Research has shown that yoga helps increase telomere length and telomere activity levels [20] and improves cognitive function in patients with MDD. In major depressive disorder, there is a reduced level of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) [21], which leads to low mood, insomnia, restlessness and heightened sensitivity. Previous studies showed that yoga is associated with an increase in brain GABA levels, helps to improve mood, and reduces depressive symptoms [22].

This case study offers persuasive evidence of the patient's development as a result of a comprehensive evaluation and monitoring procedure. Additionally, the lack of side effects supports yoga as a secure supplemental therapy for depression. The single-subject design and absence of a control group restrict its generalisability. Research with a larger sample size and randomised controlled studies should be done in the future to validate these findings and evaluate the long-term impacts of yoga treatment in various populations. Additionally, figuring out which aspects of yoga therapy are best for mental health could assist in improving treatment plans. Individuals with depression often struggle with a lack of motivation and low energy, which contributes to decreased adherence rates. Thus, regular and long-term practice of yoga is always a challenge. As a single-case study, the findings cannot be generalised to the broader population of patients with MDD. Individual variability in baseline characteristics, medication adherence, and engagement with the yoga intervention may significantly influence outcomes. The absence of a control group prevents improvements from being attributable solely to the yoga intervention. Additionally, the short follow-up period limits conclusions about the sustainability of cognitive and emotional improvements. Future studies should incorporate randomised controlled designs with more extensive, diverse samples and more extended follow-up periods to validate these preliminary observations.

Patient perspective

The patient stated, "Now I am doing fine; yoga has a significant effect on my health than medication. I had lost hope of getting better again, but yoga brought me new positivity in my life and restored my hope."

Conclusion

This case study demonstrates the significant impact of yoga therapy as an add-on to conventional medicine in improving depressive symptoms and cognitive functioning in a patient with major depressive disorder (MDD). The attention network test showed a remarkable improvement following the yoga intervention. This suggests that yoga may be an effective adjunct therapy for managing depression, especially in areas where conventional treatments have not been effective in MDD. However, these findings also highlight the need for more comprehensive studies, such as randomised controlled trials with large sample sizes and more considerable follow-up periods, to make these results more reliable and determine the components of yoga therapy most beneficial for mental health.
