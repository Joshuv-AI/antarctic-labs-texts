Incorporating in vivo demethylation and reduction into mercury mitigation for sustainable development
Authors: Huan Zhong, Mengjie Wu, Wenli Tang, Pei Lei, Shouying Li, Fei Dang, Chengjun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mercury pollution, in conjunction with other contaminants, hampers progress toward the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals. The Minamata Convention on Mercury, which came into force in 2017, represents a critical step in combating this global issue. However, evaluating its effectiveness faces a major challenge, that is, establishing a clear link between reduced mercury emissions and lessened human exposure to neurotoxic methylmercury. Here, we propose that a previously overlooked methylmercury detoxification pathway in primary producers holds the potential to bridge the gaps in the emission-exposure nexus. A comprehensive understanding of this in vivo methylmercury demethylation and mercury reduction will enhance the effectiveness of global mercury mitigation efforts, accelerating our pace toward a sustainable future.

The specter of mercury (Hg) poisoning still lingers, more than 70 years after the outbreak of Minamata Disease (MD) in the 1950s. MD is a severe form of Hg poisoning due to the consumption of fish and shellfish contaminated by methylmercury (MeHg)—a potent neurotoxin. MeHg can be biomagnified up to 10 million times along food chains [1], harming wildlife and human health, and causing an estimated annual decline of 12 million intelligence quotient (IQ) points in human newborns [2]. These detrimental impacts hamper the achievement of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly eliminating hunger (SDG 2), protecting good health and well-being of wildlife and humans (SDGs 3, 14, and 15), and beyond (Fig. 1). Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of Hg's journey along food chains and throughout Earth's ecosystems is crucial. Uncertainties between Hg mitigation and MeHg exposure reduction

The Minamata Convention on Mercury (MC), a global treaty designed to combat Hg pollution and its associated environmental and health risks, entered into force in 2017 with over 120 signatory countries. This international effort has yielded positive results. Substantial reductions in anthropogenic Hg emissions have led to a decrease in atmospheric Hg concentrations and their subsequent depositions onto the Earth's surface. For instance, China experienced an approximately 30% decrease in total anthropogenic Hg emissions between 2010 and 2020 [3].

However, the quantitative evaluation of the extent to which global efforts to reduce Hg emissions translate into a reduction in human exposure to MeHg remains challenging. For each unit reduction in Hg emissions, human exposure to MeHg is expected to decline by only one-third to one-half [1], highlighting the uncertainties in the linkage between Hg emission reduction and MeHg exposure mitigation (Fig. 1). This mismatch is partly attributed to the poor understanding of how MeHg is produced or degraded through its journey in the ambient environment and along food chains. All these transformation processes may either increase or decrease the concentrations of MeHg in the environment and along food chains, thus obscuring the relationship between Hg emission reduction under the Minamata Convention framework and subsequent MeHg exposure changes.

Upon emissions and atmospheric deposition into the terrestrial or aquatic ecosystems, Hg undergoes complex transformation processes, including Hg methylation and MeHg demethylation. Particularly, MeHg—alongside its microbial formation—is subject to rapid demethylation, which significantly alters the MeHg concentrations in environmental compartments, including soil, sediment, and water. For instance, the rate of MeHg microbial degradation can be as high as 2.8–5.8 ​d−1 in sediments [4], while that for photochemical degradation of MeHg ranges from 0.04 to 0.6 ​d−1 in sunlit surface waters [5].

Residual MeHg in the environment then finds its way into food chains, where it is efficiently transferred to higher trophic levels. During the entry and trophic transfer through food chains, MeHg is also subject to transformations in vivo. A typical example is that MeHg can be synthesized within the intestinal microbiota of fish [6]. Despite the evident advances made in understanding MeHg transformation processes in the ambient environment [7], the mechanism underlying in vivo MeHg demethylation, particularly during its entry and transfer through food chains, is far from clear. This critical knowledge gap contributes to the uncertainties in evaluating the effectiveness of Hg emission mitigation efforts in reducing MeHg accumulation in human food webs and thus dietary exposure to this potent neurotoxin. Now, efforts have been made to address this knowledge gap for primary producers, which have recently been found to demethylate most of their accumulated MeHg, as discussed below.

Hidden demethylation pathways at the entry of food chains

Among the known entry points for MeHg in food chains are primary producers—including phytoplankton and plants—organisms that create their own food from inorganic substances. These vital organisms were long thought to be mere MeHg “accumulators” [8]. Eight plant species [10] and four phytoplankton species [11], including paddy rice (Oryza sativa L.), the freshwater alga Chlorella vulgaris, the marine diatom Chaetoceros gracilis, and two cyanobacteria, namely Microcystis and Synechocystis, have been recently examined for in vivo demethylation and identified as unexpected “demethylators” of MeHg, instead of mere “accumulators”. These primary producers rapidly demethylate MeHg internally and efficiently expel up to 86% converted inorganic mercury (Hg2+) into the air as gaseous Hg (Hg0) within days, creating a high-efficiency detoxification pathway that transforms neurotoxic MeHg into less toxic inorganic mercury and ultimately to gaseous Hg0, thereby removing the majority of MeHg in food webs back into the atmosphere (Fig. 2) [10,11]. This rapid chain reaction is triggered by previously unrecognized light-independent reactions between MeHg and singlet oxygen (1O2)—a common reactive oxygen species (ROS)—generated within phytoplankton and plants. MeHg within organisms generally binds with thiols, which elongates the C–Hg bond of MeHg and then enables the attack of 1O2, leading to the degradation of MeHg. This pathway occurs irrespective of light conditions or microbial activities, differing fundamentally from the two well-recognized demethylation pathways in natural environments—photolysis and microbial degradation of MeHg—but exhibits comparable and even faster demethylation rates. The occurrence of this novel pathway of in vivo MeHg demethylation in phytoplankton and terrestrial plants may extend to other organisms, as ROS generation is common in life. Addressing these challenges in elucidating ROS-induced MeHg demethylation in organisms requires sensitive in situ tracing techniques capable of speciating Hg in vivo, such as Nanoscale Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry (NanoSIMS) and Synchrotron Radiation-Based Soft X-ray Electron Spectroscopies. These findings, along with recent reports on MeHg demethylation within animals [12], challenge the prevailing belief that natural degradation of MeHg occurs primarily before it enters food webs, particularly in surface waters, soils, and sediments where light or demethylating microbes are abundant.

Scientific implications

The uncovered transformation pathways play a crucial role in MeHg buildup in food webs and the associated risks, as well as their projections under future scenarios. Traditionally, MeHg concentrations in food webs were believed to be principally determined by the net MeHg production in the environment, as well as the uptake and trophic transfer of MeHg along food chains. The quantification of these two key processes formed the foundation for modeling and predicting MeHg fluxes from the environment into food webs. Now, MeHg transformation at the very beginning of food webs must be counted as another crucial factor, as primary producers are found to play the role of “demethylators”, removing a significant part of accumulated MeHg from food chains, in addition to the long-recognized role of “accumulators”, transferring MeHg from the environment into food chains. However, phytoplankton demethylation may not completely prevent MeHg entry into food webs, given its accumulation in higher trophic levels (e.g., fish). Instead, site-specific variations in phytoplankton demethylation—determined by community composition, food web structure, and other factors—could contribute to the differences in fish MeHg accumulation [13].

Beyond mitigating MeHg fluxes into food webs, in vivo MeHg demethylation and Hg reduction also play a key role in the global Hg cycle. A large portion of MeHg demethylated by this natural process (reaching up to 86% of that accumulated by primary producers) can be re-emitted into the air [9], revealing a previously unknown route of how Hg makes its way from water and soil to the air. This suggests that primary producers serve as highly effective “biological pumps”, pumping Hg from Earth's surface into the atmosphere, though the relative importance of this hidden natural Hg0 source is to be explored. Therefore, ignoring this crucial pathway—mediated by the ubiquitous primary producers that account for about 95% of terrestrial biomass and 20% of marine biomass [14,15]—could introduce substantial uncertainties in predicting Hg fluxes among the hydrosphere, pedosphere, atmosphere, and biosphere. Consequently, this process of Hg0 release from primary producers, coupled with established processes of atmospheric Hg deposition and subsequent methylation in the environment, can help to better understand the Hg cycle, particularly via the ambient environment-primary producer nexus (Fig. 2). It should be noted, however, that plant-pumped Hg0 could also be further transported and deposited in ecosystems with active Hg methylation, such as marshes and other natural wetlands, potentially offsetting the detoxification effects via in vivo demethylation and reduction. While uncertainties remain due to species variations (e.g., species-dependent MeHg demethylation and Hg reduction rates) and complex interplays between environmental factors (e.g., climate) and organisms, these findings highlight the critical need to include this hidden Hg0 source in current global Hg cycling models.

Regulatory and sustainable implications

Quantifying in vivo transformations among Hg species (e.g., MeHg, Hg2+, and Hg0) in food webs determines our ability to project atmospheric Hg concentrations and human MeHg exposure in the context of the MC and the corresponding global Hg mitigation efforts. Currently, this hidden yet efficient demethylation pathway of MeHg at the entry of food chains is absent in regional and global models of MeHg biogeochemical cycling. This would add uncertainties in linking Hg concentrations upon emissions into the atmosphere and MeHg contents ending up in organisms, including humans, considering that in vivo demethylation in primary producers efficiently reduces up to 37%–90% of accumulated MeHg within 120 ​h [10]. Importantly, recent estimates suggest that the change in human exposure is no more than 1/2 of the reductions in Hg emissions [2]. Incorporating the newly identified in vivo MeHg demethylation and Hg reduction pathways and exploring additional hidden pathways of Hg transformation in human food webs will help explain such latency and disparities between Hg emissions and MeHg exposure, contributing to food security and good health and well-being (SDGs 2 and 3).

This hidden pathway can also have an impact on food safety (SDG 2). ROS, which trigger this rapid in vivo MeHg demethylation pathway in plants and phytoplankton, are naturally or artificially produced at different stages of food supply, including farming, food processing, production, and cooking. While the occurrence of ROS-induced MeHg demethylation is yet to be elucidated and quantified in future studies, this newly discovered demethylation reaction provides future avenues for reducing MeHg concentrations in food and thus mitigating dietary exposure, e.g., by artificially strengthening demethylation reactions. The potential has recently been illustrated in a national-scale estimate of the role of ROS-induced demethylation in rice-derived MeHg exposure in China: it is estimated that the total Hg concentrations in 11 provinces of China would exceed the national limit in the absence of ROS-mediated demethylation, highlighting the importance of plant demethylation in controlling human exposure [16].

In addition, this hidden pathway may also contribute to water management. Aquatic environments are hotspots of MeHg production and biomagnification, posing a severe threat to aquatic life [17,18]. The efficient MeHg demethylation and subsequent release of gaseous Hg0 to the atmosphere observed in various phytoplankton species—a chain process removing Hg from the sensitive ecosystems to the air—suggests the possibility of restoring Hg-contaminated waters and protecting aquatic wildlife from MeHg exposure by harnessing in vivo MeHg demethylation and Hg reduction. Such a nature-based solution could contribute to cleaner water and the protection of life below water, thus advancing the progress of SDGs 6 and 14.

Overall, this natural, efficient demethylation pathway holds immense potential to safeguard planetary health and facilitate the achievement of SDGs. Investing in research on the regulation of key factors affecting MeHg demethylation within organisms can lead to more effective protection of wildlife and human health from MeHg exposure. These joint efforts are crucial to safeguard the health and well-being of all life on our planet, aligning with SDGs 2, 3, 6, and 14, and leading the way towards a sustainable future.

1)Recommendations for academia

Recommendations for academia

It is imperative to conduct further studies on ROS-induced Hg demethylation within organisms and the resulting release of gaseous Hg0 to air, including but not limited to microbes, phytoplankton, plants, and animals. This knowledge is vital to enhance our ability to quantitatively predict MeHg accumulation in wildlife and humans, and the previously unnoticed “U-turn” of Hg from food chains into the atmosphere. Currently, demethylation potentials of MeHg are only available for primary producers, including a few species of terrestrial plants and phytoplankton. Whether this light-independent in vivo demethylation is ubiquitous in organisms at various trophic levels and across various ecosystems, and how efficient it is, are largely unknown. These knowledge gaps hinder the incorporation of this hidden process into mechanistic models, thereby limiting our ability to evaluate the effectiveness of MC in mitigating MeHg bioaccumulation and human exposure.

Meanwhile, comprehending the key factors influencing the efficiency of this novel demethylation pathway is a prerequisite for predicting MeHg bioaccumulation and reducing the risks through this natural process of in vivo demethylation. Notably, the level of 1O2 and other ROS within organisms is sensitive to factors such as ambient temperature and CO2 concentrations [19]. This sensitivity suggests that changing climate could affect MeHg demethylation rates, potentially reshaping MeHg entry into food webs by altering MeHg removal from organisms. To fully understand MeHg dynamics along food chains, future studies are needed to quantify the effects of climate change on in vivo ROS-triggered MeHg demethylation.2)Recommendations for stakeholders and industries

Recommendations for stakeholders and industries

For the food and agriculture sectors, stakeholders need to consider harnessing this novel pathway to improve food safety and security. At the farming stage, agricultural practices (e.g., fertilization) and environmental parameters (e.g., dissolved organic matter) would impact the physiology of crops, which could, in turn, affect the in vivo production of ROS and thus MeHg demethylation [10]. Industries and farmers can utilize this knowledge to develop nature-based solutions to efficiently demethylate MeHg accumulated in crops at a lower cost, compared to expensive soil remediation measures, e.g., biochar amendment, soil washing, and thermal treatment [20].

This rationale may also apply to post-harvest food production stages. ROS are intentionally or unintentionally produced during food processing, production, and cooking. For example, food processing, especially decontamination postharvest, often increases ROS production through methods like ultrasonication and non-thermal interventions [21]. Similarly, oil-based cooking generates substantial amounts of ROS [22]. It should be noted that ROS may also lead to changes in protein function by causing crosslink or cleavage of proteins, and vitamin degradation through oxidation [23]. Currently, there are no reports of ROS-induced MeHg demethylation in food at post-harvest stages. Further studies should be conducted to test whether such reactions occur and how they might affect MeHg concentrations in foodstuffs and subsequent dietary exposure. Particularly, understanding ROS-mediated MeHg transformation during food production, processing, and preservation will establish a scientific basis for developing precise predictive models of dietary MeHg exposure in global populations. Understanding and potentially regulating these processes could demethylate MeHg and reduce its concentrations in foods ready for human consumption. These efforts could complement existing global Hg mitigation strategies by further reducing biotic MeHg concentrations, enhancing global food safety [10]. These integrations could prove critical, given emerging evidence that observed declines in biotic MeHg concentrations do not correspond proportionally to reductions in Hg emissions and atmospheric concentrations [2].

The water industry may also benefit from these findings. Similar to that adopted in the food and agriculture sector, nature-based solutions utilizing aquatic plants hold immense potential. Aquatic plants are already widely used to efficiently remove nutrients in water, e.g., nitrogen and phosphorus, through a process known as phytoremediation [24]. Selecting aquatic plant species that excel in both nutrient removal and MeHg demethylation offers a promising nature-based solution for eutrophic lakes facing the dual threats of nutrient overload and constant MeHg production [25].3)Recommendations for management and policymaking

Recommendations for management and policymaking

For scientists and policymakers, incorporating MeHg demethylation in primary producers and other possible organisms into the current framework of effectiveness evaluation of the MC and global Hg mitigation efforts is urgent and crucial. This is essential to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the true impact of Hg mitigation strategies.

Specifically, scientists should assess the effectiveness of Hg mitigation efforts using refined models of Hg biogeochemical cycling (rice-paddy biogeochemistry model) and bioaccumulation (e.g., biotic ligand model and physiologically based pharmacokinetic model). These models should explicitly account for hidden yet efficient processes, e.g., by integrating the in vivo demethylation rates of MeHg in primary producers. Additionally, routine monitoring programs and data analysis for MeHg concentrations in organisms should be adjusted to incorporate the effects of in vivo demethylation. These adjustments are necessary because the observed MeHg concentrations within organisms depend not only on environmental MeHg concentrations but also on the efficiency of in vivo demethylation. By making these adjustments, we can obtain a more accurate picture of how effective global Hg mitigation strategies are in reducing environmental and biological MeHg concentrations.

Looking ahead, policymakers should prioritize research and development efforts to fully elucidate natural demethylation processes across the entire food supply chain—from production and processing to preservation. Addressing these knowledge gaps is essential to exploring the potential for enhancing demethylation processes to reduce MeHg concentrations in food. To advance this goal, we recommend establishing a dedicated funding program focused on nature-based demethylation techniques and incentivizing collaborations between industry and academia. Priority research areas identified in this study include elucidating ROS-induced MeHg demethylation mechanisms in human food chains and optimizing bioaugmentation, phytoremediation, dietary interventions, and environmental management strategies to enhance this process.

Furthermore, policymakers should consider developing integrated mitigation strategies that incorporate demethylation enhancement. Our analysis of demethylation pathways indicates that relying solely on emission reduction may be insufficient for comprehensive risk mitigation. Therefore, effective long-term solutions must integrate front-end emission reduction efforts (e.g., flue gas treatment) with downstream demethylation enhancement approaches. Given the transboundary nature of Hg pollution and its bioaccumulation in global food webs, this integration requires coordinated implementation across national, regional, and international scales. However, a critical knowledge gap remains in understanding synergistic effects between these intervention types. Consequently, dedicated research into these synergies is urgently needed to inform efficient resource allocation and maximize mitigation efficacy. Finally, promoting information sharing and best practices for both emission reduction and demethylation techniques logically follows from our findings on diverse Hg exposure pathways and is crucial for optimizing global mitigation efforts to safeguard long-term health outcomes worldwide.
