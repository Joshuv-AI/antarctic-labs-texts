Fear of cancer recurrence mediates the association between symptom burden and readiness for return to work in patients with lung cancer
Authors: Siyu Guan, Yan Wang, Jing Chen, Mingfang Li, Zhiqi Yang, Yantong Xie, Xinyi Li, Yaqian Huang, Yuqi Qiu, Jie Mei, Fangjia Shen, Yueli Wang, Jun Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveReadiness for return to work (RRTW) is a critical stage in the return-to-work process and deserves significant attention. Researchers have found that RRTW is related to symptom burden and fear of cancer recurrence (FCR). This study aimed to (1) describe RRTW among lung cancer patients, (2) examine the relationships among symptom burden, FCR, and RRTW, and (3) explore the mediating role of FCR between symptom burden and RRTW.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 208 lung cancer patients from February 2023 to May 2025 at a tertiary hospital in China. The RRTW Scale, the Fear of Progression Questionnaire-Short Form (FoP-Q-SF), M.D. Anderson Symptom Inventory-Chinese Version (MDASI-C) and the MDASI Lung Cancer-Specific Module (MDAS-LC) were used.ResultsThe contemplation dimension scored highest (3.36 ​± ​1.09) in RRTW, and the actions-evaluation dimension scored lowest (2.57 ​± ​0.97). Symptom burden was significantly positively associated with the actions-behavior dimension of RRTW (r ​= ​0.183, P ​< ​0.01). FCR showed positive correlations with all four RRTW dimensions (r ​= ​0.147–0.272, P ​< ​0.05). The structural equation model showed that FCR significantly mediated the relationship between symptom burden and RRTW.ConclusionsFCR had a significant mediating effect on symptom burden and RRTW. These findings highlight the importance of assessing symptom burden and FCR in postoperative lung cancer care and provide a basis for future research to explore strategies that may enhance RRTW.Keywords: Lung cancer, Readiness for return to work, Symptom burden, Fear of cancer recurrence, Mediating effect

Introduction

Lung cancer, one of the most prevalent malignancies, remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally, with approximately 1.8 million deaths reported in 2022.1 In China, it continues to have the highest incidence and mortality rates among all malignancies,2 representing a major challenge to public health. Ongoing advances in early screening, diagnostic, and treatment techniques have led to a gradual improvement in the overall survival rate of lung cancer patients in recent years.3 Meanwhile, many survivors still face various post-treatment challenges, such as severe physical symptoms, psychological distress, and difficulties in social reintegration.4 These challenges are particularly pronounced among patients of working age, who often face uncertainty about returning to work (RTW) following treatment. Returning to work not only facilitates the resumption of social functioning, enhances psychological health, and improves quality of life, but also helps reduce socioeconomic burdens and healthcare costs.5 Readiness for return to work (RRTW), as the critical stage of the RTW process, deserves great attention because it plays a key role in predicting successful work resumption.6

RRTW refers to the level of preparedness to resume employment following absence due to illness and treatment.7 RRTW spans the entire transition process from clinical treatment to functional rehabilitation.8 It represents an essential initial stage in the process of work resumption, and a higher level of RRTW can facilitate successful return to the workplace.6 However, studies have shown that lung cancer patients generally exhibit low levels of RRTW,9 with only 60.3% successfully returning to work within two years.10 Research indicates that returning to work prematurely may hinder recovery, impair post-return adaption, and reduce patients' confidence in coping with work.11,12 In contrast, excessive delay in returning to work may negatively affect the reconstruction of occupational identity and economic recovery.11,12 Therefore, determining the optimal timing for return to work is crucial, and this requires an accurate evaluation of the patients’ RRTW. Among the influencing factors related to RRTW, physical symptoms and psychological factors such as fear of cancer recurrence (FCR) may be substantial predictors of RRTW.13,14

Symptom burden refers to the degree of discomfort caused by symptoms that an individual subjectively perceived.15 Lung cancer patients commonly continue to experience multiple symptoms at discharge, such as cough, sputum, and pain.16 These symptoms interfere not only with patients' daily life but also with their capacity to work.16 Studies have shown that adverse symptoms may affect work resumption and directly influencing patients' RRTW.17 An elevated perceived symptom burden can diminish survivors’ motivation, confidence, and capacity to resume employment, thereby delaying their recovery and workplace reintegration.18 Among breast cancer populations, the findings have indicated a negative correlation between symptom burden and RRTW.14 However, no studies have been conducted to explore the relationship in patients with lung cancer.

Alongside symptom burden, FCR is considered to be another key predictor of RRTW,13 which refers to fear or concern regarding the possibility of cancer returning or progressing, typically manifesting as heightened worry and emotional distress.19 As a common and major psychological challenge faced by cancer survivors, the persistent fear and anxiety can significantly affect a patient's occupational functioning, posing barriers returning to work.20 Studies have shown that patients with lung cancer generally experience a moderately high level of FCR.21 FCR is recognized as a major factor affecting the return-to-work experience in cancer patients, with individuals becoming empowered to resume employment after overcoming FCR.22 Consequently, previous studies have indicated that FCR influences RRTW among individuals with chronic heart failure.23 Similar findings have been reported in patients after percutaneous coronary intervention, where FCR was negatively associated with RRTW.13 However, no studies to date have examined the relationship between FCR and RRTW in patients with lung cancer.

Although previous studies have established that symptom burden and FCR are associated with RRTW, the underlying mechanisms connecting them in cancer patients remain unclear.13,14 According to the Stress and Coping Theory,24 individuals confronted with stressors undergo a process of cognitive appraisal that may evoke emotional responses, which subsequently influencing coping behaviors and functional outcomes. In our study, when patients encounter symptom burden as a stressor, they may develop FCR, which in turn affects their preparedness for return to work. FCR has been identified as a mediator between physical symptoms and health-related functional outcomes. Among patients with head and neck cancer, FCR plays a critical mediating role between physical symptoms and quality of life.26 Given the prolonged disease courses and complex recovery processes of both lung and head and neck cancer, and considering that RRTW and quality of life are important functional health outcomes in postoperative cancer rehabilitation, we assume that FCR may mediate the relationship between symptom burden and RRTW in lung cancer patients. Investigating the relationships among symptom burden, FCR, and RRTW is essential, as it may inform targeted clinical practices and psychosocial interventions to improve functional recovery and ultimately facilitate their return to work.

Therefore, the purpose of our study was to: (1) describe the RRTW among patients with lung cancer; (2) examine the relationship among symptom burden, FCR, and RRTW; and (3) investigate the mediating effect of FCR between symptom burden and RRTW. The conceptual model is schematized in Fig. 1.

Methods

Participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at a tertiary hospital in Zhanjiang, China. A total of 208 participants were recruited through convenience sampling between February 2023 and May 2025. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) aged 18–60 years; (2) diagnosed with lung cancer as the primary tumor; (3) had undergone surgical resection; (4) were diagnosed during employment and had not yet returned to work; (5) were able to communicate orally and/or in writing; and (6) provided informed consent to participate. Patients were excluded if they had metastatic or recurrent disease, other malignancies, or severe mental or cognitive disorders. Additionally, individuals who received definitive chemoradiotherapy or radiotherapy alone were excluded to ensure sample homogeneity by focusing on postoperative patients. According to methodological recommendations for structural equation modeling (SEM), a minimum sample size of 200 participants is required.27

Measures

Demographic and clinical data

A self-designed general information questionnaire was utilized to gather demographic data (e.g., age, gender, education, family monthly income, and the type of work before illness) and clinical information (e.g., age, cancer stage,48 type of surgery, and treatment method).

Readiness to return to work

RRTW scale was employed to assess patients' readiness to return to work.28 The scale consists of two sections: the first, intended for individuals who have not yet returned to work, includes 4 dimensions (pre-contemplation, contemplation, actions-evaluation, actions-behavior); the second, intended for those who have already returned to work, includes 2 dimensions (uncertain maintenance, active maintenance). Each item of the scale is rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (disagree completely) to 5 (agree completely). The present study adopted a multidimensional approach, treating the four dimensions as distinct but interrelated constructs. Based on the conceptual meaning of its items, the precontemplation dimension was reverse-coded so that higher scores across all dimensions consistently indicated greater readiness. The score for each dimension is calculated by summing the item scores within that dimension, and no overall total score is defined. The Cronbach's α of its Chinese version ranged from 0.75 to 0.84.29 This study included patients who had not returned to work, utilizing the first section of the RRTW scale.

Symptom burden

Symptom Burden was assessed by M.D. Anderson Symptom Inventory-Chinese Version (MDASI-C) and the MDASI Lung Cancer-Specific Module (MDAS-LC). The MDASI-C was originally developed by the M.D. Anderson Cancer Center in Texas and was adapted for application in China by Wang et al.30 It consists of two sections: the first evaluates the severity of 13 commonly reported cancer-related symptoms, and the second measures patients' perceived interference of these symptoms across 6 aspects of daily functioning. MDAS-LC31 focus on lung cancer-specific symptoms and includes six items: cough, expectoration, hemoptysis, chest tightness, constipation, and weight loss. Both scales employ an 11-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 0 (no symptoms or interference) to 10 (most severe symptoms or highest level of interference). The combined use of the MDASI-C and MDAS-LC exhibited excellent internal consistency, indicated by a Cronbach's α of 0.925.

Fear of cancer recurrence

The Chinese version of the Fear of Progression Questionnaire-Short Form (FoP-Q-SF)32 was used to evaluate FCR in the present study. The 12-item scale comprises two dimensions: physical health (fear related to physiological health) and social/family (fear related to social and family concerns), with a total score ranging from 12 to 60. A total score of ≥ 34 suggests psychological dysfunction related to fear of recurrence. Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale where patients rate their experiences from “never” to “always”. Higher scores reflect more severe fear of recurrence. The Cronbach's α of the FoP-Q-SF was 0.883, with Cronbach's α for the two subscales reported as 0.838 and 0.842.32

Procedure

The first author personally collected the data and clearly explained the study to the patients. Patients were requested to fill out questionnaires on their own, including a self-designed general information questionnaire, RRTW, FoP-Q-SF, MDASI-C and MDAS-LC, after completing the informed consent form. All completed questionnaires were promptly returned and immediately verified for completeness.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 25.0 and AMOS 26.0 software were used for the data analysis. Statistical tests were two-sided, with P ​< ​0.05 considered statistically significant.

Descriptive statistics were performed for demographic and clinical data, RRTW, symptom burden, and FCR. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to investigate the correlations among RRTW, symptom burden and FCR. To address potential confounding, univariate analyses followed by multivariable regression were used for RRTW dimensions, FCR, and symptom burden to screen relevant demographic and clinical covariates. Time since surgery and main source of family economy emerged as significant predictors and were included as control variables in the structural equation model (Supplementary Table S1). Normality of the study variables was examined, and the distributions met the assumptions required for SEM.50

Structural equation modelling (SEM) was then conducted to confirm the mediating role of FCR between symptom burden and RRTW. A bias-corrected bootstrap procedure involving 5000 resampled iterations was applied to estimate the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the coefficients corresponding to direct, indirect, and total effects. Statistical significance was determined when the 95% CIs did not include zero. Model fit was considered acceptable when the χ2/df ratio was < 3.0, the confirmatory fit index (CFI) and goodness-of-fit index (GFI) were ≥ 0.90, and the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) was ≤ 0.08.33

Discussion

This study served as an inaugural investigation to demonstrate that RRTW is associated with symptom burden and FCR in patients with lung cancer. Furthermore, it identified the mediating role of FCR in the relationship between symptom burden and RRTW.

In this study, lung cancer patients exhibited the highest mean score in the contemplation dimension of RRTW. This indicates that, following initial treatment, patients gradually develop a desire to reintegrate into society and resume normal life, which is consistent with the findings of Wen et al.34 This may be influenced by several factors, including the financial burden of treatment, societal role expectations, and the need for self-actualization. Especially for middle-aged and young patients, work serves not only a source of income but also as a crucial means of rebuilding self-identity.35 The lowest score was the action-evaluation dimension, suggesting patients have difficulties in self-assessing work abilities and confidence. This may be due to the fact that most patients were only within 3 months post-surgery, with limited physical recovery, which may reduce their confidence and readiness to return to work.36 It contrasts with the results reported by Aasdahl et al.,37 possibly due to the greater availability of professional guidance, structured vocational rehabilitation programs, emotional support, and other supportive services in other countries before patients return to work.9 This study highlights a mismatch between psychological motivation and functional capacity in postoperative lung cancer patients during the return-to-work process. Therefore, clinical healthcare providers should not only enhance postoperative patients’ willingness to return to work, but also emphasize vocational rehabilitation assessment and provide personalized guidance and support to facilitate successful return to work among postoperative patients.

In terms of symptom burden, the mean score in this study was 1.30 ​± ​0.72, which is lower than that reported by Butler et al.,38 suggesting a relatively low overall symptom burden among patients. This may be caused by that a greater proportion of our patients were diagnosed at cancer stage I.49 Early-stage tumors are generally associated with milder symptoms and minimal interference to daily life. Additionally, traditional Chinese cultural values, such as “Golden Mean” and Taoist thoughts like “Go with the Flow”,39 may encourage patients to adopt a calm attitude toward illness, facilitating psychosomatic balance through lifestyle adjustments and alleviating postoperative symptom burden. Notably, both symptoms and symptom interference scores in this study was lower than that reported among newly diagnosed lung cancer patients in Taiwan.40 This difference may be explained by the fact that our patients were in the postoperative recovery phase, a period during which symptoms tend to gradually relieved. Furthermore, most patients in this study were married, and spousal support during recovery may have enhanced their coping capacity and reduced the impact of symptoms on daily functioning.

With regard to FCR, we found that lung cancer patients scored 2.25 ​± ​0.58, indicating a moderate level of fear. This suggests that postoperative patients commonly experience concerns about disease recurrence.21 After surgery, patients enter the follow-up phase and are no longer receiving intensive treatment. Without ongoing care, they may develop psychological issues such as anxiety and depression, which can intensify their FCR.41 In this study, the physical health dimension of FCR scored higher than the social/family dimension, possibly due to patients' heightened concerns about treatment side effects and disease progression. In addition, this may also be attributed to the fact that 65.4% of participants in our study had a junior middle school education or below. Limited health literacy may hinder patients’ ability to obtain and understand disease-related information, potentially increasing FCR level.

Concerning the correlation results, this study revealed that symptom interference was positively corrected with the “actions-behavior” dimension of RRTW, suggesting that patients who perceive greater disruption in daily functioning are more likely to take proactive actions toward returning to work. Symptom interference may activate behavioral coping strategies in cancer patients, motivating them to take further actions to restore normal social functioning.42 This finding contrasts with studies in breast cancer patients.14 It may be related to differences in occupational stress, social support, and symptom perception across cancer types, which can influence patients' decisions about returning to work. Notably, our study did not find a significant association between symptoms and RRTW. This highlights that perceived functional interference of symptoms may play a more critical role in behavioral RRTW than symptom severity.43 Therefore, to support lung cancer patients in returning to work, healthcare providers should comprehensively assess symptom burden, with particular attention to patients’ subjective perceptions of functional interference.

In this study, FCR was positively correlated with all four dimensions of RRTW, indicating that it may act as a psychological driver that heightens awareness of health threats and prompt patients to consider and prepare for returning to work.25 As FCR levels increase, patients become less likely to disregard returning to work, and more willing to consider and actively take work-related preparatory actions. When faced with disease threats and recurrence risk, patients may attempt to alleviate anxiety and maintain life stability by reestablishing their work roles.44 This effect may be particularly evident among middle-aged and young patients, who often face financial pressure and family responsibilities, FCR may prompt patients to reevaluate the value of work, thereby stimulating their motivation to take action.45 However, Zhang et al. shown that excessively high FCR may lead to anxiety and avoidance behaviors, which hinder functional recovery and delay the return-to-work process.46 Taken together, these findings suggest that the role of FCR may depend on its intensity and context, serving as a motivational factor at moderate levels but becoming maladaptive when excessive. Therefore, healthcare providers should monitor changes in patients' FCR levels, identify its mechanisms influencing return-to-work behaviors, and implement personalized psychological support and vocational rehabilitation strategies to promote successful work reintegration among postoperative lung cancer patients.

Furthermore, SEM revealed a mediating role of FCR in the relationship between symptom burden and RRTW, indicating the positive mediating role of FCR in this association. This finding aligns with the Stress and Coping Theory,24 which posits that stressors such as physical symptoms may trigger emotional responses that in turn subsequently influence coping behaviors and functional outcomes. Previous studies have shown that symptom burden is closely related to FCR, which in turn may influence the willingness of cancer survivors to return to work by affecting functional and role participation.51 As symptom burden increases, patients are more likely to worry about disease progression, particularly when certain symptoms are misinterpreted as signs of recurrence, further intensifying their fear of recurrence.47 Moderate level of FCR may prompt patients to adopt coping strategies aimed at regaining control and restoring normalcy, thereby enhancing readiness to return to work even when physical recovery is incomplete.24,52 Within the Chinese cultural context, where family obligations and economic responsibilities are emphasized,53 moderate FCR is more likely to be translated into concrete actions such as actively returning to work. These findings provide a theoretical basis for improving patients’ readiness to return to work in clinical practice. Healthcare professionals should strengthen symptom management and psychological assessment, while incorporating vocational rehabilitation and individualized psychological support to facilitate the return-to-work process.

Implications for nursing practice and research

This study focused on postoperative lung cancer patients and explored how symptom burden impacts RRTW through the mediating role of FCR. These findings contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of RRTW status in lung cancer patients and provide a basis for individualized interventions. It is recommended that healthcare providers conduct comprehensive assessments of both physical and psychological conditions during postoperative follow-up, with particular attention to symptom burden and FCR. Given the mediating role of FCR, early identification and targeted management are essential. Nurses should apply validated tools to monitor FCR and implement tailored interventions that integrate symptom management, psychological counseling, and vocational rehabilitation. These strategies may facilitate patients’ psychological adjustment, improve functional recovery, and enhance the likelihood of successful return to work. Future nursing research should further explore longitudinal outcomes and evaluate the effectiveness of multidisciplinary support approaches across diverse patient populations.

Limitations

This study has several inherent limitations. First, it was conducted at a single public hospital using a convenience sample, which substantially compromises the generalizability and reproducibility of the findings and represents a central limitation of this work. Moreover, as all participants were surgical patients, most were stage I (73.5%), resulting in a stage distribution not representative of the general population and further limiting external validity. Future studies should adopt multi-center designs and larger, more representative samples across different stages to further validate our conclusions. Second, the cross-sectional design of this study constrains the capacity to infer causal relationships among symptom burden, FCR, and RRTW. Further longitudinal research is needed to confirm these findings with greater certainty. Third, although detailed oncological characteristics were provided, perioperative pulmonary function data were not systematically collected because such tests were not routinely performed during postoperative follow-up at our institution. This absence may have limited our ability to fully account for potential confounding effects. Finally, data for this study were collected through self-reported questionnaires, which may be prone to response bias such as overestimation or underestimation. Future research should consider combining self-reported questionnaires with objective measures to gain a more comprehensive understanding of patients’ RRTW.

Conclusions

In this study, both symptom burden and FCR played important roles in RRTW in lung cancer patients. Our results highlighted that FCR significantly mediates the relationship between symptom burden and RRTW, suggesting that patients' psychological responses are integral in promoting their return-to-work process. These findings have practical implications, highlighting the value of assessing symptom burden and FCR in postoperative lung cancer care. Incorporating individualized symptom monitoring together with appropriate psychological support may help address patients’ concerns and facilitate their functional recovery. Future studies are warranted to further explore effective strategies and multidisciplinary approaches that can promote successful reintegration into the workplace.
