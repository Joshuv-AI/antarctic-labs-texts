Impact of background input on memory consolidation
Authors: Martina Lamberti, Nikolaos Kikirikis, Michel J. A. M. van Putten, Joost le Feber
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Memory consolidation involves repeated replay of new information by the hippocampus, which transfers memories to the neocortex for long-term storage. This occurs mainly during slow wave sleep, a phase characterized in the cortex by low cholinergic tone and low afferent input. High cholinergic tone has been shown to hamper memory consolidation, probably mediated by reduced network excitability (the ease of activity propagation in a network). We used cortical neuronal networks on multi electrode arrays to investigate whether low background input contributes to memory consolidation. Networks received focal electrical stimuli to memorize, with or without background afferent input (global optogenetic stimulation). Background stimulation hampered memory formation and consolidation, confirming the importance of low background input. Moreover, it lowered network excitability, similar to high cholinergic tone. These findings suggest that high network excitability is a critical feature of slow wave sleep that facilitates memory consolidation.

Introduction

It is well-known that sleep is important for long-term memory consolidation1–3. In-vivo, consolidation of declarative memory can be subdivided into two specific processes4. Rapidly after learning, memories are temporarily stored in the hippocampus in a process generally referred to as synaptic consolidation. During the second phase, called systems consolidation, memories are slowly transferred to the neo-cortex. This stage of memory encoding requires repeated activation of cortical areas by the hippocampus5–7, and occurs mainly during slow wave sleep (SWS). During this phase of deep sleep, synchronous population discharges known as sharp waves, induce network bursts in the hippocampus8 and interact with oscillatory cortical rhythms, which is believed to facilitate memory consolidation9. Despite the many studies that investigated systems consolidation during SWS, little is currently supported by experimental evidence10. Consequently, the underlying mechanisms of memory consolidation remain ill-understood, as well as the conditions that determine why SWS is more suitable for memory consolidation than the awake state.

Several prominent differences between the awake state and SWS in the cortex have been suggested to support memory consolidation. During slow-wave sleep, the cholinergic tone is low, which has recently been shown to facilitate memory consolidation11. In that study, cholinergic tone was shown to lower network excitability, which is defined as the ease of activity propagation in a network. Sufficiently high network excitability was suggested as a critical determinant for memory consolidation. Other work showed that external input may reduce network excitability12. Thus, strongly reduced sensory input, as occurs during slow wave sleep13, may be another possible determinant for memory consolidation, leading to a higher network excitability compared to the awake state.

Cultures plated on multi electrodes arrays (MEAs) allow investigation of cellular and network mechanisms involved in memory14–16. In these in-vitro models, random networks of cortical neurons develop that become spontaneously active after about a week, and mature in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 3$$\end{document}≈3 weeks17,18. Activity patterns typically include frequent synchronous events referred to as network bursts, alternated by periods of more dispersed activity19–22. In-vivo, network bursts typically occur during early brain development23,24 and during several sleep stages25–27, which are characterized by low afferent input13,28. This suggests a relationship between afferent input and the occurrence of network bursts. The typical low frequency (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 1$$\end{document}<1 Hz) of alternation between network bursts and dispersed firing in cultured networks is in line with the in-vivo slow oscillations in the sleeping cortex26,27,29. Given the absence of input, the low cholinergic tone, and the oscillatory activity patterns, untreated in-vitro networks are more similar to the in-vivo cortex during SWS than to the awake27,29.

In the absence of external input, mature networks develop an equilibrium between activity and connectivity. Activity and connectivity mutually affect each other, and both become stable on a time scale of hours to days in mature cultures30. This is possible only when activity and connectivity are in equilibrium, with dominant activity patterns that support existing connectivity. Changes in connectivity are fundamental for long-term memory formation31. Previous studies have shown that external focal stimulation induces new activity patterns in networks of cortical neurons under SWS-like conditions (low cholinergic tone)11,16,32. These new patterns induce connectivity changes in the network, disrupting the existing activity-connectivity balance11,16. If SWS conditions are maintained and external stimuli are repeated, the response pattern becomes integrated into the repertoire of spontaneous activity patterns, which is interpreted as the formation of memory traces16. Consequently, further repetition of that stimulus no longer induces connectivity changes. This reflects that the network has reached a new equilibrium, which won’t be disrupted if the same stimulus is given again after some time (hours), meaning that the new memory traces have been consolidated. Recent work showed that this process critically depends on NMDA receptor activation33.

It remains an open question whether background input, as normally present during the awake phase, hampers memory consolidation. The current study investigated this question by using in-vitro random networks of dissociated cortical neurons. We used the focal (electrical) stimulation protocol for the induction of memory traces16 and added randomly timed global (network wide) optogenetic stimulation to mimic background input. Global optogenetic stimulation by itself was shown not to induce connectivity changes in the network34, and consequently, these global stimuli are not memorized by the network. Thus, we focally stimulated networks with or without random background global stimulation to investigate whether the presence or absence of random external input affected memory consolidation in-vitro.

Methods

In this study, we used randomly grown networks from dissociated cortical neurons, cultured on multi electrode arrays. Several cultures were transfected with an AAV virus to express ChannelRhodopsin-2 (ChR2). Of the transected cultures, some were used to determine the effects of background optogenetic stimulation on network excitability and others to study the effects of background activation on memory formation, with background stimulation (referred to as the BackOpto group) or without (referred to as ChR2). These were compared to non-transfected cultures (CTRL). The CTRL group was obtained and described in earlier work33.

Measurement set-up

Experiments were performed in a setup outside the incubator, consisting of a MC1060BC preamplifier and FA60s filter amplifier (both MultiChannelSystems GmbH, Reutlingen, Germany). This set-up was placed under a Plexiglas hood that received a constant flow (2 L \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {min}^{-1}$$\end{document}min-1) of a humidified gas mixture that contained 95% compressed air and 5% \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {CO_2}$$\end{document}CO2. In addition, the setup maintains a standard temperature of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$36^\circ $$\end{document}36∘C. Measurement began after a 15 minute accommodation period. Cortical network signals were recorded from 59 electrodes using a custom-made Lab-View program. Analog signals were band-pass filtered (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2^{nd}$$\end{document}2nd order Butterworth 0.1 – 6 kHz) before sampling at 16 kHz. Noise estimation was continuously updated for each electrode during the entire recording time, as described in the work of Stegenga et al.41. Whenever voltage traces exceeded 5.5 times the estimated root-mean-square noise level (ranging 3 – 5 µV), a possible action potential was detected. For each detection, a time stamp and the electrode number were stored. In addition, (6 ms) wave shape was stored for off-line artifact detection and removal using an algorithm adapted from12.

Focal electrical stimulation

To induce and consolidate memory traces, we used focal electrical stimulation through one of the electrodes (biphasic rectangular current pulses of 200 µs per phase, negative first) with a stimulation frequency of 0.2 Hz. For each culture, we first probed all electrodes at various amplitudes (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$16 - 24$$\end{document}16-24 µA). These amplitudes were low enough to avoid electrolysis. Then, we selected 2 different stimulation electrodes that showed clear network responses (consistently increased activity during time intervals \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$>20$$\end{document}>20 ms after the stimulus). From the tested stimulus amplitudes we chose the lowest one that induced a network response after at least \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$50\%$$\end{document}50% of all stimuli.

Background global optogenetic stimulation

For background global optogenetic stimulation, we used a power LED on a SinkPAD-II20 mm Star Base (Blue (470 nm)74 lm@700 mA from Luxeon-StarLEDs). The LED was placed approximately 7 cm above the top of the MEA. A Faraday cage, based on a stainless steel mesh, was placed between the LED and the MEA to reduce possible electrically induced artifacts by the LED power cables37. The duration and intensity of light pulses were set to induce a network response ( light pulses had 100 ms pulse width and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 2.5$$\end{document}≈2.5 klx intensity). Shorter pulses yielded highly variable stimulus responses. Random background activity was generated using light pulses with interstimulus intervals (ISIs) drawn from a uniform distribution between a set minimum (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 10$$\end{document}≈10 ms) and maximum (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 1$$\end{document}≈1 s). This resulted in a mean frequency of background stimulation of 5 Hz.

Experimental Design

Effects of background stimulation on network excitability

Before starting experiments, we visually verified the responsiveness to light stimulation in all cultures in real-time raster plot. A culture was considered responsive if a global network burst in the raster plot was evident upon all single light pulses of 100 ms duration. Then, we determined the minimum light intensity that triggered a clear response, which was further used. To study the effects of background global optogenetic stimulation on network excitability, we tested different stimulation frequencies. All optogenetic stimulation used ISIs taken from a uniform distribution with a set minimum and maximum, such that mean stimulation frequencies equaled 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, or 5 Hz. The experimental protocol consisted of a 15 min baseline recording, followed by cycles of 15 min of optogenetic stimulation at a specific mean frequency and a 15 min period of spontaneous activity. This cycle was repeated for each stimulation frequency in random order (see Supplementary Information, Figure S1). We tested whether optogenetic stimulation affected activity patterns as recorded during stimulation or after cessation of the stimulus. To that end, we compared the mean firing rate and network bursting frequency to their equivalents before stimulation. In addition, we compared network excitability as computed before and after the 15 min of stimulation (see Supplementary Information Figure S2).

Memory experiments

For both control (CTRL) and virally transfected (ChR2) cultures we first recorded 1 h of spontaneous activity at the beginning of the experiment (Baseline). Then, cultures received focal stimulation through one of the two selected electrodes (electrode A), during 10 minutes (12 pulses per minute), followed by 1 h of no stimulation. This sequence was repeated 5 times. Then, we switched to electrode B and applied the same sequence of stimulation and recording. Finally, this sequence was performed again using electrode A. Thus, cultures were electrically stimulated during 15 stimulation periods in total, of 10 minutes each (Figure 1B). The total duration of the experimental protocol was 17 h and 30 min. In background stimulation (BackOpto) experiments, cultures received additional random optogenetic stimulation, starting 1 min before and ending 1 min after each period of electrical stimulation. Network excitability

We use the term network excitability to refer to the ease of signal propagation in a network. Here, we used two different metrics to quantify network excitability, one based on stimulus responses (average network response: ANR) and one on spontaneous activity (mean single pulse response: SPR).

Network excitability during opto-stimulation To study the effects of background optogenetic stimulation on network excitability during stimulation, we tested different mean optogenetic stimulation frequencies (0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, and 5 Hz). We quantified network responses as the number of spikes recorded in the 100 ms interval after cessation of each light pulse. In all cases network activity dropped to baseline values within 100 ms after the light pulse. To account for differences between cultures, we normalized all ANR values to that at 0.2 Hz, which was the lowest frequency that was used in all cultures (see Supplementary Information Table S1).

SPRs can be obtained by deconvolving the autocorrelation from CFPs. Network dynamics in CTRL and ChR2 cultures

To evaluate the effect of networks dynamics in CTRL and ChR2 cultures, we developed an algorithm that detects network bursts. For each action potential (AP) that was not (yet) part of a network burst, we determined the number of electrodes that recorded at least one action potential in the following 100 ms interval. If this number was larger than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$60\%$$\end{document}60% of the number of active electrodes (threshold), the algorithm detected a network burst. The start of a network burst was defined as the timestamp related to the first included AP, and the end as the timestamp corresponding to the last AP before the amount of electrodes with detected spikes dropped below the threshold again. This allowed us to estimate network burst duration as well. A detection was considered valid only if there was no other network burst detected in a specified exclusion range of 0.5 s before the detected network burst. Figure 3 A and B show examples of raster plots and detected bursts in CTRL and ChR2 cultures, respectively. The algorithm provides an array containing all network burst time stamps. This array was used to compute the total number of network bursts, mean network bursting frequency and mean network bursts duration. The algorithm also calculates the amount of spikes within network bursts (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\mathrm{{bursts}}}$$\end{document}Nbursts), summing up the recorded spikes between the start and end of all detected network bursts, as well as the number of spikes outside network bursts, by subtracting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\mathrm{{bursts}}}$$\end{document}Nbursts from the total number of recorded action potentials. To evaluate the accuracy of the algorithm, we visually inspected raster plots. Figure 3A and B show examples of raster plots and detected network bursts in CTRL and ChR2 cultures, respectively. This analysis was applied on the first hour of baseline recordings in CTRL and ChR2 groups.

Memory consolidation

Formation and consolidation of memory traces were assessed as described in previous work11,16. In short, changes in functional connectivity induced by each stimulation period (as described under Experimental design/ Memory experiments) were quantified by Euclidean distances (EDs) between the obtained connectivity matrices M for all chunks (see ’Functional connectivity analysis’). ED between two connectivity matrices, obtained at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{1}$$\end{document}t1 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{2}$$\end{document}t2 was calculated using the following equation:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} ED(t_{1},t_{2}) = \sqrt{\sum \limits _{i=1}^n \sum \limits _{j=1}^n (M_{ij}(t_{1}) - M_{ij}(t_{2}))^2}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}ED(t1,t2)=∑i=1n∑j=1n(Mij(t1)-Mij(t2))2.We first computed EDs between all chunks within baseline, and averaged them to obtain an estimate of spontaneous connectivity fluctuations: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ED_{\mathrm{{baseline}}}$$\end{document}EDbaseline. To quantify possible connectivity changes induced by stimulation on electrode A, we computed EDs between the last chunk of baseline and every chunk in each of the (5) periods of spontaneous activity that followed stimulation at electrode A. These distances are averaged per spontaneous activity period and referred to as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ED_{\mathrm{{0}}}$$\end{document}ED0. The same procedure was repeated for electrode B, but now with reference to the last chunk before the first stimulation at electrode B. Similarly, after return to electrode A, the last chunk before the first (second round) stimulation at electrode A was used as reference (see Figure 1). In all computations, we considered only the nonzero elements of the M matrices. These analyses were applied to CTRL, ChR2, and BackOpto cultures to determine the effect of background activity on memory trace formation and consolidation.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS statistics for Windows (IBM, Inc., Chicago, IL, version 28.0.1.0(142)) or MATLAB (The Mathworks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA, version R2022b). First the Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check for normality of data distributions. All analyses to determine the effect of optogenetic stimulation frequency on network excitability during stimulation were done using ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test (depending on normality). In all memory experiments, the significance of temporal differences in the effectiveness of stimulation was analyzed by one-way ANOVA (if normally distributed) or Kruskal-Wallis (if non-normal). The effects of ChR2 expression on network bursting behavior, and network excitability during baseline, were tested for significance with a one sample t-test, in case of normality, or Kruskal-Wallis otherwise. Significance of connectivity changes was assessed using repeated measurements ANOVA, in case of normality, or repeated measurement Friedman test otherwise. Assessment of possible effects of time and ChR2 expression alone or with background optogenetic stimulation on network excitability was performed with two way repeated measurement ANOVA, with condition and time as independent variables. P-values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 0.05$$\end{document}<0.05 were considered to indicate significance.

Discussion

We investigated the importance of low background afferent input for memory consolidation in in-vitro neural networks. For this purpose, we adapted an existing protocol to induce memory traces using focal electrical stimulation16. We applied the protocol in cultures not expressing channelrodopsins (CTRL group) and in cultures that expressed channelrhodopsins, with superimposed background optogenetic stimulation (BackOpto group) or without (ChR2 group). The presence of background global stimulation reduced network excitability and hampered memory consolidation compared to control cultures. Interestingly, cultures in the ChR2 group, without light stimulation, showed increased uncorrelated (background) activity in the network. Moreover, in this group of cultures, the formation of memory traces hardly exceeded the detection limit, and network excitability tended to be lower.

Our findings are in line with in-vivo studies that showed the benefit of resting periods with reduced afferent inputs, like naps or wakeful rest, for memory consolidation3,42–47. Subjects undergoing periods of wakeful rest after a learning task had better memory performances than subjects kept awake and active3,42,45,47. A recent study suggested that reduced interfering background input, due to eye closure, during these resting periods improves memory consolidation3. During the awake state, background afferent input appears as stimuli that are uncorrelated to the event that is repeatedly replayed for memorization. These inputs may hamper the ability to focus, exhaust the brain, and distract from the main information3,42,46,47. These results strengthened the hypothesis that it is important to protect newly acquired memories against interference by afferent input3,46, but in-vivo investigation of underlying mechanisms has shown to be challenging.

In-vitro networks receive no afferent input unless provided by stimulation. This offers the opportunity to study the effects of afferent input in a fully controlled environment. Background afferent input was mimicked by random global optogenetic stimuli. These stimuli may interfere with the formation and consolidation of memory traces that are formed by focal electrical stimulation but are unlikely to induce memory traces by themselves. Earlier in-vitro work showed that randomly appearing inputs48 or global optogenetic stimulation34 did not induce significant connectivity changes. As memory formation requires connectivity changes16, these inputs are unlikely to induce memory traces. Here, we used global optogenetic stimulation to simulate background afferent input. This significantly reduced network excitability during stimulation (Figure 2), possibly due to increased short-term synaptic depression in the network or to a larger subset of neurons in their refractory period.

Cultures in the ChR2 group tended to have lower network excitability (Figure 2B). These cultures showed increased uncorrelated background activity in spontaneous recordings (Figure 3), possibly due to leaking ChR2 channels or unintended opening due to environmental light. Increased uncorrelated activity has been associated with decreased network excitability in a study by Wagenaar et al., who electrically stimulated cultures with increasing frequencies to suppress synchronized network bursting12. Pharmacologically enhanced uncorrelated firing has also been associated with network excitability31. Alternatively, the expression of ChR2 might directly affect neuronal or synaptic excitability. Thus, viral transfection might reduce network excitability, but this seeming reduction was not significant. Moreover, Network excitability in BackOpto cultures during baseline did not show reduced values. This suggests that the slight differences between network excitability in CTRL and ChR2 cultures were not caused by the viral transfection.

Memory consolidation was obstructed in ChR2 cultures that received background optogenetic activation (Figure 4). This suggests that the absence of afferent input during slow wave sleep is important for long-term memory consolidation. Earlier work showed that low cortical cholinergic tone, as occurs during slow wave sleep, also enhanced memory consolidation11. Background global optogenetic stimulation reduced network excitability (Figure 4), comparable to the excitability reduction induced by high cholinergic tone11. These findings together suggest that network excitability at the time of repeated activation with a focal stimulus determines the ability to consolidate memory traces. Memory trace formation was possible in the ChR2 group but far less prominent than in CTRL, which is consistent with the slightly lower network excitability in this group. Failure to form and consolidate memory traces might, in the BackOpto group, have been related to decreasing effectiveness of electrical stimulation or to decreasing network excitability after cessation of light stimulation. However, responsiveness to focal electrical stimulation was well maintained during the entire memory protocol (Figure S3), and network excitability in periods of no stimulation remained unchanged in all experimental groups. The fact that background optogenetic stimulation reduced excitability when ongoing but not after cessation suggests that high network excitability is particularly important at the time of repeated activation and may be less important during periods in between. This seems in good analogy to memory consolidation during slow-wave sleep, which is also interleaved with other sleep phases, characterized by lower network excitability. Interestingly, the analogy between our results and in-vivo observations suggest that the brain is able to actively modulate brain network excitability to optimize specific brain functions.

This study had a few limitations. One limiting factor is that we cannot exclude intrinsic differences between cultures in the three experimental groups (CTRL, ChR2, and BackOpto). Viral transfection had to be applied on the day after plating, leading to the use of different cultures in the CTRL and ChR2/ BackOpto groups. Even though we used different cultures in each group, network dynamics were similar. Network bursting frequencies and network burst duration did not differ between groups (ChR2 BackOpto and CTRL). Significant differences were observed in uncorrelated spiking behavior outside network bursts between CTRL and ChR2 cultures, but not between these groups and cultures used in the BackOpto experiments. This suggests that different uncorrelated firing is not related to the viral transfection but is more likely to be due to intrinsic differences between cultures. Baseline network excitability tended to be lower in ChR2 than in CTRL cultures, but the difference was not significant. The higher baseline network excitability measured in the BackOpto group before the first light stimulation suggests that differences in network excitability were unlikely to result from the viral transfection. Differences were probably related to intrinsic differences between cultures in different groups. Still, lower network excitability may have contributed to the less prominent formation and consolidation of memory traces in the ChR2 group. Another limitation was the restriction on the optogenetic stimulation mean frequency. To achieve some randomness in the timing of the light pulses, we set the average interval between two stimuli to 100 ms. This limited the mean stimulation frequency to 5 Hz, which allowed for positive as well as negative deviations from the average ISI. Another limitation was the choice to apply background optogenetic stimulation only during the periods of focal electrical stimulation. It might have been interesting to apply background stimulation throughout experiments, which might affect memory performance. This modality was not possible because background stimulation interfered with functional connectivity estimation. CFP connectivity is based on synchrony of activity, and any type of stimulation generates widespread synchronous activity. Overheating of the LEDs might be an additional problem.

In conclusion, background stimulation hampers memory consolidation and instantaneously lowers network excitability. Memory formation was also obscured in ChR2 cultures, which showed increased dispersed activity and a slightly lower network excitability. These results suggest that differences in network excitability during the awake and slow-wave sleep states determine why memory consolidation occurs mainly during slow wave sleep.
