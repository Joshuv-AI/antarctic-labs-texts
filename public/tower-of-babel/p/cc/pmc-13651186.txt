Shift work and early vascular aging: a systematic review
Authors: Huilong Xie, Linjie Shu, Ran Cheng, Min Hu, Jingwen Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeople worldwide are increasingly engaged in shift work across modern industries. This might have long-term health implications, including early vascular aging (EVA) characterized by modified vascular structures, impaired vascular function, and changed circulatory indicators. Evaluating the relationship between EVA indicators and shift work could provide valuable insights into changes in vascular health. However, this relationship remains a matter of debate.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review across five databases to identify the relationship between shift work and EVA, with outcomes based on vascular structures, functions, and circulatory indicators.ResultsThis systematic review included 33 articles published up to February 2025, including 28 cross-sectional and five longitudinal studies. Night shift, 24-hour shift, and on call shift were all examined in this review. Available longitudinal literature was consistent in showing a relationship between the continuous years spent working shifts and increased pulse wave velocity (n = 3), as well as increased thickness of the carotid artery (n = 2). However, evidence from cross-sectional studies have been inconsistent, with insignificant correlations reported for vascular structures (4/10) and functions (4/8). Across various samples and settings, shift work was found to be positively associated with inflammatory factors and negatively associated with antioxidant levels (17/19).ConclusionsVariations in organizational and regional policies result in differing rest periods and working conditions among shift workers. The association between shift work and EVA remains uncertain due to inconsistencies across studies and the lack of a standardized definition of shift work. Future research should establish widely accepted evaluation systems and incorporate individual lifestyle factors to enable more effective and targeted intervention strategies for shift workers.Graphical abstractGraphical abstract of the general information in this review Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28426-6.

Background

Shift work as a phenomenon in which individuals are engaged in irregular or “non-standard” working hours, is driven by the development of new technologies and expansion of basic services [1]. Standard evening or night schedules, rotating shifts, split shifts, casual or on-call shifts, 24-hour shifts, irregular schedules, and other non-daytime schedules are all included in the full scope of shift work [2]. Evidence based on data of two million participants showed that shift work was associated with adverse vascular events (including myocardial infarctions, coronary events, and ischemic strokes) [2]. A more recent study revealed that both current and cumulative lifetime exposure to shift work were associated with atrial fibrillation and coronary heart disease [3].

Vascular aging serves as an independent predictor for the future diagnosis of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) and is likely a key contributor to CVD development [4]. A famous physician, Thomas Sydenham, once said, “A man is as old as his arteries” [5]. This aligns with recent recognition that arterial age, rather than actual chronological age, is inextricably linked to accelerated growth of aortic aneurysm [6]. As such, early vascular aging (EVA) is regarded as a signal vascular deterioration beyond chronological age [7, 8]. In clinical practice, the manifestations of vascular aging is evaluated by measuring changes in vascular structures and functions [9]. In addition, circulatory aging biomarkers enable objective quantification of senescence and facilitate the early detection of endothelial dysfunction with preventive potential [10, 11].

It has been proposed that accurate and multidimensional parameters could yield more systematic and nuanced insights into the EVA status [7]. Structurally, it is manifested as vessel wall thickening, calcification, and lumen narrowing [12]. Functionally, it affects the vascular system’s ability to maintain homeostasis during aging [13]. Circulatory indicators, such as inflammatory markers, oxidative stress, and thrombosis-related factors, also contribute to an in-depth understanding of EVA [14]. However, existing systematic reviews focus exclusively on associations between shift work and several vascular parameters (i.e., blood pressure [15–18] and vascular stiffness [19]), with inconsistent correlations reported. The relationship between shift work and vascular aging mechanisms—particularly their associations with structures [12] and circulatory biomarkers [20] central to vascular senescence—remains underexplored. In this study, we sought to integrate evidence from observational studies on shift work and its relationship with EVA through vascular structures, functions, and circulatory indicators.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews; https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/ PROSPERO, CRD42023468245, and it was conducted using the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) program [21].

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive literature search in PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Google Scholar databases from their inception to February 28, 2025. The search strategy was developed using the population, exposure, comparison, outcome, and study design (PECOS) principles (Supplementary Table 1). In each electronic database, a predefined keyword search mode was used to search titles, abstracts, and full texts. Language restrictions were applied, including only English publications, while there was no restriction on publication date. The search terms for each database were provided in detail (Supplementary Table 2), and the full search strategies for all databases were outlined (Supplementary Table 3).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

For research that met the inclusion criteria, they had to: (1) be an observational study, which included shift workers and control group as participants; (2) Include several inspection items to test the condition of blood vessels; (3) be published in the English language before and including February 28, 2025. Studies that included shift workers without differentiating them from the general population were excluded. Similarly, when shift work was mentioned only as a minor variable in an article, the data could not be extracted. For minimizing the contribution of additional elements to exposure and outcome, any intervention-type studies were discarded.

Study selection and data extraction

We conducted a preliminary search in PubMed by analyzing text words in titles and abstracts, as well as indexing terms used to describe the articles. Subsequently, we performed an extensive search across the remaining databases using the identified keywords and index items. Finally, we manually searched for studies within the reference lists to minimize the omission of articles. In addition, Data extraction to be done in excel. The following data would be extracted from each article: (1) author; (2) date; (3) study design; (4) sample size; (5) sex; (6) mean age; (7) exposure; (8) study duration; (9) follow-up; (10) type of worker; (11) measure of EVA; (12) main results; (13) confounding factors; (14) limitation. A third reviewer would be invited to perform a 50% random screening after each of the two reviewers had collected a 50% characterization of the literature. Any differences were resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached, or the opinion of a third-party reviewer was sought.

Risk of bias assessment

The study quality assessment tools of The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute [22] were conducted to evaluate the risk and bias. These tools systematically assessed various aspects of study design and execution to identify potential biases. Each study was independently assessed by two reviewers. In cases where discrepancies arose due to a variance of more than two items considered by the reviewers, a third reviewer was consulted to mediate and resolve the differences through collaborative discussion aimed at achieving consensus. The quality of the included studies was categorized according to a 0–100% scale, with four distinct categories: poor (0% ≤ proportion ≤ 25%), fair (25% < proportion ≤ 50%), good (50% < proportion ≤ 75%), and excellent (75% < proportion ≤ 100%) [23].

Data synthesis

The assessment of EVA was a multifaceted approach, necessitating the incorporation of various indicators. We intended to synthesize our findings by examining the vascular structures, functions, and circulatory indicators. However, conducting a meta-analysis was deemed unfeasible owing to the variability in shift policies across companies, countries, and regions. This variation resulted in different shift-working populations being disproportionately affected by the burdens of vascular aging. Thus, a narrative synthesis of the findings was developed, following established guidance on the conduct of narrative synthesis in systematic reviews [24].

Results

Study characteristics

In total, 1050 records were retrieved through electronic database searches, and 19 papers were included from the references of two related systematic reviews [2, 19] (Fig. 1). Out of the initial 1,069 records, 539 were found to be ineligible during the screening of titles and abstracts. Following this screening process, 128 full-text articles were retained for further review, ultimately leading to the inclusion of 33 studies that met the predefined inclusion criteria.

The general information of the content was included in the article (Graphical abstract). A total of 57,335 participants (18 countries) aged from 20 [25] to 74 [26] were included, and the sample sizes varied significantly, ranging from 44 [27] to an extensive 10,475 [28]. Table 1 compiles structures, functions, and circulatory indicators reflecting EVA metrics from the included studies. The studies were mostly from Germany (n = 3) [26, 28, 29], Japan (n = 3) [30–32], Norway (n = 3) [33–35], United States (n = 2) [36, 37], Argentina (n = 2) [38, 39], Brazil (n = 2) [40, 41], China (n = 2) [42, 43], Finland (n = 2) [44, 45], Italy (n = 2) [46, 47], India, (n = 2) [25, 48], Jordan (n = 2) [49, 50], Turkey (n = 2) [51, 52]; the rest came from other countries [27, 53–57]. Articles were published between 2005 and 2024, with 66.67% of them published in the last decade. 28 articles utilized cross-sectional research methods [25–32, 35–43, 45–47, 49, 50, 52–57], while the other five studies employed a cohort study design [33, 34, 44, 48, 51]. Most studies included industrial employees (n = 10) [27, 32–35, 38, 39, 55–57] and medical employees (n = 10) [25, 40, 43, 46, 47, 49–52, 54]; two included drivers (n = 2) [42, 53]; two included civil servants (n = 2) [36, 41]; one included retired employees (n = 1) [37]; the other eight studies evaluated different occupation (n = 8) [26, 28–31, 44, 45, 48]. All studies measured indicators of EVA associated with shift work [25, 27–47, 49–57]. The characteristics of the studies, with emphasis on the relationship between shift work exposure and EVA measures, were summarized (Table 1). Subsequently, we mapped the relationship between shift work and key indicators of EVA in a structured diagram to improve clarity (Fig. 2).

Assessment of study quality

Quality assessment was conducted for all included studies (Supplementary Tables 4–6) [22]. Most of the included studies (18/33) were in fair quality [25, 27–31, 38, 42, 43, 45, 47, 49, 50, 52–55, 57], some (13/33) were in good [26, 32, 35–37, 39–41, 44, 46, 48, 51, 56], and two (2/33) were excellent articles [33, 34].

Vascular structures

Vascular structures were estimated by carotid intima-media thickness (CIMT) (n = 11) [26, 28, 29, 32, 34, 37, 41, 44–46, 53], maximal carotid intima-media thickness (MAX CIMT) (n = 6) [29, 34, 35, 44, 45, 53], maximal bulb carotid intima-media thickness (MAX bulb CIMT) (n = 1) [53], arterial plaque (n = 4) [32, 34, 35, 45], and peak width of skeletonized mean diffusivity (PSMD) (n = 1) [26] in twelve studies. Most studies (10/12) showed statistically significant relationships of shift work with worse vascular structures.

A study of factory employees in Norway reported that, compared with daytime workers, shift workers had higher smoking prevalence, engaged in less physical activity, and exhibited increased MAX CIMT associated with long-term shift work [34, 35]. Similarly, another study found that shift workers had higher rates of smoking and bedtime eating, along with lower exercise adherence compared with day workers, and identified habitual smoking as an independent predictor of elevated CIMT [32]. Conversely, a German investigation revealed CIMT progression was unrelated to current night shift status but significantly associated with historical shift frequency in former night workers, despite comparable smoking prevalence disparities between shift and non-shift populations [28]. South African driver surveys revealed a high prevalence of cardiovascular risk in this occupationally sedentary population, with night shifts not associated with elevated CIMT [53].

Emerging evidence further elucidated that male sex [41, 45], increasing age [29], and cumulative shift work exposure were key factors associated with increased CIMT. An 11-year Finnish cohort study reported that weekend shift work was associated with the greatest MAX CIMT increase among middle-aged men, but not with other work schedules [44]. Similarly, a study of former hospital shift workers in Italy found that CIMT remained elevated even after cessation of shift work when compared with day workers [46]. In contrast, studies from the United States observed that the association between shift work and CIMT was no longer significant following retirement [37]. Notably, a recent German study found no significant difference in CIMT between current and former shift workers, but reported a significant increase in PSMD, which indicated that subtle cerebrovascular changes could be detected through advanced neuroimaging techniques [26].

Vascular functions

For vascular function, indicators of blood flow conduction velocity (e.g., pulse wave velocity (PWV) [33–35, 42, 48, 55, 56], arterial stiffness index [28], and cardio-ankle vascular index (CAVI) [32]) were the most common assessment used (n = 8), followed by aortic augmentation index (Aix Ao) (n = 1) [55], flow-mediated dilation (FMD) (n = 2) [37, 48], peripheral arterial tone index (PAT index) (n = 1) [57], and reactive hyperaemia index (RHI) (n = 1) [28].

Evidence from longitudinal studies in India and Norway consistently indicate that shift workers exhibit greater adverse vascular changes than day workers, as reflected by progressive increases in PWV over follow-up periods ranging from six months to four years [33, 34, 48]. Notably, within the shift-worker group, PWV nearly doubled from baseline during the follow-up period [33]. Similar trends were observed in China, where each additional year of sedentary shift work corresponded to a measurable PWV increase [42]. In addition, two studies linked shift work schedule to vascular function indicators. Romanian manufacturing workers exposed to two or more rotating shifts showed increased PWV and Aix Ao [55]. The Gutenberg Health Study reported that a higher cumulative exposure to occupational shifts was associated with increased arterial stiffness and reduced RHI compared to standard work schedules [28].

In an Australian study, shift work remained associated with a reduced PAT index compared with day work, despite a greater proportion of shift workers engaging in regular physical activity [57]. Further, a Japanese study found that CAVI, a derivative indicator of PWV, was more strongly related to daily lifestyle factors such as smoking [32].

Circulatory indicators

Circulatory indicators representing aging status of blood vessels included inflammatory markers [30, 34–36, 38, 39, 46–49, 54], oxidative stress parameters [25, 27, 40, 43, 51, 52], and thrombosis-related indicators [31, 34, 36, 49, 50]. Shift work was correlated with increased oxidative stress parameters, including C-reactive protein (CRP) [25, 30, 35, 36, 46, 47, 54], white blood cell (WBC) [36, 46], interleukin-1β (IL-1β) [46], interleukin-6 (IL-6) [30, 36, 46], tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [36], malondialdehyde (MDA) [25], thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) [40], hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) [40], glutathione peroxidase (GPx) [40], oxidised purines [43], and leukocyte counts [38, 39]. Additionally, shift work was associated with increased thrombosis-related indicators such as atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) [50], thrombospondin-1 (TSP-1) [49], plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) [49], homocysteine [36], dyslipidemia [31], monocytes, and lymphocytes [47]. Conversely, shift work was associated with decreased antioxidant parameters, including ferric reducing/antioxidant power (FRAP) [27, 43], superoxide dismutase (SOD) [40], catalase (CAT) [25, 40], nitrite [40], and total proteins [25].

Inflammatory markers

Shift work was consistently associated with elevated inflammatory markers across multiple countries. Studies in India and Japan reported higher CRP and IL-6 levels among shift workers compared to day workers [30, 48]. In Croatia and Italy, shift workers showed increased CRP, WBC, IL-1β, lymphocyte, and monocyte counts relative to day workers [46, 47, 54]. Similar patterns were observed in the United States, data from the BCOPS study showed that police officers working night shifts exhibited a higher level of CRP, WBC, IL-6, and TNF-α compared to those working day or evening shifts [36]. Moreover, an Argentine study found that despite night shift workers who had a regular three-day break, their white blood cell counts remained higher than those of day shift workers [39].

Oxidative stress and antioxidants markers

Across studies from India, Brazil, and Iran, shift work was consistently linked to increased oxidative stress markers, including MDA, H2O2, GPx, and TBARS, together with reductions in antioxidant parameters such as FRAP, SOD, catalase, nitrite, and serum total proteins [25, 27, 40]. In China, frequent and severe sleep deprivation during medical practice was correlated with increased oxidised purines and decreased FRAP [43]. By contrast, research in Türkiye reported no significant changes in H2O2 levels or Trolox among medical staff who had adequate rest and voluntarily participated in night shifts [52].

Thrombosis-related indicators

Studies in Jordan and Japan found that night shift workers had elevated TSP-1 and PAI-1 levels [49], reduced high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels, increased low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels, and elevated triglyceride (TG) concentrations compared with day shift workers [31]. Another study in Japan, involving 12 large companies, reported an increase in the AIP (log [TGs/HDL]) among night shift workers [50]. A study comparing police officers in the United States on evening, night, and day shifts showed no significant differences in endothelin-1, E-Selectin, intercellular cell adhesion molecule-1, and vascular cell adhesion molecule [36]. Similarly, a longitudinal study in Norway reported that, although shift workers had higher levels of soluble vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 and soluble intercellular adhesion molecule-1 than day workers at baseline, there were no significant changes from baseline over the three-year follow-up [34].

Discussion

This current review investigated shift work and its relationships with EVA by including 33 studies which identified evidence on vascular structures, functions, and circulatory indicators under exposure to different types of shift work (e.g., irregular shifts, weekend shifts, and fixed night shifts). This review found great diversity in work schedules, rest arrangements, work types, and work intensities across different occupations and countries. This diversity prevented the review from drawing consistent conclusions regarding the association between shift work and EVA. To develop more targeted strategies for alleviating the impact of shift work on EVA, further detailed information about the specific lifestyles of workers—whether involving physical activity, mental activity, or both—is required. Understanding the variations in work schedules and collective lifestyle behaviors underlying these changes will offer critical insights for designing targeted policies tailored to both specific and broader shift worker populations.

Shift work and vascular structures

The thickening of the intima-media layers of the arteries (represented by CIMT) is regarded as an early sign of vascular aging [12] and increased CIMT is associated with a higher risk of cardiovascular events, including stroke and myocardial infarction [58, 59]. Both CIMT and MAX CIMT were positively correlated with consecutive years of shift work [29, 46]. However, other evidence did not observe this obvious association, which was probably due to wide range of shift work years for the included individuals [28, 32, 41]. More recently, an increased PSMD was observed in current shift workers than workers separated from that over one year, indicating potential cerebrovascular abnormalities induced by this working statue [26]. Shift work lasting for more than one year was positively correlated with increased CIMT [29], and this effect even persisted for two years following the cessation of shift work [46]. It was still unclear whether CIMT thickening was due to a metabolic “memory” or to lasting lifestyle habits acquired during shift work. After retirement, no significant difference in CIMT was observed between former shift workers and former day workers [37], which might be explained by a long duration of normal lifestyle that mitigated the influence of shift work of vascular aging.

Sex differences may have varying effects on the impact of shift work on the process of vascular aging: male shift workers experienced a more pronounced increase in CIMT and MAX CIMT thickening [45], since males may be more sensitive to hormones, social patterns, and dietary factors than females [41, 45]. In addition, male workers engaged in specific shifts (i.e., weekend shifts) were deprived of normal vacation and experienced considerable social jetlag, which might have contributed to CIMT and MAX CIMT thickening [44]. Detailed investigations on sex variance in work stress, hormones, and social patterns can assist in exploring vascular structures aging, offering policy insights to this susceptible population.

Shift work and vascular functions

In the above-mentioned study, structural changes focused on alterations in blood vessels (such as vessel thickening, calcification, and lumen narrowing), whereas functional assessments provided insights into how these changes impacted the vascular system in coping with aging and maintaining homeostasis. PWV served as one of the most widely used and optimal measures for assessing vascular function and was recognized as a key marker of vascular aging [13].

The continuous years of shift work might greatly affect the progression of increased PWV. Although six months of shift work exposure did not result in a statistically significant increase in PWV, an upward trend in PWV was observed [48]. Longer exposure to shift work might have induced more profound changes, as longitudinal studies over three–four years provided evidence of a causal relationship for the impact of shift work on EVA [28, 33, 34]. Young shift participants were almost ten years younger than daytime participants, whose physical fitness, especially PWV, was typically better than that of older day workers [35]. However, after three to four years of shift work, the young engaged in shift work demonstrated a significant increase in PWV, surpassing that of the elder workers exposed to regular daytime hours [33, 34]. Additionally, a longer span of shift work was also associated with a decreased RHI [28], and this study suggested that an increased frequency and years of shift work should also be taken into consideration in the text of vascular aging process, as evidenced by the increase in PWV and Aix Ao [42, 55].

Middle-aged shift workers had a lower PAT index (a vasodilation measurement) than day shift workers, but retired shift workers showed no difference in FMD (another vasodilation measurement) [37, 48, 57]. This may have been due to long-term lifestyle adjustments normalizing blood vessel aging.

Shift work and circulatory indicators

Incorporating circulatory indicators, including inflammatory markers, oxidative stress, and thrombosis-related indicators, offered a more comprehensive understanding of EVA [14]. In this review, these indicators helped to capture dynamic aspects of vascular function that were not always apparent from static structural or functional assessments alone.

The amplitudes of an individual’s circulatory indicator levels exhibited fluctuations between morning and afternoon, indicating an inherent circadian rhythmicity in these biomarkers [51]. Irregular work-life patterns, such as on-call status, holiday deprivation and excessive work time [43], could have interfered with this internal rhythmic system and triggered potential inflammatory mechanisms [38, 39, 46]. Moreover, fixed night shifts completely broke the daytime work-life balance [30, 31, 36], inevitably leading to a social jetlag that further impacted their internal rhythms and pro-inflammatory pathways [40]. The relationship between these reactions and shift work might have been amplified in individuals with high body mass index [36], further accelerating the progression of EVA.

Potential mechanisms

The circadian rhythm of the human body is primarily regulated by the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus, which acts as the central pacemaker to coordinate the periodic activities of metabolic, immune, cardiovascular, and other physiological systems, thereby maintaining homeostasis [60]. Exposure to light during nighttime can increase the excitability of the sympathetic nervous system, suppress melatonin secretion, elevate blood pressure, and reduce heart rate variability, collectively contributing to autonomic imbalance [61]. Animal studies have demonstrated that long-term reversal of the light-dark cycle alters the DNA methylation patterns of key circadian genes such as CLOCK and BMAL1, impairing myocardial repair mechanisms and exacerbating cardiovascular risk [62]. Circadian misalignment induces the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, thereby promoting vascular inflammation and accelerating the progression of atherosclerosis [60]. Circadian disruption also enhances mitochondrial production of ROS, while impairing nitric oxide synthesis, leading to endothelial dysfunction and further aggravation of atherosclerotic processes [63]. Moreover, irregular eating patterns associated with shift work disrupt the circadian rhythm of the gut microbiota, facilitating increased translocation of lipopolysaccharides into the bloodstream and thereby contributing to chronic low-grade vascular inflammation [64].

Strengths and limitations

As far as we know, this is the first systematic review for the relationship between shift work and EVA, which employed comprehensive approaches (including vascular structures, functions, and circulatory indicators) to evaluate EVA and a wide range of shift work patterns in social science. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, due to the diverse shift work policies across different occupations, companies, and countries, and the heterogeneity in EVA measures, a quantitative meta-analysis could not be performed. In addition, many behavioral data on shift workers were derived from self-reports, which are prone to errors from recall bias and social desirability. Moreover, the categories and definitions of personal lifestyle and scheduling information (e.g., smoke, bedtime eating, night shift patterns) varied across studies, limiting detailed analyses of shift workers. Secondly, as most studies were cross-sectional and relied on observational designs with volunteer samples or self-reported data, their representativeness was limited and causal relationships could not be determined. Third, we relied on free-text terms because key concepts (e.g., EVA) lacked timely or consistent MeSH indexing; in the future, the inclusion of relevant MeSH terms may further improve the comprehensiveness of searches. Additionally, as only English-language publications were included, the review may be subject to publication bias, and the omission of important references cannot be entirely excluded.

Conclusions

This systematic review examined EVA through vascular structures, functions, and circulatory indicators, highlighting its multifaceted nature. The impact of shift work on EVA may vary depending on work type (e.g., physical labor, mental labor, or a combination of both), shift schedules (e.g., night shifts, on-call duties, 24-hour rotations), and regional policies. However, the diversity across regions and types of workers, together with the absence of a standardized definition of shift work, prevents this review from drawing firm conclusions about its association with EVA. To develop more effective and personalized intervention strategies for different populations of shift workers, there is a critical need for widely accepted shift workload evaluation systems, as well as consideration of individual-level lifestyle factors.
