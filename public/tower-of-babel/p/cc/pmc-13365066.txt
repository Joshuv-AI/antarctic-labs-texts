Molecular Characterization of Methicillin and Aminoglycoside-Resistant Staphylococcus Species from Drinking Water and Clinical Samples in Dutsin-Ma, Northwestern Nigeria
Authors: Emmanuel Dayo Alabi, Kamala Abdullahi, Habib Suleiman Mahmud, Ayodele Timilehin Adesoji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMethicillin-resistant Staphylococcus species (MRSS) are a global public health threat, causing substantial morbidity, mortality, and economic burden. Understanding the characteristics of MRSS from various niches can support the control of these bacterial species. Herein, we examined the prevalence, biofilm formation, hemolytic activity, and selected aminoglycoside resistance genes in MRSS isolates from potable water and clinical samples in Dutsin-Ma, northwestern Nigeria.MethodsA total of 200 samples, including 100 water and 100 clinical samples, were collected and analyzed. Staphylococcus species was recovered following standard microbiological procedures. Antibiotic susceptibility testing (AST) and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination were performed using Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion and microdilution methods, respectively. Methicillin-resistant isolates were defined as those resistant to cefoxitin with elevated oxacillin MICs. Coagulase production, biofilm formation, and hemolytic activity were assessed using slide coagulase test, Congo red agar and blood agar, respectively. Multidrug resistant (MDR) isolates were selected for PCR to detect tuf and selected aminoglycoside resistance genes. Fisher’s exact test was performed to check for a significant difference in their occurrence (p ≤ α 0.05). ResultsThirty (15%) isolates were putatively identified, comprising both coagulase-positive staphylococci (CoPS) and coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS): 19 (63.3%) from the clinical samples and 11 (36.7%) from the water sources. Of these, 17 strains (41.2%, 7/17) from wound swabs and 58.8% (10/17) from water samples were MDR and methicillin resistant. Virulence traits were present in isolates from both sources; however, there was no significant difference (p ≤ α 0.05) in their occurrence in CoPS and CoNS isolates from both source. Of the selected MDR strains, 16 /17 (94.1%) revealed at least one virulence trait (coagulase, hemolysis, or biofilm). PCR confirmed that 23.5% (4/17) of the selected MDR isolates exclusively from water samples harbored at least one of ant(4’)-Ia, aph(2’)-Ib, aph(3’)-IIIa, or aac(6’), which encode aminoglycoside resistance.ConclusionThese findings suggest the potential role of potable water as a dissemination route for MDR MRSS in the study locality. Further studies are needed to identify the circulating species and sequence types (STs), and to evaluate the prevalence of other antibiotic resistance genes.

Introduction

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus species (MRSS) infections pose significant global public health challenges, particularly in regions with inadequate environmental sanitation and compromised water quality [1]. The genus Staphylococcus includes numerous clinically significant opportunistic human pathogens that cause a wide range of infections, from minor skin conditions to life-threatening illnesses, in both community and hospital settings [2, 3]. Staphylococcus species are also implicated in a wide spectrum of human infections, ranging from superficial skin and soft tissue infections to severe and life-threatening diseases such as infective endocarditis, pleuropulmonary infections, bloodstream infections, and device-associated infections occurring in both community and healthcare settings [4, 5].

Ensuring sufficient water supply is crucial for maintaining healthy rural and urban environments; however, approximately 2.2 billion people worldwide still lack access to safe drinking water [6]. In Nigeria, lack of access to safe drinking water is a pervasive issue, with approximately 66.3 million people without access to potable water [7].

The presence of antibiotic-resistant bacterial pathogens in water is exacerbated by poor waste management and discharge of untreated sewage into water bodies [8, 9]. Although most research on water scarcity has focused on the quantity of water, the quality of water delivered to consumers can also be compromised, particularly without proper safety measures. Regions that experience water shortages may also encounter serious water quality challenges [10], because many households store water in storage tanks within their buildings to manage the inadequate supply resulting from frequent service disruptions. Such practices may significantly threaten the microbiological quality of water [11]. Recent studies in Nigeria have documented the presence of some WHO priority antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB) that are widespread in drinking water sources and water intended for human use, and public health concerns regarding their presence have increased [12–14].

Furthermore, there are consistent reports of the widespread detection of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in diverse water environments, including wastewater [15], lakes [16], rivers [17], groundwater [18], and municipal drinking water [19, 20]. Additionally, it has been documented that MRSS can survive and persist in water and spread within communities and hospital settings [21], and chlorine treatment can selectively increase their relative abundance, thereby, posing a potential risk to human health [22]. Tan et al. [23]. also highlighted that the biologically activated carbon (BAC) used in drinking water distribution systems (DWDSs) can promote ARB proliferation owing to biofilm development. Global epidemiological analyses have also identified Staphylococcus aureus and coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) as the most frequent causative agents of bloodstream infections (BSIs) [24, 25].

The dynamic regional evolution and widespread resistance of Staphylococcus species, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, have been reported in both clinical and environmental sources [8, 25]. Olalekan et al. [25]. highlighted the significant burden of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and unexpected predominance of the ST772 Bengal bay clone, indicating the need for localized studies to understand regional variation. Furthermore, the potential interconnections between humans and ecological reservoirs in many semi-urban and rural communities in Nigeria remain poorly understood. Dutsin-Ma and its environs represent a rapidly growing area with a limited supply of municipal treated water and the nonexistence of wastewater treatment infrastructure, and informal healthcare waste disposal, which may contribute to the emergence and dissemination of multidrug-resistant pathogens.

Furthermore, the potential of potable water to serve as a reservoir for MRSS and its contribution to human infections remains under-researched in peri-urban settings in Nigeria, where potable water is often sourced from various sources. Although presumed safe, potable water may act as an overlooked route for disseminating MDR pathogens [10, 26]. Therefore, analyzing both potable water and clinical samples from the same locale can provide insights into their epidemiology and possible dissemination pathways within the One Health framework and inform targeted public health interventions. To fill these knowledge gaps, this study investigated the prevalence of MRSS, compared virulence factors, and selected aminoglycoside resistance genes in isolates recovered from potable water and clinical samples collected in Dutsin-Ma, northwestern Nigeria.

Methods

Study Area

This study was conducted in Dutsin-Ma, the administrative headquarters of the Dutsin-Ma local government area of Katsina State, Nigeria. Geographically, the town lies at latitude 12°27’17’’N and longitude 7°29’29’’E. Dutsin-Ma covers an area of approximately 527 km² and has a projected population of 264,588 residents as of 2021 [27]. The area is characterized by a savanna landscape with predominantly flat plains, interspersed hills, and the prominent Zobe dam, which plays a crucial role in supporting agricultural activities through fishing and irrigation [28]. Sampling sites included the Dutsin-Ma town water treatment plant (main reservoir), which processes raw water from the Dutsin-Ma Dam. This plant is responsible for treating water to meet potable water standards and supplying the treated water to various parts of Dutsin-Ma town. Additionally, the Zobe water treatment plant, which utilizes water from the Zobe Dam, provides water to selected areas within Dutsin-Ma town and the Katsina metropolis. The Zobe plant maintains its largest reservoir at one of the central hills within Dutsin-Ma.

Sampling Sites

The inclusion of multiple water sources was used to track water-use patterns in Dutsin-Ma. Although the town has two water treatment plants, residents do not rely exclusively on treated municipal water because of inadequate water supply. Instead, potable water is obtained from diverse sources, including boreholes, water vendors, and treated supplies. To ensure the representativeness of the actual exposure pathways, we sampled across these multiple sources. In addition, household water storage containers were included to assess the potential for post-collection contamination and cross-transmission within homes, which is common in similar settings. Water samples were collected from multiple sources, including treated water reservoirs, street taps, household storage containers, boreholes, and water vendor containers (Fig. 1), and clinical samples (wound swabs) were obtained from the Mallam Mande General Hospital in Dutsin-Ma. Mallam Mande General Hospital was selected because it is the only secondary healthcare facility in the study area. The hospital is located at the center of the town, and its effluent is discharged into the Taruma Dam in Dutsin-Ma, which is used for fishing, washing, recreation, and irrigation activities. Sample Collection, Bacterial Isolation, and Biochemical Characterization

A total of 200 samples were collected, comprising 100 samples each from water and clinical sources. Water samples were collected from Dutsin-Ma treated water (n = 10), Zobe treated water (n = 10), street taps (n = 5), water vendors’ containers (n = 20), dogon karfe household containers (n = 10), Shema quarters borehole (n = 5), Police barracks borehole (n = 10), Dangaje household containers (n = 10), Katarda quarters household containers (n = 10), and Akashe naira borehole (n = 10). Samples were collected between July and October 2024 as part of the sampling campaign (Fig. 1). Grab samples (25 mL each) were collected using sterile 50 mL bottles, stored in an ice-packed box, and immediately transported to the Microbiology Laboratory at the Federal University Dutsin-Ma, Katsina State, Nigeria, for analysis. From each sample, a 100 µL aliquot was plated on mannitol salt agar (MSA) and blood agar plates (BA) and incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h.

In parallel, 100 clinical wound swab samples were collected from patients, including adults (> 18 years, n = 67) and adolescents (13–17 years, n = 33), in the surgical ward (n = 25), accident and emergency ward (n = 25), female ward (n = 25), and general outpatient department (n = 25) of Malam Mande General Hospital, Dutsin-Ma. Of these patients, 54 were female and 46 were male (Fig. 1). The samples were immediately transported in ice-packed boxes to the Microbiology Laboratory, Department of Microbiology, Federal University Dutsin-Ma, streaked on MSA and BA (L: S Biotech, San Diego, USA), and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h [29]. After incubation, presumptive Staphylococcus isolates were initially examined for mannitol fermentation and hemolytic activity, followed by Gram staining and biochemical tests, including catalase, coagulase, and oxidase assays. Distinct colonies from pure cultures were then picked and stored on nutrient agar slants at 4 °C for further analysis.

Antibiotic Susceptibility and Surrogate Cefoxitin Disk Diffusion Testing

Antibiotics included in AST panel were selected based on documented prescriptions recorded in patients’ charts during the sampling period. The data were prospectively collected at the time of sample collection. When categorized according to the WHO AWaRe classification [30], the prescriptions were distributed as follows: Access group (73%), Watch group (27%), and Reserve group (0%). The antibiotic susceptibility profiles of the isolates were assessed using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method, following standard procedures. The cefoxitin disk diffusion test was performed to screen for methicillin resistance, because it is a more potent inducer of the mecA regulatory system [31]. Commercial antibiotic disks (Oxoid, UK) were used, including cefoxitin (30 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), and erythromycin (15 µg). Bacterial suspensions were prepared in sterile Mueller-Hinton broth (MHB) (L: S Biotech, San Diego, USA) and adjusted to an optical density of 0.5 at 600 nm (OD600). A 0.1 mL aliquot of each suspension was evenly spread onto sterile Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA) (L: S Biotech, San Diego, USA) plates. Antibiotic disks were then aseptically placed on the surface of the agar, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Zones of inhibition were measured and interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2024 guidelines [32]. Multidrug-resistant (MDR) Staphylococcus species were selected based on their resistance to ≥ 3 classes of antibiotics for further analysis [33].

Determination of Oxacillin Minimum Inhibitory Concentration

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of oxacillin was determined using the microbroth dilution method, according to CLSI guidelines [32]. Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213 and ATCC 43300 served as susceptible and resistant control strains, respectively.

Assay for Biofilm Formation and Hemolysin Production

The ability of the selected MDR Staphylococcus isolates to form biofilm was evaluated using the Congo red agar (CRA) method, with minor modifications to a previously described protocol [34]. The selected MDR Staphylococcus isolates were grown on nutrient agar (L: S Biotech, San Diego, USA) containing 0.08% (w/v) Congo red (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) and supplemented with 5% (w/v) sucrose. The isolates were streaked onto the medium and incubated aerobically at 35 °C for 24–48 h. Biofilm-producing Staphylococcus strains developed black colonies, whereas non-biofilm-producing strains produced red colonies [34]. Hemolytic activity was assessed using nutrient agar enriched with 5% sheep blood, as previously described with minor modifications [35, 36]. Blood agar plates were prepared by supplementing nutrient agar (L: S Biotech, San Diego, USA) with 5% sheep blood. Isolates were streaked onto the plates and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 18–24 h. The hemolytic activity of the bacteria was then assessed and recorded accordingly.

Extraction of Genomic DNA of Selected MDR Staphylococcus Species Isolates

The genomic DNA of selected MDR Staphylococcus isolates was extracted from a 24-hour culture (104–106 CFU/mL) using the AccuPrep® genomic DNA extraction kit (Daejeon, Republic of Korea). Briefly, 20 µL of Proteinase K was added to a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube, followed by 200 µL of the bacterial sample. For all samples, 200 µL protein solubilization buffer (PSB) was added, mixed thoroughly, and incubated at 60 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 100 µL isopropanol was added, and the lysate was transferred to a spin column and centrifuged at 8,000 rpm for 1 min. After transferring the column to a clean tube, sequential washes were performed with 500 µL of tris-buffers 1 and 2, followed by centrifugation at 8,000 rpm for 1 min, and final centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 1 min ensured the removal of ethanol.

The column was then transferred to a clean elution tube, and 200 µL of elution buffer (or nuclease-free water) was added. After incubation at room temperature for 1–5 min, DNA was eluted by centrifugation at 8,000 rpm for 1 min. The eluted DNA was immediately stored at − 20 °C until further analysis.

Detection of tuf and Aminoglycoside Resistance Genes

Uniplex PCR was performed to detect the elongation factor (tuf) gene for the identification of Staphylococcus species, as previously described by Li et al. [37]. Statistical Analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics version 31.0.1.0 (49) was used to perform Fisher’s exact test to determine the statistical significance of virulence factors between methicillin-resistant coagulase-positive Staphylococcus species and coagulase-negative Staphylococcus isolates (p ≤ 0.05).

Discussion

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus species present a significant obstacle in managing hospital- and community-acquired infections [41]. In this study, we recovered Staphylococcus species from water intended for human use and from wound swabs in Dutsin-Ma town. The occurrence and antibiotic susceptibility patterns of staphylococcal isolates from water sources suggest that human activities adversely affect water quality. This inference is supported by a growing body of literature that reported the presence of pharmaceutical residues in water systems, which can promote the selection and dissemination of resistant bacterial species in the environment [42–44].

Of the 200 samples obtained in this study, 30% putative staphylococcal isolates were recovered (Table 2). The higher prevalence (19%) of Staphylococcus species in clinical samples may be attributed to the fact that Staphylococcus species are part of the normal human skin flora and commonly colonizes the anterior nares, and can easily be shed to colonize wounds and surgical incisions [45, 46]. In contrast, the lower prevalence (11%) in water samples could be due to the pre-treatment processes the water has undergone [6]. This finding is consistent with the report by Adesoji et al. [6], who documented a higher prevalence of S. aureus in clinical samples compared with water sources in the study area. However, Oladipo et al. [47]. reported a higher prevalence of Staphylococcus species (78.87%) in clinical samples and hospital effluents in Ile-Ife, southwest Nigeria.

Antibiotic susceptibility profiles of the Staphylococcus species from both water and clinical samples revealed high resistance rates to several classes of antibiotics (Table 3). All isolates (n = 30; 100%) from both sources were resistant to cefoxitin, a surrogate marker for MRSA. This rate substantially exceeds the typical hospital-based MRSA prevalence reported in Uganda, which ranges from 45 to 60%, with environmental rates generally below 100% [48, 49]. Such universal resistance suggests possible clonal dissemination of MRSA and MRSS strains or environmental contamination linked to selective pressure in healthcare or community settings. These findings underscore the need for stringent antibiotic stewardship, improved infection control measures and enhanced environmental monitoring.

Furthermore, resistance to gentamicin was also markedly high, with 100% resistance observed among water isolates and 84.2% among clinical isolates, exceeding the 83.1% reported in a retrospective study in Ethiopia [50]. The elevated resistance in water isolates may reflect the dissemination of aminoglycoside-modifying enzyme genes through horizontal gene transfer in aquatic environments and antibiotic contamination stemming from anthropogenic activities [26, 51]. The presence of resistant strains in water is particularly concerning, as it may facilitate the persistence and spread of aminoglycoside resistance genes, potentially compromising the clinical efficacy of gentamicin in northwestern Nigeria.

Resistance to erythromycin was high among water isolates (90.9%) and substantial among clinical isolates (52.6%), aligning with reported sub-Saharan African macrolide resistance rates of 6–53% [52]. This pattern may be driven by erm-mediated inducible macrolide–lincosamide–streptogramin B (MLSB) resistance, potentially co-selected with MRSA clones, thereby enhancing persistence in both environmental and clinical settings [53]. In contrast, ciprofloxacin resistance was moderate in water isolates (45.5%) and lower in clinical isolates (21.1%), aligning with the 45% and 17% reported in CoPS and CoNS strains recovered from surfaces and medicotechnical materials at the university hospital Center of Abomey-Calavi/Sô-Ava in Benin [54]. The relatively low resistance observed in clinical isolates suggests retained therapeutic utility; however, the elevated resistance levels in environmental isolates indicate possible ongoing selective pressure, warranting cautious use and continuous surveillance. This pattern may reflect the overuse and misuse of these antibiotics in both clinical and veterinary settings, which have been reported to contribute significantly to the emergence and spread of resistant bacterial strains [55, 56].

In total, 56.7% (17/30) MDR strains were identified in both clinical and water samples (Table 4), exhibiting complete resistance to cefoxitin with elevated MIC values for oxacillin. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus spp. have emerged as a growing risk to public health in both clinical and non-clinical settings. This resistance is primarily mediated by the mec gene, which is located on the staphylococcal cassette chromosome mec (SCCmec) [57]. Although Staphylococcus species are less common in water than E. coli, they can still serve as useful indicators for assessing the dynamics of antibiotic resistance in the environment [58].

Biofilm formation and hemolytic patterns in MDR strains isolated from both sources revealed variations in their virulence. Clinical isolates exhibited a higher ability to form biofilms (100%) than those from water sources (60%) (Fig. 1). In contrast, isolates from water samples showed greater (50%) hemolytic activity (including β and α hemolysis) than clinical isolates (42.8%) (Table 5). However, no significant difference was observed in the virulence traits between methicillin-resistant CoPS and methicillin-resistant CoNS isolates (p ≤ α 0.05). Virulence factors, including biofilm formation and hemolysin production, enable Staphylococcus species to evade innate and adaptive immune defenses, thereby promoting their survival, proliferation, and dissemination within the host body. These attributes not only heighten staphylococcal pathogenicity but also pose a significant public health concern, as biofilms shield pathogens from antimicrobial agents that diffuse into bacterial cells [59, 60]. This facilitates persistent infections and serves as a hotspot for the transfer of ARGs to other clinically relevant pathogens via horizontal gene transfer. Interestingly, Den Reijer et al. [61] revealed that hemolysins also play a role in the formation of biofilms by S. aureus. Thus, MDR Staphylococcus species may be transmitted from both sampling sources and can cause difficult-to-treat infections, particularly among immunocompromised patients in both community and hospital settings.

Amplification of the elongation factor gene was performed for the identification of the Staphylococcus species (Fig. 2). Fragments of the tuf gene are commonly used as targets for analyzing mixed communities of Staphylococcus species [62–64]. Furthermore, the detection of the ant(4´)-Ia, aph(2’)-Ib, aph(3´)-IIIa, and aac(6´) resistance genes among MDR strains validates the observed high phenotypic resistance to gentamicin (an aminoglycoside) in the isolates (Fig. 3). The widespread empirical use of gentamicin as an anti-staphylococcal agent in clinical and veterinary settings within low-resource environments is likely to promote the selection of resistant strains [65]. The exclusive presence of these genes in isolates recovered from potable water samples suggest the potential role of water sources as reservoirs and hotspots for the dissemination of diverse resistance mechanisms [26, 66–68]. Future studies should investigate alternative genetic mechanisms that may underlie aminoglycoside resistance in the clinical isolates, given that the targeted aminoglycoside resistance genes were not detected in phenotypically resistant clinical isolates (Table 5).

Conclusion

This exploratory study provides initial insights into the potential role of potable water as a dissemination route for MDR and biofilm-forming MRSS in water intended for human use in the Dutsin-Ma town. The prevalence of MDR and MRSS strains in such water sources suggests the growing impact of anthropogenic activities on the dissemination of MRSS beyond clinical settings. The detection of virulence traits, including hemolytic activity and biofilm formation coexisting with ARGs, underscores the potential of these organisms to persist, spread, and cause recalcitrant infections. The presence of targeted ARGs exclusively in isolates from water sources further emphasizes the urgent need for improved water quality monitoring.

Owing to resource constraints, we were unable to sequence the tuf amplicon for species identification and randomly selected targeted ARGs based on predefined criteria for molecular analysis. This approach is acknowledged as a limitation of the study. Therefore, further research is warranted to comprehensively elucidate the resistome, virulome, plasmidome, circulating species, sequence types (STs), clones, and their genetic relatedness using whole-genome sequencing, particularly to validate potential transmission between the two sources. Our findings underscore the need for improved hygiene practices and the adoption of integrated One Health approaches for surveillance, enhanced water quality monitoring, and strengthened antibiotic stewardship to curb the spread of MRSS in both environmental and clinical settings in northwestern Nigeria.
