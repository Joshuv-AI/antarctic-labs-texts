Relationships between self-efficacy beliefs and personal factors in final-year medical students
Authors: Lea Jebram, Sigrid Harendza
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedical students entering postgraduate training often feel ill-prepared for clinical practice. This has implications for physician well-being and the quality of patient care. Self-efficacy represents an important contributing factor in the transition process. Identifying student characteristics associated with self-efficacy is important in order to design possible interventions to enhance self-efficacy. Therefore, we assessed the relationship between final-year medical students’ general self-efficacy with their personality traits, need for cognitive closure, and medical knowledge in order to identify factors contributing to the assessment and improvement of self-efficacy.MethodsSelf-assessed data regarding general self-efficacy was collected from 188 final-year medical students who participated in a first day of residency simulation between March and September 2023. We also assessed several personal factors including the HEXACO Personality Inventory Revised (HEXACO-PI-R), a short scale to measure Need for Cognitive Closure (16-NCCS), and students’ medical exam grades (M2). We ran multiple correlation and multiple linear regression analyses across all variables. We then added a second analysis each with general self-efficacy and HEXACO domains Emotionality and Extraversion on facet level, which both showed significant associations with self-efficacy in the respective first analysis.ResultsAcross all variables, self-efficacy correlated significantly negatively with Emotionality and positively with Extraversion. Both domains also significantly predicted self-efficacy accordingly in the regression model. On a facet analysis, Sentimentality (Emotionality) and Sociability (Extraversion) did not correlate significantly with self-efficacy. In the regression model only the facets Sociability (Extraversion) and Fearfulness (Emotionality) significantly predicted lower self-efficacy. Furthermore, self-efficacy correlated significantly and negatively with the Need for Cognitive Closure (NCC). Additionally, significant positive and negative correlations of the NCC with both Emotionality and Extraversion, respectively, as well as a significant positive correlation for exam grades (M2) and Extraversion were found.ConclusionsSelf-efficacy contributes to medial students’ preparedness for clinical practice and can be predicted by several personal factors. Students should learn and reflect about these factors and need to be supported in increasing their self-efficacy and its self-assessment towards their transition to postgraduate training. Construction of a specific instrument for self-efficacy assessment utilizing the insight provided by the findings is proposed as a next step.

Background

Undergraduate medical students’ transition to postgraduate training is accompanied by ever-increasing responsibility, workload, and stress [1]. Many residents report insufficient preparedness for medical practice relating to specific skills and practices [1–4] as well as being aware of capabilities and confidence in abilities [5, 6]. Feeling unprepared for medical practice causes additional stress for medical students [7] which can lead to burnout and poor patient care [8]. Preparedness for practice mainly involves acquiring adequate competences and skills [9–12]. However, it also relates to qualities like capability, adaptability, self-confidence, the ability to cope with uncertainty [13], and self-efficacy [14].

Self-efficacy is often associated with preparedness [1] and explains varied levels of learner confidence and coping with failure [15]. Medical students’ self-efficacy does not simply depend on how far they have progressed through the curriculum [16], but also on their personal backgrounds and experiences [17]. It has been shown that self-efficacy is also developed through experiences like observing role models, receiving feedback, and experiencing anxiety or fear [18]. Such contextual factors raise the need for context specific self-efficacy assessment [19]. In order to understand which factors most affect the development of self-efficacy in medical students it is crucial to investigate the relationship of self-efficacy to personal factors. Results from the general population are not equal for all age groups [20] and may also not apply to medical students, since this population’s personality differs from the normal population [21], e.g. with higher scores and therefore skewed distributions in personality traits conscientiousness, agreeableness, openness [22], and extraversion [23]. Furthermore, there have been propositions of nonlinear relationships in the medical field for personal factors like facets of extraversion [24] and empathy [25]. Since personality traits are shaped by culture [26], medical students develop their personality influenced by the culture they find in medical school and hospital environments [27].

In previous studies of medical students, higher perceived self-efficacy was shown to correlate positively with tolerance for uncertainty [28] and increased emotional intelligence [29], but negatively with anxiety [30]. Furthermore, student self-efficacy and curiosity were reported as protective factors against depression or anxiety whereas intolerance of uncertainty was identified as a risk-factor for these [31]. Self-efficacy was influenced positively by conscientiousness and openness, but negatively by neuroticism [32]. However, these medical student samples were mostly cross-sectional regarding the year of advancement, and personality traits conscientiousness and neuroticism differ between the years of study [33], with their influence on performance changing with experience [24, 34].

Final-year medical students often feel unprepared for clinical practice despite transition programs such as performance-related feedback [3], mentoring programmes [35, 36] or transition courses from undergraduate to postgraduate training [8]. Therefore, we focused on student self-efficacy and its influencing factors to further address this perceived lack of preparation. Investigating a homogeneous study population of final-year students is of particular interest, since previous study results rely on cross-sectional student samples and results from the general population regarding self-efficacy and relating constructs’ influences are not generally applicable to the medical student population. Therefore, here we explore self-efficacy and factors influencing it in final-year undergraduate medical students participating in a simulation of a first day of residency. The results from this exploratory study could potentially identify important aspects for interventions in the medical curriculum to help develop medical students’ self-efficacy.

Methods

Study design and participants

Between March and September 2023, 188 final-year undergraduate medical students were recruited from the German universities of Hamburg (n = 95, vertically integrated 6-year curriculum, N = 340 total final-year students per year, participation rate 27.9%) and Freiburg (n = 93, regular 6-year curriculum, N = 320 total final-year students per year, participation rate 29.1%) to participate in a validated, competence-based simulation of the first day of residency [37, 38]. Sample size was calculated using G*Power Version 3.1.9.6 [39] which indicated a total sample size of n = 178 for a medium effect of 0.15 with type I error probability set to 0.05 and a resulting power of 0.95 for a multiple linear regression model with n = 11 predictors. Participation was voluntary and on a first-come, first-served basis. Participants filled out an online questionnaire at home prior to the simulation including the following instruments: general self-efficacy scale (GSES) [40], HEXACO Personality Inventory-Revised (HEXACO-PI-R) [41] and a short scale to measure need for cognitive closure (16-NCCS) [42]. Furthermore, they provided sociodemographic information (age and gender) and their result of a national knowledge exam (M2) before entering the final year. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Chamber of Physicians, Hamburg (PV3649) and all participants provided informed written consent for participation which was voluntary as well as anonymous.

Instruments

General self-efficacy scale (GSES)

The German version of the general self-efficacy scale (GSES) [40] was used to assess self-efficacy. The scale includes 10 items assessing one’s belief to be able to perform novel or difficult tasks or cope with adversity, e.g., “I am confident that I could deal efficiently with unexpected events”. Answers are given on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = “not at all true” 2 = “hardly true”, 3 = “moderately true”, 4 = “exactly true”). Internal consistency for the GSES was α = 0.83, comparable with previous assessments where internal consistencies varied between α = 0.75 and α = 0.91 [43]. Scores are calculated by summing up all 10 items, resulting in scores ranging from 10 to 40 where higher scores indicate higher self-efficacy.

HEXACO personality inventory – revised (HEXACO-PI-R)

For personality assessment, we used the German, self-assessment version of the HEXACO Personality Inventory – Revised (HEXACO-PI-R) [41]. The HEXACO-PI-R covers six personality dimensions (Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, Emotionality, Extraversion, Honesty-Humility, and Openness to Experience) with a total of 60 items, each on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “strong agreement”, 5 = “strong disagreement”). For the purpose of this study the Likert scale was inverted ranging from 1 = “strong disagreement” to 5 = “strong agreement”. Internal consistencies for the HEXACO-PI-R dimensions were α = 0.68 for Agreeableness, α = 0.77 for Conscientiousness, α = 0.77 for Emotionality, α = 0.72 for Extraversion, α = 0.76 for Honesty-Humility and α = 0.64 for Openness to Experience.

16-need for cognitive closure scale (16-NCCS)

Need for cognitive closure (NCC) was assessed with the German short scale to measure need for cognitive closure (16-NCCS) [42]. containing 16 items on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree”, 6 = “strongly agree”). The 16-item version is based on a 15-item scale [44], originally derived from a version with 47 items to measure need for closure [45]. Internal consistency for the 16-NCCS was α = 0.77.

National knowledge exam (M2)

The national knowledge exam (M2) is part of the exams which are required to obtain a German Medical License. It takes place before the final year of a six-year medical undergraduate curriculum and consists of 320 clinical multiple-choice questions. Examination marks range from 1 = “very good” to 6 = “insufficient”. With a mark lower than 4 the exam is failed.

Data analysis

Data were processed and analyzed with R Version 4.2.2 [46]. We inverted raw item values where indicated and calculated scale values for each instrument. To examine relations between variables self-efficacy, personality dimensions, need for cognitive closure, and knowledge, multiple correlations were computed using Spearman’s rank correlation from the “correlation”-package [47]. Additionally, to ensure interpretability across participants from both Hamburg and Freiburg, we examined group differences regarding age, sex, and all multiple correlation variables. Depending on homogeneity of variance we conducted multiple t-tests as well as a Chi-Square-test for differences regarding sex. We further included affiliation as a group variable in the multiple correlation to check for potential differences. Moreover, in order to gain more detailed insight into significant correlations of self-efficacy and personality dimensions, additional multiple correlations were computed on facet level of the respective HEXACO-PI-R dimensions.

We further ran a multiple linear regression model for self-efficacy as dependent variable to determine statistical significance of the predictor variables’ influence on self-efficacy. We also included age, sex, and affiliation to either the University of Hamburg or Freiburg as demographic control variables. Similar to the multiple correlation, we also ran a second model with facets of significant HEXACO-PI-R predictors from the first model instead of the respective domain. All statistical tests were conducted with a general alpha level of 0.05 and p-values for multiple correlations were adjusted using Bonferroni’s correction [48] to address the issue of multiple testing.

Including affiliation to either Hamburg or Freiburg as a group variable in the multiple correlation showed no considerable difference, indicating interpretability across all participants. Discussion

The aim of this study was to identify factors that may influence self-efficacy in final-year medical students. Such identification may be a potential means to address the improvement of self-efficacy and thus students’ lack of feeling prepared for clinical practice. We found significant correlations of self-efficacy with the personality traits Emotionality (negative correlation) and Extraversion (positive correlation). These results are in line with existing findings in the medial field indicating that general self-efficacy correlates negatively with Neuroticism, the corresponding domain of Emotionality in Big-5 personality measurements, and positively with extraversion [32, 49]. Both Emotionality and Extraversion also significantly predicted self-efficacy in the multiple regression model. On facet-level, no significant correlations where found for either Sentimentality (one of the four facets of Emotionality), or Sociability (one of the four facets of Extraversion) while both domains overall correlated significantly with general self-efficacy. However, when controlled for other predictor variables in the multiple regression model, significant estimates were only obtained for facets Fearfulness and Sociability, both negatively predicting self-efficacy.

Sentimentality refers to a tendency to feel strong emotional bonds with others. In the medical field, empathy revolves mainly around its cognitive rather than its emotional component [50], because a degree of emotional distance is needed for patient related work [51, 52] and can even improve patient-related outcomes [53]. Hence, a curvilinear u-shaped association where a moderate amount of sentimentality has a positive influence on self-efficacy could explain the lack of significant correlation with students’ self-efficacy. Overall, the negative correlations of most facets of Emotionality with self-efficacy reflect existing findings. Individuals show higher self-efficacy when they are less anxious or frustrated [54, 55], cope better with stress and frustration [56], and have better emotional regulation and resilience [57]. Only Fearfulness significantly predicted lower self-efficacy in the regression model, indicating no partial variance explained by the other facets independent of the remaining predictor variables in the model. Fearfulness has been shown to correlate negatively with risk taking and sensation seeking [58]. Therefore, fearfulness can hinder medical students’ willingness to enter complex medical situations, an obstacle in clinical postgraduate training in which dealing with unknown or unfamiliar situations occurs frequently. It has also been shown that fearful medical students experience increased distress, affecting their patient care and overall professional behaviour [59].

Sociability reflects the students’ tendencies to enjoy conversation and other social interaction, and did not significantly correlate with self-efficacy in our study. A curvilinear u-shaped association with a measure of extraversion, as a means of being adventurous, seeking social interaction, and exploring the environment, has been previously shown in the medical field [24] and could be a sign for being able to manage professional boundaries [60, 61]. However, multiple regression analysis showed that Sociability significantly predicts higher self-efficacy when controlled for other predictors. Higher sociability refers to preferring solitary activities rather than social interaction. This reflects the positive effects of solitude [62] and a positive correlation of self-efficacy and solitude capacity has been described [63]. This finding is particularly interesting since all other facets of Extraversion did not significantly predict self-efficacy albeit showing significant positive correlations. This indicates that when controlled for the remaining facets as well as other influences on self-efficacy, only Sociability has a significant and negative effect on self-efficacy. Notably, while Extraversion as a domain significantly predicts higher self-efficacy with higher scores, Sociability has a negative influence. This suggests that utilizing Extraversion alone as a domain predicting self-efficacy, rather than Extraversion’s facets, leads to biased results in medical student samples. We did not find an influence of Conscientiousness or Openness on self-efficacy in final-year medical students, a result that had been found in another study, but using a different general self-efficacy scale and medical students from different years of study [32]. Note that the influence of conscientiousness on academic success has previously been shown to change with progression in undergraduate medical education [34].

The Need for Cognitive Closure (NCC) showed a significant negative correlation with general self-efficacy. This finding aligns with previous research on ambiguity tolerance, the opposite of NCC. Ambiguity tolerance increased in medical students who experienced themselves as competent [64]. Experiencing mastery has been shown to raise perceived self-efficacy [65, 66]. In clinical practice, decisions are often affected by contradictory information, ambiguity and uncertainty [67]. A higher NCC thus seems detrimental for finishing clinical tasks under time constraints and could affect an individual’s experience of competence, and consequently the belief in their capabilities. In turn, excess information that is clinically irrelevant leads to information overload, increasing physicians’ mental load and error rates [68]. In the multiple regression model, need for cognitive closure did not significantly predict self-efficacy, thus indicating that need for cognitive closure does not explain variance in self-efficacy when other personal factors’ influences are controlled for.

Our results also show significant positive correlations of the M2 grade (a measurement of academic achievement) with Extraversion, indicating better exam results with lower Extraversion since low exam grades resemble better achievement. Due to the social activity aspect of extraversion, it has been shown to correlate positively with less learning engagement due to diverging priorities [23, 56], thus explaining a negative correlation with academic achievement. Medical students with higher extraversion have, however, been shown to show greater empathy with simulated patients [69], leading to greater patient satisfaction [70]. Communicating more empathetically was, however, not correlated with successful gathering of important medical information [71]. Thus, while extraversion negatively relates to many clinical knowledge assessments, it relates positively to patient satisfaction and feedback, thereby contributing to physicians’ self-efficacy. In turn, improving relationship-centered communication skills for clinical practice not only improved patient satisfaction, but also physician self-efficacy [72].

Our study’s findings are limited, because assessment of personality traits with instruments designed for the general population can lead to right-skewed results with samples in the medical field [24]. For general self-efficacy, there is no indication of a right-skewed distribution in the field, but correlations with personal factors can be limited due to associations of self-efficacy with specific aspects in the medical field. These factors might not be captured in an instrument designed for general assessment. Also, controlling for other factors not included in our study may be important in studying student self-efficacy. For example, while we included some demographic data, the inclusion of more control variables, e.g. mother tongue, would have been advisable. Furthermore, there is substantial research on the interrelation between culture and personality [26] which we did not address as potential control variables in our study. Also, the generalizability of results across all final-year students is somewhat limited since we only assessed just over 25% of the total target population in one year.

On the other hand, a strength of this study is that data was collected from final-year medical students facing a simulated first day as a resident, thus being most likely concerned with their preparedness for work and their self-efficacy in the simulation. By assessing personality traits using the HEXACO instead of the Big-5 model, the Honesty-Humility trait was included which is relevant in the medical field as it is related to professionalism [73].

Our findings suggest the value of medical students’ reflecting on and being aware of their own personality, since this has a bearing on self-efficacy and the ability to enter clinical work. While personality traits are relatively stable across undergraduate medical studies [74], understanding their contribution to self-efficacy further provides information about which students may benefit from reflective interventions to enhance self-efficacy. Such reflections on personality traits and their influences on self-efficacy and professional behaviour could become part of courses on professional identity formation [75] or in trainings simulating clinical work situations [28, 37, 76]. Longitudinal implementation of such reflective courses could also support medical students in their decision whether they wish to pursue clinical disciplines for their postgraduate training. Additionally, the question of why students feel unprepared should also be addressed in future research. This is especially important since individual differences regarding aspects that contribute to students’ feelings of unpreparedness could also influence how self-efficacy can be utilized as a means to improve feelings of preparedness.

Conclusions

Since general self-efficacy contributes to medical students’ preparedness for clinical practice, students should be aware of the personal factors we identified to have an influence on self-efficacy. Understanding and reflecting what factors influence self-efficacy is important for adequate self-assessment, and possibly for designing interventions to support students in increasing their self-efficacy as they transition into clinical practice. A next step could involve constructing an instrument to specifically assess medical self-efficacy at the beginning of clinical practice. Our findings support the development of such an instrument by providing insight into what aspects are relevant for self-efficacy in final-year medical students.
