The effect of complex prebiotics on quality and functional properties of synbiotic fermented milk
Authors: Yang Liu, Xiaoxiao Liu, Longfei Zhang, Qiming Li, Hengxian Qu, Dawei Chen, Kai Wang, Ruixia Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrebiotic-probiotic synergy is vital to fermented milk's overall performance. This study investigates the effects of complex prebiotics (CPs) on the quality and functional properties of probiotic-fermented milk. Results indicated that CPs improved the physicochemical properties and sensory characteristics of fermented milk. Meanwhile, CPs supplementation improved the flavor profile of fermented milk by increasing the levels of key aroma compounds, including aldehydes (e.g., decanal increased by 18.80 % in the St-Lr + CP3 group) and ketones (e.g., 3-hydroxy-2-butanone increased by 16.37 % in the St-Lf-Lp-Lr + CP4 group). Moreover, CPs supplementation significantly enhanced the ACE inhibitory activity (up to 73.74 % in the St-Lp group) and α-amylase inhibition (with a 27.69 % improvement observed in the St-Lf group), while also strengthening the antimicrobial effects of fermented milk against Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Listeria monocytogenes, and Bacillus subtilis. These findings demonstrate the great potential of CPs in the development of health-promoting dairy products and their applications in the food industry.Keywords: Complex prebiotics, Fermented milk, Physicochemical and sensory properties, Flavor profile, Health-promoting activity

Introduction

Prebiotics are substrates that can be selectively utilized by the host microbial community to provide health benefits. Commonly used prebiotics include inulin, fructooligosaccharides (FOS), galactooligosaccharides (GOS), xylooligosaccharides (XOS), and stachyose (Davani-Davari et al., 2019; Palaniappan et al., 2021; Ta et al., 2024). In addition to direct consumption, prebiotics are often added to fermented food systems alongside probiotics, enhancing the growth/colonization of probiotics while providing additional benefits to the fermented products, such as enhanced flavor profiles and functional attributes (Gibson et al., 2017). Compared to single prebiotic addition, complex prebiotics (CPs) offer superior benefits in improving probiotic fermentation performance and overall product quality. For instance, the complex prebiotic (CP) containing 0.05 % FOS, 0.20 % GOS, 0.30 % inulin, 0.20 % stachyose, and 0.30 % XOS increased the adhesion rate of probiotics to 3.98 %, approximately 32.20 % higher than that observed with 0.50 % FOS only (Liu et al., 2023). Moreover, combined use of inulin and GOS has shown more pronounced improvements in the texture and flavor release of fermented milk compared to their individual use (Chi et al., 2024). Therefore, incorporating CPs represents an effective strategy for enhancing both the quality and functional properties of fermented products.

Synbiotic fermented milk is a type of flavored dairy produced through the synergistic fermentation of probiotics and specific prebiotics. In synbiotic fermented milk, replacing single prebiotics with CPs has emerged as a new trend for enhancing product quality (Garcia-Burgos et al., 2020; Saleem et al., 2024). For different strains used in synbiotic fermented milk, it is necessary to select an appropriate CPs formulation based on their overall preference. For example, inulin and GOS are suitable combinations for significantly improving the texture and flavor of Lactobacillus helveticus-fermented milk (Chi et al., 2024). A formulation using FOS, inulin, GOS at a ratio of 2:2:1 has been found effective in enhancing the quality and flavor of Lactobacillus gasseri JM1-fermented soybean milk (Zheng et al., 2024). Therefore, when adding CPs to synbiotic fermented milk, formulation optimization should be tailored to specific probiotic strains to achieve an enhancement in product quality.

Our research group previously screened and identified three probiotic strains for fermented milk: Limosilactobacillus fermentum grx08 (L. fermentum grx08), Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus grx10 (L. rhamnosus grx10), and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum grx16 (L. plantarum grx16). All these probiotics possess both desirable fermentation characteristics and notable health benefits. L. fermentum grx08 exhibited significant modulatory effects on oxidative stress in the liver, heart, and kidneys of obese rats, enhancing the antioxidant capacity of these organs to varying degrees (Qu et al., 2022). L. plantarum grx16 has been proven to inhibit the activity of intestinal amylase and lipase, reducing starch and triglyceride absorption, thereby alleviating insulin resistance and lipid accumulation in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease rats (Qu et al., 2023). L. rhamnosus grx10 demonstrated excellent tolerance to environmental stresses such as temperature, pH, and bile salts, along with notable antibacterial and adhesion capabilities, making it beneficial for maintaining intestinal health (Liu et al., 2023). However, the CP formulations suitable for these three probiotic strains remain unknown. Moreover, the effects of specific CPs on the quality and functional properties of synbiotic fermented milk produced by these probiotics require further elucidation to facilitate the industrial development of innovative synbiotic dairy products. Therefore, the current study designed and optimized CPs formulations for three novel probiotic strains. Then, comprehensive evaluation methods including texture analysis, sensory evaluation, and flavor profile analysis were used to elucidate the effects of different CPs on the quality of synbiotic fermented milk. Furthermore, changes in the functional properties of the synbiotic fermented milk, including ACE inhibitory activity, α-amylase inhibitory activity, and antimicrobial capacity, were also investigated following CPs supplementation.

Materials and methods

Reagents and materials

L. fermentum grx08 (CGMCC No. 7695), L. plantarum grx16 (CGMCC No. 10921), L. rhamnosus grx10 (CGMCC No. 2526), and S. thermophilus grx02 (CGMCC No. 2525) were provided by Key Laboratory of Probiotics and Deep Processing of Dairy Products, Yangzhou University. Whole milk powder and skim milk powder were purchased from New Zealand Dairy Ltd. Inulin, FOS, GOS, stachyose, and XOS were obtained from Shanghai Haoyue Biotechnology Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Commercial fermented milk containing prebiotics were obtained at local supermarket (Yangzhou, China). The de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) Broth, MRS Agar, M17 Broth, MRS Agar (Sugar Free), Luria-Bertani (LB) Broth, and LB Agar were products of Haibo Biotechnology Ltd. (Qingdao, China). Angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE, derived from rabbit lung), α-amylase (derived from Bacillus licheniformis). All chemical reagents mentioned in this study were at least of analytical grade.

Optimization of the CPs

S. thermophilus grx02 was activated using M17 Broth. L. fermentum grx08, L. plantarum grx16, and L. rhamnosus grx10 were activated using MRS Broth. All strains activated from glycerol tubes up to the second generation were plated into sterilized skimmed milk at a 3 % inoculum, fermented to curd, and set aside at 4 °C. Using the count of live bacteria as an evaluation index, the optimal combination of prebiotic additions was subjected to orthogonal experiments using the quadratic orthogonal rotation design. The orthogonal experiment was designed using a quadratic orthogonal rotation method, incorporating five factors (FOS, GOS, inulin, stachyose, and XOS), each at five levels. The concentration levels for each prebiotic were set based on preliminary experiments, ranging from 0.3 % to 0.7 % (w/v). The group with the highest count of viable bacteria was selected as the best combination of CPs. The MRS solid medium was used for viable bacteria enumeration based on colony formation.

Preparation of synbiotic fermented milk

The optimized formulation of CPs was added to a solution containing 12 % bovine milk and 6 % sucrose, respectively. After thorough mixing, the mixture was subjected to high-pressure homogenization treatment at 20 MPa. Following pasteurization, 0.01 % (v/v) S. thermophilus grx02 and 3 % (v/v) probiotic strains (L. fermentum grx08, L. plantarum grx16, L. rhamnosus grx10) were inoculated into the sterilized substrate. The inoculated mixture was then incubated in a 37 °C incubator for fermentation, with the fermentation process terminated when the pH reached 4.5. The St-Lf system refers to milk fermented by S. thermophilus grx02 + L. fermentum grx08; the St-Lp system refers to milk fermented by S. thermophilus grx02 + L. plantarum grx16; the St-Lr system refers to milk fermented by S. thermophilus grx02 + L. rhamnosus grx10, and the St-Lf-Lp-Lr system refers to milk fermented by S. thermophilus grx02 + L. fermentum grx08 + L. plantarum grx16 + L. rhamnosus grx10. The CPs used in the St-Lf, St-Lp, St-Lr, and St-Lf-Lp-Lr groups were named as CP1, CP2, CP3, and CP4, respectively.

Determination of pH and acidity of fermented milk

The pH value of the samples was measured using pHS-3E meter (Inessa Scientific Instruments Co Ltd., Shanghai, China) after calibration.

Determination of the texture of fermented milk

The texture parameters including hardness, adhesion, cohesiveness, elasticity, gumminess, and chewiness of fermented milk samples were measured according to the protocol of Shiby and Mishra (2008) with some modifications. An A/BE probe was installed on an Enhanced Load Sensor (Mecmesin Limited Co., Ltd., Slinfold, UK), with the instrument set to a pre-compression speed of 1.0 mm/s, a post-compression speed of 1.0 mm/s, a trigger pressure of 0.1 N, a test height of 65 mm, and a compression level of 50 %.

Sensory evaluation and acceptability test of fermented milk

The scoring system described by Li et al. (2025) was referenced with slight modifications. Sensory evaluation of fermented milk samples was performed by 15 professional panelists from the dairy laboratory. While ethical approval was not required for this sensory study, all participants were thoroughly briefed on the objectives and specifics of the test, and they provided informed consent to partake in the study. To facilitate comparative evaluation, two commercially popular fermented milk products were included alongside the samples developed in this study.The sensory evaluation indicators include color, acidity, sweetness, odor, smoothness, and viscosity acceptability. Details of the sensory evaluation are shown in Table S4. A total of 100 consumers were interviewed at three locations (Hanjiang, Guangling and Jiangdu) in Yangzhou using the central location testing method (Kim et al., 2010). Consumers were selected based on gender and age, ranging from 18 to 70 years. Each participant evaluated the samples using a 9-point hedonic box scale from ‘dislike extremely’ to ‘like extremely’. Six fermented milk samples (four developed in this study and two commercial products) were labeled with random three-digit codes and simultaneously presented in randomized order using white paper cups. The results of consumer acceptability test experiment were shown in Fig. S1.

Analysis of volatile flavor of fermented milk

The flavor compounds of fermented milk samples were determined referring to the method of Fan et al. (2023).

Observation on the microstructure of fermented milk

The microstructure of the fermented milk sample was analyzed following the method of Kristo et al. (2011), with slight modifications. Briefly, samples were fixed in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde for over 4 h, followed by sequential dehydration using graded ethanol solutions (30 %, 50 %, 70 %, 80 %, 90 %, 100 %), with each step lasting 10 min. Then, the dehydrated samples were wrapped with tin foil and then frozen in liquid nitrogen for more than 24 h. The freeze-dried samples were coated with platinum film and examined using S-4800 II Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (Hitachi, Japan) at a magnification of 3000 × .

Determination of ACE inhibitory activity of fermented milk

The ACE inhibitory activity was assessed using the method outlined by Wang et al. (2021) as slightly modified for this study. Briefly, 5 mL of fermented milk sample was centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected, pH-adjusted, and filtered. Then the supernatant sample was incubated with sodium tetraborate buffer, and Hippuryl-L-histidyl-L-leucine, followed by the addition of ACE solution. After incubation, the reaction was terminated with 1 M HCl.

Hippuric acid was extracted using ethyl acetate, and the absorbance of the extracted acid at 228 nm was measured for assessing ACE inhibitory activity. Subsequently, the inhibition rate was computed with reference to equations from the literature.

Determination of α-amylase inhibitory activity of fermented milk

α-amylase inhibitory activity was assessed via Kim et al. (2004) the method with minor modifications. The assay involved incubating 100 μL human salivary α-amylase with 100 μL sample at 37 °C for 5 min, then adding 250 μL 1 % starch solution to initiate reaction. Post-incubation, 200 μL DNS reagent terminated the reaction, then heated at 100 °C for 15 min. Absorbance at 540 nm was measured, and α-amylase activity calculated via the formulas in the references.

Determination of the ability of fermented milk to inhibit pathogenic bacteria

Escherichia coli (CICC10899), Salmonella Typhi (CICC25299), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CICC10351), Bacillus subtilis (CICC10012), Listeria monocytogenes (CICC21533), and Staphylococcus aureus (CICC10201) were inoculated in LB broth. The overnight-cultured bacteria (200 μL) were spread evenly on solidified LB agar medium using a spreading stick. Wells with a diameter of 6 mm were punched into the agar using a sterile steel tube, and 100 μL of sample was injected into each well.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate. Statistical analysis and graphical representation were performed using Microsoft Excel 2016, IBM SPSS Statistics 25, GraphPad Prism 10.4.1, and Origin 2021. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Duncan's multiple range test was applied for post-hoc analysis, and the value of p < 0.05 was considered to indicate significant differences.

Results and discussion

Formulation of CPs

The optimized formulations of CPs are shown in Table S1–S3. For L. fermentum grx08, the optimized formulation of CPs (CP1) includes 0.49 % FOS, 0.37 % GOS, 0.62 % inulin, 0.48 % stachyose, and 0.28 % XOS. For L. plantarum grx16, the optimal formulation (CP2) consists of 0.61 % FOS, 0.33 % GOS, 0.46 % inulin, 0.48 % stachyose, and 0.14 % XOS. The optimized formulation of CPs for L. rhamnosus grx10 (CP3) comprises 0.58 % FOS, 0.44 % GOS, 0.47 % inulin, 0.53 % stachyose, and 0.24 % XOS. For the multi-strain fermentation system, the optimized formulation of CPs (CP4) contains 0.56 % FOS, 0.38 % GOS, 0.52 % inulin, 0.49 % stachyose, and 0.22 % XOS.

Effects of CPs on pH and acidity of fermented milk

The pH value and acidity of fermented milk are direct indicators reflecting the microbial activity and organic acid content within the system (Rashwan et al., 2023). The acidity of different groups (Fig. 1B) showed that the four-bacteria combination St-Lf-Lp-Lr group reached 72.34 ± 0.15°T, significantly higher than the two-bacteria combination groups (p < 0.05). With the addition of the CP, the St-Lf-Lp-Lr + CP4 system exhibited the highest acidity among all groups, increasing to 75.73 ± 0.94°T (p < 0.05).

The acidity of the St-Lf-Lp-Lr group was significantly higher than that of other groups, and it further increased when synergistically fermented with CP4, which may be attributed to cross-feeding effects among three strains. Synergistic metabolism likely accelerated lactose catabolism: L. rhamnosus grx10 efficiently hydrolyzes lactose via β-galactosidase, decomposes inulin (accounts for a high proportion in CP4), providing glucose/galactose for L. fermentum grx08 and L. plantarum grx16, which in turn enhance organic acid production. This aligns with multi-strain systems where metabolic complementarity improves substrate utilization (Gaggìa et al., 2010).

These results indicated that the effects of CPs on fermented milk acidity were strain-dependent. The multi-strain system showed increased acidity via cross-feeding synergies, while certain two-strain groups had decreased acidity due to prebiotic prioritization over lactose.

Effects of CPs on texture and microstructure of fermented milk

Hardness and adhesion are core indicators of the texture in fermented milk. Hardness of fermented milk is primarily determined by the three-dimensional network structure formed by milk protein (Li et al., 2023), while adhesion is affected by the protein content in fermented milk and exogenous additives. Enhancing hardness and adhesion within an appropriate range contributes to the texture stability of fermented milk.

As shown in Fig. 2A, the hardness of fermented milk exhibited an overall increasing trend with the addition of CPs. This increase is consistent with microstructure observations of fermented milk (Fig. 2G), where CP-treated groups showed a denser, more uniform protein-polysaccharide network in fermented milk with tightly cross-linked matrices (Guggisberg et al., 2009; Li et al., 2023). Among all CP additions, CP2 led to the greatest enhancement in hardness in the St-Lp group, with values rising from 0.31 ± 0.02 N to 0.40 ± 0.02 N. This effect stems from the ability of inulin and FOS to bind to proteins, which stabilizes the gel and enhances the hardness of fermented milk (Chi et al., 2024). Notably, hardness is also a key factor influencing the chewiness of fermented milk. Therefore, the improvement in chewiness observed in the St-Lp + CP2 group (Fig. 2F) can be partially attributed to the enhancement in hardness induced by CP2 supplementation. Moreover, the addition of CP2 resulted in an increase in the adhesion of the fermented milk in the St-Lp group, with values rising from 1.16 ± 0.38 mJ to 1.45 ± 0.28 mJ (Fig. 2B). This enhancement is partly attributed to more efficient utilization of FOS and inulin (which together account for 53 % of CP2) by L. plantarum grx16 for the synthesis of adhesive EPS, thereby strengthening interfacial interactions between proteins and polysaccharides (Chi et al., 2024).

Collectively, these results indicate that CPs addition improve the overall texture properties of fermented milk mainly by enhancing hardness, adhesion, and chewiness.

Sensory characteristics and acceptability test of fermented milk as affected by CPs

The structure and flavor of prebiotics, as well as the acids produced by probiotics during metabolism, both influence the sensory characteristics of fermented milk (Mohammadi & Mortazavian, 2011). As shown in Fig. 3, the addition of CPs generally improved the sensory attributes across groups. Notably, the supplementation of CP2 and CP3 led to significant improvements in the smoothness scores (p < 0.05) of fermented milk in the St-Lp and St-Lr groups, respectively. In contrast, the viscosity score of the St-Lr group, which was 5.70 ± 0.10 without CP3, further increased to 6.10 ± 0.20 after co-fermentation with CP3 (p < 0.05). These changes induced by CP2 and CP3 were primarily attributed to the synergistic effects of GOS and FOS contained in these CPs. These components improved the smoothness of fermented milk by enhancing hydrophilic thickening, filling gaps between casein micelles, and reducing granularity and surface roughness (Ng et al., 2018). Additionally, they promoted the production of exopolysaccharides by lactic acid bacteria, thereby increasing the overall viscosity of the fermented milk (Chi et al., 2024). These results revealed that the synergistic interaction among multiple prebiotics in the CPs had a positive impact on enhancing the sensory attributes of fermented milk. The acceptability test of fermented milk showed that our developed product was comparable to Commercial Product 1 in terms of consumer acceptance and showed significantly higher acceptability than Commercial Product 2 (Fig. S1). Specifically, our products outperformed the commercial samples in terms of acidity, sweetness, and viscosity, while no significant differences were observed in ordor and color (Table. S4).

Notably, the St-Lr + CP3 group showed significant improvement in odor scores (Fig. 3), with significant increases in OAV values for decanal (from 780.00 ± 40.00 to 926.67 ± 56.67) (Table S7). Decanal is a key component of fruity and floral aromas and may enhance the perception of “naturally fermented flavors” in sensory tests (Tong et al., 2018). In contrast, the St-Lf-Lp-Lr + CP4 group exhibited higher ketone levels. For instance, the OAV of 3-hydroxy-2-butanone increased by 16.36 % upon CP4 addition. This elevation showed a strong correlation with elevated levels of 2-heptanone (OAV increased from 48.79 ± 0.29 to 60.93 ± 0.43) and 2-nonanone (fruity, OAV increased from 25.37 ± 0.98 to 45.85 ± 1.71) (Table S7). These changes are key contributors to the smoother and more rounded odor characteristics of the fermented milk induced by CP4 (Yu et al., 2024).

The change in volatile flavor compounds can be attributed to prebiotics modulating probiotic metabolism, thereby enhancing the biosynthesis of volatile compounds. FOS and XOS in CPs promote the production of short-chain fatty acids and intermediate metabolites through cross-feeding between Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus, accelerating the formation of aldehydes and ketones (Roupar et al., 2022). On the contrary, the OAV reduction of hexanoic acid (from 23.45 ± 0.15 to 13.17 ± 0.15) in the St-Lf + CP1 group attenuated the unpleasant fatty taste and improved the acceptability of the odor (Table S7).

The L. rhamnosus grx10 in the St-Lr group promotes acetaldehyde production using CP3, while L. plantarum grx16 in St-Lp enhances the synthesis of 2-heptanone, demonstrating the role of prebiotic-strain synergies in flavor modulation. These changes indicate that CPs increased volatile compound levels and improved the overall aroma profile, leading to better odor score in sensory evaluation.

Effect of CPs on ACE inhibitory activity of fermented milk

As shown in Fig. 5, the ACE inhibition rates of fermented milks with the addition of CPs led to a general enhancement (p < 0.05). Especially, the ACE inhibition rate in the St-Lp group increased to 73.74 % after addition of CP2, which was significantly higher than that in the untreated St-Lp group (p < 0.05). This improvement may be attributed to the higher proportion of FOS in CP2, which enhanced the metabolic activity of L. plantarum grx16 by activating β-fructofuranosidase and the phosphotransferase system. As a result, the biosynthesis and accumulation of ACE-inhibitory bioactive compounds were promoted, thereby significantly boosting the overall ACE inhibitory capacity of the fermented milk (Chen et al., 2015). Meanwhile, FOS and inulin could enhance the proliferation and metabolic activity of probiotic strains, thereby indirectly promoting the efficient enzymatic hydrolysis of milk proteins. This process leads to the generation of bioactive peptides with ACE-inhibitory activity, ultimately improving the overall ACE inhibitory capacity of the fermented milk (Chand et al., 2021; M. Mohammadi et al., 2021). However, further peptide analysis is required to validate our hypothesis.

Effect of CPs on the α-amylase inhibitory activity of fermented milk

Inhibition of α-amylase, which catalyzes the hydrolysis of starch to glucose, slows the digestion and absorption of carbohydrates, thus helping to ameliorate diabetes caused by insufficient insulin secretion (Justino et al., 2018).

As Shown in Fig. 6, the α-amylase inhibitory rate in the St-Lp group was 69.18 %, which was significantly higher than the other groups without CPs (p < 0.05). After the addition of CPs, the activity of the fermented milk increased to varying degrees, with the St-Lp group reaching the highest level of 75.43 %. Mahmoudi et al. (2022) reported that the symbiotic fermented milk supplemented with inulin exhibited a 55.00 % increase in α-amylase inhibitory activity at the end of the storage period. Shafi et al. (2019) found that intervention with synbiotic fermented milk containing FOS in hyperglycemic rabbits resulted in reductions of 62.90 %, 71.50 %, and 57.00 % in blood glucose, urea, and creatinine levels, respectively. Considering that CP2 contains a high proportion of inulin and FOS (accounting for 52.97 % of CP2), it is hypothesized that the enhancement in α-amylase inhibitory activity observed in the St-Lp + CP2 group can be attributed to the synergistic effect of these two components.

Effect of CPs on the antimicrobial activity of fermented milk

The inherent antimicrobial properties of fermented milk play a crucial role in determining its quality throughout storage. As shown in Table 1, the CPs significantly enhanced the antimicrobial capacity of multiple fermented milk groups (p < 0.05), with effects showing strain specificity and compositional synergy. In the St-Lr + CP3 group, the FOS and inulin of CP3 promote rapid metabolism of L. rhamnosus grx10 to produce organic acids, which reduced the pH of fermented milk to 4.45 ± 0.02 and directly inhibited pathogenic growth via an acidic environment (Kariyawasam et al., 2020; Liong & Shah, 2005). Consistent with this, Abouloifa et al. (2020) reported that FOS promoted probiotics to produce organic acids, which reduced the biofilm formation and motility of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and the diameter of the inhibition zone increased from 11.94 ± 0.07 mm to 16.04 ± 0.17 mm.

The enhanced antimicrobial activity observed in the St-Lp + CP2 and St-Lf-Lp-Lr + CP4 groups was mainly attributed to compositional synergy. Despite similar FOS contents (0.5 % – 0.6 %) to the St-Lr + CP3 group, the St-Lp + CP2 group showed significantly improved Salmonella Typhi inhibition, with the inhibition zone expanding from 13.03 ± 0.14 mm to 14.47 ± 0.10 mm (Table 1). This enhancement is hypothesized to result from the synergistic effect of FOS and sucrose, with sucrose potentially stimulating probiotic production of hydrogen peroxide, which directly disrupts pathogenic cell membranes (Mortaz et al., 2022; Xi et al., 2020). The St-Lf-Lp-Lr + CP4 group exhibited a larger Salmonella Typhi inhibition zone (from 15.86 ± 0.24 mm to 17.49 ± 0.19 mm, Table 1), likely due to co-metabolism enriching short-chain fatty acids (e.g., acetic and butyric acids) and antimicrobial peptides, thereby exerting a dual inhibitory effect through pH reduction and direct disruption of pathogenic cells (Abouloifa et al., 2020; Pranckute et al., 2016).

Although CP2 lacked a high proportion of FOS and was primarily composed of GOS and inulin, it may have promoted the secretion of antimicrobial proteins or exopolysaccharides by L. plantarum grx16, thereby indirectly inhibiting cell wall synthesis in Gram-positive bacteria (e.g., Bacillus subtilis). This indicates that different CP formulations can trigger diverse antimicrobial mechanisms through strain-specific metabolic pathways (e.g., glycolysis, pentose phosphate pathway), rather than relying solely on acid-mediated inhibition.

Collectively, the antimicrobial efficacy of CPs stems from a synergistic interplay among component composition, strain metabolism, and bioactive products. Future research should be carried out using metabolomic approaches to dissect key antimicrobial component differences across groups, thereby providing a basis for precision formulation design.

Potential product applications

The enhanced physicochemical, sensory, and functional properties observed with CPs supplementation suggest broad application prospects. The complex prebiotic-probiotic formulations can be used to develop various types of synbiotic dairy products, such as set yogurt, stirred yogurt, and drinking-type fermented milk. Given their improved health-promoting activities, these formulations are also suitable for functional products aimed at managing blood pressure and glycemic levels. Furthermore, they can be adapted for plant-based or lactose-free fermented products, thereby expanding their appeal to a broader range of consumers. Overall, our findings provide a foundation for developing diverse, health-oriented fermented foods and beverages.

Conclusion

This study explored the effects of CPs on the quality and functional characteristics of symbiotic fermented milk. CPs improved physicochemical properties and volatile flavor profiles of fermented milk, thereby enhancing the holistic quality attributes. Furthermore, CPs enhanced ACE inhibitory activity, α-amylase inhibitory ability, and broad-spectrum antibacterial capacity against various pathogenic microorganisms. These findings highlight the potential of the synergistic effect between compound prebiotics and probiotics, providing new insights into innovative applications of CPs and the development of functional fermented foods.
