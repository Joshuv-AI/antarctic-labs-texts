Taxonomy, function and plasmids of microbial soil communities of Polish salt graduation towers
Authors: Karol Ciuchcinski, Agata Bluszcz, Lukasz Dziewit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalt graduation towers create hypersaline environments that host specialized microbial communities, offering unique opportunities to study their adaptations to salinity. In this study, we present a comprehensive catalogue of data recovered from soil samples collected across three salt graduation towers in Poland (Ciechocinek, Konstancin-Jeziorna and Busko-Zdrój). Our investigation includes total metagenomic and 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing of nine collected soil samples, as well as metaplasmidome sequencing from most saline samples at each location. We established both solid and liquid enrichment cultures for these high-salinity samples, followed by hybrid long- and short-read sequencing. We also used multiple state-of-the-art tools to fully describe and characterize the recovered sequences. Overall, this comprehensive dataset integrates metagenomic, enrichment culture, 16S rRNA amplicon, and (meta)plasmidome sequencing data with corresponding physicochemical soil parameters, providing a valuable resource for comparative analyses, method development, and studies of microbial diversity and adaptation across saline environments.Subject terms: Soil microbiology, Biodiversity

Background & Summary

Increasing salinity is one of the leading causes of soil degradation, with recent reports showing that over 10% of the global land area is affected by salt accumulation1. This issue is particularly severe in arid and semi-arid regions, where improper irrigation practices and natural processes exacerbate salinization2. Furthermore, global warming intensifies the problem by increasing evaporation rates and altering precipitation patterns, which contributes to salt buildup in soils3. This trend poses a significant challenge, threatening ecosystem health, agricultural productivity and food security worldwide. Alongside these large-scale processes, unique anthropogenic hypersaline environments, such as salt graduation towers, provide valuable model systems for studying microbial adaptation to salinity.

In recent years, salt graduation towers have attracted increasing interest. Historically, those structures have been used for salt production, but nowadays they are primarily known for their therapeutic potential. The inhalation of brine-infused air present next to the towers, called halotherapy, has been proposed as an alternative therapy for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, allergies, and other respiratory diseases4,5. Despite the long-standing tradition, evidence supporting the effectiveness of halotherapy is scarce4,5.

Outside of their medical use, salt graduation towers create unique, artificial saline environments. The constant influx of salt drives microbial growth and adaptation, leading to development of diverse bacterial, archaeal and fungal communities6,7. As such, those structures constitute an excellent model for research regarding halophiles, particularly their diversity, adaptation mechanisms and ecology. While the microbial communities of such environments have been studied the specific contributions and diversity of plasmids within the soil fraction near graduation towers remain less characterized6.

This dataset was generated to facilitate the characterization of soil microbial communities, particularly in terms of plasmid diversity, associated with three salt graduation towers in Poland: Ciechocinek, Busko-Zdrój, and Konstancin-Jeziorna. While metagenomic and taxonomic analyses were performed on nine soil samples to provide a broad overview of microbial diversity and functional potential in each sampling site, we prioritized the most saline sample from each site to maximize insights into plasmid-mediated adaptation mechanisms. Enrichment cultures were established using both solid and liquid media, followed by targeted metaplasmidome isolation and hybrid sequencing. Combined, those methods enabled high-confidence recovery and structural characterization of plasmid sequences. Additionally, complementary metagenomic sequencing provided functional genomic context for plasmid analyses, while physicochemical soil parameters such as electrical conductivity and elemental composition offered environmental context for interpreting plasmid-mediated functions (Fig. 1).

Schematic representation of the study workflow. Soil samples from three Polish salt graduation towers underwent parallel processing: (I) direct DNA extraction for metagenomic and 16S rRNA sequencing; (II) enrichment cultures leading to WGS and hybrid plasmidome sequencing. In summary, this dataset offers unprecedented insights into halophilic microbial communities from artificial saline environments created by salt graduation towers. It serves as a valuable reference for comparative studies with other saline habitats—both natural and artificial—and contributes to advancing our understanding of microbial adaptation strategies under extreme conditions.

Methods

Sampling site description

Soil samples were collected from three salt graduation tower sites in Poland: Ciechocinek (52°53′16.7172″ N, 18°47′4.866″ E), Busko-Zdrój (50°27′33.12″ N, 20°42′57.2688″ E), and Konstancin-Jeziorna (52°5′10.2948″ N, 21°6′46.44″ E) in March 2024. These sites are referred to as C, B, and K, respectively, throughout the manuscript. The three locations vary in their immediate surroundings and management; however, each site is characterized by distinctly anthropogenic salinity, resulting from the ongoing operation of the graduation towers. At these towers, concentrated brine continuously drips onto the surrounding soil, producing clearly defined hypersaline patches. For this study, sampling areas were established adjacent to zones of active saline drip, ensuring maximal exposure to elevated salinity conditions. Specifically, at sites C and K, samples were collected approximately 20 cm from the base of the tower structure; at site B, the sampling location was approximately 1 m from the tower base. No vegetation was observed within a 0.5 m radius of any sampling point.

Soil sample collection and storage

At each location, three biological replicate samples were collected in a triangular pattern (Fig. 1). These replicates are referred to using the site letter followed by the replicate number: C1, C2, C3 (from Ciechocinek), B1, B2, B3 (from Busko-Zdrój), and K1, K2, K3 (from Konstancin-Jeziorna). From each biological replicate, two technical replicates were collected for chemical analyses and two for microbiological analyses, resulting in a total of 12 soil samples per sampling site. For each replicate, the top 10–15 cm of soil was first removed, and then approximately 100 ml of soil was scraped using a flame-sterilized, stainless-steel spatula and placed into sterile Whirl-Pak® bags (Nasco Sampling LLC, Chicago, IL, USA). Ground temperature was measured at the same depth immediately after soil removal. Following sampling, the collected soil was transported to the laboratory in a portable cooler box. Upon arrival, one set of technical replicates was stored at 4 °C (working samples), while the other constituted a backup and was frozen at −20 °C. DNA extraction was performed within 24 h of initial sample collection.

Physicochemical analysis

Soil samples were analyzed for elemental composition (total carbon, nitrogen and hydrogen) using CHNS Flash EA 1112 Series apparatus (Thermo Finnigan, Waltham, MA, USA). Concentrations of Na, K, Ca, and Fe were determined by atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) using Solaar M atomic absorption spectrometer (TJA Solution, Northampton, UK). All analyses were conducted at the Laboratory of Environmental Instrumental Analyses (University of Warsaw, Warsaw, Poland). Electrical conductivity (EC) values were measured following the standard operating procedure for soil electrical conductivity, soil/water, 1:5 (FAO, 2021). Briefly, soil samples were air-dried, ground, and sieved to ≤2 mm particle size. The prepared soil samples were then mixed with distilled water in a 1:5 ratio (w/v), shaken for 60 minutes at 180 rpm, and left to settle for 30 minutes. EC measurements were taken by immersing a conductivity probe in the resulting supernatant. Obtained EC1:5 values were then converted to ECR values using a generalized model proposed by Smagin et al. and samples were classified based on Richards scale8,9. Soil organic matter (SOM) was determined by loss on ignition (LOI). For this, soil subsamples were weighed in triplicate, dried overnight at 100 °C, and subsequently combusted in a muffle furnace at 550 °C for 24 hours; SOM content was calculated as the percentage of mass lost upon ignition. Particle size distribution was analyzed by mechanical dry sieving of samples that were air-dried at room temperature for 72 hours. The samples were shaken for 60 minutes on a sieve shaker (Retsch, Haan, Germany) at an amplitude of 1.2 mm/g, and the resulting fractions were weighed to classify soil texture according to the USDA guidelines. For soil pH, a 1:5 (w/v) soil-to-deionized water suspension was prepared, stirred for 60 minutes, and allowed to equilibrate for an additional 60 minutes before the pH was measured using a calibrated electrode.

Metagenomic DNA isolation and sequencing

Total metagenomic DNA was isolated from each biological sample using the Qiagen PowerSoil DNA isolation kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). To reduce isolation bias, DNA extractions were performed separately from each technical replicate and then pooled within each biological sample. All isolations were performed according to the manufacturer’s standard protocol, using 250 mg of soil per extraction. Prior to lysis, the soil was homogenized using the FastPrep-24 instrument (MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA, USA; 40 s at 6.0 m/s). Following the isolation, concentration of obtained DNA was measured using the Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) and its purity was determined using NanoReady spectrophotometer (Life Real, Hangzhou, China), both pre- and post-pooling. DNA integrity was evaluated by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Following the isolation, the metagenomic DNA was sequenced using Illumina MiSeq instrument in 2 × 150 bp mode at the Genomics Core Facility (Centre of New Technologies, University of Warsaw, Warsaw, Poland).

Full-length 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing and community profiling

Aliquots of the total metagenomic DNA isolated from soil samples (as described in previous section) were used as template for full-length 16S rRNA gene amplification and sequencing. The sequencing was performed using PacBio Revio platform, with Kinnex 16S rRNA library preparation kit (Novogene, Cambridge, UK) and using the universal bacterial primers 27 F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492 R (5′-GNTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). Obtained full-length 16S rRNA amplicon data was processed by Novogene using in-house scripts based on the QIIME2 package10. Relative abundance of bacterial classes within samples based on observed amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) can be seen in Fig. 2.

Relative abundance of bacterial classes based on 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing (ASVs) across the nine soil samples.

Preparation of enrichment cultures

To target potentially low-abundance microbes and facilitate plasmid recovery, enrichment cultures were established using Marine Broth medium (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) in both liquid and solid forms11. From each location (C, B, K), the single biological replicate sample exhibiting the highest electrical conductivity was selected as inoculation source: C1, B1 and K3. For liquid cultures, 12 g of soil from each sampling site was added to 120 mL of prepared medium and incubated at 16 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 72 hours. For solid medium cultures, flasks with 90 ml of distilled water, 90 mg of sodium pyrophosphate and 5 g of glass beads were sterilized, and then inoculated with 10 g of soil, and finally shaken at 16 °C, 160 rpm for 24 hours. The resulting bacterial suspensions were plated on Marine Broth agar at dilutions of 10−1, 10−4, 10−5, and 10−6. Plates were incubated at 16 °C for one week.

Throughout this manuscript, these enrichment cultures are referred to using the original sample designation followed by a suffix indicating the culture type: ‘H’ for liquid and ‘Z’ for solid medium culture: C1H, C1Z, B1H, B1Z, K3H, and K3Z.

DNA Isolation from enrichment cultures

Following incubation, DNA was isolated from both liquid and solid enrichment cultures. For liquid cultures, 3 × 1.5 mL samples were collected from each culture, centrifuged (5 min, 6000 × g), and the resulting pellets were frozen at −20 °C for later metagenomic DNA isolation. Additionally, 50 mL samples were collected, centrifuged (15 min, 6000 × g), and the pellets were frozen at −20 °C for metaplasmidome isolation. For Busko-Zdrój samples, centrifugation was performed twice due to low pellet yield.

For solid cultures, bacterial colonies were washed from 10−1 dilution plates using 2 mL of 0,9% saline solution. Next, the bacterial suspensions were centrifuged, and the pellets were frozen at −20 °C for later DNA isolation. Obtained bacterial pellets were used for DNA isolation. Metaplasmidome isolation was performed using the Qiagen Plasmid Midi kit (Qiagen). Metagenomic DNA was isolated using the EURx Bacterial & Yeast Genomic DNA Purification Kit (EURx, Gdańsk, Poland). For metagenome isolation, the DNA obtained from each of the pellets was pooled within each sample. DNA quality and quantity were assessed using NanoDrop and Qubit instruments, respectively, as mentioned before.

Sequencing of genomes and plasmidomes from enrichment cultures

Following the isolation, total genomic and plasmidomic DNA obtained from enrichment cultures was sequenced using Illumina MiSeq instrument in 2 × 150 bp mode at the Genomics Core Facility (Centre of New Technologies, University of Warsaw). Additionally, the plasmidomic DNA was also sequenced using the Oxford Nanopore platform using v14 chemistry, native barcoding ligation kit and R10.4.1 flowcells (genXone, Złotniki, Poland).

Bioinformatic pipeline

To prepare the sequencing data for analysis, quality-based filtering and trimming were performed on both short- and long-read datasets. Short-read Illumina data was first assessed for quality using FastQC (v0.12.0), then processed with fastp (v0.23.4, using --detect_adapter_for_pe and default parameters otherwise), and re-evaluated with FastQC to detect any remaining abnormalities12,13. Long-read Nanopore data was handled analogously using nanoQC (v0.9.4) and filtlong (v0.2.1, with --keep_percent 90 and --min_length 1000)14,15.

The quality-controlled short-read Illumina data from the nine soil samples and six enrichment culture samples was then used for metagenome assembly. Assemblies were generated separately for each sample with the MEGAHIT assembler (v1.2.9), utilizing 40 CPU threads, 120 GB of memory, and the --meta-large preset. Contigs shorter than 1,000 bp were removed from the final assembly files16. Coding sequences (CDS) were predicted on the filtered contigs (≥1,000 bp) using Pyrodigal (v3.6.3), a Python binding to Prodigal (v2.6.3)17,18.

Functional annotation of the predicted protein sequences was conducted with eggNOG-mapper against the eggNOG database, applying default parameters19,20. PFAM domain identifiers were extracted from the eggNOG-mapper output for downstream analyses.

Plasmid contigs were recovered using SPAdes (v4.1.0)21. Each metaplasmidome and enrichment plasmidome sample was initially assembled with metaSPAdes, incorporating long-read data via the–nanopore option, using 40 CPU threads, and default parameters. The resulting assembly graphs (assembly_graph_with_scaffolds.gfa) were further processed with metaplasmidSPAdes (v4.1.0) using the --assembly-graph flag and the same input libraries as the initial run22. Assembly parameters were set to -k 55 with the –only-assembler option enabled. Contigs shorter than 1,000 bp were removed from the final dataset.

Following assembly and recovery, plasmid contigs were processed and annotated with the following tools and databases (default parameters unless noted): pyrodigal (v3.6.3), GeNomad (v1.11.0), MOBsuite (database version 2023_02_22), DIAMOND (v2.1.11.165), MOBFinder (v1.0), Rfam (database version 14.10), cmscan with–cut_ga (v1.1.5), and eggNOG-mapper (v2.1.12)17,19,23–28. Results were parsed and merged using Python 3 with the pandas library.

Finally, to evaluate the quality, completeness, and read support of all assemblies, quality-filtered sequencing reads from each sample were mapped back to their respective assemblies. For short-read Illumina mapping was conducted for both metagenome and putative plasmid assemblies using Bowtie2 (v2.5.4) with the --very-sensitive preset29. Long-read mapping was performed exclusively for putative plasmid assemblies using Minimap2 (v2.28) with the -ax map-ont preset30. The resulting SAM alignments from Bowtie2 and Minimap2 were converted to BAM format, sorted by coordinate, and indexed using samtools (v1.9) view, sort, and index commands respectively31. Coverage metrics, including the percentage of mapped reads, mean coverage depth per contig, and breadth of coverage per contig, were calculated using CoverM (v0.7.0) in contig mode with the -m mean covered_fraction flags32.

The complete command-line workflow, containing all parameters, is provided in a series of bash scripts available at the University of Warsaw Research Data Repository33.

Technical Validation

Multiple quality control measures were implemented during the DNA isolation process. Each soil sample was processed in duplicate and subsequently pooled to minimize potential isolation biases and ensure representative genetic material recovery. The quality of extracted DNA was systematically assessed using multiple complementary methods. Spectrophotometric analysis via NanoReady instrument (Life Real) was employed to evaluate potential contamination, targeting optimal absorbance ratios of A260/A280 ≈ 1.8 and A260/A230 ≈ 2.0, although these requirements were not strictly enforced given the challenging nature of the halophilic soil samples. Precise DNA quantification was performed using Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen) to determine accurate concentrations prior to library preparation. Additionally, gel electrophoresis was conducted as a supplementary quality control measure to assess DNA integrity and fragment size distribution, which was particularly critical for samples designated for long-read sequencing platforms (PacBio and Nanopore).

Physicochemical analyses (CHNS, AAS, EC) were conducted at the Laboratory of Environmental Instrumental Analyses (University of Warsaw). All measurements were performed in triplicate, and mean values are reported in the dataset. Analyses utilized standard operating procedures where possible and routinely calibrated equipment.

Following DNA sequencing, the quality of obtained data was assessed using methods appropriate for the obtained data type and sequencing platform. For 16S rDNA amplicon data, obtained reads were processed according to standard qiime2 protocol, and Shannon index was calculated and used to generate alpha rarefaction curves (Fig. 3)10. The curves for all samples approached a plateau, indicating that the sequencing depth was largely sufficient to capture the majority of the ASV diversity present in these samples.

Alpha rarefaction curves for all nine collected soil samples based on Shannon’s diversity index.

Next, metagenome sequencing quality was assessed using FastQC and the results were combined and visualised using MultiQC. A summary of the quality check results can be found in Fig. 4.

Combined results of metagenome sequencing quality control. Violin plots summarizing five key fastp quality control metrics for all metagenomic sequencing datasets: percentage of duplicate reads (% duplication), number of reads after filtering (Reads After Filtering), GC content (GC%), percentage of reads passing filter (% PF), and percentage of adapter sequences (% adapter).

Overall, all metrics present in the FastQC reports lie within expected norms, with no sample containing any significant contaminations or overrepresentations. The QC checks confirmed high per-base quality scores across reads and low levels of adapter contamination, indicating the raw sequencing data was suitable for downstream processing and assembly. Similarly, long-read (Nanopore) data quality was assessed using nanoQC and reads were filtered using filtlong as described in Methods, section 2.8. The resulting quality metrics (e.g., read length distribution, quality score profiles after filtering) confirmed the suitability of the filtered long-read data for use in hybrid assembly.

The quality of (meta)genomes assemblies generated using MEGAHIT was evaluated using standard metrics. Assembly statistics, including N50, L50, total assembly length, and number of contigs > = 1,000 bp, were calculated for each of the 15 assemblies (9 soil, 6 enrichment) using seqkit2 (v2.10.0)35. Assembly accuracy and completeness were further assessed by mapping reads back to the contigs using Bowtie2. High mapping rates were observed for the enrichment culture assemblies (92.6%–97.4%), indicating these assemblies accurately represent the majority of the sequenced reads from those samples. Mapping rates for the raw soil metagenome assemblies were lower (17.8%–55.3%), likely reflecting the higher complexity and potential presence of greater sequence divergence or uncaptured diversity in the original soil environment relative to the enrichment cultures. Across all 15 assemblies, the breadth of coverage (mean_cover_frac >0.993) was consistently high, suggesting that the assembled contigs are generally well-supported by reads across their length. Furthermore, to assess sequencing depth for functional and protein diversity represented in the assemblies, rarefaction curves were generated based on unique protein clusters (clustered at 50% identity via mmseqs2) and unique PFAM functional domain identifiers (annotated via eggNOG-mapper) (Figs. 5, 6). Obtained curves (Fig. 6) based on PFAM identifiers generally approached saturation, suggesting that the sequencing depth adequately captured the functional potential profile of the communities. However, curves based on protein clusters (Fig. 5) showed less saturation, indicating that while the dominant functions were likely recovered, deeper sequencing might be needed to reveal additional lower-abundance protein families, as expected in complex metagenomes.

Finally, the quality of the recovered plasmid contigs was assessed in several ways. Firstly, hybrid assembly was used, combining short (Illumina) and long (Oxford Nanopore) reads using metaSPAdes, as described in Methods section. The goal of this approach was specifically to improve the accuracy and contiguity of recovered plasmid contigs. Obtained putative plasmid contigs (> = 1,000 bp) were further evaluated using complementary bioinformatic tools. geNomad assessed plasmid likelihood based on sequence features and gene content. MOBsuite and MOBFinder were used to identify plasmid-associated features like relaxases and oriTs, predicting conjugal transfer groups. The cmscan command form INFERNAL suite was used with the Rfam database to determine presence of any ncRNAs, particularly those with plasmid-specific function. Contigs consistently identified as plasmid-like or carrying plasmid-specific loads provide higher confidence. A summary of these predictions, alongside basic information regarding each plasmid contig (length, GC content) is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Furthermore, the structural integrity and read support for these plasmid contigs were assessed by mapping both Illumina and Nanopore reads back to the putative plasmid assemblies using the methods described in Methods section. Overall mapping rates were relatively low (1.4%–27.0% for Illumina; 0.85%–4.0% for Nanopore), likely reflecting the presence of non-plasmid DNA in the isolates. However, the breadth of coverage for Illumina reads was very high (mean_cover_frac >0.99), indicating that the assembled contig regions are well-supported across their length. Nanopore read breadth of coverage was more variable (0.065–0.987), suggesting varying support from long reads across samples. Usage Notes

This dataset provides a valuable resource for studying microbial diversity, functional potential, and plasmid-mediated adaptations in artificially created hypersaline environments. It offers a detailed snapshot of microbial communities from soil surrounding three distinct salt graduation towers in Poland. While the limited number of replicates precludes definitive statistical conclusions, the dataset enables exploratory analyses of the potential influence of environmental conditions, such as salinity gradients and elemental composition, on microbial diversity and functionality. The combination of metagenomic and targeted metaplasmidome sequencing facilitates in-depth exploration of plasmid content and its role in microbial adaptation to high salinity, particularly through metal ion transport and resistance mechanisms. Additionally, use of enrichment cultures combined with hybrid sequencing approaches (short- and long-read) provides unique insights into low-abundance or hard-to-capture plasmids that are often missed by standard metagenomic approaches. However, when utilizing data from the enrichment cultures, users should consider that these represent subsets of the community selected under specific laboratory conditions. Overall, this dataset serves as a valuable reference that can complement other datasets for broader comparative studies across saline ecosystems, both natural and anthropogenic, and supports future investigations into the ecological and biotechnological potential of halophilic microbes and their plasmids.

No custom scripts, tools or software was used during the generation of data described in this manuscript. Usage of all tools, along with any non-default parameters has been detailed in the Methods section, along with version information for each tool and/or database.
