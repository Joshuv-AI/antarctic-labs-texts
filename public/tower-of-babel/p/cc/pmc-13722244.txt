Knowledge and awareness of benign prostatic hyperplasia among the general population in Makkah, Saudi Arabia: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Ahmed A. Alzahrani, Abdullah O. Krenshi, Yazan A. Shater, Mohammad H. Ghassal, Albadr A. Alghamdi, Muath A. Mardah, Ahmad S. Alahmadi, Mohamed E. Mustafa, Mokhtar M. Shatla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a common age-related condition that can significantly affect quality of life and lead to serious urinary complications. Public awareness is essential for early recognition and timely management.Aim:This study aimed to evaluate the level of knowledge and awareness of BPH among adult men in Makkah, Saudi Arabia.Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted between December 2025 and January 2026 using a previously validated questionnaire via convenience sampling. Adult men aged ≥18 years residing in Makkah were included, while healthcare professionals and health-related students were excluded. Knowledge was assessed across multiple domains, and scores ≥60% were considered good. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.Results:A total of 411 participants were included (median age: 40 years, IQR 23–50). Only 34.3% had previously received information about BPH. The mean knowledge score was 20.48 ± 5.13, and 44.6% demonstrated good knowledge. Notable gaps were identified in awareness of symptoms, risk factors (particularly obesity and diabetes), complications, and diagnostic methods. Prior exposure to information was significantly associated with better knowledge (P = 0.033), especially when obtained from healthcare providers (P = 0.004) or educational campaigns (P = 0.011). Younger age groups were independently associated with higher knowledge levels (OR = 2.69, 95% CI: 1.22–5.90, P = 0.014).Conclusions:Knowledge of BPH among men in Makkah is suboptimal, with important deficiencies in clinically relevant areas. Targeted educational strategies and healthcare-based awareness programs are needed to improve early recognition and reduce preventable complications.

Introduction

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a common, nonmalignant enlargement of the prostate that predominantly affects aging men.[1] Globally, the prevalence nearly doubled from approximately 50.7 million cases in 1990 to over 112.5 million in 2021, reflecting a 122% increase driven by aging populations.[2] In Saudi Arabia, BPH is a leading urological concern; a community-based study in the Eastern Province reported high rates of lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTSs) among men aged 50 years and older, with severity increasing with age.[3]

BPH arises from hyperplasia of stromal and epithelial cells in the transitional zone, which compresses the urethra and produces LUTS such as urinary frequency, nocturia, urgency, hesitancy, weak stream, and incomplete bladder emptying.[45] Although not life-threatening, BPH significantly impairs quality of life and may cause acute or chronic urinary retention, recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs), bladder calculi, hematuria, and renal impairment in severe cases.[5] The etiology of BPH is multifactorial, with agerelated hormonal changes playing a significant role. Declining testosterone relative to estrogen, combined with sustained intraprostatic dihydrotestosterone (DHT), promotes cell proliferation and tissue growth.[6]

Despite growing national and international attention, public awareness of BPH in Saudi Arabia remains suboptimal, particularly regarding causes, symptoms, risk factors, and management options.[3] No comprehensive study has assessed BPH knowledge in Makkah, a city with unique demographic, cultural, and healthcare characteristics. This study, therefore, aimed to evaluate BPH awareness—spanning symptoms, risk factors, complications, diagnostic methods, preventive measures, and treatment options—among the general population in Makkah.

Beyond its descriptive value, this study carries direct implications for both clinical practice and public health. Clinicians in Makkah frequently encounter BPH patients at advanced disease stages, reflecting not only disease severity but also failures in early symptom recognition and health-seeking behavior. The knowledge gaps identified here provide practitioners with an evidence base for targeted patient education. At the population level, these gaps offer a quantitative foundation for designing community screening programs and health literacy campaigns suited to the local sociocultural context. As Saudi Arabia advances its Vision 2030 health transformation—with its emphasis on preventive care and reducing avoidable specialist burden—such data are essential for justifying investment in urological outreach initiatives.

Methods

Study design and participant recruitment

A previously validated questionnaire was used to conduct a cross-sectional study on BPH in the general population living in the Makkah region, Saudi Arabia, from December 2025 to January 2026.

The minimum required sample size was 385 participants, calculated using OpenEpi v3.0, based on a total male population of 1,533,291 (General Authority for Statistics, 2022), assuming an expected frequency of 50%, a design effect of 1, and a 95% confidence level. A total of 466 participants were recruited. After excluding those who did not meet the eligibility criteria, the final sample included 411 participants. Convenience sampling was employed. Men aged 18 and over from Makkah who were fluent in Arabic and had practical communication skills were selected. The exclusion criteria were designed to ensure the validity of the results. Participants who did not complete the survey or provide consent were excluded. Additionally, individuals under 18, those residing outside Makkah, healthcare professionals working in the health sector, and students enrolled in health-related fields were excluded from the study. These criteria were established to focus on the relevant population and to eliminate potential biases that could affect the research outcomes.[3]

Study tool

The questionnaire for this cross-sectional study was carefully developed to assess knowledge and awareness. It was translated from English to Arabic and back to ensure clarity and accuracy. The questionnaire was divided into two main sections: (1) Socio-demographic Information: including basic demographic data like age, education level, employment status, and region of residence. Socioeconomic status was self-reported and assessed using (low/moderate/high). It also assessed personal and family history of BPH and prior information received, aiming to profile respondents and identify potential correlations with BPH prevalence or awareness.[7] (2) BPH awareness, which consists of nine questions, five of which were “choose-all-that-apply” questions covering definition, typical age of onset, risk factors, symptoms, complications, diagnosis, prevention, and treatment.

Each correct response scored one point (maximum 36). A score of ≥22 (≥60%) was classified as good knowledge, while scores of ≤21 were classified as poor knowledge, adapted from previously published criteria.[3]

Data collection

The questionnaire was a self-administered, anonymous electronic questionnaire in Arabic and disseminated via online platforms and social media targeting Makkah residents. Participation was entirely voluntary, and no personally identifiable information was collected to maintain participant confidentiality and privacy. To minimize bias, the questionnaire was anonymized and back-translated for linguistic equivalence, and healthcare professionals were excluded to eliminate professional knowledge bias. No missing data were encountered as the electronic questionnaire required responses to all items before submission.

This sampling method was chosen for its practicality, cost-effectiveness, and efficiency in reaching many respondents within limited resources and time. Researchers presented the study and its objectives to potential participants in Makkah; those who agreed received a questionnaire link to complete.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS v25). Descriptive statistics included frequencies, percentages, mean ± Standard deviation (SD), median, and interquartile range (IQR). Chi-squared tests assessed categorical associations, independent-samples t-tests compared mean knowledge scores, Pearson correlation examined continuous relationships, and binary logistic regression identified independent predictors of BPH knowledge. Sociodemographic variables (age group, education, employment, and living standard) were considered as potential confounders and predictors of BPH knowledge. Prior exposure to BPH information and its source were assessed as effect modifiers as they may influence the relationship between these variables and knowledge level. No formal sensitivity or interaction analyses were prespecified or conducted; subgroup analyses were limited to information source as an exploratory secondary objective. Statistical significance was set at P value < 0.05.

Results

A total of 411 participants were included in the study. The median age of participants was 40 years (IQR: 23–50; range: 18–73). The largest age group was 18–29 years (n = 156, 38%), followed by ≥50 years (n = 114, 27.7%), 40–49 years (n = 99, 24.1%), and 30–39 years (n = 42, 10.2%). Most participants held a bachelor’s degree (n = 276, 67.2%), while 21.9% had high school education or less and 10.9% had postgraduate qualifications. The majority reported a moderate living standard (82.2%), and most were employed (55.6%) [Table 1].

Table 1 also shows that 83.2% of participants reported no history of BPH. A family history of BPH was reported by 40 participants (9.7%), while 29 participants (7.1%) reported a personal history. Only 141 (34.3%) had previously received information about BPH; among these, the most common source was the Internet or social media (25.1%), followed by family or friends (11.7%), healthcare providers (10.9%), educational campaigns (5.1%), and television or radio (4.1%).

The overall knowledge score for BPH had a mean of 20.48 (SD 5.13). Based on the predefined cutoff, 183 participants (44.6%) demonstrated good knowledge of BPH, whereas 228 participants (55.4%) demonstrated poor knowledge [Graph 1].

Regarding BPH definition [Graph 2], 178 participants (43.3%) correctly identified it as a noncancerous prostatic enlargement; 56.7% responded incorrectly or reported not knowing. Regarding the typical age of onset, of all participants, 246 (59.9%) correctly identified men aged ≥40 years.

Graph 3 shows the percentage of participants who correctly identified each risk factor. A significant proportion of respondents (75.7%) recognized aging as a risk factor for BPH. In contrast, 59.9% did not view obesity as a risk factor and 64.7% were unaware of the role of diabetes mellitus (DM) as a contributing factor.

Risk factor awareness

Table 2 assesses participants’ knowledge of BPH symptoms. Feeling of incomplete bladder emptying as a symptom was correctly identified by 52.8% of the participants. On the other hand, 63% did not associate nocturia with BPH. Similarly, 53.3% overlooked increased urinary frequency as a symptom. While 49.4% identified difficulty starting urination as a symptom, 41.1% failed to recognize a weak urine stream as a symptom of BPH.

Graph 4 shows the percentage of participants who correctly identified the possible complications of BPH. Urinary retention was acknowledged by 62.5% and recurrent UTIs by 57.7%, yet 70.8% were unaware of kidney failure and 72% of bladder stone formation as possible sequelae [Graph 4].

Complication awareness

Table 4 presents the results of the Chi-squared analysis between demographic data and level of knowledge, which showed no statistically significant association between knowledge level and age group (P = 0.120), educational level (P = 0.245), occupation (P = 0.538), living standard (P = 0.168), or family history of BPH (P = 0.309). However, participants who had previously received information about BPH were significantly more likely to have higher knowledge levels than those who had not (P = 0.033). Specifically, obtaining information from a physician or other healthcare provider was significantly associated with higher knowledge levels (P = 0.004), as was receiving information through educational lectures or campaigns (P = 0.011). No significant associations were observed between knowledge level and information from the media, family or friends, or television/radio (P > 0.05 for all).

A Pearson correlation was conducted to examine the relationship between score and age. The results indicated a very weak negative correlation, r (411) = -0.013, P = 0.797, suggesting no significant linear relationship between the two variables.

Table 5 presents the results of a binary logistic regression analysis conducted to identify demographic factors associated with knowledge of BPH. In the univariate models, each demographic variable was entered separately. The results identified age as the only significant univariate predictor of good knowledge (OR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.71–0.97, P = 0.021). Education, living standard, and occupation were not significant. Discussion

This cross-sectional study among adult men in Makkah, Saudi Arabia, demonstrated that overall knowledge of BPH was suboptimal, with clear deficiencies across several domains, with only 44.6% achieving good knowledge despite a predominantly young, educated sample. Knowledge gaps were prominent across multiple domains, including BPH definition, risk factors (obesity, diabetes), LUTS recognition, complications (renal failure, bladder stones), and preventive strategies. Most participants had never received BPH information; among those who had, digital and social media predominated over formal healthcare sources. Multivariable analysis identified younger age (18–29 years) as associated with better knowledge, while education, occupation, and living standard were not independent predictors. These findings underscore the inadequacy of current awareness levels and the need for targeted educational interventions.

The current study’s results are in line with earlier studies conducted both domestically in Saudi Arabia and abroad, which also found inadequate public awareness of BPH. According to a cross-sectional study conducted in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia, the majority of participants had poor knowledge and understanding of BPH symptoms, risk factors, and therapeutic choices.[3] This aligns with our findings where 44.6% achieved a good knowledge level, confirming a persistent national deficit in urological health literacy.

Our results are further corroborated by a separate national survey conducted by Alzahrani et al.,[7] which found that while many men acknowledged BPH as an age-related condition, significant gaps persisted in awareness of key clinical elements. However, an important point of divergence appears when examining specific knowledge domains; while Alzahrani et al.[7] reported that 44.5% of men nationwide successfully associated nocturia with BPH, a more profound deficiency was observed in our research, where 63% failed to recognize nocturia and 53.3% overlooked increased urinary frequency. Furthermore, 59.9% and 64.7% of respondents were unaware of obesity and diabetes mellitus as contributing factors, respectively. This specific metabolic risk factor ignorance mirrors national trends identified by recent investigations in the Kingdom where metabolic risk factors remain under-recognized.[78]

Regionally, our results build upon prior data from Elnemr et al.,[9] which established a Western Province baseline and highlighted a similar demand for structured community health education. This pattern extends internationally; similar findings were reported in Bilaspur, India, where participants demonstrated poor understanding of BPH causes, consequences, and treatment.[10] Evidence from Almughais et al.[11] across broader Saudi cohorts further underscores that public comprehension of chronic urological conditions remains moderate, with awareness campaigns unevenly distributed across administrative regions. This study uniquely differentiates itself from prior regional works[39] by providing explicit multivariable regression evidence that younger urban demographics in Makkah are increasingly receptive to health messaging, yet require clinically validated, primary-care-led channels to translate exposure into accurate disease recognition.

Several factors might explain the low level of knowledge shown in this study. First, only a small proportion of participants had previously received information from reliable sources, with the majority relying on digital media and social networks rather than healthcare professionals or organized health campaigns.[379] This reliance on unofficial sources might lead to an erroneous or incomplete understanding of the situation. Second, despite the significance of early identification of symptoms and risk factors, BPH is frequently viewed as a condition affecting older men, which may lessen its perceived personal relevance among younger adults. General understanding may be limited by cultural sensitivities and the private nature of health issues associated with the prostate and urinary function, which may further hinder communication, information sharing, and health-seeking behavior.

Additionally, the lack of organized community screening programs and national awareness campaigns in Saudi Arabia may contribute to persistent knowledge gaps as few significant associations exist between most sociodemographic variables and knowledge levels. As noted in other regional research studies,[379] the lack of substantial correlations between most sociodemographic factors and knowledge levels indicates that these inadequacies are pervasive and not limited to certain subgroups. These findings highlight the need for comprehensive, population-based educational programs that prioritize improving understanding of risk factors, early symptoms, potential complications, and preventive measures to facilitate early diagnosis and management of BPH across diverse age and social groups. These findings highlight the need for structured awareness programs delivered through primary healthcare centers and community-based educational initiatives.

Strengths of this study include its being the first to comprehensively assess BPH knowledge in Makkah, addressing a regional literature gap not covered by prior Saudi studies in the Eastern Province and Taif.[39] The use of a validated, back-translated questionnaire enhances linguistic and cultural rigor. The sample size (n = 411) exceeded the minimum requirement (n = 385), and the analytical approach—incorporating Chi-square, t-tests, Pearson correlation, and multivariable logistic regression—enabled robust assessment of predictors. Excluding healthcare professionals and health students minimized professional-knowledge bias, ensuring findings reflect genuine general-population awareness. Comprehensive coverage of seven knowledge domains (definition, risk factors, symptoms, complications, diagnosis, prevention, treatment) enables a holistic characterization of awareness.

Several limitations merit acknowledgment. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Convenience sampling limits generalizability; the predominantly young, educated sample may underrepresent older or less educated men at higher BPH risk. Self-reported data are susceptible to recall and social desirability biases. The questionnaire assessed knowledge only, without exploring attitudes, healthcare-seeking behaviors, or access barriers—dimensions critical for translating awareness into action. Finally, the low explained variance (Nagelkerke R² =0.053) in the logistic regression model suggests unmeasured factors—such as health literacy, cultural perceptions, and exposure to public health campaigns—substantially influence knowledge levels. Regarding external validity, the findings mainly apply to urban, well-educated men in Makkah. They may cautiously extend to similar major Saudi cities but cannot be assumed for rural populations, older age groups, or other Arab countries without further research.

Based on these findings, structured, evidence-based BPH awareness campaigns should be implemented through primary healthcare centers in Makkah, targeting the identified knowledge gaps. Strategic use of digital platforms—given their dominant reach—in collaboration with healthcare institutions can enhance dissemination of scientifically accurate content. Future research should employ probability-based sampling and longitudinal or interventional designs to assess the effectiveness of educational programs on BPH knowledge and health-seeking behavior.

Conclusion

The study found that awareness of BPH is generally below the desired level, with only 44.6% demonstrating good knowledge. Significant gaps were found despite the moderate mean knowledge score (20.48 ± 5.13 out of 36), particularly regarding symptoms, complications, and specific risk factors. Demographic characteristics such as occupation, living standard, and educational level were not independently associated with knowledge levels. Crucially, greater knowledge was strongly associated with receiving information from healthcare providers or educational campaigns, highlighting the importance of organized professional health education. These findings should be interpreted considering the study’s cross-sectional design, convenience sampling, and self-reported data, which limit causal inference and generalizability. Future studies should target more representative samples, particularly older and underserved populations, while assessing attitudes, healthcare-seeking behaviors, and barriers to care. In light of these results, targeted, evidence-based awareness campaigns are necessary to address knowledge gaps and encourage early detection and prevention of BPH.

Ethical considerations

This study has been approved by the Umm Al-Qura University Institutional Research Board (IRB), Makkah, Saudi Arabia, with approval number (HAPO-02-K-012-2025-12-3032) on the 4th of December 2025. Ethical standards have been strictly adhered to throughout the research process. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013). All survey responses are collected anonymously, ensuring that no identifying or private information is gathered. Participants provided consent after the study purpose was explained and before completing the questionnaire. The confidentiality of all responses is rigorously maintained in accordance with ethical guidelines and no study activities commence until the necessary approvals are received.

Informed consent

All participants were informed of the study purpose, and participation was voluntary; informed consent was obtained within the questionnaire prior to completion.
