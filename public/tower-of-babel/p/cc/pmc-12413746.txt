Prognostic role of the baseline neutrophil‒eosinophil ratio in cancer patients: a meta-analysis and systematic review
Authors: Tian Liu, Xiaochun Gao, Chaoqun Geng, Yu Yao, Guiming Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInflammation impacts the prognosis of numerous types of tumors. Inflammatory indicators such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio, and neutrophil-to-eosinophil ratio (NER) have emerged as potential prognostic markers and are closely correlated with the outcomes of cancer patients. However, the connection between NER and cancer prognosis remains incompletely understood. Therefore, we conducted a meta-analysis to investigate the potential of the inflammatory marker NER as a prognostic indicator in cancer patients.MethodsA thorough search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library, with a cutoff date of August 2024. Relevant data were extracted, and hazard ratios (HRs) and relative risks (RRs), along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were calculated to assess the prognostic impact of the NER on overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and the objective response rate (ORR). Stata version 18 statistical software was utilized for the meta-analysis of the literature that met the inclusion criteria.ResultsSeven cohort studies encompassing a total of 1,336 cancer patients were included in this meta-analysis. These findings indicate that lower NER is associated with improved PFS in cancer patients. Additionally, in cancer patients undergoing immunotherapy, lower NER levels are linked to a better ORR. A lower NER is correlated with improved OS and ORR in patients with metastatic renal cancer who are receiving immunotherapy.ConclusionIn cancer patients, elevated NER is associated with poorer PFS and ORR. Similarly, high NER levels in patients with metastatic renal cell carcinoma undergoing immunotherapy are linked to worse OS and ORR. The inflammatory marker NER, which serves as an efficacious prognostic indicator for cancer patients, offers profound insights into related cancers in the context of immunotherapy. In the future, high-quality prospective studies are warranted to corroborate these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-03981-1.Keywords: Inflammatory marker, Neutrophil‒eosinophil ratio, NER, Cancer, Prognosis

Introduction

The incidence and mortality rates of cancer are rapidly increasing globally, with statistics revealing that there were 19.96 million new cancer cases and 9.74 million cancer-related deaths worldwide in 2022 [1]. The development of cancer is associated with numerous factors, including genetic predispositions and environmental influences [2]. The prognosis of cancer varies widely depending on the specific disease and treatment approach. Given that inflammation has been demonstrated to play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis and progression of cancer, hematological inflammatory parameters, such as neutrophils, platelets, C-reactive protein, and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), can serve as indicators of the systemic inflammatory status in cancer patients [3–5]. It is increasingly acknowledged that inflammation and immune cells play significant roles in the initiation and development of tumors [6]. As a result, inflammatory biomarkers have garnered increasing attention in recent years as potential predictive and prognostic indicators in cancer.

Neutrophils and eosinophils are crucial markers of inflammation in hematology. Neutrophils play a pivotal role in inflammation, functioning as effectors of both innate immunity and the adaptive immune response [3]. Eosinophils have the ability to infiltrate the tumor microenvironment and may bolster antitumor responses through degranulation, which exerts direct cytotoxic effects on neoplastic cells [7]. Recent clinical investigations have additionally indicated that baseline eosinophil counts serve as predictors of treatment response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) across various cancer types [8]. In recent years, the neutrophil-to-eosinophil ratio (NER), a novel inflammatory indicator, has garnered significant attention from numerous researchers. The baseline and posttreatment NERs have been correlated with prognosis in numerous cancer types, particularly among patients undergoing treatment with ICIs [3, 9, 10].

The advent of ICIs has ushered in a new era in cancer treatment. Immune checkpoints, which are a series of molecules expressed on immune cells that are capable of modulating the level of immune activation, function as gatekeepers during the body’s immune response to prevent excessive immune system activation [11]. ICIs augment the antitumor immune response of patients by blocking immune checkpoints that suppress the body’s immune function [12]. ICIs represent the primary modality of tumor immunotherapy owing to their favorable biological activity, stable response, potent therapeutic efficacy in treating metastatic and chemotherapy-resistant malignancies, and ability to improve the clinical prognosis of patients with a diverse array of cancers [13, 14]. For example, immunotherapy based on ICIs can substantially increase overall survival (OS) and objective response rates (ORRs) in patients diagnosed with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [15]. Consequently, ICIs have emerged as a cornerstone treatment for a multitude of advanced cancers.

Considering the significance of the baseline NER in predicting cancer prognosis, particularly in patients receiving immunotherapy, we conducted a meta-analysis and systematic review to comprehensively assess the associations between the baseline NER and clinical outcomes among cancer patients.

Materials and methods

Search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines [16]. This research protocol has been registered with the registration number PROSPERO 2025 CRD420251107925(Available from https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251107925).A comprehensive literature search was performed across the PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library databases. Equivalent search terms, including “neoplasms,” “cancer,” “tumor,” “neutrophil-to-eosinophil ratio,” and “NER,” were utilized for each of the four databases. The specific search details are outlined in Supplementary Material 1. Additionally, the references cited within the retrieved literature were scrutinized to identify any potential studies that might be eligible for inclusion in the analysis. The search was conducted until August 2024. Furthermore, only studies published in English were considered. In terms of study design, only case‒control studies and cohort studies were included in the analysis.

The chosen research articles were imported into Endnote X9 software, which facilitated the removal of duplicate studies and subsequent screening processes.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criterion for this study included patients with physician-diagnosed cancer. Additionally, pretreatment laboratory tests and comprehensive follow-up data, including measures pertinent to prognosis, such as OS, progression-free survival (PFS), or ORR, were available. Cohort studies were included in our analysis if they reported hazard ratios (HRs) for OS and PFS and relative risk (RR) for ORR following treatment and if these risk estimates were adjusted for potential confounding factors.

The exclusion criteria for this study included animal experiments; studies where raw data were not available; and other types of articles, such as conference abstracts, short communications, commentaries, editorials, brief reports, position papers, and hypothesis-generating statements. These were excluded because of their lack of scientific value.

Literature data extraction and quality evaluation

The titles and abstracts of the studies were independently screened by two researchers, followed by a full-text evaluation. In cases of disagreement regarding the inclusion of an article, the matter was resolved through discussion with a third researcher. All relevant data were extracted by a dedicated investigator, encompassing the assessment of study characteristics, risk of bias, and outcome measures. The extracted data included the following: first author, year of publication, country of origin, type of cancer studied, outcomes (HR or RR, 95% confidence intervals (CI)), and the number of samples reported in the article. For studies where the HR could not be directly obtained, we employed the Intercloud meta-analysis website (http://www.biocloudservice.com/wordpress/meta/HR/index.html) to facilitate the calculation and extraction of the HR. The cohort studies incorporated in this research demonstrate fundamental aspects of the study design, including the provision of eligibility criteria and clear definitions of outcomes such as OS, PFS, and ORR. Each study featured a representative number of cases and reported the effect size after adjusting for potential confounding factors. The Newcastle–Ottawa quality assessment scale was utilized to assess the methodological quality of the studies included in this study [17]. The Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale comprises a total of nine points, with studies scoring more than six points being deemed high-quality research.

Statistical methods and data analysis

STATA 18 software (STATA Corporation, College Station, TX, http://www.stata.com)was used for data analysis, specifically for calculating and combining HRs or RRs along with their 95% CIs. Dichotomous variables were analyzed via HRs or RRs, and the results are presented as 95% CIs. We evaluated the degree of heterogeneity among the studies by calculating the I-squared (I²) statistic and the Cochran Q statistic [18]. A Q test with a p value less than 0.1 and an I-squared (I²) value greater than 50% were considered indicative of significant heterogeneity. In cases where I² exceeded 50%, substantial heterogeneity was suggested, suggesting the use of a random effects model for analysis. Conversely, when the conditions for significant heterogeneity were not met, a fixed effects model was utilized. Statistical significance was determined when the p value was less than 0.05. In cases of significant heterogeneity, we conducted subgroup analysis or sensitivity analysis to investigate the potential underlying causes of the heterogeneity. All reported p values were two-sided, and a p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

In total, a total of 5124 articles were obtained from four databases. After screening the titles and abstracts, 27 potential articles were selected for further consideration. Following a thorough review of the full texts, a total of seven studies were included in the analysis. All seven articles were cohort studies, and they compared the prognostic differences between patients in the low baseline NER group and those in the high baseline NER group. These studies encompassed a range of cancers, including renal cell cancer [19–22], hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [23], head and neck cancer [24], and melanoma [25]. OS

Among the seven studies included, four incorporated OS as an outcome metric, exclusively involving patients diagnosed with metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC), and three of them were clear cell carcinomas. There were 544 patients in total, all of whom were receiving immunotherapy. Among patients with mRCC who were treated with immunotherapy, those with a low baseline NER exhibited superior OS compared with those with a high baseline NER (HR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.35–0.60, p < 0.05; Fig. 2). Furthermore, the patient heterogeneity within this group was minimal, prompting the utilization of a fixed-effects model for analysis (I²=25.9%<50%, p = 0.256). The low heterogeneity within this group was further confirmed through sensitivity analysis (Supplemental Fig. 2 A). Owing to the limited number of studies included, the funnel plot indicated a potential risk of bias in this study (Supplemental Fig. 2B).

Forest plot of the relationship between NER and OS in mRCC patients treated with ICIs. (NER: Neutrophils-Eosinophils ratio; OS: overall survival; mRCC: metastatic renal cell cancer; ICIs: Immune checkpoint inhibitors)

PFS

The cohort encompassed five studies, including three focusing on patients with mRCC, one on head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and one on HCC, collectively comprising a total of 1104 patients. Among the cohort, patients with liver cancer underwent surgical treatment, while the remaining four studies involved immunotherapy for the respective patient populations. Within this patient group, individuals with a low baseline NER exhibited superior PFS than those with a high baseline NER did (HR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.50–0.87, p < 0.05; shown in Fig. 3). Due to the high heterogeneity among this patient group, a random effects model was employed for analysis (I²=87.8%>50%, p < 0.001). Sensitivity analysis revealed that the primary source of heterogeneity stemmed from the studies conducted by Zhuang et al. and Shao et al. (Supplemental Fig. 3 A). Additionally, the funnel plot further indicated a significant level of heterogeneity within this patient cohort (Supplemental Fig. 3B).

Forest plot of the relationship between NER and PFS in total patients. (NER: Neutrophils-Eosinophils ratio; PFS: Progression-free survival)

After excluding the study by Zhuang et al. and Shao et al., we conducted a subgroup analysis, which still revealed that patients with a low baseline NER exhibited superior PFS compared with those with a high baseline NER (HR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.43–0.69, p < 0.05; shown in Fig. 4), and the heterogeneity was reduced (I²=0.0%, p = 0.639). Sensitivity analysis further demonstrated a reduction in heterogeneity after excluding the study by Zhuang et al. and Shao et al. (Supplemental Fig. 4 A). The funnel plot also indicated a decrease in publication bias, with all studies falling within the 95% CI (Supplemental Fig. 4B).

Forest plot of the relationship between NER and PFS in subgroup patients. (NER: Neutrophils-Eosinophils ratio; PFS: Progression-free survival)

ORR

A total of six studies were included in this patient cohort, with four focusing on mRCC, one on head and neck cancer, and one on melanoma, collectively encompassing a total of 743 patients. All patients in this cohort underwent immunotherapy. Notably, those with a low baseline NER exhibited a better ORR than those with a high baseline NER did (RR = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.17–1.84, p < 0.05; Fig. 5). Given the low heterogeneity observed within this patient group, a fixed effects model was deemed appropriate for the analysis (I²=33.5%<50%, p = 0.185; Fig. 5). The low heterogeneity among this patient group was further confirmed through sensitivity analysis (Supplemental Fig. 5 A). Additionally, the funnel plot indicated minimal bias, with all studies falling within the 95% CI (Supplemental Fig. 5B).

Forest plot of the relationship between NER and ORR in total patients. (NER: Neutrophils-Eosinophils ratio; ORR: Objective response rate)

Furthermore, we conducted a subgroup analysis specifically for patients with mRCC. A total of 527 patients with mRCC were included in this analysis. Among patients with mRCC, those with a low baseline NER continued to have a better ORR than those with a high baseline NER did (RR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.15–2.06, p < 0.05; Fig. 6). Given the low degree of patient heterogeneity within this group, a fixed effects model was employed for analysis (I²=31.5%<50%, p = 0.223, Fig. 6). Sensitivity analysis further confirmed the low heterogeneity among this patient cohort (Supplemental Fig. 6 A). Additionally, the funnel plot indicated a low risk of bias, with all studies falling within the 95% CI (Supplemental Fig. 6B).

Forest plot of the relationship between NER and ORR in subgroup patients. (NER: Neutrophils-Eosinophils ratio; ORR: Objective response rate)

Discussion

In recent years, the correlation between NER levels and the prognosis of cancer patients has garnered significant attention. In this study, we meticulously screened and included seven studies for a meta-analysis that included various types of tumors. Our objective was to evaluate the potential of the baseline NER as a prognostic indicator. Our findings revealed that high baseline levels of NER were associated with a poorer prognosis in cancer patients. Across all studies included in our meta-analysis, elevated NER levels correlated with adverse outcomes in terms of OS, PFS, and ORR. In the subgroup analysis focusing on mRCC patients treated with ICIs, high NER levels were found to be associated with inferior OS and ORR. These findings may provide valuable insights for mRCC patients in selecting appropriate treatment regimens and developing follow-up strategies tailored to their risk profile. Our research is consistent with the results of Sahin TK et al. [26].Compared with the results of Sahin TK et al., our research conducted subgroup analysis and all showed statistical significance. Moreover, it expanded the research on the ORR indicator and supplemented their study. And each of the included studies had a high Newcastle-Ottawa Scale score, thus the overall quality of the research was high. The results are reasonably reliable.

During the analysis of NER and PFS, we observed a notable decrease in heterogeneity upon excluding the studies by Shao et al. and Zhuang et al. There might be several reasons for this. Firstly, the differences in disease types and treatment methods are significant. Shao et al. are focusing on liver cancer and employing surgical treatment, whereas the other studies are centered on mRCC treated with immunotherapy. Secondly, in the studies by Shao et al. and Zhuang et al., the disparity in the number of participants between the two groups was excessively large. Finally, in Zhuang’s study, the selected NER cutoff point was based on the quartile, whereas in the other three studies, the median was chosen as the better cutoff point.

The role of inflammation in cancer has been increasingly scrutinized to elucidate the mechanisms underlying carcinogenesis, resistance to therapy, and cancer progression/metastasis. This research has been instrumental in advancing our understanding and, consequently, improving the prevention and treatment of cancer [27–29]. Among the various inflammatory indicators, the NLR has garnered the most attention in research, whereas the NER has received relatively less scrutiny. Furthermore, a standardized cutoff value for NER has not been established, leading to variations in the NER cutoff utilized across the included studies. The majority of researchers select the better cutoff on the basis of statistical methods aimed at maximizing sensitivity and specificity. While an optimal cutoff point may enhance the ability to differentiate between patients, it simultaneously introduces the potential for study bias [30]. Moreover, establishing prognostic reference values for cancer patients is challenging owing to the diversity of cancer types and the consistent treatment of NER as a dichotomous variable across all studies. Furthermore, the robustness of the meta-analysis results is compromised by the varying critical values of NER employed.

ICIs have transformed the therapeutic landscape for advanced cancers [31]. ICIs rejuvenate dysfunctional or exhausted cytotoxic T cells, empowering them to exert potent antitumor effector functions. This, in turn, facilitates effective and sustained control over previously refractory cancers [32, 33]. Despite the substantial improvement in prognosis that ICIs can offer to patients with advanced cancer, only 20–30% of patients respond to these therapies [31]. Hence, the selection of suitable patients for ICI treatment is paramount. mRCC is a heterogeneous malignancy characterized by high immune infiltration and a robust response to ICIs [34–36]. In patients with mRCC treated with ICIs in this study, a lower baseline NER was correlated with improved OS (HR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.35–0.60) and ORR (RR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.15–2.06). Among cancer patients undergoing immunotherapy, a lower baseline NER is indicative of a better ORR (RR = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.17–1.84).

The impact of baseline NER level on cancer prognosis has been confirmed in various types of tumors. Tucker et al. reported that a low NER level is closely associated with the prognosis of mRCC patients treated with immunotherapy [21]. The research conducted by Pozorski et al. indicates that a low baseline NER level is associated with the OS and ORR of melanoma patients treated with anti-PD-1 therapy [25]. The low baseline NER level is also associated with a lower recurrence rate in patients with liver cancer who received surgical treatment [23]. Furthermore, the decrease in NER levels after treatment can also serve as an early predictive biomarker for the future response of immunotherapy to mRCC [8]. The NER level is not only related to cancer prognosis, but also to respiratory system diseases. The study have shown that an increase in NER level can lead to an increase in adverse reactions in non-eosinophilic inflammation group of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [37].

Although high baseline NER has been shown to be closely associated with poor prognosis in cancer patients, particularly those undergoing immunotherapy, this meta-analysis has certain limitations. First, the number of included studies and research subjects was limited, and all the studies were retrospective in nature. Second, there may be reporting bias, as studies with negative results may not have been published. Third, the sample sizes of the included studies were small, and the methodological quality of these studies was poor, which could lead to bias. Fourth, the statistical power of Cochran’s Q test was limited when the number of studies was small [18]. Fifth, the number of patients studied and analyzed was insufficient for conducting further subgroup analyses; for instance, only one study explored the association between the NER and PFS in surgically treated HCC patients. Additionally, a few studies have indicated that the level of NER after treatment is associated with patient prognosis [8, 38, 39]. More robust evidence is needed to support these conclusions.

NER is composed of neutrophils and eosinophils. Eosinophil count is influenced by multiple factors, including age, gender, smoking history and comorbidities [40]. In this study, most of the included studies did not take smoking as a research factor, so this might lead to research bias. Moreover, due to the differences in age and gender among the included studies, this might be another cause of bias. Besides, for patients undergoing immunotherapy, different side effects of immunotherapy drugs may also lead to differences in eosinophil counts, which may also cause research bias.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this meta-analysis and systematic review demonstrated that the NER can serve as a predictive tool for pretreatment assessment in cancer patients, particularly in those with mRCC receiving ICI therapy. The inflammatory marker NER, a novel inflammatory indicator for cancer patients, offers valuable insights into cancer-related immunotherapy. In the future, high-quality prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings.
