Temozolomide-Derived AIC Is Incorporated into Purine Synthesis in Glioblastoma
Authors: Mark L. Sowers, Tuvshintugs Baljinnyam, Jason L. Herring, Bruce Chang-Gu, Linda C. Hackfeld, Hui Tang, Sandra Hatch, Pablo Valdes, Kangling Zhang, Lawrence C. Sowers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Glioblastoma (GBM) is a lethal brain tumor with limited therapeutic options. Temozolomide (TMZ), a standard-of-care chemotherapeutic agent, exerts its cytotoxicity by alkylating DNA, which triggers a DNA damage response and depletes ATP and NAD+. However, TMZ also releases the byproduct 4-amino-5-imidazole carboxamide (AIC), which is believed to be a benign metabolite. We considered the possibility that AIC from TMZ could enter the de novo purine synthesis pathway, contributing to AMP and NAD+ synthesis and thus potentially antagonizing the anticancer activity of TMZ. The purpose of this article is to determine if AIC from TMZ can be incorporated into cellular purines. Using mass spectrometry with isotope-labeled TMZ, we demonstrate that the AIC derived from TMZ is incorporated into AMP and NAD+ in glioblastoma cell lines. Further, we performed an analysis of publicly available transcriptomic data from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) databases. Our analyses demonstrate that de novo purine synthesis is upregulated in GBM relative to the normal brain. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that a drug metabolite of TMZ, AIC, can be incorporated into de novo purine synthesis, which is upregulated in GBM.

Introduction

The outcome of patients with high-grade glioma remains poor despite combined therapy with surgical resection, radiation, and chemotherapy with Temozolomide (TMZ). While life expectancy without intervention is less than 3 months, combined therapy increases overall median survival to approximately 15 months. − Most patients relapse within the first two years of therapy, and as yet, there are no proven effective second-line therapies. The standard of care for the treatment of gliomas has not changed substantially in 20 years, resulting in a current debate regarding the need to re-evaluate the therapeutic role of TMZ in glioma therapy. New insights are needed to advance glioma therapy.

TMZ, an imidazotetrazinone, is an atypical monofunctional alkylating agent developed in the 1980s. Originally called methazolastone, TMZ is a prodrug that requires hydrolysis to form a methyldiazonium cation (Figure ), which methylates heteroatoms on purine bases in DNA. , While N 7-methylguanine (N7meG) is the predominant reaction product, O 6-methylguanine (O6meG) is considered to be the most biologically important. The alkylation of DNA by TMZ triggers a DNA damage response that activates poly-ADP polymerase (PARP) and consumes NAD+. The excessive consumption of NAD+ in response to DNA damage can result in the depletion of NAD+ and impaired ATP synthesis resulting in tumor cell death. −

Hydrolysis of Temozolomide forms AIC and its active methyldiazonium alkylating agent.

In solution, TMZ must first undergo hydrolysis to release the methyldiazonium cation (Figure ). Multiple studies have examined the pH-dependence of TMZ hydrolysis, − first to the active metabolite 5-(3-methyltriazen-1-yl)­imidazole-4-carboxamide (MITC), which then yields the methyldiazonium cation and AIC. TMZ is stable in the acidic environment of the stomach but, in plasma, hydrolyzes with a half-life of about 1.8 h. The TMZ byproduct, AIC, is usually described as an inactive metabolite that is excreted. ,,, Due to our interest in DNA repair, we recognized AIC as a potential intermediate in purine synthesis.

Studies in the 1950s, which predated the synthesis of TMZ, showed that AIC could be incorporated into purine biosynthesis − (Figure ). Conzelman et al. showed that radiolabeled AIC was incorporated into the purines of tumor-bearing mice. AIC can be converted to the corresponding ribonucleotide by adenine phosphoribosyltransferase (APRT) or hypoxanthine phosphoribosyl transferase 1 (HPRT).

More recent reviews of purine synthesis do not mention the utilization of AIC, , perhaps because AIC is not a significant endogenous metabolite. If AIC from TMZ could be incorporated into AMP and NAD+ in replicating tumor cells, it could antagonize the chemotherapeutic efficacy of TMZ and, therefore, should be investigated. The experiments reported here were designed to test whether AIC from TMZ is incorporated into AMP and NAD+ in glioblastoma cells. We further investigated the expression levels of genes involved in purine synthesis in glioblastoma to ascertain the plausibility of the biochemical utilization of AIC.

Materials and Methods

Reagents and Culture Media

5-Amino-4-imidazole carboxamide 13C2, 15N (AIC+3) (Santacruz Biotechnology, no. 217134), was obtained commercially. Temozolomide + 1 (TMZ + 1), AIC + 1, and hypoxanthine + 1 (Hx + 1) were synthesized in the laboratory with 13C-enriched formic acid, as described in the Supporting Information. Stock solutions of TMZ + 1 and Hx + 1 were prepared using DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide). The final concentration of DMSO did not exceed 1% of the total culture media volume. AIC stock solutions were prepared using molecular biology grade water. For cell culture, high glucose DMEM (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium), FBS (fetal bovine serum), penicillin/streptomycin, DMEM/F12 media (ATCC, #30-2006), human EGF (epidermal growth factor), human bFGF (basic fibroblast growth factor), and B-27 supplement (Fisher, #17504044) were used.

Cell Culture, Tumorsphere Formation, and Cell Line Validation

Short tandem repeat (STR) profiling of all cell lines was performed routinely to ensure authenticity. U87-MG cells (U87) were purchased (ATCC, HTB-14) and maintained using DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin and used up to passage 15.Patient-derived xenograft GBM cell lines, including GBM10, , GBM39, and GBM85, were obtained from the Mayo Clinic Brain Tumor Patient-Derived Xenograft National Resource in serum-free media. These cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 media supplemented with EGF and bFGF (both at 20 ng/mL), 1x B-27 supplement, and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin and used up to passage 10. All cell culture experiments were performed under a 10% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. For adherent monolayer culture, U87 cells were seeded (4 × 105 cells/cm2) and incubated overnight in the humidified incubator with 10% CO2 at 37 °C to allow for adherence to the culture surface. To form tumorspheres, U87, GBM10, GBM39, and GBM85 cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 media supplemented with EGF, bFGF (both at 20 ng/mL), B-27 (1×), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin for 3 days. A pH of 7.5 was carefully maintained, particularly for TMZ treatment experiments, because TMZ has pH-dependent hydrolysis.

Cell Treatments and Isotope Tracing Experiments

To track the incorporation of AIC, hypoxanthine, and TMZ into AMP and NAD+, cells were treated with AIC+3, Hx + 1, or TMZ + 1 at varying concentrations [0, 10, 20, 50, 75, and 100 mM] for 24 h. Prior to these experiments, cell culture media was pre-equilibrated with or without 1 mM sodium formate. Media equilibration was performed by incubating DMEM in a humidified incubator with 10% CO2 at 37 °C for 2 h. We previously determined that this was the time required to reach a pH of 7.5. To collect adherent monolayer cells, culture media was aspirated, cells were rinsed with 1× PBS (phosphate buffered saline), prior to trypsinization, and cells were collected in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. For collection of tumor spheres, culture media was collected, and semiadherent spheres were rinsed with PBS and collected. All adherent spheres were trypsinized and collected by using DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Finally, all cells were centrifuged at 300g for 5 min. The resulting cell pellets were washed with 1× PBS and frozen at −20 °C.

Brightfield Microscopy

Brightfield images of cultured cells including U87 (both adherent monolayer and tumorspheres), GBM10, GBM39, and GBM85 were captured using an Echo Revolve R4 microscope (Echo, ca#: RVL-020-000-08) with a Universal Plan Fluorite 4× objective (Olympus).

Extraction of AMP and NAD+

The method for extraction of AMP and NAD+ was adapted from a previously described metabolites extraction protocol. Briefly, frozen cell pellets in a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube were immersed in 200 μL of preheated (80 °C) buffered ethanol (75% ethanol, 25% 10 mM HEPES (4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid), pH 7.1) and incubated at 80 °C for 3 min on a block heater. After sonication at 40% duty for 10 cycles and centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant containing extracted metabolites was transferred to a new clean tube. The supernatant (100 μL) was completely dried under vacuum using a SpeedVac and then redissolved in 35 μL of 1% formic acid.

LC–MS/MS Mass Spectrometry

The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (buffer A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (ACN, solvent B). The gradient elution program was as follows: at time 0, 1% solvent B; at 5 min, 2% solvent B; at 7 min, 80% solvent B; at 10 min, 98% solvent B; at 15 min, 98% solvent B; at 15.1 min, 1% solvent B; at 16 min, 1% solvent B; and at 20 min, 1% solvent B. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min, the total run time was 20 min, and the column was maintained at 35 °C. The autosampler was set to 5 °C, and the injection volume was 2.0 μL. Data acquisition was controlled using the Agilent MassHunter workstation.

Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) experiments were conducted on an Agilent 6490 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer coupled to an Agilent 1200 microbore HPLC system. The analysis was performed using an XSelect HSS T3 column (100 Å, 3.5 μm, 2.1 × 150 mm). The following parameters were used for positive-ion electrospray ionization: ion source temperature: 280 °C; nebulizer gas (N2) flow: 13 L/h (42.0 psi); capillary voltage: 3.5 kV. SRM mode was employed for quantitative analysis, with collision energies optimized through a series of test samples. MRM transitions are listed in the Supporting Information.

Synthesis and Characterization of Isotope Standards

Enriched AIC (AIC + 1), hypoxanthine + 1 (Hx + 1), and TMZ (TMZ + 1) were synthesized in the lab using 13C-enriched formic acid (Cambridge Isotopes). Synthesis products and intermediates were characterized by NMR, HPLC-UV, and high-resolution mass spectrometry. TMZ + 1 was purified by HPLC after synthesis. Detailed methods and results of the characterization of intermediates can be found in Supporting Information.

Bioinformatic Analysis of Public Gene Expression Data

Gene expression data of GBM cancer patients (TCGA-GBM) from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA; http://cancergenome.nih.gov/) and noncancer patients from Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) was accessed through the UCSC Xena platform (https://xenabrowser.net, 36). For gene expression comparisons between TCGA and GTEx, log2-transformed gene expression data were obtained as “RSEM expected_count (DESeq2 standardized)” from the UCSC Xena platform TCGA TARGET GTEx study. In total, 1142 GTEx CNS samples, including 105 brain cortex samples, were used for gene expression comparison with 158 TCGA-GBM samples (140 IDHWT primary tumors, 5 normal adjacent tissue, and 13 recurrent tumors). For Kaplan–Meier analysis, 140 patients with IDHWT GBMs were divided into high and low gene expression cohorts by median gene expression values obtained as “Illumina HiSeq” from the UCSC Xena platform. Survival curves were generated and statistical significance was determined by the log-rank test. All statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad PRSIM 10.

Results

We developed a targeted mass spectrometry assay to quantify the isotopic enrichment of AMP and NAD+, two key metabolites derived from the purine biosynthesis pathway (Figure S1, Table S1). By supplementing cell culture media with isotope-enriched metabolites, we tracked how much of the isotope label was incorporated into AMP and NAD+ 24 h after supplementation. This allowed us to determine the relative contributions of labeled precursors to unlabeled intracellular AMP and NAD+.

AIC Incorporation into De Novo Purine Synthesis in U87 Cells

We hypothesized that 4-amino-5-imidazole carboxamide (AIC) could be incorporated into de novo purine biosynthesis. To test this, U87 cells were incubated with AIC + 3, an isotope-labeled form of AIC (13C2 15N), at concentrations of 0, 10, 25, 50, and 100 μM for 24 h (Figure A). Isotopomers of AMP and NAD+ were quantified by using LC–MS/MS. Under both adherent and sphere-forming conditions, we observed a concentration-dependent incorporation of AIC + 3 into AMP and NAD+. At the highest concentration of AIC + 3 tested, 100 mM, we measured that 62% and 51% of the total intracellular AMP and NAD+ were derived from AIC + 3, respectively (Figure C). We then repeated this experiment using U87 tumorsphere cultures grown in serum-free medium (Figure B). Again, we saw a similar incorporation of AIC + 3 into AMP and NAD+ at 56% and 36%, respectively (Figure D).

Validation of AIC Incorporation Using Patient-Derived GBM Cell Lines

To validate these findings in more clinically relevant models, we tested three patient-derived xenograft (PDX) glioblastoma (GBM) cell lines: GBM10, GBM39, and GBM85, obtained from the Mayo Clinic Brain Tumor PDX National Resource. These cell lines were cultured under serum-free conditions, and all three are characterized as IDH1/2 wild-type with TERT promoter mutations (C228T or C250T): GBM10: MGMT unmethylated, EGFR amplification, and mesenchymal subtype; GBM39: MGMT methylated, EGFRvIII altered, and mesenchymal subtype; and GBM85: MGMT methylated, EGFR mosaic amplification, and proneural subtype.

Consistent with our results in U87 cells, incorporation of AIC + 3 into AMP + 3 and NAD + 3 was observed in all three PDX lines in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure ). However, isotope enrichment at 24 h was lower in PDX cell lines compared to that in U87 cells. At the highest concentration of AIC + 3, PDX GBM cell lines had an enrichment ranging from 20 to 26% for labeled AMP and from 12 to 16% for NAD+. This may be attributed to the slower rate of proliferation of these cell lines compared to the more historical U87 cell line. These data confirm that GBM cells can efficiently utilize exogenous AIC for purine biosynthesis.

TMZ-Derived AIC Integration into Purine Pathways

Given the significant incorporation of AIC into purine biosynthesis, we hypothesized that AIC released from TMZ hydrolysis would similarly be incorporated. Therefore, we synthesized isotope-labeled Temozolomide (TMZ + 1) to track the fate of TMZ-derived AIC + 1 (Figure A). As part of the synthetic scheme, we also synthesized isotope-labeled AIC + 1 and Hx + 1. We use these metabolites as controls to validate incorporation into the de novo and salvage pathways, respectively (Figure B). Furthermore, because TMZ hydrolysis is highly sensitive to pH, we carefully monitored our culturing conditions to maintain a pH of 7.5.

See Materials and Methods and Supporting Information for details on synthesis and characterization. (B) Isotope-labeled hypoxanthine, AIC, and TMZ were incubated at various concentrations with U87 cells for 24 h grown under serum-containing adherent conditions. Metabolites were extracted, and isotopic enrichment of AMP and NAD+ was measured by LC–MS/MS, as described in the Materials and Methods section. We confirm our prior data using commercially purchased AIC + 3 as it aligns with our synthesized AIC + 1. Similarly, using isotope-labeled TMZ + 1, incorporation of TMZ-derived AIC was nearly identical to that of AIC + 1. When we also tested if Hx could be salvaged via the purine salvage pathway, U87 cells were even more efficient at using Hx than AIC to generate AMP and NAD+. Error bars represent four independent replicate injections.

Next, we supplemented U87 cells with varying concentrations of TMZ + 1. At 100 mM TMZ + 1, we measured 56% AMP and 45% NAD+ derived from TMZ + 1 (which yielded AIC + 1). In our control experiment with AIC + 1, we saw a nearly identical enrichment of AMP and NAD+ of 56% and 49%, respectively (Figure B). As expected, the incorporation patterns were nearly identical for AIC + 1 and TMZ + 1 into AMP and NAD+ enrichment. This confirms that TMZ hydrolysis efficiently generates an AIC that can then be aberrantly incorporated into de novo purine biosynthesis. Hx + 1 demonstrated significant incorporation into AMP and NAD+. At 50 μM, approximately 70% of AMP and 62% of NAD+ were derived from Hx + 1, with little to no enrichment beyond this concentration.

Gene Expression Analysis of Purine Pathways in GBM Patients

To determine whether de novo and salvage purine pathways are upregulated in GBM IDH-wildtype, we analyzed RNA-sequencing data from the TCGA-GBM and GTEx cohorts using the Xena platform. Normal brain cortex from the GTEx study and adjacent normal tissues from the TCGA-GBM data set were used as controls. Samples with IDH1 or IDH2 mutations were excluded from the analysis.

The expression of all enzymes involved in de novo purine biosynthesis were significantly upregulated in GBM tissues compared to normal brain cortex (Figure ). Inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase 2 (IMPDH2) was upregulated in GBM, while IMPDH1 expression remained unchanged. However, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed an unexpected finding: only IMPDH1 expression was associated with worse overall survival (Figure S2). This suggests that while de novo purine biosynthesis is a common feature of GBM, the contribution of individual enzymes to patient outcomes may vary. More importantly, the lack of survival stratification based on overall pathway activity could reflect the ubiquitous nature of this metabolic reprogramming in GBM, emphasizing its fundamental importance across tumor subtypes.

In the salvage pathway, APRT was significantly upregulated, while HPRT1 showed a lower expression in GBM tissues. Interestingly, HPRT1 expression varied widely across normal CNS regions in the GTEx data set (Figure S3), suggesting potential regional variations in purine salvage pathway activity. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that both de novo and salvage purine synthesis pathways are active in GBM.

Discussion

Temozolomide (TMZ) is currently the standard chemotherapeutic agent for the treatment of glioblastoma. Glioblastoma (GBM) is currently incurable, and additional chemotherapeutic agents are needed. Emerging approaches are combining TMZ with other agents; however, it has been suggested that the pharmacodynamics of TMZ must be more fully understood before combinations can be optimized. ,

TMZ is an alkylating agent that methylates DNA, triggers a DNA damage response, activates PARP, and consumes NAD+. TMZ must first be hydrolyzed to generate the DNA alkylating species, and AIC is a byproduct of the hydrolysis (Figure ). While generally thought to be inert, we considered the possibility that AIC might enter into purine synthesis and support the generation of AMP, ATP, and NAD+ (Figure ). AIC can be converted to the corresponding ribonucleotide by APRT or HPRT1 and then continue in the de novo purine synthesis pathway.

TMZ enriched with 13C in the AIC moiety is not commercially available and, therefore, was synthesized for this study. Briefly (Figure A), 4,5-diamino-6-hydroxypyrimidine was ring-closed with 13C-enriched formic acid, forming enriched hypoxanthine. The pyrimidine ring of hypoxanthine was opened with ammonium hydroxide, yielding enriched AIC. AIC was then reacted with N-methyl carbamoylimidazole and the product ring closed using sodium nitrite, yielding enriched TMZ + 1. Validation and characterization of these analogs are given in Supporting Information. Using these enriched analogs, we demonstrate here that AIC is incorporated into cellular purines in the U87 cell line and in patient-derived glioblastoma cell lines (Figures , , and ), confirming expectations based upon work published 70 years ago. −

TMZ is an oral agent. It is stable in the acidic environment of the stomach but hydrolyzes at the neutral pH of the blood. TMZ is given daily with radiation followed by several daily cycles of adjuvant TMZ. Only a small fraction of TMZ alkylates DNA; however, eventually all of the TMZ is hydrolyzed, generating AIC. The AIC generated could promote purine synthesis; however, it is as yet unknown if the AIC generated antagonizes the chemotherapeutic efficacy of TMZ. In a study that predates the synthesis of TMZ, Connors et al. demonstrated in an animal model that exogenous AIC, as well as purine nucleosides, could reverse the toxicity of an alkylating agent toward subcutaneously implanted tumors, suggesting that AIC-mediated antagonism could occur.

GBM is metabolically reprogrammed to meet a high demand for nucleotides, particularly purines, which are essential for DNA replication and energy metabolism. The gene expression data reported here demonstrate that both de novo and salvage purine biosynthesis pathways are overexpressed in GBM, relative to normal brain tissue (Figure ). These findings align with prior studies that identified elevated de novo purine synthesis in GBM. − De novo purine synthesis can drive tumorigenesis as well as therapy resistance.

The purine salvage pathway reduces the metabolic burden for purine biosynthesis by recycling free bases such as adenine, hypoxanthine, and guanine from the tumor microenvironment. AIC would enter the de novo purine synthesis pathway and should be added to this list of salvageable metabolites that would be found in the tumor microenvironment of TMZ-treated tumors. Purines salvaged from dead and dying cells may then be used to augment DNA replication and repair as well as replenish NAD+, , which can facilitate drug resistance. Prior studies have shown that purine nucleosides and free bases also can protect cells from radiation-induced damage by supporting DNA repair. −

AIC can be phosphorylated to the ribonucleotide, AICAR. As the ribonucleotide, AICAR can enter the de novo purine pathway and eventually become AMP and NAD+ at micromolar concentrations of TMZ clinically achievable, as shown here. The additional NAD+ generated could replenish NAD+ consumed through DNA alkylation and PARP activation, effectively antagonizing the chemotherapeutic efficacy of TMZ. At higher concentrations, AICAR can also mimic AMP and act as an AMPK activator , and AICAR can alter cell metabolism by mechanisms unrelated to AMPK. , These additional properties of AICR are generally seen with high micromolar or millimolar concentrations of TMZ, AIC, or AICR. ,−

In summary, the results provided here establish that glioblastoma cells have a pathway for salvaging the AIC. We further demonstrate that the AIC derived from TMZ can enter this pathway. As the antitumor activity of both alkylating agents and radiation depend upon diminishing AMP and NAD+, factors that replenish these metabolites would tend to antagonize antitumor activity. While future studies will be required to determine the significance of these pathways in treated patients, they suggest (1) that TMZ analogs that do not yield AIC may be more effective, and (2) AIC salvage may prove to be an effective strategy for rescuing normal cells from alkylating agent and radiation damage.
