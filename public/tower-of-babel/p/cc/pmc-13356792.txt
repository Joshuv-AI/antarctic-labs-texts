Violence prevention in primary care: a qualitative study of barriers and facilitators in general practice
Authors: Stefanie Mache, Monika Bernburg, Annika Würtenberger, David A. Groneberg, Volker Harth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare staff in primary care settings are increasingly exposed to verbal and physical aggression. Although violence prevention measures are available, implementation remains inconsistent. This study aimed to explore facilitators and barriers to the use of violence prevention strategies, with a particular focus on the roles of staff and leadership in general practice.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative study involving semi-structured interviews with thirty physicians and medical assistants working in German general practices. The interview guide addressed perceptions of individual roles, leadership support, organisational structures, and existing preventive measures. We analysed the data using qualitative content analysis following Mayring.ResultsWe found that the use of violence prevention strategies is influenced by interrelated individual, organisational, and leadership factors. Key facilitators included strong leadership engagement, clear communication of expectations, structured staff training, and individual competencies such as resilience and prior experience. Major barriers included high workload, staff shortages, limited financial and time resources, and a lack of formalised procedures. Leadership emerged as a central determinant: visible commitment, active involvement, and resource provision by practice leaders enhanced staff adherence and confidence. In contrast, insufficient leadership prioritisation hindered the systematic use of preventive measures.ConclusionsEffective violence prevention in general practice requires a multi-level approach integrating individual, organisational, and leadership dimensions. Strengthening leadership engagement, improving resource allocation, developing practical guidelines, and embedding training into routine workflows are important for overcoming existing barriers. Future efforts should prioritise the development of a proactive prevention culture, with leadership playing a pivotal role in enabling and supporting staff in managing patient aggression.Clinical trail numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-15086-w.

Introduction

Workplace violence against healthcare workers is a widespread and persistent global challenge that negatively affects staff wellbeing, quality of care, and organisational functioning [1]. The International Labour Organization (ILO) defines workplace violence as “any action, incident or behaviour that departs from reasonable conduct in which a person is assaulted, threatened, harmed or injured in the course of, or as a direct result of, their work” [2]. This definition encompasses physical violence, verbal abuse, threats, and psychological harassment, highlighting the broad spectrum of risks faced by healthcare staff.

While research on workplace violence in healthcare has traditionally focused on hospital settings, particularly emergency departments, growing evidence indicates that violence is also highly prevalent in primary care. However, despite a pooled prevalence of 63.1% for any form of workplace violence among general practitioners reported in a systematic review and meta-analysis (based on study-specific recall periods, predominantly 12-month prevalence) [3], this setting remains comparatively underexplored, with limited systematic data available [4].

General practice staff frequently encounter verbal aggression, threatening behaviour, and occasionally physical incidents. These experiences have been shown to contribute to increased stress and burnout among healthcare professionals and may negatively impact the quality of care provided [3, 5].

The Effective violence prevention requires a comprehensive and systematic approach. International frameworks emphasise the integration of organisational policies, environmental measures, staff training, and individual competencies [2]. A range of preventive strategies, including alarm systems, de-escalation training, and structured protocols, has been developed, with evidence supporting the effectiveness of multimodal interventions [6]. However, their implementation in medical care remains inconsistent and often unsystematic [7, 8].

At the same time, general practice differs from hospital settings in important ways, including smaller team structures, limited resources, and less formalised organisational procedures, which may affect the implementation of preventive measures [9].

Previous research suggests that violence prevention is influenced by multiple interrelated factors. At the individual level, competencies such as communication skills, de-escalation strategies, and resilience support staff in managing aggressive situations [10]. At the organisational level, structural conditions, including workload, staffing, and availability of resources, play a crucial role in enabling or constraining implementation [11, 12]. In addition, the presence of clear guidelines and standardised procedures is essential to ensure consistent and effective preventive practices [12, 13].

Leadership has been identified as a key determinant for the successful implementation of workplace safety initiatives, including violence prevention. Engaged leadership can promote a positive safety climate by prioritising prevention, allocating resources, and supporting staff training and adherence to protocols [14]. In contrast, limited leadership commitment, unclear expectations, or inconsistent communication may hinder the uptake and sustainability of preventive measures [15]. Particularly in general practice, where organisational structures are often less formalised, leadership may play a central role in shaping how prevention strategies are implemented in everyday practice.

Despite growing awareness of workplace violence in primary care, there is still limited evidence on how violence prevention measures are implemented in general practice and which factors facilitate or hinder their adoption. In particular, the interaction between individual, organisational, and leadership-related factors remains insufficiently understood.

Study aim

This study addresses this gap by exploring the implementation of violence prevention strategies in general practice. It focuses on identifying individual, organisational, and leadership-related factors that facilitate or hinder the adoption of preventive measures from the perspectives of both physicians and medical assistants.

Methods

Study design

We employed a qualitative study design to explore the perspectives of general practice staff on violence prevention in outpatient primary care. This approach enabled us to gain an in-depth understanding of participants’ experiences, including perceived facilitators and barriers to the use of preventive strategies, the role of leadership, and the influence of organisational conditions. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [16].

Participants and recruitment

We recruited general practitioners (practice owners and employed physicians) and medical assistants working in German general practices. Eligibility criteria included at least six months of work experience in the current practice and regular direct contact with patients and their attendants. We excluded administrative staff due to differences in responsibilities.

We initially applied a purposive sampling strategy to ensure the inclusion of information-rich cases from diverse practice settings, including single-handed and group practices as well as urban, suburban, and rural locations, in order to capture structural differences influencing violence prevention. Subsequently, we used a snowball sampling approach, whereby we asked initial participants to forward the study invitation to colleagues. We actively sought to recruit participants from practices with varying organisational structures and patient populations to capture a broad range of perspectives.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured interviews conducted via telephone or video conferencing, depending on participant preference. The interview guide was developed based on existing literature on workplace violence prevention and leadership. Interviews were conducted in German. For transparency and accessibility, the interview guide provided in the Supplementary Material (Appendix A) represents an English translation of the original German version.

It covered the following topics:

The guide was reviewed within the research team and piloted with two general practitioners to ensure clarity and relevance; minor revisions were made accordingly. The two practitioners involved in the pilot testing were not included in the final study sample.

Data collection was conducted between June and September 2025 by a trained researcher experienced in qualitative methods. All participants received study information in advance and provided written consent. Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently using an iterative approach. Recruitment continued until thematic saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new relevant themes emerged in the data.

Data analysis

All transcripts were anonymised and analysed using qualitative content analysis according to Mayring [17]. A combined deductive–inductive approach was applied: initial codes were derived from the interview guide, while additional subcodes were developed inductively from the data.

Coding and initial analysis were conducted by the primary researcher using MAXQDA (version 12, VERBI GmbH, Berlin, Germany). To enhance analytical rigour, one transcript was independently coded by two researchers. Coding differences were discussed and resolved through consensus, and the coding framework was subsequently refined where necessary. The agreed coding scheme was then applied by the primary researcher to the remaining transcripts.

Emerging codes and interpretative uncertainties were regularly discussed within the research team to ensure consistency and to validate analytical decisions. This iterative process informed the ongoing refinement of the coding framework and thematic development. The analysis followed iterative steps of paraphrasing, abstraction, and condensation to identify key themes and patterns. Illustrative quotations were translated into English by the research team.

This approach ensured analytical consistency and enhanced the credibility of the findings.

Use of AI software

In the preparation of this manuscript, AI-based language tools (specifically ChatGPT, Elicit, Deepl Translate) were utilized to assist with the literature search and to improve the clarity and accuracy of the English language. Due to limited personnel resources and the absence of access to professional translation services, these tools supported ensuring linguistic correctness and coherence. All intellectual content, analysis, and interpretation remain the sole responsibility of the authors. The use of AI tools did not influence the scientific integrity or originality of the manuscript.

Ethical considerations

The study involved human participants and was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf, Germany (LPEK-0274). Participants provided informed consent prior to participation.

Role of practice leadership in violence prevention

Leadership emerged as a central factor influencing the implementation of violence prevention. Participants described substantial variation in leadership awareness and engagement across practices.

Supportive leadership was characterised by clear communication, active involvement in training, and responsiveness to staff concerns. Such engagement fostered a preventive culture and strengthened staff confidence.

Several staff members reported that their practice leaders actively engaged in violence prevention and implemented suggestions from employees.Our practice owner takes our concerns seriously and often adjusts procedures when we bring up safety issues with difficult patients. (Medical assistant, female, urban group practice)

Our practice owner takes our concerns seriously and often adjusts procedures when we bring up safety issues with difficult patients. (Medical assistant, female, urban group practice)

The perceptions of staff and practice leaders regarding the awareness of practice leadership about violence from patients and relatives varied. Some participants described strong interest and support from practice leaders in violence prevention, with employee safety being the primary focus.

Conversely, other participants reported that practice leadership lacked understanding of the everyday work realities of staff and the emotional and professional burdens caused by patient aggression. Leadership was sometimes perceived to register only severe incidents, take no further action, and consider verbal aggression in particular as a minor issue.

As a result, participants from different practices expressed the impression that, due to a lack of awareness, leadership does not actively engage in violence prevention unless specific measures are formally required or mandated.Sometimes it feels like the practice owner doesn’t really see what we go through. If a patient yells at us, it’s just considered part of the job, and nothing changes. (Medical assistant, female, urban general practice)

Sometimes it feels like the practice owner doesn’t really see what we go through. If a patient yells at us, it’s just considered part of the job, and nothing changes. (Medical assistant, female, urban general practice)

Other staff indicated that practice leadership was unwilling or unable to dedicate time to it. Role of staff in violence prevention

Staff (both GPs and medical assistants) reported varying levels of awareness regarding violence prevention. Factors influencing awareness included frequency of aggressive incidents, personal interest, and previous training in de-escalation or communication strategies. Staff with formal de-escalation training were particularly aware of prevention measures and often acted as role models, promoting good practice among colleagues.Because I’ve been trained in de-escalation, I notice more incidents and speak up about them. Violence shouldn’t be normalized. It’s not part of our job to be abused. (Medical Assistant, female, rural group practice)

Because I’ve been trained in de-escalation, I notice more incidents and speak up about them. Violence shouldn’t be normalized. It’s not part of our job to be abused. (Medical Assistant, female, rural group practice)

Other participants admitted that they sometimes acted unconsciously or minimized incidents, particularly verbal aggression, or aggression from patients with cognitive or health-related impairments. Violence prevention often took a backseat due to workload pressures.You just get used to it (...) verbal insults or aggression become part of daily work, (...) and you move on. It shouldn’t be like that, but the pace of the practice leaves little room to address it properly. (General Practitioner, male, urban group practice)

You just get used to it (...) verbal insults or aggression become part of daily work, (...) and you move on. It shouldn’t be like that, but the pace of the practice leaves little room to address it properly. (General Practitioner, male, urban group practice)

Some practices showed lower awareness among staff due to lack of training or the absence of formal violence prevention programs. Additionally, awareness of different forms of aggression, such as sexualized or discriminatory behavior, was limited. Staff emphasized the need for systematic sensitization and documentation to ensure even minor incidents are taken seriously.Definitions of violence are vague. Where does aggression start (...) and what crosses the line? We need clear guidelines, because staff aren’t always sure. (Medical Assistant, female, rural group practice)

Facilitators and barriers to implementation

Participants described the implementation of violence prevention in general practice as shaped by a complex interplay of facilitating and inhibiting factors across individual, organisational, environmental, and leadership levels. These factors were closely interconnected and often reinforced or weakened each other in practice.

Facilitators

A central facilitating factor was active and visible leadership engagement. Participants emphasised that when practice owners or senior physicians explicitly prioritised violence prevention, communicated clear expectations, and acted as role models, preventive practices were more consistently implemented. Leadership engagement was particularly effective when combined with regular team communication and responsiveness to staff concerns.When the practice owner takes time to discuss safety, (...) we all notice it. It sends a clear signal that managing aggressive patients is important, (...) not optional. (General Practitioner, female, senior physician, urban group practice)Seeing our lead handle a tense situation calmly (...) shows me how it should be done. Leadership by example matters a lot. (Medical Assistant, male, suburban group practice)

When the practice owner takes time to discuss safety, (...) we all notice it. It sends a clear signal that managing aggressive patients is important, (...) not optional. (General Practitioner, female, senior physician, urban group practice)

Seeing our lead handle a tense situation calmly (...) shows me how it should be done. Leadership by example matters a lot. (Medical Assistant, male, suburban group practice)

Safety equipment such as alarm systems, lockable rooms, and spatial arrangements supporting controlled interactions increased staff’s perceived safety and enabled more confident handling of critical situations.Even a silent alarm makes a difference. Barriers

Despite the presence of facilitating factors, participants consistently reported substantial barriers limiting the implementation of violence prevention measures.

High workload and staff shortages

One of the most prominent barriers was high workload combined with staff shortages.

Participants consistently mentioned that staffing levels and high patient volume often made implementing preventive measures challenging. Time pressure often prevented staff from applying de-escalation techniques or adhering to established protocols, particularly during peak hours or when staffing levels were low.Even if you know the steps, (...) if only one of us is on duty, it’s almost impossible to manage a difficult patient safely. (Medical Assistant, Female, urban, solo practice)Sometimes you’re alone with multiple patients and need to prioritize immediate care over prevention. That’s a constant challenge. (General Practitioner, male, urban group practice)

Even if you know the steps, (...) if only one of us is on duty, it’s almost impossible to manage a difficult patient safely. (Medical Assistant, Female, urban, solo practice)

Financial constraints

A lack of financial and personnel resources was identified as a key factor limiting the support provided by practice leadership as well as the implementation of violence prevention measures in general practice.

Participants reported that investments in safety infrastructure, additional staffing, or regular training were often perceived as difficult to realise within tight budgets.We would like to have more training and safety measures, (...) but with our small team and tight budget, there’s simply no capacity to implement anything formal. Discussion

Principal findings and comparison with existing research

This study explored perceptions of violence prevention in general practice from the perspectives of general practitioners (GPs) and medical assistants (MAs). Findings indicate that effective violence prevention in primary care emerges from the interplay of leadership commitment, organizational structures, environmental resources, and individual competencies.

These results are consistent with prior research in healthcare settings, which highlights the importance of organizational support, safety climate, and staff training as central components of workplace violence prevention [6, 15, 18, 19]. Extending this body of research, our findings suggest that these factors operate differently in outpatient primary care, where smaller team structures, less formalized protocols, and closer patient–provider relationships shape both the perception and management of violent incidents.

In contrast to hospital-based settings, where violence prevention is often embedded in institutional policies and standardized procedures, general practices appear to rely more strongly on informal processes and individual competencies, potentially increasing variability in prevention practices.

Leadership as a central facilitator

Leadership engagement was identified as a pivotal facilitator. Practice owners and senior GPs who prioritized safety mechanisms and communicated clear expectations fostered a stronger culture of prevention. This aligns with research in hospital and emergency care settings showing that leadership behavior significantly influences safety climate and staff adherence to preventive practices [20]. Zohar and Luria’s multilevel model of safety climate emphasizes that leadership at the organizational level shapes group norms and safety behaviors at the frontline [21]. Similarly, Huang et al. found that managerial support strongly correlates with staff engagement in safety practices [22], suggesting that visible leadership commitment enhances the implementation of preventive measures.

Nevertheless, our findings revealed that leadership commitment was sometimes reactive rather than proactive, primarily responding to severe incidents while underestimating verbal aggression. This mirrors hospital-based research indicating that verbal and low-level incidents are frequently underreported and insufficiently addressed unless they escalate or meet formal reporting thresholds, thereby limiting opportunities for preventive action [23, 24]. Evidence further suggests that healthcare safety systems often remain incident-driven, with organisational responses typically triggered by serious events rather than minor but recurrent forms of aggression [18, 25, 26].

Environmental and organisational strategies

Participants described environmental measures (e.g., panic buttons, secure rooms) as supportive, but only effective when coupled with organizational procedures. This finding aligns with Gillespie et al. (2013), who emphasized that environmental adaptations must be integrated within organisational systems (e.g., reporting mechanisms, incident analysis) to reduce workplace violence effectively [27]. Arnetz and Arnetz (2001) similarly reported that combined interventions (technical, procedural, and behavioural) yielded greater improvements than isolated efforts [6].

Structured guidelines and regular team-based training were identified as key facilitators for managing workplace aggression. This is consistent with broader evidence indicating that formalised protocols and repeated skills training improve staff consistency in responding to aggressive behaviour and support safer care environments [10]. In particular, de-escalation training has been shown to enhance healthcare workers’ confidence, improve behavioural management, and may reduce the severity of aggressive incidents, thereby reinforcing participants’ reports of improved preparedness following structured training sessions [7, 10, 28].

Individual competence and psychosocial factors

Individual factors such as personality traits, communication skills, resilience, and professional experience were perceived as highly relevant. This corroborates prior research showing that communication competence and emotional regulation play crucial roles in preventing escalation [11]. In particular, experienced staff in our study reported stronger abilities to recognise early warning signs of aggression, which aligns with findings from healthcare violence research indicating that clinical experience and situational pattern recognition are associated with more effective conflict management [29, 30]. Participants also highlighted that emotional strain and fatigue could undermine even well-trained responses. This is consistent with evidence showing that high work demands and burnout negatively affect staff performance, safety behaviours, and the ability to respond effectively to challenging situations [11, 31]. Together, these findings underscore the need for prevention strategies that address both skill acquisition and psychosocial support.

Barriers to implementation

Barriers identified, such as workload, staff shortages, financial constraints, and lack of awareness, are consistent with broader literature on healthcare workplace violence. High workload and limited staffing have been repeatedly reported as inhibitors of prevention efforts in both hospital and outpatient settings [12]. Similarly, lack of awareness and inconsistent use of protocols have been identified as common obstacles to implementing violence prevention programs [11, 32].

Importantly, the interaction between barriers and facilitators noted by participants, where strong leadership could mitigate some organizational constraints, parallels implementation science frameworks that emphasize the importance of contextual enablers in translating evidence into practice [33]. Such frameworks suggest that a multi-pronged approach, addressing leadership, resources, and staff training, is more likely to yield sustainable improvements than interventions focusing on a single strategy [7, 28].

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study lies in its integration of perspectives from both leadership (practice owners and senior physicians) and frontline staff (general practitioners and medical assistants), covering a range of practice settings including single-handed and group practices in urban, suburban, and rural areas. The qualitative design allowed for an in-depth exploration of the complex, interrelated factors influencing violence prevention in general practice, capturing nuances in perceptions, behaviors, and organizational culture that are often overlooked in quantitative surveys.

Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. The study sample was context-specific, focusing solely on general practices in Germany, which may limit the transferability of the findings to other healthcare systems with different organizational structures, regulatory environments, or cultural norms regarding workplace violence. The data are self-reported, which introduces the potential for recall bias and social desirability bias, particularly regarding sensitive topics such as perceived leadership support or personal competencies in de-escalation. The focus on perceptions and experiences rather than objective outcome data, such as the frequency, severity, or resolution of violent incidents, constrains the ability to draw conclusions about the actual effectiveness of specific preventive measures. Finally, the recruitment strategy, which combined purposive and snowball sampling, may have introduced selection bias by over-representing participants with a particular interest in violence prevention.

Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the organizational, environmental, and individual factors that shape violence prevention in general practice, highlighting areas for targeted intervention and further research.

Implications for further research

The findings of this study highlight several avenues for future research on violence prevention in general practice:

Quantitative evaluation of interventions

While our study identified perceived facilitators and barriers, there is a need for quantitative research to measure the effectiveness of specific preventive measures (e.g., panic buttons, de-escalation training, leadership initiatives) on actual incident rates and severity. Longitudinal studies could examine whether the implementation of multilevel strategies reduces the frequency and impact of violent encounters over time.

Role of leadership in primary care

Leadership emerged as a key determinant of a positive safety climate. Future research should explore which leadership behaviors, communication strategies, and organizational policies most effectively foster a culture of prevention in outpatient settings, compared with hospital environments. Comparative studies across different types of primary care practices (single-handed vs. group practices) would be particularly informative.

Individual and team-level factors

Our findings suggest that personality traits, professional experience, and communication competence influence the ability to prevent or de-escalate violence. Future research could examine how individual differences interact with team dynamics to shape outcomes, potentially informing targeted training or support programs.

Integration of implementation science frameworks

The interplay of environmental, organizational, and individual-level factors indicates a need to apply implementation science frameworks in primary care. Research could test strategies for sustaining violence prevention programs and overcoming barriers such as resource limitations, staff turnover, and competing clinical priorities.

Cultural and contextual influences

As the study was conducted exclusively in Germany, cross-national research is needed to better understand how cultural, legal, and organizational contexts influence perceptions of violence, reporting practices, and intervention effectiveness. Such studies would support the development of adaptable, context-sensitive prevention strategies.

Focus on subtle and non-physical violence

Future studies should pay greater attention to verbal, psychological, and discriminatory forms of violence, which were often underestimated by leadership and staff, but have significant impact on staff well-being. Interventions targeting these forms may require specific training and reporting mechanisms.

Economic evaluation

Considering that financial constraints were repeatedly cited as barriers, cost-effectiveness studies of preventive measures in primary care could inform decision-making and justify investment in safety infrastructure and training programs.

Practical implications

The findings suggest that effective violence prevention in general practice requires a combination of organizational and individual-level strategies rather than isolated interventions. In particular, aligning leadership engagement, structured training, and clear organizational procedures appears central to creating a consistent and sustainable approach to managing aggression in primary care settings.

For practices with sufficient resources, comprehensive strategies may include the implementation of standardized protocols, regular de-escalation training, and environmental safety measures such as alarm systems or secure consulting spaces. Embedding these measures into routine workflows and organizational policies can further enhance their effectiveness and long-term sustainability.

However, for practices operating under resource constraints, lower-cost interventions may provide substantial benefit. These include fostering visible leadership commitment, encouraging open team communication about aggressive incidents, and integrating basic de-escalation strategies into everyday practice routines. Strengthening team awareness and informal support structures may represent a feasible first step toward improving safety without requiring major financial investment.

Conclusion

This study provides in-depth insights into how violence prevention is perceived, implemented, and experienced in German general practices. Findings indicate that effective prevention relies on a combination of leadership engagement, structured organizational measures, individual competencies, and supportive environmental resources. Leadership plays a central role in setting priorities, modeling behavior, and facilitating participation of staff in preventive initiatives. At the same time, individual factors such as communication skills, resilience, and practical experience are crucial for managing aggressive situations and maintaining a safe work environment.

Despite existing strategies, barriers such as high workload, staff shortages, limited financial resources, and inconsistent awareness of preventive measures persist, highlighting the need for context-sensitive interventions. The study underscores that a proactive and structured approach to violence prevention, which integrates environmental, organizational, and individual dimensions, is essential to protect healthcare staff and improve workplace safety.

Future efforts should focus on developing tailored training programs, strengthening leadership support, and systematically embedding violence prevention into routine practice workflows. Additionally, research should evaluate the effectiveness of specific interventions and consider objective outcomes, such as the reduction in frequency or severity of violent incidents, to guide evidence-based policy and practice improvements. By addressing these factors, general practices can foster safer environments for both staff and patients and promote a culture in which workplace violence is recognized, reported, and effectively prevented.
