Drug‐Coated Balloon‐Based Strategy for De Novo Coronary Artery Disease Across Complex and High‐Risk Indicated Percutaneous Coronary Intervention Risk Categories: A Multicenter Registry Study
Authors: Dong Oh Kang, Sunwon Kim, Ae‐Young Her, Eun‐Seok Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEvidence regarding the risk‐stratified effectiveness of drug‐coated balloon (DCB)‐based PCI for de novo coronary artery disease (CAD) remains limited, particularly in patients undergoing complex and high‐risk indicated percutaneous coronary intervention (CHIP PCI).AimsTo compare the efficacy and safety of DCB‐based PCI versus DES‐only PCI for de novo CAD across CHIP risk categories.MethodsPatients treated with DCB alone or with a hybrid DCB/drug‐eluting stent (DES) strategy between 2010 and 2023 were retrospectively analyzed (DCB‐based group). The comparator group included patients treated exclusively with second‐generation DES from a Korean multicenter registry. The primary endpoint was the 2‐year incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE): cardiac death, myocardial infarction, target vessel revascularization (TVR), target lesion thrombosis, and major bleeding.ResultsTotal 2022 propensity score‐matched pairs were stratified by CHIP score into low (0–2; 1660 pairs), intermediate (3–4; 304 pairs), and high (≥ 5; 91 pairs) risk groups. DCB‐only treatment accounted for 70.8% of the DCB‐based group, with hybrid approaches more frequent in higher‐risk patients. Two‐year MACE rates increased with CHIP risk in both groups, but remained consistently lower in the DCB‐based group: 5.3%, 6.3%, and 20.0% versus 10.9%, 16.9%, and 31.2% for DES‐only PCI (log‐rank p < 0.001). DCB‐based PCI was associated with lower MACE risk across all CHIP categories (all p < 0.05), with no significant interaction (p‐interaction = 0.819). TVR reduction was significant in the low‐to‐intermediate risk (p‐interaction = 0.056), and major bleeding declined with increasing CHIP risk, most notably in the high‐risk group (p‐interaction = 0.044).ConclusionsDCB‐based PCI was associated with significantly lower 2‐year MACE rates across all CHIP risk categories and may offer a potential alternative to DES‐only strategies in selective patients. Clinical trial registration number REAL‐DCB registry (NCT04619277), PTRG‐DES registry (NCT04734028).

Introduction

Drug‐coated balloon (DCB) therapy is increasingly recognized as a viable alternative to drug‐eluting stent (DES) for the treatment of obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD) [1, 2]. Its efficacy in in‐stent restenosis and small vessel disease has been well established, leading to growing interest in its application to broader and more complex lesion subsets [1, 2, 3, 4]. By minimizing permanent metallic implantation, DCB‐based revascularization–whether used alone or in combination with DES–may reduce stent‐related complications, particularly in patients with complex CAD [5, 6]. This strategy also offers procedural flexibility, allowing operators to individualize treatment according to lesion characteristics and intraprocedural findings [3]. Despite these potential advantages, real‐world evidence regarding the safety, effectiveness, and optimal application of DCB‐based strategies in de novo CAD across diverse clinical risk categories remains limited [7].

Advances in procedural techniques, device platforms, and adjunctive pharmacotherapy have expanded the boundaries of percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) to increasingly complex and high‐risk scenarios [8]. To quantify patient and procedural complexity, several risk models have been proposed. Among these, the Complex and High‐risk Indicated PCI (CHIP) score–developed by the British Cardiovascular Intervention Society (BCIS)–has been validated as a practical and predictive tool for procedural and clinical risk stratification [9]. By integrating comorbidities and anatomical factors, the CHIP score provides a structured framework for patient classification and facilitates meaningful comparisons across PCI strategies. Utilizing the BCIS‐CHIP score, we conducted a stratified analysis to evaluate the efficacy and safety of a DCB‐based PCI strategy compared with a DES‐only approach for de novo coronary lesions. Methods

Study Population and Enrollment

This study included patients from a multi‐center DCB registry (REAL‐DCB registry; NCT04619277), involving four university‐affiliated hospitals in South Korea. Between October 2010 and December 2023, we retrospectively identified patients who underwent successful PCI for de novo CAD using either a DCB‐only approach or a hybrid DCB/DES strategy. Figure illustration 1, 1 shows the flowchart of study enrollment. Eligible patients had clinically significant de novo CAD requiring revascularization. Exclusion criteria were: (1) failed PCI of the target lesion, (2) prior coronary artery bypass surgery (CABG), (3) cardiogenic shock at presentation, or (4) thrombolytic therapy during initial myocardial infarction (MI), (5) missing baseline left ventricular ejection fraction data. The comparator arm included patients treated exclusively with a DES‐only strategy, drawn from the PTRG‐DES registry (NCT04734028) [10]. This nationwide consortium pooled data from 32 Korean academic centers, including 13,160 patients treated with DES, of whom 11,226 received second‐generation devices between January 2013 and August 2018. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of all participating centers. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and procedures followed relevant guidelines and regulations.

Procedural Steps and Patient Management

DCB treatment followed established international and Asia‐Pacific consensus recommendations [1, 2]. All target lesions were initially managed with a provisional DCB‐based strategy, with the final choice between standalone DCB and hybrid DCB/DES therapy determined flexibly based on lesion preparation results [3]. Lesion preparation involved semi‐compliant, non‐compliant or specialty balloons, targeting a balloon‐to‐artery ratio of 0.8–1.0. DCB was applied when residual stenosis was ≤30% and no flow‐limiting dissection was present (Thrombolysis in MI [TIMI] flow grade 3). In cases of residual stenosis > 30%, impaired blood flow (TIMI < 3), or clinically instability, DES implantation was performed. For long or diffuse lesions with uneven responses to preparation, a hybrid strategy was adopted, with focal DES implantation in suboptimal segments and DCB in adequately prepared areas. Even when angiographic criteria were satisfied, final treatment decisions remained at operator discretion, particularly in the cases of persistent chest pain, ST‐segment changes, or progressive dissections. All DCB used were paclitaxel‐coated with an iopromide excipient (SeQuent Please, B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany). Inflation was maintained for at least 30 s, ideally ≥60 s, with balloon coverage extending ≥2 mm beyond the pre‐dilated segment. Final angiograms were obtained ≥5 min after DCB application to confirm vessel patency. Patients received dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), the duration of which was individualized based on patient and lesion characteristics. The DES‐only group received standard‐of‐care PCI and pharmacological therapy according to contemporary guidelines.

Chip Risk Score Categories

The BCIS‐CHIP score was used to assess overall clinical and procedural complexity [9]. This scoring system integrates seven patient‐related and six anatomical/technical factors to estimate the risk of adverse outcomes. Based on the total score, patients were categorized as low (score 0–2), intermediate (score 3–4), or high (score ≥ 5) risk groups.

Clinical Follow‐Up and Study Endpoints

Patients were followed through scheduled outpatient clinic visits or telephone interviews. The primary outcome was the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) within 2 years, defined as a composite of cardiac death, non‐fatal MI, target vessel revascularization (TVR), definite target lesion thrombosis, and major bleeding. Cardiac death was defined as any death not attributable to non‐cardiac causes [11]. MI required cardiac biomarker elevation with typical symptoms or objective evidence of ischemia. TVR included any clinically indicated repeat PCI or CABG of the target vessel. Thrombosis and major bleeding were classified according to the Academic Research Consortium (ARC)−2 criteria, with major bleeding defined as Bleeding ARC types 3–5 [11].

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Between‐group comparisons used Student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Pearson's chi‐square or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Kaplan–Meier methods estimated cumulative event rates, with differences assessed by log‐rank test. To reduce confounding, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using logistic regression incorporating the BCIS‐CHIP score and key clinical/procedural variables (age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, smoking, prior MI, prior PCI, prior cerebrovascular accident, chronic kidney disease, acute MI presentation, left main or left anterior descending [LAD] artery involvement, number of diseased vessels, ejection fraction, and chronic total occlusion [CTO]). A 1:1 nearest‐neighbor matching without replacement was applied with a caliper of 0.2 SD of the logit of the score. Hazard ratios for clinical outcomes were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.1.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline Clinical Characteristics

A total of 2022 propensity score‐matched patient pairs were analyzed and stratified into CHIP‐defined risk categories: low (0–2; 1660 pairs), intermediate (3–4; 304 pairs), and high (≥5; 91 pairs). The distribution of CHIP scores is illustrated in Figure 2, and baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Following PSM, most variables were well balanced across treatment groups, except for peripheral artery disease and clinical presentation. Patients in the DES‐only group more often had peripheral artery disease and presented with stable angina, whereas unstable angina was more frequent in the DCB‐based group. As CHIP risk increased, patients were older, more often female, and had higher comorbidity burden. Shorter DAPT durations (≤ 6 or 6–12 months) were more frequent in the DCB‐based PCI, particularly in low‐to‐intermediate risk patients (both p < 0.001).

Procedural and Lesion Characteristics

Table 2 summarizes procedural characteristics. Among DCB‐treated patients, 70.8% (n = 1431) underwent DCB‐only PCI, and 29.2% (n = 624) received a hybrid strategy. DCB‐only therapy was more common in lower CHIP categories, decreasing with higher risk: 73.1% (1214 of 1660) in low, 56.9% (173 of 340) in intermediate, and 48.4% (44 of 91) in high‐risk groups. This suggests hybrid strategies were increasingly adopted in complex and high‐risk scenarios. Procedural factors included in the PSM (left main or LAD involvement, number of diseased vessels, and CTO) were well balanced between groups within each risk category. In lower‐risk groups, LCX lesions were more frequently treated with DCB, whereas RCA lesions were more common in the DES‐only group. As CHIP risk increased, procedural complexity also rose, reflected by more left main/LAD involvement, multivessel disease, CTO lesions, and longer device lengths.

In the DCB‐based cohort, the median number of DCBs used was 1.0 per patient, with a median balloon diameter 2.5 mm and a length 25.0–30.0 mm. Smaller‐diameter DCBs (≤ 2.5 mm) were used more frequently with higher CHIP scores, while use of larger‐diameter DCBs (≥ 2.75 mm) declined, indicating a shift toward smaller vessel intervention with DCB in more complex cases. In terms of DES use, there were notable differences between treatment groups. The DES‐only group showed a higher overall stent burden, with greater stent number and length across all CHIP strata. In contrast, the DCB‐based group tended to use fewer DESs, but with relatively larger diameters for hybrid therapy. The use of small‐diameter DES (≤ 2.5 mm) remained consistently higher in the DES‐only group, further highlighting the distinct procedural characteristics between the two strategies.

Procedural details regarding intravascular imaging, lesion preparation, and bail‐out stenting were available only for the DCB‐based PCI cohort and are presented in Supporting Information S1: Table 1. Intravascular imaging was used in 49.2% of patients, specialty balloons in 35.4%, non‐compliant balloons in 18.7%, and rotational atherectomy in 0.4%. Bail‐out stenting after DCB treatment was required in 27 patients (1.3%), all of whom were classified as undergoing hybrid PCI.

Clinical Outcomes by Treatment Strategy and CHIP Risk Category

Two‐year clinical outcomes are shown in Table 3. Overall, DCB‐based PCI was associated with significantly lower MACE rates than DES‐only PCI (6.2% vs. 12.6%, p < 0.001). The DCB‐based group had lower rates of cardiac death (0.9% vs. 2.2%, p = 0.002), target lesion thrombosis (0.1% vs. 0.6%, p = 0.003), TVR (2.4% vs. 6.5%, p < 0.001), and major bleeding (1.6% vs. 3.3%, p < 0.001). Stratified analysis demonstrated a stepwise increase in MACE across CHIP scores in both groups. In the DCB‐based cohort, 2‐year MACE rates were 5.3% (low), 6.3% (intermediate), and 20.0% (high) (log‐rank p < 0.001; Figure 3A). Corresponding rates in the DES‐only group were markedly higher at 10.9%, 16.9%, and 31.2% (log‐rank p < 0.001; Figure 3B). Kaplan–Meier curves showed steadily increasing events with CHIP scores in the DES group, whereas increases were attenuated in the DCB‐based, especially at low‐to‐intermediate risk.

Across all CHIP categories, DCB‐based PCI was consistently associated with lower MACE compared with DES‐only PCI: hazard ratio (HR) 0.47 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.35–0.62; p < 0.001) in the low‐risk group, 0.35 (95% CI: 0.20–0.63; p < 0.001) in the intermediate‐risk group, and 0.49 (95% CI: 0.26–0.92; p = 0.026) in the high‐risk group. No interaction was observed between treatment effect and CHIP strata (p‐for‐interaction=0.819), indicating a consistent benefit across all risk levels. TVR reduction with DCB‐based PCI was significant in the low‐risk (HR 0.33, 95% CI: 0.21–0.52; p < 0.001) and intermediate‐risk (HR 0.32, 95% CI: 0.13–0.80; p = 0.015) groups, with borderline interaction (p‐for‐interaction = 0.056). Major bleeding decreased progressively with increasing CHIP risk, with the most pronounced reduction observed in the high‐risk patients (HR 0.08, 95% CI: 0.01–0.65; p = 0.018), supported by a significant interaction (p‐for‐interaction = 0.044).

The DCB‐only and hybrid PCI groups showed comparable 2‐year MACE rates, with both groups demonstrating numerically lower event rates than the DES‐only group in the overall population and across CHIP risk strata.

Discussion

In this multi‐center registry study, a DCB‐based PCI strategy for de novo CAD was associated with significantly lower 2‐year MACE compared with propensity score‐matched DES‐only PCI across all CHIP‐defined risk categories. Although MACE rates increased with higher CHIP scores in both groups, the DCB‐based approach consistently demonstrated lower event rates. TVR reduction was most evident in the low‐to‐intermediate risk patients, whereas major bleeding reduction was particularly pronounced in the high‐risk group. Notably, procedural patterns also differed: DCB‐only therapy predominated in lower‐risk patients, while hybrid PCI combining DCB and DES was more common in higher‐risk cases. These findings support DCB‐based PCI as a feasible and effective alternative to DES‐only PCI, adaptable across diverse patient profiles and levels of procedural complexity.

Potential Advantages of DCB and the Evolving Concept of DCB‐Based PCI

DES substantially reduced restenosis compared with bare‐metal stents but remain associated with a sustained annual risk of stent‐related adverse events of ~2%, with no plateau over time [12]. These risks are particularly heightened in complex lesions, where permanent metallic scaffolds increase procedural difficulty and long‐term complications [13, 14]. Metallic stents may provoke chronic vascular inflammation, delay endothelial healing, and impair vasomotion, mechanisms linked to neoatherosclerosis and late stent failure even with newer‐generation DES [15]. DCB therapy addresses these limitations by delivering antiproliferative agents without leaving a permanent implant. This reduces the risks associated with durable implants, such as stent thrombosis, delayed endothelialization, and the need for extended DAPT [16, 17], while preserving native vessel architecture and physiology [18] and enabling late lumen enlargement—a phenomenon rarely observed with stent‐based interventions [19, 20].

Building on these advantages, the “DCB‐based PCI” concept has gained attention as a strategic approach to reduce reliance on permanent metallic implants [2, 3]. Within this framework, DCB is used as the default therapy following adequate lesion preparation, with a predefined yet flexible option for DES implantation in suboptimal segments [3, 21]. Unlike conventional “bail‐out stenting,” a reactive measure after failed DCB treatment, the DCB‐based approach represents a proactive, lesion‐specific strategy designed to minimize metallic burden. The procedural patterns observed in our study were consistent with this concept, with treatment strategies tailored to clinical risk and lesion complexity. While further technical standardization and consensus guidelines are needed, the DCB‐based PCI approach represents a meaningful advancement in contemporary revascularization, integrating considerations of long‐term safety with personalized procedural decision‐making.

BCIS‐CHIP Risk Score and Stratified Analysis

Most previous studies comparing DCB and stent‐based strategies have focused on specific lesion types or selected populations [22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28], limiting their generalizability to routine practice. Real‐world CAD management frequently involves patients with multiple comorbidities and complex lesions underrepresented in trials. The BCIS‐CHIP score provides a pragmatic framework by integrating both clinical and procedural risk [9]. Its additive, point‐based structure allows easy implementation in daily practice without requiring complex calculations.

Our stratified analysis confirmed a stepwise increase in MACE across CHIP categories in both groups, validating its prognostic value. Importantly, the DCB‐based strategy attenuated the adverse impact of increasing CHIP risk. Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrated flatter event trajectories in the DCB‐based group, particularly at low‐to‐intermediate risk, where outcomes were even better than those in low‐risk DES‐treated patients. This benefit may derive from minimizing permanent implants and reducing antithrombotic exposure, thereby lowering both thrombotic and bleeding risks. The flexibility of the DCB‐based strategy, allowing selective transition to DES or hybrid PCI, further enhances adaptability to complex anatomical and clinical scenarios [27]. While these findings are promising and support broader application of DCB‐based PCI beyond traditionally favorable lesion subsets, further validation through prospective, adequately powered studies in high‐risk populations is needed to confirm these observations and further refine patient selection.

Distinct Application Patterns of DCB‐Based PCI Across Chip Risk Categories

Treatment patterns in our study revealed that DCB‐only PCI was most often applied in lower‐risk patients, while hybrid approaches were favored in higher‐risk groups. Specifically, standalone DCB therapy was successfully used in 73.1% of low‐risk, 56.9% of intermediate‐risk, and 48.4% (44 of 91) of high‐risk patients. In high‐risk patients, DCB was frequently limited to small‐vessel lesions (≤ 2.5 mm) as an adjunct to DES, reflecting a selective strategy to reduce stent burden without compromising procedural safety.

The DCB‐only and hybrid PCI groups showed similar 2‐year MACE rates, with both groups demonstrating numerically lower event rates than the DES‐only group across CHIP risk strata. This finding supports the procedural flexibility of a DCB‐based strategy, allowing selective DES implantation when DCB‐only treatment is unsuitable. However, given the nonrandomized treatment allocation and global three‐group comparisons, these results should be considered exploratory. These findings suggest that, even among experienced DCB operators, DES remains an important component of revascularization in anatomically and clinically complex cases, whereas DCB was selectively used when procedural conditions are favorable. These observations highlight the importance of tailoring therapy based not only on lesion preparation and angiographic appearance but also on patient comorbidities and procedural complexity. Such risk‐adjusted use of DCB aligns with the principles of personalized revascularization and may enhance the real‐world applicability of this approach.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as an observational registry, residual confounding cannot be excluded despite rigorous PSM incorporating a wide range of variables. Unmeasured or unknown factors may still have influenced the outcomes. Second, procedural details such as balloon‐to‐artery ratio, preparation techniques, and thresholds for accepting dissections could have varied by operator discretion. While all procedures were performed according to expert consensus guidelines [1, 2], further procedural standardization is needed. Third, treatment periods differed between the groups (DCB‐based: 2010–2023; DES‐only: 2013–2018), raising the possibility of temporal bias despite restricting the DES group to second‐generation devices. Fourth, detailed intravascular imaging findings and corresponding data on imaging use were unavailable for the DES‐only cohort, limiting direct comparisons of procedural guidance and more refined lesion characterization. Finally, although over 2000 matched pairs were analyzed, the number of patients in higher CHIP categories was relatively small, reflecting historical underrepresentation of CHIP PCI [9, 29], despite its increasing relevance with an expanding high‐risk population. Larger, dedicated studies, particularly randomized trials and subgroup analysis from ongoing or future large‐scale DCB trials, are warranted to validate these findings and guide optimal patient selection across the CHIP spectrum.

Conclusions

A DCB‐based PCI strategy was associated with significantly lower 2‐year MACE compared with DES‐only PCI across all CHIP‐defined risk categories in patients with de novo CAD. These results support DCB‐based PCI as a safe, effective, and adaptable revascularization approach, with particular value in reducing bleeding in high‐risk patients and reducing revascularization in lower‐risk groups. While this approach presents a promising alternative to DES‐only PCI, further validation in larger and more diverse populations is needed, along with clearer procedural guidelines and standardized technical protocols.
