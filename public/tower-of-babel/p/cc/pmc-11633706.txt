One‐year seizure freedom and quality of life in patients with drug‐resistant epilepsy receiving adjunctive vagus nerve stimulation in Japan
Authors: Naoki Tani, Maxine Dibué, Ryan Verner, Stephany Mai Nishikawa, Charles Gordon, Kensuke Kawai, Haruhiko Kishima
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAmount of seizure‐free days is a critical determinant of quality of life (QoL) in patients with drug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE). The fractions of patients experiencing prolonged periods of seizure freedom with adjunctive vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) have yet to be assessed on a large scale.MethodsRetrospective analysis of patients in the Japanese VNS prospective observational registry who experienced at least 1 year of seizure freedom from all seizures, focal seizures, or tonic–clonic seizures (TCS), as well as patient‐reported change in QoL in these groups.ResultsThe study included 362 patients with DRE, 147 were female (40.6%), and the median age at VNS implant was 23.0 years (range: 1.0–73.0). After 36 months of adjunctive VNS, the cumulative proportion of patients experiencing at least 1 year of complete seizure freedom was 11% (38/356) with an average duration of seizure freedom of 19.4 months. INTRODUCTION

Complete seizure freedom is rare in drug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE) 1 and the efficacy of treatment strategies for DRE is typically assessed by quantifying a response rate defined as the proportion of patients experiencing a 50% or greater reduction in seizure frequency.

Recent findings suggest that the number of seizure‐free days may also be a critical determinant of activities of daily living and quality of life (QoL) in patients with DRE. For example, in a study of patients experiencing DRE with the etiology of Dravet Syndrome or Lennox–Gastaut Syndrome, seizure‐free days (SFD) were found to have a greater positive impact on QoL than reduction in seizure frequency, 2 thus raising the question whether SFDs may be a more relevant endpoint in randomized controlled trials investigating epilepsy treatments. Similarly, SFDs per 28 days was shown to be a significant predictor of QoL using the scale EuroQoL 5 Dimensions Youth version (EQ‐5D‐Y) scale and with each additional seizure‐free day (per 28 days), there was a statistically significant increase in utility assessment of patient's QoL by 0.005. 3

Interestingly, these studies assessed the relationship between QoL and cumulative SFDs over a certain time period but did not assess consecutive seizure‐free days. Although there are studies showing superior QoL outcomes in those patients with 12‐month seizure freedom after resective epilepsy surgery (compared with those who are not seizure‐free), 4 to the best of our knowledge there is a lack of literature assessing whether duration of seizure freedom is clinically meaningful and associated with QoL change in patients with DRE. Despite this, recent studies of DRE treatments increasingly report seizure‐free periods as end points, choosing varying definitions of seizure freedom ranging from 3 to 12 months and sometimes only considering freedom from a specific seizure type. 5 , 6 This obviously limits comparability of seizure freedom rates across studies.

An operational definition from the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) states that a patient can be considered seizure‐free following an intervention after a period without seizures has elapsed equal to three times the longest pre‐intervention inter‐seizure interval during the previous year. 7 , 8 This “rule of three” definition is challenging to follow in clinical studies of DRE treatments, as pre‐intervention seizure‐frequency data may not be available for 12 months. Nonetheless, reporting 12‐month seizure freedom as an end point in clinical trials of DRE may be aligned with the ILAE definition as most clinical trials require a minimum seizure frequency (at least one seizure) over the baseline period.

Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS therapy) is an established adjunctive therapy for DRE and has been in clinical use for over 25 years 9 ; however, the proportion of patients with VNS experiencing prolonged periods of seizure freedom in a large‐scale study has yet to be assessed. Here we report on a post hoc analysis of adult and pediatric patients who received adjunctive VNS Therapy for DRE and experienced “seizure‐free periods of 12 or more months.” We grouped the patients as having experienced freedom from all seizures (total seizures), bilateral tonic–clonic seizure type (TCS), or focal seizures. The primary outcomes of this dataset, seizure‐frequency reduction, and responder rate have been previously published. 10

METHODS

The VNS post‐marketing surveillance registry included all patients implanted in Japan and has been previously described. 10 It was designed as a multicenter, open‐label, long‐term, prospective observational study of the clinical efficacy and safety of adult and pediatric patients with DRE receiving adjunctive VNS therapy.

Patients in this registry underwent VNS implantation between July 2010 and December 2012 at 52 sites in Japan. The study was registered in the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN) Clinical Trials Registry in Japan (UMIN ID: UMIN000014728). To receive adjunctive VNS therapy and be included in the registry, patients had to have received a diagnosis of DRE and not be candidates for resective epilepsy surgery. In Japan, VNS is approved as an adjunctive treatment for both focal‐ and generalized‐onset seizures in patients of all ages with drug‐resistant epilepsy. Therefore, in this prospective real‐world study, patients with both focal and generalized epilepsies were included without any age limitation.

Here we report on a retrospective analysis of the proportion of patients in the registry who experienced at least 1 year of seizure freedom from three separate seizure counts: total seizures, generalized TCS only, or focal seizures only.

Effectiveness and safety assessments

The data were recorded using study‐specific Case Report Forms (CRFs). The CRFs collected during the study documented the total number of seizures by type that the subject experienced in between study visits. The data were documented by clinicians based on information provided by patients, guardians, and caregivers. Seizure diaries were not collected; however, information (e.g., medical record, clinic notes, or seizure diaries) used to support seizure‐frequency data was documented at the site to support source data verification. Baseline seizure frequency was calculated from seizure‐frequency information provided at the first study visit and before the start of stimulation. The duration of this survey period to collect baseline seizure‐frequency data varied among patients with a median survey period of 75 days (IQR 59 days). Seizure frequency was reported as the number of seizures occurring daily, weekly, monthly, or yearly. Daily to monthly seizures were recorded as the mean for the previous 3 months. Yearly seizures were recorded for the previous year.

Seizure types were classified in the study according to the 2010 ILAE proposal 11 but for this retrospective analysis, seizures types are referred to according to the 2017 ILAE classification. 12 As differentiating between focal to bilateral TCS and bilateral TCS is often challenging, both have been categorized together as TCS. Therefore, focal to bilateral TCS were not included in the focal seizure analysis. Seizure types included in the focal seizure category were focal‐aware seizures which could be sub‐categorized as motor, sensory, cognitive/emotional, or other and focal seizures with impaired awareness.

The relative change of seizure frequency compared with baseline is presented for the 36‐month follow‐up visit and for the stimulation start visit (i.e., after implantation but before initiation of VNS therapy) to account for any surgical elements that may have contributed to a change in seizure frequency, for example, general anesthesia or an implantation effect. For inclusion in the seizure freedom calculations by seizure type, patients must have reported having that type of seizure in the year prior to study enrollment. Among other end points, the frequency of every seizure type, self‐assessed and/or family‐assessed QOL and stimulation parameters were collected at the start of stimulation, and after 3, 6, 12, 24, and 36 months of follow‐up.

Safety was monitored by assessing the incidence of all adverse events from the date of VNS implantation surgery.

At every study visit, patients, guardians, or caregivers were asked whether the patient's QoL was markedly improved, improved, unchanged, or worsened compared with prior to VNS implantation.

Statistical analyses

Change in seizure frequency from pre‐implantation baseline was calculated for each subject over each seizure collection interval beginning 3 months after implantation of VNS therapy and continuing through completion of 36 months of adjunctive treatment with VNS therapy. Seizure‐frequency reduction was calculated by baseline seizure frequency per day minus post‐implant seizure frequency per day. Mean and median percent seizure reduction, calculated as seizure reduction per day divided by the baseline seizures per day, were provided. Responder rate, defined as the proportion of subjects with greater than or equal to 50% reduction in seizure frequency per day compared with baseline, was provided along with the 95% confidence interval (CI; Clopper‐Pearson method). Subjects included in the analysis had to have had non‐zero baseline seizures per day and at least one post‐baseline visit.

Seizure freedom was defined as a subject completing a follow‐up visit and recording no seizures during the latest interval. For subgroup analysis, seizure freedom was defined as recording no seizures of the specific seizure type that was present in the year prior to VNS therapy in the latest interval. Subjects included in the analysis had non‐zero seizure rate at the pre‐implant visit and had at least 12 months of consecutive post‐implantation follow‐up: seizure‐free at visits at 3, 6, and 12 months; seizure‐free at annual visits at 24 or 36 months; or any combination of these two criteria.

The cumulative seizure freedom rate of subjects experiencing at least one 12‐month period of seizure freedom was reported through 36 months. For subjects with at least 12 months of seizure freedom, the mean total time of seizure freedom was calculated.

For the QoL analysis, number, and percent of subjects in each category (marked improvement, improvement, no change, and worsening) were provided for seizure‐free and not seizure‐free subjects at 36 months, along with a 95% CI (Clopper and Pearson method).

The analyses were performed for the total seizure group, focal seizure‐only group, and TCS only group. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS Version 9.4.

RESULTS

Study population

The demographic and baseline characteristics of the 362 patients in the Japanese VNS Post Market Registry who were included in the efficacy analysis have been previously described. 10

Briefly, the study population included 147 female patients (41%) and 215 male patients (59%), and the age at seizure onset was a mean of 9.1 (±11.6) years and a median of 5 years (range of 0–64 years). All patients had a diagnosis of DRE with a median seizure frequency of 10.3 per week. A total of 225 patients reported focal seizures and 184 patients reported tonic–clonic seizures. The median duration of epilepsy prior to VNS therapy was 13 years (range: 0–61 years); and the patients had taken a median of 5 (range: 1–17) antiseizure medications (ASMs) prior to enrollment. The median age at VNS implantation was 23 years with 147 (41%) patients who were 18 years of age or younger and the age range at implant was 1–73 years. About half of the patients had prior intracranial surgery: 97 patients (27%) had undergone resective epilepsy surgery and 82 patients (23%) had undergone corpus callosotomy.

For the present analysis, 360 patients were included in the efficacy population as two patients who had zero recorded seizures during the baseline period prior to VNS implantation were excluded. Seizure‐frequency data were available from 360 patients at stimulation start, 356 patients at 3 months, 354 patients at 6 months, 351 patients at 12 months, 341 patients at 24 months, and 332 patients at 36 months. The numbers correspond to a retention rate of 92% over 36 months of treatment with adjunctive VNS therapy.

The primary end points of the registry were re‐analyzed to confirm the basis of the present seizure‐freedom analysis of patients based on total seizures, focal seizures, and TCS. The findings have not been previously reported. As summarized in Table 1, there was a significant increase in response rate at 36 months for all three seizure groups.

One‐year seizure freedom

Results of the analyses of cumulative 12‐month seizure freedom and duration of seizure freedom are summarized in Figure 1. Of the 360 patients who underwent implantation and initiated stimulation, 12‐month seizure freedom status could be evaluated in the 356 patients (98%) who had at least 12 consecutive months of follow‐up in the study through the 36‐month visit.

Cumulative 12‐month seizure‐free rate and duration of seizure‐free intervals.

At 36 months, only 7.9% (28/356) of patients were completely seizure‐free since the 24‐month visit and 10.7% (38/356) of patients had experienced at least 12 months of seizure freedom from all seizures at some point within the 36‐month follow‐up period. In these patients, the average seizure‐free interval was 19.4 ± 9.4 months.

The cumulative 12‐month seizure‐freedom rate from focal seizures throughout 36 months was 25.3% with the average seizure‐free interval being 25.1 ± 9.63 months. 12‐month seizure freedom was highest for patients with TCS: at 36 months, 48.9% (90/184) of patients who experienced TCS at baseline were free from TCS while the cumulative proportion of patients having experienced an interval of 12 months or longer of no TCS was 54.9% (101/184). The average duration of seizure freedom from TCS was 29.3 ± 8.19 months and thereby was longer than for total seizures or focal seizures.

When excluding the 25 patients who experienced TCS in the year prior to VNS, but not in the 3‐month baseline period before implantation, the cumulative proportion of patients having experienced an interval of 12 months or longer of no TCS was 49% (78/159).

While seizure‐frequency reduction and 12‐month seizure freedom rates were generally similar in adults and pediatric patients, 12‐month seizure freedom may have been slightly more frequent in younger children (0–12 years of age) compared with adolescents (13–18 years of age): 9.8% vs. 4.9% for total seizures; 32.5% vs. 22.2% for focal seizures, and 54.2% vs. 43.2% for TCS, respectively (Table S1). However, these results must be interpreted with caution as sample sizes were small in these pediatric subgroups.

Quality of life and seizure freedom

Among the 333 patients with QoL data at 36 months of adjunctive VNS therapy, 54.7% (n = 182) of patients reported their QoL to be either markedly improved or improved; 44.1% (n = 147) of patients reported their QoL to be unchanged and 1.2% (n = 4) of patients reported their QoL to have deteriorated (Table 2). 82.1% (23/28) of patients who were completely seizure‐free for at least 12 months at the 36‐month visit experienced either markedly improved or improved QoL compared with 52.0% (159/306) of patients who were not completely seizure‐free (Table 2). Of the 28 patients who experienced at least a year of seizure freedom at the 36‐month visit, 11 patients (40.7%) reported that their quality of life was markedly improved in contrast to only 10.9% of patients who were free from focal seizures and 11.2% who were free from TCS at 36 months. From this QoL perspective, 12‐month seizure freedom from focal and from TCS had a similar impact: 63.0% (29/46) of patients who were free from focal seizures (Table 3) and 61.8% (55/89) of patients who were free from TCS (Table 4) reported either markedly improved or improved QoL at 36 months.

Safety

Safety of all registered patients in this study was monitored by assessing the incidence of all adverse events starting on the day of VNS implantation and have been previously reported in detail. 10 No unanticipated device‐related adverse events occurred, and adverse events were generally similar to those observed in prior VNS trials. 13 , 14 The most frequently reported adverse events were change in voice or hoarseness (n = 58; 15.1%) and coughing (n = 50; 13.0%). VNS explantation occurred in 13/385 patients in the safety population; none of the explants were performed due to lack of efficacy or patient choice; removal was due to infection (n = 6), high lead impedance (n = 6), and the need for MRI (n = 1). Fourteen deaths occurred in patients included in the study of which six deaths were due to a sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP). Using each subject's last recorded visit with data, all 385 subjects accumulated 1042 patient‐years of VNS therapy exposure under study. Therefore, the all‐cause mortality rate in this study was 13.4 per 1000 patient‐years, in agreement with the all‐cause mortality rate of 13.3 per 1000 patient‐years (95% CI, 12.9–13.7) found by Ryvlin et al. 15 in their analysis of 40 443 patients with 277 661 patient‐years of VNS therapy.

DISCUSSION

As the number of SFD may be a critical determinant of activities of daily living and QoL in patients with DRE, here we report on a post hoc analysis of adult and pediatric patients who received adjunctive VNS therapy for DRE and experienced “seizure‐free periods of 12 or more months.” The results of the analysis confirm prior long‐term VNS seizure freedom rates 16 and offer new perspectives by providing 12‐month seizure‐freedom rates for specific seizure types allowing for comparison to adjunctive therapies reporting this increasingly utilized metric. For example, after a mean follow‐up period of 5.4 years, 12.9% of patients treated with responsive neurostimulation (RNS) had experienced 12‐month seizure freedom from disabling focal or generalized tonic–clonic seizures 17 which does not appear to differ greatly from the 10.7% 12‐month total seizure freedom rate at 36‐months reported for VNS in this analysis.

With a responder rate of 78.2% and a median seizure‐frequency reduction of 96.5%, the present analysis found VNS to be especially effective in reducing TCS. TCS is often considered to be the most debilitating seizure type for many patients. An analysis of 379 adults with DRE found that although patients experiencing TCS had fewer seizures per day than those experiencing other seizure types, seizure severity and levels of depression and anxiety were higher in patients with TCS and those with high TCS frequency had 82% lower odds of working. 18 In the present analysis of 12‐month seizure freedom from TCS only, patients had a moderate incremental impact on QoL with more than half of patients who still experienced TCS reporting QoL improvement. Nevertheless, TCS reduction may be relevant for SUDEP and injury risk reduction and reducing the impact on cardiovascular health. 19 , 20

In our retrospective analysis, no validated quantitative metrics of QoL change were available; however, other recent studies have investigated the impact of seizure‐free intervals on QoL. More recently, Strzelczyk et al. evaluated the impact of seizure‐free intervals on QoL by investigating the use of a composite score based on physical, psychosocial, and care requirements of patients with Dravet Syndrome and their association with seizure frequency, days with no seizures, and longest seizure‐free intervals. 21 Patients with higher composite symptom scores reflecting greater disease severity were more likely to experience more frequent TCS. Intervals between TCS were shorter and days without any TCS were fewer in patients with higher disease severity. The authors conclude that although their study clearly showed that extended periods remission from TCS are associated with overall better physical and psychosocial functioning in DS, further research is needed to investigate the importance of seizure freedom independently from seizure counts.

The study was limited by the inherent biases applicable to retrospective analyses. The exact duration of seizure‐free periods could not be calculated as seizure frequency was only evaluated at 12‐month intervals after 12 months of VNS. Detection of 12‐month seizure‐free intervals could be limited by patient recall bias, but this impact is expected to be reduced by the categorical nature of seizure freedom as compared with the precision required to recall seizure counts. Also, the inability to distinguish between bilateral and focal‐onset TCS may limit the interpretation of results. Furthermore, only a very rudimentary QoL assessment was conducted in the present study, which does not allow for quantitative analysis of QoL changes across different QoL domains.

In addition, this was a real‐world open‐label observational study lacking a control arm, which confounds the interpretation of the results. The real‐world design of the study also permitted changes in ASMs across the 3‐year observation period, which introduces bias. However, the median number of ASMs in use per patient remained constant in patients experiencing at least 1‐year of seizure freedom from focal seizures but showed a trend toward reduction (from 4 to 3) from the 12‐month visit to the 36‐month visit for patients experiencing at least 1 year of seizure freedom from all seizures and from TCS (see Table S2). Finally, some patients with DRE experience temporal dynamics with prolonged periods of seizure freedom (especially from TCS) even without adjunctive therapeutic interventions. 7

Clinical relevance

Complete seizure freedom following adjunctive VNS therapy is rare, but durable remission from TCS seizures appears to be achievable in roughly half of patients. The median reduction of TCS at 36 months was 96.5% and in the patients with 12‐month seizure freedom from TCS, the average seizure‐free interval was 29.3 months corresponding to 81% of the follow‐up period. 12‐month seizure freedom from TCS only had a moderate incremental impact on patients QoL but may be clinically relevant, for example, due to SUDEP risk. Considering the high effectiveness of VNS on TCS found in this analysis, patients experiencing impairment due to TCS may be promising candidates for VNS.
