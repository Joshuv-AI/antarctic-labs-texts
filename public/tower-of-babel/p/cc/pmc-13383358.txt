Risk factors for diuretic resistance in hospitalized patients with acute decompensated heart failure: a retrospective study
Authors: Yue Cui, Tao Liu, Yingyue Hu, Lai Wei, Yimin Zhong, Yixuan Zhao, Caiying Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the independent risk factors for diuretic resistance (DR) and its clinical impact in hospitalized patients with acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF).MethodsThis single-center retrospective study consecutively enrolled 387 patients with ADHF admitted between January 2022 and June 2025. Based on an operational definition (intravenous furosemide ≥ 80 mg/day with failure to achieve an average daily body weight reduction ≥ 1 kg or a net negative fluid balance ≥ 1,000 mL within 72 h), patients were classified into a DR group (n = 132) and a non-DR group (n = 255). Demographic characteristics, comorbidities, laboratory parameters at admission, and medication history were collected and compared between groups. Multivariable logistic regression analysis (backward stepwise method) was performed to identify independent risk factors for DR, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate the predictive performance of identified factors. A nomogram was then constructed to visualize the combined predictive model.ResultsThe incidence of DR among patients with ADHF was 34.11%. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) use within one week prior to admission, chronic kidney disease stage 3, and New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class III-IV were independent risk factors for DR. Higher serum sodium levels, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and serum albumin levels were identified as protective factors. Log-transformed and standardized N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) was a very strong predictor of DR. Using the Youden index, the optimal cutoff value of baseline NT-proBNP for predicting DR was 1,003.19 pg/mL (sensitivity 81.06%, specificity 80.39%).ConclusionNT-proBNP and eGFR demonstrate good predictive value for DR. The occurrence of DR is significantly associated with prolonged hospitalization and an increased risk of renal function deterioration.Keywords: Acute decompensated heart failure, Diuretic resistance, Risk factors, Nonsteroidal Anti-inflammatory drugs, Chronic kidney disease

Introduction

Heart failure represents the end-stage manifestation of various cardiovascular diseases, with persistently high morbidity and mortality, and has become a significant global public health burden. Effective decongestion is the cornerstone of hospitalized treatment for acute decompensated heart failure (ADHF), and intravenous loop diuretics are the central pharmacological agents to achieve this goal [1]. However, in clinical practice, some patients fail to achieve adequate relief of volume overload symptoms despite standard diuretic therapy, a phenomenon referred to as diuretic resistance (DR) [2]. The occurrence of DR indicates poor response to conventional treatment and is associated not only with prolonged hospitalization and increased treatment costs but also with significantly higher risks of adverse short-term and long-term outcomes, including increased rates of rehospitalization and mortality. DR incidence in patients with ADHF ranges from 20% to 50%, making it a critical bottleneck in the clinical management of ADHF [3].

Currently, the mechanisms underlying DR in ADHF patients remain incompletely understood. They may involve alterations in renal hemodynamics, neurohormonal activation, electrolyte disturbances, renal function impairment, and underlying comorbid conditions [4]. Identifying the risk factors for DR is clinically important, as it can facilitate early recognition of high-risk patients, optimize therapeutic strategies, and improve prognosis. Although previous studies have explored related risk factors, inconsistencies remain due to differences in study populations, sample sizes, and assessment criteria, highlighting the need for further validation and supplementation.

Therefore, this study employed a retrospective design to collect clinical data from hospitalized patients with ADHF and systematically analyzed the potential risk factors contributing to the development of DR. We aim to provide a reference for early identification of high-risk patients and the development of individualized intervention strategies, ultimately improving therapeutic outcomes and long-term prognosis in patients with ADHF.

Participants and methods

Participants

This single-center retrospective study included hospitalized patients with ADHF admitted between January 2022 and June 2025.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) diagnosis of ADHF according to the 2022 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC) Heart Failure Guidelines [5]; (3) receipt of intravenous loop diuretic therapy during hospitalization; (4) availability of complete and traceable baseline clinical data.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) severe medical conditions or structural diseases, including but not limited to congenital heart disease, hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy, severe valvular disease requiring urgent surgery, or chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage 4 or higher (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²); (2) hospitalization duration less than 72 h; (3) pregnant or lactating women; (4) incomplete, unreliable, or untraceable clinical data that could compromise data reliability.

Sample size calculation

This retrospective cohort study included 387 consecutively enrolled eligible ADHF patients. Literature reports that the incidence of DR in ADHF patients is approximately 30–40% [6, 7], which corresponds to an estimated 116–155 events (DR cases) in this cohort. According to the empirical rule requiring at least 10 outcome events per predictor variable for multivariable logistic regression, the sample size was sufficient to support stable and reliable modeling for 12–15 predictor variables.

Data collection and variable definitions

Clinical data were retrospectively extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. Variables and definitions were as follows: (1) Demographic data: age (years), sex (male/female), body mass index (BMI, kg/m²). (2) Medical history and comorbidities: heart failure type, based on left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF): heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF, LVEF ≤ 40%) or heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF, LVEF ≥ 50%); heart function classification: assessed using the New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification [8] and analyzed as class III-IV versus I-II; etiology of heart failure: ischemic versus non-ischemic cardiomyopathy; comorbidities: hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, and atrial fibrillation (all recorded as yes/no); CKD stage: according to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines, divided into stages 1–2 (eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) and stage 3 (eGFR 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m²). (3) Clinical parameters at admission: first laboratory tests after admission and prior to intravenous diuretic administration, including electrolytes: serum sodium (Sodium, mmol/L), serum potassium; renal function: serum creatinine, with eGFR calculated using the CKD-EPI formula (mL/min/1.73 m²); nutritional and inflammatory marker: serum albumin (Albumin, Alb); heart failure biomarker: N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP); complete blood count: hemoglobin (Hemoglobin, Hb). (4) Medication history: use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) within 1 week prior to admission (yes/no); initial daily dose of intravenous loop diuretics, converted to furosemide equivalent (mg/day); long-term oral diuretic therapy: pre-admission regular use of loop diuretics (yes/no).

Outcome measures and key variable definitions: (1) DR was defined according to the 2019 HFA/ESC Position Paper on Diuretic Treatment in Acute Heart Failure and validated operational criteria used in large-scale clinical studies [9]. DR was diagnosed if patients failed to achieve either an average daily body weight reduction ≥ 1 kg or a net negative fluid balance ≥ 1,000 mL within the first 72 h of hospitalization, despite treatment with intravenous furosemide equivalent ≥ 80 mg/day [10]. This definition reflects inadequate decongestion under standardized intravenous loop diuretic therapy and is widely adopted in clinical research on ADHF. (2) Secondary outcomes: length of hospital stay (days), in-hospital mortality (yes/no), and renal function deterioration at discharge [11], defined as an increase in serum creatinine ≥ 26.5 µmol/L or ≥ 50% from the admission value (yes/no).

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using SPSS version 27.0, with data organization and additional analyses performed in R version 4.5.0. Continuous variables were first tested for normality. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the independent sample t-test; non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To improve model stability and clinical interpretability, NT-proBNP was natural log-transformed and standardized as a Z-score before inclusion in regression models. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariable analysis were entered into multivariable logistic regression. Variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated to assess multicollinearity (VIF < 5 indicated no significant collinearity). Forward stepwise regression (likelihood ratio test) was used to identify independent predictors, and a nomogram was constructed based on the final model. The predictive discrimination of individual factors and the combined model for DR was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).

Results

Incidence of DR and baseline clinical characteristics

A total of 387 consecutive patients with ADHF who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled in this study. All patients received intravenous loop diuretic therapy during hospitalization and had complete clinical data available. Based on the DR definition, 132 patients (34.11%) were assigned to the DR group and 255 patients (65.89%) to the non-DR group. As shown in Table 1, there were no significant differences between the two groups in age, sex, BMI, heart failure type, most comorbidities, or long-term oral loop diuretic use (all P > 0.05). However, the DR group had a significantly higher proportion of patients with NYHA class III-IV (69.70% vs. 39.22%, P < 0.001), CKD stage 3 (62.12% vs. 29.41%, P < 0.001), and NSAID use within one week prior to admission (35.61% vs. 10.59%, P < 0.001). The initial daily intravenous furosemide equivalent dose was also higher in the DR group (211.30 mg vs. 192.04 mg, P = 0.003).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis of independent risk factors for DR

All variables with P < 0.05 in Tables 1 and 2 were entered into an initial multivariable logistic regression model, followed by backward stepwise regression (exclusion criterion P > 0.05) to identify independent predictors (Table 3). The final model showed that NSAID use (OR = 4.821, 95% CI: 2.068–11.239), CKD stage 3 (OR = 3.699, 95% CI: 1.840–7.433), and NYHA III-IV (OR = 2.739, 95% CI: 1.378–5.445) were independent risk factors for DR. Conversely, higher serum sodium (OR = 0.829, 95% CI: 0.751–0.916), higher eGFR (OR = 0.912, 95% CI: 0.883–0.943), and higher serum Alb (OR = 0.844, 95% CI: 0.767–0.929) were protective factors. Log-transformed and standardized NT-proBNP was a very strong independent predictor (OR = 14.031, 95% CI: 6.538–30.113), indicating that each one SD increase in NT-proBNP was associated with an approximately 13-fold higher risk of DR. The VIF for all variables was < 2, indicating no significant multicollinearity.

ROC analysis on predictive performance of risk factors

To further evaluate the discriminative ability of independent predictors for DR, ROC curves and AUCs were calculated (Fig. 1; Table 4). NT-proBNP showed the best predictive performance (AUC = 0.861, 95% CI: 0.821–0.901, P < 0.001), indicating excellent discrimination for DR; eGFR ranked second (AUC = 0.757, 95% CI: 0.707–0.808, P < 0.001). Other factors, including serum Alb, serum sodium, CKD stage 3, NYHA class III-IV, and NSAID use, had AUCs ranging from 0.625 to 0.682. Although these values were statistically significant (all P < 0.001), they indicated moderate to fair discriminative ability. Using the Youden index, the optimal cutoff value of baseline NT-proBNP for predicting DR was 1,003.19 pg/mL, with a sensitivity of 81.06% and a specificity of 80.39%.

Construction of a predictive nomogram

Based on the seven independent predictors identified in multivariable logistic regression analysis, a nomogram was constructed to visualize the combined predictive model for diuretic resistance (Fig. 2). The total points accumulated from each predictor correspond to the predicted probability of DR. For example, a patient with log-transformed NT-proBNP of 7.0 (~ 1096 pg/mL), eGFR of 50 mL/min/1.73 m², NYHA class III-IV, NSAID use, CKD stage 3, serum sodium of 140 mmol/L, and albumin of 35 g/L would have a total points of approximately 100, corresponding to a DR risk of about 50%.

Discussion

Analysis of risk factors for DR

In this study, NSAID use within one week prior to admission was identified as the strongest independent risk factor for DR (OR = 4.821). The underlying mechanism is primarily related to NSAIDs non-selectively inhibiting cyclooxygenase, reducing the synthesis of prostaglandins that mediate renal vasodilation, thereby antagonizing the vasodilatory effect of loop diuretics and potentially directly reducing renal blood flow and glomerular filtration rate. Previous studies have shown that ADHF patients using NSAIDs require higher furosemide doses to achieve equivalent diuretic effects and have prolonged hospital stays [12]. These findings highlight the importance for clinicians to carefully review recent medication history in ADHF patients and, whenever possible, avoid NSAIDs. For patients requiring analgesia or anti-inflammatory treatment, alternatives with minimal impact on prostaglandin synthesis should be considered, and renal function should be closely monitored.

CKD stage 3 was another key risk factor in this study (OR = 3.699). The kidney is the target organ for diuretics. In CKD, reduced nephron number, intrarenal hemodynamic abnormalities, impaired tubular function, and neurohormonal overactivation collectively reduce the delivery of diuretics to tubular sites, lower effective concentrations, and enhance sodium reabsorption. Testani et al. confirmed that baseline eGFR is a strong predictor of diuretic efficiency [2]. Our study further specifies the risk associated with CKD stage 3 (eGFR 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m²), consistent with findings from the DOSE-AHF trial by Felker et al., in which renal dysfunction was identified as a major predictor of poor diuretic response [13]. This suggests that ADHF patients with CKD require closer monitoring of diuretic response and may benefit from early combination therapy targeting different nephron segments [14].

NYHA class III-IV was also an independent risk factor for DR (OR = 2.739). Severe cardiac dysfunction is associated with low cardiac output and systemic hypoperfusion, triggering strong neurohumoral compensation that causes renal vasoconstriction, reduces renal blood flow and GFR, and limits diuretic delivery [15]. Low cardiac output also leads to renal venous congestion and elevated interstitial pressure, impairing glomerular filtration. Previous studies have shown that low cardiac output and renal venous congestion are independently associated with poor diuretic response and renal function deterioration [16]. Therefore, actively improving cardiac function and optimizing systemic and renal perfusion are fundamental to alleviating DR in these patients.

Analysis of protective factors for DR

Protective factors identified in this study included higher serum sodium, eGFR, and serum Alb levels, which were inversely associated with DR risk. Normonatremia or mild hypernatremia often reflects relatively stable volume status and lower neurohormonal activation, supporting renal perfusion and diuretic efficacy [17]. Preserved eGFR directly indicates normal glomerular filtration, ensuring sufficient diuretic reaches tubular sites [18]. Alb, the primary binding protein for furosemide and other loop diuretics, may facilitate proximal tubular secretion and maintain an appropriate distribution volume, ensuring effective intratubular drug concentrations [19]. Previous studies also identified hypoalbuminemia as an independent predictor of DR and mortality in ADHF [20]. Clinically, correcting hyponatremia, preserving renal function, and improving nutritional status to increase serum Alb may enhance diuretic responsiveness.

Notably, log-transformed and standardized NT-proBNP was a very strong independent predictor of DR (OR = 14.031) and showed the highest discriminative ability in ROC analysis (AUC = 0.861). NT-proBNP is a sensitive marker of ventricular wall stress and volume overload; marked elevation reflects severe ventricular remodeling, increased filling pressures, and neurohormonal activation, all of which create an unfavorable environment for diuretic efficacy [21]. Prior studies by Blázquez-Bermejo Z et al. have indicated that changes in NT-proBNP predict chronic DR [22]. By applying log transformation and standardization, our study clearly quantified this effect: each one SD increase in log-transformed NT-proBNP was associated with an approximately 13-fold higher risk of DR. This supports the use of NT-proBNP as a convenient clinical tool for early identification of high-risk DR patients and suggests that such patients may require earlier or intensified diuretic strategies, including combination therapy or consideration of ultrafiltration.

Clinical impact of DR

This study found that DR patients had significantly longer hospital stays and higher rates of renal function deterioration at discharge, while in-hospital mortality did not differ significantly between the two groups. These results are consistent with previous studies, highlighting the negative impact of DR on short-term outcomes in ADHF [23]. The prolonged hospital stay in DR patients is primarily due to persistent volume overload requiring repeated adjustments of diuretic regimens (dose escalation, combination therapy, or continuous renal replacement therapy), prolonging treatment duration [24, 25]. DR is often accompanied by electrolyte disturbances (e.g., hypokalemia, hyponatremia) and renal function deterioration, requiring additional time for correction. The higher incidence of renal impairment at discharge is partly related to higher diuretic doses and longer treatment courses, which can reduce renal blood flow and exacerbate interstitial edema, and partly due to underlying CKD and severe cardiac dysfunction, which reduce renal reserve. The lack of significant difference in in-hospital mortality may relate to limited sample size and active in-hospital interventions (e.g., correcting electrolytes, optimizing cardiorenal function) but does not imply DR has no impact on long-term prognosis. Previous studies have shown that DR patients have significantly higher rates of post-discharge rehospitalization and long-term mortality, emphasizing the need for close follow-up after discharge, including monitoring of renal function, electrolytes, and cardiac function, as well as guidance on appropriate medication use (e.g., avoiding NSAIDs), and timely treatment adjustments to improve long-term outcomes [26]. The nomogram constructed in this study provides a practical tool for bedside risk stratification of DR, enabling clinicians to estimate individual patient risk at admission and potentially guide early intensified diuretic strategies.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, regarding study design, the single-center design with a relatively small sample size (n = 387) limits the generalizability of the findings, and the retrospective nature may have introduced selection bias. Second, concerning the definition of DR, we adopted an operational definition based on weight loss and fluid balance, which is practical in clinical settings. However, we did not perform spot urinary sodium assessment at two hours after diuretic administration, a more accurate marker recommended by recent guidelines, and this limits the granularity of our diuretic response evaluation. Third, regarding confounding variables, we could not control for all potential confounders due to retrospective data collection. Unmeasured variables (e.g., precise volume status, dietary intake, timing of medication administration, hemodynamic parameters) and uncollected data (e.g., baseline guideline-directed medical therapy use, escalation to advanced therapies such as ultrafiltration or inotropes) may have influenced our findings. Therefore, causality cannot be firmly established. Future prospective studies should incorporate comprehensive hemodynamic and biomarker monitoring, including urinary sodium measurements, to better elucidate DR mechanisms and optimize individualized management.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study confirms that in hospitalized ADHF patients, pre-admission NSAID use, CKD stage 3, NYHA III-IV, and NT-proBNP are independent risk factors for DR, while higher serum sodium, eGFR, and Alb are protective factors. NT-proBNP demonstrated good predictive value for DR. DR was significantly associated with prolonged hospitalization and increased risk of renal function deterioration at discharge. These findings underscore the importance of comprehensive baseline assessment, early identification of high-risk patients, and implementation of individualized, multifaceted strategies to prevent and overcome DR, which is crucial for improving hospital outcomes and long-term prognosis in ADHF patients.
