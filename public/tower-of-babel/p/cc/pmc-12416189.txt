Genetic insights into the interaction between chronic hepatitis B virus infection and metabolic syndrome
Authors: Juanjuan Zou, Yijing Zhang, Xiaojing Sun, Yan Wang, Yanzhong Li, Ze-Hua Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe association between chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection and metabolic syndrome (MetS) remains controversial. We aimed to analyze the causal effects of chronic HBV infection on MetS components and vice versa. Mendelian randomization (MR) was applied to explore the genetic association of chronic HBV infection with both metabolic risk factors and metabolic diseases using summary-level data from GWAS. Further colocalization and mediation analyses were performed for traits with significant causal relationships. The effect of HBV on lipid metabolism was validated by in vitro assays. In European populations, the MR analyses did not support causal relationships between chronic HBV infection and metabolic traits. In East Asian populations, chronic HBV infection was associated with decreased low-density lipoprotein (LDL) and reduced risk of coronary artery disease (CAD). Reversely, CAD showed negative causal effects on chronic HBV infection risk. Colocalization analysis revealed that the association between chronic HBV infection and CAD was most likely driven by distinct rather than shared causal variants. Mediation analysis identified LDL as a major mediator in the causal effect of chronic HBV infection on CAD and aspirin use as the primary mediator in the causal effect of CAD on chronic HBV infection. In vitro experiments suggested that HBV may inhibit glucose plus insulin-induced lipogenesis in hepatocytes. Our results provide genetic evidence of chronic HBV infection as a protective factor against dyslipidemia and CAD and reveal the potential causal effect of CAD on genetically proxied chronic HBV infection via aspirin treatment in East Asian populations.

Introduction

Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a global public health threat especially in the Western Pacific and African regions. Approximately 254 million people are estimated to live with chronic hepatitis B (CHB), which causes a great burden of HBV-related complications including liver cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and liver-related deaths [1]. Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is another major health concern worldwide, and comprises a series of metabolic disorders such as obesity, elevated blood pressure, insulin resistance and dyslipidemia, resulting in an increased risk of metabolic diseases including hypertension, type 2 diabetes (T2D), metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and cardiovascular diseases (CVD). The incidence and prevalence of MetS in parallel with obesity and T2D have been increasing rapidly over the past decades. The estimated prevalence of MetS has been reported to be 20%–40% in Asia, and 24.3% in Europe, and has increased from 36.2% in 1999–2000 to 47.3% in 2017–2018 in the United States [2,3]. Consequently, a striking increase in the comorbidities of CHB and MetS has recently been witnessed. However, whether and how CHB interacts with MetS remains enigmatic.

Several observational studies have explored the association between CHB and MetS but the results vary. While some studies have found that CHB increases the risk of MetS, others have discovered inverse associations between CHB and MetS [4]. A recent meta-analysis of 13 community or hospital-based studies reported that CHB was associated with a decreased risk of MetS and some of its single components [5]. Another population-based study enrolling 180,359 participants in southern Taiwan found that CHB was inversely associated with MetS only among lean subjects, but not in the general population [6]. However, the mechanisms underlying the associations remain unclear. Moreover, the heterogeneity of the enrolled subjects and potential confounding factors restrained the interpretation of the observational results and these data were unable to provide evidence on the causal relationships between CHB and MetS. Mendelian randomization (MR) is an approach utilizing genetic instrumental variables to investigate the causal inference between exposure and outcome, which can overcome confounding and reverse causality and facilitate the assessment the causal effect [7]. Therefore, MR is advantageous in determining the causal relationship between CHB and MetS.

In the present study, we analyzed the interaction between CHB and MetS by bidirectional MR using genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of European and East Asian ancestry and identified the negative association between CHB and coronary artery disease (CAD) in East Asian populations. Further colocalization and mediation analyses were conducted to disentangle the potential mechanisms underlying the association. Through these analyses, we provided novel insights into the genetic association between CHB and MetS.

Methods

Study design

As the potential interactions between HBV infection and metabolic alterations have been observed in both clinical and experimental investigations, a bidirectional MR design was leveraged [5,6,8,9]. This study used publicly available summary-level data from GWAS of both European and East Asian ancestry to conduct bidirectional MR analyses, investigating the causal relationships of CHB with metabolic risk factors including adiposity, blood pressure, glycemic and lipid traits and metabolic diseases including hypertension, T2D, MASLD and CAD. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology-Mendelian Randomization Reporting Guidelines (STROBE-MR) (Supplementary Table S1). Subsequently, colocalization was conducted for two traits with significant bidirectional causal relationships. Mediation analyses were also performed to identify potential mediators of the causal associations. The study design was shown in Figure 1. All GWAS had been approved by relevant review boards and ethical approval was not required for the MR analyses based on summary-level data. The study protocol was reviewed by Ethics Committee in Scientific Research of Shandong University Qilu Hospital, and the ethical approval was exempted [Review No. KYLL-202503(YJ)-041]. Data sources for CHB

The CHB GWAS data used for genetic analysis in European ancestry were from Sakaue et al. [10], which consisted of 145 cases and 351,740 controls from the UK Biobank. The CHB GWAS data in East Asian ancestry were obtained from Ishigaki et al. [11], which comprises 1,394 cases and 211,059 controls primarily from the BioBank Japan (Supplementary Table S2). CHB was diagnosed using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th edition (ICD-10) (ICD-10: B18.0/B18.1) by physicians at recruitment [10,12]. The genetic instruments were selected as previously reported [13,14]. Briefly, genetic variants associated with CHB at genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10−8) without linkage disequilibrium (LD) (defined as R2 > 0.001 or physical distance < 10,000kb) were identified from the summary-level GWAS data. The clumping procedure used the European and East Asian reference panel from the 1000 Genomes Project separately. The F statistic for each genetic instrument was estimated and genetic instruments with F statistics greater than 10 were considered to be of adequate instrument strength (Supplementary Table S3 and S4) [15].

Data sources for metabolic risk factors

Metabolic risk factors were divided into four categories: adiposity traits including body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), waist hip ratio (WHR) and body fat percentage; blood pressure traits including systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure; glycemic traits including glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), fasting glucose (FG), fasting insulin (FI), and 2-hour glucose after an oral glucose challenge (2hGlu); and lipid traits including triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and high-density lipoprotein (HDL). Summary-level data on adiposity traits were obtained from the UK Biobank consisting of up to 462,166 individuals of European ancestry and from the Taiwan Biobank containing 21,978 East Asian participants [16]. Genetic associations with blood pressure traits were extracted from the International Consortium of Blood Pressure (ICBP) including 757,601 European individuals, and the Pan-UK Biobank including 2,564 East Asian individuals [17]. Summary-level statistics on glycemic traits were obtained from The Meta-analysis of Glucose and Insulin-related Traits Consortium (MAGIC) which included up to 200,622 individuals of European ancestry and up to 35,619 individuals of East Asian ancestry [18]. Genetic associations with lipid traits were extracted from the Global Lipids Genetics Consortium (GLGC) consisting of 1,320,016 European individuals and 146,492 East Asian individuals [19]. Detailed information on the included studies is provided in Supplementary Table S2. The genetic instruments were selected as described above. For traits without sufficient genetic variants at genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10−8), associated variants at suggestively genome-wide significance (p < 5 × 10−6) were used. The threshold has been shown to be sufficient to include more eligible instrumental variables [20,21].

Data sources for metabolic diseases

The metabolic diseases included in the MR analyses were hypertension, T2D, MASLD, and CAD. Summary-level statistics for hypertension in European and East Asian ancestry were extracted from the UK Biobank (54,358 cases and 408,652 controls, ICD-10: I10) and China Kadoorie Bank (12,298 cases and 69,440 controls, ICD-10: I10), respectively [22,23]. Genetic associations with T2D were from FinnGen consisting of 65,085 cases and 335,112 controls in European ancestry and Asian Genetic Epidemiology Network (AGEN) containing 77,418 cases and 356,122 controls in East Asian ancestry [24,25]. For MASLD, only European GWAS data were used in the MR analysis as no East Asian GWAS data were publicly available. Summary-level data were extracted from the largest MASLD GWAS meta-analysis conducted in four cohorts of European ancestry: Electronic Medical Records and Genomics (eMERGE) network, UK Biobank, Estonian Biobank (EstBB) and FinnGen, comprising 8,434 cases and 770,180 controls [26]. Genetic associations with CAD were derived from the CARDIoGRAMplusC4D consortium consisting of 18,467 cases and 45,264 controls in European ancestry and BioBank Japan containing 32,512 cases and 146,214 controls in East Asian ancestry. Similarly, the genetic instruments were selected as described above.

Cell lines and treatment

Human HepG2 cells were sourced from National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Shanghai, China) and HepG2.2.15 cells were obtained from Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The cells were cultured in DMEM containing 5.5 mM D-glucose,10% fetal bovine serum, 100 units/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin, and incubated in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37°C and passaged every 2 days by trypsinization. Cells were fasted overnight, followed by 30 mM glucose (Aladdin, Shanghai, China) and 100 nM insulin (TOCRIS Bioscience, Bristol, UK) treatment for 24 hours.

BODIPY staining and measurement of triglyceride and cholesterol

Hepatocytes were first fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde followed by incubation with 1:1000 BODIPY 493/503 and Hoechst 33,342 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for lipid and nuclei staining. Fluorescence confocal microscopy was performed using a confocal microscope (Nikon, Melville, NY, USA). Measurement of intracellular triglyceride and total cholesterol levels was performed using kits obtained from Applygen (Beijing, China) according to manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA isolation and quantitative RT-PCR analysis

Cells were homogenized in TRIzol Reagent (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and total RNAs were reversely transcribed to cDNA using SuperScript II reverse transcriptase (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and Oligo d(T). The resulting cDNA was subjected to real-time PCR with gene-specific primers in the presence of SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, San Jose, CA, USA) using the StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, San Jose, CA, USA), as described previously [27]. The expression of mRNA was determined by 2−ΔΔCT and normalized by β-actin. The following quantitative RT-PCR primer sequences were used: CATGTACGTTGCTATCCAGGC (forward) and CTCCTTAATGTCACGCACGAT (reverse) for β-actin; GCGCCTTGACAGGTGAAGTC (forward) and GCCAGGGAAGTCACTGTCTTG (reverse) for SREBP-1c; GGACAGAGCAACTACGGCTT (forward) and GTGTCGTTGGTGCTCATCGT (reverse) for FAS; CTTGCGATATGCTGTGGTGC (forward) and AAGTTGATGTGCCAGCGGTA (reverse) for SCD1; CAGCTGCACATCACAGGGAA (forward) and GTACATCGGAACAGGCGGAT (reverse) for SREBP-2; GACTTGTGCATTCAAACATAGCAA (forward) and GCTGTGGCAGGGAGTCTTGGTACT (reverse) for HMGCS; AGCTTGCCCGAATTGTGTGTG (forward) and CCTGTTGTGAATCATGTGACTTT (reverse) for HMGCR.

Statistical analysis

We used the inverse variance-weighted (IVW) method under multiplicative random effects as the primary method for the bidirectional MR analysis. For traits without sufficient genetic instruments to conduct IVW analysis, Wald ratio method was used instead. Cochran’s Q value was analyzed to evaluate the heterogeneity of instruments’ estimates in MR associations. Other MR methods we used for sensitivity analyses included weighted median (WM) and weighted mode. Weighted median was able to provide a causal estimate assuming that over half of the weight came from valid instrumental variables [28]. Weighted mode estimated unbiased causal effect assuming that instrumental variables contributing to the largest cluster was valid [29]. MR-Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) analysis was also conducted to examine the consistency of results and potential pleiotropy. In some of the analyses, MR-PRESSO was unable to be conducted due to insufficient genetic instruments. Except for MR-PRESSO global test, horizontal pleiotropy was also examined by MR-Egger intercept analysis [13,30].

Bayesian colocalization analysis is a statistical method to assess whether two association signals are colocalized using single variant summary statistics generated by GWAS [31]. We conducted colocalization analysis to examine whether the bidirectional associations between CHB and CAD were affected by the same or distinct causal variants. The tested genomic regions were generated within a window of ± 1 Mb from the lead SNPs which were used as genetic instruments for CHB and CAD. The posterior probabilities were calculated for five hypotheses: H0, no association with either trait; H1 and H2, association with trait 1 or trait 2; H3, association with both traits but two independent causal variants; and H4, association with both traits with a shared causal variant. A posterior probability greater than 0.80 was considered to support the tested hypothesis [32].

For significant associations, mediation analysis was performed to identify the mediating pathways. The two-step MR method based on the estimates from the IVW analyses was used to assess the indirect effect of exposure on outcome via potential mediators [33]. Standard errors for indirect effects were generated and confidence intervals were estimated with the delta method, and the mediated proportions were calculated using the “product of coefficients” method [34].

To adjust for multiple comparisons, Bonferroni-corrected significance levels of p value <3.57 × 10−3 (0.05/14) for metabolic risk factors and p value <1.25 × 10−2 (0.05/4) for metabolic diseases (p value <1.67 × 10−2 (0.05/3) in East Asian MR analyses) were used. The association with a nominal p value <0.05 but without Bonferroni adjusted significance was considered suggestively significant. For other analyses, a two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using the TwoSampleMR, MendelianRandomization, MR-PRESSO and coloc packages in R software (v4.3.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [35,36].

For in vitro experiments, data were expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was evaluated using unpaired two‐tailed Student’s t‐test. Differences were considered significant at p value < 0.05.

Results

Causal effect of CHB on MetS in European populations

We first examined the causal effects of CHB on metabolic risk factors in European populations. As shown in Supplementary Figure S1, MR analyses did not support significant causal effects of CHB on adiposity, blood pressure, glycemic, or lipid traits in European ancestry after adjusting for multiple test (all p > 3.57 × 10−3). The effects of CHB on HbA1c reached suggestive significance in the MR analyses using IVW (β = 0.01, 95%CI: 0.00 to 0.02, p = 0.042) and WM methods (β = 0.01, 95%CI: 0.00 to 0.02, p = 0.027) but not in MR analyses using other methods (Supplementary Figure S1 and Supplementary Table S5). We next explored the causal effects of CHB on metabolic diseases including hypertension, T2D, MASLD and CAD in European populations. Similarly, the results did not support significant causal effects of CHB on the metabolic diseases (all p > 1.25 × 10−2) (Supplementary Figure S2 and Supplementary Table S6). Collectively, the MR analyses were unable to provide supporting evidence on the causal effect of CHB on MetS in European populations.

Causal effect of MetS on CHB in European populations

We then investigated the causal effect of MetS on CHB in European populations. As shown in Supplementary Figure S3, the MR analyses did not support significant causal effects of metabolic risk factors on CHB as examined in IVW methods (all p > 3.57 × 10−3) (Supplementary Table S7). Meanwhile, only hypertension showed suggestive effect on CHB in the MR analysis using IVW methods (OR = 6.96, 95%CI: 1.17 to 41.51, p = 0.033) (Supplementary Figure S4). Similarly, the MR results did not support significant causal effects of other metabolic diseases on CHB (all p > 1.25 × 10−2) (Supplementary Table S8). Taken together, no robust results supporting the effects of MetS on CHB were obtained in European populations.

Causal effect of CHB on MetS in East Asian populations

Next, we investigated the causal effect of genetically proxied CHB on MetS in East Asian populations. We first examined the causal effects of CHB on metabolic risk factors including adiposity, blood pressure, glycemic, and lipid traits (Figure 2 and Supplementary Table S9). Notably, CHB was significantly associated with decreased levels of TC (β = −0.04, 95%CI: −0.06 to −0.02, p = 1.87 × 10−4) and LDL (β = −0.03, 95%CI: −0.05 to −0.02, p = 1.71 × 10−4) in IVW analyses and MR analyses by other methods showed consistent results which implied that the genetic associations were robust. Pleiotropy tests by MR-Egger intercept analysis and MR-PRESSO global test showed no horizontal pleiotropy (all p > 0.05). Additionally, CHB was suggestively associated with decreased BMI (β = −0.04, 95%CI: −0.08 to −0.00, p = 0.028), decreased HbA1c (β = −0.01, 95%CI: −0.02 to −0.00, p = 0.027), decreased TG (β = −0.02, 95%CI: −0.04 to −0.00, p = 0.016) and increased HDL (β = 0.03, 95%CI: 0.00 to 0.06, p = 0.025) in MR analyses using IVW methods. MR analyses in other methods also showed supportive results as estimates were in the same direction as IVW (Supplementary Table S9) [37]. These data indicate that CHB is associated with lower levels of TC and LDL and may exert beneficial effects on metabolic risk factors in East Asian populations. We then explored the causal effects of CHB on metabolic diseases in East Asian populations. Due to the fact that there lacks MASLD GWAS in East Asian ancestry, we only analyzed the association of CHB with hypertension, T2D and CAD. Meanwhile, negative association of CHB with T2D was also observed in IVW methods (OR = 0.97, 95%CI: 0.95 to 0.99, p = 0.015). Causal effect of MetS on CHB in East Asian populations

We further examined the causal effect of MetS on CHB in East Asian populations. As shown in Figure 4, only BMI was suggestively associated with a reduced risk of CHB (OR = 0.41, 95%CI: 0.20 to 0.87, p = 0.02) in IVW analysis. As there were no sufficient proxying SNPs, MR analyses using other methods and horizontal pleiotropy test were unable to be conducted. None of the metabolic risk factors showed significant effects on risk of CHB after adjusting for multiple test (all p > 3.57 × 10−3) (Supplementary Table S11). Interestingly, although the metabolic risk factors were insufficient to affect CHB risk, we observed the causal effect of CAD on a lower risk of CHB (OR = 0.79, 95%CI: 0.66 to 0.93, p = 0.006) by MR analysis in IVW method (Figure 5). The results were strengthened by MR analyses using other methods. Pleiotropy tests by MR-Egger intercept analysis and MR-PRESSO global test showed no horizontal pleiotropy (all p > 0.05) (Supplementary Table S12). Moreover, T2D was also significantly associated with a decreased risk of CHB (OR = 0.87, 95%CI: 0.78 to 0.95, p = 0.004) in IVW analysis (Figure 5). However, the significance was not supported by other MR methods including WM, and weighted mode (all p > 0.05). No significant association of hypertension with CHB was identified (OR = 1.22, 95%CI: 0.71 to 2.10, p = 0.474). These results suggested that most of MetS components may have no effects on risk of CHB. Colocalization of CHB and CAD in East Asian populations

Colocalization is another powerful statistical approach for understanding the relationships between traits and diseases. Unlike MR, colocalization is aimed to assess whether two traits are affected by the same or distinct causal variants [32]. Considering the significant bidirectional causal relationships between CHB and CAD by MR analyses, we performed colocalization analysis to determine whether the two diseases share the same causal variants. The results showed that the association between CHB and CAD was most likely to be driven by distinct rather than shared causal variants (PPH3 = 1.00, PPH4 = 8.22 × 10−9) (Supplementary Table S13). These data suggest that the CHB and CAD may not share a common genetic mechanism.

Mediation analysis of association between CHB and CAD in East Asian populations

We then explored the mediation pathways involved in the association between CHB and CAD in East Asian populations using a two-step MR approach. We first screened the potential mediators in the causal effect of CHB on CAD. Among the metabolic risk factors and diseases, TC and LDL were identified as mediators in the inhibitory effect of CHB on CAD. The indirect effect of CHB on CAD via TC was −0.02 (95%CI: −0.03 to −0.01, p = 9.21 × 10−4) and the mediation proportion was 16.30% (95%CI: 6.66% to 25.95%), while the indirect effect of CHB on CAD via LDL was −0.02 (95%CI: −0.03 to −0.01, p = 6.22 × 10−4) and the mediation proportion was 15.13% (95%CI: 6.46% to 23.80%), indicating that the mediating effect of TC was mainly contributed by LDL (Figure 6(A) and Supplementary Table S14). These data indicate that the protective effect of CHB on CAD risk is partially mediated by the inhibitory effect of CHB on LDL levels. Conversely, we explore the potential mediators in the causal effect of CAD on CHB. Metabolic traits including BMI, uric acid and T2D, allergy and autoimmune multi-traits, social and behavioral traits including education and drinking, and treatment traits including statins and aspirin use were examined. Intriguingly, CAD was negatively associated with risk of allergy (OR = 0.86, 95%CI: 0.80 to 0.92, p = 2.80 × 10−5) and autoimmune diseases (OR = 0.85, 95%CI: 0.76 to 0.96, p = 7.28 × 10−3), while positively associated with statin use (OR = 1.73, 95%CI: 1.46 to 2.05, p = 1.68 × 10−10) and aspirin use (OR = 1.54, 95%CI: 1.45 to 1.64, p = 5.60 × 10−44). Moreover, in addition to BMI and T2D which has been shown to be associated with reduced risk of CHB in aforementioned analysis, autoimmune diseases (OR = 0.59, 95%CI: 0.46 to 0.76, p = 3.44 × 10−5) and aspirin use (OR = 0.64, 95%CI: 0.42 to 0.99, p = 0.044) were found to be associated with reduced CHB risk while drinking was associated with increased risk of CHB (OR = 1.46, 95%CI: 1.17 to 1.82, p = 7.36 × 10−4). Collectively, only aspirin treatment was identified as a significant mediator (indirect effect: β = −0.19, 95%CI: −0.38 to −0.00, p = 0.046) and the mediation proportion was 79.11% (95%CI: 1.47% to 156.75%) (Figure 6(B) and Supplementary Table S15). Taken together, aspirin showed a protective effect on CHB risk and largely mediated the association of CAD with reduced risk of CHB.

Effect of HBV on glucose plus insulin-induced lipogenesis in hepatocytes

To further investigate the role of HBV in lipid metabolism, we established an in vitro model of lipogenesis by glucose plus insulin treatment in both HepG2 and HBV-replicating HepG2.2.15 cells, which has been shown to be sufficient to stimulate lipid accumulation [38]. As shown in Figure 7(A), the lipid droplets were induced by glucose and insulin treatment in HepG2 cells, while the induction was significantly attenuated in HepG2.2.15 cells. Further quantification of triglycerides and total cholesterol revealed an inhibitory effect of HBV on lipid accumulation in hepatocytes (Figure 7(B,C)). Moreover, we detected the mRNA levels of genes involved in triglyceride and cholesterol biosynthesis. As shown in Figure 7(D–F), the transcriptional levels of SREBP-1c, FAS, and SCD1 were elevated by glucose and insulin treatment in HepG2 cells, which were significantly lower in HepG2.2.15 cells. On the other hand, the expression levels of SREBP-2, HMGCS, and HMGCR were significantly depressed under glucose plus insulin-induced condition in HepG2.2.15 cells compared to HepG2 cells (Figure 7(G–I)). Collectively, these data suggest that HBV may inhibit glucose plus insulin-induced lipogenesis by suppressing expression of SREBP-1c and SREBP-2 in hepatocytes. Discussion

Although the relationship between CHB and MetS has been examined by several observational investigations, inconsistent results are obtained. Some studies have observed that CHB is associated with decreased risk of MetS, especially in East Asians [5,6]. However, the causal inference and underling mechanisms are lacking. In the present study, we analyzed the causal relationships between CHB and MetS and showed evidences on the negative effect of CHB on MetS from genetic perspectives and identified the potential mediators in East Asian populations, which provided explanations for the clinical observations and gave clues to further mechanistic explorations.

One of the major findings of the present study is the identification of CHB as a protective factor against MetS in East Asian populations. It has been shown by a meta-analysis enrolling predominantly East Asian studies that CHB is associated with decreased risk of MetS and some of its components [5]. Consistently, our MR analyses showed that CHB was significantly associated with decreased levels of TC and LDL and was suggestively associated with decreased BMI, HbA1c, and TG and increased HDL. Besides, CHB was also found to have inverse impact on the risk of T2D and CAD in East Asian populations. However, the reverse MR analyses did not find significant causal effect of metabolic risk factors on CHB. In fact, there have been several observations demonstrating the favorable effect of CHB on lipid profiles in East Asians. In a large cohort of healthy Korean adults, HBsAg seropositivity was associated with lower risk of development of dyslipidemia [39]. Similarly, a study enrolled 7,695 Taiwanese adults showed that subjects with HBV infection had lower serum TC and LDL levels [40]. It has been reported that the serum levels of adiponectin were significantly higher in CHB patients compared with healthy controls, which was found to be associated with HBsAg and HBV DNA and was considered to contribute to the improved lipid profile [41]. Moreover, HBV and HBx protein were shown to be able to regulate apolipoprotein metabolism, leading to decreased levels of Apolipoprotein C3 (ApoC3) and apolipoprotein B (ApoB) [42]. In line with these findings, our in vitro data showed that HBV may inhibit glucose plus insulin-induced lipogenesis by suppressing expression of SREBP-1c and SREBP-2 in hepatocytes. However, the exact mechanisms underlying the effect of HBV on lipid metabolism under physiological conditions can be quite complicated, which need to be further explored in in vivo models.

The present study also demonstrates a close genetic link between CHB and CAD in East Asian populations. Several lines of evidence support the genetic interaction of the two diseases. First, the bidirectional MR analyses showed significantly inverse causal effects of CHB on CAD and vice versa. Moreover, the genetic associations between CHB and CAD were enhanced by the colocalization analysis, which also provided the implication that the genetic interaction between CHB and CAD was driven by distinct causal variants. Importantly, the mediating pathways in the bidirectional associations were also identified. Several studies have implied that CHB is associated with reduced risk of CAD. A large population-based study consisting of 20,460 participants demonstrated that concomitant HBV infection could protect from atherosclerosis in patients with MASLD [43]. Similarly, a retrospective, longitudinal, and propensity score-matched study found that chronic HBV infection was associated with a decreased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in patients with diabetes [44]. Notably, these two studies were both conducted in East Asian populations. In accordance with these findings, our data also suggest that CHB is a protective factor against CAD, and the effects are largely mediated by the favorable role of CHB in decreasing LDL level which has been considered as a major risk factor of CAD [45]. Unexpectedly, CAD showed a negative causal effect on CHB in the reverse MR analysis. As the metabolic risk factors have been shown to have limited influence on the genetically proxied CHB, we conducted a comprehensive mediation analysis and identified aspirin use as a major contributor mediating the causal effect of CAD on CHB. In fact, aspirin has been demonstrated to be associated with a reduced risk of HCC and lower liver-associated mortality in patients with CHB [46,47]. Moreover, experimental investigations also show that platelets play an important role in the pathogenesis of HBV-related liver disease by sustaining inflammation and aspirin treatment is able to diminish the number of intrahepatic HBV-specific CD8+ T cells and HBV- nonspecific inflammatory cells, alleviate liver fibrosis and inhibit the development of HCC in a mouse model of CHB [48]. Additionally, CAD may alter host immune status to exert an impact on chronic HBV infection. It has been shown that proinflammatory cytokine-producing CD8+ T cells are characterized in atherosclerosis, which can produce systemic IFN-γ [49]. However, whether CAD is able to alter HBV-specific immune response needs to be further clarified.

In our study, we discovered that CHB had a negative effect on MetS, especially dyslipidemia and CAD in East Asian populations, while the causal effects were not observed in European populations. The disparities can be attributed to multiple factors. First, the genotypes of HBV are distributed differently in European and East Asian populations with genotypes B and C the dominant viruses throughout East Asia and genotype D most common in Europe [50]. The differences in HBV genotypes can lead to divergent host immune responses and affect the clinical outcomes. Consequently, the differently dominant HBV genotypes in European and East Asian populations may exert varied effects on MetS via regulating host metabolic pathways [51]. Second, the metabolic traits are shaped by different genetic backgrounds and dietary habits in European and East Asian populations [52,53]. The ethnic-specific genetic architecture of MetS may confer distinct liability to the effect of CHB. It has been reported that elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT) is associated with lower triglycerides in East Asians but not in Europeans [54,55]. Moreover, the ethnic difference in diet habits may also influence the HBV’s effect on MetS by affecting gut microbiota [56,57].

There are several potential application scenarios of our study. First, although we found that CHB protected against MetS in East Asian populations, the effects were modest. Therefore, CHB and MetS should be managed simultaneously in clinical practice. For patients with concomitant CHB and MetS, similar management as MetS patients without CHB should be provided. Second, as the lipid-lowering effect of CHB may be influenced by the stages of disease and antiviral therapy, monitoring of lipid profiles and adequate treatment are recommended [43]. Third, as our data revealed that aspirin may exert beneficial effects in CHB, antiplatelet therapy can be recommended in patients with both CHB and CAD. However, the effect of aspirin on chronic HBV infection could be greatly varied in East Asian populations. Therefore, further studies are needed to identify effective dose and duration of aspirin treatment on chronic HBV infection.

Our study has some limitations. First, due to the low prevalence of chronic HBV infection in European populations, the number of CHB cases was quite small in the European GWAS data. It should be cautious that the absence of genetic associations between chronic HBV infection and MetS in European populations can be caused by the limited case numbers, which potentially inflates type II errors. Therefore, GWAS including more CHB cases in European ancestry is needed to validate our MR results. Second, as the East Asian GWAS data for some traits are limited, it should be noted that some of our analyses may have overlapping participants. However, our MR approach was supported by a recent simulation study which showed that two-sample MR methods can be safely used for one-sample MR performed within large biobanks, expect for MR-Egger [58]. However, residual bias cannot be ruled out, and more GWAS in East Asian populations are needed to further validate our results. Third, other factors such as viral factors, immune factors, and other medications may also sever as mediators in the interaction between chronic HBV infection and CAD and further studies are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms. Fourth, our results are specific to East Asian populations and their applicability to other ethnic groups remains uncertain. Therefore, further studies in different populations are needed to confirm our results.

In conclusion, our study provided evidence on the protective role of chronic HBV infection for metabolic risk factors, especially dyslipidemia, which contributes to the association of CHB with a reduced risk of CAD in East Asians. Reversely, CAD also had negative causal effects on CHB via aspirin use. These findings provide genetic insights into the association between chronic HBV infection and MetS and highlight the potential role of HBV in host metabolic regulation.
