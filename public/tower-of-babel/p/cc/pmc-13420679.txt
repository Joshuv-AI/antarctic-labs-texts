Infolding is an uncommon valve dysfunction (0.7% to 3.15%) after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) associated with complications such as patient-prosthesis mismatch, paravalvular leak (PVL), thrombosis, stroke, hemodynamic instability, and emergency surgery.1 It most commonly occurs with the Evolut platform due to its nitinol self-expanding frame, tall skirts, and large valve sizes.2 Risk factors include bicuspid anatomy, high annular ellipticity, large prosthesis size (≥29 mm), improper loading, over- and undersizing, resheathing, vascular kinking, lack of predilatation, asymmetric calcification, and uneven expansion.1,3,4

Recognizing valve infolding is only the first step, as management depends on timing of detection. Early identification, before the point-of-no-recapture (PNR) and when recapture and repositioning remain possible, helps prevent complications.3 Fluoroscopy typically shows a vertical-line sign, seen as a linear density from frame overlap, with segmental narrowing best seen on a 360° sweep.2 Echocardiography confirms the diagnosis showing a “Pac-Man” appearance.1 Prevention relies on thorough pre-TAVR computed tomography (CT) planning for precise sizing and anatomical assessment. Proper valve loading and fluoroscopic checks help prevent nonconcentric crimping and deformation.3

Infolding before complete deployment is ideally managed by recapturing and retrieving the valve, followed by implantation of a new prosthesis. In cases of persistent dysfunction, immediate implantation of a new prosthesis should be considered, with the strategy tailored to the patient's anatomical characteristics.1 If final deployment has already been performed and severe deformation is identified, cautious balloon postdilatation may be attempted to optimize transcatheter heart valve (THV) configuration and hemodynamic parameters, bearing in mind the associated risk of valve embolization.1 Repositioning or valve replacement are alternative strategies, while severe infolding may require redo-transcatheter aortic valve placement (redo-TAV) or surgical conversion.4

Valve embolization or “pop-out” is rare (0.1% to 3.7%), but carries a 9-fold mortality risk.5 It may embolize into the ascending aorta (38%), left ventricle (LV) (31%), or descending aorta (23%).5 Management depends on embolization location, with LV embolization generally requiring surgery, whereas ascending aorta embolization requires snaring, anchoring, balloon-assisted retrieval, or valve-in-valve procedure.

Case Summary

A 73-year-old Filipino man with moderate aortic stenosis (valve area 1.07 cm2) presented with 3 months of progressive exertional dyspnea. His medical history includes controlled hypertension, 10–pack-year smoking, and triple coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) 14 years prior. His medications include aspirin, telmisartan, nebivolol, rosuvastatin, and ezetimibe. He recently reported worsening NYHA functional class II symptoms.

On admission, laboratory tests were unremarkable. Electrocardiogram showed sinus bradycardia with trifascicular block. Transesophageal echocardiogram demonstrated severe aortic stenosis (valve area 0.91 cm2; peak velocity 3.28 m/s) with a heavily calcified aortic valve, fused commissures, mild aortic regurgitation (AR), and LV ejection fraction of 69%. Coronary angiography demonstrated nonsignificant right coronary artery disease, with patent left internal mammary artery to left anterior descending artery, saphenous vein to posterior descending artery, and saphenous vein to obtuse marginal artery grafts. He opted for TAVR over surgical route due to his prior CABG and high surgical risk.

CT aortography measured the perimeters of the sinus of Valsalva, annulus, sinotubular junction, and LV outflow tract (Figures 1A to 1D), degree of annular calcification, and calcium score (Figures 1E and 1F). Coronary cusp-to-ostial and sinus of Valsalva heights, and aortic root angle were measured (Figures 1G to 1J). Based on these findings, a 34-mm Evolut Pro+ valve was selected.

The patient underwent TAVR with a 34-mm Evolut valve, but structural valve failure manifested as infolding, deformation, and embolization, necessitated a same-procedure bailout redo-TAV. Post procedure, he developed high-degree atrioventricular block requiring permanent pacemaker. The patient also developed pneumonia and acute respiratory distress syndrome, managed in the intensive care unit with antibiotics, diuretics, rate control, and anticoagulation. Repeat echocardiography showed well-seated valves with good function (valve area 1.3 cm2, Doppler velocity index 0.29, peak gradient 29.74 mm Hg), and no PVL. The patient's condition improved, and he was discharged 13 days post-TAVR.

Procedural Steps

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with transesophageal echocardiographic and fluoroscopic guidance. Vascular access was obtained via the left femoral and bilateral radial arteries, and a SENTINEL cerebral protection system was deployed. Simultaneous aortic and LV pressures showed a peak-to-peak gradient of 30 mm Hg. Predilatation was deferred due to low pre-TAVR gradient and to minimize procedural time and radiation exposure. Valve loading was assessed by both the manufacturer specialist and implanting physician and passed inspection. A 34-mm Evolut Pro+ valve was positioned 1 to 2 mm at the noncoronary cusp (NCC) and 2 to 3 mm at the left coronary cusp (Figure 2A, Video 1) under the cusp-overlap view with aortographic confirmation. A linear density was noted within the first 2 struts, possibly indicating early infolding; however, deployment proceeded as it was not deemed significant. Postdeployment aortography showed clustered struts near the NCC with a vertical-line sign, consistent with valve infolding (Figure 2B, Video 2). Postdilatation with a NuMED Z-MED 25-mm balloon with rapid pacing (180 beats/min) was attempted to correct the infolding but instead resulted in outward deformation and valve embolization upward into the ascending aorta (Figures 2C and 2D, Video 3). Cineangiography demonstrated severe AR and part of the prosthesis above the native aortic valve annulus, consistent with valve “pop-out” (Video 4). Echocardiography confirmed significant AR, stent deformation along the NCC with a “Pac-Man” appearance, and significant PVL (Figures 3A and 3B, Video 5).

Valve recapture was considered but not performed due to the intraoperative unavailability of snare catheters. Therefore, bailout options that included redo-TAV vs open surgery were considered. Given his prior CABG and high surgical risk, a same-procedure redo-TAV with a tall-in-tall strategy was chosen. There was also less concern about native coronary access because the patient was bypass-graft dependent. A second 34-mm Evolut Pro+ valve was used, as short balloon-expandable valves were not commercially available locally. It was implanted slightly deeper than the index valve (2-3 mm at the NCC and 3-4 mm at the left coronary cusp) (Figure 4A, Video 6). Fluoroscopy revealed stent underexpansion without a vertical-line sign (Figure 4B). Postdilatation with a 26-mm NuMED Z-MED balloon with rapid pacing (180 beats/min) successfully corrected the underexpansion, resolving both infolding and deformation (Figure 4C, Video 7).

Redo-TAV Placement

(A) Deployment of the second Evolut 34 mm Pro+ valve to the point-of-no-recapture proceeded without incident. (B) Postdeployment imaging showed lower valve positioning at 2 to 3 mm along the NCC and 3 to 4 mm deep at the LCC, with a narrower-than-expected functional zone (arrows), consistent with valve underexpansion. (C) Balloon dilatation with rapid pacing was performed to address the underexpanded area and revealed no frame deformation or infolding. Final aortography demonstrated 2 well-aligned valve-in-valve placements, with no systolic gradient, clear dicrotic notch, and improved LV end-diastolic pressure (Figure 4D, Video 8). Postoperative echocardiography confirmed spherical valve geometry with minimal PVL, and trivial AR (Figures 5A to 5C, Video 9).

Complication Prevention Strategy/Discussion

Valve infolding, deformation, and embolization could have been prevented with early recognition. Likely contributors include improper valve loading, oversizing, and eccentric calcification. A linear density in the first 2 struts before deployment suggested early infolding despite inspection, highlighting the need for meticulously documenting crimping and loading checks before deployment. Valve oversizing with radial force mismatch could have also contributed. Although >20% oversizing is generally recommended for Evolut valves and improves outcomes by reducing conduction disturbances, annular rupture, and paravalvular leak, larger valves (>29-34 mm) and bicuspid anatomy are more prone to infolding due to lower shear force in the functional zone.6 In this case, imaging confirmed a trileaflet aortic valve and a 29-mm valve with intentional oversizing could have achieved good outcomes with lower infolding risk. Eccentric calcification of the annulus and LV outflow tract can cause uneven pressure distribution and expansion on the nitinol frame during deployment.7 This may be mitigated by predilatation to enlarge the orifice area, facilitate smoother prosthesis passage, and promote uniform expansion by reducing radial counterforces.7 The combination of significant NCC calcification, exacerbated by oversizing, and misloading, likely contributed to infolding and deformation. Although resheathing and recapturing the valve before reaching the PNR was considered, the authors elected to proceed with full deployment and subsequent postdilatation, which worsened the deformation and led to embolization into the ascending aorta. Snaring of the embolized THV to remove it from the annulus was considered; however, this was not performed due to the intraoperative unavailability of snare catheters.

Redo-TAV using a tall-in-tall (Evolut-in-Evolut) strategy carries significant risks, including coronary obstruction, impaired coronary reaccess, and conduction disturbances. Optimal alignment of the inflow portions of both valves is critical, as tall frame and high neoskirt of Evolut valves increase the likelihood of coronary obstruction and complicate future reaccess.8 Evolut-in-Evolut procedures are frequently associated with a high risk of coronary inaccessibility, reported in up to 71% of cases.9 A short-in-tall strategy could reduce this risk if alternative platforms were available at the time of procedure. Embolization of the index valve into the ascending aorta further heightened the patient's risk of coronary obstruction and reaccess difficulty, although this was less concerning because the patient's left coronary circulation was bypass-graft dependent. The remaining concern involved potential obstruction of, or future access to, the native right coronary ostium.

Ideally, comprehensive assessment of the index TAVR should precede implantation of a second valve, including evaluation of the coronary risk plane, valve-to-coronary and valve-to-sinotubular junction distances, neoskirt level, and coronary sinus and ostium heights.10 However, in urgent same-procedure bailout scenarios where detailed analysis is not feasible, careful fluoroscopic guidance with aortography can help mitigate these risks.

Conclusions

Early detection of valve dysfunction, ideally before reaching the PNR using imaging, is essential to minimize complications and improve procedural outcomes, as management differs before and after valve deployment. This case highlights that same-procedure redo-TAV can serve as an effective bailout strategy, particularly high surgical risk patients and in resource-limited settings.
