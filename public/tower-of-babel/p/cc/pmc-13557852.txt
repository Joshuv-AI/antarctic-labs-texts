EZR-ROS1-rearranged lung adenocarcinoma presenting as diffuse cystic lung disease mimicking lymphangioleiomyomatosis: a case report
Authors: Ziyin You, Wanda Bi, Hui Cao, Yan Wang, Chenjun Shi, Ying Zhou, Li Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiffuse cystic lung disease (DCLD) in women of reproductive age is often attributed to lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM), but malignancy should be considered when atypical features are present.Case DescriptionWe report the case of a 27-year-old never-smoking woman initially diagnosed with LAM based on high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) findings, who was subsequently found to have metastatic lung adenocarcinoma on supraclavicular lymph node biopsy. Her first HRCT showed diffuse thin-walled lung cysts, and in 6 months, the second HRCT showed diffuse cysts (2–14 mm) with heterogeneous cyst-wall thickness plus a left upper-lobe patchy opacity, as well as beaded pleural-based fissural nodules in the right lung. Meanwhile, her serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) was 21.96 ng/mL and the supraclavicular node biopsy confirmed metastatic lung adenocarcinoma (cT4N3M1a, stage IVA). DNA-based next-generation sequencing (NGS) identified an EZR-ROS1 rearrangement. After 8 months of crizotinib therapy, she achieved a partial response (PR) with CEA normalization. As of 77 months after treatment initiation, the patient maintains a PR without progression or documented acquired resistance.ConclusionsIn a LAM-like cystic background, the presence of superimposed focal opacities or nodules, fissural beading, and heterogeneous cyst-wall thickness should prompt an expedited malignancy work-up rather than an HRCT-only diagnosis, as this case demonstrates that systematic evaluation of atypical features in presumed LAM can identify treatable oncogene-driven malignancies amenable to highly effective targeted therapy.

Highlight box

Key findings

• We report a case of advanced non-small cell lung cancer presenting as diffuse cystic lung disease initially misdiagnosed as lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM).

• The diagnosis of lung adenocarcinoma was initially suggested by a comprehensive assessment of atypical imaging features, including patchy opacities, fissural nodular beading, and heterogeneous cyst-wall thickness. This was confirmed by lymph node biopsy and subsequent genetic testing, which identified an EZR-ROS1 fusion. The patient was subsequently treated with crizotinib, leading to a favorable long-term outcome.

What is known and what is new?

• In women of childbearing age with no smoking history presenting with diffuse bilateral cystic lung disease, LAM is often the initial clinical consideration.

• In patients with a suspected diagnosis of LAM, the identification of additional solid abnormalities beyond cystic changes on imaging should raise suspicion for malignancy, prompting prompt physical examination and diagnostic workup that may identify treatable driver oncogene-positive malignancies, enabling the initiation of highly effective targeted therapy.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• For cystic lung disease, the presence of superimposed atypical features should redirect the diagnostic pathway from non-invasive surveillance toward aggressive tissue sampling. This approach is essential, as it may uncover oncogene-driven malignancies with available targeted therapies that can fundamentally alter the disease trajectory and achieve durable responses.

Introduction

Diffuse cystic lung disease (DCLD) is a heterogeneous entity (1). In women of reproductive age, lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM) is frequently suspected; however, in rare cases, vigilance for lung cancer is required. Lung cancer associated with cystic airspaces is increasingly recognized as a distinct, under-recognized subtype of lung cancer with unique imaging and pathological features, with a reported incidence ranging from 0.4% to 16.1% and adenocarcinoma being the most common histological type (2). Research on solitary cystic lung cancer has reported that 3.7% of lung cancer cases are associated with cystic airspaces, and 0.46% of operable lung cancers present as cysts (3,4). Diffuse cysts as a pulmonary manifestation of lung cancer are even rarer and are easily overlooked on imaging, which may lead to diagnostic delays and an unfavorable prognosis. Herein, we report an EZR-ROS1-rearranged lung adenocarcinoma presenting as diffuse cystic change mimicking LAM, summarize practical warning signs, and highlight a favorable survival outcome achieved following highly effective targeted therapy. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2026-0312/rc).

Case presentation

A 27-year-old never-smoking East Asian woman with no family history of LAM or cancer presented with acute-onset cough and dyspnea. Initial high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) revealed diffuse bilateral thin-walled lung cysts with left pneumothorax. She was treated with closed thoracic drainage and discharged with a presumptive LAM diagnosis.

Six months later, she was evaluated at Daping Hospital for recurrent cough. Physical examination revealed a palpable right supraclavicular lymph node. Repeat imaging showed persistent diffuse cysts (2–14 mm) with atypical features including a 9.4-cm left upper-lobe opacity, beaded pleural-based fissural nodules, and notable heterogeneity in cyst-wall thickness (Figure 1). Serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) was elevated to 21.96 ng/mL.

Supraclavicular lymph node biopsy confirmed metastatic adenocarcinoma (Figure 2) with positive immunohistochemistry for cytokeratin 7 (CK7), thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1), and Napsin A. According to the 8th edition of the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) classification of lung cancer, clinical staging was assigned as cT4N3M1a (stage IVA) based on the large primary lesion, supraclavicular nodal involvement, and pleural-based fissural metastases. Two weeks later, targeted DNA-based next-generation sequencing (NGS; Onco1021-MRD-T panel) identified an EZR-ROS1 fusion at 19.8% variant allele frequency. The fusion was defined as an in-frame rearrangement involving exon 9 of the EZR gene and exon 33 of the ROS1 gene (Figure 3). Abdominal CT showed no renal angiomyolipoma or renal masses, and no chylous pleural effusion/ascites or other extrapulmonary manifestations suggestive of LAM were documented. Deep peripheral-blood sequencing did not detect pathogenic variants in tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) genes. Serum vascular endothelial growth factor-D (VEGF-D), a recognized diagnostic biomarker for LAM, was not measured because it is not part of routine clinical practice and is not readily available in our region. Alternative diagnoses including Birt-Hogg-Dubé syndrome, pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis, and lymphoid interstitial pneumonia were systematically excluded based on clinical, radiographic, and laboratory findings. Notably, the absence of dermatologic manifestations, negative smoking history, and atypical cystic distribution argued against these etiologies.

Consequently, crizotinib was initiated at 250 mg twice daily but required dose reduction to once daily within 1 week due to grade 3 nausea and vomiting. Response assessment was performed according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1. The left upper lobe patchy opacity was the only measurable lesion and was selected as the sole target lesion. The beaded nodules along the right fissure were classified as non‑measurable non‑target lesions due to their small size (<10 mm). The diffuse cystic lung lesions were also non‑measurable because they lacked a solid component. At 8 months, the target lesion diameter decreased by 58% (from 9.4 to 3.9 cm), exceeding the 30% threshold for partial response (PR). No new lesions appeared, and the non‑target lesions (both the beaded nodules and cystic lesions) showed no unequivocal progression (Figure 1D-1F). Concurrently, serum CEA normalized (Figure 4), and we observed qualitative improvement in cyst‑wall thickness (Figure 5). Three episodes of spontaneous pneumothorax during treatment were managed with closed thoracic drainage (Figure 4D). As of 77 months after treatment initiation, the patient maintains a PR without progression or acquired resistance (Figure 1G-1I). Discussion

To our knowledge, this case represents one of the few reported cases of EZR-ROS1 rearranged lung adenocarcinoma presenting with a LAM-like radiographic pattern and demonstrates the longest documented progression-free survival with first-generation ROS1 inhibitor therapy in this clinical context. For our patient diagnosed in 2018, next‑generation ROS1 inhibitors such as entrectinib [Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved in 2019] and repotrectinib (FDA approved in 2023) were not yet approved. Lorlatinib, although FDA-approved in 2018, was indicated primarily for ALK‑positive non‑small cell lung cancer, and high‑level evidence supporting its use in ROS1positive patients was lacking at that time. Therefore, the selection of crizotinib, which was clinically available in China at that time, represented a reasonable clinical decision consistent with the treatment era and drug accessibility. Although newer agents have since demonstrated superior efficacy in some settings, crizotinib provided durable disease control for this patient. This case underscores a diagnostic pitfall in DCLD: anchoring the diagnosis in a young woman with diffuse cysts and pneumothorax to LAM. While LAM often shows diffuse, relatively uniform, round, thin-walled cysts, an HRCT pattern alone is insufficient for a definite diagnosis without confirmatory criteria (5). Our patient had malignancy warning signs superimposed on an overall LAM-like pattern: patchy opacity, fissural beading, heterogeneous cyst-wall thickness, elevated CEA, and palpable supraclavicular lymphadenopathy, prompting biopsy of the accessible lymph node and immediate molecular testing.

A literature review has identified eleven similar cases of lung adenocarcinoma presenting as diffuse cystic disease (6-16). In terms of biological characteristics, most previously reported similar cases demonstrated superimposed solid lesions and/or lymphadenopathy (10/11). Oncogenic drivers (EGFR/ALK) were detected in 4 of 7 cases that underwent reported molecular testing. More importantly, all patients who received targeted therapy achieved a favorable outcome (alive/stable disease), underscoring the importance of early recognition of warning signs and timely molecular testing (Figure 6 and Table S1). Conversely, a recently reported case presenting with diffuse bilateral pseudo-cystic lesions harbored no actionable oncogenic driver, was treated with chemotherapy rather than targeted therapy, and presented with advanced multi-organ metastatic disease (16)—reinforcing that early molecular testing and the detection of an actionable driver are what enable the favorable, targeted-therapy outcomes seen in the other cases.

The mechanisms underlying cystic change development in lung adenocarcinoma remain incompletely understood but may involve check-valve airway obstruction, ischemic injury, or dysregulated matrix metalloproteinase activity (1). The observation that cyst-wall thickness decreased concurrently with tumor regression during targeted may reflect direct tumor-mediated pathogenesis. However, given that we cannot definitively exclude coexisting LAM, the possibility that some of the cystic lesions or wall‑thickness changes are related to LAM cannot be completely ruled out. Therefore, the observed association should be interpreted with caution, and the mechanistic inference remains speculative.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as a single-case report, the findings are inherently limited in generalizability. Second, serum VEGF-D was not measured at the time of diagnosis because this test is not part of routine clinical practice and is not readily available in our region. Therefore, the absence of serum VEGF-D testing and the lack of histologic sampling of the cystic parenchyma prevent definitive exclusion of coexisting LAM. In addition, negative peripheral-blood testing for TSC1/2 variants cannot exclude sporadic LAM because low-level mosaicism may not be detected. Third, RNA‑based NGS confirmation of the ROS1 fusion was not performed, despite its known superiority over DNA‑based NGS for detecting fusions such as ROS1, because the initial DNA‑based NGS had already detected the fusion and the patient responded well to targeted therapy. Moreover, the remaining formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor sample was exhausted at the time of this retrospective study, precluding additional RNA‑based testing. Finally, we acknowledge that the patient’s initial evaluation for presumed LAM was performed at another hospital. Consequently, we lack detailed information regarding multidisciplinary team discussion, tumor marker assessment, positron emission tomography/computed tomography, video-assisted thoracic surgery considerations, and the informed consent process at that time. This is an inherent limitation of this retrospective case report. Our institution’s evaluation began 6 months later, ultimately leading to the diagnosis of lung cancer. Despite these limitations, the absence of extrapulmonary LAM features and the long-term parallel improvement of parenchymal/fissural abnormalities and cyst-wall thickness with tumor control make LAM unlikely to be the primary driver in this patient.

Conclusions

Systematic evaluation of atypical radiographic features in presumed LAM may identify treatable oncogene-driven lung adenocarcinoma. Recognition of warning signs including focal opacities, fissural beading, and heterogeneous cyst walls should prompt expedited tissue diagnosis and molecular characterization. When oncogenic drivers are identified, targeted therapy can achieve sustained disease control exceeding conventional expectations, as demonstrated by the exceptional 77-month progression-free survival in this EZR-ROS1 positive case.
