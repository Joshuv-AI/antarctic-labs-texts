Local brain network alterations in prodromal and De Novo Parkinson’s disease: basal ganglia functional connectivity mediates the association between regional homogeneity and motor manifestations
Authors: Zhiying Guo, Jingzhe Li, Jianxia Xu, Yubing Chen, Danyan Rong, Yi Xing, Jingru Ren, Miao Yu, Weiguo Liu, Junjie Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectiveRecently, three motor phases of Parkinson’s disease (PD) have been proposed (a silent period when nigrostriatal loss begins, a prodromal motor period with subtle focal manifestations, and clinical PD) accompanying the early loss of nigrostriatal dopaminergic pathways and the dysfunction of the basal ganglia (BG) loop, which produce vital network alterations that impact motor coordination. However, the relationships among local brain abnormalities, BG-based functional connectivity (FC) and motor performance in early PD remain unclear. Thus, our study aims to characterize local brain activity dysfunction and large-scale network disorganization, and further explore the potential neural pathway from local dysrhythmia to network-level dysfunction ultimately to clinical motor deficits.MethodsWe enrolled 32 healthy controls (HC), 20 prodromal PD (pPD) patients without mild motor signs (pPD-NMMS), 42 pPD patients with MMS (pPD-MMS), and 58 de novo PD (dPD) patients. We calculated the regional homogeneity (ReHo) and FC values of BG subregions and differences among groups. Furthermore, correlation analyses between ReHo, FC values, and motor sub-scores were analyzed. Additionally, a mediation analysis was conducted to investigate the mediating function of FC-distinct brain regions between ReHo values and motor performance.ResultsCompared with the other groups, pPD-NMMS patients had increased ReHo values in the right lingual gyrus (LING). However, the pPD-MMS group had increased FC values in the left globus pallidus (GP), the bilateral ventromedial putamen (vmPu) to sensorimotor regions, and the left vmPu, the left dorsal caudate (dCa) to visual cortex regions. Both ReHo values in the right LING and FC values of the left GP and the left vmPu were correlated with tremor scores. Eventually, mediation analyses revealed that FC values between the left GP and the right postcentral gyrus (PoCG), and between the left vmPu and the right inferior occipital gyrus (IOG) mediated the relationship between ReHo values in the right LING and tremor scores.ConclusionAlong with the transition from the prodromal to the manifested period and the development of motor signs, network alterations of BG loop may play an important mediating role between local alterations in occipital-related brain regions and tremor performance.Keywords: Early Parkinson’s disease, Local brain network alterations, Basal ganglia loop, Motor manifestations

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is one of the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorders [1] which is diagnosed by crucial motor symptoms, including bradykinesia, tremor, rigidity, and postural instability [2]. Clinically manifest PD is preceded by a potentially long prodromal period [3]. Markers for prodromal PD (pPD) are still under investigation [4] (e.g., constipation, olfactory loss, and so on). The accumulation of such prodromal markers within an individual increases the likelihood of developing PD and the occurrence of motor markers, for example, mild motor signs (MMS), seems to mark a late stage in pPD [4]. Some scholars thought that the presence of MMS had been linked to lower dopamine transporter imaging and a higher risk of developing PD later [5]. However, the understanding of the neuropathological mechanisms of MMS in the current study is still insufficient. Therefore, distinguishing pPD individuals with and without MMS may help to capture different stages within the early disease continuum.

Recently, the initial evolution of PD can be divided into the following three distinct periods, depending on the severity of the motor phenotype and degeneration of the substantia nigra (SN) striatum [6]: (1) a motor silent period, in which the degeneration of the SN striatum begins but functional compensation occurs; (2) a prodromal motor period, in which there are subtle breakthroughs in local motor manifestations as a result of abnormalities in the motor network; and (3) a clinically confirmed period with the diagnostic motor symptoms. From a clinical point of view, motor features are not apparent during the motor silent phase but begin to become noticeably mild during the premotor phase. From this, it can be seen that the basal ganglia (BG) motor circuitry plays a major role in the early phase from subclinical to clinically manifest motor dysfunction [6]. Nevertheless, how BG-related regions and pathways are altered across these early stages remains unclear.

Neuroimaging provides a useful non-invasive means of addressing this question. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) detects spontaneous cerebral activity through blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signals and has become an important approach for investigating the neurobiological mechanisms of PD [7]. In general, rs-fMRI measures are broadly categorized into functional segregation and functional integration. Functional segregation focuses on regional intrinsic neural activity and includes metrics such as amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (ALFF) and regional homogeneity (ReHo), whereas functional integration emphasizes interactions among spatially distant brain regions using methods such as functional connectivity (FC), independent component analysis (ICA), functional connectivity density (FCD), and graph-theoretical analysis (GA) [8]. Previous studies have shown that ReHo reflects the local synchronization of spontaneous neural voxels, making it particularly sensitive to subtle abnormalities in local functional coordination. Importantly, local functional abnormalities may influence large-scale network dynamics and alterations in ReHo have been associated with changes in remote functional connectivity, suggesting a potential link between functional segregation and integration [9, 10]. Thus, examining both ReHo and BG-related FC values may provide complementary insight into local and network-level dysfunction in early PD.

Accumulating evidence supports the presence of both local and inter-regional functional abnormalities in early PD. At the local level, previous studies in idiopathic rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (iRBD), a high-risk population for PD, have identified abnormal spontaneous activity of ReHo in the putamen [11]. In PD, widespread ReHo abnormalities have been reported, and these alterations were associated with motor severity and were partially normalized after levodopa administration [12]. In addition, ReHo alterations appear to vary across early Hoehn and Yahr (H-Y) stages and have been linked to both disease stage and cognitive performance [13]. More recently, a meta-analysis identified robust PD-related abnormalities mainly involving the visual, somatomotor, and attentional networks, further supporting the biological relevance of ReHo alterations in PD [14]. At the inter-regional level, several rs-fMRI studies have also demonstrated altered BG-based FC in iRBD patients, carriers of PD-related gene mutations without motor manifestations, and patients with early diagnosed PD [15–18]. However, whether ReHo changes are associated with BG-related network alterations across the full spectrum of early motor PD spanning the motor silent period, the prodromal motor period, and the early clinically confirmed stage remains unclear. Notably, previous work in schizophrenia has suggested a link between regional and inter-regional connectivity deficits [19], providing a conceptual basis for examining this question in PD.

To address these gaps, we took advantage of our prospective, community-based pPD cohort, one of the largest general community cross-sectional pPD cohorts in eastern China [20]. In the present study, we included healthy controls (HC), pPD patients without MMS (pPD-NMMS), pPD patients with MMS (pPD-MMS), and de novo PD (dPD) patients to characterize alterations in ReHo and BG-based FC patterns across different stages of early PD. Moreover, we further examined whether BG-based FC mediates the association between ReHo alterations and motor dysfunction.

Methods

Participants

This study enrolled 152 subjects, including 32 HC, 62 pPD patients, and 58 dPD patients. Each subject was right-handed. Patients with pPD were recruited from a community-based prospective study performed by the Department of Neurology of the affiliated Brain Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. The community was screened for pPD using a standardized structured questionnaire. The concerned questions were about male sex, occupational solvent exposure, frequent pesticide exposure, family history, coffee or tea use, smoking status, SN hyperechogenicity, rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (RBD), constipation, olfactory loss, excessive daytime somnolence, symptomatic hypotension, urinary dysfunction, severe erectile dysfunction, and depression/anxiety. Then, two experienced neurologists examined those with possible/probable pPD. The whole screening process can be referred to in the previously published studies of our group [20–23]. Based on the latest Movement Disorder Society (MDS) research criteria, patients with pPD were identified if the probability of pPD ≥ 80%.

The Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale part III (UPDRS-III) scores, originally published in 1987, used in the pPD groups excluded items not particularly specific for parkinsonian disorders, such as action or postural tremor score [24, 25]. According to the MDS research criteria [4], possible subthreshold parkinsonism on expert examination is defined as the UPDRS-III scores > 3 excluding action tremor. So, our study classified pPD participants into two subgroups: the pPD-MMS group (UPDRS-III > 3) and the pPD-NMMS group (UPDRS-III ≤ 3). In this study, the inclusion criteria were individuals who had lived in the area for more than a year and were between the age of 50 and 80 years. The exclusion criteria were: (1) a history of Parkinson’s disease, atypical or secondary parkinsonism; (2) a history of taking anti-parkinsonism drugs; (3) Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score < 27; (4) other neurologic diseases, including cerebrovascular disorders, head injury, and encephalitis; (5) hyperthyroidism, cancer, severe psychiatric illness, or other systemic illness; (6) difficulties in finishing the clinical assessment and MRI.

Patients with dPD were recruited from the Movement Disorder Clinic at the Department of Neurology of the Brain Hospital affiliated with the Nanjing Medical University. The inclusion criteria for dPD patients were as follows: (1) The patient must be newly diagnosed with PD according to the diagnostic criteria for PD established by MDS in 2015 [26]; (2) not have been treated yet; (3) have more than a 30% improvement rate in the UPDRS-III by the standard acute levodopa challenge test; (4) have early stage PD (modified H-Y ≤ 2.5); and (5) have had a follow-up through hospital visits for at least 1 year. The exclusion criteria included: (1) a history of atypical or secondary parkinsonism; (2) MMSE score < 27; (3) other neurologic diseases, including cerebrovascular disorders, head injury, and encephalitis; (4) hyperthyroidism, cancer, severe psychiatric illness, or other systemic illness; (5) difficulties in finishing the clinical assessment and MRI.

The study was approved by the ethics committee of the Affiliated Brain Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Clinical assessments

Demographic characteristics of participants were recorded, including age, gender, education, age at onset, and duration of disease. Motor function was evaluated using the UPDRS-III scores as well as modified H-Y stages. A total of 12 questions (items 18–31) in the UPDRS-III divided PD motor symptoms into 4 different symptom categories: bradykinesia (total scores for items 18, 19, 23, 24, 25, 26, and 31), tremor (total scores for items 20 and 21), rigidity (scored for item 22), and postural instability (total scores for items 27, 28, 29, and 30) scores [27]. The MMSE was used to assess cognition. Depression was evaluated by using the 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-24). Clinical RBD symptoms were evaluated utilizing the REM sleep behavior disorder questionnaire-Hong Kong (RBDQ-HK) or Polysomnography (PSG). The Non-Motor Symptoms Questionnaire (NMSQ) was used to quantify non-motor symptoms. All the evaluations were completed before the MRI scan.

MRI imaging acquisition

A 3T MRI scanner was utilized to conduct magnetic resonance imaging (Siemens, Verio, Munich, Germany). Each subject was supine, immobilized by foam pads and a typical birdcage head coil to prevent head movement. The participants were asked to close their eyes and be silent while being awake and unconcerned. Axial anatomical images were acquired using a T1 fluid-attenuated inversion recovery sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2,530 ms; echo time (TE) = 3.34 ms; field of view (FOV) = 256 mm × 256 mm; matrix = 256 × 192; slice thickness/gap = 1.33/0.5 mm; flip angle (FA) = 7 degrees; bandwidth = 180 HZ/PX; 128 slices were used to cover the entire brain for image registration and functional localization. Functional pictures were captured in the same slice orientation using a gradient-recalled echo-planar imaging pulse sequence. In total, 240 volumes were collected. (TR = 2000 ms; TE = 30 ms; FOV = 220 mm × 220 mm; matrix = 64 × 64; thickness/gap = 3.5/0.63 mm; FA = 90 degrees; bandwidth = 2,232 HZ/PX; slice numbers = 31).

MRI imaging data pre-processing

The fMRI data were preprocessed utilizing the toolbox for Data Processing and Analysis for (Resting-State) Brain Imaging (DPABI Version 5.2, http://rfmri.org/dpabi) based on the MATLAB 2018b platform (http://www.mathworks.com/products/matlab). For magnetization equilibrium, the first 10 volumes of each functional section were removed. Then, the remaining 230 volumes were corrected for slice timing with the middle slice as a reference. The volumes were realigned for head motion correction. Next, the individual T1-weighted images were co-registered to the mean realigned functional image generated during realignment and then segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) tissue maps using a unified segmentation algorithm. Later, they were normalized into the standard Montreal Neurological Institute space with diffeomorphic anatomical registration via exponentiated Lie algebra (DARTEL). A Friston 24-parameter model was used to regress out head motion effects from the realigned data [28]. CSF, white matter, and the global signals, as well as the linear trend were also regressed as nuisance covariates. Then they were resampled to a 3 mm × 3 mm × 3 mm voxel size and smoothed using a 6 mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel [29]. Temporal band-pass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz) was applied. We were required to exclude participants having a mean framewise displacement (FD) > 0.5 mm or with a head motion exceeding a maximum translation of 3 mm or rotation of 3° to minimize the potential effects of head motion based on a previous study [30], but there were no excluded subjects in the present study. Among groups, we could not detect any appreciable variations in mean FD Jenkinson (Kruskal-Wallis H test, P = 0.949).

Regional brain function analysis

The above preprocessing steps were repeated, excluding the smoothing step. Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (KCC) [31] of the BOLD image data was calculated using DPABI software. In this way, the fMRI data of each subject could be obtained as a corresponding ReHo image. The ReHo maps were then normalized and Gaussian smoothed. The smoothed ReHo maps will be used in the next step of statistical analysis.

Seed-based functional connectivity analysis

All the above preprocessing steps were repeated. Ten subregions of the basal ganglia were selected from the Human Brainnetome Atlas [32] for this study, including the bilateral ventral caudate region (vCa), the bilateral globus pallidus region (GP), the bilateral ventromedial putamen region (vmPu), the bilateral dorsal caudate region (dCa), and the bilateral dorsolateral putamen region (dlPu) as the regions of interest (ROIs) (Fig. 1). Within each ROI, a seed reference time course was obtained. Voxel-wise FC analyses were performed on the seed reference and the entire brain for each ROI. Fisher’s r-to-z transformation helped normalize each voxel’s correlation coefficients to Z scores. A complete brain Z-score map was produced for every ROI on each subject.

Statistical analysis

The data were examined with the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) statistical software package (version 26.0). Mean and standard deviation (SD) represented the continuous demographic and clinical data. The normality assumption of data was analyzed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Among groups (HC, pPD-NMMS patients, pPD-MMS patients, and dPD patients), the differences in continuous variables with normal distributions were tested using the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). In contrast, the continuous variables with non-normal distributions were analyzed with the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Meanwhile, categorical variables were analyzed with the Chi-square test. For pairwise comparison in each analysis, the Bonferroni test was applied after the ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis H test. Two-sample t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used to determine the differences between the two group levels. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value of 0.05.

The DPABI statistics program was employed to analyze the fMRI data. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed on both ReHo and FC values to compare the differences among groups and the results of the ReHo and FC study were corrected using the Gaussian random field (GRF) theory (voxel-level p < 0.001, cluster-level p < 0.05; voxel-level p < 0.001, cluster-level p < 0.05/10) with age, gender, education, HAMD scores, mean FD Jenkinson, and gray matter volumes as covariates. The FC values of significantly affected areas were extracted using DPABI and made comparisons between groups by SPSS. The following results of ReHo and FC values were presented using Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) brain regions, but AAL labels were used solely for anatomical annotation to identify and describe the spatial localization of ROIs.

The correlations of significant ReHo and BG-based FC values were calculated in each group. Correlation analyses were performed to investigate the relationship among ReHo values, BG-based FC values, and UPDRS-III sub-scores adjusting for sex, age, education, and HAMD scores in PD groups. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

In addition, this study evaluated the mediation effects utilizing a bootstrap resampling technique in compliance with standard protocols for psychological research. In particular, as previously suggested in the literature, we created 5,000 resamples employing percentile bootstrapping [33]. This approach improves the estimation of confidence intervals for indirect effects and thus strengthens the framework for mediation analyses. The mediation analyses were conducted in the combined PD cohort, including pPD-NMMS, pPD-MMS, and dPD participants. In our model, ReHo values were specified as the independent variable, BG-based FC values as the mediating variable, and UPDRS-III scores and their sub-scores as the dependent variables, with sex, age, education, and HAMD scores included as covariates.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

In our study, thirty-two HC without a history of neurologic or psychiatric diseases were recruited and matched to pPD and dPD patients in terms of age, gender, and education. We classified the subjects into four groups: HC (n = 32), pPD-NMMS patients (n = 20), pPD-MMS patients (n = 42), and dPD patients (n = 58). The demographics and clinical characteristics were represented in Table 1. No significant differences existed in gender, age, years of education, or MMSE among groups. In PD subgroups, there were no significant differences in the NMSQ scores. However, there were significant differences in UPDRS-III, bradykinesia, tremor, rigidity, postural instability, and RBDQ-HK scores among PD subgroups. HAMD-24 scores also differed among the four groups. In our investigation, only six pPD patients (NpPD−NMMS = 5, NpPD−MMS = 3) with RBD were proven by PSG.

Functional connectivity differences among groups

Significant differences of FC values among the four groups were observed in the left globus pallidus (GP), bilateral ventromedial putamen (vmPu), and left dorsal caudate (dCa) (GRF-corrected, voxel-wise p < 0.001, cluster-wise p < 0.05/10). The pPD-MMS or dPD group both had noticeably higher FC between the left GP and the right postcentral gyrus (PoCG), the left vmPu and the right inferior occipital gyrus (IOG), the left vmPu and the right precentral gyrus (PreCG), the right vmPu and the left paracentral lobule (PCL), the left dCa and the right fusiform gyrus (FFG) than the HC or pPD-NMMS group (GRF-corrected, voxel-wise p < 0.001, cluster-wise p < 0.05/6). A visualization of the values of significant brain regions was also presented (Table 3; Fig. 3).

Associations between regional and inter-region brain functional alterations in groups

In each group, the relationship between the distinct ReHo values and FC connections was assessed. In HC, ReHo values in the right LING negatively correlated with the FC values between the left vmPu and the right IOG (r = − 0.459, p = 0.008). In the pPD-NMMS group, there was no correlation between ReHo values in the right LING and BG-based FC values. In the pPD-MMS group, ReHo values in the right LING negatively correlated with the FC values between the left GP and the right PoCG, the left dCa and the right FFG (r = − 0.400, p = 0.009; r = − 0.447, p = 0.003). In the dPD group, ReHo values in the right LING were positively correlated with FC values between the right vmPu and the left PCL (r = 0.363, p = 0.005) (Fig. 4).

Mediation analyses between ReHo alterations and motor deficits

The exploratory mediation analyses revealed that the association between ReHo values in the right LING and tremor scores was significantly mediated by the left GP-based FC values of the right PoCG (β = −0.274, 95% CI [− 0.647, − 0.015]) and the left vmPu-based FC values of the right IOG (β = −0.233, 95% CI [− 0.554, − 0.007]), with indirect effects accounting for 30.11% and 25.60% of the total effect, respectively (Fig. 6).

Discussion

The current study investigated the differences in the ReHo values and FC values of BG subregions among four groups, revealing increases significantly in the ReHo values of the right LING in the pPD-NMMS group and BG-based FC values in the pPD-MMS group. Among these findings, the ReHo values of the right LING were negatively correlated with tremor scores, and the FC values between the left GP and the right PoCG, the left vmPu and the right IOG were positively correlated with tremor scores. Moreover, we also indicated that the relationship between the ReHo values of the right LING and tremor scores may be potentially mediated by the FC values between the left GP and the right PoCG, and between the left vmPu and the right IOG. In summary, these findings suggest that local alterations in occipital-related brain areas may be associated with motor manifestations in early PD, potentially through BG-related connectivity changes.

ReHo results of this study suggested that the pPD-NMMS patients showed an increase in the right LING compared with HC, pPD-MMS patients, and dPD patients. In the pathogenesis of PD, in addition to the classical sensorimotor network, there are some non-motor networks involved, such as the visual network [34]. Previously, it has been noted in the literature that the right LING is an important component of the medial visual cortex of the brain [35], which integrates and processes visual information with the lateral visual cortical areas. At the whole brain level, through enhancement of functional activities of the visual network, the integration of multiple sensations and the regulation of posture and movement are primarily driven by visual information [36]. The present study observed that the visual cortical region showed an increase in activity already in pPD patients at the motor silent stage, speculating that a compensatory effect of this region may have occurred in ultra-early PD, whereas a decrease in the region activity was observed at the prodromal motor stage, a phenomenon that may be related to the fact that the pPD-MMS patients in our study were in a later stage of pPD closer to the clinically diagnosed stage.

Recent research indicates that mechanisms operating during the silent motor period are limited to the nigrostriatal projection [6]. Nevertheless, a more extensive motor network is impacted by compensatory and pathogenic changes during the prodromal motor phase [6]. In our investigation, the pPD-MMS group had the highest FC values of the left GP and bilateral vmPu with the sensorimotor network, including the left PCL, right PoCG, and right PreCG. In PD, its failure is pertinent to motor difficulties, including akinesia and bradykinesia [37]. As we all know, the GP is an output nucleus of the BG and works in conjunction with the striatum to integrate motor signals and output spatially aligned neurons that regulate the force of movement [38]. Whereas, the right PreCG, right PoCG, and left PCL are the main areas constituting the sensorimotor areas and the major cerebral cortex for the feedback regulation of somatic movements [39]. Of these, the PreCG participates in the formation of the brain motor centers, the PoCG is responsible for receiving somatic perceptions and sending out neural impulses, and the PCL is associated with movements and sensations in the contralateral calf and foot, all playing an important role in the cortico-BG-thalamo-cortical motor loop [40].

An intriguing finding in the present study was that the connections between the vmPu and sensorimotor areas were richer than in dlPu. This initially seems to refute the conventional thinking that earlier denervation of dopaminergic neurons in dlPu leads to the emergence of motor deficits [41]. In terms of susceptibility to dopaminergic neurodegeneration, the putamen shows clear regional differences, i.e., the dorsolateral region is more susceptible than the ventromedial region [42]. Previous literature has shown that the BG is regionally divided into three functional regions, where the dorsolateral striatum (posterior/dorsolateral putamen) is involved in sensorimotor functions and the dorsomedial striatum (caudate nucleus and anterior/ventromedial putamen) in associative functions [43, 44]. The two functional regions regulate different forms of behavior in instrumental behavioral processing, where the preferential loss of dopamine in the sensorimotor area may lead to a severe deficit in habitual control in PD patients, who may therefore be forced to progressively rely on goal-directed behaviors, which are mediated by the relatively preserved associative striatal processing in animals and humans [45, 46]. The medial ventral shell nucleus plays a crucial role in the integration and regulation of motor, higher cognitive, and limbic functions [47]. Hence, it’s possible that the substantial dlPu damage leads to striatal functional reorganization to support motor actions. Meanwhile, Schneider et al. discovered that compensatory modifications to the BG loop may be required in maintaining motor function and that the vmPu, due to its later damage, may be involved in this functional reorganization while retaining the relative functional integrity of the overall BG loop [48]. Additionally, earlier research has demonstrated that the vmPu receives dopaminergic innervation from less susceptible neurons in the medial area of the substantia nigra which is influenced only in later phases of PD [49]. In addition, Helmich et al. [50] analyzed FC in asymptomatic Leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2) gene mutation carriers (considered at high risk for PD) and found increased FC in the ventral anterior Pu (which is less affected by dopaminergic depletion) versus the parietal cortex. Thus, these further confirm the reliability of our findings that this alteration of enhanced connectivity after mild impairment of motor symptoms may be an early surrogate to adapt to neurodegenerative lesions.

In the pPD-MMS group, we also observed enhanced FC values between the left vmPu and the right IOG, as well as between the left dCa and the right FFG, relative to the HC and pPD-NMMS groups, and these FC values also showed a trend toward being higher than those in the dPD group. Among these, the right IOG and FFG both belong to the occipital lobe, and the left vmPu and left dCa are crucial elements of cortico-striatal circuits and have afferents from various parts of the brain [51]. While less often discussed in the pathophysiology of PD than the motor pathway, the occipital cortex is critical in transmitting information regarding proprioception and body placement in the environment during the generation of motion plans [52, 53]. Previous findings concern the activity of the occipital lobes in all PD patients, which could explain the ongoing desire to monitor their movements with their eyes [54]. Increased connection of the occipital lobe was also discovered in other pPD studies [15]. Thus, the enhanced connectivity of these areas could be connected to a greater reliance on visuospatial abilities and visual information processing, which could be an explanation of the above results.

While our results revealed enhanced BG-baesed FC values in the pPD-MMS group relative to the other two groups, we uncovered no evidence pointing to alterations in the reverse direction (i.e., decreases in functional connectivity in the pPD-MMS group). Similarly, pPD patients have been shown to have higher BG-cortical connections than controls, which indicates potential compensatory effects [55]. However, our study stands in contrast with some studies investigating prodromal PD by means of functional imaging, and those findings revealed reductions in BG-based FC values in the prodromal group [56]. Despite the wide variation between study settings and analysis methods, the compensatory strategy of BG found in our study still awaits further confirmation.

Based on the above results, brain regions sensitive to changes in brain function screened in local and global metrics were explored for their linkages. In each group, particularly from healthy states to the silent motor period to the prodromal motor period stage and finally to the clinically diagnosed phase, ReHo values in the right LING ranged from correlating with FC values in occipital-related brain regions involved in non-motor loops to correlating with FC values in sensorimotor brain regions involved in motor loops. In the healthy brain, ReHo values in the right LING had already correlated with FC values in occipital-related brain regions involved in BG loops. A plausible explanation for this result is that processing of visual information, for example, object processing, occurs in V2, V4, and the lateral occipital complex, especially in the right hemisphere [57]. Once motor symptoms appear, even minor motor impairments, the BG motor loops mainly in the left GP are affected by compensatory changes. When in the clinically diagnosed stage, the brain area associated with ReHo values in the BG loop shifted from the left GP to the right vmPu, and the correlation coefficient changed from negative to positive. However, after a certain stage in the progression of disease, the degenerative character would inevitably outweigh any compensatory processes that might have taken place. These findings demonstrate correlated changes between brain networks at different stages of PD.

In our mediation model, the FC values between the left GP and the right PoCG, as well as the FC values between the left vmPu and the right IOG, showed significant indirect effects on the association between ReHo values in the right LING and tremor scores. However, given the cross-sectional nature of the data, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a definitive causal pathway. Rather, they suggest that local occipital functional synchronization, BG-related FC, and tremor severity were statistically interrelated within the tested model. This interpretation is consistent with the conceptual framework of ReHo, which reflects local functional homogeneity and may be linked to remote functional connectivity and large-scale network dynamics [10]. In this context, altered local synchronization in the right LING may be associated with tremor severity partly through BG-related connectivity changes, rather than acting as a primary tremor generator. This interpretation is also biologically plausible. Visual-sensorimotor interactions are important for motor control and motor learning [58], and visual dysfunction is increasingly recognized as an early feature of PD. Patients with PD may rely more heavily on visual cues during multisensory integration, even when visual self-motion perception is impaired [59, 60]. In addition, tremor expression in PD is thought to involve not only BG dysfunction but also abnormal sensory integration during movement control [61, 62]. Helmich et al. proposed that tremor-related activity may arise in the BG and propagate through motor cortical and cerebello-thalamo-cortical circuits, thereby maintaining and amplifying tremor rhythms [63]. Within this framework, altered ReHo values in the right LING may indirectly relate to tremor severity by influencing the processing of visual information within BG-related motor circuits. This view is also in line with observations that patients with more severe tremor may show relatively preserved visuospatial performance and a slower rate of color vision decline [64, 65].

Several limitations in this study should be considered. First, this was a cross-sectional study including participants at different early phases of PD. Therefore, although the groupwise pattern may suggest stage-related differences, caution is needed when interpreting these findings in terms of disease progression or temporal relationships among local functional alterations, BG-centered connectivity changes, and motor manifestations. This caution is particularly important given recent evidence that PD may comprise distinct subtypes, each with its own imaging progression trajectory and clinical progression rate [66]. Second, it remains unknown whether prodromal participants will eventually convert to clinically established PD, and how the observed ReHo and FC alterations relate to future clinical evolution. Longitudinal studies with repeated clinical, motor, and neuroimaging assessments are therefore warranted. Third, the UPDRS-III scores are based on subjective clinical judgment rather than objective movement quantification. Future studies should explore more sensitive and reliable tools for detecting subtle motor changes, such as three-dimensional motion analysis techniques [67]. In this regard, future work should leverage recent advances in MRI-based intelligent methods to translate neuroimaging markers into clinical tools for early PD identification [68].

By and large, our findings suggest that, during the transition from the silent motor period to the prodromal motor phase and ultimately to clinically manifest PD, regional functional alterations in occipital-related brain areas may contribute to tremor manifestations partly through their effects on BG-related functional circuits. Although long-term longitudinal follow-up is still required, this pattern of brain network alteration may represent a candidate neuroimaging marker for detecting early network changes relevant to disease evolution and for guiding future longitudinal studies of neuroprotective interventions.
