Prevalence and correlates of cardiovascular prevention gaps among adults with cardio-kidney-metabolic risk: A hospital-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Nawsherwan, Tanzeel Yousaf, Sardar Ali, Musa Bin Bashir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular prevention gaps may arise when adults with cardio-kidney-metabolic (CKM) risk have inadequate risk awareness, screening, or preventive-care engagement. Evidence integrating clinical risk burden with digital and artificial-intelligence (AI) health-literacy indicators is limited. We assessed the prevalence and correlates of cardiovascular prevention gaps in a hospital-based sample of Chinese adults, hypothesizing that gaps would be common and would cluster with greater CKM burden.MethodsIn this hospital-based analytical cross-sectional study, 542 adults attending Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University (January–April 2026) were recruited by convenience sampling from 700 invited (563 returned or initiated the survey; 21 incomplete responses excluded). The primary outcome, cardiovascular prevention gap, required at least one cardiovascular/CKM risk indicator together with at least one prevention-related deficit in risk awareness, recent screening, perceived risk, regular-care access, or care barriers. Characteristics were compared by gap status. A non-circular multivariable logistic-regression model excluded the CVD risk-factor count, regular-clinic access, and cost barrier; because current smoking showed near-complete separation, the model was estimated with Firth penalisation. Analyses were exploratory.ResultsOverall, 377 of 542 participants had a cardiovascular prevention gap (prevalence 69.6%). The most frequent components were lack of lipid-status awareness (55.5%), no regular clinic access (52.0%), and no lipid screening within 12 months (50.2%). Participants with gaps were older (41.4 vs. 36.0 years; P < 0.001), more often male (53.8% vs. 41.2%) and current smokers (25.2% vs. 1.8%), and had higher CKM burden (high CKM risk 82.2% vs. 15.2%). In the adjusted non-circular model, older age was independently associated with prevention gap (adjusted OR 1.04 per year, 95% CI 1.03–1.07). Current smoking showed a strong apparent association (Firth-penalized OR 20.9, 95% CI 7.1–62.1) that largely reflected its role in the risk-eligibility definition and attenuated when smoking was excluded as a qualifying risk factor. eHealth literacy, AI health-literacy, digital-health use, and other sociodemographic and healthcare-access factors were not independently associated.ConclusionsCardiovascular prevention gaps were common and clustered with adverse CKM profiles and were concentrated among older adults. These exploratory, single-center findings support integrated prevention that combines risk awareness, routine screening, accessible preventive care, and CKM risk identification, with particular attention to older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28496-6.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death worldwide and a growing burden in China, where population ageing, urbanization, and lifestyle transition have increased the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, obesity, and smoking [1, 2]. In China, CVD has consistently ranked as the leading cause of death and continues to rise alongside a large and accumulating risk-factor burden [3]. Many of these risk factors develop silently before overt disease, creating an important window for early screening, risk communication, and preventive intervention. Yet persistent shortfalls in awareness, treatment, and control of major cardiometabolic risk factors indicate that available preventive strategies are not consistently translated into preventive action among adults at risk [4, 5].

Contemporary prevention frameworks increasingly recognize the interconnection of metabolic, renal, and cardiovascular health. The cardio-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome construct describes the clustering of adiposity, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and CVD within shared biological pathways [6]. Kidney-risk markers, including reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate and albuminuria, frequently coexist with these conditions and predict adverse cardiovascular outcomes [7]. Multidimensional cardiovascular-health metrics, such as the American Heart Association’s Life’s Essential 8, similarly emphasize the combined contribution of behavioral and biological factors [8]. Prevention efforts focused on isolated risk factors may therefore overlook adults with broader CKM vulnerability.

A central public-health problem is the mismatch between measurable cardiovascular or CKM risk and engagement with preventive care. We conceptualize this mismatch as a cardiovascular prevention gap: the co-occurrence, in an individual who already has cardiovascular or CKM risk, of one or more prevention-related deficits, inadequate risk awareness, absent or delayed screening, low perceived risk, limited access to regular care, or barriers to preventive services. Although prevention guidelines emphasize comprehensive risk assessment, blood-pressure and lipid management, diabetes prevention, kidney-risk evaluation, and equitable access to preventive care [9], adults with accumulating risk in real-world settings may remain unaware of their status or may not convert awareness into action. Considering these deficits together, rather than in isolation, may be better captured where prevention pathways break down and where intervention is most needed.

Digital and artificial-intelligence (AI) health technologies are increasingly proposed to support cardiovascular prevention through self-monitoring, risk education, and chronic-disease management [10]. China has one of the world’s largest digital-health and mobile-health user populations, and online health services and AI-enabled health information are being adopted rapidly [11, 12]. However, the benefits of digital information depend on eHealth literacy the capacity to seek, understand, appraise, and apply electronic health information [13, 14] and AI-based tools place additional demands on users’ ability to interpret and appropriately trust algorithmic health advice [15]. Where access, literacy, and trust are unevenly distributed, such tools may widen rather than narrow prevention inequalities. Examining digital-health use, eHealth literacy, and AI-related health-literacy indicators alongside clinical risk burden may therefore clarify who is being left behind in cardiovascular prevention.

Previous studies have largely examined cardiometabolic risk factors, healthcare access, health literacy, and digital-health use as separate domains. Fewer have integrated these factors within a single prevention-gap framework that simultaneously considers CKM risk burden, cardiovascular-health status, screening and awareness, healthcare-access barriers, lifestyle and psychosocial factors, eHealth literacy, and AI health-literacy indicators particularly in hospital-based Chinese populations. Such integration matters because prevention gaps are unlikely to reflect biological risk alone; they may instead arise from the convergence of clinical risk, social context, healthcare access, health behavior, and information-related capacity.

We therefore conducted a hospital-based analytical cross-sectional study to assess the prevalence and correlates of cardiovascular prevention gaps among adults attending a tertiary hospital in China. We hypothesized a prior that cardiovascular prevention gaps would be common and would cluster with greater CKM risk burden and less favorable cardiometabolic profiles. Because evidence on the non-clinical correlates of prevention gaps is limited, we additionally examined on an exploratory basis, without prespecified directional hypotheses sociodemographic, lifestyle, psychosocial, healthcare-access, digital-health, eHealth-literacy, and AI health-literacy factors in relation to prevention-gap status. By characterizing prevention gaps across clinical and non-clinical domains, the study aims to inform risk-stratified and equity-oriented cardiovascular prevention.

Methods

Study design, setting, and participants

This was a hospital-based cross-sectional study, reported in line with the STROBE guideline [16]. It was carried out at Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University, a tertiary teaching hospital in Wuhan, China, from January to April 2026. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older attending the hospital’s health management (medical check-up) centre and general outpatient clinics; to take part, a person had to be able to give informed consent and complete the questionnaire. People who declined to take part, were under 18 years, or did not provide enough information to determine the primary outcome were excluded. This setting was chosen because attendees routinely have their height and weight measured, their blood pressure taken, and fasting blood samples drawn. As a result, the measurements needed to assign a cardio-kidney-metabolic (CKM) risk stage were collected in the same way for everyone, which kept missing data to a minimum.

Recruitment used convenience sampling: during clinic hours, trained research assistants approached eligible adults who were available and willing to take part. After giving written informed consent, each participant completed the questionnaire and a clinical assessment of body measurements, blood pressure, and fasting blood tests at the same visit. Of 700 eligible adults invited, 563 started or returned the survey (80.4%); after 21 incomplete responses were removed, 542 remained for analysis (77.4% of those invited). No target sample size was calculated in advance; the study included all eligible adults recruited during the fixed study period, so the regression analyses should be regarded as exploratory. Because participants came from a single hospital and were recruited by convenience sampling rather than random selection, the findings may be affected by selection and volunteer bias.

Questionnaire and measures

The questionnaire covered sociodemographic details, healthcare access, cardiovascular screening history and risk awareness, lifestyle and psychosocial factors, digital-health use, and artificial-intelligence (AI)-related health-literacy. The full questionnaire is provided as an Additional file. Where possible, established scales were used: the eHealth Literacy Scale (eHEALS) [17], the short-form International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-SF) [18], the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) [19], and sleep items based on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [20]. AI health-literacy, AI-information use, and trust in AI health advice were measured with study-specific questions, because no validated scale was available; these results are reported descriptively and interpreted with caution. Item-level responses for some scales were not retained, so internal-consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α) could not be calculated in this sample, although the published scales have established reliability and validity.

Clinical and laboratory measures included body measurements, blood pressure, self-reported cardiometabolic history, fasting glucose, HbA1c, a lipid profile, creatinine, and the urine albumin-creatinine ratio (ACR). The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation [21]. Derived indicators for example, obesity, elevated blood pressure, and reduced eGFR were defined using prespecified cut-offs, listed in Supplementary Table S1 [22, 23]. Each cardiometabolic risk indicator counted both conditions already diagnosed (from self-reported history) and abnormalities first detected at the study visit (from the measurements); the split between diagnosed and newly detected cases is reported descriptively. CKM stage was assigned from 0 to 4 using criteria adapted from the American Heart Association CKM advisory [6], with high CKM risk defined as stage 2 or higher. A cardiovascular health score (0–100, where higher is better) was derived from the Life’s Essential 8 framework [8]. To keep the data internally consistent, all derived indicators, CKM stages, the cardiovascular health score, and the risk-factor count were recalculated directly from the original measurements.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was the cardiovascular prevention gap, defined as a binary (present or absent) variable. A participant was classified as having a prevention gap if they had at least one cardiovascular or CKM risk indicator together with at least one prevention-related deficit. The deficits were: not knowing one’s own blood-pressure, glucose, or lipid status; no blood-pressure, glucose, or lipid screening in the past 12 months; no regular clinic or usual source of care; perceiving one’s own cardiovascular risk as low; and avoiding or delaying care because of cost. These deficits were not mutually exclusive, so a participant could have more than one. Each deficit was given equal weight, because there is no agreed basis for weighting them differently and a simple unweighted count is transparent and reproducible. To confirm that the findings did not depend on this choice, the analysis was repeated using stricter definitions (requiring at least 1, 2, or 3 deficits), and the number of deficits per participant was examined. Secondary outcomes included CKM stage, high CKM risk, the cardiovascular health score, and individual awareness and screening indicators.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were done in R version 4.4.2. Continuous variables were summarized as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as numbers and percentages. Participants with and without a prevention gap were compared using Welch’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors independently associated with the prevention gap. Because cardiovascular and CKM risk helped define the outcome, CKM stage, high CKM risk, and the risk-factor count were treated as descriptive only, not as predictors; to avoid this overlap, the risk-factor count, regular-clinic access, and cost barrier to care were left out of the model. Current smoking which is itself one of the qualifying risk factors was present in almost all participants with a prevention gap, causing near-complete separation. The model was therefore fitted using Firth’s penalised likelihood [24], which gives stable estimates in this situation; standard (unpenalised) estimates are shown alongside for transparency, and the penalized estimates are treated as the main results. The smaller outcome group provided only about seven participants per variable in the model, below the usual rule of ten, so the regression should be read as exploratory and hypothesis-generating; collinearity was acceptable (highest variance inflation factor 2.4). In a further sensitivity analysis, the smoking association was re-estimated after removing current smoking as a qualifying risk factor, to assess how much of the association simply reflected the way the outcome was built. Subgroup prevalence is reported with Wilson 95% confidence intervals. Because no data was missing for the outcome or the model variables, the complete-case analysis included all 542 participants (Supplementary Table S3).

Results

Participants and prevalence of the prevention gap

Of 700 eligible adults invited, 563 returned or started the survey (80.4%); after 21 incomplete responses were removed, 542 were analyzed (77.4% of those invited and 96.3% of returned surveys). The participant flow is shown in Supplementary Figure S1. Overall, 377 of 542 participants had a cardiovascular prevention gap a prevalence of 69.6% (95% CI 65.6–73.3) and the remaining 165 (30.4%) had no gap. Components of the prevention gap

The individual prevention-gap components are shown in Fig. 2, with their definitions in Supplementary Table S2. The three most common were lack of lipid-status awareness (301 participants, 55.5%), no regular clinic or usual source of care (282, 52.0%), and no lipid screening in the previous 12 months (272, 50.2%). These were followed by lack of glucose-status awareness (245, 45.2%), no blood-pressure screening (243, 44.8%), no glucose screening (241, 44.5%), lack of blood-pressure-status awareness (233, 43.0%), low perceived cardiovascular risk (229, 42.3%), and cost barrier to care (150, 27.7%).

Most participants had several deficits at once (Supplementary Figure S3): 220 (40.6%) had five or more, and only 16 (3.0%) had none. The prevalence was robust to the threshold used to define a gap: requiring at least two deficits gave a prevalence of 62.0% (336/542), and at least three gave 50.6% (274/542).

Baseline sociodemographic, lifestyle, and psychosocial characteristics

Baseline characteristics by gap status are shown in Table 1. Participants with a prevention gap were older than those without (mean 41.4 vs. 36.0 years; P < 0.001), more often male (53.8% vs. 41.2%; P = 0.009), more often in the lowest income group (36.6% vs. 25.5%; P = 0.039), and far more often current smokers (25.2% vs. 1.8%; P < 0.001). Health insurance, residence, education, employment, physical activity, sleep, and perceived stress did not differ significantly between the groups.

Cardiometabolic and cardio-kidney-metabolic profile

The cardiometabolic and CKM profile is shown in Table 2. Because CKM and cardiovascular risk contributed to the outcome definition, these comparisons are descriptive. Participants with a prevention gap had a consistently less favorable profile: higher body mass index (26.4 vs. 24.6 kg/m²; P < 0.001) with more obesity (16.7% vs. 2.4%); more central obesity (49.3% vs. 30.9%; P < 0.001); and more elevated measured blood pressure (37.7% vs. 6.1%; P < 0.001). They also more often had elevated LDL-C (28.9% vs. 3.6%), elevated triglycerides (46.9% vs. 27.3%), and albuminuria (17.8% vs. 1.2%; all P < 0.001), and a lower mean eGFR (101.2 vs. 109.1 mL/min/1.73 m²; P < 0.001).

Adjusted non-circular correlates of the prevention gap

The adjusted non-circular model is shown in Fig. 4, with full estimates in Supplementary Table S4. After adjustment, older age was independently associated with the prevention gap (adjusted OR 1.04 per year, 95% CI 1.03–1.07; P < 0.001). Current smoking showed a very large apparent association (Firth-penalised OR 20.93, 95% CI 7.05–62.15; standard OR 26.89, 8.03–89.96; P < 0.001), reflecting near-complete separation 95 of 98 current smokers had a prevention gap. Because current smoking is itself one of the qualifying risk factors, this association is partly built into the outcome: 30 of the 98 smokers qualified as at-risk only because they smoked. When current smoking was removed as a qualifying risk factor (sensitivity analysis), the separation disappeared and the prevalence among current smokers (65/98, 66%) became essentially the same as among non-smokers (282/444, 64%), confirming that the strong smoking association was largely definitional. No other sociodemographic, healthcare-access, lifestyle, psychosocial, digital-health, or AI-literacy variable was independently associated with the prevention gap. The model included about seven participants in the smaller outcome group per parameter, and multicollinearity was acceptable (maximum variance inflation factor 2.4).

Subgroup prevalence

Subgroup prevalence estimates with Wilson 95% confidence intervals are shown in Supplementary Figure S2. Prevention-gap prevalence was higher in men than women (74.9% vs. 64.2%), in the lowest income group than in higher groups (76.7% vs. 66.4% and 65.5%), and in uninsured than insured participants (74.0% vs. 67.5%). It was similar across residence and education, and slightly lower among users of health apps (67.8% vs. 71.5%) and AI health information (66.5% vs. 72.1%). These estimates are descriptive and were not adjusted.

Discussion

In this hospital-based cross-sectional study of 542 Chinese adults, a cardiovascular prevention gap was common, affecting 69.6% of participants. The most frequent shortfalls were not knowing one’s lipid status, having no regular source of care, and not having had lipid screening in the past year. Prevention gaps clustered with less favourable cardiometabolic and cardio-kidney-metabolic (CKM) profiles, including higher adiposity, blood pressure, glucose and lipid levels, kidney-risk markers, and lower cardiovascular health scores. In the adjusted non-circular model, older age was the one variable independently associated with the prevention gap; the strong apparent association with current smoking largely reflected how the outcome was defined, and digital-health and artificial-intelligence (AI) health-literacy indicators were not independently associated.

The high prevalence of awareness and screening shortfalls is consistent with the well-documented gaps in the awareness, treatment, and control of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidaemia, both globally and in China [3–5]. Many cardiometabolic risk factors develop silently, so these shortfalls represent missed opportunities to intervene before overt cardiovascular disease [1, 2]. That lipid-related shortfalls were the most prominent points to lipid awareness and screening as particularly under-addressed in routine care in this setting.

Prevention gaps were concentrated among participants with the highest clinical risk. This fits the CKM framework, which recognises that adiposity, dysglycaemia, dyslipidaemia, hypertension, and kidney disease share biological pathways and tend to cluster [6], and it supports integrated prevention rather than the management of single risk factors in isolation [6, 8]. Because CKM burden contributed to the definition of the outcome, however, this clustering is descriptive and should not be read as an independent association.

Older age was the only robust, non-circular correlate of the prevention gap (adjusted OR 1.04 per year, 95% CI 1.03–1.07). Older adults accumulate more cardiometabolic risk yet may not translate that risk into timely screening and preventive care. In practical terms, this identifies older adults as a priority group for case-finding, risk communication, and linkage to regular care, although the exploratory design means the finding should be confirmed in larger studies.

The very large apparent association with current smoking requires careful interpretation. Current smoking is itself one of the risk factors that qualifies a person as “at risk,” and being at risk is half of the prevention-gap definition. A current smoker therefore meets the risk criterion automatically and becomes a gap case as soon as they have any screening or awareness shortfall. When smoking was removed as a qualifying risk factor in a sensitivity analysis, the near-complete separation disappeared and the prevalence among smokers became almost identical to that among non-smokers. The strong smoking odds ratio is thus largely an artefact of the outcome construction rather than a clean epidemiological signal, and we report it transparently with this caveat [24].

Digital-health use and eHealth and AI health-literacy indicators were not independently associated with the prevention gap. This should not be taken as evidence that health literacy is unimportant, since health literacy is consistently linked to health outcomes and engagement with care [14, 25, 26]. Rather, the benefit of digital tools and literacy may depend on conditions that were not captured here, such as affordable access, trust, and connection to structured follow-up. Digital health can support self-monitoring and risk education [10], but it can also widen inequalities when access and skills are unevenly distributed [27], and AI-enabled tools raise further questions of transparency, bias, and governance [15, 28]. In a setting with high but uneven digital engagement [11, 12], digital and AI tools are therefore best embedded within broader prevention systems rather than used alone.

Although income and insurance were not independently associated after adjustment, prevention gaps were descriptively more common among low-income and uninsured participants, in keeping with the social determinants of health, which shape prevention opportunities through income, insurance, and continuity of care [29]. These patterns, together with the clustering of gaps among higher-risk adults, suggest that prevention programmes should not stop at identifying elevated clinical risk; they should also check whether individuals know their risk status, have had recent blood-pressure, glucose, and lipid screening, and are linked to a regular source of care. Given the rising burden of obesity and excess adiposity [30], bundled approaches that combine screening, risk communication, referral, and follow-up as promoted by structured primary-care programmes such as WHO HEARTS and guideline-based risk assessment [9, 31–33] are likely to be more effective than interventions targeting single behaviours or single risk factors, and should attend to affordability, coverage, and accessible preventive pathways.

This study has several strengths. It used an integrated framework spanning sociodemographic, lifestyle, psychosocial, healthcare-access, digital-health, and CKM domains; all derived indicators were recomputed directly from the source measurements; and a non-circular model with Firth penalisation and prespecified sensitivity analyses was used to separate clinical-risk clustering from genuinely independent correlates.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design cannot establish temporal sequence or causality. Second, recruitment used non-probability convenience sampling at a single tertiary hospital, so the findings may not generalize to the wider population and are subject to selection and volunteer bias. Third, self-reported awareness, screening, and digital-health measures may be affected by recall and reporting bias. Fourth, several questionnaire items, including the AI health-literacy measures, were study-specific and not externally validated, and item-level data were not retained, so internal-consistency reliability could not be assessed. Fifth, the outcome was a composite whose components differ in clinical meaning, and equal weighting is a simplification, although the prevalence was robust across alternative thresholds. Finally, the smaller outcome group provided only about seven participants per model parameter, so the regression was underpowered and exploratory; wide confidence intervals and residual confounding are possible, and the null findings for digital and AI literacy should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

In this hospital-based sample of adults with cardio-kidney-metabolic risk, cardiovascular prevention gaps were common and clustered with adverse cardiometabolic and CKM profiles. Older age was the one independent, non-circular correlate; the strong association with current smoking was largely a consequence of the outcome definition; and digital-health and AI health-literacy indicators were not independently associated. These exploratory, single-centre findings support integrated, risk-stratified, and equity-oriented prevention that combines CKM risk identification, risk communication, routine screening, and accessible preventive care, with particular attention to older adults.
