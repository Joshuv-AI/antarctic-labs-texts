Stroke Caregiver Needs in a Population‐Based Study
Authors: Roshanak Mehdipanah, Lewis B Morgenstern, Madeline Kwicklis, Melinda A Smith, Erin Case, Lynda D Lisabeth
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMost individuals who have experienced a stroke require comprehensive rehabilitation beyond initial medical care, with informal, unpaid caregivers playing a crucial role in aiding recovery and daily activities. Caregivers face significant burdens due to unmet care needs of their care recipients. This study examines the community needs of individuals who have experienced a stroke and their caregivers, while also exploring their awareness and use of existing services in Nueces County, Texas.MethodsThe study used concept mapping and interviews. Concept mapping, a mixed‐method approach, collected qualitative input on perceived needs by a subsample of caregivers from the parent study. Participants then sorted and rated perceived needs. Using this information, multidimensional scaling and cluster analyses mapped relationships between perceived needs and determined their importance and priority. Interviews were completed with all caregivers in the parent study. Responses were analyzed for ethnic differences when possible.ResultsThe following 5 clusters were derived from the concept mapping (N=16), listed in order of highest ratings of importance: medical care needs, financial and legal resource needs, caregiver support needs, daily care needs, and housing and transportation needs. Interview findings (N=228) showed that both Mexican American (n=168) and non‐Hispanic White (n=60) caregivers had low awareness and use rates for services like caregiver support and senior centers, with significant ethnic disparities in stroke information awareness that disappeared upon adjustment.ConclusionsBy understanding the needs of caregivers and individuals affected by stroke, we can better support caregivers in managing the condition and help them thrive in this role.Keywords: ethnicity, health disparities, informal caregivers, stroke needsSubject Categories: Ischemic Stroke

The needs of individuals who have experienced a stroke are complex and extend beyond the acute phase of initial care to include physical, social, cognitive, and emotional needs. 1 , 2 Once individuals have received intensive medical treatment to stabilize their conditions and prevent further complications, individuals after a stroke may begin a rehabilitation with the goal of restoring lost function and adapting to their new conditions. 3 During this period, the role of caregivers is crucial in addressing the changing needs of these individuals. In the United States, most of these caregivers are unpaid and considered to be informal. 4 These individuals tend to be family, friends, or neighbors who often assist with activities of daily living, such as bathing, meal preparation, and medication management, as well as providing emotional support, often without receiving financial compensation for their aid. Unpaid caregivers play a critical role in the health care system by assisting individuals in need of care to remain at home and maintain their quality of life. 5

Unpaid caregiving has been associated with caregiver strain or burden, impacting the caregiver's mental and physical well‐being. 6 Some studies have also linked intensive and demanding caregiving to poor physical health among ethnic minority caregivers, including Hispanic individuals. 7 , 8 Hispanic caregivers also tend to be younger than non‐Hispanic White (NHW) caregivers, limiting the education and employment opportunities of mostly women caregivers. 9 , 10

Regardless of ethnic differences in stroke outcomes and caregiver strain, studies have shown that individuals who have experienced a stroke and their caregivers continue to describe unmet needs in various areas including medical treatment, information, and support services. 11 These effects can be further exacerbated due to the existing ethnic disparities that exist in the health care system. 12 , 13 , 14 To address these unmet needs, more evidence is needed to understand the community needs of individuals who have experienced a stroke and their caregivers and to explore whether there exist unique needs by ethnicity.

This study examines the community needs of individuals who have experienced a stroke and their caregivers in Nueces County, Texas. We focus on this county as it is home to one of the largest Mexican American (MA) populations in the United States, where ≈61% of residents identify as MA, of whom the majority are US born (92%). 15

METHODS

This study forms part of the BASIC (Basic Attack Surveillance in Corpus Christi) project in Nueces County. 16 The BASIC project is an ongoing study that investigates cerebrovascular events, including ischemic stroke and intracerebral hemorrhage, among residents of Nueces County, using both active and passive surveillance techniques. Data were collected during October 2019 to November 2021 in Nueces County.

To examine the needs of community‐residing individuals who have experienced a stroke and their unpaid caregivers (hereafter, “caregiver”), this study used a multiphase mixed‐method design that integrates qualitative (concept mapping [CM]) and quantitative data (closed questions on interview), to help better understand individuals' needs. 17 CM is a mixed‐method approach that merges qualitative data collection with quantitative analysis to generate visual representation of shared dimensions of needs as perceived by caregivers. 18 Quantitative data obtained through interviews helped validate and quantify findings from the CM, while allowing the exploration of ethnic differences. This integrated approach ensured a comprehensive understanding of the diverse and complex needs of stroke survivors and their caregivers, contributing to both in‐depth insights and broader applicability for intervention and support programs. Approval for this study was obtained from the University of Michigan and the institutional review boards of the participating hospitals. Anonymized raw data may be available on the basis of appropriate requests and existing institutional review board approvals and data‐sharing agreements in place. Interested researchers should contact the research team.

Study Sample

Caregivers are identified as those primarily responsible for aiding patients with stroke in the community, with comprehensive methods outlined in previous publications. 16 During the baseline interview, participants in the BASIC study were asked to identify their main caregiver if care was required. Caregivers had to meet certain criteria: They had to be aged at least 18 years, residents of Nueces County, not experiencing cognitive impairment (with a score of at least 4 on a 6‐item cognitive screening tool), and not employed by a formal caregiving agency early. Once eligibility criteria were met, caregivers were invited to participate. After 90 days, they were asked whether they had provided informal care during the period, and if they had, they were then enrolled in the study. If a new primary caregiver emerged within 90 days who had not previously been enrolled, this new individual was recruited. MA caregivers were characterized as individuals who identified themselves as White, American Indian, belonging to none/other racial categories or not recorded/missing, and Hispanic ethnicity. NHW caregivers were defined as those who identified themselves as White race and non‐Hispanic ethnicity. All other participants were excluded due to their small representation in the sample. All participants provided informed consent.

Data Collection

Concept Mapping

Participants who had cared for an individual who had experienced a stroke within the past year were contacted via phone and invited to participate. Those who expressed interest were invited to participate in the sessions. CM consists of 2 sessions with participants, generation of statements and structuring of statements, where data are collected. 18 The first session, generation of statements, included an introduction to the study and a brainstorming session completed in the form of a focus group. Sessions were held over 2 days to accommodate participant schedules and lasted between 1 and 1.5 hours each. The brainstorming session consisted of only 1 focus question asked of all participants: What are some of the needs of stroke survivors and people that help take care of them in Nueces County? The term needs was further defined to encompass medical, financial, social, or cultural requirements. At the end of each session, the research team reviewed the responses, making edits and grouping similar statements together for a definite list of statements for the second session.

As part of the second session, structuring of statements, participants were asked to participate in 2 tasks: sorting and rating. This session occurred ≈6 weeks following the initial session and took place online using the Concept Systems GroupWisdom website. During the sorting task, participants were tasked with categorizing statements into thematic piles they identified. In the task, participants were presented with statements and asked to assess their importance and priority for action using a 5‐point Likert‐type scale. This session lasted between 1.5 and 2 hours. Due to the adaptable nature of CM, additional participants were incorporated into the study during the second session. This technique has been used in other studies, and to minimize potential bias, participants were asked if additional needs were not represented in their piles. 19 , 20 This inclusion was necessary as some caregivers could not attend the initial sessions due to scheduling conflicts.

Interviews

During the 90‐day poststroke interviews, caregivers were asked to indicate whether they were aware of programs addressing various needs caregivers and people recovering from a stroke may have, including respite care, in‐home support, and information on stroke. These programs were based on an asset mapping activity that was completed to examine existing resources for stroke survivors and their caregivers within Nueces County. If the caregiver answered yes to being aware of such programs, they were asked to name the program and whether they had ever used them for the participant who has experienced the stroke.

Demographic Data

Demographic data for both participants and caregivers were also obtained from prior data collected at baseline as part of the larger study. These included characteristics such as the age and sex of both the stroke survivor and their caregiver, caregiver education level and employment status, stroke survivor insurance coverage, prestroke function assessed using the modified Rankin Scale, prestroke cognitive status measured by the Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly, functional outcomes at 90 days based on activities of daily living and instrumental activities of daily living, initial National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, caregiving duration, presence of other caregivers (paid or unpaid), and whether the stroke survivor and caregiver lived together.

Statistical Analysis

The analysis for the CM was conducted using the same online platform Concept Systems GroupWisdom as the one used for data collection in session 2. Using the sorting data from the groups, a similarity index was generated and used in nonmetric multidimensional scaling, a multivariate analysis technique, to visually depict the degrees of similarity and relationships among the statements. Subsequently, hierarchical cluster analysis was used to separate the map into clusters representing distinct themes. The determination of the final number of clusters was based on the alignment between cluster content and themes, ensuring the avoidance of irrelevant groupings. Rating data were used to compute the average importance and priority ratings for each statement. After defining the cluster groups, average rankings were calculated by cluster and used to generate cluster rating maps. These maps depicted clusters, with layers indicating the average importance and priority ratings for each group. The presence of more layers within a cluster suggested a higher perceived importance or priority. Titles for the clusters were derived from descriptive labels provided by participants during the sorting activity.

Interview results were reported by ethnicity for awareness and use of each type of resource identified addressing a need. Tests of the bivariate association between awareness/use and ethnicity were performed using χ2 tests. Variables with counts <5 in the smallest cell were not tested a priori due to power and distributional assumption concerns, resulting in a total of 22 tests. To account for multiple testing, we used the Hochberg method to control the Family‐Wise Error Rate.

RESULTS

A summary of characteristics of caregivers and their care recipients is provided for both the CM and interview portions of the study.

A total of 16 caregivers participated in the CM portion of the study (56.2% MA and 43.8% NHW), and 228 caregivers participated in the interview (74% MA and 26% NHW). Within the CM participants, the average age was 66 years, mostly women (69%), and mostly a spouse of the care recipient (50%). Most participants had more than a high school diploma (81%) and earned >$50 000. Half of the participants were retired, while most of the other half (44%) were employed full time, and most lived with the care recipient (63%) and on average provided 90 days of care (Table 1).

MA caregivers who completed the interview were on average younger (55 years) compared with NHW caregivers (58 years), while caregivers were more likely to be women for both groups. Caregivers tended to be children (42% among MA caregivers and 30% among NHW caregivers) or spouses (39% in MA caregivers and 53% among NHW caregivers). NHW caregivers tended to have higher education levels (70% with more than high school education compared with 44% in MA caregivers), earn >$50 000 a year (52% compared with 23% in MA caregivers). MA caregivers tended to be employed full‐time (41%) or unemployed/on leave of absence (29%), while NHW caregivers were mostly retired (38%) or employed full‐time (30%). Both caregiver groups were mostly living with their care recipients and had spent an average of 95 days (MA caregivers) and 92 days (NHW caregivers) of caregiving.

Compared with the interview sample, on average CM caregivers were caring for slightly older adults (73 years compared with 66 years). Care recipients in the CM sample also had higher numbers of individuals with a prestroke modified Rankin Scale score of 4 to 5 (38% compared with 18% in the total sample), while there were significantly more MA care recipients (22%) with a level 4 to 5 prestroke modified Rankin Scale score compared with NHW care recipients (8%). This indicates that the care recipient had moderate to severe disability before the stroke. Following the stroke, care recipients in the CM group tended to have a higher National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, indicating a more moderate to severe stroke compared with those in the total sample who on average had a minor stroke on the basis of National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score interpretations. 21 All caregivers in the CM sample reported their care recipient having health insurance while 24% of the MA care recipients and 15% of the NHW care recipients within the interview sample had no health insurance coverage.

Among all items, both MA and NHW caregivers were most aware of rehabilitation services (75.6% MA caregivers and 81.7% NHW caregivers), while MA caregivers were least aware of online caregiver support programs (4.8%) and NHW caregivers were least aware of caregiver training programs (5.0%). “Information on stroke” awareness showed significant ethnic differences (95% CI, −32.5 to −3.9; raw P value=0.009), with only 27% of MA caregivers having awareness compared with 45% among NHW caregivers. However, when corrected for multiple testing, it was no longer significant (Family‐Wise Error Rate=0.202). Looking at items that caregivers were able to name among those they were aware of, MA caregivers were mostly able to name services for each item, except for “online caregiver support” with 25% naming a service, “senior center or day care” with 30% naming a service, and respite care with 35% naming the service. NHW caregivers were also mostly able to name services except for “respite care,” with 0% naming a service, and “senior center or day care,” with 38% naming a service. When MA caregivers were asked about services they were aware of, “legal counseling” and “senior center or day care” were among the least used, with only 16% and 13% of caregivers using them, respectively. Among services NHW caregivers were aware of, “senior center or day care” was only used by 13% of caregivers, while “respite care” was also used by 13% of them. Sample sizes for named and used services did not lend for the exploration of ethnic differences.

DISCUSSION

This study used multiple methods to examine the needs of caregivers and stroke survivors in Nueces County. The CM findings identified multiple needs across 5 themes, including medical care, daily care, caregiver support, financial and legal resources, and housing and transportation needs. We also interviewed participants about existing resources by asking them about their awareness and use of them. Where possible, we explored ethnic differences in the awareness of caregivers. Below, we examine some of the identified needs and the existing gap in access to services that address those needs.

Medical Care Needs

The medical care needs cluster was rated as the most important and highest priority by caregivers. This included more information and awareness about strokes, in addition to medical and treatment support following a stroke. During the sessions, caregivers stressed how hard it was to coordinate care across the multiple medical teams involved in the care of their care recipient, and the lack of attention they received from their medical doctors resulting in more needed information to better understand their care recipient's medical situation. These findings were supported by our interview responses for “information on stroke” services item, where only 27% and 45% of the MA and NHW caregivers were aware, respectively. Furthermore, both caregiver groups had low percentages of awareness (9% in MA caregivers and 10% in NHW caregivers) when it came to “medical management” support. This indicates a gap in accessing existing services that could address some of these more prevalent needs. The emphasis on the lack of stroke information, corresponds with existing research highlighting the limited availability and suitability of information and existing barriers to accessing stroke information. 23 , 24 This is particularly true among minoritized ethnic groups, including Hispanic populations, where research shows a significantly lower stroke awareness compared with NHW populations. 25 In 2022, the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke released a report with recommendations meant to reduce inequities in access and delivery of acute stroke care, including enhancing diversity in the stroke workforce, expanding telestroke services, developing educational resources and standardizing secondary stroke prevention measures. 26 Our findings point to the benefits of these programs, particularly enhancing diversity in the stroke workforce and providing specific training aimed to improve clinicians' understanding of socioeconomic and cultural factors that can influence minoritized populations, including MA caregivers, to ensure more impactful care. 27 , 28

Caregiver Support Needs

Caregiver support needs were also rated as very important and high priority among caregivers, supporting existing research findings. 1 , 29 The common themes in this cluster included lack of support for dealing with both short‐ and long‐term care of the stroke survivor, resulting in increased anxiety and mental load. Some even mentioned that the balance with other family obligations made being a caregiver even more challenging. Existing research has shown that individual factors like time restrictions and increased demands related to caregiving, could limit access to services. 11 However, our interview results showed a lack of awareness with less than 15% of MA and NHW caregivers being aware of services such as respite care, caregiver training and both in‐person or online caregiver support, which is similar to findings from other research, particularly among minoritized caregivers. 13 , 14 This highlights a critical need for targeted outreach and education efforts to improve accessibility and use of these vital resources.

Financial and Legal Resource Needs

During the CM sessions, almost all participants discussed their concerns around financial support and legal resources needed for the care of the stroke survivor. These findings align with existing studies showing a dissatisfaction with legal and financial advice. 30 , 31 In these discussions, some caregivers mentioned that obtaining medical power of attorney was critical to advocate for the medical care of stroke survivors, particularly those who had cognitive limitations. Again, our interview findings showed that participants had limited knowledge of existing services to assist with financial and legal issues, let alone benefits to which they were entitled. Research has shown that financial costs linked to caregiving possess a substantial burden for caregivers, even after controlling for sociodemographic characteristics, 32 particularly among minoritized populations. 33 Furthermore, the costs associated with seeking legal assistance are a major barrier, while many are not aware of benefits for which they or the care recipient may qualify. More programs are needed to ease financial burden and offer social and economic support to family caregivers to enhance the well‐being of both caregivers and their care recipients.

Similar trends were observed across the remaining clusters where existing resources and services in the community did not match the needs of caregivers in our sample. Future research can focus on examining barriers and facilitators to accessing these existing services, since the needs for them have been established through this research.

Limitations

While our study contributes significantly to the existing literature, there were some limitations. To increase our sample size, for the second session we recruited additional individuals from those who had expressed interest but were unable to attend the first session. While it is recommended for participants to attend both sessions, it is not mandatory, and similar practices have been used in other studies. 22 Nonetheless, we asked participants to inform us of any additional needs we may have missed, and none were provided. This was expected given that we reached saturation at the end of each of our focus group sessions. Despite our efforts in recruitment and retention, our sample size was underpowered and did not allow for stratified analyses based on ethnicity or other sociodemographic characteristics. Future studies aiming to explore these relationships should consider recruiting larger samples to accommodate potentially lower retention rates. Additionally, like other traditional qualitative methods, the CM findings were not designed to generalize findings to a broader population but rather intended to offer insight and context into the perceived needs of caregivers and stroke survivors. We recognized that premorbid disability factors were higher in this sample and influence perceived needs of individuals who have survived a stroke and their caregivers. However, in another study, we showed that the needs covered in this report continue to be significant after controlling for various prestroke measures including premorbid modified Rankin Scale. Furthermore, given the limitations in sample size, we did not examine the possible impacts of factors like cognitive functioning and psychiatric symptomatology in our sample. However, we are confident given the range of health status among our participants that we were able to identify the critical needs of individuals following a stroke and their caregivers. Regardless of these limitations, our findings provide important insight that can inform future studies and programs regarding the most prevalent and high‐priority needs of this population in Nueces County.

CONCLUSIONS

This study provides insight on caregivers' perceptions on the needs of individuals who have survived a stroke in Nueces County. Our findings highlight the need for more information related to stroke and treatment, along with more support for caregivers including more access to financial and legal resources. Our findings also showed that despite existing resources in the community that could address these needs, caregivers have limited awareness about them, resulting in low rates of use. More research is crucial to explore this gap in needs and access to service. Furthermore, although our sample was limited, our findings indicated some differences across MA and NHW caregivers in their awareness regarding existing resources. It is critical for future studies to examine barriers and opportunities in accessing resources to account for possible ethnic differences that could be attributed to cultural and socioeconomic factors. As the number of stroke survivors continues to increase, addressing these gaps in care is critical to fostering optimal recovery and quality of life for stroke survivors and the caregivers, while reducing health disparities.
