A SEER based SHAP interpretable prognostic nomogram for overall survival in surgically treated patients with colorectal cancer and synchronous liver metastasis
Authors: Shaowei Jiang, Lulu Qi, Jiaqi Yuan, Haiyun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) with liver metastasis is associated with poor prognosis and substantial heterogeneity in survival outcomes. Accurate risk stratification is essential for individualized treatment planning, yet reliable and interpretable prognostic tools remain limited. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and validate a prognostic model for overall survival (OS) in a predominantly surgically treated cohort of patients with CRC and synchronous liver metastasis at diagnosis based on Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) data, and to enhance model interpretability using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis.MethodsData of patients with CRC with liver metastasis diagnosed between 2011 and 2018 were obtained from the SEER database. OS was used as the endpoint. Kaplan-Meier analysis and univariate and multivariable Cox regression were performed, and multivariable Cox regression was used to identify independent prognostic factors. A nomogram was constructed, and its performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). SHAP analysis was further applied to improve model interpretability.ResultsKaplan-Meier analysis showed a poor overall prognosis, with a median OS of 25 months. Multivariable Cox regression identified age, tumor location, differentiation, metastatic stage, preoperative carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), perineural invasion, bone metastases, metastasectomy, postoperative chemotherapy, and lymph node ratio (LNR) as independent prognostic factors for OS. Based on these variables, a nomogram was developed to predict 12-, 36-, and 60-month OS. The model showed good discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility in both the training and validation sets. SHAP analysis further improved model interpretability by showing the relative contribution of each predictor to survival estimation.ConclusionsWe developed and internally validated a prognostic nomogram for OS in patients with CRC with liver metastasis based on SEER data. The model showed acceptable predictive performance and may provide supportive information for individualized prognosis evaluation in this population.Keywords: Colorectal cancer (CRC); liver metastasis; overall survival (OS); nomogram; Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database (SEER database)

Highlight box

Key findings

• A prognostic nomogram was developed and internally validated for predicting overall survival in patients with colorectal cancer (CRC) with synchronous liver metastasis using Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results data.

• Age, tumor location, differentiation, metastatic stage, preoperative carcinoembryonic antigen, perineural invasion, bone metastases, metastasectomy, postoperative chemotherapy, and lymph node ratio were identified as independent prognostic factors.

What is known and what is new?

• Colorectal cancer with liver metastasis has heterogeneous survival outcomes, and conventional staging systems may not fully capture individual prognostic differences.

• By integrating clinicopathological and treatment-related variables with SHapley Additive exPlanations interpretation, the model provides transparent survival predictions and identifies the relative importance of individual predictors.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The proposed nomogram may assist clinicians in postoperative risk stratification and individualized follow-up planning for patients with CRC and liver metastasis. Future studies with external validation are needed before routine clinical implementation.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a major public health problem worldwide. Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) 2020 estimated approximately 1.9 million new CRC cases and 935,000 deaths, ranking CRC third for incidence and second for cancer mortality globally (1). Long-term burden analyses show that the absolute numbers of incident cases, deaths, and disability-adjusted life-years have risen markedly over the past three decades, reflecting population growth, aging, and the continued effects of modifiable exposures such as diet, adiposity, alcohol use, and smoking, although the magnitude of risk varies considerably across regions and development levels (2). The near-term outlook is equally sobering. Based on GLOBOCAN projections, the global CRC burden may reach about 3.2 million new cases and 1.6 million deaths by 2040, with particularly sharp relative increases expected in countries undergoing rapid socioeconomic transition (3). In the United States, CRC continues to account for substantial mortality, and recent epidemiologic reports suggest that the disease is increasingly diagnosed at advanced stage while a growing proportion of cases occur before age 55 years (4). This age shift is no longer confined to a handful of Western populations. Contemporary registry-based analyses from 50 countries and territories show rising early-onset CRC incidence in more than half of the settings studied, often at a time when rates in older adults are stable or decreasing (5). Taken together, these patterns deepen the clinical and societal burden of advanced CRC and sharpen the need for better risk stratification in metastatic disease.

For patients with CRC and liver metastasis, prognosis is highly heterogeneous. Population-based data show that patients with metachronous hepatic metastases have better survival than those with synchronous disease, and that survival gains over time have not been uniform across these subgroups (6). Trial-level pooled analyses likewise indicate that liver metastasis is an adverse prognostic feature in unresectable disease, with effects that vary by treatment line and therapeutic context (7). In practice, patients differ substantially in metastatic timing, disease extent, treatment exposure, and biological behavior, so conventional stage groupings alone are often insufficient for individualized counseling. Clinical prediction models and nomograms can combine routinely available patient, tumor, metastatic, and treatment variables into a patient-specific survival estimate, and they remain appealing because they are easy to apply in daily oncology practice. Nevertheless, a recent review of colorectal liver metastasis (CRLM) prediction models found wide variation in predictors, endpoints, and validation strategies, which has limited generalizability and real-world uptake (8,9). The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program offers a useful foundation for such work because it supports population-level cancer research through standardized registry data on demographics, tumor characteristics, initial treatment, and survival outcomes in the United States (10). We therefore aimed to develop and validate an interpretable prognostic nomogram for overall survival (OS) in patients with CRC with liver metastasis using SEER data and to apply SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to clarify the contribution of individual variables and improve model transparency. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1095/rc).

Methods

Patient selection

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Patients were identified from the SEER database. We included patients diagnosed between 2011 and 2018 with malignant CRC and synchronous liver metastasis at presentation. Eligible cases were aged 18 years or older, had a single primary tumor, and had histological confirmation based on positive pathology. Primary tumor sites were limited to the colorectum, including the cecum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure, transverse colon, splenic flexure, descending colon, sigmoid colon, overlapping lesions of the colon, colon not otherwise specified, rectosigmoid junction, and rectum. Patients with nonmalignant tumors, multiple primary malignancies, absence of liver metastasis at diagnosis, or without pathological confirmation were excluded.

The following variables were extracted for analysis: age, sex, race, primary tumor location, histological type, tumor differentiation, metastatic stage, tumor size, preoperative carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), perineural invasion, pulmonary metastases, bone metastases, brain metastases, history of primary tumor surgery, history of metastasectomy, postoperative chemotherapy, postoperative radiotherapy, and lymph node ratio (LNR). Age was categorized as ≤60 and >60 years. Tumor location was grouped as right colon, left colon, or rectum. Histological type was classified as adenocarcinoma, signet ring cell carcinoma, or other histological types. Differentiation was recorded as well: moderately, poorly, or undifferentiated. Metastatic stage was categorized as IVA, IVB, or IVC. Tumor size was divided into <5 and ≥5 cm. Preoperative CEA was classified as normal or elevated, perineural invasion as present or not present, and pulmonary, bone, and brain metastases as yes or no. Treatment-related variables were coded as yes or no, including primary tumor surgery, metastasectomy, postoperative chemotherapy, and postoperative radiotherapy. LNR was grouped as 0–0.2, >0.2–0.6, and >0.6. The predicted outcome is 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software. Eligible patients were randomly divided into a training set and a validation set at a ratio of 7:3. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages, and differences between groups were compared using the chi-square test. OS was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was first used to screen potential prognostic factors, and variables with statistical significance were subsequently entered into multivariable Cox regression to identify independent prognostic factors. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Based on the results of the multivariable Cox analysis, a nomogram was constructed to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS. Model performance was assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). In addition, SHAP analysis was applied to improve the interpretability of the model. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Because metastasectomy and postoperative chemotherapy are treatment-related variables determined after diagnosis, the primary nomogram was defined as a post-treatment prognostic evaluation model rather than a pretreatment prediction tool.

Results

Patient characteristics analysis

A total of 2,473 patients with CRC with liver metastasis were included in the study, including 1,731 patients in the training set and 742 patients in the validation set. The baseline clinicopathological characteristics of the two cohorts are summarized in Table 1. In the overall population, 53.26% of patients were older than 60 years, 54.79% were male, and 74.12% were White. The most common primary tumor location was the left colon (46.70%), followed by the right colon (36.19%) and rectum (17.10%). Adenocarcinoma was the predominant histological type (86.45%), while signet ring cell carcinoma accounted for only 0.44% of cases. Most tumors were moderately differentiated (70.60%), and 60.74% of patients had a tumor size of ≥5 cm. In terms of metastatic burden, 68.01% of patients were classified as stage IVA, 28.06% as stage IVB, and 3.92% as stage IVC. Elevated preoperative CEA was observed in 81.80% of patients, and perineural invasion was present in 35.91%. Regarding metastatic patterns and treatment characteristics, pulmonary, bone, and brain metastases were identified in 14.84%, 2.75%, and 0.32% of patients, respectively. Nearly all patients underwent primary tumor surgery (99.88%), and 31.30% received metastasectomy. Postoperative chemotherapy and radiotherapy were administered in 78.37% and 8.94% of patients, respectively. For LNR, 58.15% of patients were in the 0–0.2 group, 31.70% in the >0.2–0.6 group, and 10.15% in the >0.6 group. No significant differences were observed between the training and validation sets across all baseline variables (all P>0.05), indicating good comparability between the two cohorts.

Survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier analysis of the entire cohort demonstrated a persistently declining OS probability over time. Among the 2,473 included patients, 1,940 deaths occurred during follow-up. The median OS was 25 months. As illustrated in Figure 1, survival decreased markedly during the early follow-up period and continued to decline thereafter, reflecting the poor prognosis of patients with CRC with liver metastasis.

Kaplan-Meier survival curves of patients. CI, confidence interval; OS, overall survival.

Univariate and multivariable Cox regression analysis

Univariate Cox regression analysis showed that age, race, primary tumor location, histological type, differentiation, tumor size, metastatic stage, preoperative CEA, perineural invasion, bone metastases, pulmonary metastases, history of metastasectomy, postoperative chemotherapy, postoperative radiotherapy, and LNR were significantly associated with OS (all P<0.05). Specifically, older age (>60 years), right-sided primary tumors, signet ring cell carcinoma, poor or undifferentiated differentiation, tumor size ≥5 cm, advanced metastatic stage, elevated preoperative CEA, perineural invasion, bone metastases, pulmonary metastases, and higher LNR were associated with worse survival, whereas metastasectomy, postoperative chemotherapy, and postoperative radiotherapy were associated with improved survival. Sex, brain metastases, and history of primary tumor surgery were not significantly associated with OS in the univariate analysis (Table 2).

Variables with statistical significance in the univariate analysis were subsequently entered into the multivariable Cox regression model. After adjustment, age >60 years remained an independent adverse prognostic factor (HR =1.41, 95% CI: 1.26–1.58, P<0.001). Compared with left-sided colon cancer, right-sided primary tumors were associated with significantly worse OS (HR =1.33, 95% CI: 1.17–1.50, P<0.001), whereas rectal tumors were not independently significant. Poor differentiation (HR =1.22, 95% CI: 1.07–1.39, P=0.003) and undifferentiated histology (HR =1.59, 95% CI: 1.19–2.13, P=0.002) independently predicted poor survival. Metastatic stage was also independently associated with prognosis, with patients in stage IVB (HR =1.55, 95% CI: 1.34–1.79, P<0.001) and stage IVC (HR =2.10, 95% CI: 1.60–2.77, P<0.001) showing significantly higher mortality risk than those in stage IVA. Elevated preoperative CEA (HR =1.76, 95% CI: 1.50–2.06, P<0.001), perineural invasion (HR =1.15, 95% CI: 1.03–1.29, P=0.02), bone metastases (HR = 1.98, 95% CI: 1.45–2.70, P<0.001), and higher LNR levels (>0.2–0.6: HR =1.31, 95% CI: 1.16–1.48, P<0.001; >0.6: HR =1.95, 95% CI: 1.64–2.32, P<0.001) remained independent risk factors. In contrast, metastasectomy (HR =0.67, 95% CI: 0.59–0.76, P<0.001) and postoperative chemotherapy (HR =0.40, 95% CI: 0.35–0.46, P<0.001) were independently associated with better survival. Race, histological type, tumor size, pulmonary metastases, and postoperative radiotherapy did not retain statistical significance after adjustment (Table 2).

Model construction

A nomogram for predicting 12-, 36-, and 60-month OS was established based on the independent prognostic factors identified in the multivariable Cox regression model. The final model included age, tumor location, differentiation, metastatic stage, preoperative CEA, perineural invasion, bone metastases, history of metastasectomy, postoperative chemotherapy, and LNR. As illustrated in Figure 2, each predictor was assigned a weighted score proportional to its regression coefficient, and the sum of these scores corresponded to individualized survival probabilities at different time points. Among all variables, postoperative chemotherapy contributed the largest number of points, indicating the strongest influence on survival prediction, whereas metastatic stage, bone metastases, preoperative CEA, metastasectomy, age, and LNR also showed substantial contributions.

Model validation

The predictive performance of the nomogram was evaluated using ROC curves, calibration curves, and DCA. As shown in Figure 3, the model demonstrated good discrimination in both the training and validation sets at 12, 36, and 60 months. The AUCs in the training set were 0.815, 0.794, and 0.791 for 12-, 36-, and 60-month OS, respectively, whereas the corresponding AUCs in the validation set were 0.819, 0.803, and 0.819, respectively (Figure 3A-3C). Calibration plots further showed good agreement between the predicted and observed survival probabilities in both cohorts. In the training set, the calibration curves for 12-, 36-, and 60-month OS are shown in Figure 4A-4C, while the corresponding curves in the validation set are presented in Figure 4D-4F. Overall, the calibration curves were close to the ideal reference line, indicating satisfactory predictive accuracy. DCA also confirmed the clinical utility of the nomogram. As shown in Figure 5, the model provided a favorable net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities in both the training and validation sets, supporting its potential value in clinical decision-making.

Discussion

The present study extends the prognostic literature on CRC with synchronous liver metastasis by showing that routinely available clinicopathologic and treatment-related variables can separate postoperative survival risk with reasonable consistency. In our cohort, adverse outcomes clustered around features that clinicians already recognize as markers of more aggressive disease, including older age, right-sided primary location, poor or undifferentiated histology, more advanced metastatic stage, elevated preoperative CEA, perineural invasion, bone metastasis, and a higher LNR. By contrast, patients who underwent metastasectomy and those who received postoperative chemotherapy had better survival, suggesting that prognosis in this setting is determined not only by what the tumor is at diagnosis but also by whether it remains amenable to meaningful multimodal treatment. The internal validation findings, together with the model interpretation analyses, reinforce this point because treatment-related factors ranked alongside biologic variables among the most influential contributors to predicted risk. This is important from a clinical standpoint. In synchronous liver-metastatic disease, fixed host and tumor characteristics do matter, but they do not operate in isolation. The practical question is whether those baseline risks can be modified by complete local treatment of metastatic deposits, systemic control of occult disease, and careful multidisciplinary sequencing. In that sense, the nomogram may be more useful when presented as a decision-support framework rather than a purely descriptive score. It also fits with the direction of recent CRLM literature, which increasingly views prognosis as the combined product of tumor biology, disease distribution, and treatment convertibility rather than any single anatomic descriptor (11-15).

The independent factors identified here are also biologically plausible and broadly concordant with contemporary evidence. Older age remained associated with worse survival, which in a population-based cohort likely reflects a composite of frailty, comorbidity, lower tolerance of intensive treatment, and competing mortality, even though age alone should not exclude carefully selected patients from liver-directed therapy (11-13). Primary tumor location also behaved as expected. Right-sided primaries have repeatedly shown worse outcomes in metastatic disease and after local treatment of liver metastases, probably because sidedness captures meaningful biologic divergence rather than simple anatomy, including more aggressive molecular phenotypes and different treatment sensitivity (11,12,15). Poor and undifferentiated tumors similarly point to greater invasive capacity and early hematogenous spread, which makes their adverse signal unsurprising (13,14). Elevated preoperative CEA was an especially coherent finding, since both dynamic preoperative CEA behavior and pooled analyses after hepatectomy have linked high or rising CEA levels with occult tumor burden and early recurrence. Perineural invasion likely marks a particularly invasive tumor-stroma interaction and has recently been shown to carry substantial prognostic weight in synchronous CRLM treated with chemotherapy and surgery (16-18). Bone metastasis is uncommon in CRC, but when present it usually signals widely disseminated, biologically unfavorable disease and consistently portends poor survival (19,20). The adverse effect of a higher LNR is also credible because LNR integrates nodal tumor burden with the adequacy of nodal assessment and has shown prognostic value both in resected synchronous CRLM and in broader stage IV populations (21,22). Finally, the protective associations of metastasectomy and postoperative chemotherapy are clinically meaningful, although they should be interpreted with appropriate caution because both variables are partly influenced by patient selection, residual fitness, and postoperative recovery. Even so, recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses support the view that complete local treatment when feasible and appropriately selected perioperative or adjuvant systemic therapy can translate into longer survival, especially in synchronous disease (23-26).

The subgroup with rectal primaries deserves particular attention because rectal cancer with synchronous liver metastasis poses a sequencing problem that is more complex than standard colon-liver metastatic disease. Pelvic control, the possible need for pelvic radiotherapy, the risk of local symptoms, and the timing of total mesorectal excision must all be balanced against the fact that liver disease usually drives survival. This is why metastasectomy remains central. In selected patients, resection or durable local eradication of metastatic deposits is still the only realistic route toward long-term disease control, and recent reviews of resectable rectal cancer with synchronous liver metastases continue to support aggressive multimodal strategies with curative intent when technically and biologically appropriate (27). At the same time, recent comparative studies make it clear that no single sequence is universally best. The liver-first strategy is particularly attractive when the rectal primary is asymptomatic and the dominant threat is hepatic tumor burden, whereas other patients may benefit from short-course pelvic radiation with systemic therapy, combined surgery, or more conventional primary-first treatment (28-30). This flexible approach fits current practice better than dogmatic sequencing. It also explains why multidisciplinary care matters so much. Decisions about the order of chemotherapy, pelvic treatment, hepatectomy, and rectal resection depend on resectability, radiologic response, liver reserve, pelvic symptoms, and the probability of achieving complete macroscopic clearance. Recent advances have widened the therapeutic envelope. Modern liver surgery increasingly favors parenchymal preservation, repeat local treatment when appropriate, and minimally invasive approaches in selected cases. In parallel, conversion therapy has expanded the pool of patients who can eventually undergo curative-intent liver treatment, while perioperative systemic therapy helps address micrometastatic disease and refine biologic selection (11,12,25,26). Interventional oncology is also gaining a clearer role. Thermal ablation and related image-guided techniques are now a genuine complement to surgery, especially for small lesions, deep lesions, or situations in which preservation of future liver remnant is critical (12,31). In other words, the favorable signal associated with metastasectomy in our model should be interpreted within a broader treatment philosophy: for rectal cancer with liver metastasis, durable survival often depends on getting the sequence right, not just performing one operation (11,12,25-32).

Limitations

Several limitations should be stated clearly. First, this was a retrospective observational study, so residual confounding cannot be eliminated. The apparent protective effects of metastasectomy and postoperative chemotherapy are clinically plausible, but they should still be interpreted as treatment-associated rather than strictly causal because access to those interventions depends on factors such as disease extent, postoperative recovery, and clinician selection. Second, the use of SEER brings well-known constraints. SEER treatment fields are limited to first-course therapy, chemotherapy is commonly coded as yes versus no/unknown, and organ-metastasis variables at diagnosis capture only selected metastatic sites rather than the full biology and distribution of liver disease. The database also does not provide several variables that now shape CRLM decision-making, including performance status, comorbidity burden, exact systemic regimens, targeted or immunotherapy exposure, radiologic response, margin status, liver lesion number and bilobar distribution in a treatment-relevant format, RAS/BRAF/MSI status, and longitudinal biomarkers such as ctDNA. These omissions are especially relevant because current CRLM care and modern prognostic modeling increasingly rely on dynamic rather than static information (11,12,33-35). Third, the cohort reflects a largely surgically managed US population, so generalizability to nonoperative patients, other health systems, or more contemporary molecularly stratified populations is inherently limited. Future work should therefore move in two directions. One is external, preferably prospective, validation in independent multicenter cohorts. The other is model enrichment through molecular data and serial biomarkers. Recent reviews of CRLM prediction models, dynamic prognostic modeling with longitudinal laboratory markers, and ctDNA meta-analyses all point in the same direction: the next generation of tools will likely be more accurate if they update risk over time rather than relying entirely on baseline clinicopathologic snapshots (33-35). Unspecified details include the exact chemotherapy regimen, use of targeted therapy, surgical margin status, liver tumor burden indicators, and molecular analysis. The absence of these variables may introduce residual confounding and limit the reliability of individualized risk estimates in contemporary multidisciplinary practice; therefore, the nomogram should be regarded as an initial SEER-based prognostic tool that requires external validation and further refinement using molecular, biomarker, and detailed treatment data.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we developed and validated a prognostic nomogram for OS in patients with CRC who had synchronous liver metastasis at the time of initial diagnosis using a large population-based cohort from the SEER database. The model incorporated routinely available clinicopathological and treatment-related variables and showed satisfactory discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. In addition, SHAP analysis enhanced model interpretability by illustrating the relative contribution of individual predictors to survival estimation. The findings suggest that age, tumor location, differentiation, metastatic stage, preoperative CEA, perineural invasion, bone metastases, metastasectomy, postoperative chemotherapy, and LNR play important roles in prognosis assessment. This nomogram may serve as a practical tool for individualized risk stratification and follow-up planning in this patient population. However, because nearly all patients in this cohort underwent primary tumor resection, the findings should be interpreted as being most applicable to a selected surgical population rather than the broader population of patients with colorectal liver metastases. Nevertheless, further external validation and prospective studies are needed before widespread clinical application.
