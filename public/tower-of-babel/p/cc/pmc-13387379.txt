Auricular Acupressure for Depressive and Anxiety Symptoms in Women with Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder: Protocol for a Pilot Randomized Sham-Controlled Trial
Authors: Yi-Meng Zeng, Si-Yu Liu, Jing-Han Qiu, Ji-Yuan Shi, Jun-Ting Tong, Qian-Yi Zhang, Jun-Xiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePremenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) induces disabling anxiety and depression, impairing mental health and academic performance in Chinese female undergraduates with high prevalence. Auricular acupressure (AA), a non-invasive Traditional Chinese Medicine modality, has potential in emotional regulation but lacks robust evidence for PMDD. This pilot study seeks to evaluate the feasibility, adherence, preliminary safety and efficacy of AA for PMDD-related emotional symptoms, as well as its potential neurophysiological mechanisms, so as to inform the design of subsequent large-scale RCTs.Patients and MethodsSixty-six eligible participants with PMDD will be randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the active AA group or the sham AA group. The intervention will be administered once weekly for 3 consecutive menstrual cycles, totaling 12 sessions; participants will perform self-acupressure three times daily for 10 minutes per session, with auricular plasters retained for at least 2 days per application to ensure compliance. Primary outcomes (severity of depressive and anxiety symptoms) will be assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) at baseline and post each cycle’s final intervention. Secondary outcomes include emotional state (measured by the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule-20 [PANAS-20]) and health-related quality of life (HRQoL). Additionally, brain activity will be quantified via electroencephalography. Adverse events will be closely monitored throughout the trial.Trial Registration NumberITMCTR2025002451 (International Traditional Medicine Clinical Trial Registry).Expected Results and ConclusionThis study is designed to collect feasibility data and preliminary evidence on the safety and efficacy of AA, alongside initial insights into its underlying mechanisms. Findings will optimize protocols and sample size for future RCTs, facilitating AA’s clinical translation for PMDD.Keywords: anxiety, depression, premenstrual dysphoric disorder, traditional Chinese medicine, auricular acupressure, complementary therapy, randomized controlled trial, electroencephalography

Introduction

Premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) is a cyclical neuropsychoendocrine disorder characterized by recurrent luteal-phase emotional distress, physical discomfort, and cognitive impairment, with symptom remission typically occurring within 7 days post-menstruation. Misinterpretation of these symptoms as normal premenstrual variations often leads to underdiagnosis and undertreatment.1 Epidemiological data indicate a global prevalence of 1.8–5.8% among reproductive-aged women, with marked regional disparities; notably, a 32.6% prevalence has been reported in Chinese female undergraduates.2 PMDD exerts a profound negative impact on multiple life domains, including interpersonal relationships, family functioning, social engagement, and occupational/academic performance.3

Emotional symptoms—particularly anxiety and depression—represent the most debilitating component of PMDD, causing greater functional impairment than physical manifestations.1,4 These mood disturbances exacerbate during the late luteal phase, coinciding with heightened emotional sensitivity relative to healthy individuals.5 Critically, PMDD is associated with a 7-fold increased risk of suicide attempts and a 4-fold higher prevalence of suicidal ideation compared with age-matched controls.6

First-line therapies for PMDD include psychosocial interventions (Cognitive Behavioural Therapy, Mindfulness-based Cognitive Therapy, Interpersonal Psychotherapy), lifestyle modifications (Dietary Optimization, Structured Exercise), and pharmacotherapy (Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors, Hormonal Contraceptives). While recent meta-analyses have demonstrated that Dialectical Behaviour Therapy benefits youth with emotional disturbances including depression, dedicated studies on PMDD are still limited.7 However, these approaches are constrained by three key limitations: variable efficacy coupled with high-cost burdens, poor adherence to behavioural interventions, and dose-dependent adverse effects (e.g, gastrointestinal distress, neuroautonomic dysregulation, sexual dysfunction).8–10 Given these limitations, there is an urgent clinical need for complementary therapies that enhance adaptive emotional responses while improving tolerability and minimizing adverse effects.

Auricular acupressure (AA)—a standardized Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) modality—has emerging evidence to support its efficacy in managing emotional symptoms.11,12 This non-invasive intervention involves mechanical stimulation of specific auricular points (eg, TF4-Shenmen, AT4-Subcortex) using Vaccaria seeds, which modulates neural pathways via vagus nerve activation and regulates stress-responsive brain regions (e.g, limbic system, locus coeruleus, hypothalamus).13 While AA has shown promise in perimenopausal depression14 and chemotherapy-related anxiety,15 empirical evidence for its use in PMDD remains preliminary. Current evidence is predominantly limited to self-reported psychometric assessments (e.g, Hamilton Depression Rating Scale [HAM-D], Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale [GAD-7]). To address this gap, our pilot trial adopts a multimodal evaluation framework integrating validated psychological scales (e.g, Chinese version of CTDP-DSM-5 [CTDP-C]) with quantitative electroencephalography (EEG) biomarkers. Specifically, resting-state EEG spectral analysis will preliminarily characterize AA-induced neuromodulatory effects, yielding exploratory mechanistic insights into its potential therapeutic actions.

Materials and Methods

Sample Size

For sample size calculation, we assume an effect size of 0.8 for GAD-7 score reduction in AA recipients, based on a recent meta-analysis reporting a standardized mean difference (SMD) of −1.11 (95% confidence interval [CI]: −1.61 to −0.61).16,17 For depression, we assume an effect size >0.8 for Patient Health Questionnaire 9-item scale (PHQ-9) score reduction, consistent with prior literature.16,18,19 We adopt a conservative effect size of 0.7, with a 95% CI and statistical power of 0.8, yielding a required total sample size of 52 participants. Accounting for a 20% dropout rate, the final sample size is set at 66 participants (33 per group) to ensure adequate statistical power.

Study Design

This is a single-blind, pilot randomized sham-controlled trial approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. 2025BZYLL1008). The study flowchart and treatment schedule are presented in Figure 1 and Table 1. Participants will be recruited from the Research Center of the Nursing School between December 1, 2025, and December 1, 2026, to evaluate the efficacy and underlying mechanisms of AA for managing PMDD emotional symptoms. Following written informed consent, participants will be randomly assigned to the active AA group or the sham AA group. The auricular plasters for both groups are identical in appearance, with differences in two aspects: the AA group uses Vaccaria seeds (in contact with the skin) on 5 therapeutic auricular acupoints, aiming to elicit Deqi (the characteristic needling sensation in TCM), while the sham group uses smooth silicone on 5 non-therapeutic ones to avoid inducing any Deqi sensation (Figure 2C).

Inclusion Criteria

Eligible participants are female college students aged 18–35 years with a confirmed diagnosis of PMDD based on the CTDP-C,2 a validated 25-item self-rating scale used to evaluate symptoms experienced across a 12-month period. Diagnosis requires symptoms to occur in most menstrual cycles of the past year, dichotomous (yes/no) scoring indicating significant distress/functional impairment, and validation via a DSM-5-algorithm. Additional criteria include regular menstrual cycles (21–35 days) and access to internet-connected devices for study participation. The CTDP-C has demonstrated good validity and reliability in Chinese PMDD populations.2

Exclusion Criteria

Exclusion criteria include: current or planned pregnancy; breastfeeding; comorbid psychiatric disorders (e.g, severe depression, bipolar disorder) that could confound PMDD assessment; psychotropic medication use within the past 3 months; hormonal contraceptive use; prior psychiatric hospitalization; suicide attempts; ongoing alcohol or substance abuse; external ear injury; and significant medical conditions (e.g, endocrine, cardiovascular disorders) that might interfere with safety or outcome evaluation. Participants are also excluded if they receive concurrent psychotherapy, pharmacotherapy, or TCM interventions during the trial.

Dropout Criteria

Participants will be classified as dropouts if they fail to complete the study protocol, including voluntary withdrawal. Investigators may also exclude participants for protocol non-compliance or serious adverse events.

Randomization and Blinding

Participants will be randomized 1:1 to the AA or sham AA group using random numbers generated by SPSS 20.0 software (IBM, Cary, NC, USA). This process will be conducted by a statistician not involved in study implementation or outcome assessment. To maintain confidentiality, randomization sequences will be sealed in opaque envelopes, which practitioners will open only to confirm group assignment before administering the intervention. Practitioners will be prohibited from discussing treatment details with participants. While psychological scales are self-completed by participants, all analysts conducting EEG data processing and analysis will remain blinded to group allocations. Participant blinding efficacy will be evaluated using the Bang Blinding Index (BBI), with a score range of 0.0 to 0.2 indicating successful blinding.

Interventions

Active AA is initiated in the premenstrual phase (5 days before anticipated menstruation) and continues until day 2 of menstruation. The five therapeutic auricular points (TF4-Shenmen, CO15-Heart, CO12-Kidney, CO18-Liver, CO10-Endocrine) are selected in accordance with TCM theory and anatomical distribution of auricular vagal innervation (Figure 2A and B), a point combination and intervention protocol previously validated in studies of emotional regulation.11,18,20 Point location is verified using an electrical resistance detector prior to treatment. After skin disinfection, sterile auricular plasters embedded with Vaccaria seeds are applied to the designated points. To ensure treatment adherence, participants perform standardized self-acupressure three times daily (morning, afternoon, evening). Each point is pressed for 10 seconds followed by a 5-second pause until local redness or tenderness occurs, aiming to elicit the characteristic Deqi sensation (e.g, soreness, distension, warmth). Treatment is alternated between the left and right ears, and plasters are kept in place for a minimum of 2 days to sustain continuous stimulation.

The sham group receives identical procedural scheduling but with five non-therapeutic points (LO5-Eye, LO1-Teeth, SF1-Shoulder, SF4-Finger, TG1-External Nose), chosen for their lack of association with emotional regulation pathways (Figure 2B). For acupoint locations, refer to Table 2. Two modifications minimize therapeutic stimulation: participants are instructed to apply only gentle pressure, and Vaccaria seeds are replaced with flat, smooth, desensitized silicone pieces of specified thickness. The larger, circular contact surface of the silicone reduces concentrated pressure, thereby effectively diminishing Deqi sensations.

Result

Primary Outcome

Assessment of Depression and Anxiety

Participants’ depressive and anxiety symptom severity is assessed using validated psychological scales at baseline (week 0) and post-intervention (weeks 4, 8, and 12), time points corresponding to key stages of the AA intervention. The PHQ-921 and GAD-722 are used to measure depressive and anxiety symptoms as primary outcomes, respectively. The PHQ-9 is a valid, reliable 9-item self-rating scale for assessing depressive symptom severity. Each item is scored from 0 (not at all) to 3 (almost every day), with a total score range of 0–27; higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms.21 The GAD-7 is a valid, reliable 7-item self-rating scale for assessing generalized anxiety disorder severity. Each item is scored from 0 (not at all) to 3 (almost every day), with a total score range of 0–21; higher scores indicate more severe anxiety symptoms.22

Secondary Outcomes

Assessment of Emotional State and Social Functioning

To quantify the effect of AA on participants’ emotional states, we employed the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule-20 (PANAS-20),23 which comprises two 10-item subscales for assessing positive and negative affect, respectively. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very slightly or not at all; 5 = extremely). The PANAS-20 has demonstrated good validity across diverse populations and settings. Furthermore, participants’ Health-related quality of life (HRQoL)24 is assessed using the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36)—a widely used, psychometrically validated self-report instrument evaluating eight health domains: physical functioning, role limitations due to physical problems, bodily pain, general health perceptions, vitality, social functioning, role limitations due to emotional problems, and mental health. Raw scores are transformed into a standardized 0–100 scale (higher scores = better health status), with domain scores aggregated into two summary measures: the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and the Mental Component Summary (MCS). In the context of PMDD, the SF-36 provides a comprehensive assessment of how premenstrual symptoms impact physical, emotional, and social functioning.

Brain Activity Recording Based on EEG

To investigate the neural correlates of AA, participants’ brain activity will be recorded using an Emotive Flex EEG system (Emotiv Inc., USA) at both baseline (week 0) and after the final session (week 12). Participants will wear a 32-channel EEG cap with two mastoid references (CMS and DRL) and Ag/AgCl electrodes positioned according to the international 10–20 system; the sampling rate is set to up to 256 Hz. Recordings are obtained during a resting-state task, a paradigm widely used in cognitive neuroscience to detect neurocognitive states, whose resting-state neural activity patterns are robustly associated with various psychopathological conditions.25–28 During recording, participants are instructed to remain still, relax, and minimize eye and body movements. The resting task includes two 5-minute conditions: eyes open (fixating on a central cross on a screen), and eyes closed (relaxing without falling asleep). For both conditions, participants are asked to avoid engaging in directed thought processes.

EEG data preprocessing includes filtering, bad channel detection, re-referencing, and artifact rejection. Two key metrics are calculated: (1) Power spectral density (PSD) for major frequency bands (δ: 1–4 Hz, θ: 4–8 Hz, α: 8–13 Hz, β: 13–30 Hz, γ: 30–100 Hz) targeting frontal/parietal as well as occipital regions to capture activity related to alertness, attention, and emotion via Welch’s method or Fast Fourier Transform (FFT); (2) functional connectivity analysis (e.g, phase-locking value) to quantify phase synchrony between channels and capture inter-regional interactions within frontal-parietal and occipital-parietal networks. Integrating these multidimensional EEG metrics will reveal the neural mechanisms behind AA-mediated relief of depressive and anxiety symptoms among participants with PMDD.

Safety Evaluation

Although AA is generally considered a safe intervention, participants undergo systematic assessment for treatment-related discomfort throughout the trial. All adverse events are meticulously documented in case report forms (CRFs). The Ethics Committee will conduct a rigorous review to determine the causality between adverse events and the intervention, informing decisions on study continuation. The incidence of adverse events is calculated as (number of participants with adverse events/total sample size) × 100% to evaluate the safety profile of AA.

Statistical Analysis

To evaluate the effect of AA on reducing emotional symptoms in PMDD, both intent-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) analyses will be conducted for the 12-week intervention period (0, 4, 8 and 12 week). Baseline demographic characteristics between groups will be summarized using means and standard deviations.

Then, generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMM) will be employed to estimate clinical outcomes (GAD-7 and PHQ-9) between groups (AA vs sham AA). For each scale, the outcome variable will be the observed score at each assessment time point (baseline, 4,8 and 12-week follow-up). Fixed effects will include treatment group, time, their interaction, and relevant covariates, including baseline scores of the corresponding scale and age. And subject will be included as a random effect to account for within-subject correlation. All statistical tests will be conducted using a significance level of α = 0.05.

For brain activity analyses, repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) will be used to compare EEG band-specific power (δ, θ, α, β, γ) and functional connectivity analyses (e.g, phase-locking value), respectively, with treatment group (AA vs sham AA) as the between-subject factor and time (baseline vs 12 weeks) as the within-subject factor. Post-hoc t-tests conducted only if the F-test indicates a significant main effect. All statistical tests will be conducted using a significance level of α = 0.05.

We will also conduct additional Pearson or Spearman correlation analyses to probe the association between changes in band-specific power or inter-regional phase synchrony (12- vs 0-week) and corresponding PHQ-9/GAD-7 scores, specific on AA group. Statistical significance of correlations will be assessed via permutation testing (5,000 random permutations to generate an empirical null distribution), with p-values calculated as the proportion of permuted correlations exceeding the observed correlation coefficient. All statistical analyses will be performed using R and MATLAB with open-source toolboxes and custom scripts.

Discussion

Despite increasing interest in managing the emotional symptoms of PMDD, existing studies remain limited by inconsistent intervention protocols and a lack of standardized, objective outcome measures. These gaps highlight the need for a well-defined research protocol to evaluate the efficacy and safety of AA and to clarify its therapeutic mechanisms on anxiety and depression in female undergraduates with PMDD. The present protocol describes a pilot randomized sham-controlled trial designed to address these limitations through standardized intervention delivery and validated assessment tools.

AA is one of the most prevalent complementary and alternative therapies globally, with emerging evidence supporting its potential in managing affective disorders.11,12 As a non-invasive technique, it offers advantages such as low cost, high feasibility, and wide acceptance, factors that make it particularly suitable for PMDD intervention research. However, prior research investigating AA for emotional symptoms have reported mixed results: some found comparable efficacy between AA and sham AA in alleviating anxiety, with no statistically significant between-group differences.20,29 A key reason of these studies is their focus on comparing different auricular acupoints while keeping stimulation approaches (especially total stimulation intensity) uniform. This uniformity may heighten therapeutic expectations, potentially amplifying placebo effects. In clinical practice, both acupoint selection and stimulation parameters are critical determinants of treatment outcomes.30,31 To address this, the present protocol integrates auricular neural distribution with TCM auricular theory, with groups differentiated based on acupoint selection and, more crucially, stimulation intensity. Specifically, the AA group receives intense pressure at therapeutic acupoints via spherical Vaccaria seeds to elicit distinct Deqi sensations, whereas the sham group uses flat, smooth silicone plasters to deliver gentle pressure on non-therapeutic acupoints without triggering Deqi.

A major challenge in clinical AA research is controlling Deqi sensations to ensure meaningful treatment effects. Therefore, strict quality control measures are essential to ensure the reliability of findings. All participants will receive standardized training from certified acupuncturists and nurses on AA techniques (pressing intensity, frequency, duration) to ensure consistent stimulation. Participants will also record their sensations during self-acupressure and post-session skin changes, providing empirical data to monitor stimulation effects.

To further elucidate the efficacy and underlying neural mechanisms of AA in mitigating anxiety and depressive symptoms among young women with PMDD, this pilot trial will establish a multimodal evaluation framework integrating validated psychological rating scales and quantitative resting-state EEG biomarkers. EEG represents a non-invasive technique for recording whole-brain neural activity; all participants will be fitted with a 32-channel EEG cap, and pre- versus post-intervention comparisons will identify AA-induced neural alterations implicated in emotion regulation.

An underlying limitation of this pilot trial is the absence of a follow-up period, a design choice made to focus on evaluating the immediate feasibility and acceptability of AA in a young adult population. Given that PMDD symptoms fluctuate across the menstrual cycle, the lack of longitudinal data means we cannot determine whether any improvements are sustained or whether they coincide with natural symptom variations. This is particularly relevant because AA is self-administered, and long-term adherence may be influenced by symptom patterns and daily routines. Future research should incorporate brief, cycle-aligned follow-up assessments to clarify the durability of effects and identify key time points for intervention reinforcement. Additionally, integrating objective measures of adherence, such as pressure-sensitive plasters or electronic trackers, would reduce reliance on self-report and more accurately reflect how adherence impacts treatment outcomes, thereby providing a stronger basis for refining protocols in subsequent RCTs.

Conclusion

This pilot study protocol will develop a multimodal assessment paradigm combining subjective psychometric scales and objective resting-state EEG recordings for interventional research into PMDD. The study will adopt a dual-control comparative design to strengthen its internal validity, and the resulting empirical findings will inform the design of subsequent large-scale definitive RCTs. Beyond these methodological improvements, this protocol highlights the translational value of AA as a low-burden complementary intervention for young women with PMDD. Furthermore, the integrated multimodal assessment and testing system outlined herein provides a key reference for future mechanistic research on TCM external therapies targeting emotional dysfunction.
