First in vitro feeding of the Australian marsupial tick, Ixodes hirsti Hassall, 1931, with preliminary microbiome profiling and observations on the nymphal morphology
Authors: Vincent C Duru, Bahar E Mustafa, Ian Beveridge, Charles Gauci, Khawla Elati, Abdul Ghafar, Ard M Nijhof, Abdul Jabbar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial tick feeding systems (ATFS) provide a valuable alternative to animal-based models for studying tick biology. Ixodes hirsti, an Australian tick species that parasitises marsupials, remains understudied due to challenges in laboratory maintenance. Here, we report the first successful in vitro feeding of I. hirsti larvae, provide preliminary microbiome profiles of unfed larvae and larvae recovered after artificial feeding and present the first molecularly confirmed morphological description of the nymphal stage. Field-collected engorged females of I. hirsti were allowed to oviposit under laboratory conditions. Hatched larvae were artificially fed on blood using silicone membranes supplemented with kangaroo hair and/or kangaroo hair extract. Microbiomes were characterised by 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing, while scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and sequencing of 16S rRNA and cox1 genes were used for morphological and molecular characterisation of nymphs. Membranes treated with hair extract alone yielded the highest attachment rate (71%), whereas kangaroo hair-treated membranes produced superior feeding performance, with shorter time to engorgement (9.26 ± 1.00 days) and a higher engorgement weight (0.91 ± 0.01 mg). Exploratory microbiome profiling showed that fed larval pools had numerically lower microbial richness and evenness than unfed larval pools, although these differences were not significant. A total of 80 microbial taxa were shared between groups, whereas seven and 17 taxa were unique to fed and unfed larvae, respectively. Stenotrophomonas was more abundant in fed larval pools, while Coxiella-like and Rickettsia-like endosymbionts were detected in both fed and unfed larvae. These findings demonstrate that ATFS can be adapted for wildlife-associated ticks with specialised host preferences and provide a practical framework for investigating the biology and microbial ecology of ticks.Keywords: Artificial tick feeding, Ixodes hirsti, In vitro, Marsupial tick, Microbiome, Morphology, Ultrastructure, Tick biology

Introduction

Ticks are ubiquitous ectoparasites and among the most important arthropod vectors of pathogens of medical and veterinary significance. More than 900 tick species have been recognised globally, of which approximately 70 occur in Australia (Bedford, 1931; Guglielmone et al., 2010; Mans et al., 2011; Barker et al., 2014). Of these, 14 are soft ticks (Argasidae) and 56 are hard ticks (Ixodidae) (Barker et al., 2014). Although Australia’s tick fauna is unique, several species, including Argas persicus, Otobius megnini, Haemaphysalis longicornis, Rhipicephalus australis and Rhipicephalus linnaei (formerly included within Rhipicephalus sanguineus sensu lato) (Šlapeta et al., 2022) were introduced during European colonisation (Barker et al., 2014; Greay et al., 2016).

Owing to its geographical isolation and strict biosecurity measures, Australia is free of many globally prevalent tick-borne diseases (TBDs) (Graves et al., 2016; Greay et al., 2016). Nevertheless, tick bites in Australia are associated with a diverse range of human and animal health conditions. These include infections caused by viruses (e.g. mammalian orthoreovirus, Colorado tick fever-like orbiviruses), bacteria (e.g. Rickettsia australis, Rickettsia honei, Coxiella burnetii, Anaplasma bovis, “Candidatus Neoehrlichia arcana”, and “Candidatus Neoehrlichia australis”), and protozoans (e.g. Babesia vogeli, B. gibsoni, B. bigemina, and Theileria orientalis) (Graves et al., 2016). In addition to pathogen transmission, tick bites can trigger allergic syndromes (Brown and Hamilton, 1998), tick paralysis (Roberts, 1961), tick-induced mammalian meat allergy (van Nunen, 2018), post-infectious fatigue (Watts et al., 2008), and the debilitating symptom complexes attributed to ticks (DSCATT) (Schnall et al., 2022).

Australia is globally renowned for its unique and geographically distinct wildlife, with more than 80% of species occurring nowhere else in the world (Legge et al., 2023). This unique biodiversity, shaped by millions of years of isolation, includes marsupials (e.g. kangaroos, wallabies, possums, bandicoots, koalas and wombats), monotremes (platypus and echidna) and diverse reptile, amphibian and avian taxa (Legge et al., 2023; Woinarski and Fisher, 2023). However, these species are increasingly threatened by climate change, habitat destruction, invasive species, wildfires and emerging infectious diseases (Woinarski and Fisher, 2023; Cope et al., 2024; Lynch et al., 2025), with approximately 40% of Australian marsupial species now considered at risk of extinction (Woinarski and Fisher, 2023). In this context, parasites and vectors associated with native wildlife, including ticks, represent an underexplored interface between biodiversity conservation, animal health and emerging disease risk. Understanding the biology of wildlife-associated ticks is therefore increasingly important for both biodiversity conservation and One Health efforts.

Ixodes hirsti Hassall, 1931, also known as Hirst’s marsupial tick, is a three-host tick parasitising Australian marsupials (Barker et al., 2014) and is distributed along coastal and subcoastal regions from the Blue Mountains in New South Wales to Albany in Western Australia (Laan et al., 2011), including Tasmania and Kangaroo Island (Oorebeek et al., 2009; Laan et al., 2011). The immature stages are known to feed on passerine birds in southern Australia, whereas adults parasitise a range of marsupials, including kangaroos, wallabies and koalas (Oorebeek et al., 2009). Despite its ecological relevance and potential involvement in tick paralysis (Roberts, 1961), the biology, life history and vector potential of I. hirsti remain poorly understood (Oorebeek et al., 2009; Laan et al., 2011).

A major limitation to experimental investigation of this species has been the inability to maintain and study immature stages under laboratory conditions. Previous attempts to feed the larval stage of I. hirsti on experimental hosts such as rats and chickens were unsuccessful, with larvae failing to attach or feed on either host species (Laan et al., 2011). Consequently, key aspects of its developmental biology remain unresolved. Earlier work by Roberts in 1960 described the immature stages as ‘unknown’ (Roberts, 1960), and although Laan et al. (2011) later reported a putative nymph, this identification was based on locality assumptions and lacked confirmation through breeding or molecular characterisation, limiting confidence in species assignment.

Artificial tick feeding systems (ATFS) for haematophagous arthropods have gained increasing attention as alternatives to animal-based models, in line with the principles of Replacement, Reduction and Refinement (3Rs) (Nijhof and Tyson, 2018). ATFS provide powerful platforms for studying tick physiology, host-pathogen interactions and acaricide screening under standardised conditions (Kröber and Guerin, 2007; Nijhof and Tyson, 2018; Mustafa et al., 2026). However, their application to Australian ticks has been limited, with only a small number of studies involving Ixodes holocyclus and cattle ticks (Kemp et al., 1975, 1989; Stone et al., 1983; Mustafa et al., 2026). More recently, Ghafar et al. (2026) demonstrated a reproducible membrane-based feeding system for adult H. longicornis, highlighting the potential of such systems for controlled laboratory studies.

The tick microbiome is increasingly recognised as an important component of tick biology, with potential roles in internal homeostasis, development, vector competence, pathogen colonisation and tick-host co-evolutionary dynamics (Wu-Chuang et al., 2021). However, microbiome data for many Australian wildlife-associated tick species remain scarce. For poorly studied species such as I. hirsti, preliminary characterisation of tick-associated bacterial communities can provide useful baseline information and guide future studies, particularly when new laboratory feeding systems are being established (Wu-Chuang et al., 2021).

In this study, we established, for the first time, an in vitro feeding system for larvae of Ixodes hirsti using kangaroo-derived attachment stimuli and evaluated feeding performance under controlled laboratory conditions. We also generated preliminary microbiome profiles from pooled unfed larvae and larvae recovered after artificial feeding and provided the first molecularly confirmed ultrastructural description of the nymphal stage. Together, these data address key gaps in the biology of this Australian wildlife-associated tick and provide a foundation for future studies on its development, artificial feeding optimisation and tick-associated microbial communities.

Materials and methods

Collection and maintenance of ticks

Engorged female I. hirsti ticks were collected from wild eastern grey kangaroos (Macropus giganteus) at a wildlife shelter at Bullengarook, Victoria, Australia (37°31′S, 144° 29′E) during routine grooming by wildlife shelter staff. Ticks were identified morphologically using standard dichotomous keys (Roberts, 1970). Live ticks were maintained in a desiccator with the lower compartment containing saturated magnesium sulphate solution to maintain a relative humidity of approximately 85–90%. Eggs laid by engorged females were incubated under these conditions, and larvae were used for experiments at 14 weeks post-hatching. Artificial tick feeding experiments were carried out between May and June 2025, corresponding to autumn in Australia.

In vitro feeding of I. hirsti larvae

Silicone membrane preparation

Silicone-coated goldbeater skin membranes for artificial tick feeding were prepared as previously described (Kröber and Guerin, 2007; Krull et al., 2017). Membrane thickness was measured using a micrometer screw gauge (Eisco Labs, Rochester, USA) and membranes with a thickness of 30–50 μm were selected for larval feeding.

Artificial tick feeding system (ATFS)

Cylindrical glass tubes (30 mm inner diameter, 2 mm wall thickness, 65 mm height) were attached to silicone membranes using Elastosil E41 silicone adhesive (Wacker Chemie, Munich, Germany) and left to cure overnight under a 500 g weight to ensure uniform adhesion. Feeding units were excised from the membrane sheets and tested for leakage by immersion in 70% ethanol for 30 min. After drying, units were subsequently sterilised under ultraviolet (UV) light for 1 h and placed in autoclaved 50 ml glass beakers using rubber rings to suspend them 5–10 mm above the base.

Bloodmeal and attachment stimuli

Feeding units were treated with kangaroo hair and/or kangaroo hair extracts as attachment stimuli. Hair was collected from freshly road-killed kangaroos via the Anatomic Pathology Unit at the Melbourne Veterinary School and surface-decontaminated by UV exposure for 1 h. Hair extracts were prepared by sequential immersion of 2.5 g of fresh kangaroo hair in chloroform: methanol mixtures (2:1, 1:1, and 1:2, i.e. 8 ml:4 ml; 6 ml:6 ml and 4 ml:8 ml, respectively) for 2 h each, with the final extraction performed at 45 °C. Supernatants were combined, filter-sterilised and stored at −20 °C until use. For each feeding unit, 100 μl of extract was applied to the membrane and allowed to evaporate for at least 2 h prior to larval introduction.

Defibrinated bovine blood (Serum Australis, Tamworth, Australia) was supplemented upon arrival with 2 g/l glucose, aliquoted, and stored at 4 °C. Bovine blood was used because it was commercially available, quality controlled and obtainable in sufficient volume for artificial feeding experiments; marsupial blood was not readily available in sufficient quantities under standardised and ethically practical conditions. Immediately before use, adenosine triphosphate (ATP; 0.1 M in 0.9% NaCl; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA), glucose (2 mg/ml), and gentamicin (5 μg/ml; Sigma-Aldrich) were added. The bloodmeal was warmed to 37 °C before feeding.

In vitro feeding procedure

To evaluate the effects of attachment stimuli, feeding experiments were conducted using three treatment groups: (i) hair; (ii) hair extract; and (iii) hair and hair extract. Feeding was conducted in three separate experiments. The hair and hair-and-hair-extract treatments were included in all three experiments, whereas the hair extract treatment was assessed only in Experiment 3. Experiments 1 and 2 used two feeding units per treatment, and Experiment 3 used one feeding unit per treatment, resulting in a total of 11 feeding units. Approximately 60–200 larvae were introduced into each unit. Exact per-unit larval numbers could not be reliably determined because unfed larvae were very small, highly mobile and frequently clustered during transfer; therefore, larval numbers are reported as approximate ranges rather than precise unit-level counts. Following treatment of membranes, feeding units were surface-decontaminated under UV light for 1 h and larvae were introduced immediately thereafter. Units were sealed with polyurethane foam plugs (K-TK e.K., Retzstadt, Germany) to permit gas exchange while preventing larval escape.

Feeding units were suspended in 50 ml glass beakers containing 5 ml of pre-warmed bloodmeal. Feeding was conducted in complete darkness in an incubator (NuAire, USA; model NU-5741E) at 27 °C, 70–80% relative humidity and 5% CO2. Beakers were placed on a heating plate (Ratek, Melbourne, Australia) set to 37 °C to maintain blood temperature. Blood was replaced twice daily (10–14 h intervals) under sterile conditions in a biosafety cabinet. During each replacement, feeding units were transferred to fresh autoclaved beakers containing a newly prepared bloodmeal. The external surface of feeding units and membrane undersides were rinsed with 0.9% NaCl, and 1% nystatin in phosphate-buffered saline was applied when fungal contamination was observed. Attached larvae were counted visually during routine blood changes but were not manually detached from the membrane. Only dead larvae and larvae that had naturally detached after engorgement were removed from the feeding units. Nymphs obtained after moulting were deposited in the Australian National Insect Collection (ANIC), Canberra, Australia (database number: ANIC 48-006621).

Sample preparation for DNA extraction

Pooled samples of unfed (n = 30 larvae per pool) and fully fed (n = 30 larvae per pool) larvae were prepared, with two biological replicate pools per group (total n = 4 pools). Pools were surface-sterilised by immersion in 5% sodium hypochlorite (LabCo Scientific, Brisbane, Australia) for 5 min with gentle agitation, followed by two rinses in sterile distilled water as described previously (Hoffmann et al., 2020). Samples were then stored in 70% ethanol until DNA extraction. Pooling was performed to increase DNA yield and obtain group-level microbiome profiles. However, this approach precluded assessment of individual-level microbiome variation (Dada et al., 2018). Because only two pooled biological replicates were analysed per feeding status, the microbiome analysis was considered exploratory and intended to provide preliminary profiles of unfed larvae and larvae recovered after artificial feeding.

Genomic DNA was extracted using the Qiagen Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Samples were triturated, resuspended in T1 buffer with proteinase K, and incubated at 56 °C overnight. DNA was eluted in 60 μl nuclease-free water and stored at −20 °C. DNA quantity and quality were assessed using a Qubit fluorometer and NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific).

16S rRNA library preparation and microbiome sequencing

Microbial communities were profiled using 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) sequencing targeting the V4 region. Libraries were prepared using a two-step PCR protocol and sequenced using the NextSeq 1000/2000 P1 XLEAP RGTS Kit (600 cycles) on an Illumina NextSeq 1000 platform to generate paired-end reads (Ghafar et al., 2020). Amplification was performed using modified universal primers 515F/806R (Supplementary Table S1) (Apprill et al., 2015; Parada et al., 2016). Negative controls (nuclease-free water) and positive controls (ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community DNA Standard) were also included in each PCR/sequencing round. The bovine bloodmeal was not subjected to microbiome sequencing; therefore, potential microbial contributions from the bloodmeal could not be assessed.

Morphological and molecular characterisation of nymphs

Nymphs obtained from in vitro feeding were used for morphological and molecular characterisation. For scanning electron microscopy (SEM), six nymphs were cleaned in absolute glycerol, dehydrated through graded ethanol (70–100%), air-dried, and mounted on conductive carbon tape. Samples were examined using a Phenom XL G3 desktop SEM at 10 kV. Morphometric measurements were performed using an Olympus BH-2 microscope equipped with an ocular micrometer. All measurements of nymphal structures are reported in micrometres (μm) as the range followed by the mean in parentheses.

For molecular characterisation, genomic DNA was extracted from a pool of three nymphs using the GeneJET Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. PCR amplification targeted partial fragments of mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1) and 16S ribosomal RNA (16S rRNA) loci using published PCR primers (Ghafar et al., 2023) (Supplementary Table S1). PCR products were purified and Sanger sequenced, and consensus sequences were assembled using Geneious Prime v.2026.0. Sequences were deposited in GenBank under the accession numbers PZ299444 (16S rRNA gene) and PZ299448 (cox1 gene).

Statistical and microbiome data analysis

Artificial feeding data were analysed in R v.4.3.2 using the packages dplyr, rstatix, ggplot2 and kableExtra. Data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD), and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Attachment and engorgement rates were visualised using grouped bar plots with 95% confidence intervals. Temporal attachment patterns were assessed by calculating the cumulative proportion of attached larvae at 0–48 h and 48–72 h post-exposure for each treatment. For feeding experiments, the feeding unit was considered the experimental unit. Individual larvae within the same feeding unit were treated as subsamples because they shared the same membrane, bloodmeal and environmental conditions. Fisher’s exact tests were used for exploratory comparisons of attachment and engorgement counts, and results were interpreted cautiously because larvae within the same feeding unit were not fully independent.

Given the limited number of pooled biological replicates, microbiome findings were interpreted as exploratory and descriptive. Microbiome analysis was performed using QIIME 2 v.2024.2 (Bolyen et al., 2019). Demultiplexed reads were denoised, quality-filtered and merged using DADA2 (Callahan et al., 2016), and primer sequences were removed using Cutadapt. Taxonomic assignment of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) was performed using a naïve Bayes classifier trained on the SILVA database (release 138.2) (Bokulich et al., 2018). Data were imported into R for downstream analysis using phyloseq and vegan. Contaminant taxa were identified using the decontam package (Davis et al., 2018) (frequency method, threshold = 0.5) based on DNA concentration measured using a Qubit fluorometer.

Alpha diversity was assessed using complementary metrics that capture different aspects of within-sample bacterial community structure. Observed richness estimates the number of detected ASVs, Faith’s phylogenetic diversity incorporates the phylogenetic breadth of detected ASVs, Shannon’s diversity accounts for both richness and relative abundance, and Pielou’s evenness evaluates how evenly reads are distributed among detected taxa (Shannon, 1948; Pielou, 1966; Faith, 1992; DeSantis et al., 2006). Alpha diversity metrics were calculated on rarefied data and compared using Kruskal-Wallis tests (P < 0.05). Rarefaction was performed to standardise sequencing depth across samples to 200,000 reads per sample, with 100 iterations. Community dominance was assessed using Simpson’s index (Simpson, 1949) and the Berger-Parker index (Berger and Parker, 1970), which were included as supplementary measures to describe whether communities were dominated by one or a few taxa rather than being evenly distributed.

Beta diversity was assessed using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, Jaccard dissimilarity, and weighted and unweighted UniFrac distances (Jaccard, 1908; Bray and Curtis, 1957; Lozupone et al., 2011). Ordination was performed using principal coordinates analysis (PCoA), and three-dimensional PCoA visualisations were generated using plotly in R. Differences in community composition were tested using PERMANOVA with the “adonis2” function using 999 permutations (Dixon, 2003), and homogeneity of multivariate dispersion was evaluated using betadisper. Taxonomic composition was summarised as relative abundance at phylum, family and genus levels. Differential abundance analyses using DESeq2 (Love et al., 2014) and ALDEx2 (Fernandes et al., 2013) were performed as exploratory analyses only, and results were interpreted cautiously because of the limited number of pooled biological replicates.

Results

Effect of attachment stimuli on feeding initiation

In vitro feeding of I. hirsti larvae was conducted across three separate experiments using a total of 880 larvae. Exploratory Fisher’s exact tests indicated that attachment stimulus significantly affected feeding initiation at the early stages, both at 48 h (P < 0.001) and 72 h (P = 0.0004) post-exposure. Holm-adjusted pairwise comparisons are presented in Supplementary Table S2. Across all replicates, larvae in hair-only treatments showed the highest early attachment, with 149/458 (32.5%) attached by 48 h, increasing to 227/458 (49.6%) by 72 h (Fig. 1).

Attachment dynamics and feeding behaviour

Larvae exhibited typical questing and exploratory behaviour prior to attachment, followed by anchoring and cluster-feeding, particularly along the edges of feeding units. Aggregation and feeding of the larvae increased over successive days until repletion (Fig. 2). The overall attachment rates differed numerically among treatments, but the differences were not statistically significant (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.4). Because larvae were clustered within feeding units, these comparisons were interpreted cautiously. The highest overall attachment rate was observed in hair extract units (49/69; 71%), followed by units with hair only (296/458; 64.6%) (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Feeding performance and post-feeding outcomes

More than half of the larvae in all treatments fed to engorgement (Table 1). At the individual-larva level, larvae in hair-only units exhibited a shorter mean time to engorgement compared with those in treatments containing hair extract alone or in combination (one-way ANOVA, F(2, 520) = 31.45, P < 0.001). Tukey’s post-hoc test showed that larvae in hair-only units had a shorter mean time to engorgement than those in hair-and-hair-extract units and the hair-extract-only unit, whereas the two extract-containing treatments did not differ significantly. This analysis was considered exploratory because larvae were clustered within feeding units.

Mean engorgement weight ranged from 0.66 to 0.91 mg, with the highest value recorded in the hair-only treatment (0.91 ± 0.01 mg; Table 1, Supplementary Fig. S2). Treatments containing only hair extract supported engorgement but were associated with longer feeding duration (10.00 ± 0.00 days) and lower engorgement weight (0.66 mg; Table 1). Exploratory microbiome profiling of unfed and artificially fed larvae

Because microbiome sequencing was performed on two pooled biological replicates per feeding status, these results are presented as exploratory group-level profiles. DNA sequencing of pooled fed and unfed larvae generated 299,053 and 338,502 raw reads for unfed samples and 404,736 and 448,235 reads for fed samples. More than 96% of reads passed quality filtering, with a mean post-trimming read length of 253 bp in both groups.

Alpha diversity analysis of rarefied data showed numerically higher microbial richness and evenness in unfed larval pools than in fed larval pools across the measured indices (Fig. 3). However, these differences were not statistically significant for observed richness (χ2 = 2.4, df = 1, P = 0.121) or evenness (χ2 = 2.4, df = 1, P = 0.121) (Fig. 3). Given the limited number of pooled biological replicates, this pattern should be interpreted cautiously and considered preliminary. Simpson’s and Berger-Parker dominance indices were both numerically higher in fed than unfed larval pools, consistent with their lower evenness, though not statistically significant (Fig. 4).

After decontamination, 104 taxa were retained, with 3 taxa removed as contaminants. Of these, 80 microbial taxa (76.9%) were shared between fed and unfed larvae, seven taxa (6.7%) were unique to fed larvae, and seventeen taxa (16.4%) were unique to unfed larvae. At the phylum level, Pseudomonadota dominated both groups. At the family level, Lysobacteraceae and Rickettsiaceae were relatively more abundant in fed and unfed larvae, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S3). At the genus level, Stenotrophomonas was more abundant in fed larval pools, while members from the genera Coxiella and Rickettsia were detected in both groups (Supplementary Fig. S3). BLAST analysis of selected partial Coxiella 16S rRNA sequences showed 100% sequence identity with Coxiella-like endosymbionts previously identified in H. longicornis and R. australis. The obtained 16S rRNA Rickettsia sequences could not be assigned to the species level due to the highly conserved nature of this gene region. Exploratory differential abundance analyses identified multiple taxa with apparent differences between fed and unfed larval pools; however, these results should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited number of pooled biological replicates and the artificial feeding conditions (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Remarks

The laboratory-reared nymphs showed a combination of characters consistent with I. hirsti, including a broadly oval idiosoma, prominent lateral carinae, a well-defined marginal groove, absence of coxal ridges, prominent external spurs on coxae I-III, absence of an external spur on coxa IV, a basis capituli with cornua absent, a lanceolate and acutely pointed hypostome with 2/2 dentition, and an anal groove surrounding the anus anteriorly with the posterior arms converging behind it. These characters closely resemble the putative Ixodes nymph described by Laan et al. (2011), particularly in overall body shape, prominent carinae, scutal proportions, hypostome shape, 2/2 dentition, number of hypostomal tooth rows, and posteriorly converging anal grooves, although the present specimens were somewhat smaller in idiosomal and hypostomal dimensions. The nymphs also resemble I. trichosuri as described by Roberts (1960), sharing an oval body, absence of cornua, a pointed 2/2 hypostome, and posteriorly converging anal grooves. The present material differed from both prior descriptions, however, in lacking an external spur on coxa IV, whereas both Laan et al. (2011) and Roberts (1960) reported a minute spur on coxa IV (Supplementary Table S3). Together with laboratory rearing from morphologically identified females and molecular confirmation using 16S rRNA and cox1 sequences, this combination of characters supports the identification of the nymphs as I. hirsti.

Discussion

In this study, we established, for the first time, an in vitro feeding system for larvae of I. hirsti, an endemic Australian wildlife-associated tick, and demonstrated that host-derived attachment stimuli can support feeding under controlled laboratory conditions. We also provided exploratory microbiome profiles of pooled unfed larvae and larvae recovered after artificial feeding and presented the first molecularly confirmed ultrastructural description of the nymphal stage. Together, these findings address a longstanding methodological constraint on the study of this species and provide a framework for future investigations of its biology and vector potential.

To our knowledge, this is the first successful artificial feeding of the immature stages of I. hirsti. Previous attempts to feed larvae on experimental hosts, including rats and chickens, were unsuccessful due to failure of attachment and feeding (Laan et al., 2011). The establishment of a membrane-based system therefore represents a key methodological advancement, enabling controlled experimental studies that were previously not feasible. Artificial feeding systems are increasingly used for haematophagous arthropods and align with the principles of Replacement, Reduction and Refinement (3Rs) (Nijhof and Tyson, 2018), while providing standardised platforms for studying tick physiology and host-pathogen interactions. Since their inception in the early 20th century (Hindle and Merriman, 1912), in vitro feeding systems have been applied to numerous hard and soft tick species (Kröber and Guerin, 2007; Militzer et al., 2021; Castro-Santiago et al., 2023; Elati et al., 2024; Pature et al., 2025; Ghafar et al., 2026), yet their application to Australian ticks has remained limited (Stone et al., 1983).

Membrane characteristics and attachment stimuli were critical determinants of feeding success and the use of a thin silicone-coated goldbeater skin membrane was central to the feeding success achieved in this study. Membrane thickness and elasticity are known to be critical determinants of tick attachment and feeding performance, particularly for immature stages with short hypostomes (Pature et al., 2025). In our study, we used a goldbeater skin coated with a silicone layer having a thickness of 30–50 μm, because the average length of the hypostome of I. hirsti larvae is about 120 μm (Laan et al., 2011). Using such a thin membrane resulted in attachment rates exceeding those reported for I. ricinus larvae under comparable conditions (Militzer et al., 2021).

Attachment stimuli play a key role in promoting feeding initiation in ticks, providing mechanical and chemical cues that are central to the transition from questing to attachment (Crooks and Randolph, 2006). The use of animal hair and hair extracts has been shown to enhance attachment in several tick species (Krull et al., 2017; Militzer et al., 2021). In our study, kangaroo hair significantly improved early feeding performance compared with hair extract treatments. This contrasts with earlier reports that I. hirsti larvae respond primarily to shade and heat but not host odour or CO2 (Oorebeek et al., 2009). This discrepancy may reflect differences in experimental design, particularly the use of non-natural hosts in previous studies, as chicken feathers were used as a substitute for host-derived cues (Oorebeek et al., 2009). Other studies that have used experimental rats and chickens to investigate the feeding behaviour of I. hirsti larvae have also reported no larval attachment and survival beyond 24 h on either host species (Laan et al., 2011).

Environmental conditions further contributed to feeding success. The use of 5% CO2, elevated temperature and high relative humidity is consistent with conditions known to activate host-seeking and feeding in other Ixodes species (Duijvendijk et al., 2017; Breitbach and Lai, 2024). The attachment dynamics observed in our study closely resemble those reported for R. australis and Hyalomma spp. under comparable artificial feeding conditions (Trentelman et al., 2017; Elati et al., 2024).

Feeding performance metrics for I. hirsti larvae differed from those reported for other species, with longer times to engorgement and intermediate engorgement weights relative to I. ricinus, Hyalomma spp. and Amblyomma spp. (Militzer et al., 2021; Elati et al., 2024; Pature et al., 2025). These differences may reflect a combination of species-specific biology and artificial-feeding conditions, including membrane properties, blood source, attachment stimuli and incubation conditions.

In this study, moulting success from engorged larvae to nymphs was low, consistent with the central role of attaining a critical weight for successful ecdysis (Koch, 1986; Kaufman, 2007; Richards et al., 2017; Pature et al., 2025). However, a precise critical engorgement weight for I. hirsti could not be determined in the present study because individual engorged larvae were not tracked through moulting. Fungal contamination and post-detachment handling may also have affected larval survival and moulting, although contamination was not quantified systematically. In addition, there are currently no data on the critical engorgement weight of naturally fed I. hirsti larvae, limiting direct comparison. Several factors may have contributed to this low moulting success, including the use of bovine blood supplemented with antibiotics rather than the natural hosts’ bloodmeal (Oorebeek and Kleindorfer, 2008), prolonged pre-feeding starvation (Krull et al., 2017), off-host environmental stress, and potential seasonal diapause effects (Belozerov, 1964). The use of bovine rather than marsupial blood may have influenced feeding duration, engorgement weight, moulting success and microbiome composition, because it differs from the natural bloodmeal of I. hirsti. Future studies should compare artificially fed and naturally fed larvae and link individual engorgement weight with moulting outcome to determine the developmental threshold required for successful ecdysis in I. hirsti.

As an exploratory component of this study, we profiled bacterial communities in pooled unfed larvae and larvae recovered after artificial feeding. Blood-feeding represents a major physiological transition in ticks, altering nutrient availability, oxidative stress and immune activity (Horn et al., 2009; Kopáček et al., 2018), and has been associated with contraction of microbial diversity in other tick systems (Brinkerhoff et al., 2020; Militzer et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2024). In the present study, fed larval pools showed numerically lower microbial richness and evenness than unfed pools, and Stenotrophomonas was more abundant after artificial feeding. The presence of Coxiella-like endosymbionts and Rickettsia spp. is consistent with findings from other studies on the microbiome of Ixodes ticks (Kumar et al., 2022; Militzer et al., 2023). The greater relative abundance of Stenotrophomonas in fed larval pools may reflect its metabolic versatility and tolerance of oxidative conditions during blood digestion (Ryan et al., 2009). However, as gentamicin was included in the bloodmeal to limit contamination during artificial feeding, its potential impact on microbiome composition cannot be excluded and may have contributed to the observed reduction in diversity and possibly selection for tolerant taxa, including Stenotrophomonas. In addition, because the bovine bloodmeal was not sequenced, we could not determine whether any detected bacterial taxa were derived from the larvae, the bloodmeal or the artificial feeding system. Therefore, these microbiome results should be interpreted as preliminary profiles generated under the artificial feeding conditions used here, rather than definitive evidence that haematophagy alone altered the larval microbial community.

Our study also provides a detailed ultrastructural characterisation of I. hirsti nymphs using SEM, complemented by molecular confirmation based on mitochondrial gene markers. Previous studies have been limited in their ability to accurately describe the immature stages of this species, largely due to challenges in collecting nymphal stages and the absence of robust morphological keys for the subgenus Sternalixodes (Laan et al., 2011; Kwak, 2017). Notably, Laan et al. (2011) described a putative Ixodes nymph presumed to be I. hirsti; however, this identification lacked confirmatory breeding data and molecular validation using established genetic markers, thereby limiting confidence in species assignment.

Within the subgenus Sternalixodes, the morphology of I. hirsti nymphs most closely resembles that of Ixodes trichosuri (Roberts, 1960). Roberts (1960) described I. trichosuri nymphs as possessing an oval idiosoma measuring approximately 1000 × 700 μm when partially engorged, with visible lateral carinae and marginal grooves. Similarly, Laan et al. (2011) reported an oval idiosoma with prominent lateral carinae and marginal grooves in their putative I. hirsti nymph. These features are consistent with those observed in our specimens, which exhibited a broadly oval idiosoma with well-defined marginal grooves and dimensions comparable to those previously reported (Fig. 6A and B).

Despite these similarities, several distinguishing features were observed. In I. trichosuri, all coxae bear external spurs, with those on coxa IV described as minute (Roberts, 1960). Laan et al. (2011) also described a minute spur on coxa IV in their putative nymph. In contrast, we found prominent external spurs only on coxae I-III, with no spurs seen on coxa IV (Fig. 6C). This represents a potentially important diagnostic difference, as members of the subgenus Sternalixodes have been reported to exhibit variation in coxal spur morphology (Roberts, 1960).

The basis capituli of our I. hirsti nymph was rectangular and lacked cornua, consistent with descriptions of I. trichosuri nymphs by Roberts (1960). Roberts (1960) described the hypostome of I. trichosuri as approximately 300 μm in length, acutely pointed, with 2/2 dentition and around 16 rows of teeth. In our study, the hypostome was slightly shorter (mean 280 μm), lanceolate and acutely pointed, with 2/2 dentition comprising approximately 18 rows of teeth. In contrast, Laan et al. (2011) reported a longer hypostome (∼400 μm) for their putative specimen.

The configuration of the anal groove represents another key diagnostic feature within Sternalixodes. In I. holocyclus, the anal groove converges posteriorly but does not fully close, remaining narrowly open (Roberts, 1960). In contrast, both I. trichosuri and I. hirsti exhibit anal grooves that converge and meet posterior to the anus. This shared characteristic supports the close morphological relationship between these two species while distinguishing them from other members of the subgenus.

Overall, the morphological similarities between I. hirsti and I. trichosuri nymphs, coupled with the limited availability of detailed descriptions for other species of Sternalixodes, limit the reliable differentiation of immature stages based solely on morphology. This highlights a broader gap in the taxonomy of the subgenus, where immature stages remain poorly characterised.

Furthermore, molecular analyses confirmed the identity of our specimen as I. hirsti, with BLAST searches of cox1 and 16S rRNA sequences yielding 99–100% identity to reference sequences and high query coverage. The integration of morphological and molecular data therefore provides robust evidence for species identification and will be important for resolving species boundaries and improving diagnostic frameworks for immature ticks within this subgenus.

The microbiome component of this study should be interpreted within clear limitations. Sequencing was performed on two pooled biological replicates per feeding status, with each pool comprising 30 larvae. This design increased DNA yield and enabled group-level profiling, but it limited statistical power and prevented assessment of individual-level microbiome variation. In addition, naturally fed I. hirsti larvae were not available for comparison, and the microbiome of the bovine bloodmeal was not characterised. The use of gentamicin in the bloodmeal may also have altered bacterial community composition and selected for antibiotic-tolerant taxa. Therefore, observed differences between unfed larvae and larvae recovered after artificial feeding cannot be attributed solely to blood-feeding and should not be interpreted as evidence that artificial feeding reproduces natural host feeding. Future studies incorporating naturally fed larvae, bloodmeal controls, antibiotic-free feeding where feasible, individual-level sampling and larger numbers of biological replicates will be required to define natural microbiome dynamics in this species.

Despite these limitations, this study establishes a practical framework for the artificial feeding of a wildlife-associated tick species and provides a foundation for future investigations into its biology, development and potential vector competence. The ability to experimentally manipulate feeding in I. hirsti represents an important advance for controlled laboratory studies of Australian wildlife-associated ticks and creates new opportunities to investigate I. hirsti feeding biology, development and tick-associated microbial communities under controlled laboratory conditions.

Conclusion

This study establishes the first in vitro feeding system for I. hirsti larvae and shows that attachment stimuli are important for feeding initiation under artificial conditions. Exploratory microbiome profiling generated preliminary baseline data for unfed and artificially fed larvae, including apparent differences in diversity and taxonomic composition between pooled groups. Morphological and molecular analyses also provide new insights into the biology of this species. Together, these findings provide a novel experimental framework for in vitro feeding and microbiome assessment in a wildlife-associated tick. Further optimisation across life stages will be essential to improve feeding efficiency and to enable deeper investigation of the biology and potential vector competence of I. hirsti.

Ethical approval

Engorged ticks were collected opportunistically from wild eastern grey kangaroos during routine grooming by wildlife shelter staff.
