Taxonomic revision of the genus Blastobotrys with description of seven novel species and three new combinations
Authors: M. Groenewald, M. Hernández-Restrepo, A. Zandijk, M. Smith, D. Gouliamova, C. Decock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Blastobotrys is a genus of yeasts that primarily reproduce asexually; however, it also encompasses sexual species that were formerly classified within the genus Trichomonascus. Blastobotrys species are known for their diverse origin as they can be found in numerous locations worldwide and on various types of substrates. During a series of independent sampling events in Belgium, Bulgaria, and France, a set of strains were newly isolated from basidiomes, decaying wood, and insects. As a result of this research, seven novel species were identified and described, including previously deposited strains from the CBS collection. This was done by means of a combination of phylogenetic analysis, using the ITS regions and the D1/D2 domain of the large subunit (LSU) rRNA gene, and physiological characteristics. Additionally, based on phylogenetic data generated during this study and phylogenomic analyses available from published data, three new combinations of species previously included in Trichomonascus are also proposed. Furthermore, using the data from metabarcoding studies included in the GlobalFungi database and those known from Blastobotrys strains available from the CBS yeast collection, the currently known distribution and substrate range of Blastobotrys strains are discussed.Citation: Groenewald M, Hernández-Restrepo M, Zandijk A, Smith M, Gouliamova D, Decock C (2026). INTRODUCTION

The yeast genus Blastobotrys (Trichomonascaceae, Dipodascales, Dipodascomycetes, Saccharomycotina) (Groenewald et al. 2023) is widely distributed and reported worldwide, except Australia, where it remains unreported to date (Kurtzman et al. 2011, https://theyeasts.org). Species of Blastobotrys are able to grow on and have been isolated from a wide and uncommon variety of substrates such as mushrooms (Basidiomycetes), insects and other animals including humans, plant debris, wood (including archaeological), moss, soil, dung, compost, nectar, food (honey), paper, air/dust, wall painting/plaster, seawater, and linoleum (Kurtzman et al. 2011, Visagie et al. 2023, https://theyeasts.org). Although isolates mostly originate from environmental substrates (natural or anthropogenic), some species such as B. adeninivorans, B. mokoenaii, B. raffinosifermentans, B. proliferans, B. serpentis and Trichomonascus ciferrii (formerly Stephanoascus ciferrii), have been isolated from humans as the causal agents of ear, eye or skin infections (Cosio et al. 2024), from pulmonary infections in a cystic fibrosis patient (Al-Obaidi et al. 2021), and an invasive fungal infection in an immunocompromised patient (Shiomi et al. 2024). Human infections are a rising concern since there is an increase in antifungal drug resistance in this yeast group (Kumar et al. 2014, Cosio et al. 2024, Shiomi et al. 2024).

On the other hand, Blastobotrys is a potentially valuable source for industrial processes that are conducted in different stress conditions, as it has been found that many species are osmo-, halo- and/or thermotolerant. They can assimilate a variety of substrates and have oleaginous traits (Kurtzman et al. 2011, Sanya et al. 2021). For instance, B. adeninivorans and B. raffinosifermentans can grow on a high glucose concentration (50, 60 or 70 %) (Kurtzman et al. 2015, Sanya et al. 2021). At the same time, Blastobotrys strains have been used for basic biochemical studies, to produce single-cell proteins, and represent a promising source for fatty acid-based fuels and oleo chemicals with application in food, feed and beyond (Sanya et al. 2021). Blastobotrys adeninivorans is an example of a thermotolerant species. It has an optimal growth at 30–40 °C with tolerance up to 48 °C and can survive a few hours at 55 °C (Kunze & Kunze 1996, Malak et al. 2016, Bischoff et al. 2017).

Taxonomically, Blastobotrys includes mostly asexual yeasts once included in other genera such as Candida, Sympodiomyces, or Trichosporon (Kurtzman & Robnett 2007, Visagie et al. 2023). Some species also produce a sexual morph and were included in Stephanoascus or Trichomonascus (Visagie et al. 2023). The type species of Trichomonascus, T. mycophagus, has never been cultivated and still lacks DNA data to confirm its phylogenetic affinity. It will hardly be feasible to identify this species again based on morphological characters and its taxonomical placement will remain unknown. Furthermore, Visagie et al. (2023) proposed that, although Trichomonascus (Jackson 1947) is an older name and therefore has nomenclatural priority (McNeill et al. 2012), Blastobotrys (von Klopotek 1967) should be adopted as many more species have been described in the latter. Currently, 36 species of Blastobotrys are described and listed in the MycoBank database, a depository for fungal names (Crous et al. 2004).

Lachance (2012) proposed that, to distinguish between novel species that were found to reproduce sexually and asexually, the expression forma asexualis (f.a.) should be used for the latter.

During ongoing yeast isolation events in recent years, several Blastobotrys isolates were subjected to phylogenetic (ITS and LSU) and physiological analysis. Only a few isolates were identified as currently known species, including B. arbuscula and B. aristatus, whereas the majority of isolates were shown to represent undescribed species. They are described below as B. bolitophagi sp. nov., B. dehoogii f.a. sp. nov., B. hennebertii f.a. sp. nov., B. jacksonii sp. nov., B. laricis f.a. sp. nov., B. plurivorus f.a. sp. nov., and B. pulvis sp. nov. Additionally, after including several Trichomonascus strains previously deposited in the CBS culture collection they were shown as relatives to the type species B. niveus, and transferred to Blastobotrys. The geographic distribution and substrate preferences of the new species are also assessed.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Isolates

Sixteen fresh Blastobotrys strains were isolated from basidiomes, deadwood, paper, and insects. During the attempts to isolate pure cultures of Basidiomycetes from their basidiomes or decaying wood, 13 strains from Belgium and France were isolated as culture contaminants on 2 % malt agar supplemented with 50 ppm chloramphenicol (MA2.50) and later purified on the same media. The strain MUCL 56024 = CBS 17993 was isolated using a “swab” methodology from mouldy archival papers stored in a cellar. In this particular case, the “swab” method involved collecting with a swab the moulds (and yeasts) on mouldy archival documents stored in an archive room, and then, in the laboratory, swabbing the same swab onto MA2.50 culture medium and then transferring each individual colony to a second Petri dish, containing 2 % malt agar (MA2), for purification. The strains CBS 12434 and CBS 12455 were isolated from a darkling beetle (Bolitophagus interruptus) collected in Podgorie area, Bulgaria. After identification of the insects at the National Museum of Natural History, Bulgaria, it was stored at −80 °C until dissection, washed sequentially with 70 % ethanol and 0.7 % sterile saline solution, and their guts dissected under a dissecting microscope on sterile Petri dishes. The final saline wash and crushed gut suspensions (in 0.7 % sterile saline) were plated onto acidified yeast extract–malt extract agar (YMA; pH 3.5, adjusted with HCl). Plates were incubated at room temperature for 3 d, after which yeast-like colonies were selected and subcultured until pure cultures were obtained (Dimitrov et al. 2024). Additional strains were obtained from the collection of the Westerdijk Fungal Biodiversity Institute, Utrecht, The Netherlands (CBS) and from the Mycothèque of the Université catholique de Louvain (BCCM/MUCL) (Table 1). All novel strains were deposited in the CBS, CIRM (INRA Marseille), and BCCM/MUCL. Taxonomic novelties were deposited in MycoBank (Crous et al. 2004).

Morphological and physiological examination

Colony characteristics and cell morphology were examined on glucose-peptone-yeast extract agar (GPYA) for 1–2 wk of incubation at 24 °C.

Physiological characteristics were tested using an API 32C system (bioMérieux) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The tests were performed for 14 d with an interval of 3–4 d. Fermentation tests and growth on nitrogen sources, tested by the auxanography method, were done for 14 d described by Kurtzman et al. (2015). All tests were done in duplicate and incubated at 24 °C. Growth at 6 °C to 33 °C at 3 °C intervals, and at 37 °C and 40 °C was tested on GPYA for 2 wk.

Sexual reproduction of all newly isolated strains was tested on V8-juice (V8) agar, yeast extract-malt extract (YM) agar, 5 % Difco Malt and yeast carbon base supplemented with 0.01 % ammonium sulphate (YCBAS) agar (Kurtzman et al. 2015) and regularly examined up to 3 mo.

DNA extraction and sequencing

The protocols for DNA extraction, PCR, and sequencing followed Stielow et al. (2015). The primers used for PCR amplification were V9G (de Hoog & Gerrits van den Ende 1998) and LR5 (Vilgalys & Hester 1990) to amplify the ITS regions (ITS 1, ITS 2 and the intervening 5.8S rRNA gene), and the D1/D2 domain of the large subunit (LSU) rRNA gene. The amplicons were sequenced in both directions using the primers LR0R (Vilgalys & Hester 1990) and LR5 for the D1/D2 domain, whereas the primers V9G and ITS4 (White et al. 1990) were used for the ITS regions. The BigDye Terminator v. 3.1 Cycle Sequencing kit (Applied Biosystems) was used according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, and the products were analysed on an ABI Prism 3730xl DNA Sequencer (PerkinElmer).

Phylogenetic analysis

The ITS and LSU sequences generated during the study as well as Blastobotrys and Trichomonascus sequences retrieved from GenBank and that of the outgroups and used in the phylogenetic analysis are listed in Table 1. Sequences generated in this study were deposited in GenBank (Table 1), and the alignments in Zenodo (doi: 10.5281/zenodo.18338587).

Sequence alignments were performed in MAFFT online v. 7 (Katoh et al. 2017) with the default settings and visualized and edited in MEGA v. 7 (Kumar et al. 2016) and Geneious Prime v. 2023.2.1 (Biomatters Inc., New Zealand). SequenceMatrix v. 1.7.8 was used to concatenate the ITS and LSU datasets after individual loci alignments. Phylogenetic analyses of individual and combined loci were performed with Maximum Likelihood estimation (ML) and conducted with IQ-TREE v. 2.1.2 (Nguyen et al. 2015, Minh et al. 2020), incorporating ultrafast bootstrapping (UFBoot2; Hoang et al. 2018). The phylogenetic trees were visualized in FigTree v. 1.4.2 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree) and edited with Adobe Illustrator CC 2024 (USA).

Species descriptions

The novel species descriptions were done in concordance with the guidelines published by Aime et al. (2021).

Distribution and range of substrate

The geographic distribution and substrate preferences of the new taxa and of Blastobotrys species worldwide were analysed using data retrieved from the GlobalFungi Database (Větrovský et al. 2020) v. 5 (released on 16 November 2023; https://globalfungi.com/). GlobalFungi Database stored mostly data obtained through high-throughput environmental fungal DNA sequencing. This version of the database comprises 84 972 samples from 846 studies, totalling 593 399 355 ITS sequence variants worldwide. It uses the taxonomy based on UNITE v. 10.0 (4 April 2024) including only SH numbers for undescribed species. Each of the ITS sequences of our new taxa was subjected to ITSx extractor using the sequence editor SEED2 v. 2.1.34 (Větrovský et al. 2018), and ITS1 and ITS2 haplotypes were analysed separately following Réblová et al. (2022). This information is provided in the notes associated with each new species described. Furthermore, an additional query was executed on the taxon search functionality within the same database, employing the generic name Blastobotrys, with the objective of assessing the global distribution and substrate preferences of other described species.

The general discussion is also enhanced by the incorporation of information pertaining to supplementary publicly accessible Blastobotrys strains, which was obtained from the CBS strain database (https://wi.knaw.nl).

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Molecular species characterization

The combined dataset (ITS+LSU) consisted of 1705 characters (ITS: 734 and LSU: 971). Of these, 592 characters were informative (ITS: 344, LSU: 248), and 984 were invariable (ITS: 317, LSU: 667). The best-fit models of evolution selected according to the BIC criterion were TVM+F+I+G4 for ITS and GTR+F+I+G4 for LSU.

The resulting phylogenetic tree (Fig. 1) showed that our new strains were dispersed in seven different clades, which were interpreted as seven undescribed species. They are formally described in the taxonomic section as B. bolitophagi (CBS 12434, CBS 12455), closely related to B. peoriensis and B. americana; B. dehoogii (CBS 181.75, CBS 17996, CBS 17997), related to B. xishuangbannaensis; B. jacksonii (CBS 17991, CBS 18002), related to B. baotianmanensis; B. hennebertii (CBS 17993, CBS 17994) related to B. aristatus, B. bombycis and B. elegans; B. pulvis (CBS 232.85, CBS 17999, CBS 18000), related to B. navarrensis and B. proliferans; and B. plurivorus (CBS 137.78, CBS 17992, CBS 18001), related to B. farinosus and B. fungorum. Blastobotrys laricis (CBS 17998) forms a single branch that does not cluster with any of the known Blastobotrys species (Fig. 1).

Three of our new strains were found to also belong to known Blastobotrys species: CBS 18003 = MUCL 58098 and CBS 18004 = MUCL 58241 are identified as B. aristatus, whereas CBS 17995 = MUCL 38854 is identified as B. arbuscula (Fig. 1).

From the analyses of the combined dataset (ITS+LSU) (Fig. 1), as well as the phylogenomic approach used by Opulente et al. (2024) it is evident that the Trichomonascus ex-type strains of T. apis, T. ciferrii, and T. petasosporus cluster within the clade comprising currently described Blastobotrys species and are affiliated to the Blastobotrys type species, B. niveus. Considering the phylogenetic (Fig. 1) and phylogenomic (Opulente et al. 2024) results and the lack of a specimen representing the type species of Trichomonascus, T. mycophagus, and DNA thereof, we propose to transfer T. apis, T. ciferrii, and T. petasosporus to Blastobotrys.

Basionym: Trichomonascus apis G. Péter et al., Int. J. Syst. Evol. Microbiol. 59(6): 1551. 2009. MB 514356.

Blastobotrys bolitophagi M. Groenew., M.T. Sm, Hern.-Restr. & Gouliam., sp. nov. MB 857494. Fig. 2.

Etymology: bolitophagi (Lat., noun); named after the insect from which the ex-type strain was isolated from, Bolitophagus interruptus.

Typus: Bulgaria, Podgorie area, isolated from an imago of Bolitophagus interruptus (Tenebrionidae, Insecta), Oct. 2009, D. Gouliamova (holotype designated here preserved in a metabolically inactive state, CBS 12455; ex-type strain CBS 12455 mating type h+).

Description: After 2 wk at 24 °C on GPYA, the culture is off-white or ivory coloured, soft and slightly wrinkled. Budding cells globose, 2–3 µm diam. or ovoid and elongate, 2–4 × 2.5–6 µm. Hyphae hyaline, septate, 1.5–2.5 µm wide. Pseudo hyphae hyaline. Secondary conidia not observed.

Asci with ascospores were observed after 2 wk at 24 °C by mixing strain CBS 12455 with CBS 12434 on YMA and MEA. Asci are persistent and crowned with a cylindrical cell. Asci contain one hat-shaped ascospore.

Physiological characteristics: Fermentation of D-glucose is positive and is absent for D-galactose, sucrose, maltose, lactose, raffinose and trehalose. Carbon compounds assimilated are D-glucose, sucrose, melibiose, D-galactose, lactose, trehalose, maltose, cellobiose, methyl-α-D-glucoside, L-sorbose, D-xylose, L-arabinose, D-ribose, erythritol, D-mannitol, D-sorbitol, inositol, D-gluconate, glucosamine, N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, potassium-2-keto-D-gluconate and D-glucuronate, while growth is absent on raffinose, melezitose, L-rhamnose, glycerol and lactic acid.

Growth occurs on L-lysine, cadaverine, nitrate and D-glucosamine HCl as sole nitrogen compounds and is negative on ethylamine. Growth occurred at 6 °C up to 30 °C and is absent at 37 °C. Growth in the presence of 0.01 % cycloheximide is positive.

Additional material examined: Bulgaria, Podgorie area, isolated from an imago of Bolitophagus interruptus (Tenebrionidae, Insecta), Oct. 2009, D. Gouliamova, strain CBS 12434, mating type h−.

Distribution and substrates: This species is only known from the type locality Bulgaria, growing on imago of Bolitophagus interruptus. No identical ITS sequences were recovered in the GlobalFungi database.

Notes: The two B. bolitophagi strains CBS 12455 and CBS 12434 have 1 nt difference in their ITS and are identical in their LSU. The ex-type strain (CBS 12455) differs from the ex-type strain (CBS 10340) of its closest relative, B. peoriensis, with 36 nt (12 gaps) and 5 nt (no gaps) in the ITS (500 nt) and LSU (847 nt) regions, respectively. Blastobotrys peoriensis can assimilate raffinose, glycerol, and melizitose whereas B. bolitophagi not. However, B. bolitophagi can assimilate trehalose and B. peoriensis not (Table 3).

PHYSIOLOGICAL CHARACTERISTICS

Strains of all seven novel species were positive for the assimilation of D-glucose, galactose, trehalose, maltose, cellobiose, D-xylose, L-arabinose, erythritol, D-mannitol, D-sorbitol, glucosamine, D-glucoronate, N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, Lysine, and cadaverine and can growth from 6 °C up to 30 °C and at a concentration of cycloheximide of 0.01 %.

Strains of all seven novel species fermented D-glucose but were negative for lactose, maltose, raffinose, sucrose, trehalose, and xylose.

All strains were negative for the assimilation of melezitose and for growth at 37 °C. Compounds that had dissimilar assimilation profiles among the species are listed in Table 2.

MATING CAPABILITIES

Strains with complementary mating types for a specific species are indicated with or h+ or h−. This designation was used to specify complementary mating types in heterothallic species in the genera Metschnikowia, Clavispora, or Kodamea (Kurtzman et al. 2011).

Sexual reproduction was only observed in B. pulvis and B. bolitophagi. Ascospores were observed after 1 wk at 24 °C on YCBAS sporulation medium in B. pulvis, by mixing strain CBS 232.85 (mating type h+) either with CBS 17999 or CBS 18000, both belonging to the complementary mating type h−. In B. bolitophagi, ascospores were observed after 2 wk at 24 °C on YMA and MEA by mixing strain CBS 12455 (mating type h+) with CBS 12434 (mating type h−). These two species are heterothallic and need strains of complementary mating types to go into the sexual live cycle.

DISTRIBUTION AND SUBSTRATE RANGE

The known distribution range and substrates of the newly described taxa revealed distinct patterns. Certain species, including B. bolitophagi, B. jacksonii, B. laricis, and B. pulvis, appear to be restricted to their type localities and substrates. They have no matches in datasets of environmental sequences. This suggests that they are either rare or have been sampled insufficiently.

Blastobotrys dehoogii is to date restricted to Europe. In addition to our records from Germany and France, this species is represented by two environmental records, made on deadwood in the Czech Republic. Blastobotrys hennebertii, inversely, shows a disjunct distribution. In addition to our isolates from Belgium, it is known from environmental records made in Brazil and China. This species is reported from diverse substrates including soil, sediments, water, paper, and timber. By contrast, B. plurivorus is widely distributed across Europe (Austria, Belgium, Czech Republic, Estonia, France, Germany, Hungary, Netherlands, Sweden, and UK) and it is also known from North America (Canada), West Asia (Iran), and St. Helena (Peak Dale). Its range of substrate includes soil, deadwood, shoot, air, dung, and other fungi.

According to the GlobalFungi database, members of Blastobotrys are present in 1356 “environmental” samples. Asia accounts for 51.84 % and Europe 26.36 %. This may reflect the intensity of samplings (Fig. 9A). Regarding to the substrate type, most of the samples come from soil (70.5 %, including topsoil) but, interestingly, several records originate from deadwood (10.32 %), air (4.72 %), and water (3.61 %) (Fig. 9B). The most common biome is forest (59.44 %) followed by cropland (11.5 %), and woodland (7.15 %).

On the other hand, amongst 182 strains deposited in the CBS collection, 47.25 % were isolated from North and South America with the most from Panama (42/86) and the USA (36/86). Europe accounted for 36.26 % with the Netherlands (22/66) and France (11/66) as the countries where the genus was the most abundantly found (perhaps the most abundantly sampled) (Fig. 9C). Asia and Africa are represented only by 11 and 9 strains, or 6 and 5 %, respectively. Three strains (1.65 %) were isolated from the Arctic, and five strains (2.7 %) are from unknown origin (Fig. 9C).

Blastobotrys strains have a wide range of substrates, as indicated in Fig. 9D. More than 50 % originated from natural environments, with approximately 40 % from insects, 11 % from soil, 7.5 % from deadwood, and 7 % were associated with other (mostly) basidiomycetous fungi. Approximately 4 % were associated with foodstuffs.

This contrasts with the data from environmental samples, for which soil is the most frequent substrate. This also likely reflects both the intensity and the selection of sampled substrates between environmental sampling and culture dependant sampling. As an illustration of this, approximately 40 % of the Blastobotrys isolates from the CBS collection, from which data were used, were isolated during a five-year survey of yeast diversity associated with insects in the USA and Panama. Nonetheless, it is still clear that natural environments are the most important niche for this genus that include plants, soil, air, water, insects, and other fungi. Anthropogenic habitats and substrates such as food, walls/paintings, paper, or decaying timber, is also a non-negligible reservoir of rare or undescribed species.

Blastobotrys species are not commonly known as human disease-causing agents. Nonetheless, 3 % of the strains available worldwide were associated with humans, and belong to B. ciferrii (Gunsilius et al. 2001, Cheng et al. 2004), B. chiropterorum (Furman & Ahearn 1983), B. proliferans (Quirin et al. 2007), B. mokoenaii, B. raffinosifermentans, and B. serpentis (Kumar et al. 2014, Al-Obaidi et al. 2021, Cosio et al. 2024, Shiomi et al. 2024). Our strains of the novel species could not grow at 37 °C and are most likely not able to induce or be associated with human disease.
