Clinical development and guiding theory of transcranial magnetic stimulation: a literature review
Authors: Zhanxiang Lin, Jinling Cheng, Cheng Tan, Zicai Liu, Dongmiao Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTranscranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is widely used as a painless, non-invasive, and well-tolerated neuromodulation technique for various psychiatric and neurological disorders. Evidence from reviews, clinical trials, and basic research indicates a promising future for this approach. However, individual differences and the complexity of these disorders often lead to TMS failing to achieve the desired therapeutic effect. The key to overcoming this limitation lies in optimizing TMS parameters, including stimulation mode, target location, pulse number, frequency, coil, and the connectivity of the target brain network. Nevertheless, most studies have focused on one or a few parameters and have yet to provide comprehensive guidance for clinical practice.ObjectiveThis review aims to trace the developmental trajectory of TMS, outlining the primary stimulation types and parameters, coils, theoretical models, target sites, targeting techniques (particularly personalized targeting techniques), among others, with a view to providing assistance to clinical practice and future research.MethodsPubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar databases were searched for articles on the clinical evidence and methodology of TMS from inception to August 2025. The search terms included ‘Transcranial magnetic stimulation’, ‘TMS’, ‘Stimulus type’, ‘parameters’, ‘theoretical model’, ‘coils’, ‘target’ or ‘personalied positioning’, etc.ResultsWe have identified the primary TMS modalities, stimulation parameters, coil types and orientations, theoretical models, target sites, and targeting techniques that have been developed. These findings may provide valuable insights for broader TMS applications, and future research on personalized TMS targeting techniques and their accuracy/reproducibility warrants further investigation.ConclusionA comprehensive understanding of TMS is essential for overcoming challenges in its clinical application. Optimizing the selection of coils, stimulation protocols, theoretical frameworks, and target sites is critical to enhancing treatment efficacy. Given the persistent inter- and intra-individual variability observed in current studies, future research should focus on precise targeting, elucidating therapeutic mechanisms, and improving reproducibility.Keywords: Transcranial magnetic stimulation, stimulation parameters, personalized, review

Introduction

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is a relatively new, non-invasive brain stimulation technique that creates induced currents in the brain by generating brief, rapidly changing magnetic fields whose pulses in the coils excite neurons at desired locations in the cortex [1,2]. Many disorders are characterized by disruption of neural circuits and neurogenesis, such as deficits in hippocampal activity and volume in patients with depression [3]. Neural circuits are networks of different types and functions of neurons in the brain formed through complex connections. In these circuits, excitatory and inhibitory activities interact with each other, and the final effect is determined by the net result of multiple neuronal activities canceling each other out. TMS is able to preferentially depolarize different neural circuits depending on the direction of the current induced in the brain [4]. In simple terms, the magnetic field generated by the TMS coil causes changes in the direction of induced current flow in the brain, and different current directions interact with different neural circuits [4]. The interaction of TMS with neural circuits triggers neurophysiological and behavioral effects, including activation of activity in the corresponding areas of the brain [5], evoked muscle contractions (called motor evoked potential, MEP) [6], and direct co-stimulation of the peripheral nervous system [7], among others, to achieve therapeutic effects.

Currently, TMS technology is widely used in the clinical treatment of psychiatric disorders such as depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), anxiety, and schizophrenia [8], and neurological disorders such as movement disorders, Alzheimer’s disease (AD)/mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and epilepsy [9]. Nonetheless, TMS has certain limitations. Its limitations are, on the one hand, related to the psychiatric or neurological disorders to which it is mainly applied, the etiology of which is still largely unknown [10], making it very difficult to develop a precise treatment strategy. On the other hand, the limitations are related to TMS itself, mainly due to the difficulty of determining the optimal stimulation target and coil position during operation [11]. There is still room for improvement in the depth of stimulation in the clinical application of existing TMS technology, which has not yet reached its theoretical maximum [12]. In addition, the mechanism of TMS is not fully understood, which is closely related to the complex structure and function of the brain [13]. Therefore, for general practitioners in particular, reliance on generic TMS solutions alone is not sufficient, and a thorough and comprehensive understanding of the principles and applications of TMS is essential.

Given the excellent potential of TMS for clinical applications, there is an urgent need for in-depth optimization of treatment protocols to overcome existing limitations. Studies have shown [14] that key parameters such as stimulation site, intensity, and direction are critical for optimal electric field distribution and cortical activation. Thus, there are many opportunities to improve the efficacy of TMS, but only if the underlying principles of TMS and its associated neuropathology are well understood. In addition, inter-individual differences in effects are common, and this heterogeneity may be due to the over-universal nature of TMS protocols, allowing them to be widely applied to different patient populations [15]. Personalized targeting techniques are expected to overcome this challenge and reduce variability in clinical outcomes [16]. However, despite the gradual increase in international clinical trials, reviews, and basic research on TMS, a comprehensive review of TMS therapeutic parameters and methodology is still lacking. In this study, we will examine the historical development of TMS and delve into the aspects of its commonly used stimulation types, coil types and orientations, guiding theories, stimulation targets, and targeting strategies in clinical practice, with the aim of providing references for future research and clinical applications.

Methods

This article reviews the existing literature on the development history of TMS, common stimulation modes, coil types and their orientations, theoretical models, stimulation targets, and related targeting strategies. At the same time, it focuses on reviewing some personalized target positioning methods in current TMS technology. The data sources include relevant literature published on PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, and information collection was conducted using EndNote. The search time range was from the establishment of the database to August 2025. The search terms included ‘Transcranial magnetic stimulation’, ‘TMS’, ‘stimulus type’, ‘parameters’, ‘theoretical model’, ‘coils’, ‘target’ or ‘personalized positioning’, etc. The language of the retrieved studies was not limited. The inclusion criteria were: (1) clinical trials with TMS as the main intervention measure; (2) review articles, such as clinical practice guidelines, expert consensus, application progress, etc., on TMS. The exclusion criteria were: (1) duplicate literature; (2) inability to obtain the full text; (3) titles and abstracts obviously irrelevant to the research topic, such as not involving the development of TMS, equipment introduction, theoretical models, targets, or positioning methods, etc.; (4) grey literature, such as papers that have not undergone peer review. Two authors independently evaluated the titles and abstracts of the existing literature to screen the literature based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Subsequently, a detailed full-text review was conducted on the eligible literature to ensure the extraction of the required literature information for the description of each chapter.

Results and discussion

TMS development history

In 1985, Baker et al. [17] successfully achieved magnetic stimulation of the motor cortex on the human scalp for the first time using a TMS coil based on Faraday’s principle of electromagnetic induction, replacing the transcranial electrical stimulation (TES) method previously used by Merton et al. [18]. This breakthrough laid the foundation for the subsequent development and application of TMS technology and opened a new chapter in TMS in neuroscience research and clinical practice. In 1990, Tofts et al. [19] proposed a model for the distribution of TMS-induced induced currents in the central nervous system. The proposed model provides insight into its potential for neuromodulation and neuroscience research and provides an important theoretical basis for future understanding of the mechanisms of TMS action within the brain. In 1997, Paus et al. [20] used positron emission tomography (PET) to observe metabolic activity in brain regions during TMS stimulation of the cortex. This study laid the foundation for an approach that combines TMS with brain imaging techniques to promote a deeper understanding of brain function and neuroplasticity. The 2010s were a decade of rapid progress in the TMS field. Since 2000, a series of major technological advances have been made in TMS, including the optimization of TMS parameters, breakthroughs in navigation and localization techniques [21–24], the emergence of multi-channel TMS [2,25], closer integration of TMS and imaging [10,26], and the introduction of the concept of personalized treatment [27]. In particular, in 2008, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) first approved TMS as a treatment for drug-resistant depression [28]. This approval gave a significant boost to the clinical use of TMS. To date, a large number of meta-analyses have shown [29–35] the effectiveness of TMS in psychiatric or neurological disorders. Particularly in the treatment of major depressive disorder (MDD), Canadian clinical treatment guidelines have recommended repetitive TMS (rTMS) as the treatment of choice for MDD [36]. Of course, TMS technology is widely used not only for clinical disease treatment but also as a neuroscience tool in areas such as diagnostic assessment [37,38] and monitoring of diseases [39–41]. Main types of TMS

TMS technology has progressed significantly in just 40 years and includes many different types of developments. These types are categorized based on differences in stimulus parameters, stimulus patterns, and stimulus locations. Classification is mainly based on pulse patterns and includes single-pulse stimulation (spTMS), paired-pulse stimulation (ppTMS), rTMS, Theta Burst Stimulation (TBS), Quadripulse Stimulation (QPS), and paired-associative stimulation (PAS). Of course, other types exist, such as triple stimulation techniques and combined TMS-ECG techniques, all designed to further improve the effectiveness and application of TMS and to deepen the understanding of the interactions between the brain and other physiological systems. spTMS

spTMS is the most basic type of TMS, and spTMS techniques will always be needed to evaluate target effects even when performing other types of TMS [47]. A single time-varying magnetic field applied at no fixed frequency is typically used to activate different components of the neural circuitry of the motor cortex in a stimulus intensity-dependent manner [48]. This was also the first stimulus used by Baker et al. [17] in 1985. A single stimulus can depolarize neurons and evoke measurable effects [49], so the technique is mostly used for routine electrophysiological examinations. For example, it is commonly used to study motor thresholds and phosphene thresholds [50], but also in areas such as examining corticospinal excitability [51]. Changes in key neuroelectrophysiological indices such as MEP and RMT obtained by spTMS have important reference values, especially in predicting recovery of motor function after stroke [52]. In addition, the technique has achieved remarkable results in the clinical treatment of migraine with aura and other conditions [42].

ppTMS

The ppTMS technique first appeared in 1993 in a study by Kujirai et al. [53] on inter-cortical inhibition in the human motor cortex. Typically, this technique utilizes the simultaneous application of two different intensities of stimulation to specific parts of the brain at specific times and intensities, or the use of two stimulators in different parts of the brain. The ppTMS technique is commonly used to test various neural circuits involved in intracortical inhibition or facilitation [54]. For example, the excitability of intracortical circuits was assessed in vivo using TMS in the primary motor cortex (M1) [55]. Furthermore, Mimura et al. [56] showed that the neurophysiological functions of GABAergic and cholinergic neural circuits could be assessed by four ppTMS paradigms—short- and long-interval intracortical inhibition (SICI/LICI), intracortical facilitation (ICF), and short-latency afferent inhibition (SAI)—which assess the neurophysiological function of GABAergic, glutamatergic, and cholinergic neural circuits, respectively. The collection of these physiological indices can help to distinguish AD, MCI and the normal population more accurately.

rTMS

The rTMS technique was first used in a 1995 study by George et al. [57] on improving mood in depressed patients. It is a technique that uses changing magnetic fields to repetitively stimulate specific areas of the brain, a non-invasive stimulation method that affects brain metabolism and neural activity by altering the electrical activity of nerve cells through induced electrical currents [58]. This technique has a sustained biological effect even after the stimulus is stopped [59]. It is also regarded as an effective clinical treatment tool, and the clinical efficacy of rTMS for many neurological and psychiatric disorders has been recognized in evidence-based guidelines endorsed by the International Federation of Clinical Neurophysiology (IFCN) [60]. rTMS can be further divided into high-frequency rTMS (HF-rTMS, ≥ 5 Hz, which usually increases neural excitability) and low-frequency rTMS (LF-rTMS, ≤ 1 Hz, which usually decreases neural excitability) [61]. The key mechanism of rTMS is the induction of neuroplasticity from the level of synapses to neural circuits, an effect that exhibits complex and subtle variations across brain regions and time scales, thus affecting the clinical outcome of psychiatric treatment [62].

TBS

The TBS technique was first developed as a more effective form of rTMS in 2005 in Huang et al.’s [63] study of the human motor cortex. The technique consists of three short pulses with internal high-frequency (50 Hz, in the gamma range) low-intensity pulses [54]. There are two main programs: continuous TBS (cTBS) and intermittent TBS (iTBS). iTBS is often thought to act as an excitatory agent in neural circuits, whereas cTBS is often thought to act as an inhibitory agent [64]. The TBS technique has been used as an effective tool for ameliorating psychiatric or neurological disorders and for inducing synaptic plasticity [65]. In 2024, a systematic review by Jemna et al. [66] also affirmed its applicability for treatment in the field of neurology. And TBS can produce effects more rapidly than conventional rTMS, which is expected to improve patient adherence to treatment and reduce the severity of adverse effects.

It is particularly noteworthy that although TBS stands out among numerous rTMS protocols for its ability to induce persistent physiological effects following brief, low-intensity stimulation, its efficacy remains highly controversial. This uncertainty is especially pronounced regarding the reliability and reproducibility of cTBS (inhibitory) and iTBS (excitatory) protocols. For instance, Gamboa et al. [67] observed that iTBS, which typically exhibiting excitatory effects, shifted to inhibitory effects when stimulation duration doubled, while inhibitory cTBS transformed into a facilitative effect under the same conditions. This phenomenon suggests that the neural effects of TBS may be influenced by multiple factors. A meta-analysis by Chung et al. [68] summarized that pulse number, stimulation frequency, and brain-derived neurotrophic factor polymorphism are all important factors influencing TBS effects. Furthermore, Ozdemir et al. [69] employed TMS-EEG technology for the first systematic assessment of the direct responses to iTBS and cTBS in the human motor cortex. Their results revealed that TBS-induced neuromodulatory effects exhibit not only significant inter-individual variability but also substantial intra-subject variability. Recent reviews have also indicated that the effects of rTMS protocols (such as iTBS and cTBS) on neuroelectrophysiological indicators and behavior may arise from mechanisms other than changes in the excitability of the stimulated brain regions or networks [70]. Moreover, no rTMS protocol has been definitively classified as either excitatory or inhibitory [70]. Therefore, conclusions from studies relying on traditional assumptions (i.e. iTBS is excitatory, cTBS is inhibitory) without independent neurophysiological validation of TBS effects on direct cortical or brain network responses should be interpreted with caution.

QPS

QPS is a new direction in rTMS that induces plastic changes in the brain. Unlike traditional spTMS, it delivers four pulses to the cerebral cortex in a specific frequency and timing pattern in an interleaved manner [71]. In conventional rTMS, monophasic pulses are often considered to be more stimulating than biphasic pulses, and the monophasic pulses used in QPS may help to enhance the efficacy of QPS [71]. Thus, in theory, QPS may trigger stronger neuronal responses relative to conventional spTMS. Hamada et al. [72] were the first to use the QPS technique to induce plasticity in the human motor cortex in 2007 and demonstrated that QPS was more effective than PPS in inducing plasticity in motor cortex. A study by Viganò et al. [73] in 2024 also successfully validated the ability of the Quadripulse rTMS technique to alter visual cortical responses. Relevant studies [74,75] have provided evidence in support of the safety of the QPS technology, and these further affirm its therapeutic potential. In addition, a review by Matsumoto et al. [45] indicated the feasibility of QPS in the treatment of neurological and psychiatric disorders such as Parkinson’s disease, epilepsy, cerebrovascular disease, and major depressive disorder.

PAS

PAS is a stimulation technique that combines TMS and peripheral nerve electrical stimulation (PNES) to induce plastic changes in the human motor system. In 2000, Stefan et al. [76] were the first to successfully achieve long-duration enhancement of the motor cortex of the brain by combining PNES with TMS. PAS is now used as an effective tool for inducing neuroplasticity and promoting recovery after nerve injury [77]. In addition, the therapeutic effect of the PAS program varies, depending mainly on the number of pulses, the frequency of TMS and PNES, and the stimulation intervals between the two [78]. For example, Sathyan et al. [78] compared the effects of three different PAS protocols on the rehabilitation of patients with spinal cord injury and showed that a stimulation paradigm combining 20 Hz rTMS (three pulses at 96% stimulator output) with high-frequency PNS (six pulses, 100 Hz) was more effective in enhancing the MEP at 60 min post-stimulation. Furthermore, Wiratman et al. [79] demonstrated that the PA-QPS protocol combining QPS and PNES induced greater plasticity and fewer sequelae in LTD-like effects than QPS alone.

The stimulation coil is a key part of TMS, and its efficacy is achieved precisely by the precise targeting of the coil to the corresponding functional areas of the brain. The most common types of TMS coils on the market today are circular, figure-of-eight, double-cone and Hesed. Circular coil

Circular coil was the first to appear, producing a ‘crater-like’ distribution of magnetic fields. It has a wide stimulus area, high output stimulus intensity, and easily triggers motor evoked potentials, but is poorly focused [80]. Because the head is curved rather than flat, the distance between the outer edge of the circular coil and the cortex around the stimulation point changes due to the curvature, thus affecting the stimulus intensity. Therefore, round coils are suitable for peripheral nerve stimulation, nerve detection, and larger cortical ranges. In addition, it has been shown that as the size of the circular coil increases, the depth of stimulation increases, but the focusing of the magnetic field decreases [81]. Therefore, when using a round coil, the appropriate coil size should be selected based on the required stimulation depth and focusing needs, and greater care must be taken in positioning the cortical target.

Figure-of-eight coil

The figure-of-eight coil can be viewed as consisting of two circular coils placed tangentially in the same plane. Although the direction of the current in the two coils is opposite, the direction of the current in the centre of the junction between the two is the same. Here, the turns of the two coils are added together, and the strength and direction of the magnetic field are superimposed, so the magnetic induction at the connection is large. With the figure-of-eight coil, the induced electric field creates two vortices in the brain that eventually merge in the centre, allowing local and focal brain stimulation [82]. However, the available evidence shows that the figure-of-eight coil can only stimulate the superficial regions of the cerebral cortex, and the depth of stimulation is about 2–3 cm below the skull [83]. Although increasing stimulation intensity reaches deeper brain structures, it may significantly increase the risk of adverse events [84]. In addition, unlike circular coils, figure-of-eight coils have a smaller stimulus area, shallower stimulus depth, and stronger focus, which better balances the focus and depth of the induction field [82]. Ørskov et al. [85] also compared the variability of the two coils, and their findings indicated that although there was no significant difference in the mean short-interval intracortical inhibition parameter between the two, subjects with the figure-of-eight coil had less variation in mean inhibition from 1–3.5 ms and they also experienced less pain and discomfort. Currently, the figure-of-eight coil is widely used in clinical and scientific work and is universal for all types of indications.

Double-cone coil

The double-cone coil originates from the figure-of-eight coil, which consists of two concave coils at an angle (not in the same plane) with opposite current directions. This special angular design fits the shape of the head, and the large coil size allows the electric field generated by the double-cone coil to remain strong enough to stimulate deeper in the brain, typically up to 30 mm below the skin [86,87]. Although similar in shape and structure to the figure-of-eight coil, the double-cone coil is considered to be more suitable for cerebellar stimulation and is capable of generating a greater depth of stimulation [88]. However, because of its special design, it may not be very suitable for stimulation of the temporal lobe, spinal nerve roots, and other areas. The expert guide states [89] that the double-cone coil can be used to stimulate areas of the brain that are difficult to reach with the figure-of-eight coil, such as the leg motor area or medial prefrontal cortex, and may be effective in reaching the cingulate gyrus, insula, and cerebellum. In addition, the double-cone coil has been shown to be one of the best choices for lower limb TMS stimulation in clinical practice [90].

Hesed coil

Hesed coil was first developed by Roth et al. [91] and was designed to effectively stimulate deeper brain regions without increasing the electric field strength in superficial cortical areas. The coil is uniquely constructed so that multiple coil elements are oriented tangentially to the scalp surface, minimizing non-tangential coil elements, and the flexible base adapts to the curvature of the scalp for maximum magnetic coupling. Thanks to its special structure and large size, the hesed coil is able to provide deep brain stimulation by generating an electric field that decays more slowly, without the need to increase the intensity to extreme levels to achieve a stronger stimulation effect in the cortical areas [92]. In addition, it has been noted that [93,94] hesed coil magnetic field covers a wide area; many brain regions can be modulated and, can be activated simultaneously on both sides of the motor cortex or synchronously modulate neural networks. Guadagnin et al. [95] found through finite element modelling that the use of the hesed coil may be more effective in reducing the impact on other brain regions that do not require stimulation. This also reduces the incidence of discomfort and adverse reactions to some extent. The Hesed Coil is now widely used as a deep stimulation coil in the treatment of various psychiatric and neurological disorders [96].

Other coil types

In addition to the common ones mentioned above, there are many other types of stimulation coils for TMS. In any case, however, the design of all TMS coils is accomplished by a combination of the required power or energy, the focus and depth of penetration of the induced electric field, the coil size, and the balance between mechanical properties [97]. Considering that the EMP may raise the temperature of the stimulation coil, which in turn affects patient use, the TMS coils include a variety of types, including naturally cooled, air-cooled, internally circulated/externally liquid-cooled, and semiconductor-cooled. TMS coils can also be structurally divided into different types, such as empty core and iron core. It has been shown [98,99] that the incorporation of high-permeability cores results in a significant increase in electrical efficiency and focal length compared to empty core TMS coils, generates a stronger electric field, permits greater charge recycling, and generates less heat in the coil. However, there are obvious drawbacks, such as high weight, easy saturation of magnetic flux, and the problem of self-heating caused by eddy currents in the iron core. In addition, there are many versions of TMS coils for the same class. For example, there are nearly 30 different types of hesed coil, which are designed to reflect different subdural depths, stimulation volumes, and stimulation areas to meet the needs of different diseases [100]. Of course, there are many other novel TMS coil designs, such as head RF coils integrated with multichannel 3-axis TMS coil systems [101], novel angle-tuned toroidal coils [102], Halo-V assembly coils [103], and C-shaped miniature coils for rodent experiments [104].

It is worth noting that the direction of coil placement is also an important factor in the effectiveness of TMS treatment. However, the direction of the coil does not appear to have changed since TMS was first used to treat depression. A recent review by Cerins et al. [105] noted that the interaction of coil orientation with gyral anatomy can alter E-field strength, peak E-field location, and influence network targeting. Therefore, a comprehensive consideration of TMS coils in terms of head shape, stimulation site, coil type, and orientation must be an area worthy of in-depth research in the future.

Theoretical model of TMS application

Although TMS has been studied for three decades and significant progress has been made, we have identified a variety of complex effects and interactions. However, due to the complexity of the human brain, the mechanisms behind many of its functions are still not fully understood. This situation has prompted scientists to propose models to explain how TMS affects brain function and to guide its use in the clinic. Inter-hemispheric competition model

The competition model suggests that TMS stimulation acts by triggering competition between neurons in the cerebral cortex. The inter-hemispheric competition model is one of its most classical competition models, and a systematic review has reported [106] the dominance of this model in the application of rTMS after stroke. The inter-hemispheric competition model suggests that there are connections and checks and balances between the two hemispheres as they pass through the corpus callosum (CC) [106]. The CC is a key structure in the structural balance between the cerebral hemispheres [107] and also mediates inter-hemispheric signal propagation [108]. Voineskos et al. [109] showed that TMS stimulation is signaled through the CC and the evidence supports the role of the CC in the functional asymmetry of homologous cortical areas. Stroke, for example, although usually occurring unilaterally, causes changes in cross-hemispheric function and effective connectivity [110]. Rocha et al. [111] found that motor cortex excitability in stroke patients showed higher inter-hemispheric asymmetry compared with healthy patients. Therefore, based on this model, we can apply different frequencies of TMS to increase the excitability of the affected hemisphere or inhibit the excitability of the healthy hemisphere for the purpose of inducing inter-hemispheric signaling and re-establishing homeostasis. A randomized controlled trial (RCT) by Du et al. [112] on the effects of high-frequency and low-frequency rTMS on motor recovery in early stroke patients also validated this model theory. However, a study by McCambridge et al. [113] suggests that this theoretical model may be overly simplistic and might not apply, especially to stroke patients with more severe brain damage.

Compensatory model

The compensatory model is based on neural plasticity. One of the fundamental properties of the brain is its ability to adapt its processes to a variety of complex external or internal changes, and this inherent neuroplasticity facilitates, at least to some extent, the spontaneous repair process under the influence of disease [114]. The compensatory model assumes that when one hemisphere is damaged, the homologous region of the healthy hemisphere or the undamaged region surrounding the lesion will achieve functional compensation for the affected side in the form of axonal sprouting, regeneration, and functional reorganization of the cortex [115,116]. Thus, based on this model, we can apply excitatory TMS to stimulate undamaged potential repair areas around the lesion. Alternatively, TMS can be used to activate the homologous region on the healthy side to achieve the goal of promoting compensatory repair mechanisms such as cerebral cortical functional reorganization. In addition, Nahas et al. [117] applied navigated rTMS to stimulate the residual visual areas surrounding the visual cortex lesions in patients with brain injuries. Their results demonstrated a significant improvement in patients’ visual acuity. Similarly, Wang et al. showed that applying HF-rTMS to the undamaged cortex of severely impaired stroke patients can harness the positive compensatory effects of the contralateral hemisphere, thereby promoting motor function recovery. However, although these studies provide some support for the compensatory model, their findings appear to contradict the interhemispheric competition model from a fundamental perspective. Consequently, these two models may not fully explain the mechanisms underlying neural rehabilitation in all patients with brain injuries. The study by McCambridge et al. [113] found that upregulation of cortical excitability contralateral to the lesion may benefit patients with more severe damage. This suggests that the compensatory model may be more suitable for patients with more severe brain damage, contrary to the ‘inter-hemispheric competition model’ in terms of the target population.

Bimodal balance recovery model

The bimodal balance recovery model is a new model proposed by Di Pino et al. [115] as a response to some of the reported ineffectiveness of competing or surrogate models and the limitations of the model, which is over-simplified and not suitable for all stroke patients. The bimodal balance recovery model introduces a new concept of ‘structural reserve’ (i.e. the extent to which neural pathways and connections are preserved after injury) and relates interhemispheric homeostasis and functional recovery to the preserved structural reserve of the lesion [115]. Based on this model, we can apply the inter-hemispheric competition model when the damage is light (high structural retention) and the compensatory model when the damage is heavy (low structural retention). More recently, a systematic review by Lin et al. [106], incorporating 70 studies, validated the bimodal balance recovery model theory, suggesting that in MEP+ patients, direct promotion of the ipsilateral hemisphere may be more effective than attempting to indirectly promote ipsilateral lesion excitability by inhibiting the contralateral lateral hemisphere. In contrast, promoting the contralateral lesion hemisphere may be more applicable to MEP- patients in whom the contralateral hemisphere may play an important compensatory role. The results of an RCT by Wang et al. [118], which also explored the efficacy of optimized rTMS based on bimodal balance recovery models, showed that low-frequency rTMS was more effective than high-frequency rTMS in restoring upper limb motor function in stroke patients with high corticospinal tract (CST) integrity; conversely, in patients with low CST integrity, the high-frequency rTMS was more effective in improving upper limb motor function. In addition, Liu et al. [119] showed that activation of ipsilateral M1-CC-contralateral M1 (iM1-CC-cM1) parvalbumin (PV) neurons improved the excitatory/inhibitory balance, thereby reducing neurological deficits and motor dysfunction after ischemic stroke in rats. This provides an experimental basis for bimodal balance recovery models in clinical rehabilitation, i.e. the inter-hemispheric competition model is a better predictor of recovery than the compensatory model in the presence of high structural reserve. Meanwhile, due to the introduction of the ‘structural reserve’, which takes into account the remaining networks of the brain to a large extent, the Bimodal Balance Recovery model has a wider range of applicable populations compared to the previous two models. It can improve the therapeutic effect through individualized treatment [115].

Diaschisis model

The concept of diaschisis was first introduced by Monakow [120] in 1914. The diaschisis model hypothesizes that at least some of the clinical symptoms are caused by impaired function between interconnected brain regions [121]. This theoretical model was also validated by the studies of Grefkes [122] and Santos et al. [123]. The former has been shown, by analysis of functional neuroimaging data, to demonstrate that after focal injury, brain networks reorganize their structural and functional deconstructive structures to compensate for the lesion itself and remote effects. The latter suggests that cortical reorganization can be explained in terms of excitatory-inhibitory homeostasis; the restoration of functional networks is paralleled by an increase in excitability throughout the cerebral cortex. Thus, based on this model, we can formulate TMS treatment strategies in terms of modulating the connectivity within the stimulated network. A recent study by Labeit et al. [124] has also shown that stroke lesions result in extensive damage to the swallowing network, and that excitatory stimulation of the bilateral or cerebellum, guided by the diaschisis model, is more helpful in the rehabilitation of the swallowing network.

In addition to its guiding significance in clinical rehabilitation, the Diaschisis model provides a theoretical framework for understanding the modulatory effects of TMS on brain functional networks. A growing body of research has demonstrated that the effects of TMS extend far beyond local stimulation sites, encompassing network-level modulation of distant brain regions. For example, Eldaief et al. [125] found that TMS can modulate resting-state intrinsic brain activity in a frequency-dependent manner, supporting its ability to alter brain function at the network level. Fox et al. [126] further proposed that combining TMS with resting-state functional connectivity MRI enables more precise targeting of specific functional connectivity networks, thereby enhancing its dual value in basic research and clinical treatment. Additionally, TMS has been widely used to investigate the causal mechanisms underlying specific cognitive functions within brain networks. For instance, Hebscher et al. [127] reviewed how episodic memory network properties can be examined using non-invasive brain stimulation techniques. Cabre et al. [128] reviewed the development of TMS in basic and clinical neuroscience, proposing that TMS has become an essential tool for probing and controlling brain system-level functions. Therefore, guided by the Diaschisis model, TMS can be employed not only for treating brain network dysfunction but also as a non-invasive causal research method, providing a powerful tool for understanding the dynamic organization and regulatory mechanisms of large-scale brain functional networks.

Other guiding theories

In addition to the primary models described above, numerous other guiding theories provide valuable insights for studying TMS and its clinical applications. For example, Haggie et al. [6] presented a spiking neural network model of the motor cortex capable of simulating and interpreting TMS responses in corticospinal neurons.

It is important to highlight that the theory proposing that ‘TMS can induce an increase in the strength of spontaneous neural oscillations in the target area, producing an ‘entrainment’ effect’ is increasingly recognized as a key framework for explaining the effects of rhythmic TMS on brain function. The underlying mechanism involves rhythmic TMS, when applied externally to the brain, interacting with neural oscillations at corresponding frequencies in specific brain regions until synchronization occurs, thereby modulating the brain’s intrinsic neural oscillatory activity [129]. Different frequencies of neural oscillations correspond to distinct brain states and play crucial roles in various functions such as cognition, perception, and behavior. For example, the study by Albouy et al. [130] demonstrated that theta-rTMS enhances theta oscillatory power in the intraparietal sulcus (IPS) and promotes theta-phase synchronization between the IPS and the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC). This enhancement was significantly correlated with improved auditory working memory accuracy in participants, suggesting that TMS optimizes the neural mechanisms underlying memory processing by modulating endogenous theta oscillations. Furthermore, Hermiller et al. [131] showed that TBS significantly impacts hippocampal function, potentially due to resonance between the stimulation rhythm and endogenous theta-gamma coupling oscillations within the hippocampus, thereby enhancing hippocampal memory encoding. These studies provide strong evidence for the role of TMS in modulating endogenous neural oscillations—especially theta oscillations—in the regulation of cognitive functions. Specifically, in processes such as working memory and memory formation, TMS demonstrates significant potential to optimize brain function by modulating neural oscillatory patterns.

TMS stimulation target and target positioning

Stimulation targets

The stimulation target is the brain region or structure selected for TMS application. Common targets for stimulation include areas of the motor cortex, frontal lobe, temporal lobe, parietal lobe. These regions are thought to have specific effects that can occur after TMS stimulation, affecting cognitive, emotional, motor, and other functions. M1 is a traditional target for TMS in the treatment of motor symptoms such as hemiparesis and spasticity, where stimulation elicits a cluster of MEPs in surrounding muscle groups [132]. However, according to a review by Tome et al. [133], the current therapeutic range of stimulation of this target includes pain, headache, fatigue, dysphagia, speech and voice disorders, sleep disorders, cognitive dysfunction, disorders of consciousness, anxiety, depression, bladder dysfunction. The exact choice of target must therefore depend on the specific research or therapeutic objective.

Take the DLPFC, for example. It plays a critical role in enhancing cognition and is involved in several executive functions [134], making it a common target for the treatment of psychiatric disorders such as depression [135], neurodegenerative conditions such as AD, MCI, and dementia [136]. However, the results of a cross-diagnostic meta-analysis by Kan et al. [137] suggest that stimulation of this target has different effects on different symptoms, with a broad range of efficacy from craving > depression > (pain, general cognition, working and declarative memory, cognitive control, motor coordination, and obsessive-compulsive symptoms). Although the DLPFC is the most widely used stimulation site for the treatment of depression, and its efficacy and safety have been confirmed by many previous studies [138–140], the DLPFC is located in the larger prefrontal cortical area of the brain, and other parts of this region may also have potential stimulatory effects. A review by Downar et al. [141] provides evidence to support the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (DMPFC), ventromedial prefrontal cortex (VMPFC), ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC), and frontal polar cortex (FPC) as other stimulus targets for the treatment of depression. A meta-analysis by Lorentzen et al. [142] showed that the DLPFC, medullary cerebellum (MC), left parietal cortex (L-TPC), and left superior temporal sulcus (L-STS) can be used as stimulation targets in schizophrenia and indicated that targeting the DLPFC and using TMS with a stimulation frequency of >1 Hz was most effective. In addition, a study by Khurshid et al. [143] also reported that stimulation of targets in the motor cortex, DLPFC, orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) helped to alleviate OCD symptoms. Therefore, when performing TMS studies or treatments, it is crucial to find an appropriate stimulation target to ensure accuracy, safety, control of stimulation parameters, and reduction of non-specific effects.

Limitations of traditional targeting

TMS targeting is commonly used in clinical practice, including the ‘5 cm method’ and the ‘BeamF3 method’. The ‘5 cm method’, first used by George et al. [57], searches for hotspots in the primary motor cortex by eliciting a motor response in the contralateral abductor digitorum brevis muscle and then placing a stimulation target 5 cm anterior to this location along the parasagittal line. Although the ‘5 cm method’ is simple and easy to use, it does not take into account individual differences in head size, shape, and location of brain regions [144]. This is likely to result in the target being located outside the optimum area by the ‘5 cm method’, which obviously does not ensure the accuracy and reliability of the experimental results. Nauczyciel et al. [145] evaluated the accuracy of using the ‘5 cm method’ to locate targets in depressed patients; the distance between the actual ‘reference’ DLPFC and its standard determination was greater than 20 mm in 54% of subjects. The ‘BeamF3 method’ is a positioning method that combines the International 10–20 system (a standardized method of placing EEG electrodes) with the F3 position. The advantages of this method lie mainly in the fact that it takes into account inter-individual differences in cortical anatomy or cranial size, as well as the fact that it takes only 20% of the time of the 20-10 system [146]. However, the BeamF3 method does not take into account the shape of the head [147] but selects an approximate position, which may lead to inaccurate localization of the F3 region. Sanjuan et al. [148] also evaluated the ‘Beam-F3 method’ and stated that although the localization method is cost-effective and easy to implement, the use of Beam-F3 to calculate the F3 position may hinder the evaluation of the effectiveness of therapies applied to this position. It is worth noting that a recent RCT for the treatment of major depressive disorder by Trapp et al. [149] showed no statistically significant differences in any of the measurements between the Beam F3 and 5.5 cm groups. Therefore, the Beam F3 localization method may not be superior in improving efficacy. Due to the limitations of traditional positioning techniques, we need more targeted treatment options to improve the accuracy of TMS.

Personalized targeting

The TMS paradigm has evolved from traditional manual positioning to combined central and peripheral stimulation, and many studies [150–152] have shown that combined central and peripheral stimulation has significantly greater efficacy compared to single nervous system stimulation. To date, TMS targeting has become increasingly personalized, with personalized targeting techniques combining information on individual anatomy, functional characteristics, brain imaging data, and neural pathway theories for the benefit of humans. Some common types of personalized targeting are listed below.

Personalized localization of neuronavigation based on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

MRI-based neuronavigation techniques employ neuroimaging for enhanced target localization and are capable of elucidating certain aspects of individual brain structural variability. The results of a systematic review by Modak et al. [153] indicated that it may be more effective to use structural or functional neuroimaging to personalize treatment compared to standard TMS. MRI can be classified according to the purpose of its application into two main categories: structural MRI (sMRI), which is based on Brodmann partitioning, and functional MRI (fMRI), which is dependent on blood oxygen level (BOLD). MRI has fine anatomical detail and high sensitivity to pathological changes [154], so sMRI-based neuronavigation systems will take into account variations in the anatomy of the individual brain and have the opportunity to apply maximal currents to specific anatomical sites. Although some studies [155] have reported better efficacy of sMRI-based neuronavigation compared to standard ‘5 cm’ localization, some studies [156,157] have also indicated that MRI-guided neuronavigation localization failed to demonstrate a significant clinical advantage in comparison. This ineffectiveness may be related to the small sample size, the type of TMS, etc. On the other hand, because different symptoms map to different neural circuits and therefore require different stimulation targets for treatment [158], localization by sMRI alone in combination with neuronavigation techniques may be insufficient.

Not only does fMRI-guided neuronavigation localisation take into account individual anatomical variability, but the haemodynamic signals from fMRI also support the idea of localising functional brain connectivity [159]. Thus, fMRI can compensate for some of the shortcomings of sMRI. Stanford neuromodulation therapy (SNT) is the use of fMRI to individually target the left DLPFC region most relevant to subgenual ACC (sgACC) function, and Cole et al. [160] showed that this accelerated iTBS protocol (i.e. SNT) performed well in terms of both safety and efficacy for MDD. fMRI can be divided into resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) and task-related fMRI (trfMRI). rsfMRI does not require the subject to perform a specific task but rather performs the imaging while the subject is resting. This property makes rsfMRI easier to manipulate in clinical or research settings and more widely used in TMS treatment. Currently, functional brain connectivity has become one of the hotspots for identifying targets for TMS stimulation [161]. The vast majority of the cerebral cortex consists of a number of different networks of associated regions. A review reported [162] that several brain networks have been studied using fMRI, such as the default mode network (DMN), central executive network (CEN), and salience network (SN), language, sensorimotor, auditory, and visual networks. A study by Brakowski et al. [163] also showed that MDD is strongly associated with disrupted connectivity in the DMN, CEN, SN, and cerebellar and thalamic circuit networks. Therefore, finding the optimal stimulation target based on the localisation of brain functional connectivity can better promote TMS to regulate abnormal networks and induce neuroplasticity. Hawco et al. [164] also verified that the level of modulation in distal cortical areas after TMS pulses correlates with resting connections to this stimulation target. MRI can detect functional connectivity of different brain regions through signals such as BOLD [165], which in turn identifies areas of activation related to specific tasks or specific brain functions (considered as potential stimulation target areas), which can then be combined with a neuronavigation system for Personalized target localisation. navigation systems for Personalized target localisation. Fox et al. [166,167] used fMRI to identify differences in functional connectivity between effective and ineffective DLPFC-stimulated areas and suggested that regions of the left DLPFC area that showed a stronger negative correlation with the sgACC could lead to greater antidepressant effects. Thanks to the clinical potential of fMRI-guided neuronavigational localisation techniques, Cash et al. [168] developed a computational method capable of determining fMRI-guided personalized coordinates more accurately, which further addresses the problem of the high inter-individual variability of DLPFC connections. Recently, Jung et al. [169] used personalized targeted stimulation of the functionally connected L-TPC in the hippocampus based on fMRI connectivity analysis, which resulted in significant improvements in cognitive and functional performance in AD patients at the end of stimulation, and also increased functional connectivity between the hippocampus and precuneus. Tang et al. [170] used individual parietal-hippocampal functional connectivity and combined sMRI and fMRI to identify TMS stimulation targets, again with very positive results. sMRI can provide higher resolution details of brain anatomy than fMRI, so using the two together for neuronavigation localization can have a 1 + 1 > 2 effect. However, most of the current personalized localisation studies based on fMRI functional brain network connectivity are small-sample prospective clinical studies, and their validity needs to be further explored and validated.

Personalized targeting based on brain networks

Brain network localization is an important aspect of personalized targeting for TMS. The principle is to use visualization techniques to reflect the connectivity of neural networks and brain regions, and then identify TMS targets. It is well known that a variety of psychiatric or neurodegenerative disorders, including MDD, can actually be attributed to neural network disorders, not only to a single brain area abnormality [171]. The advantage of brain network localization is that it can accurately identify regions of abnormal activation or diminished function and locate key nodes that can synergistically modulate multiple brain regions or functional networks, thereby improving stimulation efficiency. For example, TMS stimulation of the left DLPFC induces changes in regions such as the ACC, basal ganglia, thalamus, and limbic system, all of which are implicated in the pathophysiology of MDD [171]. This network-based targeting approach provides a solid theoretical foundation for target selection. Additionally, regarding memory enhancement, TMS targeting the hippocampal-cortical network has demonstrated promising effects [172]. Studies by Hermiller et al. [173] support a causal role for the hippocampus and its fMRI connectivity within the hippocampal-cortical network in episodic memory retrieval. Similar research has also shown [174] that TMS targeting the posterior inferior parietal cortex (pIPC) can improve memory by modulating hippocampal function through the hippocampal-cortical network. Another advantage of brain network localization is the ability to interpret the efficacy of TMS more precisely. For example, although the DLPFC is also often used as a target for the treatment of AD, crude localization procedures have mostly been used in traditional studies. Bagattini et al. [175] extracted personalised coordinates from refMRI to target two core networks associated with AD, the DMN and the fronto-parietal network (FPN). The inferior parietal lobule (IPL) in the DMN and the DLPFC coordinates in the FPN were precisely localized (with higher sensitivity/selectivity). This is expected not only to improve the efficacy of TMS but also to provide a more direct explanation of its mechanism.

In addition to fMRI, introduced in the previous section, Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) is another powerful technique for localizing brain networks, particularly for targeting deep brain structures with TMS. Unlike fMRI, which primarily reflects functional connectivity, DTI focuses on the structural connectivity between brain regions. Studies have shown [176] that structural network topology is highly consistent across individuals; thus, leveraging this property may improve the accuracy of stimulus localization, reducing the risk of coil placement errors. Nummenmaa et al. [177] used DTI techniques to quantify the effects of TMS on white matter with different coil positions and orientations. This approach helps maximize activation of prespecified white matter tracts at the individual level, enabling stimulation of therapeutically relevant pathways that cannot be directly targeted. Furthermore, recent studies have advanced the use of DTI for TMS localization, demonstrating accurate stimulation of deep brain regions guided by DTI and highlighting the potential of this approach in treating psychiatric disorders [178]. It should be emphasized that the localization of brain networks is fundamentally a methodological issue. As in vivo brain imaging technologies such as DTI continue to evolve, it will bring unique insights to brain network connectivity.

PET-based individualised localization

The pathogenesis and clinical symptoms of psychiatric or neurological disorders are closely linked to abnormal metabolic activity in specific regions of the brain. For example, patients with depression have a significant reduction in glucose metabolism in the prefrontal cortex [179]; patients with primary progressive aphasia have atrophy and hypometabolism in the anterior temporal cortex [180]; and patients with obsessive-compulsive disorder have increased glucose metabolism in the orbitofrontal brain regions, while metabolism in the middle temporal gyrus and other brain regions is decreased [181]. A large number of studies [180,182–184] have shown that metabolic levels in relevant brain regions also improve after TMS treatment of these disorders. For example, a study by Tastevin et al. [182] found that depressed patients treated with rTMS had increased metabolism in the bilateral precuneus and right temporal lobe (including the fusiform gyrus, hippocampal region, and amygdala). Therefore, it is feasible to use metabolic levels as an indicator for finding targets. And PET imaging can use radiolabeled molecules, such as glucose, to provide information on brain activity and metabolism. A study by Oh et al. [185] used PET to verify the presence of an abnormal distribution of cerebral glucose metabolism at each stage in Parkinson’s dysphagia patients. At the same time, Oh et al. suggested that cortical regions with corresponding abnormal metabolism could be used as potential targets for transcranial neuromodulation to alleviate symptoms. Using PET, Kleinjung et al. [186] found that patients with chronic tinnitus had abnormally high metabolic activity in the auditory cortex. Kleinjung et al. then used a neuronavigation system to position the rTMS coil over the area and showed a significant reduction in tinnitus in the control group compared to sham stimulation. In addition, Pytel [187] and Herwig et al. [188] have also used PET to identify areas of reduced metabolism in the corresponding cortex and combined it with neuronavigation to localize the area and ultimately stimulate the identified targets, again with positive results. However, the disadvantages of PET-based personalized targeting are clear: firstly, PET scans tend to be more expensive than MRI scans; and secondly, there is a lack of sufficient research to validate their efficacy. Therefore, it is more important to explore further for validation.

Personalized targeting based on functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS)

fNIRS is an optical neuroimaging technique that measures haemoglobin and oxyhaemoglobin levels using near-infrared light and is often combined with TMS to assess cortical excitability and haemodynamic changes [189]. The monitored cerebral oxygenation and haemodynamic status can be used by fNIRS to map the functional brain, effectively and directly guiding the localisation of fNIRS to the TMS target sites [190]. Many studies [191–194] using fNIRS have found abnormalities in oxyhaemoglobin levels around TMS treatment targets or in functionally connected regions, but the situation improves after treatment. For example, Huang et al. [191] observed reduced oxyhaemoglobin levels in the bilateral prefrontal cortex in depressed patients using fNIRS and improved oxyhaemoglobin levels in bilateral frontal pole prefrontal cortex (FPPFC), VLPFC, and left DLPFC after remission of depression. However, there have been very few fNIRS-based target localization studies to date. Chang et al. [195] evaluated the feasibility of fNIRS as a target navigator for motor facilitation in stroke patients undergoing rTMS therapy. The results showed that fNIRS-guided hotspot stimulation significantly improved elbow function in patients compared to controls. Therefore, it is feasible to use the information provided by fNIRS to guide the selection of TMS targets. At the same time, fNIRS has the advantage of being portable and less expensive than imaging technologies such as MRI and PET. In addition, since the optical properties of the fNIRS scan medium are virtually unaffected by electromagnetic interference from the operation of the TMS coil, the fNIRS source and detector phototubes can be perfectly integrated with the TMS coil to optimise the TMS parameters (e.g. target cortical area, coil orientation, distance, and pulse intensity) [196]. However, the near-infrared nature of fNIRS and its low spatial resolution mean that it can only record functional data in superficial areas of the brain and is unable to capture TMS-evoked activation within subcortical structures (e.g. the basal ganglia) or deep cortical structures (e.g. the cingulate gyrus) [197]. Given the limitations of fNIRS in combination with TMS, it is mainly useful for assessing efficacy and optimising TMS parameters. Therefore, it is uncertain whether fNIRS has a clear advantage in target localisation.

Personalized targeting of TMS under neuro-cardiac-guided

Neuro-cardiac-guided TMS (NCG-TMS) is an emerging approach first proposed by Iseger et al. [198] to functionally target interactions between depressive networks and the cardio-cerebral axis or frontal vagal pathways. This method allows personalized targeting by stimulating different sites in the DLPFC and selecting the site with the greatest heart rate slowing as the optimal target for TMS. It has been noted [199] that depression is associated with a reduction in the vagus nerve (VN), and that depressed patients typically have higher heart rates, a higher risk of cardiovascular disease, and lower heart rate variability (HRV). The main function of the VN is to regulate the activity of the autonomic nervous system (especially the parasympathetic nerves), such as heart rate and metabolism. It also bridges the heart’s functional connection to other brain structures in depression. Buschman et al. [200] also conducted a study on the VN and showed that stimulation of the VN can effectively and rapidly reduce heart rate and affect a wide range of brain regions. Therefore, greater heart rate deceleration also reflects greater regulation of parasympathetic activity. In addition, Iseger et al. [201] also summarized this in a follow-up study that stimulation of key structures of the depressive brain network, such as the DLPFC, DMPFC, and sgACC and VN, through neuromodulation, was effective in ameliorating depressive symptoms. With the DLPFC and sgACC representing nodes of the CNE and DMN respectively, and the cingulo-opercular network (CON) and DLPFC negatively correlated with the sgACC and amygdala, the connectivity of these subregions contributes to the advancement of TMS targeting in depression [202]. The DLPFC is the best target for the treatment of depression as recommended by evidence-based guidelines [60]. So, in principle, the closer the stimulation site is to the DLPFC and sgACC functional connectivity pathway, the better the efficacy. This is the embodiment of the frontal-vagus (brain-heart) network theory and the theoretical basis of NCG-TMS. Seger and colleagues have carried out a series of exploratory studies on the localization of NCG-TMS. In 2017, Iseger et al. [198] compared seven targets in the 10–20 system and showed that maximal heart rate reduction occurred at the F3 and F4 loci, with 20-40% of subjects showing maximal heart rate deceleration at FC3 or FC4. This suggests that NCG-TMS can effectively localize the DLPFC-sgACC network and that there are differences in the ‘optimal stimulation site’ between individuals. A 2020 review by Iseger et al. [201] reported that prefrontal TMS reduced high heart rate, affected low HRV, and may normalize parasympathetic function. This also indicates that, to some extent, the greater the heart rate deceleration, the better the treatment. A replication and extension study was conducted by Iseger et al. [203] in 2021, the results of which not only further validated the reliability of the previous studies but also suggested the existence of a dose-response relationship between the intensity of TMS stimulation and heart rate. A study by Kaur et al. [204] also showed opposite patterns of heart rate activity at high and low frequency NCG-TMS. So, the advantages of NCG-TMS are very clear: MRI, PET, fNIRS, and other localization modalities are more expensive and time-consuming in comparison. These methods navigate based on BOLD signals or structural targets (e.g. Brodmann areas) and do not always consider functional connectivity. The NCG-TMS-guided personalized positioning not only saves costs and time, but also takes into account individual differences and functional brain connectivity networks. However, there are fewer studies in this area, and prospective clinical RCTs with larger sample sizes should be conducted to further investigate interactions between the cardio-brain axis or frontal vagal pathways. Second, Iseger and colleagues mostly chose healthy subjects rather than a depressed population. Finally, NCG-TMS should not be limited to depression but can be attempted for any psychiatric or neurological condition associated with the mind-brain connection network. Overall, this emerging technology is well worth promoting.

Other types of TMS Personalized positioning

There are, of course, other methods of individualized TMS positioning. For example, Weise et al. [205] proposed a novel approach to localizing cortical TMS effects. The method identifies cortical areas that are effectively stimulated by identifying brain areas that show stable functional relationships between the measured effects of the induced fields in different conditions. Klooster et al. [15] also proposed a personalized target localization approach through multimodal neuroimaging to compensate for the limitations of unimodal imaging. The method locates the optimal cortical target based on functional and diffusion MRI data, and then computationally models the TMS-induced electric field to overlap with the cortical target to determine the optimal personalized target. It is worth mentioning that recently Liu et al. [206] established a population probabilistic mapping from any cortical target in Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space based on a large sample MRI dataset (N = 114) to continuous proportional coordinate (CPC) standard scalp space (MNI2CPC), to guide target positioning for non-invasive neural regulation. The method uses individual MRI imaging data and specified personalized targets, combined with constructed algorithms, to determine the optimal skull surface stimulation location for each patient. The team developed a free online query site (transcranial-brain-atlas.org/MNI2CPC) for the optimal cranial surface location corresponding to any MNI coordinate. It takes advantage of the natural benefits of the CPC standard cranial surface coordinate system for truly nerve-free navigation, manual, and precise personalized positioning. This study also compared it with conventional neuronavigation localization (i.e. SNT), which is less time-consuming but has a slightly lower online localization accuracy, although this difference can be ignored in the clinical setting. However, these methods are only at the research stage, and future clinical trials with paired centers and large cohorts are needed to further establish the validity and feasibility of these methods. Clinical challenges and recommendations

Due to the inconsistent research methodologies in current TMS studies and the presence of individual anatomical, physiological, and behavioral differences [207], the primary challenges in the clinical application of TMS are insufficient accuracy and precision. Although the introduction of neuronavigation technology has improved targeting reliability, several limitations remain, including registration errors, simplification of anatomical models, head-tracking inaccuracies, and limited realism in electric field simulation models [208]. To address these issues, Nieminen et al. [209] proposed using an electric field calculation model based on the folded cortical surface rather than a smooth surface, enhancing registration accuracy by increasing the number of scalp position sampling points, and optimizing the head-tracking system to reduce errors. Additionally, Numssen et al. [209] innovatively introduced a pulse-wise coil displacement indicator to quantify coil displacement during TMS stimulation, enabling operators to more precisely control the stimulation position, avoid coil misalignment, and improve the accuracy of electric field modeling. Notably, Burns et al. [210] recently developed an open-source tool, LocaliteQA, which extracts the actual position of each TMS pulse and compares it with the predetermined target position. This tool outputs error metrics such as the variance of pulse position, the number of pulses deviating from the target, and the Euclidean distance between the pulse and the target, thereby enhancing the reproducibility of TMS research.

Although many of the aforementioned technologies and methods can enhance the efficacy and reliability of TMS, practical application in grassroots medical settings remains challenging due to the high cost of equipment, operational complexity, and limitations in time and personnel [211]. To maximize the efficacy and safety of TMS, clinicians and operators must acquire comprehensive knowledge. First, consulting authoritative guidelines is essential [89,212,213], and the appropriate TMS stimulation mode and parameters should be selected based on these recommendations. Second, coil selection should be tailored to the required stimulation depth. For example, patients with lower limb dysfunction, whose targets are located in deeper brain regions, would benefit from a deep coil such as the H-shaped coil. Additionally, the choice of theoretical model should correspond to the severity of brain injury: the inter-hemispheric competition model is suitable for mild injuries, while the compensatory model is preferable for severe injuries. Hospitals equipped with advanced neuroimaging technology can refer to the bimodal balance recovery model or the diaschisis model for personalized brain regulation. Finally, for institutions with cutting-edge neuroimaging capabilities, integrating neuronavigation systems with fMRI, DTI, or neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging is recommended to obtain detailed and comprehensive microscopic brain structure information, thereby enabling truly personalized treatment.

Outlook

We offer new perspectives and expectations for the future clinical use of TMS: (1) Combination of multiple stimulation modes for the same disease with multiple targets to further refine the stimulation protocol (including stimulation frequency, duration, interval and coil orientation); (2) Improvements in the precise positioning of stimulation targets and coil focusing, and related methodological breakthroughs. Although personalized targeting approaches can provide more precise treatments, they can be costly and cumbersome, and most are still at the research or trial stage. Large-scale RCTs are needed before they can be used in the clinic, where personalized treatments can be specifically detailed and compared with traditional localization methods to ensure their efficacy and feasibility. The medical community has been presented with a variety of stunning personalized targeting methods, and more research is expected to advance the field; (3) The theoretical models that guide the use of TMS have not yet been harmonized. Although theoretical models can effectively guide TMS treatment strategies, there is a degree of inconsistency.

Limitations

This review has the following principal limitations: (1) The review only searched a limited number of databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Google Scholar), which may have resulted in the omission of relevant studies or evidence from other databases. This could have impacted the comprehensiveness of the summary within the TMS field, potentially leading to narrow conclusions; (2) This study employed a narrative review format rather than a more rigorous systematic review framework, which carries a risk of selection bias. Consequently, the conclusions may inadvertently overlook significant literature, particularly recent TMS research findings; (3) Impact of heterogeneous study designs: Included TMS studies varied in experimental design, sample size, application methods, and measurement metrics. This heterogeneity in study design may limit the comprehensiveness of the results, thereby affecting the accuracy and consistency of the review’s conclusions; (4) Individual differences were not sufficiently discussed or analysed in this review, and the quality of the included studies varied considerably, encompassing methodological issues and sample biases. These factors may impact the reliability and interpretability of the results and should, therefore, be considered in practical applications.

Conclusion

This review demonstrates the potential of TMS as a neuromodulation technique. Barker and colleagues introduced the concept of TMS in 1985, and since then, TMS technology has evolved from its beginnings in basic science research to become an important tool for a wide range of clinical treatments and neuroscience research. The emergence of different stimulation modalities (e.g. rTMS, TBS, QPS) all reflects the tendency to continuously optimize stimulation parameters. The future direction of TMS is likely to involve multimodal combinations and further refinement of novel stimulation modes (including stimulation frequency, duration, interval and coil orientation). For instance, combination multiple stimulation modes targeting different brain regions for the same disorder may enhance protocol precision. Additionally, advancements in accurate targeting and coil focusing, along with related methodological innovations, are expected to improve the efficacy of TMS interventions.
