Injectable contraceptives differentially affect the hypothalamic–pituitary-gonadal axis and amenorrhea incidence†
Authors: Alexis J Bick, Chanel Avenant, Carole-Keza Capitaine, Sharoné van Eck, Mu-Tien Lee, Johnson M Moliki, Sigcinile Dlamini, David W Erikson, Jenni Smit, Mags Beksinska, G Justus Hofmeyr, Donita J Africander, Pai-Lien Chen, Janet P Hapgood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHormonal contraceptives modulate the hypothalamic–pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis; however, underlying mechanisms and differences between contraceptives are underexplored. The Women’s Health Injectable Contraception and HIV trial randomised 521 women to intramuscular depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA-IM) or norethisterone enanthate (NET-EN) and showed similar decreased estradiol levels, but more amenorrhea for DMPA-IM users. This secondary study excluded for misreporting contraceptive use for 128 participants (DMPA-IM n = 65; NET-EN n = 63). Peripheral blood serum collected at initiation and one week after the 24-week injection (25 W), at peak progestin levels, was analysed for gonadal steroids, progestins and peptide hormones. While no changes were detected in peripheral gonadotropin-releasing hormone levels, DMPA-IM decreased luteinising hormone (LH) less than NET-EN. DMPA-IM increased, while NET-EN decreased follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH). Both contraceptives substantially decreased gonadal steroid levels, more so in NET-EN users for testosterone and estradiol. Post-menopausal-like hypoestrogenic effects were greater than previously reported, consistent with the substantial reduction in LH levels. Whether reduced LH levels are due to direct pituitary, hypothalamic, or supra-hypothalamic effects by progestins, is unclear. MPA, unlike NET, increased fsh expression in LβT2 cells, likely via the glucocorticoid receptor, consistent with direct effects on the pituitary by MPA in women. Amenorrhea associated in a time-varying manner with MPA and HPO hormone levels and LH/FSH, for DMPA-IM but not NET-EN users. HPO hormone profiles differ between DMPA-IM and NET-EN users and compared to pre- and post-menopausal women. Mechanisms affecting amenorrhea likely differ between contraceptives, with lower 25 W LH/FSH being consistent with more amenorrhea for DMPA-IM.

Introduction

Hormonal contraceptives (HCs) exert their anti-fertility effects predominantly by inhibition of ovulation, via modulating the hypothalamic–pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis [1–3]. The hypothalamus secretes gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) in a pulsatile manner, which stimulates the pituitary gonadotrophs to secrete luteinising hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) to regulate synthesis and release of progesterone (P4), testosterone (T) and estradiol (E2) in the ovaries [4, 5]. The HPO axis regulates all aspects of female reproduction, including the menstrual cycle, and impacts on multiple other physiological processes [4, 6–8].

While most HCs reportedly reduce serum concentrations of LH, FSH, E2 and P4 [1], limited data suggest that different HCs exhibit differential effects on the HPO axis [1, 2, 9–12]. Different progestin-only contraceptives reportedly result in varying degrees of hypoestrogenism [10, 12]. However, very little is known about the relative effects and mechanisms of HPO hormone regulation by injectable progestin-only contraceptives. The three-monthly, intramuscular (IM) injection of 150 mg of medroxyprogesterone acetate (MPA, depot-MPA, DMPA-IM) is the most common contraceptive method in sub-Saharan Africa [13]. Norethisterone enanthate (NET-EN), a two-monthly, intramuscular injection of 200 mg norethisterone (NET), is also widely used in South Africa [14, 15].

GnRH released from the hypothalamus travels to the pituitary via the hypophyseal portal vein in mammals. Given the difficulties involved in measuring GnRH in clinical trial participants in the hypophyseal portal vein in humans, several studies have successfully reported GnRH levels in the serum of peripheral blood in humans [16–27], with limited reports by others of GnRH levels in portal blood in humans [28, 29]. However, the biological significance and relationship between peripheral and hypophyseal portal vein GnRH levels are unclear from the literature and peripheral blood serum GnRH concentrations should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, the detection of GnRH pulsatility and amplitude in peripheral blood in humans has led to the conclusion that these fluctuating concentrations, although diluted, likely reflect changes in GnRH levels in the pituitary portal vein and are likely to be of hypothalamic origin [23, 24]. Although peripheral blood serum GnRH likely also contains non-pulsatile GnRH from extra-hypothalamic sources [30] (www.proteinatlas.org), the extent of this has not been determined. Furthermore, peripheral blood serum GnRH levels are likely to be biologically relevant since they reportedly also fluctuate during the menstrual cycle [16–21]. GnRH receptors have been detected in multiple peripheral tissues [30] (www.proteinatlas.org), which would presumably respond to GnRH in peripheral blood.

Early studies report that DMPA-IM inhibits the midcycle LH and FSH peaks in naturally cycling pre-menopausal women [31–33], with some studies reporting levels within the range of either the luteal or follicular phase [34–38]. E2 and P4 levels reportedly decrease to those of the follicular range for naturally cycling pre-menopausal women [10, 33–49]. Much less information is available on the effects of NET-EN on the HPO axis. Limited and non-comparative studies report that NET-EN decreases P4 and E2 levels [10, 50–58], inhibits the LH surge to values similar to follicular phase [51, 53, 59], and inhibits FSH [51, 53, 60–62]. One comparative non-randomised study reported that both DMPA-IM and NET-EN reduce P4, E2 and LH, with a greater reduction in E2 levels for DMPA-IM than NET-EN [63]. There are very limited head-to-head reports comparing LH or FSH levels in women on DMPA-IM versus NET-EN [33, 63, 64] and no reports for either contraceptive on GnRH levels. While the LH/FSH ratio can inform on low ovarian reserve or dysregulated HPO disorders such as polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) [65–67], the mechanisms affecting the LH/FSH ratio are unknown [68]. Two recent randomised studies are consistent with inhibition of gonadal steroids by DMPA-IM and suggest progestin-specific effects [9, 11, 12, 69]. Our results from the whole cohort in the Women’s Health, Injectable Contraception and HIV (WHICH) trial which randomised women to DMPA-IM or NET-EN showed that E2 levels are reduced to similar but post-menopausal levels by both methods [12], while both injectables decrease total T levels, albeit NET-EN more so than DMPA-IM [9]. The Evidence for Contraceptive Options and HIV Outcomes (ECHO) trial [70], where women were randomised to DMPA-IM, the copper intra uterine device (IUD) and a levonorgestrel (LNG)-containing implant, found that median E2 levels are reduced in DMPA-IM more than in LNG implant and IUD users [69], while T levels are similarly decreased in DMPA-IM and LNG implant users [11].

HCs also differentially affect endometrial thickness, ovarian activity, follicular development, return to fertility and bleeding patterns, including the extent of amenorrhea [1–3, 41, 54, 71–77]. Progestin-only contraceptives exhibit variable effects on ovarian activity, from normal ovarian cycles to total suppression of follicular development [1]. In addition to inhibiting ovulation [43, 78], DMPA-IM causes unpredictable uterine bleeding for the first few months after initial injection, which gradually ceases with number of injections and is followed by secondary amenorrhea in most women [43, 71, 72, 77, 78]. Several studies report that there is a shorter time to return to fertility for NET-EN than DMPA-IM [41, 54, 73, 75, 76]. Two comparative studies also showed less amenorrhea, bleeding and spotting during the first 6 months of NET-EN use compared to DMPA-IM [75, 76]. Amenorrhea is reportedly significantly higher with DMPA-IM than the LNG implant [79, 80], with only 11% of DMPA-IM users experiencing normal menstrual patterns one year after initiation [79]. We reported a significant increase in amenorrhea with use of DMPA-IM and NET-EN, but more so for DMPA-IM than NET-EN in WHICH trial participants [12]. The ECHO randomised trial found significant differences in amenorrhoea between contraceptives by 49%, >41%, >12% and regular menstrual pattern by 26%, <35%, < 87% for DMPA-IM, LNG implant, and copper IUD, respectively [81]. However, the mechanisms and the roles of HPO axis hormones in these differential effects on the female genital tract (FGT) are unclear.

These FGT effects suggest that progestin-only contraceptives are likely to have variable effects on HPO hormones and their multiple downstream physiological consequences depending on type of progestin, number of injections and time of sampling after injection. One potential source of progestin-specific effects is different relative activities via different steroid receptors [82–85]. It is well-established that both MPA and NET act via the progesterone and androgen receptors (PR and AR, respectively) in vitro [82, 84, 86–88]. However, MPA, but not NET or most other progestins used in contraception, also acts via the glucocorticoid receptor (GR) at concentrations within the range of peak serum levels in DMPA-IM and NET-EN users [82, 84].

Here we aimed to determine the individual and relative effects of DMPA-IM and NET-EN on peripheral blood serum levels of key hormones in the HPO axis, including GnRH, LH, FSH and P4, and the LH/FSH ratio in samples from the WHICH trial. We have previously reported that the WHICH trial participants, as reported for some other trials involving DMPA-IM use, exhibited substantial misreporting of non-study progestin use, particularly at baseline [89]. Our published WHICH trial results suggest that data with reduced variability from non-study progestin treatments retain randomization balance at baseline and can yield more robust findings, which can offset the limitations of a smaller sample size [89, 90]. Thus, to preserve the distinctiveness of the NET- EN and DMPA-IM arms in the current study, we excluded participants exhibiting above threshold levels of non-study progestins. We furthermore aimed to investigate molecular mechanisms of gonadotropin regulation by MPA (the active progestin in DMPA-IM) and NET (the active progestin in NET-EN) using an in vitro murine pituitary gonadotroph cell line model. We also investigated possible causal pathways between serum MPA and NET levels, HPO axis hormone serum levels, and amenorrhea, by time-varying associations.

Materials and methods

Primary study, ethics, and biosafety

This is a secondary study of the open-label randomised WHICH clinical trial (PACTR 202009758229976) [12]. Ethical approval for the WHICH trial was obtained from the Faculty of Health Sciences Human Research Ethics Committee (FHS HREC, M180528) of the University of Witwatersrand, and from the East London Hospital Institutional Ethics Committee. This research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent. Approval for this secondary study on stored biological samples was obtained from the University of Cape Town Human Research Ethics Committee and Institutional Biosafety Committee (HREC 664/2018 and IBC047–2020, respectively).

Study design and sample collection

Trial participants were randomised to either intramuscular injectable contraceptive, 150 mg DMPA-IM every 12 weeks (n = 262) or 200 mg NET-EN every 8 weeks (n = 259), at two South African research sites in Durban and East London: Wits MatCH Research Unit (MRU) and East London and Mdantsane public health clinics and hospitals (Effective Care Research Unit, ECRU) [12]. Women were aged between 18 and 40 and seeking injectable contraception. Participants were excluded if they reported that they had used DMPA-IM in the previous 6 months or NET-EN in the previous 4 months, were pregnant, were living with HIV, or were using or intending to use medication which might have interfered with biological measurements such as steroids or drugs affecting renal function, such as pre-exposure prophylactic drugs for HIV. Women were counselled on condom use and reducing HIV and STI risk. All participants provided written informed consent.

Blood samples were collected at initiation of the trial (D0) and at about seven days after the 24-week progestin injection (25 W), a time corresponding to peak serum progestin levels for the whole cohort (median 7 days, interquartile range [IQR] 7–8 days) [89]. Serum was separated and stored at −80°C. Levels of GnRH, LH, FSH, P4, T and E2 (n = 99 DMPA-IM and n = 94 NET-EN) were measured (Figure 1). We previously used ultra-high performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC–MS/MS) to determine study (MPA or NET) and non-study (LNG, nestorone, etonogestrel and gestodene) levels at D0 and 25 W in donor-matched serum samples [89]. Here we excluded from analysis any participants with any detected progestin at concentrations ≥1.5 nM at D0, or NET in the DMPA-IM arm or MPA in the NET-EN arm at concentrations ≥1.5 nM, at 25 W. We present data for 128 (DMPA-IM n = 65, NET-EN n = 63) women after this exclusion of 34% (65/193) of participants from the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) population (Figure 1). Researchers were blinded to assigned contraceptive arms during sample processing and hormone assays. Amenorrhea was recorded as any self-reported occurrence of no menstruation in the last three months [12], as for other clinical trials on contraception [79, 81]. Participants were asked to choose between the options of heavy, moderate, light or none, regarding menstruation.

Consort flow diagram. DMPA-IM: depo-medroxyprogesterone acetate intramuscular, NET-EN: norethisterone enanthate, MRU: MatCH Research Unit, ECRU: Effective Care Research Unit, mITT: modified intention-to-treat, PP: per-protocol, D0: Day 0 or baseline, 25 W: 25 weeks.

Serum hormone measurements

We measured hormone levels in parallel from matched serum samples taken at D0 and 25 W. Serum LH and FSH levels were measured by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Cobas, Elecsys: sensitivity 0.1 ≤ IU/L) at the National Health Laboratory Services at Groote Schuur Hospital (Cape Town, South Africa). Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) were used to measure GnRH (Elabscience E-EL-0071, Texas, U.S.A.; sensitivity ≤9.38 pg/mL). Serum T levels were measured by means of UHPLC–MS/MS and have been published elsewhere as part of a larger cohort study [9]. However, the T values for the women in this secondary study are reported here for comparative purposes. The limit of detection (LOD) and lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) for T were 0.0870 nM and 0.170 nM, respectively. Previously reported E2 levels from WHICH samples were measured using a chemiluminescence microparticle immunoassay (ARCHITECT E2 B7K720; analytical sensitivity ≤10 pg/mL (36.7 pM) [12]. Here we report E2 and P4 levels in WHICH serum samples measured by UHPLC–MS/MS at the Endocrine Technologies Core (ETC) facility at Oregon National Primate Research Center/Oregon Health & Science University (OHSU) in the U.S.A. E2 and P4 were extracted from 200 μL serum using dichloromethane and separated on a Raptor 2.7 μm Biphenyl 50 mm × 2.1 mm chromatography column and analysed on a Shimadzu Nexera-LCMS-8050 instrument as previously described [91, 92]. The method detection limit (MDL) for E2 and P4 was 18.3 pM and 9.54 pM, respectively. The LLOQ of E2 and P4 was 33.0 pM and 60.4 pM, respectively.

Cell culture and test compounds

The murine pituitary gonadotroph cell line LβT2 was obtained from Pamela Mellon (University of California San Diego, U.S.A.) [93, 94]. LβT2 cells remain to date the best in vitro model for mammalian gonadotroph cells since a suitable human cell line is unavailable. Cells expressing FSHβ mRNA in response to GnRH [95] were cultured and maintained as previously described [96] and only used at less than 30 passages. Cells were regularly tested for mycoplasma by Hoechst staining and fluorescence microscopy [97] and only mycoplasma-negative cells were used in this study. The hormone ligands (11b,16a)-9-fluoro-11,17, 21-trihydroxy-16-methylpregna-1,4-diene-3,20-dione (dexamethasone, DEX); 6α-methyl-17α-hydroxy-progesterone acetate (medroxyprogesterone acetate, MPA); 17α-ethynyl-19-nortestosterone (norethisterone; NET); 11β-(4-dimethylamino) phenyl-17β-hydroxy-(1-propynyl)estra-4,9-dien-3-one (mifepristone, RU486) and 4-pregnene-3,20-dione (P4) and [des-Gly10, D-Ala6]-LH-RH (GnRH) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (South Africa). LβT2 cells were stimulated with 100 nM DEX, MPA, NET alone or in combination with RU486 in 0.1% (v/v) absolute ethanol for 24 h, with the addition of 100 nM GnRH in sterile water for the last 8 h prior to harvesting. Concentrations of 100 nM ligands were used to obtain maximal responses.

Quantitative real-time RT-PCR

RNA and cDNA were prepared as previously described [98]. qRT-PCR was carried out using murine primers for lhβ: FWD 5′-GCCTGTCAACGCAACTCTGG-3′ REV 5′-CAGGCCATTGGTTGAGTCCT-3′ [99]; fshβ: FWD 5′-GTGCGGGCTACTGCTACACT-3′ REV 5′-CAGGCAATCTTACGGTCTCG-3′ [100]; and TATA Binding Protein (tbp): FWD 5′-TGCACAGGAGCCAAGAGTGAA-3′ REV 5′-CACATCACAGCTCCCCACCA-3′ [101]; with annealing temperatures of 58, 60, and 60°C, respectively.

Data analysis and statistics

UHPLC–MS/MS E2 and P4 data were analysed using LabSolutions version 5.72 (Shimadzu). Target reference ion ratios were set according to the reference ion ratio of the highest standard. Default ion allowance for peak identification was 30% relative to this target ratio. Linear regression with 1/C weighting was used to analyse calibration curves. For the immunoassays (LH and FSH) and ELISA (GnRH), samples below the LOD were assigned a value of LOD/2 to generate imputed values, while for the UHPLC–MS/MS (T, P4, and E2), values below the LLOQ, but above the LOD/MDL were assigned LLOQ/2. Values lower than LOD/MDL were assigned values of half the LOD/MDL to generate imputed values. Baseline characteristics were compared between arms using the Wilcoxon rank sum test, Fisher’s exact test or the Kruskal-Wallis test, where applicable (Supplementary Table S1). Clinical data were analysed for differences in hormone levels and their time-varying association within and between contraceptive arms by generalised linear models [102] on data transformed by Box-Cox power transformation approaches [103]. To generate a robust clinical dataset for levels of all the hormones for phases in the menstrual cycle, the WHICH mITT population in this study was categorised based on their D0 LH, P4, E2, and if necessary FSH levels (Supplementary Table S2), by a model we developed using established literature criteria [6, 104, 105]. Detailed information about the cycle phasing algorithm is in the Supplementary Methods. Statistical significance between hormone levels in the DMPA-IM or NET-EN arm at 25 W compared to those of women stratified according to the menstrual cycle phase at D0, was determined by Mann–Whitney tests. The above analyses were performed using SAS® V9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, U.S.A.). To visually compare hormone profiles for women on DMPA-IM or NET-EN at 25 W to those of women in different menstrual cycle phases, the median levels of hormones in the menstrual cycle phases, as well as those in the contraceptive arms, were expressed relative to the median level of each corresponding hormone from the follicular phase, which was set to one. For the mean range of hormone levels reported in the literature for post-menopausal women (Supplementary Table S3), the lowest mean value of each hormone (post-menopausal low) was set to one and the mean level of the corresponding hormones in the follicular phase, as well as those in the contraceptive arms, were expressed relative to that. For the in vitro data, Shapiro-Wilks normality tests were used to indicate normal distribution and were thus analysed using one-way ANOVA followed by unpaired t-tests using GraphPad Prism 9.31 from GraphPad Software, Inc. (La Jolla California, U.S.A.). Time-varying associations of hormone levels with published amenorrhea data from the WHICH trial [12] for the population of women investigated in this study, were performed using the same time-varying association statistical methods as described above. The graphical abstract and schematic were generated using BioRender, while other figures were created using GraphPad Prism. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Primary data and baseline characteristics

No significant differences were detected between contraceptives in the baseline characteristics for the population of 128 women (DMPA-IM n = 65, NET-EN n = 63) shown in Supplementary Table S1. This population had ≤1.5 nM LNG, nestorone, etonogestrel and gestodene at D0 and 25 W, ≤ 1.5 nM MPA or NET at D0, and ≤ 1.5 nM NET in the DMPA-IM arm or MPA in the NET-EN arm at 25 W [89]. The trial profile is shown in Figure 1.

DMPA-IM and NET-EN do not detectably affect GnRH levels, but decrease LH and regulate FSH levels in opposite directions

No significant change was detected in serum GnRH levels for DMPA-IM or NET-EN users in the 128 women (Figure 2A), with median levels at D0 (DMPA-IM: 137.6 pg/mL; NET-EN: 133.3 pg/mL) being similar to those at 25 W (DMPA-IM: 136.6 pg/mL; NET-EN: 141.2 pg/mL) (DMPA-IM D0 vs 25 W, P = 0.797; NET-EN D0 vs 25 W, P = 0.061) (Table 1). In addition, no significant differences were detected between the two contraceptive arms at D0, 25 W or for the change (25 W-D0) (P = 0.091) or % change (P = 0.389) in GnRH levels. However, serum LH levels were significantly (P < 0.001) and substantially reduced in both contraceptive arms from D0 to 25 W (DMPA-IM: median 5.3–3.7 IU/L, −34.3% change; NET-EN: median 6.8–3.6 IU/L, −52.6% change) (Figure 2B, Table 1). While no significant differences were detected between the two arms at D0, 25 W or for the change (25 W-D0) in LH levels, there was a significant difference (P = 0.028) in the % change. Interestingly, serum FSH levels were significantly (P < 0.001) increased after DMPA-IM use (median 4.7–5.7 IU/mL, 17.1%), but significantly (P = 0.005) reduced from D0 to 25 W after NET-EN use (median 5.3–4.3 IU/L, −15.7%), resulting in a significant (P < 0.001) difference between the contraceptive arms at 25 W and in the change (25 W-D0) and % change (both P < 0.001) in FSH levels (Figure 2C, Table 1). Despite the differential regulation observed in FSH levels, the LH/FSH ratio was substantially reduced in both the DMPA-IM (median 1.1–0.7, −44.9%) and the NET-EN (median 1.4–0.8, −42.9%) arms (both P < 0.001) (Figure 2D, Table 1). While no significant difference between the arms was detected in the (25 W-D0) change (P = 1.000) or % change (P = 0.728) in the ratios, the LH/FSH ratio was significantly (P = 0.029) higher in the NET-EN than in the DMPA-IM arm at 25 W.

DMPA-IM and NET-EN similarly decrease P4 levels, but differentially decrease T and E2 levels in this population

Having shown that use of DMPA-IM and NET-EN decreased LH and the LH/FSH ratio, but differentially regulated FSH serum levels, we investigated the effects of these contraceptives on serum levels of P4. At baseline, the median P4 levels were similar (P = 0.486) (DMPA-IM 187.6 pM, NET-EN 190.8 pM) in both contraceptive arms (Figure 2E, Table 1). The use of both contraceptives at 25 W significantly (P < 0.001) decreased median P4 levels to 76.6 pM and 30.2 pM in the DMPA-IM and NET-EN arm, respectively. No significant differences were detected between the two arms at 25 W or for the change (25 W-D0) or % change (P = 0.104, 0.631 and 0.622, respectively) in P4 levels.

We have previously shown that the use of DMPA-IM and NET-EN significantly reduces total T [9] and E2 [12] levels in serum from the whole WHICH cohort (n = 434–446). To determine whether this response is detected in the smaller population of women analysed for HPO hormone levels and after further excluding women based on misreported or non-study progestin use, we re-analysed the total T levels using only the data from the 128 women in this study (Figure 2F, Table 1). E2 levels were measured in the current study using a UHPLC–MS/MS method that is likely more specific and sensitive (see Methods) than the immunoassay previously used [12]. Similar to our published T data [9], both DMPA-IM and NET-EN significantly and substantially reduced T levels, with NET-EN (−61.1%) doing so more than DMPA-IM (−32.8%), resulting in a significant difference between the two arms in the T levels at 25 W, and the change and % change in T levels (25 W-D0) (P = 0.005, P < 0.001 and P < 0.001, respectively) (Figure 2F, Table 1). As previously reported [12], both DMPA-IM and NET-EN significantly and substantially reduced E2 levels (DMPA-IM -83.9%, NET-EN −91.9%) (Figure 2G, Table 1). However, we now detect that NET-EN reduces E2 levels significantly more than DMPA-IM (change (25 W-D0) P = 0.002, % change P = 0.002), resulting in significantly (P < 0.001) lower E2 levels at 25 W compared to DMPA-IM (DMPA-IM 40.4 pM, NET-EN 16.5 pM).

HPO axis hormone profiles in contraceptive users not only differ from each other, but also from those of naturally cycling pre-menopausal and post-menopausal women

We next determined how the profiles of HPO axis hormone levels (GnRH, LH, FSH, P4, T, E2 levels and LH/FSH ratio) in these contraceptive users at 25 W compared to those in different menstrual cycle phases from naturally cycling pre-menopausal women from the WHICH trial at D0 (see Methods and Supplementary Methods) and from values for post-menopausal women from the literature. Statistical analysis of differences is summarised in Table 2, except for post-menopausal women, for which a range of means is available from the literature (Supplementary Table S3) [21, 106–113], but not the individual data. Although the hormone profiles for the contraceptive users appear visually similar to each other (Figure 3), there are significant differences (Figure 2, Table 1), with 4/7 measures being significantly different between contraceptive users at 25 W (FSH: P < 0.001, LH/FSH: P = 0.029, T: P = 0.005, E2: P < 0.001) (Table 2). As expected, the median hormone profiles of women in the follicular, luteal and ovulatory phases of the menstrual cycle are very different (P < 0.001 for 4/7 measures for each comparison) from each other (Figure 3A and B), Supplementary Table S2). Visually, the mean hormone profile of women in the follicular phase also appears different from that of post-menopausal women (Figure 3C, Supplementary Table S3). Similarly, the mean hormone profiles for both contraceptive users appear visually different from those of post-menopausal women (Figure 3C, Supplementary Table S3). While the median hormone profiles for both contraceptive users visually resemble those for pre-menopausal women in the follicular phase (Figure 3B), there are distinct differences between the contraceptive user profiles and those of women not on contraception, both visually (Figure 3A and B) and statistically (significant differences between at least 4/7 measures for each comparison) (Table 2). No significant (P ≥ 0.055) difference in median GnRH levels was detected between women on DMPA-IM or NET-EN compared to any of the menstrual cycle phases (Table 2). Median FSH levels in DMPA-IM users were significantly (P < 0.001) higher than those in the luteal phase, but similar to those in the follicular (P = 0.107) and ovulatory (P = 0.311) phases (Table 2). However, the median levels of FSH in NET-EN users were similar (P = 0.074) to those of women in the luteal phase, but significantly lower than those in the follicular (P < 0.001) and ovulatory phases (P = 0.013) (Table 2). Interestingly, the LH/FSH ratios were significantly (P ≤ 0.018) lower in both DMPA-IM and NET-EN users, compared to pre-menopausal women (Table 2). MPA increases fshβ mRNA expression in the presence of GnRH, likely via the GR, in murine pituitary gonadotrophs

The clinical data showing effects of these contraceptives on gonatropins but not GnRH serum levels, suggest that MPA and NET may regulate gonadotropin levels via direct effects on pituitary gonadotrophs. We investigated the mechanisms of lhβ and fshβ regulation by GnRH, MPA and NET and the role of the GR in the LβT2 murine pituitary gonadotroph cell line. Stimulation with GnRH significantly increased fshβ mRNA levels by 1.5-fold (P < 0.001), relative to vehicle (Figure 4), as expected [114]. In the presence of GnRH, DEX and MPA, but not NET, significantly increased fshβ mRNA levels by 4.4- and 3.1-fold, respectively, relative to vehicle (both P < 0.0001). Upon co-stimulation with RU486, a GR and PR antagonist, the increase in GnRH-induced fshβ mRNA levels with MPA and DEX was lost, but not the effect of GnRH alone. In the absence of GnRH, co-stimulation with RU486 also inhibited MPA- and DEX-induced fshβ mRNA levels (data not shown). No regulation of lhβ mRNA by GnRH, MPA, NET or DEX was detected in LβT2 cells under these stimulation conditions (data not shown). We detected endogenous GR but not PR or AR protein by western blotting in our LβT2 cells (data not shown).

Discussion

We report for the first time robust data on the individual and relative effects of DMPA-IM and NET-EN on peripheral blood serum levels of GnRH, LH, FSH and P4, together with more robust E2 data assayed by UHPLC–MS/MS, from the randomised head-to-head WHICH trial, after correction for misreported or non-study progestin use. Ours is the first study to report levels of these endogenous hormones one week after the 3rd DMPA-IM and 4th NET-EN injection, corresponding to peak serum progestin levels at 25 W after initiation. We also used a novel translational approach to investigate direct effects on the pituitary by MPA and NET on gonadotropin levels in a gonadotroph cell line model. Towards understanding possible causal pathways and clinical relevance, we present novel association data between progestin and HPO hormone levels and amenorrhea.

Our clinical serum hormone data reveal that both contraceptives significantly decrease LH and ovarian gonadal steroid levels. The substantial % changes in these hormone levels from D0 to 25 W indicate substantial inhibition of ovarian function for both contraceptives. However, NET-EN users have a greater % decrease in LH, T and E2 (−52.6, −61.1 and − 91.9%, respectively) than DMPA-IM users (−34.3%, −32.8% and − 83.9%, respectively), as well as lower T and E2 levels at 25 W than DMPA-IM users, consistent with more inhibition of ovarian function by NET-EN than DMPA-IM. Interestingly, DMPA-IM increased (17.1%), but NET-EN decreased (−15.7%) FSH levels. Thus, while both contraceptives modulate HPO axis hormones and inhibit ovarian function, there are several significant differences in their effects on the HPO axis.

Our data showing a decrease in LH levels by both contraceptives are consistent with the literature [31, 34, 36, 38, 49, 51, 53, 59, 60]. However, limited head-to-head studies either did not detect a difference in LH levels between these contraceptives [63], or detected higher LH levels in women on NET-EN versus DMPA-IM [33]. Interestingly, our median LH levels appear to be lower than those limited mean, median or range of data previously reported for small participant numbers (n = 7–22) on these contraceptives [32, 33, 35, 37, 53, 59, 62]. Our median FSH levels at 25 W appear to fall within the range of limited reported data [33, 64]. As for LH, our median P4, T and E2 levels for both contraceptives appear to also be lower than data from previous reports [9, 35, 44, 45, 47, 48, 51, 53, 57, 75, 115–120].

Our previously reported total median T levels for the whole WHICH cohort (mITT analysis) appear similar to the current data for the 128 women in this subgroup, but the % decreases appear lower [9, 12], most likely due to confounding by mainly misreporting or non-study MPA levels, the predominant contraceptive used by this cohort before trial initiation [12]. Our previously reported E2 levels for the whole WHICH cohort (mITT analysis), however, appear quite different from the current data for the 128 women after analysis at D0 and 25 W by UHPLC–MS/MS. The current median E2 levels are lower at 25 W, particularly for the NET-EN arm (DMPA-IM 25 W 40.4 vs 76.5 pM and NET-EN 25 W 16.5 vs 69.8 pM, for current vs published [9, 12], respectively). This is likely due to the use of a more specific and/or sensitive E2 assay and less confounding by misreporting or non-study MPA levels. For example, we have previously reported that 27% of women in the NET-EN arm have detectable MPA in their serum at 25 W [89]. Mean E2 levels have been categorised as post-menopausal when they are <15 pg/mL (55 pmol/L) [121]. The majority of our 25 W median E2 levels are below this threshold (63% of DMPA-IM users and 92% of NET-EN users), which suggests that more NET-EN users have post-menopausal E2 levels compared to DMPA-IM users.

When we compare the median concentrations of the HPO hormones at 25 W with those at D0, after staging participants at D0 into follicular, luteal and ovulatory phases of the menstrual cycle (Supplementary Table S2), both contraceptives result in overall hormonal profiles that are significantly different to each other and that do not correspond to any of the menstrual cycle phases (Figure 3A and B, Table 2), nor do they resemble the lower or upper hormone levels in a range for post-menopausal hormone values from the literature (Figure 3C), Supplementary Table S3).

Possible differences between our WHICH hormone data and the literature may be due to differences in ethnicity or BMI of studied populations, different methods of hormone quantification and/or different times of sampling, since in the literature most sampling was performed at nadir before the next injection [11, 35, 38, 44, 50, 51, 54, 61, 69, 118, 122–124]. Another source is likely varying degrees of confounding due to misreporting the use of non-study progestins before and during the trials [89, 91, 125, 126]. Differences may also be due to varying number of injections, since in several reports [36, 118, 122] women are sampled within the first 3 months after the first injection, when a hormonal profile would likely be different to that after the 24-week injection [43, 71, 72].

Our results in the LβT2 cell line are consistent with a mechanism whereby DMPA-IM increases FSH levels by direct actions on pituitary gonadotrophs. Although LH and FSH proteins contain a common α-subunit and different β-subunits, we did not investigate regulation of the α-subunit since regulation of gonadotropin protein levels occurs primarily via regulation of the β-subunits [127–130]. We detected endogenous GR protein, consistent with previous reports [96, 131–133], but not endogenous PR or AR protein in the LβT2 cells, unlike others [132, 134]. Other reports have suggested that primary rodent pituitary gonadotroph cells also express PR and AR protein [132, 134–136], although evidence for this is absent for primary human pituitary gonadotrophs, for which a cell line is also not available. Taken together, the RU486 data, our lack of PR protein detection and our findings that similar responses were observed with MPA and the potent synthetic glucocorticoid, DEX, indicate that the GR is required for the MPA-induced upregulation of fshβ mRNA levels, consistent with the literature on effects with DEX in LβT2 cells [128, 132]. In contrast, our in vitro data suggest that the decrease in FSH and LH serum levels in women using NET-EN are not due to direct effects by NET on gonadotropin gene expression in pituitary gonadotrophs. We were unable to measure FSH peptide secreted into the medium due to insufficient sensitivity of the ELISA kit. However, we measured LH peptide secreted into the medium, which showed no effect of MPA or NET on LH peptide secretion in the absence or presence of GnRH, but upregulation with GnRH alone (data not shown), suggesting that the progestins also do not affect LH protein synthesis and secretion in this cell line under these conditions. Thus, while our in vitro data provide a plausible mechanism for the increase in FSH levels in DMPA-IM users, they do not do so for the decrease in LH and FSH in NET-EN users, nor the decrease in LH for DMPA-IM users.

Our clinical and time-varying analysis data suggest that the predominant mechanism by which both progestins substantially decrease ovarian function to result in hypoestrogenism, is by substantially decreasing LH levels (Figure 5). This is consistent with published observational data showing that injectable progestins decrease the mid-cycle LH surge [1–3, 34, 43, 137, 138]. However, from our data the mechanisms for decreased LH levels in women are unclear. We did not obtain in vitro evidence for a direct effect by progestins on gonadotrophs to decrease LH secretion, or for NET to decrease FSH levels. This may reflect what is happening in vivo, or it may have been due to a limitation of the cell line model. Our clinical association data are consistent with a direct effect of the progestins on gonadotroph LH levels, where changes in concentrations of MPA in the DMPA-IM arm and NET in the NET-EN arm (D0 to 25 W) show significant negative associations with the changes in LH, P4, T and E2 levels, but not with peripheral blood GnRH concentrations (Table 3). Furthermore, we detected significant positive time-varying associations for LH with P4 and T concentrations for both contraceptive arms.

Although our clinical and association data show that MPA and NET do not detectably change peripheral serum GnRH levels, it is possible that relative effects of progestins on GnRH levels measured in the hypophyseal portal vein would have differed from these. Another possible reason for not detecting changes in GnRH levels in our study is the possibility that the progestins modulate GnRH pulse frequency and/or amplitude at the hypothalamic level in such a manner that is not detected when measuring GnRH concentrations at a single post-injection time point. While GnRH pulse frequency and amplitude have been measured in human peripheral blood with frequent sampling [19, 22, 24], a single time point measured in many women would likely still inform on the mean pulse amplitude [24]. Furthermore, there may have been small changes in peripheral serum below the power of our study to detect, although our assay was highly sensitive and the GnRH levels we detected were all above the limits of detection of our GnRH assay. Although, early studies involving measurement of gonadotropin reserves but not endogenous GnRH levels in women support a mechanism of direct effects of progestins on the hypothalamus or above, these findings are difficult to interpret [32, 33, 138]. Several studies in animal models and cell lines suggest that progestogens and androgens can regulate gonadotropin levels at the hypothalamus, by regulating GnRH pulse amplitude and/or frequency, or at the pituitary gonadotropin level, by regulating synthesis and/or release of the β-subunits of LH and FSH [144–147]. Very little mechanistic information is available from studies in women, which may also not mimic effects in animal models [128, 130, 148]. Since both MPA and NET exhibit progestogenic and androgenic activity, an effect on the hypothalamus may be part of their mechanism of action, which is undetected in our study. Taken together, our peripheral blood GnRH data cannot be taken as evidence of a lack of effects by these progestins on the hypothalamus or for supra-hypothalamic effects, although our data would be consistent with such a lack of an effect. Thus, our data do not exclude the possibility that direct effects by MPA and/or NET on the hypothalamus or above contribute to the changes in gonadotropin levels for both contraceptives by changing GnRH levels or pulsatility in the hypophyseal portal vein.

The reason for the similar levels of P4, which is upstream of T in the HPO axis, at 25 W for both contraceptives, but lower levels of T in NET-EN than DMPA-IM users, was not clarified by our association data. This may be due to differential direct effects of the progestins on steroidogenesis in the ovaries. The lower E2 levels in the NET-EN compared to the DMPA-IM arm are consistent with their relative upstream T levels. Our association data between FSH and gonadal steroids are difficult to interpret but suggest that E2 levels are modulated by FSH more than LH in the DMPA-IM arm, while the opposite is indicated for the NET-EN arm. The FSH association data further suggest that T levels are modulated by FSH in opposite directions between contraceptive arms. We detected significant positive versus negative time-varying associations for MPA, compared to NET, respectively, with FSH concentrations. This is consistent with a mechanism whereby the progestins act directly in a dose-dependent manner on the pituitary gonadotrophs, with MPA increasing FSH levels but NET decreasing FSH levels. This mechanism is supported by our in vitro data. The physiological consequences of these differences in the effects of FSH between these contraceptives are unclear. One consequence of increased FSH and decreased LH is a decrease in the LH/FSH ratio, which is significantly lower in DMPA-IM versus NET-EN users.

One of the differences in clinical outcomes for these contraceptive users is significantly more amenorrhea for DMPA-IM than NET-EN [12, 76]. Much observational research has focussed on secondary amenorrhea in patients with PCOS and functional hypothalamic amenorrhea (FHA), with a view to identifying demographic and physiological factors and hormonal levels for diagnosis [149, 150]. Pre-menopausal women with FHA and post-menopausal women both exhibit amenorrhea, are hypoestrogenic and are observed to have multiple increased health risks [151]. These are assumed to be caused by the low E2 levels and include loss of bone density, increased risk of cardiovascular disease and increased rates of depression and anxiety [151–154]. However, to the best of our knowledge the hormonal causal factors and the mechanisms thereof have not been elucidated in women. Given the multiple health risks observed in these women, it is possible that these vary in women depending on type of HC and the degree and duration of amenorrhea. From our data it is clear that the degree of amenorrhea in DMPA-IM compared to NET-EN users is not simply related to lower LH or gonadal steroid levels in serum for DMPA-IM compared to NET-EN users, since these are lower (LH, E2, T) or similar (P4) in NET-EN vs DMPA-IM users, while DMPA-IM users exhibit more amenorrhea than NET-EN users.

In the DMPA-IM arm, increased amenorrhea associated in a time-varying manner with increased concentrations of MPA, and decreased concentrations of LH and gonadal steroids and a decreased LH/FSH ratio, but not with changes in GnRH or FSH concentrations. This would be consistent with a causal relationship between low endogenous gonadal steroids and amenorrhea. In contrast, the NET-EN data show no time-varying associations between amenorrhea and NET concentrations or any of the HPO axis endogenous hormone concentrations or the LH/FSH ratio. This suggests that the increase in amenorrhea in NET-EN users is regulated via a different mechanism to that of DMPA-IM users and is not directly related to the decrease in concentration of any individual hormone measured from D0 to 25 W. An LH/FSH ratio below one has been observed in women with FHA [155]. Interestingly, the LH/FSH ratio for both DMPA-IM and NET-EN users in our study is greater than one at D0, consistent with the follicular phase of naturally cycling women [156], but less than one for both contraceptives at 25 W. However, the DMPA-IM ratio (0.7) is significantly (P = 0.029) lower than the NET-EN ratio (0.8) at 25 W, consistent with the LH/FSH ratio being a reliable indicator for the degree of amenorrhea in progestin-only HC users. One mechanism to account for the increased amenorrhea in DMPA-IM vs NET-EN users could be differences in endogenous gonadal steroid levels in the FGT, as compared to in serum, possibly due to differences in tissue-specific biosynthesis and metabolism. Another possibility is that the differences between these contraceptives is related to the GR activity of DMPA-IM, which is absent for NET-EN. Events in the endometrium, specifically bleeding patterns, are essentially inflammatory responses and are regulated predominantly by progestogens as well as glucocorticoids [157]. Increased cortisol has been shown to decrease bleeding, most likely via the GR, in the endometrium [157]. Further research is required to investigate the mechanisms that regulate the degree of amenorrhea in HC users.

Although our study presents several robust and novel findings, there were some limitations, besides those mentioned previously. Our GnRH, LH and FSH data are limited to single values at D0 and 25 W. We did not investigate the effects of contraceptive use on their amplitude or pulse frequency, which may have revealed some differences within and between contraceptive groups. Furthermore, we do not present data on the levels of activin, inhibin and follistatin. Since these hormones also modulate the HPO axis [158] their levels may have offered additional insights. Although this was attempted, the methods we used were not sufficiently sensitive (data not shown). Measurement of all the analytes at a single post-injection timepoint did not allow us to investigate dynamics, but this was beyond the available funding for the clinical trial. Another limitation was the possible consequences of selection bias and on randomization balance in using subgroups after excluding for participants that misreported non-study progestin use. Excluding contaminated subjects helps maintain the separation between groups, preserving internal validity and the accurate measurement of treatment effects. Yet, this approach also reduces sample size and may introduce selection bias. We did not detect any evidence that randomization was compromised due to subgrouping in our published WHICH trial results [89, 90], nor in the current study. We also did not detect significant differences between contraceptives groups, for any demographic factors at baseline for the subgroup before (data not shown) or after (Supplementary Table S1) (DMPA-IM n = 63, NET-EN, n = 65) exclusion of participants who misreported contraceptive use based on objective verification of progestin levels (Table 1). Moreover, we also did not detect any significant differences at baseline between contraceptive groups for any of the hormone levels or the LH/FSH ratio, for the subgroup after exclusion for misreporting contraceptive use (Table 1). Furthermore, we did not adjust our statistical analysis for multiple comparisons. Given that the choice of all the hormones investigated was based on well-established HPO pathways in the literature, all planned comparisons were specified a priori rather than determined post hoc [159–161]. While false discovery rate analysis may be too stringent to detect biologically relevant changes in small sample sizes [162–164], our results should be interpreted with the appropriate caution. Despite these limitations, our data are still the most robust presented to date. Of note, the results in the mouse LβT2 cell line do not exclude the possibility that other mechanisms may be involved in regulating gonadotropin levels by direct effects of MPA and/or NET on human pituitary gonadotrophs in vivo. There may also be species-specific differences between mouse and human gonadotroph cells, such as in the sequence and regulation of their FSH promoters [165–168] by MPA and NET. Primary gonadotroph cells may also differ from a gonadotroph cell line in mechanisms of gene regulation and relative concentrations of signalling pathway components, such as PR and/or AR protein levels. Although we did detect pr mRNA (data not shown), the absence of detectable PR protein in our LβT2 cells may have excluded responses via the PR that may occur in primary gonadotrophs in women, particularly for NET. Our incubation conditions were limited to non-pulsatile GnRH stimulation, as well as the use of 100 nM progestins. It is possible that different results may have been obtained using other incubation conditions. We did not investigate direct effects of progestins in hypothalamic or ovarian cell line models as suitable cell lines are not available and such studies were also beyond the scope of this report. Nevertheless, this is the first time to our knowledge that clinical effects and in vitro mechanisms of regulation by progestins of the HPO axis have been investigated in parallel and found to be consistent for MPA for at least one key HPO hormone. Prospective studies measuring serial hormones, ovarian imaging and endometrial sampling would further connect hypoestrogenism and changes in the HPO axis to amenorrhea and fertility outcomes.

In summary, our robust findings that DMPA-IM and NET-EN decrease LH and substantially and differentially decrease ovarian steroid levels, more so than previously reported, and differentially modulate FSH levels and the LH/FSH ratio at 25 W, suggest that time of sampling, method of hormone analysis and non-study progestin levels may lead to underestimation of the effects of these contraceptives on HPO hormone levels. The HPO hormone profiles of these contraceptives are not typical of any menstrual cycle phase nor of post-menopausal levels and are cause for concern regarding potential side-effects, especially the very low E2 levels at 25 W. Our clinical and association data are consistent with a pathway whereby MPA and NET do not affect GnRH concentrations in peripheral blood serum, but do not inform on the possibility that hypophyseal portal GnRH levels or pulsatility are affected. This may result in the decrease in LH levels for both contraceptives, which likely result in decreases in gonadal steroid levels. However, the in vitro data suggest that the increase in FSH levels by DMPA-IM is due to direct effects of MPA on pituitary gonadotrophs, mediated by the GR, while the mechanisms of LH regulation by both contraceptives, and FSH by NET-EN, are unclear. Our data do not exclude the possibility that MPA and NET also have direct effects on the ovaries. While all the physiological consequences of the differential regulation of FSH by these contraceptives remain to be investigated, our data are consistent with a lower LH/FSH ratio at 25 W being predictive of more amenorrhea for DMPA-IM than NET-EN. Association data for DMPA-IM are also consistent with an MPA dose-dependent increase in amenorrhea being the result of decreased concentrations of ovarian steroids. However, NET-EN appears to cause amenorrhea less so than DMPA-IM, via a different mechanism, which appears unrelated to endogenous HPO hormone levels, and requires further investigation.
