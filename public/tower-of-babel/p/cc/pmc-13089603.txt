Multiparametric Characterization of Telomere Length in T Cells by Flow‐FISH
Authors: Macedonia Trigueros, Yacine Kharraz, Hammad Alzayat, Julià Blanco, Eugenia Negredo, Marco A Fernandez, Marta Massanella
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTelomere shortening is a hallmark of aging and immune dysfunction, offering a reliable indicator of cellular replicative history and biological age. Measuring telomere length in defined immune subsets provides critical insights into immune aging and disease‐associated remodeling. However, conventional methods often require prior cell sorting or compromise surface marker detection, limiting their ability to integrate telomere length with immunophenotyping. The advent of spectral flow cytometry enables highly multiplexed single‐sample analysis, overcoming many of these technical constraints. Here, we present a streamlined protocol that combines flow cytometry–based fluorescence in situ hybridization (flow‐FISH) with spectral cytometry to measure relative telomere length (RTL) in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). This 18‐color panel simultaneously captures RTL across 15 immune subsets, alongside five functional and phenotypic markers reflecting activation, differentiation, senescence, and exhaustion. Our flow‐FISH method integrates telomere analysis and immune profiling into a single, reproducible workflow. Key optimizations include dual fixation to preserve membrane staining, use of the 1301 cell line as an internal telomere reference, and exclusion of dividing cells via cell cycle staining to ensure accurate RTL quantification. Validation in PBMCs from older adults demonstrated the method's ability to detect expected RTL decreases with T‐cell differentiation and senescence progression, confirming its utility for studies on immune aging, chronic infection, and other age‐related conditions. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Telomeres are nucleoprotein complexes composed of repetitive DNA sequences (TTAGGG in humans) and associated proteins that cap the ends of eukaryotic chromosomes. They play a crucial role in preserving genomic stability and ensuring proper DNA replication (Blackburn, 1991; de Lange, 2005). Telomere length varies across species and chromosomes (Klegarth & Eisenberg, 2018; Karimian et al., 2024; Remot et al., 2022), and in humans, this length is highly heterogeneous, typically ranging from a few to 20 kilobase (kb) pairs, influenced by donor age, tissue type, and cell replication history (Lansdorp, 1996; Martens et al., 1998).

During cell division, cellular DNA is duplicated by the action of DNA polymerase; however, the polymerase does not completely replicate the 3′ ends of chromosomes, known as the telomeric region (O'Sullivan and Karlseder, 2010). Consequently, telomeres get shortened with each division. Accelerated telomere shortening can contribute to cellular instability and replicative senescence (RS) and may lead to cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, or even neoplastic transformation (Victorelli & Passos, 2017). Thus, telomeres are involved in the pathogenesis and clinical progression of a variety of disorders, including congenital diseases, acquired disorders of the lympho‐hematopoietic system, and age‐related conditions (Niewisch et al., 2022; Roka et al., 2023; Rolles et al., 2024).

Given the broad biological roles of telomeres—from aging to cancer development—techniques to measure telomere length are essential, serving as indicators of both a cell's replication history and its remaining proliferative capacity.

Several techniques are available to measure telomere length. The most widely used are Southern blot analysis, PCR methods for measurements of average (Cawthon et al., 2003) and chromosome‐specific telomere length (Baird et al., 2003), fluorescence microscopy with directly labeled (CCCTAA)3 peptide nucleic acid (PNA) probes (Egholm et al., 1993), and flow cytometry–based fluorescence in situ hybridization (flow‐FISH) (Rufer et al., 1998). Although Southern blotting, which measures telomere restriction fragments, remains a widely accepted technique, it has the limitation of assessing telomere length in bulk (Yu et al., 2024). There are notable technical limitations when determining telomere size within specific cell subpopulations. A classical approach, such as separating cells using magnetic cell separation or fluorescence‐activated cell sorting (FACS) followed by Southern blot or quantitative PCR (Gutierrez‐Rodrigues et al., 2014), can be time consuming and expensive. Multicolor flow‐FISH, which combines fluorescence in situ hybridization with antibody‐based immunophenotyping by flow cytometry, enables the simultaneous measurement of telomere length and cell surface markers within the same sample (Roura et al., 2016). This approach eliminates the need for magnetic separation, excludes subtelomeric DNA (de Lange et al., 1990), and provides reproducible results using internal controls (Baerlocher et al., 2002), making it a powerful tool for precise and subset‐specific telomere analysis. However, this technique is constrained by the optical configuration of conventional flow cytometers, and most commonly used fluorochromes (i.e., PE, APC, PerCP, and their tandems) may not withstand the fixation and denaturation steps required for flow‐FISH staining. As a result, the number of populations and phenotypic markers that can be simultaneously analyzed is limited when using conventional flow cytometry.

The advent of spectral flow cytometry has revolutionized this flow‐FISH approach. The capacity to utilize a greater number of fluorochromes, including polymer‐based dyes such as Brilliant Ultraviolet (BUV), Brilliant Violet (BV), Brilliant Blue (BB), Alexa Fluor dyes, and certain cFluors, increases the number of cell surface markers that can be analyzed simultaneously, thereby improving sample characterization.

Here, we present a methodology for flow‐FISH utilizing spectral flow cytometry with an Aurora Cytek cytometer, enabling the simultaneous use of 18 fluorochromes. Our technique allows the measurement of relative telomere length (RTL) across a wide range of T‐cell subsets and can be readily adapted in laboratories equipped with any modern five‐laser spectral flow cytometer with an appropriate optical configuration to detect the selected fluorochromes.

Only fluorochromes resistant to these conditions (e.g., BUV, BV, BB, Alexa Fluor dyes, selected cFluors, FITC) should be included. STAINING AND FLOW CYTOMETRY FOR Flow‐FISH TO MEASURE TELOMERE LENGTH AND IMMUNOPHENOTYPE IN PERIPHERAL BLOOD MONONUCLEAR CELLS (PBMCs)

This protocol describes a comprehensive method for measuring RTL in human T‐cell subsets using flow‐FISH combined with spectral flow cytometry immunophenotyping. The procedure integrates fluorescence in situ hybridization with a telomere‐specific PNA probe and multiparametric flow cytometry on a spectral cytometer, allowing simultaneous assessment of telomere length and detailed immunophenotypic characterization within the same sample. By following this protocol, users will be able to accurately quantify RTL across a wide range of T‐cell populations, including rare subsets, without the need for prior cell sorting. When performed correctly, the protocol yields robust and reproducible measurements of telomere length, enabling the study of telomere dynamics in relation to T‐cell differentiation, activation, senescence, and exhaustion.

COMMENTARY

Background Information

Reliable measurement of telomere length by flow‐FISH combined with spectral flow cytometry depends on careful control of multiple technical variables. Key steps such as fixation, probe hybridization, fluorochrome selection, and the use of appropriate internal controls can significantly influence signal quality and data interpretation. Due to the sensitivity of the method to experimental conditions, optimization and standardization are essential to ensure reproducibility and accurate comparison across samples and experiments.

Critical Parameters

Control cell line

The 1301 cell line is recommended as a control cell line for use with the Telomere PNA Kit/FITC for Flow Cytometry from Dako. These cells have extremely long telomeres (>25 kb) and are near tetraploid and therefore can be easily distinguished from any human sample on the flow cytometer. Other reference controls suitable for standardization of the flow‐FISH assay include calf thymocytes (CTs, a long‐telomere control), Jurkat T cells (a short‐telomere control), and standardized mononuclear cells (MNCs) from a middle‐aged donor (Kapoor et al., 2009).

Use of cell cycle staining instead of viability staining

Given that DNA content increases during S, G2, and M phases, the telomeric PNA probe yields artificially elevated signals in dividing cells. To assess baseline telomere status, only non‐dividing G0/G1 cells are analyzed. Therefore, DNA content staining (FxCycle™) is used instead of viability dyes to accurately exclude proliferating cells.

Suitable fluorochromes

The harsh denaturation and fixation steps limit the choice of fluorochromes. In general, fluorochromes derived from cyanobacterial or algal sources (i.e., PerCP, APC, PE) and tandem‐based dyes are not resistant to the protocol conditions. In contrast, polymer‐based dyes (BUV, BV, BB), Alexa Fluor dyes, some cFluors, and FITC are compatible.

Double fixation

BS3 is applied for initial fixation to protect membrane‐bound markers before denaturation at 82°C. Reagents from the Dako Telomere PNA Kit/FITC for Flow Cytometry (Vials 1 and 2, both containing formamide) are used for a second fixation during denaturation and overnight incubation.

Increasing speed of centrifugation

After the first fixation, the cell pellet density changes, requiring increased centrifugation speed, from 410 × g to 510 × g. Following the second fixation, when pellets become nearly invisible, centrifugation should be further increased to 2500 × g to ensure recovery.

Unstained and single‐stained controls

Single‐staining controls should be run through the complete protocol (including fixation and denaturation; Basic Protocol 1) to obtain accurate spectral signatures post‐treatment. For these, use only the hybridization solution without probe (Vial 1) during the second fixation, except for the telomere single‐stain control, which requires the hybridization solution with probe (Vial 2). Additionally, an unstained control (no antibodies, no probe) should be processed in parallel to capture the background autofluorescence signal.

Sequential staining

Based on previous publications (Fernandez et al., 2022; Jalbert et al., 2013; Park et al., 2024), sequential staining improves detection of sensitive markers. In Basic Protocol 1, CCR7 is stained prior to the addition of the full antibody mix to maximize signal preservation.

Instrument stability

Correct data interpretation heavily relies on instrument stability. Running the quality control daily ensures that any drift of the cytometer is compensated by the automatic adjustment of the instrument settings.

Calculations of relative telomere length

When calculating RTL in small cell populations, exercise caution: low event numbers can lead to unstable MFI values and unreliable ratios. We recommend setting a minimum event threshold (i.e., ≥200 events) to ensure robust RTL estimates.

Understanding Results

To validate the sensitivity and accuracy of our spectral flow‐FISH protocol, we analyzed RTL across T‐cell subsets in 24 PBMC samples from older adults (>60 years). MFI values were extracted, and RTL was calculated relative to the internal 1301 cell line control.

As expected, RTL progressively declined with increasing T‐cell differentiation stage—from naïve to CM, TM, and EM—in both CD4 and CD8 T‐cell populations (Fig. 3A). Interestingly, although this trend generally held true, the TD CD4 T cells exhibited slightly longer RTL than EM cells. This modest increase may reflect the lower proliferative activity of TD cells, which are often senescent or functionally inert, potentially preserving telomere length due to reduced cell division. Similarly, in CD8 T cells, the TEMRA subset displayed RTL comparable to that of EM cells, consistent with their replicative arrest. Together, these results validate the protocol's sensitivity while also highlighting subset‐specific telomere dynamics linked to differentiation and proliferative history.

Telomere length dynamics across T‐cell differentiation stages. (A) Relative telomere length (RTL) in CD4 (left) and CD8 (right) T cells across differentiation from naïve to terminally differentiated (TD) stages, showing a progressive telomere shortening with increasing differentiation. (B) Telomere length comparison in progressively senescent CD4 (left) and CD8 (right) T‐cell subsets stratified by CD57 and KLRG1 co‐expression. (C) Telomere length in naïve CD4 T cells (CD3⁺CD4⁺CD45RA⁺CD27⁺CCR7⁺) comparing recent thymic emigrants (RTEs, CD31⁺) and non‐RTE naïve cells (CD31−). (D) Telomere length in CD8 T cells with a replicative senescence (RS) phenotype (RS⁺; CD57⁺CD28−) compared with non‐senescent cells (RS−). A paired t‐test was used for analyses in (A), whereas the Wilcoxon test was applied in (B‐D).

We next examined RTL across T‐cell populations expressing the canonical senescence markers CD57 and KLRG1. Both CD4 and CD8 T cells showed significant RTL decline with senescence progression, from double‐negative (CD57–KLRG1–, non‐senescent) to single‐positive (KLRG1+, intermediate senescence) to double‐positive (CD57+KLRG1+, advanced senescence) cells. These results highlight the protocol's capacity to capture telomere erosion linked to cellular aging (Fig. 3B).

To assess thymic output and RS, we evaluated RTL in RTEs (CD31+ in naïve CD4 T cells) and non‐RTEs (CD31– in naïve CD4 T cells). RTEs had significantly longer RTL than their non‐RTE counterparts, in line with their lower replication history and more recent thymic origin (Fig. 3C). Additionally, we evaluated RTL in CD8 T cells with or without an RS phenotype. CD8+CD57+CD28– T cells (RS) showed significantly shorter telomeres compared to CD28+ cells, further confirming the assay's robustness in detecting replicative history–related telomere loss (Fig. 3D).

Together, these analyses confirm that our protocol enables sensitive and reproducible assessment of telomere dynamics across differentiation, senescence, and exhaustion states.

Time Considerations

Basic Protocol 1 spans three consecutive days, with hands‐on time distributed as follows:

Day 1: 2 hr for thawing and culturing the 1301 control cell line.

Day 1: 2 hr for thawing and culturing the 1301 control cell line.

Day 2: 4 to 6 hr for membrane and telomere staining (most time intensive).

Day 2: 4 to 6 hr for membrane and telomere staining (most time intensive).

Day 3: 2 hr for cell cycle staining and data acquisition.

Day 3: 2 hr for cell cycle staining and data acquisition.

Total hands‐on time is approximately 8 to 10 hr, depending on the number of samples. For optimal workflow, we recommend planning sample batches and cytometer booking.

The Support Protocol requires ~2 weeks, including thawing of the 1301 cell line, recovery, sequential expansion over multiple passages, and cryopreservation. Hands‐on time is 2 to 3 hr, with the remaining time required for cell growth and monitoring.

For Basic Protocol 2, the time required varies substantially depending on analysis complexity. Initial data processing and basic gating can be completed in 4 hr; however, comprehensive analysis, including detailed subset gating, quality control, statistical analysis, and figure generation, may take several days to weeks. Calculation of RTL can be streamlined using standardized analysis templates, enabling more automated calculations across populations.
