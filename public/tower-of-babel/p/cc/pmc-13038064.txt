Unveiling the developmental and tumor-suppressive roles of the p53 variant p53psi
Authors: Chiara Gorrini, Soode Jafari, Gordon Duncan, Bryan Snow, Liam Hendrikse, Louis McLoughlin, Jillian Haight, Andrew Wakeham, Chantal Tobin, Andrew Elia, Paramesh Ramachandran, Rhoda Law, Bevan Gang, Tsai-Ling Lu, Isabel Morgado-Palacin, Annick You-Ten, Thorsten Berger, Raffaella Sordella, Tak W. Mak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe TP53 gene is known to encode a powerful tumor suppressor, but alternative splicing of this gene also produces multiple isoforms whose biological functions remain poorly understood. Some of these isoforms have even been implicated in cancer progression. This study focuses on p53psi, a truncated p53 isoform generated by alternative splicing, lacking the canonical transcriptional activity of p53. p53psi expression occurs naturally in some normal cells under stress and in certain types of tumors. By generating a mouse model expressing p53psi in lieu of full-length p53, we show that forced p53psi expression leads to embryonic lethality but can also modulate tumorigenesis in the absence of canonical p53 by triggering senescence and influencing the expression of genes linked to developmental pathways.

The TP53 tumor suppressor gene is one of the most frequently mutated genes in human cancers (1). TP53 encodes a regulatory protein (p53) that suppresses tumorigenesis by controlling cell division and DNA repair. Since its discovery in 1979, p53 has been extensively studied, leading to significant breakthroughs in both basic and applied cancer research (2). At the biochemical level, p53 contains a DNA-binding domain that binds to specific promoters of numerous genes, activating their transcription. Genes activated by p53 are primarily involved in apoptosis, DNA repair, cell cycle regulation, senescence, and cell metabolism. The observation that mutated p53 proteins associated with cancer have often lost their transcriptional activities suggests that p53’s role in driving gene expression is critical for tumor suppression (3). However, only ~25% of all p53 mutations affect its DNA-binding domain, with 60% occurring outside this domain predominantly as nonsense or truncating mutations.

The spectrum of TP53 mutations in human tumors is highly diverse and often tissue specific (4). TP53 mutations tend to result in one of three major outcomes: 1) impaired ability of p53 to regulate gene transcription; 2) dominant-negative effects that interfere with wild-type (WT) p53’s functions; or 3) production of novel p53 isoforms with an unknown biological function that may even be tumor-promoting rather than tumor-suppressive. For instance, gain-of-function mutations such R175H, R248Q, and R273H support increased cell invasion, migration, proliferation, and drug resistance in a variety of cancer cell lines (5). Thus, enhancing our understanding of the functions of variant and mutated p53 proteins is essential for optimizing patient stratification and the selection of targeted anticancer treatments.

In this study, we focus on p53psi, a naturally occurring p53 splice variant whose molecular phenotype resembles that of mutated p53 proteins arising from TP53 exon-6 truncation (6). A previous analysis of a pan-cancer dataset comprising nearly 4,000 cases revealed that over 25% of all TP53 mutations are truncating, with exon-6 truncating mutations occurring at a higher than expected frequency (6). Cellular and xenograft studies have suggested that p53 lacking exon-6 reprograms cells toward a metastatic-like phenotype by promoting cell survival and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) (6). Nevertheless, this finding has been controversial because exon-6 truncation results in deletion of p53’s nuclear localization, oligomerization, and part of the DNA binding domains, thereby affecting p53’s canonical transcriptional activity.

To better understand the biological function of p53psi and to determine if it has effects like those of p53 exon-6 truncating mutations, we generated a knock-in mouse model in which the p53psi protein is expressed in place of the full-length (FL) WT p53 protein. In the C57/BL6J background, homozygous p53psi knock-in (p53KI/KI) mice are embryonically lethal. To study p53psi’s biological functions, we used mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) derived from these embryos to find that p53psi retains at least some tumor-suppressive functions. These functions appear to be executed by triggering alternative transcriptional programs that regulate senescence and differentiation.

Results

The p53psi Homozygous Knock-In Mouse Is Embryonically Lethal.

To investigate the role of p53psi in mouse development and tumorigenesis, we generated a conditional knock-in transgenic model in which only the p53psi variant protein was expressed. Briefly, a loxP-flanked minigene containing both intron 6 splice acceptors and exons 7 to 11 of the mTrp53 cDNA was inserted into the endogenous mTrp53 gene locus, preserving its promoter, intron/exon structure, and 3’UTR (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, the canonical intron 6 splice acceptor downstream of the minigene cassette was mutated, leaving only the alternative mp53psi splice acceptor intact. To generate p53psi heterozygous knock-in mice (p53psiKI/+), the knock-in targeting vector was electroporated into 129J Ola embryonic stem cells. These engineered stem cells were microinjected into E3.5 C57BL/6J blastocysts that were then transferred into pseudopregnant foster mothers. Germline transmission and expression of the p53psi allele were confirmed through tail DNA analysis following the crossing of p53psiKI/+ mice with the Flp-deleter strain (Fig. 1B).

After confirming targeted integration, p53psiKI/+ mice were crossed with the Cre-deleter mouse line to achieve ubiquitous expression of the p53psi variant while simultaneously deleting the FL WT Trp53 gene (7). The resulting mouse strain lacked the mTrp53 gene (p53null) and exclusively expressed the p53psi isoform. Remarkably, intercrossing p53psiKI/+ mice to generate homozygous p53psiKI/KI mice on the C57BL/6J background led to embryonic lethality of the p53psiKI/KI genotype after nine generations (Fig. 1C). To investigate the cause of this lethality, we analyzed embryos from p53psiKI/+ x p53psiKI/+ mating between gestational day E14.5 and birth. The number of p53psiKI/KI embryos was consistently below the expected percentage during the perinatal stage (Fig. 1C). Moreover, a significant percentage of p53psiKI/KI female embryos exhibited central nervous system abnormalities, being exencephaly the most common defect (Fig. 1 D and E). Interestingly, while most of p53null mice develop normally, a small subset, predominantly females, displayed developmental defects related to neural tube formation (5). These findings suggest that, compared to p53null animals, p53psiKI/KI mice exhibit a similar but more pronounced embryonic phenotype. Taken together, these results demonstrate that in the p53psiKI/KI mouse model, forced p53psi expression in the absence of FL WT Trp53 gene is detrimental and leads to embryonic lethality.

p53psi Protein Levels Are Regulated By Stress and Epithelial-To-Mesenchymal Transition.

Given the embryonic lethality of p53psiKI/KI mice, we used MEFs isolated from p53psiKI/KI embryos at the E14.5 stage to conduct mechanistic studies of p53psi’s function. MEFs from WT and p53null animals were included as positive and negative controls, respectively. RNA expression analysis revealed significantly higher expression of the p53psi gene in primary MEFs derived from p53psiKI/KI embryos as compared to WT primary MEFs, with expression levels almost undetectable in p53null primary MEFs (Fig. 2A). Western blot analysis of protein extracts confirmed the presence of p53psi specifically in p53psiKI/KI MEFs, with the protein migrating at a molecular weight that was lower than FL WT p53 and consistent with its truncated structure (Fig. 2B). No detectable p53psi protein was observed in WT MEF extracts, suggesting that, in WT cells, p53psi expression is below the sensitivity threshold of Western blotting, and that FL WT p53 translation is favored over that of p53psi (Fig. 2B). Measurement of cell growth rates over five days revealed small but significant differences between WT and p53null or p53psiKI/KI MEFs on day 5 (Fig. 2C).

Senturk and colleagues have previously demonstrated that the generation of the p53psi transcript from the Trp53 gene locus is associated with strong cellular stress, such as that exerted by DNA damage or treatment with toxic chemicals (8). We exposed our WT and p53psiKI/KI primary MEFs to various cellular stimuli, including genotoxic stress agents and growth factors. These treatments included gamma-irradiation (IR, 10 Gy), cisplatin (COS, 10 µM), D-sorbitol (D-SOR, 300 nM), TGF-beta (TGFb, 10 ng/mL), insulin (INS, 1 mM), and fetal bovine serum (FBS 20%, after incubation overnight in 0.5% FBS-containing medium). Western blot analysis of p53psi protein levels revealed that treatment of p53psiKI/KI (but not WT) MEFs with IR or TGFb led to the greatest increases in p53psi protein (Fig. 2 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S1).

Analysis of p53psi transcripts in irradiated and TGFb-treated cells showed that the increase in protein level was due to a similar increase in mRNA expression (Fig. 2 F and G). However, in irradiated cells, p53psi expression failed to trigger p21 expression as seen in WT MEFs, confirming the lack of canonical transcriptional p53 activity (Fig. 2H). This ability of TGFb to upregulate p53psi protein aligns with prior observations linking p53psi expression to the EMT, a key pathway influenced by TGFb stimulation (8, 9). Therefore, we investigated if p53psi expression influenced the levels of EMT-related genes. We found that TGFb significantly increased the expression of Snail 2 and Twist1 in all genotypes. Differently from p53null, p53 WT, and p53psiKI/KI MEF had high expression of all the EMT regulators analyzed (Fig. 2I). TGFb-treated p53psiKI/KI had higher expression of Zeb1, Zeb2, and Snail1 than p53null MEF.

Overall, our data in MEF show that p53psi transcript is produced by genotoxic stress (i.e. gamma-irradiation) or EMT. A p53-regulated transcriptional program has been shown to oppose EMT, and consequently, p53-null tumors present an EMT-like phenotype (10). While p53psi does not retain canonical p53 activity in irradiated cells, unlike p53, it promotes EMT, further supporting the functional divergence of this variant (6).

The p53psi Variant Drives Senescence.

DNA binding and transcriptional activities are widely considered essential for p53 tumor-suppressive roles (11). Using a mouse model expressing a p53 mutant deficient in p53 transcriptional activation, Jiang and colleagues have demonstrated that p53 can still exert tumor-suppressive functions in certain tissue types. These findings suggest that full transactivation capacity is not essential for p53 to trigger a tumor suppressor response. Instead, through the transcription of specific subsets of genes, p53 mutants may retain significant tumor-suppressive activity (12). Based on these observations, we sought to evaluate if p53psi protein retained tumor-suppressive functions. To address this question, we transformed primary MEFs from WT, p53null, and p53psiKI/KI embryos with an adenovirus coexpressing the oncogenes E1A and RASV12, an oncogenic mutant form of RAS. E1A and RASV12 are known to cooperate in transforming MEFs from a primary state to a malignant one, ultimately driving tumor development (13). In these transformed cell lineages, we assessed the basal levels of p53psi mRNA. Interestingly, E1A/RASV12 oncogenes greatly increased the expression of p53psi in WT MEFs and to a lesser extent in p53psiKI/KI (Fig. 3A), suggesting that malignant transformation positively regulated p53 alternative splicing and p53psi variant production (Fig. 3A). p53psi expression was undetectable in p53null MEFs (data not shown). In the same settings, the expression of the known p53 target p21/Cdkn1a was only induced in WT E1A/RASV12 MEFs (Fig. 3B), consistent with the observation that E1A/RASV12 expression renders MEFs susceptible to p53-dependent cell cycle arrest (14). p21/Cdkn1a induction was abolished in both p53null and p53psiKI/KI E1A/RASV12 MEFs (Fig. 3B), further confirming the transcriptional deficiency of the p53psi variant. Then, we analyzed three additional p53 targets, Puma/Bbc3, Noxa, and p16/Cdkn2a which are involved in p53-mediated tumor suppression (14, 15). Puma/Bbc3 mRNA significantly increased in WT and p53psiKI/KI E1A/RASV12 MEFs while only modestly elevated in p53null E1A/RASV12 MEFs (Fig. 3C). Noxa expression was high in all three genotypes but to a higher extent in p53psiKI/KI samples (Fig. 3D). p16/Cdkn2a expression, commonly associated with senescence, was very low in both WT or p53null E1A/RASV12 (Fig. 3E). Interestingly, p53psiKI/KI E1A/RASV12 MEFs showed a significant increase in p16/Cdkn2a expression, suggesting a potential induction of senescence in these cells.

To complement our gene expression analyses with cellular phenotypes, we assessed apoptosis, cell cycle progression, and DNA damage in transformed MEFs by flow cytometry-based assays. We performed these analyses in cells untreated or subjected to gamma-irradiation (10 Gy) to further evaluate the ability of the p53psi variant to maintain canonical p53 responses. At 24 h postirradiation, phosphorylation of histone H3 (P-H3), a marker of cell cycle progression, consistently decreased in all genotypes (Fig. 3F) (16). In both untreated and treated conditions, p53null and p53psiKI/KI MEFs maintained higher levels of P-H3 compared to WT MEFs (Fig. 3F). In the same samples, we quantified radiation-induced DNA Damage by evaluating histone H2AX phosphorylation (P-H2AX). As expected, irradiation induced a great increase of P-H2AX in both WT and p53null MEFs (Fig. 3 G and H). Strikingly, in untreated p53psiKI/KI MEFs, P-H2AX levels resembled the levels seen in irradiated cells, suggesting that chronic and unrepaired DNA Damage is present in p53psiKI/KI MEFs (Fig. 3 G and H). Moreover, consistently with undetectable p21/Cdkn1a mRNA levels observed in basal conditions (Fig. 3B), p53psiKI/KI MEFs did not accumulate p21/Cdkn1a mRNA upon irradiation when compared to WT MEFs (Fig. 3I). Similarly, both untreated and irradiated p53psiKI/KI MEFs had high levels of p16 mRNA, indicative of senescence (Fig. 3J). To corroborate this evidence, we measured the presence of senescent cells after 72 h postirradiation by a fluorescent-based detection of beta-galactosidase (b-gal) enzymatic activity, commonly present in senescent cells. Irradiation caused accumulation of senescent cells in WT MEFs samples (Fig. 3K). Although at a lesser extent, both p53null and p53psiKI/KI MEFs presented a small but significant increase in b-gal enzymatic activity, with p53psiKI/KI MEFs showing approximately twice as many positive cells as p53null samples (Fig. 3K) (17). Collectively, our data suggest that the p53psi variant lacks canonical p53 transcriptional activity but maintains the ability to drive senescence.

The p53psi Protein Retains Tumor-Suppressive Activities.

We sought to investigate if p53psiKI/KI MEFs may retain some tumor-suppressive activities by inducing senescence. To explore this hypothesis, we subcutaneously injected WT, p53null, or p53psiKI/KI E1A/RASV12 MEFs into the flanks of nude male mice and monitored tumor growth weekly until the humane endpoint was reached (1 cm3 tumor volume). E1A/RASV12-transformed MEFs lacking p53 expression grew rapidly in nude mice, reaching the humane endpoint by day 21 postinjection (Fig. 4 A–C). As previously demonstrated, tumor growth was less efficient in recipients bearing transformed WT MEFs, reflecting the ability of FL WT p53 to exert its full tumor-suppressive functions (15). Consistent with our cell culture observations, transformed MEFs derived from p53psiKI/KI animals formed tumors at a significantly slower rate compared to p53null E1A/RASV12 MEFs (Fig. 4 A–C). Together, our cellular and animal model experiments suggest that the p53psi protein retains some tumor-suppressive functions despite lacking p53 canonical transcriptional ability.

To explore the mechanisms underlying the ability of transformed p53psiKI/KI MEFs to suppress tumor growth, we measured cell proliferation and apoptosis in tumor tissues harvested from the above nude mice at the study endpoint. Measurement of Ki67 levels indicated that cell proliferation rates were comparable in p53psiKI/KI-derived tumors compared to their WT and p53null counterparts (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Because apoptosis is known to limit tumor growth in the E1A/RASV12 MEF model (18), we stained tumor tissues to detect cleaved caspase 3 (CC3) and again observed equal percentages of CC3-positive cell areas among the three genotypes (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). After ruling out effects on proliferation and apoptosis, and based on our cellular data, we stained tumor tissues for p16/Cdkn2to monitor the presence of senescent cells. Tumors from WT and p53psiKI/KI E1A/RASV12 MEFs contained a similar number of p16-positive cells when compared to tumors formed by p53null E1A/RASV12 MEFs (Fig. 4 D and E). These data suggest that reduced growth of p53psi-expressing tumors is due to senescence as shown in our previous cell-based assays.

In the absence of p53 expression, the transcription factor E2F1 triggers apoptosis by inducing the proapoptotic genes Tap73 and Noxa (19–21). Although our tissue analysis of cleaved caspase 3 did not show any differences in overall apoptosis levels between p53null and p53psiKI/KI tumors, we hypothesized that a more detailed examination of E2F1-regulated apoptotic genes might uncover some variations. First, we sought to confirm that p53psi mRNA levels in p53psiKI/KI tumors were clearly distinguishable from the other genotypes as in E1A/RASV12 MEFs. RT-PCR analysis revealed that p53psi mRNA levels were indeed present at relevant levels in WT and p53psi-expressing tumors, comparable to the ones seen in E1A/RASV12 MEFs (Fig. 4F). We then measured mRNA levels of Tap73 and Noxa in RNA extracted from all tumors and found that both genes were expressed at significantly higher levels in p53psiKI/KI tumors compared to their WT and p53null counterparts (Fig. 4 G and H). Interestingly, the extent of Tap73 and Noxa mRNA upregulation correlated with the levels of p53psi expression within the cohort. These findings support our hypothesis that p53psi can maintain tumor suppression functions by driving senescence and activating an E2F1-regulated apoptotic pathway.

p53psi-Mediated Tumor Suppression Is Linked to Developmental Pathways and Rb Mutations.

To delve deeper into the signaling pathways driving p53psi-mediated tumor suppression, we performed total RNA sequencing and cluster analysis of p53null and p53psiKI/KI E1A/RASV12 MEF-derived tumors, using biological triplicates for each condition (22). Given the variability of p53psi expression across tumors, stringent unsupervised clustering analysis identified one significantly upregulated gene and eight significantly downregulated genes in p53psi-associated tumors (Fig. 5A). The one upregulated gene was Spondin1 (SPON1), which encodes an extracellular matrix protein that is involved in cell adhesion and collagen remodeling, and may contribute to cancer cell invasion and metastasis (23). This finding aligns with prior and our current observations that exogenous p53psi expression in NSCLC correlates with the EMT and enhanced motility (8).

Genes that were downregulated in p53psiKI/KI E1A/RASV12 MEF-derived tumors included the following: 1) “Immediate early response 3” (IER3), which is associated with the Mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/Extracellular regulated protein kinases (ERK) pathway that is essential for cell proliferation and immune responses, and is often prominent in cancers (24, 25); 2) “Dual Specificity Phosphatase 2” (DUSP2), which is a known transcriptional target of WT FL p53 (26). This result suggests that p53psi cannot compensate for an absence of WT FL p53 to induce DUSP2 expression; 3) Pro-adrenomedullin, which encodes a peptide hormone involved in angiogenesis, cell proliferation, and development (27, 28). The corresponding mouse gene, Calcrl, is critical for embryonic development since Calcrl-/- embryos are embryonic lethal at E13.5-14.5; this phenotype mirrors the embryonic lethality of our p53psiKi/KI mice; 4) HODX11, which is a homeobox protein (29) and its downregulation supports a mechanistic link between p53psi and developmental pathways. Furthermore, HOXD11 dysregulation has been implicated in various cancers, including leukemia, head-and-neck squamous cell carcinoma, and ovarian cancer (30–32); 5) JunB, a transcription factor with crucial functions both in normal development and cancer progression (33, 34); 6) SLC2A3, which encodes a glucose transporter critical for glycolysis. SLC2A3 is often upregulated in multiple tumor types to meet their high energy demands, and its expression correlates with tumor progression and poor cancer patient survival (35). Overall, the expression of the p53psi variant correlated with downregulation of several genes promoting or supporting tumorigenesis, further confirming the tumor-suppressive function of this mutant. Furthermore, an enrichment analysis of the downregulated genes identified significant associations with transcription factors involved in cell development, stemness, and differentiation, such as RELB, KLF2/4/5, p63, OCT4, and GATA2 (Fig. 5B). These data bolster our conclusion that, in the absence of functional FL p53, p53psi can alter the expression patterns of genes that mechanistically link developmental programs to senescence and tumor suppression. To corroborate our mouse tumor analysis, we investigated the presence of p53 truncating mutations, including exon 6-specific mutations leading to the generation of p53psi variant (6). Analysis of pan-cancer cohorts in the human TCGA dataset revealed that Tp53 truncating mutations account for approximately 27% of all Tp53 mutations (Fig. 5C), with exon 6-specific mutations comprising about 18% of these alterations (Fig. 5D). Most truncating mutations clustered in the DNA-binding domain, including exon 6-specific R213* mutation that for instance was specifically enriched in melanoma samples compared to normal melanocytes (Fig. 5E and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Interestingly, in the TCGA dataset, this specific mutation inversely correlated with fusion mutation in the RB1 gene, a highly mutated tumor suppressor gene regulating cell cycle, cell differentiation, and E2F1-regulated signaling (Fig. 5F).

Discussion

Our study provides compelling evidence for the biological significance of a naturally occurring truncated form of p53 called p53psi. Lacking FL p53’s nuclear localization, oligomerization, and part of the DNA binding domains, p53psi is unable to directly regulate the transcription of some of p53 canonical genes. While previous studies have characterized p53psi mainly as a gain-of-function mutant, our genetic model establishes that p53psi instead retains p53 tumor suppression capacity by activating alternative pathways. Transformed MEFs expressing p53psi can initiate apoptosis through upregulation of the E2F1 transcriptional targets Tap73 and Noxa. The compensatory role of E2F1 in triggering apoptosis in the absence of p53 has been well documented (21). In addition, p53psi expression drives senescence, correlates with accumulation of unrepaired DNA Damage and expression of the proapoptotic gene Puma/Bbc3, a critical driver of p53-mediated tumor suppression (15). In MEF-derived tumor tissues, p53psi expression in the absence of FL p53 leads to the downregulation of transcriptional programs associated with cell differentiation and development, including those involving the HOX gene family and JunB. Precisely how p53psi triggers these changes remains under investigation. Numerous studies have highlighted a connection between key pathways of embryonic development and cancer. Dysregulation of developmentally regulated genes has been implicated in critical aspects of tumorigenesis, including cell proliferation, apoptosis, and EMT. The disruption of these pathways ultimately contributes to the uncontrolled proliferation and aberrant differentiation of cancer-initiating cells (36).

Our findings bolster previous research by Senturk and colleagues (8) and underscore the dual nature of p53psi as a regulator of diverse biological pathways that displays both tumor-suppressive (i.e. senescence) and oncogenic properties (i.e. EMT). In our genetic model, the interplay of these opposing functions delayed tumor growth. It is thus possible that, in human cancers, the exon-6 truncating mutations believed to generate p53psi must co-occur with potent drivers of cancer progression. Strikingly, analyses of human TCGA datasets reveal an inverse correlation between Tp53 truncating mutations and inactivating alterations in the tumor suppressor RB1, a key regulator of E2F1 signaling, as well as cell differentiation and developmental pathways. P53psi-regulated p16 expression also aligns with this finding as p16 has been previously found to be a negative regulator of the RB signaling pathway (37). Future investigations should focus on identifying these co-occurring alterations and on dissecting their mechanistic links to specific p53 mutations. Such an approach would enhance our understanding of p53-mediated tumorigenesis and could guide the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.

Materials and Methods

Generation and Characterization of p53psi Knock-In Mice.

Please refer to the SI Appendix, Materials and Methods for a detailed description of construction of mTrp53-psi knock-in targeting vector, targeted knock-in of mTrp53psi into mouse ES cells and generation of mTrp53psi (p53psi) knock-in mice.

MEF Isolation, Maintenance, Treatments, and Transformation.

Primary MEFs were derived from E14-16 embryos of three genotypes on the C57Bl/6J background: WT, p53-deficient (p53null), and p53psi homozygous knock-in (p53psiKI/KI). Briefly, heads and internal organs of age-matched mice were removed, and the remaining tissue was finely minced, thoroughly washed in PBS1X, and digested with 0.1% collagenase A (Sigma). The resulting small tissue fragments were washed again and cultured in complete DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin/streptomycin. Primary MEFs generated from these cultures were utilized up to passage 10.

For transformed MEFs, litter-matched WT, p53null, and p53psiKI/KI primary MEFs were transformed using E1A/RasV12 viral particles as previously described (19). Briefly, Ecotropic PlatE cells were seeded in a 10-cm dish in DMEM containing antibiotics-free 10% FCS and cultured overnight. PlatE cells were transfected with pBABE-Puro Ha-rasV12 plus Wzl-Neo E1A 12S or the corresponding empty retroviral vectors (38). Transfection was performed by the calcium phosphate method (39). At 72 hr posttransfection, retroviral supernatants were collected and added to 1 x 105 MEFs in 6-well plates, with polybrene added to a final concentration of 6 μg/mL. Plates were then spin-infected at 3,000xg for at least 30 min. Infected cells were incubated at 37 °C overnight, the medium was replaced the next day, and 1 µg/mL of puromycin was added to each plate to select infected cells. Transformed MEFs between passages 3 and 5 were employed in experiments.

Tumor Growth.

To assess tumor-forming capacity, transformed MEFs (1 × 106) were subcutaneously injected into the flanks of athymic nude mice (Jacksons Lab). Tumor progression was monitored by measuring each tumor’s dimensions twice weekly using digital calipers. When the humane endpoint was reached (tumor size of 1 cm3), tumor tissues were collected and processed for subsequent analyses.

Histological Staining and Analyses of Tumors (Ki67 and Cleaved Caspase 3).

To assess tumor pathology and perform immunostaining, tumors were excised and fixed in buffered formalin at 4 °C for 24 h. Fixed tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 4 µm thickness for immunohistochemistry (IHC). IHC to detect Ki67 (ab15580, Abcam) or cleaved caspase 3 (#9661, Cell Signaling) was carried out following standard procedures. Briefly, tissue sections (4 µm) were melted, dewaxed, and rehydrated to water, followed by killing of endogenous peroxidases and antigen retrieval by the sodium citrate (pH 6.0) microwave method. Sections were blocked using SP5030 (Vector Labs) in 0.2% Triton X-100 and incubated with primary antibodies indicated above for 45 min at room temperature (RT). Secondary antibody (horse x rabbit MP-7401, Vector Labs) was applied for 35 min followed by development using DAB (Vector Labs) for 4 min. Slides were counterstained with CAT hematoxylin (BioCare), washed in running water, and cover-slipped using EcoMount (BioCare). After drying, slides were digitally scanned using a Hamamatsu Nanozoomer HT2.0 scanner.

Quantitation of IHC staining was performed using QuPath (40). Briefly, a pixel classifier (Channel: hematoxylin, threshold: 0.01) was used to generate an annotation of each tissue, which was then manually curated to account for missing areas of tissue. A further pixel classifier was applied using the DAB channel to determine the positively stained area within each tissue annotation (positive thresholds were 0.15 and 0.2 for cleaved caspase 3 and Ki67, respectively). Total area and protein expression positive area (µm2) were then exported for each tissue and used to calculate the percentage positive area per tissue.

Histological Staining and Analysis of Tumors (p16).

5 µm sections were taken onto Superfrost Plus Adhesion Microscope Slides (Epredia) from FFPE tissue blocks. Sections were dewaxed and rehydrated in neoclear (cat 1.09843, Sigma-Aldrich) followed by gradient ethanol solutions. Antigen retrieval was achieved through the citric acid (pH 6) microwave method, before blocking with peroxidase blocking solution and protein blocking solution for 10 min each at room temperature (AB64261, Abcam). Sections were then incubated in a primary antibody targeting p16INK4a (# MA5-17054, ThermoFisher Scientific) at 4 °C overnight. Biotinylated secondary antibody was applied for 10 min followed by a 10 min incubation with streptavidin peroxidase, followed by development using DAB for 1.5 min (ab64261/ab64259, Abcam). Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin (Ab220365, Abcam) and scots tapwater (HS-404, National Diagnostics) before dehydration in ethanol and neoclear. Finally, coverslips were mounted using depex (13514, Electron Microscope Sciences), left to dry overnight and scanned using the Axioscan Z1 Slidescanner (Zeiss). Quantitation of IHC staining was performed using QuPath (40) accordingly to the histoscore method (41). Briefly, staining intensity was visually assessed to determine pixel intensity thresholds for weak, moderate, and strong staining. Percentage coverage of the tissue was then quantified for each staining intensity. Histoscores were then generated per tissue using the equation: (% weak staining × 1) + (% moderate staining × 2) + (% strong staining × 3). This gave a score between 0-300.

Cell treatments.

Primary MEFs (1 × 105) were seeded in 6-well plates in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific), Penicillin-Streptomycin 10,000 µ/mL (Thermo Fisher Scientific), L-glutamine 200 mM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) + 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing either D-sorbitol (Sigma, 300 nM), TGFb (Proteintech, 10 µg/mL), insulin (1 mM), or cisplatin (Sigma, 10 µM). In the case of FBS treatment, cells were serum-starved overnight in medium+0.5% FBS prior to incubation in medium+20% FBS. In the case of gamma-irradiation, cultures were exposed to 10 Gy. Treated cultures were incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 24 hr before preparation of cell lysates. Protein extracts were subjected to Western blot analysis as described below.

Cell Growth and Viability.

MEFs (1 × 104) were seeded in 6-well plates in triplicate for each genotype in standard culture medium. Over 5 d, cells were counted daily using the trypan blue exclusion method and an automatic cell counter (BECKMAN Coulter Vi-CELL XR Cell Viability Analyzer).

Flow cytometry.

MEFs (15 × 103 cells) were seeded in 6-well plates in triplicate for each genotype in standard culture medium. Cells were exposed to 10 Gy gamma-irradiation (IR), and both irradiated and untreated control cells were analyzed. Twenty-four hours postirradiation, cells were harvested, fixed, and permeabilized with BD Cytofix/Cytoperm™ Fixation/Permeabilization Kit (BD Biosciences, Cat. #554714), then stained with phospho-Histone H3, PE conjugate, (P-H3, 1:50, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. #5764S) and phospho-H2AX, FITC conjugate, (P-H2AX, 1:100, Millipore/Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. #16-202A) for an hour at room temperature, wash with BD Perm/Wash™ Buffer and then analyzed by flow cytometry. Additionally, irradiated cells were assessed for cellular senescence three days posttreatment using the CellEvent™ Senescence Green Flow Cytometry Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Catalog #C10840) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Western Blotting.

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 50 mM Tris, pH 8.0) on ice for 15 min with occasional vortexing followed by centrifugation at 10,000xg for 15 min. The protein content of supernatants was measured by BCA assay (Pierce), and 30 µg protein/sample was subjected to reducing 4–12% SDS/PAGE. Proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, which were incubated overnight with primary antibodies recognizing FL p53 (FL393, Thermo Fisher) or β-actin (Abcam). Primary antibodies were detected using an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody to rabbit IgG (GE Healthcare). Protein signals were visualized using enhanced luminol-based chemiluminescence (GE Healthcare).

RNA Extraction and Analysis by RT-PCR.

Cells were lysed in RNA lysis buffer directly in the 6-well plate PureLink™ RNA Mini Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher). Total RNA (0.5 mg) was converted to cDNA using the High-Capacity RNA-to-cDNA™ Kit as per the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher). RT-qPCR was performed using Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, California). Statistical Analyses.

Unless otherwise indicated, results are reported as the mean ± SD. The two-sided independent Student t test without equal variance assumption and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare group means were used to determine P values (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001).

RNA Sequencing and Analysis of Mouse Tumors.

Total RNA was extracted from mouse tumors as described above and resuspended in DNase- and RNase-free water (1mg). mRNA sequencing and preliminary bioinformatics analyses were performed by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The raw 75-bp paired-end reads from the sequencer were first quality-checked using FASTQC v0.11.5 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) and MultiQC v0.8 software packages (42), and then aligned to the Mus musculus genome assembly version GRCm38 (mm10) from the Genome Reference Consortium using the STAR aligner v2.5.2b (43). The aligned transcripts were quantified using RSEM v1.3.0 (44). The RSEM quantification output files were further processed using in-house R scripts to create a gene-by-sample expression matrix. This matrix of raw RSEM expected counts was input into the DESeq2 R package v1.36.0 for differential expression analysis (45). Within DESeq2, the default procedure recommended by the package vignette was generally followed. The base Mean (mean of normalized counts across samples), log2FoldChange, and the adjusted p-values obtained using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure were used to arrive at the set of top differentially expressed genes (46). Volcano plots and PCA plots were generated using the Enhanced Volcano and ggplot2 R packages, respectively.

Human Dataset Analysis.

Data source and patient cohort.

Genomic alteration data were obtained from the MSK-CHORD pan-cancer study (47) through the cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics platform (https://www.cbioportal.org/). The cohort comprised 25,040 tumor samples across multiple cancer types. Tp53 and RB1 alterations were identified in 1,487 samples (6% of the total cohort).

Mutation analysis.

TP53 mutation data were extracted and categorized by mutation type (missense, truncating, in-frame, fusion, and splicing mutations) and genomic location. Specific hotspot mutations (R213*, R280*, G245*, E204*, E138*) were identified and analyzed for their distribution across the cohort. Truncating mutations were defined as nonsense, frameshift, and splice site variants predicted to result in loss of protein function.

Gene co-occurrence and mutual exclusivity analysis.

Co-occurrence and mutual exclusivity patterns between Tp53 hotspot mutations and RB1 alterations were assessed using cBioPortal’s statistical tools.

Exon 6 mutation analysis.

The distribution of Tp53 mutations across the protein coding sequence was mapped to individual exons. The frequency of exon 6 mutations was calculated as a proportion of total truncating TP53 mutations and stratified by cancer type. Cancer types analyzed included head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, lung squamous cell carcinoma, lung small cell carcinoma, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, and lung adenocarcinoma.

Data visualization.

OncoPrint visualizations were generated using cBioPortal’s OncoPrint tool to display genomic alterations across samples. Samples were ordered by alteration status to visualize co-occurrence and mutual exclusivity patterns. Mutation distribution analyses were visualized using pie charts and bar graphs. Color coding for alterations was as follows: dark gray = missense mutation, light blue = truncating mutation, purple = in-frame mutation, red = amplification, light green = deep deletion, orange = multiple alterations.

Statistical analysis.

Mutation frequencies were calculated as percentages of the total cohort or relevant subgroups. Cancer type-specific enrichment of exon 6 truncating mutations was determined by comparing the proportion of exon 6 mutations across different tumor types.
