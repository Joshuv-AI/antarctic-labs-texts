High throughput screen reveals four compounds with novel antifungal activity, including a metal-responsive candidate with in vivo efficacy against Candida albicans
Authors: Jana Nysten, Lara Van Campenhout, Eliane Vanhoffelen, Greetje Vande Velde, Patrick Van Dijck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fungal infections represent a growing global health concern, aggravated by the paucity of effective antifungal therapies and the rapid rise of drug resistance. The development of new treatments is challenging due to the shared eukaryotic biology of fungi and human hosts, which restricts selective molecular targets and contributes to issues such as host toxicity and fungistatic activity. These challenges underscore the pressing need for new agents with distinct modes of action. In this study, we identified four promising compounds with novel antifungal activity from a high-throughput screen of 20,000 compounds against the opportunistic fungal pathogen, Candida albicans, namely Z1199266541, Z1024453766, Z56842335, and Z126932704. Subsequently, the compounds were characterized in vitro to assess their spectrum of activity, efficacy against a fluconazole-resistant isolate (CaCi-17), and effectiveness in combination treatments, followed by in vivo evaluation in a Galleria mellonella infection model. Among the candidates, Z56842335 emerged as a lead compound, effectively clearing infection caused by the Candida albicans in this invertebrate model. Mechanistic analyses revealed that the antifungal activity of Z56842335 is mitigated by supplementation with iron, copper, and zinc, indicating a metal-dependent mode of action. Notably, despite its apparent metal-binding capacity, Z56842335 displays a narrow spectrum of activity, targeting only C. albicans and, to a lesser extent C. dubliniensis. This selectivity suggests that, in addition to metal availability, species-specific factors such as differences in metal acquisition, intracellular metal buffering, or compound uptake or accumulation may contribute to susceptibility, highlighting a promising yet mechanistically intriguing avenue for further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48741-7.

Introduction

Fungal infections have become more prevalent over recent decades and now represent a serious global health threat1,2. An estimated 1.7 billion people are affected every year. Although the majority of these infections are superficial (e.g., on nails and skin), fungi can also cause severe, life-threatening systemic infections with high mortality rates3,4. Recent estimates suggest that mycoses result in approximately 3.8 million attributable deaths annually, with Candida species accounting for nearly one-third of these fatalities5. Candida albicans is a leading cause of nosocomial infections among Candida species. This opportunistic pathogen commonly colonizes the gastrointestinal tract, skin, mouth, and genitourinary tract of over 70% of the global population6. While C. albicans typically exists as a harmless commensal in its human host, it can transition to a pathogenic state under certain conditions, leading to diseases ranging from mucosal infections to life-threatening invasive candidiasis7. These invasive infections have a mortality rate of approximately 63.6%, prompting the World Health Organization to recently classify C. albicans as a pathogen of critical concern to public health that requires immediate attention5,8,9. Importantly, in addition to C. albicans, non-albicans Candida species, such as Candida glabrata (recently renamed as Nakaseomyces glabratus) and Candida auris (recently renamed as Candidozyma auris), are becoming increasingly prevalent. These species often display intrinsic or acquired resistance to commonly used antifungal drugs, thereby further complicating treatment10–12.

Despite their impact, Fungal pathogens have long been understudied, underestimated, and neglected because they were considered relatively rare, and most attention was given to bacterial and viral pathogens13,14. Consequently, only three major antifungal drug classes are available to treat invasive mycoses. Drug development is hindered by the close evolutionary relationship between fungi and humans, which limits the number of selective molecular targets and contributes to challenges such as host toxicity, fungistatic activity, and escalating antifungal resistance15–17. These challenges underscore the pressing need for new antifungal agents with distinct modes of action18.

To address this gap, a library of 20,000 compounds was screened to identify new compounds with anti-Candida albicans activity. Here, we report the discovery and characterization of four structurally distinct novel compounds with potent antifungal activity, namely Z1199266541 (Z41), Z1024453766 (Z66), Z56842335 (Z35), and Z126932704 (Z04). Hereafter, these compounds are referred to as Z41, Z66, Z35, and Z04 throughout the text and figures.

Compound Z41 displayed activity against C. albicans, Candida dubliniensis, and Candida tropicalis. In contrast, Z66 and Z04 exhibited broader antifungal spectra, with Z66 additionally demonstrating activity against Cryptococcus neoformans, Aspergillus fumigatus, and S. aureus. Furthermore, all four compounds show promise in combination therapies. Among them, Z35 showed the most favorable in vivo potential in a Galleria mellonella infection model. Its antifungal activity decreases progressively with increasing concentrations of iron, copper, and zinc, indicating that the compound disrupts fungal metal homeostasis. These metals play essential roles in cellular metabolism, the oxidative stress response, and virulence, and are tightly regulated19–22. Consistent with a metal-dependent mode of action, the addition of metal chelators enhanced the compound’s activity, and the chemical structure of the compound contains two strong chelating moieties.

Interestingly, Z35 selectively inhibits C. albicans, and to a lesser extent, C. dubliniensis, which is unexpected for a metal-chelating compound that would typically be predicted to exert broad-spectrum antifungal activity. This suggests that species-specific aspects of metal acquisition, regulation, or compound uptake may contribute to its mechanism of action. Altogether, our results highlight a promising and mechanistically intriguing avenue for further investigation.

The compounds were tested in two-fold dose–response assays against C. albicans in RPMI, YPD, and synthetic complete (SC) media with or without 10% fetal bovine serum as indicated. Growth was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 600 nm after 48 h of incubation at 30 °C. Three out of four compounds exhibited different bioactivity depending on the media used. Compounds Z66 and Z04 demonstrated activity in all media tested (Fig. 1C). Given the activity of all compounds in SC medium, subsequent experiments were continued under these conditions. To evaluate the antimicrobial spectrum of activity, the compounds were tested against a panel of clinically relevant fungal and bacterial strains (Fig. 1D). All strains used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Z41 displayed activity against C. albicans, Candida dubliniensis, and Candida tropicalis. In contrast, Z66 and Z04 exhibited broader antifungal spectra, with Z66 additionally demonstrating activity against Cryptococcus neoformans, Aspergillus fumigatus, and S. aureus. Z35 exhibited activity against C. albicans and C. dubliniensis. However, inhibition of C. dubliniensis was only visible at 24 h and had disappeared by 48 h, as this compound’s activity decreases over time. (Fig. 1D (48 h) and S1B (24 h)). Only Z66 and Z04, the compounds that exhibited the broadest spectrum of activity, showed fungicidal activity against certain species (Fig. 1D and S1A). The compounds were also tested against fluconazole-tolerant and -resistant C. albicans strains, CaCi-2 and CaCi-17, respectively24 (Figs. 1E and S1C). Our findings showed that only Z04 retained fungicidal activity against the fluconazole-resistant C. albicans strain.

Finally, checkerboard assays were performed to assess if the prioritized compounds showed enhanced activity in the presence of common antifungal drugs, such as fluconazole and caspofungin. The four compounds showed an additive effect in combination with caspofungin with FIC80 values ≥ 0.5 and < 1.0 (Fig. 1F), and indifferent effects in the presence of fluconazole with FIC80 values ranging from 1 to 1.5 (Fig. S1D).

Next, we assessed the toxicity and in vivo efficacy of the compounds using a Galleria mellonella model. G. mellonella larvae were bred in-house, and ten healthy 6th-instar larvae of 300 ± 50 mg were selected for each condition of the experiment. During the experiment, the larvae were housed individually in 12-well plates at 37 °C, without food and in the dark.

To evaluate toxicity, larvae were injected daily with 10 µL of each compound or vehicle control directly through the last proleg of the larvae into the hemocoel using a Hamilton syringe (model 701SN, 31 gauge). Larvae were treated daily with 50 mg/kg of the compound, a relatively high dose selected to assess host toxicity at elevated exposure levels and to maximize the likelihood of detecting antifungal efficacy in subsequent experiments26–28.

At this dose of 50 mg/kg, none of the tested compounds caused a significant reduction in survival, in contrast to the amphotericin B control, which resulted in substantial mortality (Fig. 2A). This suggests that the compounds do not induce severe toxicity in this model. However, larval health (scored based on movement, melanization, and survival as previously described26) declined with daily administration of Z66, Z35, and Z04 (Fig. 2B). To evaluate the in vivo antifungal efficacy, larvae were injected with 6 × 104 cells of a bioluminescent C. albicans reporter strain27 and were treated daily with 50 mg/kg of compound or vehicle control (Fig. 2C). The survival, health, and fungal load were monitored daily. Ten minutes before every bioluminescence imaging (BLI) session, the larvae were injected with 10 µL of 40 µg/g D-Luciferin into the hemocoel and were transferred to a black 12-well plate with a transparent bottom (IBL Baustoff + Labor GmbH, Austria). Subsequently, they were incubated at 37 °C for 10 min23,26,28. Bioluminescence emission was measured using an IVIS Spectrum imaging system (Revvity), as previously described23,28. Among the tested compounds, Z35 demonstrated the most promising activity. It improved survival (Fig. 2D), maintained significantly higher health scores throughout the experiment (Fig. 2E), and reduced fungal burden longitudinally, as measured by BLI, with a clear treatment effect observed as early as 24 h post-infection (p.i.) (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, larvae treated with Z35 showed the highest survival rate (90%), compared to 60% for the vehicle control, 10% for Z41, 30% for Z66, and 0% for Z04. Notably, compounds Z41, Z66, and Z04 caused increased mortality in infected larvae compared to the vehicle control. This likely reflects the combined physiological stress of C. albicans infection and compound exposure, as these compounds showed less adverse effects in uninfected larvae. Compound Z66 also induced a significant longitudinal reduction in fungal burden, with a significant decrease observed at day 4 p.i. However, this compound was associated with reduced survival and overall health, indicating potential toxicity.

The remaining compounds did not significantly reduce fungal burden. In conclusion, Z35 demonstrated the most favorable in vivo profile, combining antifungal efficacy with low toxicity in the G. mellonella model.

To elucidate the mode of action of Z35, the compound with the strongest in vivo potential, we explored the impact of metal ions on its antifungal activity. Given that the compound lost efficacy in YPD and serum, we hypothesized that metal ions could interfere with its activity. To test this, we assessed growth inhibition in the presence of biologically relevant metals at concentrations near their maximal tolerated levels29. Compounds were added at concentrations that inhibited fungal growth by ~ 85% under metal-free conditions. The addition of FeCl3, CuSO₄, and ZnSO₄ significantly restored growth (Fig. 3A), suggesting that these ions mitigate the compound’s antifungal effect. Moreover, Z35 was inactive in YPD, however, supplementation with chelator bathophenanthrolinedisulfonate (BPS; Bathophenanthrolinedisulfonic acid disodium salt hydrate) restored antifungal activity (Fig. 3B), supporting a role for metal availability in modulating compound efficacy. Dose–response assays further showed that increasing concentrations of FeCl3, CuSO₄, and ZnSO₄ progressively reduced the compound’s activity (Fig. 3C), suggesting that Z35 may interact with or sequester these metals. Furthermore, the addition of BPS and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) enhanced compound activity. Notably, both EDTA and BPS can also influence C. albicans growth by limiting the availability of essential metals. Consistent with our observations, Z35 contains two strong chelating moieties, and we hypothesize that its nitrogen atoms serve as key coordination sites for metal binding, enabling the compound to chelate Fe3, Cu2⁺, and Zn2⁺.

Discussion and conclusion

This work characterized four compounds with novel antifungal activity identified through a high-throughput screen: Z41, Z66, Z35 and Z04. Compound Z41 displayed activity against C. albicans, Candida dubliniensis, and Candida tropicalis. In contrast, Z66 and Z04 exhibited broader antifungal spectra, with Z66 additionally demonstrating activity against Cryptococcus neoformans, Aspergillus fumigatus, and S. aureus. Z35 exhibited activity against C. albicans as well as against C. dubliniensis, albeit to a lesser extent. All compounds showed additive effects when combined with caspofungin, suggesting potential for combination therapy. Among these compounds, Z35 showed the most favorable in vivo potential in a G. mellonella model, reducing fungal burden and improving overall larval health.

Our results show that Z35’s antifungal activity is modulated by the presence of iron, copper, and zinc. These essential micronutrients are involved in a wide range of biological functions, including cellular metabolism, the oxidative stress response, and virulence19–22. Supplementation with these metals progressively reduced the antifungal activity of Z35, indicating that metal availability modulates compound efficacy. Conversely, the addition of the metal chelators BPS or EDTA enhanced Z35 activity in both YPD and SC media. Because EDTA and BPS can themselves affect C. albicans growth by limiting the availability of essential metals, the enhanced activity observed in their presence may reflect multiple contributing factors. These include reduced metal availability, potential synergistic effects between metal limitation and compound exposure, or additive stress imposed by the chelators30.

Structurally, Z35 contains two nitrogen-containing moieties that could serve as coordination sites for metal binding, providing a possible explanation for the observed sensitivity of compound activity to metal availability. While our data are consistent with a model in which Z35 interacts with biologically relevant metal ions, further biochemical studies will be required to determine whether the compound directly chelates metals or instead perturbs metal homeostasis through a more indirect mechanism. Moreover, while interference with metal availability appears to be an important component of Z35 activity, additional metal-independent mechanisms cannot be excluded. Further studies will therefore be required to determine whether the antifungal effect primarily results from extracellular metal chelation or from a more direct intracellular interaction with fungal targets.

Interestingly, Z35 exhibited a narrow spectrum of activity, which is unusual for compounds whose activity depends on metal chelation, as such mechanisms might be expected to affect multiple microbial species. The selective inhibition of C. albicans, and to a lesser extent C. dubliniensis suggests that additional species-specific factors contribute to its mode of action. These may include differences in metal acquisition pathways, regulation of metal homeostasis, intracellular metal buffering capacity, or compound uptake and accumulation31. Furthermore, the narrow spectrum of activity of Z35 may underlie the compound’s minimal effect on host cells, as reflected by the reduced fungal burden and improved host health in the infection model, mitigating concerns about systemic metal chelation. However, this should be further investigated in additional infection models.

Interference with metal homeostasis represents an attractive antifungal strategy, as pathogens must carefully balance the acquisition, storage, and detoxification of metal ions during infection31,32. At the same time, host-imposed nutritional immunity limits metal availability by actively sequestering essential micronutrients to restrict microbial growth32,33. Consequently, compounds that exploit vulnerabilities in fungal metal acquisition or utilization may be especially effective in host environments with limited metal availability, such as the skin or mucosal surfaces34. Metal chelators are often considered unsuitable for systemic antifungal therapy as they can disrupt host metal homeostasis, and they are therefore mostly used to treat superficial fungal infections. However, there is a precedent for the therapeutic use of metal chelators in systemic settings, particularly by enhancing the efficacy of other antimicrobial drugs35,36. For example, EDTA can enhance the efficacy of amphotericin B in an invasive pulmonary aspergillosis rat model, without causing observable toxicity35,36. Consistent with this concept, exploring the combination treatment of Z35 and caspofungin in a systemic setting would be interesting. This is especially true given the encouraging results of Z35’s in vivo efficacy and absence of detectable host toxicity.

While several antifungal compounds exploit metal chelation, Z35 seems to be mechanistically distinct from other known classes such as pyridinones37,38, thiosemicarbazone derivatives39, siderophore-mimetic compounds (e.g., VL-2397)40, novel chelating compound (e.g. APC6)41, and adjuvant chelators such as DTPA35.

Overall, while interference with metal availability appears to represent an important component of Z35 activity, further work will be required to fully elucidate its mechanism of action. The unusual specificity of Z35 toward C. albicans, together with its in vivo efficacy, highlights a promising yet mechanistically intriguing avenue for further investigation.

Strains and growth conditions

Strains were grown in SC medium consisting of 0.19% yeast nitrogen base without amino acids (Formedium), 0.079% complete supplement mixture (MP Biomedicals), and 2% glucose with an adjusted pH of 5.5. The solution was centrifuged again for 15 min at 3750 rpm at 4 °C and the pellet was resuspended in 25 mL cold PBS. The spores were counted in a bürker chamber. The spores were stored at 4 °C for no more than one week. High-throughput screen

A library of 20,000 drug-like compounds, acquired from Enamine and made available through the VIB screening core Ghent was screened at 50 µM against C. albicans SC5314 in LoFlo medium at 30 °C, as previously described23. SC5314 and rib1Δ/Δ (positive control) were grown overnight and subsequently washed three times in LoFlo medium. The rib1Δ/Δ strain was used as a positive control because it mimics the effect of a compound that inhibits an essential pathway, specifically riboflavin biosynthesis, which represents an attractive target for the development of novel antifungal drugs25.

The cultures were diluted to OD600 0.1 in 384 well plates. An echo acoustic dispenser was used to add compound or DMSO to the plates, which were subsequently incubated for 24 h at 30 °C with shaking before measuring the OD600.

Susceptibility testing

Two-fold dose–response assays were performed in either 96-well or 384-well plates and incubated at 30 °C in dark static conditions. Overnight yeast cultures were diluted to a final density of 2 × 103 cells per well, and serial two-fold dilutions of compounds were added. Optical density at 600 nm was measured at designated time points using a spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices). Growth inhibition was reported after 48 h to capture sustained antifungal or antibacterial effects and to account for potential regrowth. For compound Z35, a visible decrease in activity was observed over time; therefore, results at both 24 h and 48 h are reported. For C. neoformans, growth was assessed after 72 h to accommodate its slower growth kinetics. For Aspergillus fumigatus, dose–response assays were performed in SC medium at 35 °C using an inoculum of 1.5 × 105 spores/mL. Bacterial dose–response assays were performed with 5 × 105 CFU/mL in Mueller–Hinton broth, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C.

Data analysis included background subtraction using medium-only controls, averaging of technical replicates, and normalization to the no-drug control. Relative growth values were visualized as heat maps. To evaluate compound cidality, treated cultures were stamped onto drug-free YPD agar plates or Mueller–Hinton agar for bacterial growth. Growth was assessed after 48 h for fungal growth and 24 h for bacterial growth.

Checkerboard assays were performed similarly to the susceptibility assays, but two compound gradients were added to the plate.

Toxicity and efficacy tests in Galleria mellonella

G. mellonella larvae were bred in-house, and ten healthy 6th-instar larvae of 300 ± 50 mg were selected for each condition of the experiment. During the experiment, the larvae were housed individually in 12-well plates at 37 °C, without food and in the dark.

To evaluate toxicity, 10 µL of compound or vehicle was injected with a Hamilton syringe (model 701SN, 31 gauge; Hamilton Company, Switzerland) through the last proleg into the hemocoel of the larvae. The larvae were injected daily and were monitored for five to six days. The health score was determined by assessing the survival, movement, and melanization of the larvae as previously described26.

To evaluate the efficacy of eradicating C. albicans in vivo, a bioluminescent C. albicans reporter strain27 was used to monitor fungal burden over the course of the infection. The larvae were infected with 6 × 104 bioluminescent C. albicans reporter cells and were treated every day of the experiment with 50 mg/kg compound or vehicle control. This dosage was selected based on previously reported antifungal studies in the Galleria mellonella model51–53 and does not directly correspond to in vitro data, as direct extrapolation is difficult, as pharmacokinetic parameters in this model are not sufficiently characterized. The survival and health, and fungal load were monitored daily. Ten minutes before every BLI imaging session, the larvae were injected 10 µL of 40 µg/g D-Luciferin into the hemocoel and they were transferred to a black 12-well plate with a transparent bottom (IBL Baustoff + Labor GmbH, Austria) and were incubated at 37 °C for 10 min23,26,28,54. Bioluminescence emission was measured with an IVIS Spectrum imaging system (Revvity) and five consecutive images were taken with open filter settings, F/stop 1, subject height 0.5 cm, medium binning, and a 30 s exposure time per image. Living Image Software (version 4.5.4) was used to quantify the total photon flux (p/s) per larva by defining a circular region of interest (ROI) with a 2.5 cm diameter.
