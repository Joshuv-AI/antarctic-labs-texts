The 3′–5′ exoribonuclease ISG20L2 contributes to 3′ terminus maturation of 18S and 28S ribosomal RNAs
Authors: Maxime Aubert, Hélène Zuber, Marine Mommert-Tripon, Sarah Le Page, Nathalie Montel-Lehry, Vanessa Soldan, Stéphanie Bosch, Dominique Gagliardi, Pierre-Emmanuel Gleizes, Marie-Françoise O’Donohue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRibosome biogenesis is a nodal process in cell growth and proliferation, and its dysfunction is associated with human congenital diseases and tumorigenesis. Despite extensive mechanistic characterization, several processing steps of human pre-ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) remain elusive. The 47S primary transcript contains three out of four rRNAs flanked by external (5′ETS, 3′ETS) and internal (ITS1, ITS2) transcribed spacers. The molecular processes leading to the removal of the 3′ external transcribed spacer (ETS), one of the earliest maturation steps, is not fully understood. Combining loss-of-function experiments and 3′-RACE high-throughput sequencing, we showed that the vertebrate-specific 3′–5′ exoribonuclease ISG20L2, a DEDDh RNase T superfamily member, is critical for efficient removal of the 3′ETS and formation of large ribosomal subunits. ISG20L2 inactivation led to accumulation of various forms of 3′-extended pre-rRNAs and disorganized peripheral dense fibrillar component, a sub-nucleolar compartment recently linked to 3′ETS processing. The function of ISG20L2 also extends to the trimming of ITS1 after endonucleolytic cleavage at site 2 and points to its contribution to the 3′ processing of U8 small nucleolar RNA precursor. Altogether, the present work uncovers the landscape of these processing steps at nucleotide resolution, and reveals the function of ISG20L2 with respect to the maturation of 18S and 28S 3′ ends.

Introduction

Over the past two decades, a full understanding of human ribosome biogenesis has attracted an increasing interest with a number of rare diseases in which this process dysfunctions [1, 2]. In human cells, three out of the four ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) (18S, 5.8S, and 28S rRNAs) stem from a long primary transcript (the 47S pre-rRNA) in which they are flanked by transcribed spacers that are eliminated in a sequential manner [3, 4]. This complex maturation process, which starts in the cell nucleoli and ends in the cytoplasm, requires the transient contribution of more than 200 ribosomal assembly factors, many of which still remain to be functionally characterized. Pre-rRNA folding and processing is intimately coupled with the sequential assembly of the 80 different ribosomal proteins (RPs) that make the small and the large ribosomal subunits (RPSs and RPLs, respectively) [5, 6]. In addition, ~150 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes drive rRNA modifications, or coordinate endonucleolytic cleavages of the transcribed spacers or folding of structural domains [7–9]. rRNA modifications include mainly 2′-O-methylations and pseudouridylations, guided by small nucleolar RNPs (snoRNPs) or catalysed by stand-alone enzymes [9, 10]. Altogether, these molecular events follow a tightly-ordered pattern to allow the proper structural assembly of ribosomal subdomains and prevent premature inter-domain contacts during this process [6, 11].

Maturation of the two ribosomal subunits has gained complexity throughout evolution. In mammals, both the transcribed spacers and rRNA sequences have increased in length, and the precise timing at which some ribosomal assembly factors intervene during the processing may differ compared to other model organisms, such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Moreover, additional maturation events involving several exo- and endonucleases have been reported. So far, most of the ribonucleases or ribozymes responsible for the endonucleolytic steps occurring in the human rRNA precursors have been identified (Supplementary Fig. S1) [12, 13]. However, some activities, such as those sustaining the removal of the 3′ external transcribed spacer (3′ETS), remain elusive. This step is believed to take place shortly after the first endonucleolytic cleavage at site A’ in the 5′ETS [14]. It involves the highly conserved, vertebrate-specific snoRNA U8/SNORD118, which ensures long-range pre-rRNA folding of 5.8S and 28S that is required for both the removal of 3′ETS and ITS2 cleavage [15–17]. Recently, the 3′–5′ exonuclease REXO5 has been implicated in the trimming of the 200-nucleotide long 3′ETS segment in Drosophila melanogaster [18]. Similarly, the release of the 3′ extremity of the 35S primary transcript in yeast S. cerevisiae by Rnt1p endonucleolytic cleavage is followed by the trimming of the remaining nucleotides by the 3′–5′ exoribonucleases Rex1-4 [19–22]. Nonetheless, the attempts to challenge their orthologs towards the human 3′ETS removal have remained unsuccessful so far.

Other studies addressing ribosome biogenesis in human cells have highlighted important divergences with baker’s yeast, with additional processing steps involving endo- and exoribonucleases. For example, the removal of the 1095 nucleotide-long human ITS1 requires three sequential endonucleolytic cleavages to release the mature 3′ extremity of the 18S rRNA sequence. Cleavages at site 2 (by RNase MRP) and then at site E (putatively by hUTP24) successively release the 21S and 18S-E pre-rRNAs [23–25]. Both precursors are then subjected to 3′–5′ exonucleolytic trimming [26–29]. RRP6/EXOSC10 has been proposed to contribute to the trimming of 21S-C pre-rRNA [28, 29], while the poly(A)-specific ribonuclease (PARN) shapes the 3′ extremity of the 18S-E pre-rRNA [30, 31]. In the cytoplasm, the release of 18S 3′ end is ultimately performed by endonuclease NOB1 and is activated by the combined binding of RIO1, the latest pre-40S assembly factor, and RPS26 to pre-40S particles [32, 33]. In contrast, removal of ITS1 after cleavage at site A2 in baker’s yeast necessitates a single endonucleolytic step performed by Nob1p, without involving any exonucleolytic trimming.

Through the screening of several 3′–5′ exoribonucleases potentially involved in the elimination of the transcribed spacers in the human primary transcript, we pinpointed ISG20L2 (interferon-stimulated exonuclease gene 20 KD-like 2; UniProt Knowledgebase accession number #Q9H9L3), an enzyme belonging to the DEDDh RNase T 3′–5′ exonuclease family, which includes two vertebrate subfamilies. ISG20L2 subgroup also encompasses ISG20 and AEN (also called ISG20L1) [18]. ISG20L2 was previously shown to be involved in 5.8S rRNA production and recently assigned to the last cytoplasmic step of 18S-E pre-rRNA maturation [34, 35]. Combining loss-of-function experiments, 3′ RACE-seq of pre-rRNAs and in situ approaches, we have conducted a thorough deciphering of ISG20L2 functions. The results lead us to restrict the role of this enzyme to the nucleus, where it contributes to the 3′ maturation of both the 18S and 28S rRNAs.

Materials and methods

Expression vectors, site-directed mutagenesis, and production of inducible cell lines

The expression vector pCMV6-XL5-hISG20L2 (Origene Technologies GmbH, Herford, Germany) was purified with a maxi prep EndoFree plasmid kit (Qiagen, Les Ulis, France). A first round of mutagenesis was performed with a Q5 site-directed mutagenesis kit (New England Biolabs, Evry, France) in order to introduce four silent mutations into the target site of the siRNA duplex siISG20L2-4 (primers SDM_L2-4_silent_Fwd and _Rev; Supplementary Table S1). The resulting pISG20L2-silent vector allowed the expression of a recombinant protein in cells in which endogenous ISG20L2 could be depleted by siISG20L2-4. The pISG20L2-silent vector was further subjected to site-directed mutagenesis in order to change the His322 residue, which interacts with the nucleophilic water molecule to allow the catalysis, into Ala (primers SDM_H > A_Fwd and _Rev; Supplementary Table S1). These constructs were subcloned in a pcDNA5/FRT/TO plasmid with two cloning primers for conditional expression of the wild-type and mutant proteins bearing a C-terminal His6-PRC-2xFlag tag (pMB187; a kind gift of Dr Katherine Bohnsack, Medical University of Göttingen, Germany). The stable inducible HeLa Flp-In T-Rex cell lines expressing ISG20L2 variants were obtained following the manufacturer’s instructions. Electroporation was used to co-transfect HeLa Flp-In T-Rex cells (Invitrogen, CA, USA) with a 9:1 ratio of pOG44:pcDNA5/FRT/TO_ISG20L2-variant_His6-PRC-2xFlag. Cells were split at <25% confluence 48 h after electroporation into new fresh medium containing 600 µg/ml of hygromycin B. Treatment with hygromycin B was maintained for several days until cell foci became identified. The foci were then pooled, and the expression of the protein of interest was checked by western blot. Cells were treated for 24 h with 1 µg/ml doxycycline to allow expression of wild-type or mutant ISG20L2-Flag proteins.

Cell culture and treatment with siRNA duplexes

HeLa and HeLa Flp-In T-Rex cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% foetal calf serum and 1× sodium pyruvate at 37°C in the presence of 5% CO2. To knock down expression of the corresponding human protein genes, each siRNA duplex (Eurogentec, Seraing, Belgium; listed in Supplementary Table S1) was added at a final concentration of 500 nM to 200 µl of cell suspension (50 × 106 cells/ml diluted in Na phosphate buffer, pH 7.25, containing 250 mM sucrose and 1 mM MgCl2). Alternatively, batches of several siRNA duplexes (500 nM each) were added as mentioned in figure legends. After electroporation at 240 V, cells were plated and collected 48 h later. Cells were then electroporated a second time in the same conditions and collected 2 days later. Control cells were electro-transformed with a scramble siRNA (Eurogentec). Complementary DNAs (cDNAs) were generated with a high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystems, Illkirch, France). Knockdown efficiency, assessed by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (PCR), using HPRT1 messenger RNA (mRNA) as an internal control, was between 70%–82%. Fragments located at exon junctions were amplified with an IQ SybrGreen supermix kit and processed on a CFX96-Real Time System PCR machine (Bio-Rad, Marnes-la-Coquette, France). The primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Samples were analysed in triplicates and repeated with independent experimental replicates.

Cell fractionation

After collection, cells were mechanically disrupted with a Dounce homogenizer and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4°C as previously described [24, 29]. The supernatant containing the cytoplasmic fraction was recovered and clarified, and the nuclei-containing pellet was washed and purified by centrifugation on a sucrose cushion. For cytoplasmic RNA extraction, 100 µl of the suspension was mixed to 1 ml TRI reagent (Molecular Research Center, OH, USA) and further extracted. For nuclear RNA analysis, the entire pellet was lysed in TRI reagent.

Analysis of ribosomes by sucrose density gradient centrifugation

HeLa cells were treated with 100 µg/ml cycloheximide (Sigma, MO, USA) and incubated at 37°C for 10 min. They were then detached by trypsin treatment, washed and mechanically disrupted with a Dounce homogenizer, with cycloheximide added to all buffers as previously described [24, 29]. A sample volume containing 0.7–1 mg of total proteins measured with Pierce Protein Assay reagent (Thermo Scientific, Illkirch, France) was loaded on a 10%–50% (w/w) sucrose gradient, and the tubes were centrifuged in an Optima X100 ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter, Villepinte, France) with a SW41 rotor at 36 000 rpm for 120 min. The gradient fractions were collected at OD254 nm with a Foxy Jr. gradient collector (Teledyne Isco, NE, USA).

Isolation of total RNAs and northern blot analyses

For total RNA isolation, 10–15 × 106 HeLa cells were collected by centrifugation for 5 min at 300 × g and 4°C. After rinsing with 10 ml cold phosphate buffered saline (PBS), each pellet was thoroughly mixed with 1 ml TRI reagent and extracted following the manufacturer’s instructions. The upper aqueous phase was further purified by phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) extractions (Sigma) prior to isopropanol precipitation. Quantifications were performed at 260 nm with an Implen NP80 spectrophotometer (Mettler Toledo, Viroflay, France). Pre-rRNA intermediates were separated on a 1.1% agarose gel containing 1.2% formaldehyde and 1× Tri/Tri buffer (30 mM triethanolamine, 30 mM tricine, pH 7.9) (3 µg total RNAs/lane) or on a 6% polyacrylamide gel (19:1) (Bio-Rad) in 1× TBE buffer containing 7 M urea (1.5 µg total RNAs/lane). RNAs were transferred to Hybond N+ nylon membrane (GE Healthcare, Chalfont Saint Giles, UK) and cross-linked under UV light. Membrane pre-hybridization for 2 h in 6× saline-sodium citrate buffer (SSC), 5× Denhardt’s solution, 0.5% sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS), 0.9 µg/ml transfer RNA (tRNA), and then incubated overnight with the corresponding 5′-radiolabelled oligonucleotide probe. After washing twice for 10 min in 2× SSC, 0.1% SDS and once in 1× SSC, 0.1% SDS, the membrane was exposed to a PhosphorImager screen. Radioactive signals were revealed using a Typhoon Trio PhosphorImager (Cytivia, Glattbrugg, Switzerland) and quantified using the ImageQuantTL software. The probes used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Pulse chase labelling analysis of pre-rRNA processing

HeLa cells were incubated in methionine-free DMEM (Invitrogen) for 30 min at 37°C, labelled for 15 min with L-methyl 3H methionine (50 mCi/ml), and rinsed twice with DMEM containing methionine. They were then immediately rinsed with ice-cold PBS, or incubated 30 min, 1 h, 1 h 30, 2 h, or 2 h 30 in DMEM containing methionine. After the corresponding chase time, cells were rinsed twice with ice-cold PBS and immediately lysed with 1 ml TRI reagent (Invitrogen). After extraction, concentrations were measured with a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA samples were separated on a 1.2% agarose gel and passively transferred to a nylon membrane as described below. The membrane was exposed for 4–6 days to Biomax KODAK TM MS films through a KODAK TM BioMax TranScreen LE.

Western blots

Whole-cell protein extracts were prepared with ice-cold cell lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1% Triton X-100, 200 mM NaCl, 1× complete EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche Diagnostics, Meylan, France)]. Protein extracts (17 µg) were separated using 8%–12% gradient polyacrylamide gels (Invitrogen) and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (GE Healthcare). After saturation, the membrane was incubated with the primary antibody (anti-ISG20L2, 1/1000 (24639-1-AP, Proteintech); anti-Flag 1/1000 (F1804, Sigma); anti-actin, 1/1000 (A4700, Sigma), before incubation with a secondary antibody coupled to peroxidase (1/10 000; Invitrogen). Immuno-detection was performed using an ECL kit (Pierce) and imaged using ChemiDoc MP Imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Mapping of pre-rRNA 3′ ends by 3′ RACE-seq

The protocol of 3′ RACE-seq was adapted from [36]. After extraction, 5 µg of total RNAs were ligated to 0.2 µM of a 5′-riboadenylated 3′ dideoxycytosine DNA oligonucleotide (3′_Modified_Adapter) for 1 h at 37°C using 10 U of T4 ssRNA Ligase 1 (New England Biolabs) in 1× of T4 RNA ligase reaction buffer (New England Biolabs; 50 mM Tris–HCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM dithiotreitol (DTT), pH 7.5) in a final volume of 50 µl. At the end of the reaction, the volume was completed to 100 µl with RNase-free water, and the purification products were subsequently purified with Nucleospin (Macherey Nagel, Hoerdt, France) or Monarch (New England Biolabs) RNA clean-up columns. RNAs were then precipitated with ethanol, sodium acetate and glycogen blue, solubilized in sterile, RNase-free water, and then quantified with an Implen NP80 spectrophotometer. The synthesis of cDNA was performed on 5 µg of ligation product using 50 pmol of the 3′-RT oligonucleotide, 10 nmol of dNTP, 0.1 μmol of DTT, 40 U of RNaseIN plus (Promega, Charbonnières-les-Bains, France), 200 U of SuperScript IV reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen), and 1× of SuperScript IV RT buffer (Invitrogen). Reactions were incubated at 50°C for 15 min, and then at 70°C for 15 min to inactivate the reverse transcriptase. Two nested PCR amplification rounds of 20 cycles were then performed. The first PCR (PCR1) was run using 1 µl of cDNA synthesized, 50 pmol of MgSO4, 5 pmol of gene-specific sense primer 1, 5 pmol of RACEseq_rev1 primer, 1 U of AccuPrime GC-Rich DNA Polymerase (Invitrogen), and 1× of AccuPrime GC-Rich Buffer A (Invitrogen) in a 25-μl final volume. PCR1 was run on a thermal cycler programmed as follows: a step at 95°C for 3 min; 20 cycles at 95°C for 30 s, 52°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 45 s; a final step at 72°C for 10 min. The second PCR (PCR2) was run using 1 µl of PCR1 product, 50 pmol of MgSO4, 10 pmol of gene-specific sense primer 2, 10 pmol of a TruSeq RNA PCR index, 1 U of AccuPrime GC-Rich DNA Polymerase (Invitrogen), and 1× of AccuPrime GC-Rich Buffer A (Invitrogen) in a 25-μl final volume. The conditions for PCR2 were as follows: a step at 95°C for 3 min; 20 cycles at 95°C for 30 s, 56°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 45 s; a final step at 72°C for 10 min. For each sample, three 25-µl reactions of PCR2 were pooled and then purified using 1.2 volumes of AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter). DNAs were then precipitated with ethanol, sodium acetate, and glycogen blue, solubilized in sterile RNase-free water, and library concentrations were determined using a Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen). Libraries were analysed on a 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent Technologies, Les Ulis, France) using a High Sensitivity DNA Kit to assess quality and estimate size distribution. Libraries were paired-end sequenced with MiSeq (v3 chemistry; Illumina, Evry, France) with 41 × 111 bp cycle settings. The respective numbers of sequencing cycles for read 1 and read 2 were adjusted according to other samples that were co-analysed. Read 1 was used to identify target transcripts, whereas read 2 was used to map 3′ extremities and analyse 3′ potential non-templated nucleotides. To compensate for the poor diversity of the amplicon libraries, 30% of a phiX control library (Illumina) were added.

3′ RACE-seq data processing

After initial data processing by the MiSeq Control Software v 2.6 (Illumina), Fastq files were analysed by a homemade pipeline adapted from [36] and composed of scripts using Python (v3), Biopython (v1.79) 52, and RegEX (v 2022.9.13) libraries. Reads with low quality bases (= < Q10) within the 15-base random sequence of the read 2 or within the 30 bases downstream of the delimiter sequence were filtered out. Sequences with identical nucleotides in 15-base random sequence were deduplicated. Next, nucleotide sequences corresponding to the forwards PCR2 primer (Supplementary Table S1) were searched into reads 1 to identify the corresponding target in the 3′ETS or ITS1. One mismatch was tolerated. Matched reads 1 and their corresponding reads 2 were extracted for further analysis. Reads 2 that contain the delimiter sequence were selected and subsequently trimmed from their random and delimiter sequences. As some of the target sequences were short, some reads 2 ran into the 5′ forwards PCR primer, the sequence of which was also removed. Reads 2 shorter than 20 nucleotides after these trimming steps were excluded from further analysis. In order to map 3′ extremities of target RNAs, the 20-nucleotide sequences downstream of the read-2 delimiter sequence were mapped to the corresponding reference sequence, which goes from the first nucleotide of the transcript that maps the forwards PCR2 primer to the end of the target RNA. Up to two mismatches were tolerated, with the exception of the first nucleotide downstream of the mapping site that had to map perfectly. To map the 3′ end position of reads 2 with untemplated tails, the sequences of the unmatched reads 2 were successively trimmed from their 3′ end with a single nucleotide trimming step, until they could be mapped to the reference sequence, or until a maximum of 30 nucleotides had been removed. For each successfully mapped read 2, untemplated nucleotides at the 3′ end were extracted and annotated according to their nucleotide tail composition as: U-tails (i.e. only composed of Us), U-rich tails (i.e. composed of a majority of Us, >70%), A-tails (i.e. only composed of As), A-rich tails (i.e. composed of a majority of As, >70%), C-tails (i.e. only composed of Cs), C-rich tails (i.e. composed of a majority of Cs, >70%), G-tails (i.e. only composed of Gs), and G-rich tails (i.e. composed of a majority of Gs, >70%). Distribution profiles shown in Figs 4– 6 and in Supplementary Figs S3–S8 display the percentages of sequences according to their 3′ end position (see relevant 3′ETS, ITS1, and U8 sequences in Supplementary Fig. S9). Percentages were calculated using the total number of reads as denominator.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization

HeLa Flp-In T-Rex cell lines allowing doxycycline-induced expression of wild-type or mutant ISG20L2-Flag proteins were grown on glass coverslips. They were treated twice with a control siRNA (scramble) or siRNA ISG20L2-4 with an interval of 48 h. Prior to fixation, HeLa Flp-In T-Rex wt and mut-ISG20L2 were treated for 24 h with 1 µg/ml doxycycline. Coverslips were washed in PBS and fixed for 30 min with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS (Electron Microscopy Sciences). After washing twice in PBS, cells were permeabilized overnight in 70% ethanol at 4°C. After two washes in 2× SSC containing 10% formamide, the following steps were performed in the dark: hybridization at 37°C for 5 h in 10% formamide, 2.1× SSC, 0.5 μg/ml tRNA, 10% dextran sulfate, 250 μg/ml BSA, 10 mM ribonucleoside vanadyl complexes, and 0.5 ng/μl of each probe (ITS2 and 5′ITS1 probes, respectively, conjugated to Cy3 and Cy5; Eurogentec). After two washes at 37°C with 2× SSC containing 10% formamide, the cells were rinsed in PBS, counter stained with Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen), and mounted in Vectashield antifade mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, CA, USA). Observations were made with a Ti-E/B inverted microscope (Nikon, Amstelveen, The Netherlands) equipped with a Lumencor Spectra fluorescence LED illumination system and an Orca-Flash4.0 camera (Hamamatsu Photonics, Massy, France). The Nikon Plan APO VC 100× oil objective (N.A. 1.4) was equipped with a motorized MCL NanoDrive piezo nanopositioner, and stacks were acquired with a 0.2 µm-z step. Images were acquired with NIS Element software and deconvolution was performed with Huygens Deconvolution software (Scientific Volume Imaging). Image analysis was performed with ImageJ software.

Electron microscopy

Cells were prefixed with 2% glutaraldehyde/2% paraformaldehyde in Sorensen phosphate buffer 0.1 M, pH 7.2, for 15 min before fixing with 2.5% glutaraldehyde/2% paraformaldehyde in Sorensen phosphate buffer 0.1 M, pH 7.2, for 2 h at room temperature. Cells were then washed in Sorensen phosphate buffer and postfixed with 1% OsO4 in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, pH 7.2 (Electron Microscopy Sciences, PA, USA), rinsed in the same buffer and pelleted, concentrated in agarose, and treated for 1 h with 2% aqueous uranyl acetate. The samples were then dehydrated in a graded ethanol series and embedded in Epon. After 48 h of polymerization at 60°C, ultrathin sections (80-nm thick) were mounted on 200-mesh Formvar–carbon-coated copper grids. Finally, sections were stained with UranyLess and lead citrate. Grids were examined with a JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope (Jeol, Croissy-sur-Seine, France) at 80 kV. Images were acquired using an Orius SC1000B digital camera (Gatan, CA, USA).

Results

ISG20L2 is required for the 3′–5′ trimming of ITS1

The 3′ end of the 21S pre-rRNA results from endonucleolytic cleavage at site 2 by RNase MRP (Supplementary Fig. S1) [23]. Then, this precursor undergoes 3′–5′ trimming up to the C-region, an ITS1 segment whose primary sequence is highly conserved in warm-blooded vertebrates, yielding the 21S-C pre-rRNA [26, 28, 37]. This gradual trimming was visualized by pulse chase analysis of rRNA synthesis with L-[methyl-3H]-methionine (Fig. 1A, left). After a 1-h chase, processing of 21S 3′ end gave rise to a continuum of intermediates with variable abundancies. This could correspond to variations in the kinetics of the exonucleolytic trimming due to strong secondary structures that have been predicted for the human ITS1 (right, domains VI to VIII) [37]. In order to uncover the mechanisms underlying the elimination of the human ITS1, we assessed the effects of siRNA duplexes targeting several 3′–5′ exoribonucleases potentially involved at this step of the processing. A close analysis of the pre-rRNA patterns visualized by northern blot showed that efficient ISG20L2 depletion alleviated exonucleolytic trimming of 21S pre-rRNA (Fig. 1B; 5′ITS1 probe). Weaker depletion of IGS20L2 by siISG20L2-3 (Fig. 1B and C) was not sufficient to yield this phenotype. Noticeably, the 21S pre-rRNA observed by northern blot in ISG20L2-depleted cells migrated above the corresponding species in control cells, suggesting that its 3′ end is processed rapidly after cleavage at site 2 under normal conditions (Fig. 1B; 5′ITS1 and ITS1-59 probes). Analysis of nuclear extracts upon extended electrophoretic separation not only confirmed this finding but also revealed the presence of shorter forms of 30S and 26S pre-rRNAs in control cells, but not in ISG20L2-depleted cells (Fig. 1E; green arrows). These species, named here 30S-C and 26S-C, likely correspond to 30S and 26S precursors shortened in 3′ in a similar way to 21S pre-rRNA processing into 21S-C (Fig. 1F).

Altogether, these data show that the 3′ trimming of ITS1 following cleavage at site 2 requires ISG20L2.

ISG20L2 depletion also led to increased levels of 18S-E pre-rRNAs, as more prominently seen with siRNA duplexes siISG20L2-2 or siISG20L2-4 (Fig. 1B), an observation also reported by Ma et al. [35], who proposed a role for ISG20L2 in 18S-E 3′ end maturation. However, 18S-E precursors bearing long 3′ extensions did not accumulate when ISG20L2 was depleted (Fig. 1B; ITS1-59 probe), unlike what was previously observed after knockdown of the 3′–5′ exoribonuclease PARN [31]. Therefore, this accumulation was not the consequence of impaired exonucleolytic trimming of the 18S-E 3′ end. Increased levels of 18S-E pre-rRNA may alternatively originate from an inversion of the canonical order of ITS1 cleavages on the 41S precursor when cleavage at site 2 is impaired [38, 39]. Prevalence of cleavage at site E then leads to the release of 18S-E and 36S pre-rRNAs (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. S1). Indeed, ISG20L2-depleted cells showed impaired cleavage at site 2 as indicated by accumulation of 41S precursors (Fig. 2A; 5′ITS1 and ITS1-5.8S probes), while exacerbated levels of 36S pre-rRNAs vouched for direct cleavage at site E (ITS1-5.8S probe). This leads us to conclude that the increased level of 18S-E pre-rRNA observed upon ISG20L2 loss-of-function does not reflect defective 3′ exonucleolytic processing of this species, but rather exacerbated cleavage at site E.

ISG20L2 shapes 28S rRNA 3′ end

In addition to precursors with impaired exonucleolytic or endonucleolytic processing of the ITS1, we also detected pre-rRNAs migrating slightly above the 36S precursor in ISG20L2-depleted cells, suggesting an additional processing defect 3′ of 28S rRNA (Fig. 2A; ITS1-5.8S probe). Similarly, pre-rRNAs migrating slightly above the 32S band were visible (Fig. 2A; ITS2, 28S-3′ETS, and 3′ETS probes), and hybridization with probes to the 3′ETS revealed the presence of 28S precursors (45S, 43S, 41S, 36S, 32S) bearing a 3′ETS extension (labelled -L for ‘long’; depicted in purple), both in total and in nuclear fractions (Fig. 2A and B; see inset for a schematical representation). This establishes a role for ISG20L2 at an early step of the processing corresponding to 3′ETS removal. Strikingly, we also visualized additional bands in ISG20L2-depleted cells, which could correspond to degradation products of aberrant pre-60S particles (purple arrowheads in Fig. 2B; 28S-3′ETS probe). Low levels of 28S-L pre-rRNAs were also detected in the nuclear fraction of ISG20L2-depleted cells, attesting that maturation in 5′ could proceed despite residual 3′ETS extensions. However, 28S-L pre-rRNA was absent from the cytoplasmic fraction, suggesting that pre-60S particles containing this species were eliminated by quality control mechanisms. In contrast, we observed no processing defect of 12S pre-rRNA nor any accumulation of 5.8S precursors (Fig. 1B), unlike what is seen after depletion of the 3′–5′ exoRNase hDIS3, a known key player for 5.8S rRNA 3′ end maturation (inset) [12, 40]. Quantification of the precursors relative to 18S rRNA confirmed that 12S pre-rRNA did not accumulate upon ISG20L2 depletion (Fig. 2C). This differs from previously published data reporting an altered 5.8S production after ISG20L2 knockdown or knockout [34, 35]. Fractionation of cytoplasmic ribosomal subunits on sucrose gradient in ISG20L2-depleted cells revealed that free large ribosomal subunits (60S peak) were less abundant than in control cells, consistent with a large subunit production defect (Fig. 2D). On the contrary, the free 40S peak increased slightly, which could be attributed to a subsequent excess of free small ribosomal subunits and/or an accumulation of cytoplasmic pre-40S particles, as attested by the increase of 18S-E pre-rRNA in this compartment (Fig. 2B).

Collectively, these data show the requirement of ISG20L2 for 3′ETS removal, in addition to its role in ITS1 trimming. They emphasize that 3′ETS removal represents a checkpoint for subsequent processing, and point to the triggering of a degradation process if this milestone is not properly reached.

A catalytic mutant of ISG20L2 impairs 3′ETS and ITS1 processing

To further demonstrate the role of ISG20L2 in 3′ETS and ITS1 trimming, we generated cell lines expressing either wild-type or H322A-mutant ISG20L2-Flag proteins upon doxycycline treatment (Fig. 3). Silent mutations in the coding sequences of the transgenes were introduced to make them unsensitive to the action of the siISG20L2-4 duplex. Upon induction, the wild-type flagged protein levels were in a comparable range as endogenous ISG20L2, while the mutant proteins were detected in higher amounts (Fig. 3D). This overexpression of catalytically-dead H322A mutant ISG20L2 induced a strong molecular phenotype, with additional pre-rRNAs bearing a 3′ extension (highlighted in purple; Fig. 3A, lane 6). This early processing defect was detrimental for cleavage at site 2, as shown by marked accumulations of 41S-L, 36S-L, 36S-C-L, and 32S-L precursors, and the concomitant decrease of the 21S pre-rRNA. The level of these unprocessed pre-RNAs was exacerbated after knockdown of endogenous ISG20L2 expression with siISG20L2-4 (lane 12, Fig. 3A and B). This also led to the accumulation of shorter pre-rRNAs species extended in 3′ detected with the 28S-3′ETS probe (purple arrowheads), likely corresponding to the degradation products of aberrant early pre-60S particles detected earlier. Despite the strong phenotype observed upon mut-ISG20L2-Flag expression alone or in combination with siISG20L2-4, the 28S/18S ratio remained moderately impacted (Fig. 3A and C), consistent with impaired biogenesis of both ribosomal subunits. On the contrary, overexpression of the siRNA-resistant wild-type enzyme rescued in part proper processing of the 3′ETS and 21S pre-rRNAs and restored the canonical processing pathway (lane 10). This is attested by the concomitant decrease of 36S and 18S-E pre-rRNAs and the disappearance of 41S-L and 32S-L precursors (Fig. 3A and B), together with the recovery of 21S-C pre-rRNAs (Supplementary Fig. S10).

Altogether, these data establish that ISG20L2 catalytic activity drives 3′ETS and ITS1 exonucleolytic trimming, and confirm that 3′ETS removal is a key transition allowing subsequent pre-60S particle maturation steps.

ISG20L2 contributes to the final steps of U8 maturation

We analysed whether loss-of-function of ISG20L2 impacted U8 snoRNA, a key-player in 3′ETS processing (Fig. 4). ISG20L2 knockdown almost doubled the amount of U8 precursors detected by northern blot relative to its mature form (Fig. 4A and B). Expression of mutant ISG20L2-Flag in ISG20L2-depleted cells did amplify this phenotype (lane 12), while expression of the wild-type protein restored mature U8 to the level observed in controls (compare lane 10 to lanes 1–2). In contrast, a subset of small non-coding RNAs, including box C/D snoRNA U3, were not affected by depletion of ISG20L2 (Fig. 4A and C). Analysis of U8 in control cells by 3′RACE-seq showed that almost 90% of the reads ended at position 137 (labelled 0 in Fig. 4D), which corresponds to the known 3′ terminal nucleotide of U8 (Supplementary Fig. S9C). The remaining reads corresponded to precursors bearing short 3′ extensions, mainly 1–4 in length, in line with data describing U8 precursors [41]. Depletion of ISG20L2 decreased the amount of mature U8 by ~40% (Fig. 4D and E). The fraction of U8 precursors increased in these samples, more specially at positions 1–4. Combining ISG20L2 depletion with mutant ISG20L2-Flag expression yielded the same phenotype, while wild-type ISG20L2-Flag expression restored partially the mature forms at the expense of U8 precursors (Fig. 4D; see also Fig. 4A, lane 10). These data suggest that ISG20L2 contributes to the 3′ end maturation of snoRNA U8 in human cells, consistent with the function of REXO5 regarding U8 maturation in D. melanogaster [18]. This makes is possible that the 3′ETS processing defect triggered by ISG20L2 knockdown is secondary to U8 incomplete maturation. However, we observed that the sole induction of mutant ISG20L2-Flag expression did not trigger accumulation of U8 precursors (Supplementary Fig. S2A and B), while 3′ETS processing was impaired in a similar manner as upon ISG20L2 knockdown (Fig. 3A), supporting the direct involvement of ISG20L2 in processing 3′ETS.

Landscape of the 3′ETS maturation analysed by 3′ RACE-seq

To gain further insight into the state of the 3′ETS in control and ISG20L2-depleted cells, we performed 3′ RACE-seq [36] with two primers, spanning either nucleotides 166–186 in the 3′ETS (primer #1), or the 28S 3′ end and the first seven nucleotides of the 3′ETS (primer #2) (Fig. 5A). The 3′ extremities revealed by the sequencing reads obtained in control cells were distributed along the entire 3′ETS, confirming that this transcribed spacer is subjected to 3′–5′ exonucleolytic degradation (Fig. 5B and C). Moreover, the differences in the profiles obtained for control and ISG20L2-depleted cells supported a role of this 3′–5′ exoribonuclease in this process. With primer #1, we detected precursors bearing long 3′ETS extensions, such as those evidenced by northern blot with the 3′ETS probe (Figs 2A and B, and 3A). Reads were obtained down to nucleotide 373 in the 3′ETS (Fig. 5B), which is 8–19 nucleotides further than the 3′ ends of the 3′ETS reported previously [42, 43]. Interestingly, this putative limit to the 3′ETS coincides with the first nucleotide of the TCCG motif located ahead of the GGGTCGACCG sequence (Supplementary Fig. S9A), which has been identified as a termination motif in the human 3′ spacer [14, 42, 44]. Upon ISG20L2 depletion, accumulation of the reads between nucleotides 366 and 369 suggested that exonucleolytic trimming operates from the first nucleotides released after transcription termination. Compared to controls, the reads obtained from ISG20L2-depleted cells also showed a marked increase at positions 263 and 267, in a highly GC-rich stretch (nucleotides 249–279; >90% GC). Of note, the length of these 3′-ETS processing intermediates was consistent with their detection by northern blot with the 3′ETS probe, which spans nucleotides 206–245 (Supplementary Fig. S9A). Nevertheless, this analysis did not reflect the strong accumulation of long 3′-ETS processing intermediates observed by northern blot. Despite bringing decisive qualitative information in terms of position and tailing of maturation intermediates, 3′ RACE-seq also comes with limitations, such as a bias in favour of smaller amplicons, or the requirement for RNA targets with a free 3′OH extremity. Although a pre-treatment with T4 polynucleotide kinase to remove 3′ phosphate and 2′–3′ cyclic phosphates did not modify the PCR amplicon patterns, the presence of other modifications impeding the ligation of the primer cannot be excluded.

With primer #2, which hybridizes on the 28S-3′ETS junction, 93%–97% of the reads were restricted to the first 126 nucleotides of the 3′ETS (Fig. 5C). Control cells showed marked accumulations at positions 20, 116–117, and 121–125, all corresponding to short G-stretches (Supplementary Fig. S9A). Strikingly, short reads spanning up to nucleotide 20 accumulated massively in IGS20L2-depleted cells (Fig. 5C). This was also observed in nuclear extracts in which ISG20L2 knockdown was performed with different siRNA duplexes (Supplementary Fig. S3A). These data emphasize the critical role of ISG20L2 for the full removal of this segment and points to a role similar to S. cerevisiae Rex1-4 or D. melanogaster Rexo5 [18, 21, 22].

After ISG20L2 knockdown, the percentage of total reads bearing non-templated nucleotides in 3′ increased drastically compared to control cells (Fig. 5D and Supplementary Fig. S4). The non-templated extensions were predominantly A-rich in ISG20L2-depleted cells. This was exacerbated for the reads obtained with primer #1 as the untemplated additions were mainly composed of 1–3 As (Fig. 5E). With primer #2, >10% of the extensions found in ISG20L2-depleted cells were either exclusively composed of Us, or U-rich (Fig. 5D), although nuclear uridylation has only been reported to be part of U6 snRNA maturation process so far [45]. Similar results were obtained upon 3′ RACE-seq analysis of nuclear fractions (Supplementary Fig. S3). The distribution of the non-templated A-tails followed the same trend as the percentage of reads per position (Supplementary Figs S3 and S4). This pleads for a role of these non-templated additions in 3′ETS processing, such as priming ISG20L2 in order to progress through GC-rich patches prone to establish secondary structures.

Similar observations were made from profiles obtained from HeLa Flp-In T-Rex cells, treated with a scramble siRNA duplex, or the siISG20L2-4 duplex (Supplementary Figs S5A and S6A). Combining siRNA depletion with expression of mut-ISG20L2-Flag led to similar patterns as siISG20L2-4-treated cells without doxycycline induction. Remarkably, siRNA-driven depletion of DDX51, URB1, or RPL3, which also led to accumulation of 3′ETS-extended precursors (Supplementary Fig. S2C), displayed different 3′RACE-seq profiles compared to those obtained after impairing ISG20L2. Although the distributions of the reads were altered compared to control cells, short reads spanning up to nucleotide 20 did not accumulate as observed in IGS20L2-depleted cells (Supplementary Fig. S6A). Moreover, reads bearing short extensions of untemplated As only accumulated upon ISG20L2 impairment (Supplementary Fig. S6B). When wt-ISG20L2-Flag was expressed in siISG20L2-4-treated cells, the 3′RACE-seq profile still showed some remaining 3′ETS-extended precursors, indicating that rescue was not complete. On the contrary, depletion of RPL26, which affects cleavage at site 2 without affecting 3′ETS removal, produced 3′RACE-seq profiles similar to those of control cells. Altogether, these data support a specific role of ISG20L2 in 3′ETS removal.

Depletion of ISG20L2 defines the limits of 21S and 21S-C pre-rRNAs

3′ RACE-seq experiments were also performed on total or nuclear RNAs with probes in the ITS1 spanning either nucleotides 786–801 (primer #3) or the conserved C-region (nucleotides 605–628; primer #4) in order to analyse 21S-C and 21S 3′ ends, respectively (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Figs S7 and S8). In control cells, the reads were distributed at most positions along the ITS1, consistent with exonucleolytic trimming (Fig. 6B). In ISG20L2-depleted cells analysed with primer #3, reads ending at position 952 strongly accumulated (Fig. 6B), a feature also prominent in nuclear extracts (Supplementary Fig. S7A) and in HeLa Flp-In T-Rex cells (Supplementary Fig. S8A). These results suggest that RMRP cleavage occurs between nucleotides 952 and 953 in vivo, two nucleotides upstream of the site previously identified by in vitro mapping [23]. Of note, position 952 did not correspond to the longest reads, as our primers could also hybridize to early precursors containing ITS1 not-yet cleaved at site 2 (e.g. 36S, 41S, or 43S), but the cumulated number of reads between nucleotides 953 and 1095 (ITS1 3′ end) was <0.4%. The accumulation of reads ending at nucleotide 952 suggests that ISG20L2 processes the ITS1 immediately after endonucleolytic cleavage at site 2.

In contrast with the high proportion of reads at position 952 in ISG20L2-depleted cells, reads in control cells peaked between nucleotides 869 and 874 in control cells. Interestingly, this pattern reflected the northern blot results showing that the 21S band in ISG20L2-depleted cells ran slightly above its counterpart in control cells (Fig. 1B; 5′ITS1 probe). These observations suggest that the 21S pre-rRNA detected by northern blot in control cells has already undergone partial exonucleolytic processing.

With a probe hybridizing inside the C-region (primer #4), shorter reads accumulated between positions 629 and 710 (Fig. 6C). The distribution profiles attested of the strong heterogeneity of 21S-C pre-rRNA 3′ ends. Despite the very low level of 21S-C pre-rRNAs observed by northern blot in ISG20L2-depleted cells, reads corresponding to these precursors were evidenced by 3′ RACE-seq, possibly due to uncomplete depletion of the enzyme under our experimental conditions. However, the proportion of the shortest reads (628–660) was significantly lower in ISG20L2-depleted cells relative to controls (P < .005, F-test, quasi-binomial distribution), suggesting a defect in exonucleolytic progression. Reads ending at position 952 were also detected with primer #4, confirming the results obtained with primer #3 (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S7B). Our analyses also showed that in both conditions, around 10%–20% of the reads were bearing non-templated nucleotides, which were mainly mono-A or short A-rich sequences (Fig. 6D and E). As for the 3′ETS, addition of non-templated As could be an intrinsic step of the exonucleolytic processing facilitating ISG20L2 progression through this highly GC-rich segment (>82% GC). However, we did not observe increased adenylation of these precursors in ISG20L2-depleted cells.

Loss of ISG20L2 catalytic activity alters nucleolar organization

Given ISG20L2’s role in rRNA processing, we examined pre-rRNA distribution by FISH in cells in which doxycycline-induced expression of mutant H322A ISG20L2-Flag protein was combined to the depletion of the endogenous enzyme after treatment with the siISG20L2-4 duplex (Fig. 7A and B, respectively). Observation of optical sections showed a drastic reorganization of pre-rRNA distribution inside the nucleoli compared to control cells, with strongly labelled, ring-shaped structures reminiscent of dense fibrillar component (DFC) surrounding the fibrillar centres (FCs), where transcriptionally-competent rDNA genes are located [46, 47]. Both the ITS2 and ITS1 signals (respectively in green and red in insets) were mainly restricted to these small spherical areas, while labelling in the rest of the nucleolar compartment seemed decreased upon ISG20L2 inactivation, in line with an early processing defect. These alterations were accompanied by changes in the global shape of the nucleoli, whose delineations were irregular and elongated rather than rounded.

These findings were further supported by a closer examination of the nucleolar sub-compartments on counterstained ultrathin sections by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 8). In control HeLa cells, three distinct morphological components were identified (Fig. 8A): (i) the FCs, the spherical structures in which lay the transcriptionally competent rDNA genes; (ii) the DFC, a tightly packed fibrillar structure surrounding the FCs corresponding to neosynthesized primary transcripts that undergo early processing steps; (iii) the granular component (GC) surrounding the DFC, which contains granules 20–25 nm in diameter corresponding to pre-ribosomal particles (predominantly pre-60S) undergoing later maturation steps. Upon treatment with ISG20L2-4 siRNA duplex or doxycycline-induced expression of mutant H322A ISG20L2-Flag protein, the nucleolar sub-compartment organization was deeply altered (Fig. 8B and D): the DFC was frequently interrupted around the FCs, and some lacunas were evidenced throughout the GC. Combining expression of the catalytically dead ISG20L2-Flag protein with depletion of endogenous ISG20L2 amplified these defects (Fig. 8E). The lacunas increased in size and were more prominent at the border with the peripheral DFC (PDFC). The DFC was more discontinuous around the FCs, or even absent in large areas. On the contrary, expression of the wild-type ISG20L2-Flag protein in ISG20L2-depleted cells rescued in part this phenotype (Fig. 8C).

Altogether, these data indicate that ISG20L2 catalytic activity is of paramount importance to maintain nucleolar morphology, further supporting the conclusion that this enzyme is crucial for early pre-rRNA processing steps.

Discussion

The present data uncover a role for ISG20L2 at two distinct steps of pre-rRNA processing, thus contributing to the maturation of the 3′ ends of the 18S and 28S rRNAs. In addition, we show that ISG20L2 probably contributes to the final steps of snoRNA U8 maturation. We provide a map of ITS1, 3′ETS, and U8 processing at nucleotide resolution. First, our results show that ISG20L2 is required for initiating ITS1 removal from 18S rRNA precursors immediately after cleavage at site 2. 3′ RACE-seq analyses allowed us to map this cleavage site between nucleotides 952 and 953, which is consistent with but not identical to former results obtained in vitro [23]. Our results show that ISG20L2 is required to initiate 21S pre-rRNA trimming up to the border of the C-region. This sequence, highly-conserved in mammals, could be a binding site for assembly factor(s), which may in turn form a physical barrier impeding exonuclease progression as previously hypothesized [28]. Our 3′ RACE-seq data with primer #4 revealed that there is no strict 3′ extremity for 21S-C pre-rRNAs but rather heterogeneous ends spanning up to nucleotide 710, consistent with the fuzzy band defining this pre-rRNA on northern blots. These data show unambiguously that ISG20L2 is essential for ITS1 exonucleolytic processing.

Second, we propose that ISG20L2 contributes to the maturation of the 3′ extremity of the 28S rRNA right after transcription termination. Contrary to other eukaryotic model organisms, little is known about 3′ETS removal in human cells. Based on work in mouse [48], it is believed that the release of the mature 3′ end of the human 28S rRNA (annotated as site 02 in Supplementary Fig. S1) takes place just after cleavage at site A′ in 5′ETS. In yeast S. cerevisiae, the release of the primary transcript occurs co-transcriptionally and is operated by cleavage in the 3′ETS segment at site B0 by the endonuclease Rnt1p [49, 50]. Rnt1p cleaves at opposite sides of a hairpin structure formed within the 3′ETS, leaving a 14 nucleotide extension at the 3′ end of the 25S rRNA [50]. The guanine nucleotide in the tetraloop 5′-AGNN-3′ is essential for the precise positioning of the double-stranded RNA-binding domain of Rnt1p, which functions together with the enzyme N-terminal domain as rulers measuring precisely the distance between the tetraloop and the cleavage site [51]. The S. pombe Rnt1p ortholog Pac1 endonuclease also cleaves the 3′ETS on both sides of a hairpin, thus leaving a 41-nucleotide long residual extension [52]. Moreover, Pac1 was also shown to remove the remaining 3′ETS segment in vitro in the presence of RAC, the ribosome assembly chaperone complex [53]. A similar mechanism might exist in plants, as the RNase III-like endonuclease AtRTL2 has been proposed to cleave the rRNA transcript 3′ end in Arabidopsis thaliana [54]. In contrast, knockdown of the human Rnt1 ortholog Drosha does not impair 47S pre-rRNA processing, nor do genetic knockouts in mouse and D. melanogaster [55–57]. Although the release of the 3′ terminus of the human primary transcript remains undescribed, the end of the longest reads obtained here by 3′ RACE-seq (i.e. 373 nucleotides) coincides with the first termination motif present in the human 3′ transcribed spacer. This suggests that ISG20L2 trims the 3′ETS from the first nucleotide released after transcription termination without prior internal endonucleolytic cleavage of this sequence. Our data also suggest that its activity is required for the full removal of the major part if not the entire 3′ETS, as attested by the accumulation of precursors bearing short 3′ extension after ISG20L2 knockdown, which does not exclude the participation of other endo- or exoribonucleolytic activities in this process.

In human cells, the absence of 3′ETS in early precursors (including the 45S pre-rRNA) indicates that its removal precedes the cleavages within ITS1 and ITS2. Although structural data regarding this early step are still lacking, cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) has provided high-resolution snapshots of eukaryotic pre-ribosomal subunit assembly, highlighting in particular early nucleolar stages that can be confronted to the genetic and biochemical data obtained so far [58, 59]. Cryo-EM studies performed on affinity-purified pre-60S particles have deciphered early steps of the sequential assembly of 25S/5.8S rRNAs in S. cerevisiae and shown how distant structural domains are folded to bring together the 5′ end (5.8S rRNA and 25S domain I) and the 3′ end (25S domain V/VI) [60–62]. The Npa1 complex and the snoRNA snR190, which base-pair with the root helices of domains I and V of the 25S rRNA, are key players in tethering these domains. In baker’s yeast, the RNA helicase Dbp7 is then required for the release of the Npa1 complex and the snoRNA snR30, and subsequent repositioning of Rpl3/uL3 to domain VI, close to the 3′ end of the 25S rRNA. This step is believed to facilitate subsequent recruitment of A3 factors (the equivalent of the complex required for cleavage at site 2 in human cells) [63]. Along this line, we propose that deficient removal of the 3′ETS in human may block these early processing steps, thereby hampering cleavage at site 2. Indeed, our 3′-RACEseq results show that lack of ISG20L2 or expression of a catalytically-dead mutant provoked a marked accumulation of rRNA precursors bearing up to 20 nucleotides of the 3′ETS, indicating that ISG20L2 plays an essential role in the final steps of 3′ETS removal. In a recent report, iCLIP experiments performed on early human pre-60S particles showed base pairing between the first nucleotides in the 3′ETS and some nucleotides of 28S rRNA 3′ end [64]. Degradation of the first nucleotides of the 3′ETS by ISG20L2 may release the mature 3′ end of the 28S rRNA, as described for Rexo5 in D. melanogaster [18], thus allowing repositioning and subsequent anchoring of RPL3/uL3. Consistently, ISG20L2 was found to interact with the early ribosomal biogenesis factors PPAN/SSF1 and SURF6/RRP14 [65, 66], which are involved in early steps of 60S maturation [6].

The vertebrate-specific U8/SNORD118 has been suggested to play a similar role as yeast snR190 [67]. Several reports have shown the importance of U8 for the folding of 5.8S and 28S rRNAs and the coordination of ITS2 and 3′ETS maturation. The 5′ domain of U8 was initially proposed to hybridize with the 5′ region of the 28S rRNA [16], while contacts between U8 and the 3′ end of the 28S were later identified in high-throughput cross-linking studies [68]. Indeed, snoRNA U8/SNORD118 depletion in human cells leads to defective pre-rRNAs bearing 3′ETS extensions [69], similar to what had been reported previously upon U8 depletion in Xenopus lævis [17]. Contrary to ISG20L2, U8 also plays an essential role in ITS2 cleavage as revealed by absence of 12S pre-rRNA in U8-depleted cells, which affected much more strongly 28S rRNA formation than 18S rRNA. In murine cells, both the DEAD-box helicase Ddx51 and the GTPase Nog1 promote U8 release from the 5.8S-28S rRNA hybrid, which is found in the structure of the large ribosomal subunit [70, 71], similar to what has been described for the RNA helicase Dbp7 and Nog1 in yeast [63, 72, 73]. Catalytic mutants of Nog1 or Ddx51 led to phenotypes similar to those obtained upon U8 deletion [71, 74]. Our results indicate that ISG20L2 depletion with siRNAs partially alters U8 snoRNA 3′ processing, resulting in the accumulation of U8 forms extended of 1–4 templated nucleotides. This opens the interesting possibility that ISG20L2 is directly involved in U8 maturation. Importantly, U8 mutations resulting in longer 3′ETS have been associated to leukoencephalopathy with calcifications and cysts (LCC) and shown to result in U8 loss of function. The additional nucleotides may generate base-paring of the 3′ end with the 5′ sequence complementary to the 28S rRNA that is essential to U8 function [75]. Therefore, the disruption of 3′ETS maturation observed in ISG20L2-depleted cells might be due in part to the partial loss of U8 function. However, this pre-rRNA processing defect was also observed when overexpressing the ISG20L2 catalytic mutant, which does not affect U8 maturation but alters drastically 3′ETS removal. This shows that ISG20L2 activity in 3′-ETS processing can be uncoupled from its potential function in U8 maturation.

A recent study has demonstrated the critical role of the early assembly factor URB1 (the human ortholog of baker’s yeast Npa1p [72]) for U8/SNORD118 binding and proper folding of the 3′ end of the 28S rRNA [64]. Interestingly, this work used super-resolution SIM microscopy to locate URB1 relative to 3′ETS, which led the authors to hypothesize that the removal of the 3′ETS was taking place at the DFC–PDFC boundary. This finding is in agreement with our TEM data, showing that depletion and/or overexpression of catalytically dead ISG20L2 strongly disrupts the connection between the PDFC and the GC. Interestingly, co-IP experiments with Flag-ISG20L2 have evidenced several RPLs and assembly factors involved in these early steps [34], strongly suggesting that ISG20L2 may be a component of these pre-ribosomal assembly complexes in human cells.

The present study reports the involvement of ISG20L2, which belongs to the superfamily of DEDDh RNase T 3′–5′ exonucleases, in the removal of 3′ETS and ITS1 in human cells. Other family members are involved in similar processes, such as the closely related 3′–5′ exoRNase Rexo5 for in the 3′ end maturation of 28S rRNA of D. melanogaster [18], or Rex1p for the 3′ETS of S. cerevisiae [21]. ISG20L2 has been recently shown to be a non-sequence-specific exonuclease, prone to digest highly GC-rich human transcribed spacers in vitro [35]. Further work will be needed to understand fully transcription termination in human cells, to uncover potential additional steps in 3′ETS removal, and to establish whether other RNases contribute to 3′ETS and ITS1 removal.
