Patient-centered communication in cosmetic eyelid surgery consultations in China: an observational transcript-based E5 study
Authors: Fan Wang, Ruth Y Y Hung, Ningshen Wang, Peng Zhang, Hailin Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElective cosmetic procedures are preference-sensitive encounters in which satisfaction depends on aligning clinically feasible options with patient’s aesthetic goals and expectations. Yet, in high-throughput outpatient services, the extent to which patient-centered communication is operationalized in real consultations remains under-documented.MethodsWe conducted an observational, transcript-based mixed qualitative and descriptive-quantitative study of 50 audio-recorded consultations for double-eyelid blepharoplasty and medial epicanthoplasty at a tertiary oculoplastics center in Shanghai, China. Consultations were transcribed verbatim, and doctors’ talk was segmented into minimal intonation units and coded to the E5 framework (Engage, Educate, Empathize, Enable, Enlist). We used thematic qualitative analysis and descriptive quantitative summaries of speaking-time distribution and strategy-specific time to characterize how communication functions were distributed and enacted in routine service delivery.ResultsConversations averaged 10.8 min; doctors accounted for 85% of speaking time. Educational talk dominated doctor’s E5-coded time (66.6%), driven primarily by step-by-step procedural explanations and brief visual demonstrations. In contrast, relational and participatory strategies were rare: Engage≈1.5% of E5 time, Empathize 3.6% (combined), and Enlist 1.7%. Background

Cosmetic eyelid surgery sits at a basic tension within elective care: patients seek aesthetic change, but clinicians must translate that desire into outcomes that are anatomically feasible, medically responsible, and acceptable to the patient. In China, requests for procedures such as double-eyelid blepharoplasty and medial epicanthoplasty are shaped by both personal aspirations and broader aesthetic norms [1, 2]. The consultation is therefore not a simple preoperative briefing. It is the point at which desire, feasibility, risk, and expectation must be brought into workable alignment, often under the practical constraints of high-volume outpatient care [3, 4].

For this reason, communication is not ancillary to quality in cosmetic consultations; it is one of its central conditions. Patient-centered care (PCC) emphasizes responsiveness to patients’ needs, values, and preferences [5], while shared decision-making (SDM) stresses the alignment of clinical judgment with what matters to the patient [6, 7]. In preference-sensitive procedures, satisfaction depends not only on technical outcome but also on whether expectations are elicited, realistic options are discussed, and patients are supported in making informed choices [8–11]. Previous research likewise suggests that trust, satisfaction, and decision quality are strengthened when clinicians communicate clearly, manage expectations effectively, and respond to patients’ emotional concerns with empathy and reassurance [12–14].

To examine how this work is actually accomplished in consultation talk, this study uses the E5 framework, which extends the classic E4 model by adding Enable to Engage, Empathize, Educate, and Enlist [15, 16]. Together, these strategies provide a practical framework for identifying how clinicians build rapport, address emotions, explain procedures, support follow-through, and invite patient participation [15–18]. The addition of Enable is especially relevant in complex, high-throughput settings, where logistical guidance and procedural navigation form part of patient-centered communication itself [15]. In cosmetic eyelid surgery consultations, where outcomes are subjective and preference alignment is critical, the E5 model offers a useful way to examine whether consultation time is concentrated primarily in information delivery or distributed more evenly across relational and participatory functions [6, 7, 11].

Despite the recognized importance of PCC and SDM in elective care, little is known about how these communicative functions are distributed and enacted in real cosmetic eyelid surgery consultations in high-volume Chinese tertiary practice. Using a mixed qualitative and descriptive-quantitative design, this study examines oculoplastic surgeons’ communication in 50 audio-recorded cosmetic eyelid surgery consultations from a tertiary hospital in Shanghai through the E5 framework. Specifically, it combines thematic analysis of E5-coded consultation talk with descriptive summaries of speaking-time distribution and strategy-specific time to characterize the communication profile that emerges in routine service delivery. In doing so, it clarifies where patient-centered communication is concentrated, where it is compressed, and how consultation practice might be better aligned with the communicative demands of preference-sensitive aesthetic care.

Methods

Data collection

This study examined communication strategies in cosmetic eyelid surgery consultations through an observational analysis of real-life doctor-patient interactions collected from the Oculoplastics Department at Ninth People’s Hospital in Shanghai, a grade-A tertiary hospital with recognized strengths in plastic surgery and oculoplastic care. This site was selected as an analytically important public-hospital case within Chinese cosmetic care because, although private hospitals have expanded rapidly in China, public hospitals remain central to healthcare utilization [19]; however, it was not intended to represent all cosmetic-surgery settings, including private aesthetic clinics.

To ensure ethical compliance, informed consent was obtained from all participants. At the start of each consultation, doctors requested verbal consent to audio-record the conversation, explaining the study’s purpose, confidentiality measures, and patients’ right to withdraw at any time. Recordings were made only for patients who verbally consented, in a closed outpatient consultation (Room 1) over a six-month period. Four oculoplastic surgeons—two attending physicians, one associate professor, and one professor—whose clinical focus was cosmetic eyelid surgery participated. During revision, years of clinical practice were directly confirmed with the participating clinicians. To preserve participant anonymity, these data are reported in aggregate in Table 1 as descriptive contextual information about clinician seniority and were not used for inferential analysis. Cosmetic consultations at this hospital are self-pay under the standard outpatient tariff; individual fee amounts were not collected. Although more than 50 eligible patients were seen during this period, some declined participation after the project was briefly explained. Demographic information was not prospectively collected in full for this observational dataset; accordingly, only the available participant and consultation-context characteristics, including patient sex as well as clinician rank and specialty, are reported in Table 1. The analytic dataset therefore comprised the 50 eligible and consenting consultations available during the study period. Because this was an observational dataset derived from routine recorded consultations rather than a prospectively designed demographic survey, participant characteristics were limited to the variables retrievable from study records; these available characteristics are now reported in full in Table 1.

Each of the 50 consultations was audio-recorded in full, capturing the natural flow of interactions. All the recordings were transcribed verbatim using a hybrid approach that combined MATLAB’s Pretrained Speaker Diarization System and WPS AI transcription1. Following this automated step, two researchers manually verified the accuracy of all 50 transcripts, correcting speaker attributions and lexical errors where necessary; accordingly, each transcript underwent two separate manual checks.

To maintain confidentiality and data integrity, audio files, and transcripts were stored on encrypted drives with access restricted to the research team. All identifying information was removed to safeguard participant anonymity.

Data analysis

This study employed a mixed qualitative and descriptive-quantitative analytic design to examine doctors’ communication strategies in cosmetic eyelid surgery consultations. The primary aim was to characterize how communication functions were distributed and enacted in routine practice, rather than to test a pre-specified statistical hypothesis. Accordingly, the quantitative component was framed as descriptive only and was used to summarize communication patterns in the recorded consultations. Formal inferential testing was not the analytic objective of this study, and an a priori power calculation was therefore not applicable to the revised quantitative approach.

To strengthen methodological integrity, we made the composition and qualifications of the analytic team explicit. The qualitative analysis was conducted by two E5-trained researchers with backgrounds in linguistics, health communication, medical humanities, and qualitative communication analysis. One researcher led transcript segmentation, E5 coding, and thematic interpretation, while the second researcher independently coded the data after calibration and contributed to interpretive validation. Researchers with methodological and technical expertise supported the analytic workflow and validation procedures. In addition, practicing oculoplastic surgeons with experience in cosmetic eyelid surgery reviewed the coded outputs and linked excerpts to assess clinical plausibility, specialist terminology, and possible diarization or transcription errors. This interdisciplinary structure allowed communicative, methodological, and clinical interpretations to be cross-checked throughout the analysis. Coding decisions were anchored in a shared codebook and explicit decision rules, discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, and clinically trained reviewers assessed the plausibility of coded excerpts and interpretations.

The first stage involved a detailed review of 50 doctor-patient consultation transcripts collected from a leading public hospital in Shanghai. Through iterative analysis grounded in the E5 model (Engage, Enable, Enlist, Educate, Empathize), key themes and patterns in communication strategies were identified. Initial observations were documented to guide development of a coding framework. We segmented doctors’ talk into minimal intonation units (boundary = audible pause ≥ 0.5 s or syntactic completion), reviewed in the context of the immediately adjacent turns. Each unit received one E5 label reflecting its central communicative intent (decision tree in Appendix A). When a unit plausibly served more than one function, coders applied the central-intent rule, giving priority to the action made most salient by morphosyntax and turn design (e.g., interrogatives/enlistment prompts over background explanation); ambiguities were resolved by consensus.

Before independent double-coding, the two E5-trained researchers completed a joint calibration process involving codebook review, pilot coding, and discrepancy resolution. The final codebook specifies operational definitions, inclusion/exclusion cues, and illustrative examples for each E5 category (Table 2; Appendix A). Agreement computed prior to adjudication met accepted thresholds (Krippendorff’s α = 0.83 overall; category-wise Cohen’s κ = 0.76–0.90).2 We used single-label coding to estimate the relative deployment of distinct communicative actions, requiring explicit affiliative cues for Engage/Empathize to avoid category inflation (Appendix A for decision rules and examples). To assess the potential impact of this choice, a stratified 10-consultation sensitivity audit permitted multi-label coding on re-coding: only 7% of units required more than one label, time-share differences between the single-label and multi-label schemes were ≤ 0.8% points for Enlist and Enable, indicating minimal impact on substantive results. Thus, although single-label coding necessarily privileges a primary communicative function, the sensitivity audit suggests that multifunctionality at this segmentation level was limited and unlikely to have altered the substantive interpretation of the overall communication profile.

Within Educate, offering visual aids and explaining procedure steps were the two largest sub-strategies. Offering visual aids averaged 173.02 s per consultation and accounted for 36.54% of doctors’ E5-coded time, while explaining procedure steps averaged 164.28 s and accounted for 32.48%. By comparison, setting realistic expectations averaged 61.44 s (13.38%), and negotiating aesthetic preferences averaged 31.26 s (7.77%). Offering visual aids alone was equivalent to approximately 26.6% of the mean total consultation length. Qualitative analysis of these educational turns showed that they commonly operated through clinician-led visual demonstration and step-by-step procedural explanation. These turns also frequently framed which surgical outcomes were presented as feasible or acceptable within the consultation.

The remaining E5 categories occupied substantially smaller proportions of doctors’ E5-coded time. In Engage, building rapport averaged 3.90 s (0.88%), and clarifying patient preferences averaged 2.60 s (0.65%). Discussion

The central finding of this study is that cosmetic eyelid surgery consultations in this high-volume tertiary public setting were efficient and information-rich, yet only weakly participatory. Doctors’ talk centered on procedural explanation and visual demonstration, while preference elicitation, emotional acknowledgement, and patient input remained comparatively compressed. In preference-sensitive aesthetic care, this imbalance matters because consultation quality depends not only on whether patients receive clear information, but also on whether their goals, concerns, expectations, and acceptable trade-offs are actively elicited and negotiated. It also matters because clinical explanation in cosmetic consultations may do more than convey information: it can help define what appears feasible, appropriate, and desirable. The discussion therefore focuses on one central implication: patient-centered cosmetic consultation requires protected moments of participation within, rather than instead of, efficient clinical explanation.

Instructional dominance and compressed participation in cosmetic consultations

The communication profile identified in this study shows a clear imbalance in how consultation time was organized. Explanation and visual demonstration structured the encounter: clinicians set the agenda, defined the problem, and guided the sequence of talk, while patients occupied a narrower interactional position centered mainly on receiving, confirming, and responding to the clinician’s account. This does not mean that educational talk is unnecessary. In cosmetic eyelid surgery, patients need clear explanations of anatomy, procedural steps, risks, limitations, and feasible outcomes. However, in elective cosmetic care, decision quality also depends on the explicit articulation of patients’ aesthetic goals, anxieties, and acceptable trade-offs [4, 5, 7, 9]. When explanation dominates the consultation, these other patient-centered functions become easier to compress.

In this setting, educational communication carries both informational and evaluative force. Visual demonstration and procedural explanation make the surgical plan legible and actionable, and they also stabilize the clinician’s framing of what the problem is, what counts as feasible, and which concerns deserve priority. Earlier work on doctor-patient communication shows that extended explanatory turns can leave concerns insufficiently explored when they are not punctuated by preference checks or invitations to respond [22, 23]. In cosmetic consultations, the consequences run deeper because explanation is closely bound up with evaluation. To explain what can be done is also to shape what becomes thinkable, reasonable, and desirable. Educational talk therefore both delivers information and orders relevance: procedural feasibility becomes highly visible, while the patient’s own aesthetic reasoning receives less interactional space [11, 24].

One way to describe this interactional pattern is “infer, inform, defer.” Surgeons working in high-volume tertiary cosmetic practice repeatedly encounter familiar requests and familiar beauty scripts circulating across media, commerce, and everyday appearance culture [25–30]. Under these conditions, clinicians may infer a likely preference, inform the patient of the feasible plan, and defer final choice to a brief closing space for later consideration. This sequence preserves efficiency and procedural clarity, which are important in high-volume tertiary practice. Yet it also narrows the space in which patient preferences can be made explicit, clarified, revised, or jointly examined. From the perspective of patient-centered care and shared decision-making, the issue is therefore not insufficient information, but insufficient participation within information delivery [5–7, 30].

This pattern has substantive consequences. PCC and SDM depend on explicit moments of preference elicitation, emotional acknowledgement, and deliberation among reasonable options [5, 6, 8]. These moments are where misaligned expectations, appearance-related anxiety, cost concerns, social pressure, and uncertainty about acceptable outcomes can become speakable. Prior work also shows that empathy and participatory invitations are core pathways through which communication builds trust and supports satisfactory decisions [7, 8, 12–14]. In the present data, however, these functions remained comparatively compressed. The result is a consultation style that can be informative and efficient while still giving limited room for the co-construction of preference, expectation, and choice.

This pattern is also reinforced by the institutional logic of high-volume tertiary public practice in China, where efficient, linear, and defensible explanation is often prioritized over slower and less predictable forms of relational exploration [31–35]. Improving communication in this setting therefore does not necessarily require long deliberative exchanges. It does, however, require brief and observable participatory moments: asking what outcome the patient hopes for, checking whether the proposed plan matches that goal, acknowledging anxiety about appearance or recovery, and inviting the patient to state what trade-offs are acceptable. Such moments would allow procedural education to remain efficient while making the patient’s own aesthetic reasoning more visible in the consultation. This compressed participatory structure also prepares the ground for the exercise of aesthetic authority, which the next section considers in a more focused way.

Beauty, normativity, and medical authority

Cosmetic surgery consultations occupy an ethically complex position in medicine. They are preference-sensitive encounters in which patients pursue aesthetic outcomes tied to self-image, but they are also normatively saturated settings in which beauty ideals are shaped by social, cultural, and commercial forces [24, 36]. This creates a central tension for the aesthetic practitioner: they must respect patient autonomy while also exercising professional judgment about what is feasible, safe, and appropriate. Read in this light, the communication pattern observed in the present study may be understood not simply as instructional dominance, but as a process through which clinical explanation can also carry aesthetic authority.

The concept of the “cosmetic gatekeeper” offers a useful but bounded interpretive lens here [24]. Aesthetic practitioners do not function only as technicians who implement patient wishes; rather, they work at a “medico-commercial juncture” where medical ethical principles, such as beneficence and non-maleficence, may intersect with the pressures of a consumer-oriented beauty market [24, 37]. In this context, visual demonstration, recommendation, and boundary-setting may do more than explain a procedure. They can also shape what the patient comes to regard as feasible, appropriate, or desirable. This authority is not necessarily problematic. Professional judgment is essential for preventing unrealistic, unsafe, or unsuitable procedures. The concern is rather that, when patient preferences are not explicitly elicited and negotiated, professional guidance may become the main force defining the aesthetic agenda.

The present study does not claim that clinicians imposed preferences on patients, nor can it determine how patients experienced these encounters. It does, however, show a communication pattern in which explanation and demonstration occupied far more space than preference elicitation and shared deliberation. This pattern suggests a process-level vulnerability: patients may receive clear procedural information while still having limited opportunity to articulate the personal meanings, concerns, and trade-offs attached to the desired change. Seen from this angle, the communicative issue in cosmetic consultations extends beyond ordinary doctor dominance. The problem is not simply that the doctor speaks more, but that explanation, demonstration, and recommendation may together define the boundaries of legitimate desire. Medical authority in cosmetic care may therefore shade into aesthetic authority, particularly when individual preferences are filtered through professional judgment that is itself entangled with wider commercial and cultural standards of beauty [11, 24, 26, 29]. This is why shared decision-making in cosmetic surgery requires some adaptation. In most clinical accounts, respecting autonomy means eliciting and honoring patient preferences [6]. In aesthetic medicine, however, preferences are socially shaped rather than purely individual, and clinicians may also have ethical reasons to question or redirect some requests [28, 36, 38]. The aim is not to treat aesthetic preference as either fully autonomous or wholly socially determined, but to show why preference-sensitive cosmetic care requires explicit communicative work through which patients’ aims can be voiced, examined, and negotiated. Aesthetic shared decision-making should therefore not mean simply accepting every stated preference, nor should it mean replacing patient preference with professional judgment. Rather, it requires a structured but brief process in which clinicians explain feasible options, invite patients to clarify what they hope to achieve, discuss limits and risks, and check whether the proposed plan matches the patient’s own priorities. In high-volume Chinese tertiary practice, such a model may be most feasible if it is built into short communication routines, such as preference prompts, expectation checks, and closing questions that invite patient input before the plan is finalized. This would preserve professional judgment while creating clearer space for patients to articulate, reflect on, and negotiate their own aesthetic aims in culturally specific contexts.

Limitations

Five limitations of this study should be noted. First, the dataset comprised 50 consultations from a single tertiary public hospital in Shanghai and did not include private cosmetic clinics, lower-tier hospitals, or less-developed regions. The findings should therefore be interpreted as characterizing one important institutional segment of cosmetic eyelid surgery practice rather than the full spectrum of cosmetic surgery consultations in China. At the same time, this setting remains analytically important because public hospitals continue to play a central role in Chinese healthcare utilization, and the study hospital is a grade-A tertiary institution with recognized strengths in plastic surgery. Nevertheless, the findings may reflect the specific pressures of a well-resourced metropolitan public hospital and may not transfer directly to other cosmetic-surgery settings. Relatedly, fuller patient-level contextual variables, such as age and consultation orientation or mindset, were not prospectively collected; accordingly, we could not examine whether communication patterns varied systematically across patient subgroups.

Second, reliance on audio recordings alone excluded non-verbal cues, such as facial expressions and body language, that are important to a fuller understanding of doctor-patient interactions. Given the sensitivity of cosmetic consultations, however, video recording posed practical and ethical challenges, which is why the present study relied on audio data only. Future work could incorporate video-based or multimodal analysis where feasible.

Third, because each minimal intonation unit was assigned one primary E5 label, multifunctional utterances may have been simplified and secondary functions may have been undercounted. However, our micro-segmentation strategy reduced this risk, and a stratified 10-consultation sensitivity audit that permitted multi-label coding found that only 7% of units required more than one label, with only small differences between the single-label and multi-label schemes, suggesting limited impact on the main findings.

Fourth, no patient-reported outcome or experience measures were collected. Accordingly, although the present analysis identifies communication patterns and process-level vulnerabilities in cosmetic eyelid surgery consultations, it cannot determine how patients themselves experienced these encounters, or whether the observed interactional patterns were associated with perceived involvement, satisfaction, trust, decisional conflict, post-consultation regret, or other aspects of patient experience.

Fifth, this study did not collect clinicians’ own reflections on shared decision-making, consultation goals, or communication constraints. Accordingly, although the interactional analysis identifies limited participatory and enlistment practices in the recorded consultations, it cannot determine how clinicians themselves understood shared decision-making in this setting or to what extent these communication patterns reflected deliberate professional judgment, institutional time pressure, medico-legal concerns, or locally normalized consultation routines.

Conclusion

This single-center study in a high-volume tertiary oculoplastic service in Shanghai shows a marked imbalance in communication during cosmetic eyelid surgery consultations, with procedural education dominating the encounter and relational and participatory strategies receiving comparatively little space. This pattern appears to reflect not only individual communicative choices, but also the structural and institutional conditions of high-throughput outpatient care, where short visits, heavy patient volumes, and hierarchical clinical norms favor efficient information delivery over sustained preference elicitation and shared deliberation. In elective, preference-sensitive care, however, such compression is not trivial, because decisions depend not only on technical feasibility but also on the articulation of personal aesthetic goals, emotional concerns, and acceptable trade-offs. Strengthening communication in this setting will therefore require both workflow redesign and targeted training that protect brief but meaningful opportunities for expectation alignment, emotional acknowledgement, and patient input within the consultation itself. In this sense, rebalancing cosmetic eyelid surgery consultations toward protected moments of elicitation, empathy, and enlistment is not merely an interpersonal refinement, but a practical quality-of-care priority in preference-sensitive aesthetic medicine.

Practice implications

The findings of this study point to several practical steps for improving communication in Chinese cosmetic eyelid surgery consultations and better aligning with PCC principles. From a technical perspective, adopting advanced simulation technologies or digital imaging systems can help patients visualize potential post-surgery outcomes [39]. Short, reusable animations illustrating key procedural steps can likewise reduce repetitive explanation. Paired with plain-language summaries and a brief teach-back (“Could you tell me how you understand the plan?”), these tools can streamline information exchange, enhance comprehension, and create space for patient input.

On the humanistic side, it is critical to expand communication-focused training within medical curricula beyond biomedical content. Incorporating modules on empathy, rapport-building, and active listening equips practitioners to better address patients’ emotional as well as technical concerns. Clinical teams can also use the E5 model as a structured workflow—e.g., a brief “E5 checklist” that prompts Engage/Empathize before Educate, and Enlist/Enable before closing—to keep patient-centered moves visible within time-limited visits.

At the systemic level, capacity and workflow adjustments can free time for participation: longer or protected first-visit slots for elective cases; team-based roles (e.g., nurse educators or counsellors for pre-visit materials and post-visit logistics); and pre-visit preference-elicitation forms or decision aids delivered through patient portals. Clear quality standards and oversight for cosmetic surgery services may also reduce pressure on tertiary public clinics, enabling more attention to engagement and shared decision-making. Together, these interventions support a balance between technical proficiency and relational care, with the aim of improving the patient-centered quality of the consultation process.
