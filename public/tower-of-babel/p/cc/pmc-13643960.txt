Understanding the multidimensional barriers to treatment adherence among Iranian patients with hypertension: a qualitative content analysis
Authors: Mahmood Karimy, Hamid Abredari, Leila Taheri, Somayeh Khademi, Mohammad Taher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimHypertension is a prevalent chronic condition requiring consistent adherence to medications and lifestyle modifications. Despite its clinical importance, many patients struggle with treatment adherence (TA), and in-depth qualitative insights into how adherence barriers are experienced and interconnected in patients’ daily lives remain limited. This study aimed to explore barriers to TA among Iranian patients with hypertension.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted in 2025 using conventional content analysis. Twenty-three outpatients with hypertension attending the outpatient clinic of Shahid Modarres Hospital, Saveh, Iran, were purposefully recruited to maximize variation in age, gender, education, and disease duration. Data were collected through in-depth semi-structured interviews and analyzed following Graneheim and Lundman’s approach using MAXQDA 10. Data saturation was achieved when no new codes or themes emerged. Trustworthiness was ensured through independent coding by two researchers, member checking, reflexive journaling, and comprehensive documentation of the analytic process.FindingsFive interconnected categories of barriers to treatment adherence emerged: mental burnout, lack of effective education and communication, sociocultural influences, financial constraints, and behavioral and lifestyle-related challenges. The analysis revealed that these barriers did not operate independently; rather, they formed an interconnected system in which mental burnout, communication gaps, sociocultural pressures, financial hardship, and behavioral challenges reinforced one another, collectively undermining long-term treatment adherence.ConclusionTreatment adherence among Iranian patients with hypertension is shaped by an interconnected system of psychological, interpersonal, sociocultural, financial, and behavioral barriers rather than isolated individual factors. These findings provide a deeper understanding of patients’ lived experiences and may inform the development of culturally responsive, patient-centered strategies to support long-term treatment adherence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28508-5.Keywords: Hypertension, Content analysis, Qualitative study, Patients’ experiences

Introduction

Hypertension is a major global health challenge. It is usually asymptomatic and is known as the silent killer [1]. It develops due to a wide range of genetic, metabolic, and lifestyle-related factors and can be primary or secondary. Primary hypertension has no clear cause, while secondary hypertension is due to underlying conditions [2]. According to the statistics of the World Health Organization, more than 1.28 billion adults in the world suffer from hypertension, two-thirds of whom live in low- and middle-income countries. Further reports show that the number of people with hypertension exceeded 1.56 billion in 2025 [3]. Another report in 2024 revealed that almost 48% of adults in the United States suffered from hypertension [4]. According to the latest nationally representative STEPS survey in Iran, hypertension affects 32.0% of adults, and this prevalence is projected to remain stable through 2025, underscoring the persistent public health burden of hypertension in the country [5] .

Ineffective hypertension management has serious health outcomes and increases the risk of cardiac and renal diseases, stroke, and premature death [6]. Almost 9%–11% of patients with hypertension develop moderate to severe chronic kidney disease, and almost 40% of them experience cardiac complications [7, 8]. Moreover, 10%–15% of these patients experience stroke, so that hypertension is the main cause of more than half of the ischemic strokes and two-thirds of the hemorrhagic strokes [9].

Hypertension treatments include antihypertensive medications and lifestyle modifications like physical exercise, weight management, healthy eating, and sodium intake restriction [10]. These strategies help reduce blood pressure through improving vascular health, sympathetic activity, and physiological processes [11]. Nonetheless, studies show ineffective hypertension management [12, 13]. For example, a study showed that only 47% of men and 38% of women with hypertension take their medications, and only 18% of men and 23% of women have effective hypertension management [12].

Treatment adherence (TA) is one of the most important influential factors on effective hypertension management [14, 15]. TA includes behaviors and choices that are based on healthcare providers’ recommendations and are essential to successful disease control and complication prevention [16]. These behaviors and choices include regular medication intake, healthy eating, physical exercise, and attendance at follow-up sessions for hypertension management [17]. TA not only reduces blood pressure, but also prevents its cardiovascular and renal complications, stroke, and premature death, and thereby improves quality of life [18]. Nonetheless, most patients do not adhere to healthcare providers’ recommendations and have poor TA [19]. Poor TA can lead to different problems such as disease progression, frequent hospitalizations, reduced physical and cognitive abilities, increased risk of complications [18], and ineffective disease control1 [18, 20].

Various factors can influence patients’ TA. Two studies reported that factors such as treatment complexity, medication side effects, negative attitudes toward treatments, weak social support [19, 21], financial problems [19], and low motivation can reduce TA [21]. A study on 2578 patients with hypertension in Brazil also showed that most of them had poor TA due to age-related, ethnicity-related, and geographical factors [22]. Another study on 321 patients with hypertension in Indonesia also found poor TA among most of them and highlighted that practical and psychological factors influenced their TA [23]. A systematic review of studies published in 2014–2024 also reported personal, social, and healthcare system-related factors as the key determinants of TA [24].

Although previous studies have attempted to address influential factors on treatment adherence (TA), most have focused on demographic or clinical correlates rather than the complex, real-life experiences and contextual barriers faced by patients with hypertension [25]. Consequently, there remain limited in-depth data about the psychological, social, cultural, and practical challenges that patients encounter in their daily self-management of TA. Most previous investigations did not explore how these multifaceted factors interact to shape patients’ adherence behaviors or capture patients’ lived experiences in their sociocultural contexts. These gaps emphasize the necessity of more qualitative and in-depth explorations based on patients’ real-life experiences. Unlike survey-based studies that primarily identify the presence of adherence barriers, a qualitative approach enables exploration of how these barriers are experienced, interpreted, and interconnected within patients’ everyday lives. This perspective views treatment adherence as a dynamic and interconnected process shaped by multiple interacting psychological, social, cultural, and structural factors. Therefore, we designed and carried out this study to explore the barriers to TA among patients with hypertension.

Methods

This qualitative study was carried out in 2025 using conventional content analysis. A qualitative approach facilitated the in-depth exploration of the TA-related experiences and perceptions of patients with hypertension. The report of the study was presented based on the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [26]. The first author performed data collection and analysis. He had extensive experience in qualitative research and conducted the interviews in the role of a nursing faculty member and researcher, with no prior, clinical, or therapeutic relationship with the participants. Conventional content analysis was chosen because the study aimed to explore participants’ lived experiences without imposing preconceived categories, allowing codes and categories to emerge directly from the data rather than being guided by existing theory.

The study setting was Shahid Modarres Hospital, Saveh, Iran. Patients with hypertension were purposefully recruited for the study. Participants were selected using purposive sampling to ensure maximum variation in age, gender, education, duration of hypertension, and medication use patterns. The purposive sampling strategy enabled the inclusion of participants with diverse lived experiences, ensuring rich and in-depth insights into hypertension treatment adherence rather than statistical representativeness. Table 1 summarizes the sociodemographic characteristics, duration of hypertension, and medication use of the 23 participants, illustrating the maximum variation achieved through purposive sampling. Inclusion criteria were consent to participate, ability to communicate in Persian, a definite diagnosis of hypertension established by a cardiologist at least six months before the study, intake of at least one antihypertensive medication, no mental or cognitive disorders, and no other chronic illness. Patients with comorbid chronic diseases were excluded to minimize the influence of disease-specific treatment regimens and adherence challenges unrelated to hypertension, thereby allowing a more focused exploration of barriers specifically associated with hypertension treatment adherence. The only exclusion criterion was unilateral withdrawal from the study.

Probing questions such as “Can you explain more?” and “Do you have any example of this?” were also asked to collect more in-depth data. In total, 23 interviews were held and digitally recorded with participants’ verbal and written consent. Each interview lasted approximately 30–60 min, depending on participants’ willingness to elaborate on their experiences and the depth of the discussion. During the interviews, the interviewer also recorded brief field notes on participants’ nonverbal behaviors (e.g., pauses, emotional expressions, and tone of voice) to enrich data interpretation during the analysis. Data collection continued until data saturation was achieved, operationally defined as the point when no new codes or themes emerged from successive interviews. Saturation was observed after 21 interviews and confirmed with two additional interviews. Data saturation was considered achieved when consecutive interviews generated no new codes, subcategories, or conceptual insights, and the final two interviews confirmed the completeness of the emerging analytical framework.

Graneheim and Lundman’s conventional content analysis was employed to analyze the data simultaneously with data collection. Interviews were transcribed word by word, and meaning units were identified and coded. Coding was performed inductively. Codes were continuously compared within and across interviews, merged when conceptually similar, and progressively refined into subcategories and categories through an iterative analytical process.

The analysis went beyond descriptive coding and aimed to identify underlying patterns and relationships among categories to develop a conceptual understanding of treatment adherence. The data were managed using MAXQDA 10 software.

Trustworthiness was ensured by using the criteria of Lincoln and Guba [27]. Prolonged engagement with the data and member checking by participants helped maintain credibility. Two researchers independently coded the interviews, achieving a 92% inter-coder agreement (simple percentage agreement), calculated by comparing independently assigned codes and dividing the number of agreements by the total number of coded segments. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus to ensure dependability. In cases of disagreement, the researchers repeatedly reviewed the original transcripts and discussed alternative interpretations until consensus was reached. All coding decisions and analytic steps were documented to enhance confirmability.

Descriptions of the study context and participants’ characteristics facilitated transferability. Reflective journaling was employed to minimize bias. Researchers reflected on and documented their positionality, assumptions, and perceptions throughout the study. Both researchers were healthcare professionals familiar with hypertension care; one shared the same cultural background as participants, which enhanced communication but also required careful reflection to mitigate bias. Group sessions with co-authors were conducted throughout the analytic process as a form of peer debriefing, during which emerging codes and interpretations were critically discussed and compared with raw data to reduce the influence of researchers’ clinical assumptions and enhance analytic rigor. In addition, bracketing was applied through iterative memo-writing and peer debriefing sessions. During these sessions, preliminary interpretations were continuously compared with raw transcripts to ensure that coding decisions remained grounded in participants’ accounts rather than researchers’ clinical assumptions.

This study has the approval of the Ethics Committee of Saveh University of Medical Sciences, Saveh, Iran (code: IR.SAVEHUMS.REC.1404.016). The aim of the study was explained to the participants, the confidentiality of their data was guaranteed, and they were informed of their right to terminate their interviews at will. They also had access to psychological counseling if they felt stressed during the interviews.

Findings

Participants were 23 patients with hypertension from different age, gender, marital status, educational background, and employment status groups. Their mean age was 56 years. Twelve participants were male and sixteen were married. Their educational level varied from elementary to a doctoral degree (Table 1). The analysis identified five interrelated categories comprising eleven subcategories that shape treatment adherence among patients with hypertension: mental burnout, lack of effective education and communication, sociocultural influences, financial barriers, and behavioral and lifestyle-related challenges. These categories formed a dynamic and mutually reinforcing system influencing treatment adherence behaviors (Table 2).

The subcategories and categories of the barriers to treatment adherence among patients with hypertension

Preliminary analysis indicated that participants’ accounts reflected a wide range of lived experiences of hypertension and treatment adherence across all identified categories and subcategories, including psychological, communicational, sociocultural, financial, and behavioral domains. These experiences were shaped by different demographic contexts such as age, gender, and duration of illness and are presented as qualitative variations in lived experience, interpreted within a purposive and qualitative approach that did not aim at statistical comparison between groups. These findings indicate complex and context-dependent patterns of treatment adherence shaped by interacting psychological, social, and structural factors.

Mental burnout

In this study, mental burnout emerged as an illness-specific and condition-specific psychological process that extends beyond conventional constructs such as anxiety, depression, general psychological distress, or treatment fatigue. Unlike previously described emotional responses to chronic illness, mental burnout reflects a cumulative and dynamic experience arising from sustained engagement with hypertension self-management over time. It is characterized by the progressive depletion of emotional and cognitive resources, persistent disease-related worry, and a perceived loss of control, which together undermine patients’ motivation and engagement in treatment.

Importantly, mental burnout in this context is not an isolated psychological state but a relational and processual phenomenon shaped by the interaction between personal experience, treatment demands, and perceived social support. The findings suggest that repeated exposure to uncertainty regarding blood pressure control, combined with insufficient interpersonal understanding, gradually transforms initial concern into emotional exhaustion and disengagement from care. This process highlights how psychological burden in hypertension is not merely a reaction to illness, but a developing trajectory embedded within long-term self-management.

Accordingly, mental burnout can be conceptualized as a bridging construct linking emotional distress, reduced treatment motivation, and social withdrawal, thereby offering a more integrated understanding of psychological barriers to treatment adherence. This conceptualization adds to existing literature by shifting attention from static psychological states to a dynamic process through which chronic disease burden accumulates and translates into non-adherence behaviors.

Importantly, this process has direct implications for treatment adherence, as it may gradually weaken patients’ consistency in long-term self-management behaviors.

1Worry about the disease course and consequences

Patients with hypertension were consistently worried about the future course of their disease and its potential complications. Their awareness of long-term consequences, such as damage to vital organs like the kidneys, heart, and brain, contributed to persistent concern and anticipatory anxiety. Many participants tended to interpret common symptoms such as headache, dizziness, or weakness as signs of severe events like heart attack or stroke, reflecting a state of heightened vigilance toward bodily sensations. In addition, blood pressure fluctuations despite adherence to treatment were often perceived as a lack of control over the disease, which further intensified their worry. These experiences suggest that ongoing uncertainty and perceived unpredictability play a key role in maintaining chronic stress and disturbing mental peace, sometimes even aggravating physical symptoms.

I’m always worried about my blood pressure fluctuations, heart injuries, or heart attack. I can’t stop thinking about this, particularly when I develop a headache or dizziness (P. 12).

I often find myself preoccupied with how my blood pressure might affect my daily activities. Even small headaches or moments of dizziness make me uneasy and keep me thinking about potential complications (P. 18).

Reduced motivation to continue treatments

Most patients with hypertension experienced fatigue and reduced motivation to continue treatments, particularly when they did not perceive significant improvement or when they experienced medication side effects. Mental strain and constant worry appeared to exacerbate this condition. Rather than being a simple lack of adherence, this reduced motivation seemed to reflect a gradual disengagement resulting from cumulative psychological burden and frustration with long-term treatment demands. Previous negative experiences and fear of medication side effects also created uncertainty and further weakened patients’ willingness to continue treatment, sometimes leading to reduced medication intake or discontinuation. These findings indicate that diminished motivation and treatment adherence are closely linked to emotional and cognitive responses to living with a chronic condition.

At times I feel completely drained by the long-term treatment. When side effects appear or when my condition does not change, I lose motivation and may skip doses without planning to (P. 3).

There are days when taking my medication feels exhausting, especially when I do not feel any real improvement. It makes me question whether continuing all these pills is worth it, and sometimes I just want to give up (P. 14).

Social isolation and loneliness

Some patients with hypertension felt that their significant others did not adequately understand their concerns and emotional experiences, which created a sense of distance despite physical closeness. This perceived lack of understanding acted as an invisible barrier that limited communication and emotional expression over time. Although hypertension is often not considered as severe as conditions such as cancer, participants believed that this perception led others to underestimate their struggles, thereby increasing their emotional burden. As a result, patients gradually reduced their participation in social interactions and were less willing to share their concerns in family or social settings. These findings suggest that social isolation is not only a consequence of mental burnout but also a factor that reinforces it by further reducing emotional support.

Although I am surrounded by people, I feel alone in dealing with this disease. It seems they think hypertension is not serious, so I avoid talking about it anymore (P. 5).

I spend time with my family, but even when I am sitting with them, I feel emotionally distant. My concerns are not taken seriously, so I prefer to stay silent instead of explaining what I feel. (P. 8).

Lack of effective education and communication

Lack of effective education and communication emerged as a structural and relational barrier that shaped patients’ understanding of hypertension and their engagement with long-term treatment. Rather than being a simple absence of information, this category reflected a breakdown in the process through which medical knowledge is translated into meaningful understanding and actionable self-management behaviors. Participants described uncertainty regarding the purpose of medications, lifestyle modifications, and follow-up care, which weakened their sense of control and reduced their motivation to actively participate in treatment. This informational gap was closely intertwined with ineffective communication from healthcare providers, where brief, directive, and one-way interactions limited patients’ opportunity to express concerns, ask questions, or receive individualized explanations. As a result, patients often perceived themselves as passive recipients of care rather than active partners in the treatment process. Over time, this pattern contributed to confusion, reduced trust, emotional disengagement, and gradual non-adherence, indicating that educational and communicational deficiencies do not operate independently but instead act as mutually reinforcing factors that undermine therapeutic engagement and continuity of care.

Inadequate knowledge about treatments

Most patients complain of receiving inadequate and unclear information about treatments. Patients with limited knowledge about the effects of medications, the importance of lifestyle modification, and the necessity of periodic medical tests may gradually lose their trust, start to show resistance, and discontinue their treatments. This indicates that inadequate knowledge not only limits understanding but also reduces patients’ sense of control and responsibility toward treatment. Patients’ inability to establish a rational relationship between their received information and their treatment-related responsibilities causes them to feel limited control over their treatment. Therefore, it can alter their active involvement in treatment and may lead to inappropriate medication intake, poor dietary adherence, and inattention to medical recommendations.

They gave me a series of pills and told me to avoid some foodstuffs, but didn’t tell me why. So, I didn’t take their recommendations seriously because I didn’t understand them (P. 20).

I received a lot of instructions from the clinic, but I never understood why each step was important. It left me confused, and sometimes I skipped parts of my treatment because it didn’t make sense to me (P. 11).

Healthcare providers’ poor communication and support

The quality of interpersonal communication among healthcare providers and patients with hypertension is a key influential factor on patients’ motivation for TA. Nonetheless, most participants reported the lack of effective communication between healthcare providers and patients. This problem causes patients to feel that they are merely the unilateral recipients of medical orders, without having the opportunity to talk about their concerns and personal experiences. This inability to communicate their fears and doubts about medications and dietary regimen gives them a sense of loneliness and social isolation and reduces their motivation for treatment continuation. Healthcare providers’ limited support and feedback for patients also weakens their relationships with their patients and leads to patients’ ignorance of treatments. This suggests that poor communication undermines not only information exchange but also the therapeutic relationship and patients’ emotional engagement with treatment. These two dimensions operate interactively and jointly shape patients’ engagement with treatment rather than functioning as isolated barriers.

The doctor just writes prescriptions and has no time to ask me whether I’m taking my medications or have any problems. I feel my pain and problems are never important to them (P. 14).

The nurse quickly reviews my medications without asking about my home management, making me feel uncared for and less motivated (P. 16).

Sociocultural impacts

Living with a chronic illness such as hypertension is not merely a personal experience; rather, it occurs within a network of familial and sociocultural relationships. According to most participants, significant others often provide recommendations that are inconsistent with medical advice, leading to confusion in treatment decisions. Some patients also experienced discomfort due to their inability to eat like others, particularly in social gatherings where food plays a central role. In addition, traditional beliefs about hypertension treatment—such as reliance on herbal remedies or discontinuation of medications after perceived recovery—negatively influenced treatment continuity. Some patients reported ignoring treatments or dietary regimens to avoid judgment or social pressure, while disagreements among family members further complicated decision-making and reduced treatment adherence. These findings suggest that sociocultural context actively shapes patients’ decision-making processes regarding treatment adherence, rather than merely influencing behavior indirectly. Therefore, the success of hypertension management is strongly influenced by the immediate sociocultural environment. The two subcategories of this category are familial and social pressures and misconceptions about hypertension and its treatments.

Familial and social pressures

For most patients, effective hypertension management and close TA depend on their interactions with family and society. Family expectations, participation in social gatherings, and inattention to dietary restrictions create mental strain and limitations. Some patients feel pressured to ignore their treatments to avoid upsetting others, while others report being mocked for adhering to medications or dietary regimens. This indicates that social pressure may lead to active or passive non-adherence as patients prioritize social acceptance over health behaviors. These experiences gradually reduce motivation for treatment continuation and contribute to a sense of isolation. Some patients may even feel compelled to neglect their health to gain approval from significant others.

At parties, people ask me why I don’t eat some types of food. Therefore, I have to ignore some aspects of my regimen in order not to upset them (P. 10).

Sometimes when my family eats regular meals, I feel like if I stick to my diet, they might make fun of me. So, I end up bending my diet rules occasionally (P. 15).

Misconceptions about hypertension and its treatments

Most patients and their family members have misconceptions about hypertension and its treatments due to limited knowledge. For example, some believe that feeling well indicates no need for continued medication, leading to self-discontinuation. Others prefer traditional remedies over prescribed treatments or assume that hypertension is only serious when symptoms are present, resulting in poor adherence in asymptomatic periods. These findings suggest that misconceptions are not merely informational gaps but directly influence treatment decisions and adherence behaviors. Such beliefs not only complicate hypertension management but also reduce trust in medical treatments and may lead to serious complications, highlighting the importance of continuous patient and family education.

I thought I didn’t need to take my medications when I felt well. I mean I thought medications were necessary when I didn’t feel well (P. 22).

When I started feeling better, I assumed my medications weren’t needed anymore. I didn’t realize that I had to continue them even without symptoms (P. 4).

Financial barriers

The heavy costs of antihypertensive medications can impose a substantial financial burden on patients and families, particularly when multiple medications are required for long-term use. In some cases, these financial constraints lead to delayed use, dose reduction, or discontinuation of treatment, which may result in serious health consequences. Besides medication-related costs, adherence to a healthy diet is also financially demanding, and patients with limited resources may rely on cheaper and lower-quality food options. Financial problems were not only associated with reduced access to treatment and appropriate nutrition but also generated psychological stress, which in turn reduced motivation for treatment continuation. Overall, financial barriers affected treatment adherence through both practical and psychological pathways, creating a cycle that undermined long-term disease management. The two subcategories of this category were the heavy cost of treatments and lack of financial resources for a healthy diet.

Heavy cost of treatments

The heavy cost of medications and medical care services imposes a heavy financial burden on patients and their families. Patients with hypertension need to continuously take antihypertensive medications for a long period of time, which gradually creates a heavy financial burden for them. Patients who cannot afford the costs may decide to reduce their medication intake. This reduces treatment effectiveness and patients’ motivation for TA and increases the risk of hypertension complications and poor TA, which in turn further increases the costs of treatment. This indicates that financial constraints lead directly to reduced adherence rather than acting only as a background factor.

I sometimes skip or delay my medications because of high costs, which makes managing my blood pressure stressful (P. 13).

Sometimes I have to skip doses or delay buying my medications because I just can’t afford them all at once. It makes managing my blood pressure really stressful (P. 9).

Lack of financial resources for a healthy diet

Adherence to a healthy diet is a cornerstone of effective hypertension management. However, financial problems reduce the ability of most patients with hypertension to use fruits, fresh vegetables, and low-salt and low-fat foods. For example, some of them may not afford to buy fresh chicken or fish and may opt for cheaper but high-fat foodstuffs such as fried potatoes. Such financial problems may add to patients’ mental strain and stress and reduce their motivation for TA. This suggests that economic limitations shape daily dietary choices and indirectly contribute to poor adherence.

Fresh chicken and fish are too expensive and I can’t buy them. Potato is relatively cheap and hence, I usually eat potato patties or even fried potato to reduce my expenses (P. 7).

I choose cheaper processed foods instead of fresh vegetables and lean proteins due to cost and financial limitations (P. 1).

Behavioral and lifestyle-related challenges

Behavioral and lifestyle-related challenges emerged as a dynamic and process-oriented barrier that influenced patients’ ability to sustain long-term hypertension management. Rather than reflecting a lack of knowledge alone, these challenges were rooted in difficulties in translating awareness into sustained behavioral change. Participants described that despite understanding the importance of medication adherence and lifestyle modification, long-standing habits, daily routines, and competing life demands often led to forgetfulness, reduced consistency, and gradual return to previous behaviors. In addition, fatigue from long-term medication use, lack of structured routines, and limited family support further weakened patients’ ability to maintain adherence behaviors over time. Resistance to lifestyle modification was also influenced by perceived difficulty, low motivation, and limited reinforcement from the social environment. These findings suggest that behavioral non-adherence is not an intentional decision but a complex interaction between individual capacity, motivation, and contextual constraints, which together hinder sustained self-management in chronic hypertension.

Inattention to and forgetfulness regarding medication intake

Although medication therapy is a key component of effective hypertension management, most patients face challenges in taking their medications. These challenges occur due to factors such as neglect, limited awareness of the consequences of discontinuation, or feeling no need to continue medications after symptom relief. Moreover, memory problems, lack of a regular medication plan, and poor family support in reminding patients may lead to forgetfulness. Frequent episodes of forgetfulness not only reduce medication effectiveness but may also lead to fluctuations in blood pressure and serious complications. Some patients may be inattentive to such forgetfulness despite its direct effects on hypertension management. This indicates that forgetfulness represents a form of unintentional non-adherence influenced by both individual and contextual factors.

I forget to take my medications when my blood pressure is in the normal range. But, I develop dizziness and heaviness when I don’t take my medications for several days (P. 1).

Sometimes I get so caught up in my daily routine that I completely forget my blood pressure pills. When I do, I start feeling weak and my head spins until I take them again (P. 6).

Resistance to changing an unhealthy lifestyle

Most patients with hypertension know that lifestyle modification has a significant role in hypertension management. Nonetheless, lifestyle modification is often challenging. Low-salt and low-fat foods, physical exercise, stress management, smoking cessation, and alcohol abstinence are essential. However, patients may fail to make these modifications due to long-standing habits, low motivation, fear of inability, lack of family or social support, and sociocultural factors. Some patients may also perceive lifestyle change as more difficult than medication adherence. This suggests that resistance to lifestyle change reflects an active behavioral barrier that may overlap with reduced motivation, rather than simple lack of awareness.

I know that I have to restrict my sodium intake and perform more exercise, but giving up my old habits has become very difficult for me (P. 3).

Changing my long-time habits feels almost impossible. I try to eat better and exercise, but most days I just fall back into my old routines because it’s too overwhelming (P. 19).

Taken together, the findings indicate that treatment adherence among patients with hypertension is shaped by an interconnected system of psychological, educational, sociocultural, financial, and behavioral factors that continuously interact and reinforce each other.

Discussion

This study explored barriers to TA among patients with hypertension. The analysis identified five interrelated categories: mental burnout, ineffective education and communication, sociocultural influences, financial constraints, and behavioral and lifestyle-related challenges. Rather than operating as independent determinants, these barriers appeared to form a multi-level, interacting system that shapes patients’ treatment-related experiences and behaviors within the Iranian healthcare context.

Although data were analyzed inductively using conventional content analysis, the WHO adherence model was used during interpretation to situate the emergent categories within a broader conceptual understanding of adherence as a multi-dimensional process.

Importantly, the findings suggest that treatment adherence is not shaped by isolated barriers, but emerges from the dynamic interaction of psychological, social, economic, and healthcare system factors that reinforce one another over time.

Mental burnout emerged as a central psychological construct influencing TA among patients with hypertension It was conceptualized as a multidimensional phenomenon comprising three interrelated dimensions: worry about disease progression and its consequences, reduced motivation to continue treatment, and social withdrawal and loneliness. Rather than representing a transient emotional response, mental burnout reflects a cumulative psychological process resulting from sustained engagement with chronic disease management and perceived difficulty in achieving treatment control.

This framing suggests that burnout operates both as a consequence and a reinforcing factor of non-adherence, providing a theoretical rather than purely descriptive interpretation of patients’ experiences. In the Iranian primary care context, these experiences appear to be shaped by cultural and healthcare system factors that intensify psychological burden.

Participants reported persistent anxiety regarding disease progression, including concerns about severe complications such as stroke and heart failure and difficulty maintaining blood pressure control despite medication use. Almost one-third of patients with hypertension suffered from anxiety, five times higher than normotensive individuals [28]. Consistent with prior studies, long-term uncertainty has been associated with reduced quality of life and increased psychological strain [29–31]. although context-dependent variations have also been reported [32].

Reduced motivation emerged as a reinforcing component of mental burnout, driven by perceived treatment inefficacy and fluctuating blood pressure, contributing to irregular medication use and disengagement from care [28, 33, 34]. Reduced motivation acts both as a consequence and reinforcing factor of non-adherence. Afika Dirga et al. observed maintained motivation despite similar challenges, highlighting individual and cultural differences [35]. This can lead to irregular medication intake and serious complications [34, 36]. Susceptibility to mental burnout may be influenced by functional health literacy and prior healthcare experiences, as limited understanding or negative clinical encounters can increase uncertainty, reduce trust in treatment, and contribute to emotional exhaustion and poor adherence.

Social isolation and loneliness was the third subcategory. Participants reported that dietary restrictions, avoidance of stressful activities, and lifestyle changes distanced them from family and friends. Similarly, chronic illness often leads to social isolation [37–39]. Yet some patients did not experience isolation despite lifestyle restrictions, suggesting cultural or individual modifiers [40]. These differences appeared influenced by demographic factors and the local Iranian social context, suggesting cultural and familial norms modify the experience of social isolation. Importantly, social isolation can create a cyclical psychological barrier, worsening loneliness, mental strain, and reducing TA [41]. These subcategories may interact in a cyclical model, where anxiety, reduced motivation, and social isolation reinforce each other and shape adherence behavior. Evidence shows that strong social and professional support, access to healthcare providers, effective coping skills, and a positive attitude can mitigate burnout and sustain adherence [42, 43]. Therefore, interventions should target both individual behaviors and social-contextual factors, particularly in culturally specific settings These subcategories form a self-reinforcing cycle in which anxiety, reduced motivation, and social isolation mutually intensify each other and progressively weaken treatment adherence. Mental burnout, including anxiety, reduced motivation, and social isolation, aligns with patient-related factors in the WHO adherence model, reflecting how psychological states directly influence adherence behavior [44]. Overall, mental burnout represents a dynamic interplay between psychological states, social environment, and healthcare system factors, reinforcing the need for context-sensitive interventions. Taken together, the findings suggest that barriers to treatment adherence do not operate in isolation but instead form a dynamic and mutually reinforcing system in which structural, social, and psychological factors continuously interact and shape patients’ adherence behavior. These domains do not operate independently; rather, they interact through feedback loops in which healthcare system constraints and sociocultural pressures amplify psychological burden, which in turn further undermines self-management behaviors. In this process, limited health literacy and previous fragmented experiences with healthcare services may increase uncertainty and reduce trust. This may translate psychological burden into unintentional non-adherence behaviors such as forgetfulness, reduced motivation, and treatment discontinuation.

The second main category of the barriers to TA among patients with hypertension was the lack of effective education and communication. Participants reported that they received inadequate and incoherent information about their disease and its management, which is in line with previous findings [45]. This highlights the need for a theoretical lens to understand how patient education influences adherence, rather than just descriptive reporting. Inadequate education and unclear explanations about medications and lifestyle reduce TA among patients with hypertension [46, 47]. Our findings revealed that poor understanding about treatments and receiving unconvincing responses from healthcare providers can cause uncertainties and worries for patients. Similarly, a study highlighted that lack of knowledge can increase patients’ anxiety and reduce their self-confidence in disease management [48]. Lack of knowledge becomes particularly prominent when patients face complex concepts of hypertension and treatment [18]. Observed in an Iranian primary care context, these findings also suggest that cultural and system-level factors shape patient comprehension and engagement.

We also found that, beyond a lack of quality education, healthcare providers’ poor communication and limited support negatively affect patients’ interactions, understanding, and TA. Most participants reported that physicians and nurses did not allocate adequate time to answer their questions, which is consistent with previous studies [33]. Poor interactive communication during therapy sessions was reported to negatively affect motivation and TA among patients with hypertension [47]. Inadequate patient education and poor communication correspond to healthcare system-related factors in the WHO adherence model, highlighting the role of provider-patient interaction and system support in sustaining adherence. The reasons for such poor communication may include providers’ heavy workload, lack of time, or insufficient training in communication skills [49]. Healthcare providers’ poor communication may cause patients to feel isolated with their medication orders and limit opportunities for participation in their treatment, which in turn can reduce adherence and increase stress, confusion, and anxiety [50]. On the other hand, some studies found that patients experienced positive relationships and improved adherence when healthcare providers were well-trained, patient-centered, and adopted a humanistic approach [18, 50]. additionally, Some studies found that better patient–provider communication did not always improve adherence, suggesting context-dependent effects [51]. Therefore, training in communication skills for healthcare providers and strengthening their interpersonal communication skills can improve patients’ TA.

The third main category of the barriers to TA among patients with hypertension was sociocultural impacts. Familial and societal pressures reflect social/economic determinants in the WHO adherence model, illustrating how cultural norms and social expectations can reinforce or hinder adherence. Similarly, a systematic review reported that social factors have a determining role in TA [52]. Most of our participants were under pressure from their families and society to comply with behavioral standards. Familial and social pressures, such as family expectations to consume high-salt diets, can cause mental stress, frustration, and reduce motivation for adherence [53]. Such pressures in traditional societies may increase patients’ resistance to medical recommendations, aggravate anxiety, reduce perceived social support, and thereby lower motivation for treatment continuation [53, 54]. Two studies highlighted that weak emotional and practical support from family members can accelerate TA reduction [36, 43].

Another sociocultural barrier to TA was misconceptions about hypertension and its treatments, mostly due to patients’ lack of knowledge and healthcare providers’ insufficient patient education. In line with previous studies [55, 56], our findings revealed that most patients believed in the inessentiality of regular medication intake or lifestyle modification after some improvements in their conditions. Moreover, cultural factors may contribute to uncertainty about treatment continuation or adherence [57, 58]. Misconceptions, inaccurate information, or negative personal experiences can reduce trust in treatments [59]. Sociocultural pressures and lack of culturally-appropriate education can reinforce resistance to lifestyle modification [60, 61]. These factors interact in a vicious cycle, complicating disease management [62, 63]. Importantly, some studies reported that sociocultural pressures did not always reduce adherence, suggesting context-dependent effects and highlighting the role of individual differences and healthcare system support [64]. However, Purposeful sociocultural interventions, culturally-appropriate education, and strong family support can improve patients’ and families’ knowledge, strengthen familial support, improve TA, enhance quality of life, and increase treatment success [65, 66]. These findings underscore the need for a theoretical framework to understand how sociocultural factors interact with patient education, motivation, and adherence, particularly in Iranian primary care or other traditional contexts.

We also found financial barriers as the fourth main category of the barriers to TA. One of the subcategories of this category was the heavy cost of treatments. Consistent with our findings, some previous studies reported that the heavy cost of medications and medical visits imposed a heavy financial burden on families and were one of the leading causes of treatment discontinuatin [67, 68]. This finding highlights the need for a broader conceptual understanding of adherence, where economic constraints interact with patient behavior and healthcare access, rather than acting as isolated factors. Economic constraints such as medication costs and limited access to healthy foods correspond to social/economic factors in the WHO adherence model, illustrating how financial barriers interact with patient motivation and lifestyle choices to influence treatment adherence. Inflation and inadequate insurance coverage of medical services can put financial strains on low-income patients [67]. On the other hand, we found the lack of financial resources for a healthy diet as another financial barrier to TA. This factor requires patients to use low-quality foodstuffs, which directly affect patients’ health and disease management. Similarly, two previous studies reported poor nutrition as a serious barrier to the management of chronic illnesses [69, 70]. Moreover, some cultural and structural factors such as a lack of knowledge about healthy eating and a lack of healthy food stores in low-income areas can aggravate patients’ nutritional conditions [71]. Observed in an Iranian healthcare context, these findings may reflect the combined effects of economic instability and limited access to supportive resources. Financial barriers can endanger patients’ health, cause them varying levels of stress, and aggravate their conditions [72]. Patients with financial problems are more likely to discontinue their treatments or reduce their medication dose, which in turn increase the risk of complications and emergency re-hospitalization [73]. Importantly, some studies suggest that financial constraints do not always lead to poor adherence, as certain patients maintain treatment through strong personal motivation, family support, or access to alternative support systems, indicating context-dependent effects [74]. Therefore, financial support interventions such as medication subsidy and low-cost education can improve patients’ TA and facilitate their access to quality treatments and healthy diets [73].

Behavioral and lifestyle-related challenges constituted the fifth main category of the barriers to TA in the present study. The two subcategories of this category were inattention to and forgetfulness regarding medication intake and resistance to changing an unhealthy lifestyle. Consistently, several studies reported forgetfulness as one of the main reasons for TA reduction and highlighted that it can increase the risk of acute complications and the need for costly treatments and can endanger patients’ health [75–77]. Resistance to modifying an unhealthy lifestyle has psychological causes such as fear of change, lack of belief in the necessity of care, and sociocultural pressures [78]. his indicates the need for a theoretical perspective, where individual behavior is understood in the context of familial, social, and cultural influences, rather than treating it as an isolated personal problem. Psychosocial interventions are therefore necessary for effective management [79]. The most important contributing factors to resistance include fatigue from continuous treatment, lack of practical education, social support, sports facilities, and healthy foods, family pressures, and difficult work conditions [78]. Significant life events and difficulty accessing healthcare services can aggravate the condition, lead to treatment discontinuation, or reduce TA. These problems, in turn, reduce treatment effectiveness, increase the risk of cardiovascular complications, and impose added mental and financial burden on patients and healthcare systems [10]. Forgetfulness and resistance to lifestyle change relate to both therapy- and patient-related factors in the WHO adherence model, emphasizing that adherence is influenced by both regimen complexity and individual coping mechanisms, as well as psychological and behavioral states. Importantly, some studies reported that certain patients maintain adherence despite these challenges, suggesting context-dependent effects influenced by individual motivation, family support, and healthcare system facilitation [80]. Taken together, these barriers form an integrated system of mutually reinforcing factors that collectively determine patients’ adherence behavior.

These factors do not operate independently but generate feedback loops that progressively weaken adherence behavior.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that TA among patients with hypertension is shaped by a dynamic and interrelated system of psychological, sociocultural, economic, and healthcare-related factors. Rather than acting independently, these factors reinforce one another through continuous feedback processes that progressively undermine patients’ capacity for sustained self-management. Mental burnout emerged as a central mechanism through which these interacting barriers translated into reduced motivation, emotional disengagement, and treatment discontinuation.

These findings highlight the importance of viewing adherence not as an individual behavioral issue, but as a context-dependent process embedded within healthcare system constraints and sociocultural conditions. Although the study was conducted in a single primary care setting in Iran, the conceptual insights may inform the development of more comprehensive and system-oriented approaches to chronic disease management.

Recommendations

The findings suggest that interventions to improve TA should address the interacting nature of barriers rather than targeting individual factors in isolation. Priority should be given to reducing the psychological burden of mental burnout by improving continuity of care, strengthening patient–provider communication, and enhancing patients’ understanding of hypertension management.

In addition, system-level interventions that reduce financial strain, improve access to affordable treatment, and integrate family and sociocultural support mechanisms may help disrupt the reinforcing cycles that contribute to non-adherence.

Rather than isolated educational or behavioral strategies, multilevel approaches that simultaneously target psychological, social, and healthcare system factors are likely to be more effective in sustaining long-term adherence.

Limitations

This study was conducted in a single public hospital in Iran, which may limit the transferability of the findings to other healthcare settings. In addition, patients with comorbid chronic conditions were excluded to allow a more focused exploration of hypertension-specific treatment adherence barriers. Consequently, the findings may not be fully transferable to hypertensive patients with multiple chronic conditions, who represent a substantial proportion of the real-world hypertensive population. Furthermore, only patients’ perspectives were explored, without the inclusion of family members or healthcare providers, which may have limited a more comprehensive understanding of treatment adherence. Finally, the findings should be interpreted in light of the specific cultural and contextual characteristics of the study setting.
