Assessment of asthma care, self-management and healthcare services access during the Sudanese war: a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Roaa N S Osman, Logien Abuelgasim Ahmad Ibrahim, Reela H Moubarak, Alaa A S Salih, Abdelwahab G A Mahmoud, Shroog M K Hassan, Amna A A Elshikh, Sohaib Mohammed Mokhtar Ahmed, Sara F I Khalil, Darelsalam Ishag Ateem Abdelrasoul, Rayan M E Elzaki, Ahmed Balla M Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAsthma poses a great burden as one of the most common long-term non-communicable diseases. The April 2023 Sudanese war has severely disrupted the healthcare system, greatly affecting asthmatic patients by limiting access to medications and diagnostic services. Therefore, this research aims to investigate asthma control, self-management and healthcare access among asthma patients in Sudan during the ongoing war.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among Sudanese patients with asthma during the ongoing war using a convenience sampling. An online questionnaire was distributed from September 2024 to June 2025. It covered sociodemographic data, the validated Asthma Control Test (ACT), self-management and healthcare access. Data were analysed using SPSS software, employing descriptive statistics, χ2 tests and linear regression, with significance set at p<0.05.ResultsAmong the 229 asthmatic patients surveyed, 60.3% had poorly controlled asthma. Since the onset of the conflict, 43.2% had not attended any follow-up visits, while 48.5% required at least one emergency room visit during the war. Despite these challenges, 86.0% reported having quick access to healthcare during active attacks. However, 17.9% were unable to obtain their medications regularly, forcing many to adopt alternative coping strategies: 6.1% sought medical advice through online consultations, 15.7% increased their prescribed doses on their own and 39.3% used whatever medications were available without prescriptions, which was significantly associated with poor asthma control (χ²=29.29, p<0.01). Regression analysis revealed dose reduction was linked to worse control (β=−3.68, p=0.004), while refugee status and better adherence to medications predicted higher ACT scores (β=2.88, p<0.001; β=0.54, p=0.028).ConclusionsThis study highlights a considerable gap in asthma control during the ongoing war, despite the availability of healthcare services in times of urgent need. Many patients adopted coping strategies such as using whatever medications were available without prescriptions or independently increasing their prescribed doses. Enhancing patient education on proper asthma self-management and improving access to healthcare services, including telemedicine consultations, could play a crucial role in achieving better asthma control.

Background

Asthma is one of the most prevalent chronic non-communicable diseases.1 It affects 260.48 million worldwide, as reported in 2021.2 It is defined as a condition caused by swelling and narrowing of the airways (bronchi) in the lungs, which makes them highly sensitive and results in persistent cough, wheezing, chest discomfort and breathlessness.3 4 The main risk factors include genetic predisposition and environmental triggers such as allergens, tobacco smoke and air pollution.4

In Sudan, asthma is a common disease associated with high morbidity and substantial economic costs.5 It ranks as the fifth leading cause of hospitalisations, following pneumonia, malaria, diarrhoeal diseases and diabetes.6 In 2017, its prevalence was estimated at 18.2%.7 The level of asthma control and management in the country has varied. For instance, a study conducted in Khartoum in 2018, before the Sudan war, reported that 84.5% of patients had uncontrolled asthma according to the Asthma Control Test (ACT) score.8 Similarly, in Atbara in Northern Sudan, 42% of patients were admitted 1–3 times, while 47.3% of asthma patients required admission to the intensive care unit.9

The war in Sudan, which broke out in April 2023, has further worsened this situation. Between April 2023 and 26 November 2024, Insecurity Insight documented 519 attacks on Sudan’s healthcare system due to the ongoing conflict between the Sudan Armed Forces and Rapid Support Forces.10 As a result, 70%–80% of emergency healthcare facilities are non-operational, and essential healthcare services are unavailable to 65% of the population.11 These disruptions have created major barriers for asthma patients in accessing timely medical care. One abstract published after the start of the war assessed the impact of these disruptions on asthma patients, particularly regarding medication access, healthcare services and health outcomes. It was reported that 65% of patients experienced significant interruptions in their medication supply, resulting in a 50% increase in exacerbations and emergency room (ER) visits. In addition, medication adherence decreased by 40% due to inconsistent drug availability, and asthma control deteriorated in 55% of patients.12

As asthma requires continuous care and follow-up, the ongoing war highlights the urgent need to examine the current healthcare situation of asthma patients in Sudan. Specifically, it is important to evaluate the availability and accessibility of appropriate treatment and care services, especially in conflict-affected regions where healthcare resources are severely limited. This study aims to address this gap by assessing asthma control, self-management and healthcare access during the ongoing war in Sudan. The findings are expected to support health organisations and relevant authorities in designing effective interventions for asthma patients. Furthermore, this research can serve as a foundation for future in-depth studies and humanitarian initiatives.

Methods

Study design and setting

This community-based observational cross-sectional study was conducted to evaluate asthma control, self-management and healthcare access among patients diagnosed with asthma in Sudan during the ongoing war. Data were collected from 27 September 2024 to 3 June 2025, across different states in Sudan and outside Sudan.

Participants and sampling

The target population included individuals aged 18 years and above, with a confirmed diagnosis of asthma prior to and after the onset of the war. Patients who lacked the cognitive ability to independently complete the questionnaire were excluded.

The sample size (n) was calculated using Cochran’s formula, based on the non-probability convenience sampling method, as follows:

assuming a 95% confidence level (Z score=1.96), an estimated prevalence of the population of 18.2%7 (p=0.182), and a 5% margin of error (e=0.05), yielding a sample size of 229 participants.

Data collection procedures and tools

Prior to data collection, a pilot study was conducted on a small sample of 30 patients to evaluate the clarity of the questionnaire. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, yielding a value of 0.71, which is considered acceptable. Minor adjustments were made based on participants’ feedback. The 30 participants involved in the pilot were later excluded from the main analysis. To ensure data integrity, duplicate responses were identified and removed by cross-checking IP addresses and timestamps. Mandatory fields in the Google Form minimised missing data, and submissions with incomplete ACT responses were excluded. Participation was voluntary and anonymous to reduce fraudulent entries.

The questionnaire (online supplemental file) was a structured, closed-ended self-report that consisted of a total of 31 questions divided into three main sections: The first section included 14 questions about sociodemographic characteristics such as age, sex, marital status, occupation, educational status, type and place of residence, displacement status, monthly income, smoking status, health insurance status and duration of asthma in years. The second section contained the ACT,13 a validated tool consisting of five questions to assess asthma symptoms and their impact over the past 4 weeks. The validated Arabic version was used without any modification. The ACT is widely used to determine the level of asthma control. Its values range from 5 (poorly controlled) to 25 (completely controlled), with higher scores indicating better asthma control. The third section was self-designed and developed based on concepts adapted from a previous research study conducted in Beijing.14 It consisted of 12 questions focused on healthcare access and self-management during the conflict and included assessment of changes in asthma symptoms, visits to clinics and ERs, and access to medications and health centres. Participants were also asked if they reduced their doses and what consequences followed this reduction, what medications they were regularly using and to what extent they adhered to medical guidelines. This questionnaire was initially developed in English and then translated into Arabic because it is the official language in Sudan, using the standardised forward-backward translation procedure to ensure content validity. The forward translation from English to Arabic and the backward translation from Arabic back to English were both carried out by the research team and then reviewed by an independent bilingual expert. However, this translation approach was not applied to the second section (ACT), as a validated Arabic version was already available in the literature.13

The questionnaire was distributed online using a Google Form, first to specific social media groups containing asthmatic patients, and then to general social media groups due to limited access to patients during the war and to ensure broad participation. Platforms used included Facebook, WhatsApp, Instagram, Telegram and Twitter.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were employed to summarise the sociodemographic characteristics, asthma-related clinical profiles and coping strategies of the study participants. Continuous variables, such as age, asthma duration and ACT scores (following the recommendations of Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) guidelines, 2024), were reported as medians with IQRs, while categorical variables, including sex, displacement status and medication access, were presented as frequencies and percentages.

To assess factors associated with poor asthma control, inferential analyses were conducted using Pearson’s χ² tests for categorical variables. Key outcome measures included asthma control status (dichotomised into well-controlled and poorly controlled based on ACT scores), ER visits, medication adherence and symptom exacerbation. The linear regression analysis was used to identify the predictors of ACT scores using all demographic variables as independent variables. All statistical analyses were performed using appropriate software such as Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) (V.27), with significance set at p<0.05.

Patient and public involvement

No patients were involved in our study design. Patients were not consulted to develop the study’s outcome or to contribute to the writing and editing of the manuscript.

Results

A total of 229 participants with asthma were included in this study, the majority of whom (93.4%) had been diagnosed with asthma before the onset of the current conflict. The median age of participants was 22 years (IQR=7), ranging from 18 to 75 years. Most participants were female (66.4%) and unmarried (77.7%). The sample was predominantly urban residents (84.3%), with the majority living with their families (76.4%). Regarding educational attainment, a significant proportion had university-level education (77.3%), while students represented the largest occupational group (65.1%). In terms of displacement status, 38.4% were refugees outside Sudan, 29.7% were internally displaced and 10.5% remained in active war zones. More than half of the participants (51.1%) reported their household income could only cover basic needs, while 70.3% had no health insurance. Most respondents were non-smokers (87.8%) (table 1).

Asthma-related characteristics revealed a median disease duration of 10 years (IQR=12), with ACT scores having a median of 18 (IQR=6). Based on ACT scores, 60.3% of participants had poorly controlled asthma. Notably, 31.4% reported worsening of asthma symptoms since the war began, while 25.3% saw improvement, and 43.2% noted no change. Regarding clinical follow-up, 51.1% visited asthma clinics irregularly, and 43.2% had not visited at all since the conflict started. Emergency room (ER) visits due to asthma were common; 48.5% reported at least one ER visit postconflict, with 19.2% presenting three times or more. Of these, the leading reasons for ER visits included rapid worsening of symptoms (14.8%), lack of response to usual treatment (14.8%) and exposure to strong triggers (9.6%) (figure 1). Access to asthma medications was significantly affected: although 68.1% managed to obtain medications regularly, 17.9% could not and 14.0% did not use any medication. Nonetheless, 86.0% reported having quick access to healthcare during active attacks (table 2).

This Pearson χ2 analysis identified several statistically significant relationships with poor asthma control. Significant associations were observed in treatment-related behaviours and outcomes: patients with poor control showed significantly higher rates of symptom worsening (χ²=13.46, p<0.01) and ER utilisation (χ²=14.44, p<0.01). Medication access patterns proved particularly important, with dose reduction due to shortages strongly associated with poor control (χ²=27.80, p<0.01), and those who reduced doses experiencing significantly worse symptoms (χ²=50.28, p<0.01). The use of non-prescribed medications showed a particularly strong relationship with poor outcomes (χ²=26.81, p<0.01). Financial barriers to medication access were significantly more prevalent among poorly controlled patients (χ²=29.29, p<0.01). Notably, while these treatment-related factors showed strong statistical significance, traditional demographic variables including age (F=1.38, p=0.24), sex (χ²=0.95, p=0.33) and displacement status (χ²=2.59, p=0.46) failed to demonstrate significant associations with asthma control status.

The regression analysis showed that refugees outside Sudan had significantly higher ACT scores compared with non-displaced participants (β=2.88, 95% CI 1.25 to 4.51, p<0.001). Conversely, patients who reported three or more ER visits since the war had significantly lower ACT scores (β=−2.12, 95% CI −3.96 to −0.28, p=0.024). Participants who did not use any medications scored higher compared with those who reduced doses due to fear of shortages (β=3.68, 95% CI 1.21 to 6.15, p=0.004). Finally, better adherence to medical guidelines was positively associated with ACT scores (β=0.54, 95% CI 0.06 to 1.03, p=0.028). Other sociodemographic and clinical factors, including age, sex, education and smoking status, were not statistically significant predictors of ACT scores (online supplemental table 1).

Discussion

This study provides important insights into the self-management and disease control of Sudanese asthma patients, as well as their access to essential medications and healthcare services during the ongoing war. The findings reveal that nearly two-thirds of patients experienced a considerable gap in asthma control, despite 86.0% reporting quick access to healthcare during acute attacks. To cope with the challenges of the war, many patients resorted to self-directed strategies, such as using whatever medications were available without prescriptions and independently increasing their prescribed doses.

Our study found that slightly less than two-thirds of participants had poor asthma control, and nearly one-third experienced worsening symptoms during the war. Asthma control was assessed using the ACT, following the GINA), 2024 guidelines. GINA is particularly valuable because it not only provides evidence-based recommendations for optimal asthma care but also offers practical strategies for managing asthma in low-resource and crisis-affected settings.15 The impact of armed conflict on asthma and related symptoms is well documented in the literature, with studies showing an increase in both prevalence and severity among civilians and military personnel across various conflict zones.16 Our findings are consistent with a previous Sudanese study conducted during the current war, which reported similar ACT scores among asthma patients.12 Before the current war, asthma control in Sudan was already poor, with a study reporting 84.5% uncontrolled cases,8 higher than in our study. This discrepancy may be explained by differences in study settings and populations. The earlier study was conducted in tertiary hospitals, where patients typically present with more severe or uncontrolled symptoms, while our community-based approach included a wider range of individuals and thus offers a more representative picture.

However, variations exist depending on context. For example, a study conducted in shelters in Damascus, Syria, found that nearly 70% of asthma patients reported worsening symptoms after displacement17—a proportion more than twice that observed in our study. This difference is likely attributable to variations in living conditions; participants in the Syrian study were confined to poorly equipped shelters, whereas our study population resided in relatively safer areas with access to the internet and more favourable environmental conditions. This interpretation is consistent with our own findings, where refugees outside Sudan demonstrated significantly higher ACT scores, probably reflecting the health benefits of residing in safer and more stable environments. Similarly, a study from Ukraine found that only 38.9% of asthma patients attending outpatient and inpatient clinics during the conflict reported increased disease severity,18 further underscoring how contextual factors influence disease outcomes. Several factors have been proposed to explain the heightened burden of asthma during and after conflicts, including psychological stress, exposure to new environmental triggers and population displacement.16 These factors are also relevant in the context of the Sudanese war19 20 and likely contributed to the deterioration of asthma control among our participants.

Notably, 6.6% of asthma patients in our study were diagnosed after the onset of the war. This may reflect both the increased exposure to risk factors during conflict and limitations in our sampling approach, which relied on online convenience sampling and may not fully capture the general population. Moreover, asthma frequently goes undiagnosed in conflict-affected areas, which may further obscure the true burden of disease.16

Nearly half of the participants reported at least one ER visit after the onset of the conflict, and one-fifth reported three or more ER visits. These findings are in line with a previous Sudanese study conducted during the current war, which found that half of the asthma patients had visited the ER.12 However, the frequency of ER visits observed in our study is substantially higher than that reported under normal conditions. For example, a US study conducted among working adults between 2011 and 2016 reported that only 9.9% had experienced an asthma-related ER visit.21

Frequent ER visits are a recognised indicator of poor asthma control and symptom exacerbation.22 This is consistent with our findings, where patients with poor asthma control showed significantly higher rates of ER utilisation. Notably, repeated ER visits in this context are more likely reflective of asthma undertreatment rather than limited access to healthcare, especially given that most participants in our study reported having rapid access to emergency care during asthma attacks. This accessibility may be attributed to residence in relatively stable or safer areas, which contrasts with the broader national context, where over two-thirds of main hospitals in conflict-affected regions are non-functional.23

The most commonly reported reasons for ER visits among participants were sudden worsening of symptoms and inadequate response to usual treatments—factors frequently highlighted in the literature as primary drivers of emergency care use among asthma patients.24

Nearly two-thirds of participants in this study were able to obtain their regular asthma medications, which may indicate relatively good access to treatment. However, access alone may not be sufficient in conflict-affected settings, as psychological stress associated with war can impair the body’s response to inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) and beta-2 agonists, leading to more severe asthma symptoms even when medications are taken as prescribed.16

Despite this, almost one-third of patients reported reducing their medication dosage due to fear of future shortages. Among these, one-fifth experienced worsening symptoms. A comparable pattern was seen during the COVID-19 pandemic in Beijing, where 6.2% of asthma patients reduced their medication doses, and nearly one-third of them experienced symptom aggravation.14 Such fear-driven behaviour is common during times of crisis. It is particularly concerning that those who reduced their doses were significantly more likely to experience a deterioration in asthma control.

The primary barriers to obtaining medications in our study were financial constraints, followed by drug unavailability and lack of transportation. This contrasts with a previous Sudanese study conducted in more directly conflict-affected areas, where roadblocks and insecurity were the predominant barriers.12 The variation in reported barriers likely reflects differences in study settings. Notably, in our study, financial barriers were significantly more common among participants with poorly controlled asthma. This is supported by findings from a study in a low-income and middle-income country, where financial constraints affected more than two-thirds of participants and were strongly associated with poor adherence.25

In terms of coping strategies during the war, over one-third of participants reported using whatever medications were available, including non-prescribed drugs, while some increased the dose of their prescribed medications. Our analysis showed that the use of non-prescribed medications was particularly associated with poor asthma outcomes. The INSPIRE study similarly noted that although patients are often willing to adjust their own asthma treatment, they frequently make inappropriate changes—such as altering the wrong type of medication during exacerbations—reflecting a longstanding gap in effective self-management education, despite guidelines promoting it for over 15 years.26 In Sudan, this behaviour may reflect patients’ tendency to use whatever is available to manage their condition on their own.

Among the few positive practices reported, online medical consultation emerged as a beneficial strategy, though used by a minority. According to existing recommendations, digital medical services are suitable for patients with mild to moderate or well-controlled asthma, while in-person outpatient care should be prioritised for those with poorly controlled or worsening symptoms.14

This study is among the first to assess the status of asthma patients during the ongoing war in Sudan. Its broad geographical coverage across various Sudanese regions adds to the strength of the findings. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Data on asthma diagnosis, asthma attacks and ER visits were self-reported and not validated through medical records, which may introduce reporting bias. Additionally, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the ability to draw causal inferences between risk factors and poor asthma control. The use of convenience sampling resulted in a sample skewed toward younger, female, urban and more educated participants, which limited the generalisability of the findings. Furthermore, asthma diagnosis relied on participants’ self-report through a single screening question, without clinical verification, which may affect diagnostic accuracy. Moreover, since data collection was conducted online, the study depended on individuals with internet access during the study period, potentially excluding vulnerable populations without connectivity and further limiting representativeness.

Conclusions

According to our knowledge, our research is one of the first to provide assessment data on asthma control, self-management and healthcare facilities accessibility among asthma patients in Sudan at the time of the conflict. It revealed that nearly two-thirds of participants had poor asthma control. Many patients reported self-management challenges, including symptom deterioration, dose reduction and use of non-prescribed drugs due to difficulty obtaining treatments and accessing healthcare facilities. Notably, only a small proportion could manage to attend regular clinic follow-up visits every 3–6 months since the beginning of the war, emphasising the impact of financial barriers and transportation difficulties.

Several recommendations can be made to improve asthma care during the current conflict. There is an urgent need to strengthen patient education on proper asthma self-management, particularly regarding the safe use of medications during emergencies. Health authorities and humanitarian actors should prioritise the continuous supply and affordability of essential asthma medications, especially ICSs, to prevent fear-driven dose reductions. Expanding access to digital healthcare services, including telemedicine consultations, could support asthma management in safer regions with internet access. Lastly, future research should include more representative sampling methods and clinical validation of data to better understand and address the needs of asthma patients in conflict settings.
