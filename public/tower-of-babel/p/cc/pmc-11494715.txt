Phylogenetic and Morphological Analysis of a Novel Tolypocladium Species Isolated from Soil in South Korea
Authors: Gyeong-Mi Song, Leonid N. Ten, Seong-Keun Lim, Soo-Min Hong, Seung-Yeol Lee, Hee-Young Jung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA fungal strain designated KNUF-22-014, belonging to the family Ophiocordycipitaceae, was isolated from a soil sample collected in Sobaeksan National Park, South Korea. Phylogenetic trees based on the combined sequences of internal transcribed spacer (ITS) regions and the large subunit of 28S rRNA (LSU) gene, as well as on five concatenated loci (LSU, ITS, translation elongation factor 1α, β-tubulin, and subunit 1 of RNA polymerase II), revealed that the isolate falls within the clade containing the genus Tolypocladium but occupies a distinct phylogenetic position. Its closest relative is T. tropicale. Morphologically, strain KNUF-22-014 can be distinguished from T. tropicale by the shape of its phialides (subcylindrical vs. lageniform), its conidiophore type (arborescent or unbranched vs. trichodermatoid), and its larger conidia size (2.1 ± 0.1 µm vs. 1.5 ± 0.1 µm). Phylogenetic analysis and morphological observations indicate that KNUF-22-014 represents a novel species in the genus Tolypocladium, for which the name Tolypocladium sobaeksanense sp. nov. is proposed.

Introduction

The family Ophiocordycipitaceae encompasses a diverse array of economically, medicinally, ecologically, and culturally important fungi [1]. Characterized by the production of asexual spores (conidia) from branched conidiophores, these fungi are commonly found in tropical and subtropical regions, residing in soil or on decaying organic matter. Notable genera within this family include Ophiocordyceps and Tolypocladium, with some species showing potential for medical applications and bioactive compound production [2,3].

The genus Tolypocladium was established by Gams in 1971 to accommodate three soil-inhabiting species: T. cylindrosporum, T. geodes, and T. inflatum, with the latter serving as the type species of the genus. According to the Index Fungorum (https://www.indexfungorum.org) and Mycobank (https://www.mycobank.org) databases, the genus currently comprises approximately 60 species. However, the affiliation of some of these species is questionable, as their original identifications were either based on presumptive host associations or based solely on morphological characteristics. Specifically, molecular data for 16 of these species are not available in the GenBank database [4]. Morphologically, members of the genus Tolypocladium are characterized by inflated phialides, sparsely branched conidiophores, and one-celled conidia produced in slimy heads [4]. Tolypocladium species are primarily soil-inhabiting fungi found in forest soils, agricultural lands, and other terrestrial environments rich in organic matter. Some species thrive in decaying plant material and leaf litter, contributing to the decomposition process. Several Tolypocladium species are entomopathogenic, parasitizing and growing on insect hosts. These fungi are predominantly located in tropical and subtropical regions, benefiting from the warm and humid conditions conducive to fungal growth and reproduction [4–6]. Overall, Tolypocladium species exhibit a diverse range of habitats, reflecting their ecological versatility and adaptation to various environmental conditions.

In the last decade, advancements in molecular phylogeny have facilitated re-identification and reclassification of several species within the genus Tolypocladium and closely related taxa [1,4,7]. Based on a combination of morphological characterization and multilocus sequence analysis (MLSA), Chaunopycnis alba, C. ovalispora, and C. pustulata were re-classified as Tolypocladium album, T. ovalisporum, and T. pustulatum, respectively [1]. Notably, several studies have utilized MLSA with five molecular markers: internal transcribed spacer (ITS) regions, the large subunit of 28S rRNA gene (LSU), translation elongation factor 1α (TEF-1α), β-tubulin (TUB), and the subunit 1 of RNA polymerase II gene (RPB1). This approach was recently successfully applied to the description of four novel Tolypocladium species: T. inusitaticapitatum [8], T. rhizomatum [9], T. subtropicale, and T. trecense [10].

This study aimed to introduce and characterize a novel Tolypocladium species, designated Tolypocladium sobaeksanense sp. nov., isolated from a soil sample collected in Sobaeksan National Park, Danyang, South Korea. This study was conducted as part of our research focused on the discovery of indigenous Korean fungal species. Species characterization was achieved through morphological and molecular analyses, and taxonomic placement within the genus Tolypocladium was confirmed based on MLSA methodology.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and fungal isolation

Samples were obtained in 2021 from the soil in the rhizosphere of deciduous trees in Sobaeksan National Park, Yeongju City, Gyeongsangbuk-do, South Korea (36°56′31.1″N, 128°31′37.3″E). The samples were processed using the standard serial dilution method. Then, 100 μL of the resulting suspensions were pipetted onto potato dextrose agar (PDA; Difco, Detroit, MI) plates, which were subsequently incubated at 25 °C for seven days. Single colonies were individually transferred onto PDA plates and then incubated at 25 °C. Several fungal strains were isolated, processed for DNA extraction, PCR, and sequencing as described below, and initially examined for classification using the obtained sequences of the ITS regions and RPB1. Among them, the strain designated KNUF-22-014 was identified as a promising candidate for the novel fungal species and was thus chosen for further comprehensive analysis. The stock culture of strain KNUF-22-014 (NIBRFGC000509833) was deposited in the National Institute of Biological Resources (NIBR) as a metabolically inactive culture.

Cultural and morphological characterization

To assess cultural and morphological characteristics, the isolated strain was cultured on PDA, malt extract agar (MEA; Difco, Detroit, MI), and synthetic nutrient-poor agar (SNA) [11]. The plates were incubated at 25 °C with a 12 h/12 h fluorescent light/dark photoperiod for 21 days [7], after which morphological characteristics were recorded. The conventional slide culture method was employed to examine isolated fungal conidia with slight modifications. PDA, MEA, and SNA blocks (5 mm × 5 mm) were placed inside the lid, covered with a sterilized coverslip, and then incubated at 25 °C in the dark for 15 days. Once the fungus colonized the coverslip, it was transferred to an objective slide for observation under an Olympus BX50 light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

DNA extraction, PCR, sequencing, and phylogenetic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted using the HiGene Genomic DNA Prep Kit (BIOFACT, Daejeon, South Korea) following the prescribed protocols from the manufacturer and was stored at −20 °C until further use. Amplification was performed for various genetic regions, including ITS, LSU, TEF-1α, TUB, and RPB1. ITS regions were amplified using primers ITS1(F)/ITS4(R) to confirm the generic placement of the isolate [10]. LSU, TEF-1α, TUB, and RPB1 genes were amplified using the primer pairs LROR(F)/LR7(R), 983(F)/2218(R), T12(F)/T22(R), and RPB1-Af(F)/RPB1-6R1asc(R), respectively [7]. PCR was performed under previously described thermal conditions [1,7,10]. Purification of PCR products was conducted using EXOSAP-IT (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), followed by sequencing at Bioneer Co. Ltd. (Daejeon, South Korea). The obtained sequences from KNUF-22-014 were deposited in the GenBank database at the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) under accession numbers LC802123 for LSU, LC802118 for ITS, LC802124 for TEF-1α, LC802126 for TUB, and LC802125 for RPB1. Ambiguous regions were removed from the alignments using SeqMan Lasergene software (DNAStar Inc., Madison, WI). Sequences were aligned with reference sequences in NCBI GenBank using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) (Table 1). Neighbor-joining (NJ) phylogenies based on the combined LSU and ITS sequences and maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenies based on the concatenated LSU, ITS, TEF-1α, TUB, and RPB1 sequences were conducted using MEGA 7.0 software, with bootstrap values based on 1000 replications [12,13].

Etymology: The specific epithet “sobaeksanense” refers to Sobaeksan National Park where the strain was isolated.

Typus: KNUF-22-014 was collected from soil from Sobaeksan National Park, Yeongju City, Gyeongsangbuk-do, South Korea (36°56′31.1″N, 128°31′37.3″E) in 2021.

Habitat: Members of the Tolypocladium genus have a broad ecological distribution and are found in diverse habitats such as soils, rotifers, lichens, truffle-like fungi, and small dipteran insects [7]. Moreover, some species were isolated from the trunk of Pinus densa, indicating that these species have an endophytic lifestyle [10]. The novel species proposed in this study was found in soil collected from the rhizosphere of a deciduous tree in the mountains of South Korea.

Cultural characteristics: On PDA, the colonies grew to a diameter of 72.7–72.9 mm and were white with floccose, aerial mycelium, while the reverse surface exhibited a yellow pigment (Figure 1(A)). On MEA, the colonies grew to 75.3–76.6 mm in diameter and were white with floccose, abundant mycelium, while the reverse side was white or pale yellow (Figure 1(B)). On SNA, colonies grew to 53.5–55.7 mm in diameter and appeared scarious with flat white mycelium, while the reverse surface was white (Figure 1(C)). Although all three media were incubated at 25 °C with a 12 h/12 h fluorescent light/dark photoperiod for 21 days, growth on SNA was notably slower than on PDA or MEA.

Morphological characteristics: Morphological structures were studied using the colonies grown on PDA, MEA, and SNA. Conidia were abundantly produced on SNA and were also observed on PDA and MEA. There were no morphological differences in conidia across the types of media. The conidia were globose, hyaline, and smooth-walled, aggregating at the apex of phialides, and 2.1 ± 0.1 μm in size based on the average measurement of 100 conidiospores (Figure 1(G)). Phialides were subcylindrical, swollen, curved, or erect, measuring 12.2 ± 4.7 μm long and 1.7 ± 0.4 μm wide (Figure 1(D–F)). Intercalary phialides were often produced and short phialides were commonly observed attached to mycelia (Figure 1(E,F)). Conidiophores were mostly unbranched and occasionally arborescent (Figure 1(D)). Chlamydospores were hyaline, smooth-walled, and intercalary (Figure 1(H)). Sexual morph was unknown (Table 2).

Phylogenetic analysis of the strain KNUF-22-014

The novel Tolypocladium species was investigated using the BLAST tool from NCBI, and the molecular markers LSU, ITS, TEF-1α, TUB, and RPB1 were examined. Based on the sequencing results, KNUF-22-014 sequence lengths were 818 bp for LSU, 584 bp, 922 bp for TEF-1α, 782 bp for TUB, and 1053 bp for RPB1. BLAST search results using the KNUF-22-014 LSU sequence showed high similarities with multiple strains. Specifically, the KNUF-22-014 LSU sequence exhibited 99.88% similarity with T. tropicale IQ35 (KF747127.1), 99.75% similarity with T. amazonense MS507 (KF747141.1), 99.63% similarity with T. album M73 (HM595579.1), 99.63% similarity with T. endophyticum MX66 (KF747157.1), and 99.51% similarity with T. pustulatum MF5368LR (AF373282.1). The ITS sequence of strain KNUF-22-014 was closely related to that of T. tropicale MX338 (KF747259.1) (99.62% similarity), but was relatively far from its next closest neighbor, T. cylindrosporum AB208110 (AB208110.1), with a 94.89% similarity. The TEF-1α sequence of the isolate was 98.65% and 96.10% identical with that of T. tropicale IQ45 (KF747093.1) and T. amazonense LA100 (KF747094.1), respectively. The TUB gene sequence of KNUF-22-014 was 97.50% and 93.79% similar to that of T. tropicale MX337 (KF747189.1) and T. endophyticum MX335 (KF747188.1), respectively. Based on the RPB1 sequence similarity, the closest phylogenetic relative of the isolate was T. tropicale IQ136 (KF747200.1) (96.45% similarity), while its next closest neighbor, T. amazonense MS533 (KF747222.1), showed a significantly lower similarity of 90.48%. Depending on the applied molecular markers, T. amazonense, T. album, T. cylindrosporum, T. endophyticum, T. pustulatum, and T. tropicale were identified as the closest phylogenetic relatives of the strain KNUF-22-014. Remarkably, T. tropicale was listed in all cases. However, none of the gene sequences provided precise identification of the novel fungal strain at the species level. Therefore, MLSA was performed using two combinations of concatenated sequences involving the abovementioned molecular markers. Considering that the sequences of all five loci were not available for the close neighbors of KNUF-22-014, a phylogenetic tree was initially constructed based on concatenated sequences of the LSU gene and ITS regions. The topology of the NJ phylogenetic tree demonstrated that the isolate belongs to the genus Tolypocladium (Figure 2). Its closest phylogenetic neighbor is T. tropicale, but KNUF-22-014 is distinct from T. tropicale and the other above-mentioned close relatives. Furthermore, the ML phylogenetic tree based on the five concatenated loci (LSU, ITS, TEF-1α, TUB, and RPB1) (Figure 3) visibly confirmed that KNUF-22-014 is separated from T. tropicale with a high bootstrap value of 100%. These results indicate that the novel strain represents a single, distinct, phylogenetically novel species within the genus Tolypocladium, for which the name T. sobaeksanense is proposed.

Discussion

Strain KNUF-22-014 was isolated in 2021 from soil samples collected in Sobaeksan National Park, Yeongju City, South Korea. This strain exhibited morphological differences from the phylogenetically closely related T. tropicale, producing an arborescent or unbranched pattern of conidiophores, subcylindrical phialides, and larger conidia. Additionally, KNUF-22-014 colonies appear white on the front and yellow on the reverse, whereas T. tropicale colonies are white on both sides when grown on PDA. Morphological analysis and molecular phylogeny confirmed the identification of KNUF-22-014 as a novel species of the genus Tolypocladium.

Tolypocladium species are known for their wide distribution and diverse habitats, often associated with various hosts, including insects like rotifers, mosquito larvae, nematodes, fireflies, and beetles, as well as substrates like soil and decaying organic matter [1,4,14]. These species mostly parasitize the truffle-like fungal genus Elaphomyces, with occasional parasitism observed in soil-dwelling cicada nymphs (Cicadidae, Hemiptera) and larvae of wood-boring beetles (Coleoptera) [14]. The entomopathogenic activity is of great interest for biological control of insect pests in agriculture and forestry. Species like T. inflatum and T. cylindrosporum are noted for their potential in biocontrol due to their ability to target specific insect pests without harming beneficial insects or other non-target organisms [4,15]. T. inflatum, T. ophioglossoides, T. paradoxum, and other Tolypocladium species are also known for their antagonistic properties against various fungi due to their ability to produce antifungal compounds [2,16]. These properties underline the ecological importance and potential practical applications of Tolypocladium species in sustainable agriculture and pest management [8,17].

Tolypocladium species have diverse ecological niches, often linked to different substrates through both asexual and sexual states. In Korea, Tolypocladium was found to be uniquely present in the wetland of Jangdo Island, exhibiting a high dominance ratio [18]. T. album was isolated from soil for the first time in Korea in 2018, and T. globosum was reported as a novel species in 2023 [19,20]. Recent research in Korea has highlighted the medical potential of Tolypocladium species. A study by Jung et al. on T. cylindrosporum FB06, isolated from marine sediment, demonstrated the neuroprotective properties of its compounds. One compound, F-14329, significantly protected brain cells in Parkinson’s disease model, helping to restore important cell proteins. This study showed the medicinal potential of Tolypocladium species, indicating they could be used for developing new treatments for neurological diseases [21].

The widespread distribution of Tolypocladium species is attributed to their excellent adaptive capacity. One of the key features of this fungal adaptation is the production of secondary metabolites [22]. Members of the genus produce a wide range of compounds with various biological activities. Among these, cyclosporine A, produced by T. inflatum, is the best known and is used in medicine as a potent immunosuppressant for transplantation, significantly improving the survival rates of patients [23]. Eight compounds, including cyclosporine A, efrapeptin D, pyridoxatin, terricolin A, malettinins B and E, and tolypocladenols A and C were isolated from T. geodes. The compounds were tested for their anticancer activities against NCI60 cancer cells, and malettinins B and E were identified as the most promising candidates [24]. Several other secondary metabolites from Tolypocladium strains, such as aphidicolin, inflatins A, B, and G, metarhizin, terpendole E, and tolypocladin A, also displayed cytotoxic activity against various cancer cells [2,25]. In addition to their anticancer properties, the abovementioned compound efrapeptin D as well as efrapeptin F exhibited high insecticidal activity against the Colorado potato beetle. Additionally, cyclosporines B, C, and D, terpendole L, and tolypyridone A were characterized as promising antifungal and/or antibacterial agents [2,16]. T. sobaeksanense as a novel species offers an additional opportunity to broaden research on bioactive compounds, making further investigation of its properties highly worthwhile.

In conclusion, the discovery of the novel Tolypocladium species, T. sobaeksanense, warrants further research into the ecological and biological roles of this species. While this study focused on its morphological and molecular characteristics, more extensive investigations are needed to fully understand its ecological interactions, distribution patterns, and potential applications in biotechnology. Future studies exploring these aspects will offer valuable insights for agricultural and biotechnological applications.
