Analysis of Lipid Profile Parameters in COPD Patients and Their Correlation with Disease Severity
Authors: Pulkit Gupta, Atul Tiwari, Ashish Ranjan, Ravikant Pandey, Ashutosh Singh, Nishtha Bassi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:To compare the status of lipid profile parameters in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) patients with controls (without COPD) and its correlation with the severity of the disease.Materials and Methods:At “Banaras Hindu University’s Institute of Medical Sciences, Sir Sunderlal Hospital in Varanasi,” a case–control study was carried out. Patients with COPD aged more than 40 years and presented to SS Hospital with an acute exacerbation were screened, and those who satisfied the inclusion and exclusion criteria were chosen for the study. Proper history and physical examination were done and complete blood count, lipid profile, renal function test, liver function test, high sensitivity C-reactive protein, arterial blood gas, spirometry, ECHO, and electrocardiogram were performed and data was collected and recorded for analysis.Results:It was discovered that there was no statistically significant increase in the lipid profile characteristics of patients as compared to controls. Lastly, there was no discernible relationship between the patient’s serum lipid profile and COPD based on data and statistics. Additionally, there was no statistically significant relationship found between the patient’s serum lipid profile and the severity of their COPD.Conclusion:Although we did not identify a relationship between lipoproteins and the severity of COPD disease, more thorough research is needed to obtain reliable findings.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is one of the top four causes of mortality globally, which is increasing day by day needing a huge part of the budget allotted to the health sector, thus leading to increased comorbidity and mortality.[12] Abnormalities in lipid parameters are quite significant. Any change in lipid metabolism raises cardiovascular risk, which in turn raises COPD patients’ mortality and morbidity rates. Lipid profile changes might be either qualitative or quantitative. Size differences in lipid parameters, a rise in low-density lipoprotein (LDL) triglyceride content, and the propensity of LDL cholesterol to generate peroxides—which have been identified as the primary cause of atherogenesis in diabetes patients—are examples of qualitative changes.[3] It has been demonstrated that body fat and lipid profiles are both significant predictors of metabolic disorders such as dyslipidemia, hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular illnesses, and hyperinsulinemia, among others.[4]

A few studies have investigated the relationship of lipid profile to lung function in individuals with obstructive lung disease. A study by Begum et al.[5] showed that the values of total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), and LDL were higher and HDL was lower in COPD patients than healthy controls. According to a study by Rao et al.,[6] individuals with COPD had considerably higher serum levels of LDL than healthy controls. The degree of airflow restriction in patients with COPD was not correlated with the serum lipid profile. In 2015, a similar study by Mitra et al.[7] demonstrated that patients with COPD had significantly higher serum levels of TC, TG, LDL, and interleukin-8 (IL8), while also having significantly lower serum concentrations of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) than controls. According to a 2017 study by Jain et al.,[8] dyslipidemia was more common in patients with moderate to severe COPD; however, there was no significant correlation between the severity of COPD and a disordered lipid profile. While there was no discernible difference in the levels of lipid markers between the COPD patients and the controls, Nillawar et al.[9] found that the high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (HS-CRP) levels were considerably higher than those of the controls.

So, in this study, an effort was made to find a correlation between lipid profiles and the severity of COPD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was a case–control study conducted at “Sir Sunderlal Hospital, Institute of Medical Sciences, Banaras Hindu University, Varanasi.” COPD patients having acute exacerbation more than 40 years of age and those meeting the selection criteria were selected for the research. According to the “2017 Global Obstructive Lung Disease” standard recommendations, the diagnosis of COPD was determined using the patient’s chest X-ray, medical history, physical examination, and spirometry data. Following usual protocols, the initial arterial blood gas (ABG) was performed in the chest ward as soon as the patient was admitted.

Inclusion criteria

Patients with COPD who are older than 40 years and have post-bronchodilator reversibility <12% and FEV1/FVC <70%.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with domiciliary oxygen therapy, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, inflammatory diseases, coagulopathies, renal diseases, hemodynamically unstable patients, malignancies, liver diseases, anticoagulant/antiplatelet medication use, long-term steroids use, drug abuse, pregnancy, alcoholics, and active smokers were excluded.

Control group

In the Indian population, those more than 40 years old are hemodynamically stable.

Methods

A proper history and physical examination were done and lipid profile, liver function test (LFT), complete blood count (CBC), ABG, renal function test (RFT), HS-CRP, ECG, spirometry, and ECHO were performed and data was gathered and recorded for examination. Patients were pursued for 30 days at the hospital and telephonically discharged.

Classification of airflow limitation severity in COPD (based on post-bronchodilator FEV1) was assessed using GOLD classification.

Spirometry

Patients who were taking inhaled bronchodilators were advised to stop taking them at least 12 h before the spirometry test. Those with post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC <70% and no substantial post-bronchodilator (salbutamol) reversibility were included in the trial. The best three efforts were totaled electronically using a conventional spirometer. A mMRC dyspnoea scale was used to further categorize the patients.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using IBM SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) version 25 utilizing chi-square and Mann–Whitney test. A P value was set at <0.05.

DISCUSSION

The mean age in the control group was 60.68 ± 7.78, whereas in the case group, it was 60.82 ± 8.68. Most people are at least 40 years old when symptoms of COPD first appear. Certain genetic conditions such as alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency can predispose younger people to developing COPD. In previous studies, the mean age group was also above 40. Similarly, in the study by Rao et al.[6] (2015), the mean age of the male patients among cases was 63.32 ± 10.73 years, and that of the female patients in the study was 63 ± 10.18 years. In the study by Jain et al. (2017),[8] 52% of the patients were between the ages of 40 and 60, and 44% were between the ages of 61 and 80 years. In the study by Ratabrata et al. (2017),[7] it was 59.6814 ± 5.33982 for controls and 60.4031 ± 5.64892 for cases, and the mean age of the cases was 61 ± 11.31 years, while the mean age of the controls was 59 ± 10 years.

This study did not show any significant association between COPD and serum lipid profile. Serum lipid profile parameters like serum TC, TG, LDL, VLDL, HDL, serum LDL/HDL, and serum cholesterol/HDL were included in this study. There was no variation in the lipid profile of the patients when compared to the control nor between lipid profile and severity of COPD.

These findings were in line with those of a study by Nillawar et al.[9] (2013), which found no discernible difference between the COPD patients’ and controls’ levels of cholesterol markers. They came to the conclusion that it was difficult to identify cases with high-risk CVD since the alterations in the lipid markers had a late chronology. Smoking alters the lipid profile in such a way that smokers have lower HDL cholesterol and greater plasma levels of LDL, cholesterol, and TG than nonsmokers. It is unknown, nevertheless, if dyslipidemia is an additional autonomous factor that could account for the CVD and death in individuals with COPD. Therefore, more research would be necessary to fully comprehend the relationship between lipid profiles in COPD patients, taking into account the fact that smoking not only causes COPD but also dyslipidemia.

The limitation of this study is its cross-sectional study design and because of its lack of causal association, it is not taken seriously, though it shows some vital information that must be either extrapolated or replicated through a well-designed prospective, randomized trial.

CONCLUSION

Although we did not identify a relationship between lipoproteins and the severity of COPD disease, more thorough research is needed to obtain reliable findings. There must be an integrated pathway connecting COPD and cardiovascular issues because statin treatment decreases mortality rates in COPD patients.
