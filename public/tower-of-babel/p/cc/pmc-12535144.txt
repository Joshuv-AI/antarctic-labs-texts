Utilizing resting motor threshold to predict cognitive function in drug-naive patients with major depressive disorder
Authors: Li Xu, Jing He, Lei Yang, Decai Du, Fan Yang, Limei Feng, Yuxiong Jin, Chao Li, Jianzhong Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is a common and disabling condition associated with significant cognitive impairments that persist over the long term, affecting patients’ social function. Traditional cognitive testing methods are time-consuming and subjective. This study investigates whether resting motor threshold (RMT), a neurophysiological measure of motor cortical excitability, can predict cognitive function in drug-naive patients with MDD, with a focus on sex-specific differences.MethodsThis cross-sectional study enrolled 158 first-episode, drug-naive patients with MDD from August 2023 to April 2024. Participants underwent comprehensive cognitive assessments using the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB) and transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) to measure RMT. Hormone levels were also assessed. The study was approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Statistical analysis included bivariate correlation and multiple regression analyses to identify factors associated with cognitive function.ResultsA total of 138 subjects completed the study. The mean age was 19.28 ± 6.84 years, with 41 males (29.7%) and 97 females (70.3%). In the overall sample, higher right RMT was associated with better attention/vigilance (CPT-IP) performance. In male patients, higher left RMT was associated with lower verbal learning (HVLT-R) scores, while higher right RMT was associated with higher HVLT-R scores. No significant correlations were found in female patients. Multiple regression analyses showed that these relationships were independent of age, education, and hormone levels.ConclusionsThis exploratory study is the first to demonstrate that RMT can predict verbal learning ability, attention, and vigilance in male patients with MDD, whereas no significant correlation was observed in female patients. This finding reveals RMT’s potential significance and highlights that sex differences necessitate consideration when developing rTMS-based treatment regimens targeting both antidepressant and cognitive outcomes. Further longitudinal studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate its clinical relevance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07393-z.Keywords: Major depressive disorder, Cognitive function, Resting motor threshold, Transcranial magnetic stimulation, Sex differences

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a severe, often chronic and disabling condition with high prevalence across diverse cultural and age groups [1]. Notably, the incidence of MDD among adolescents and adults has risen steadily in recent years [2, 3]. Beyond affective symptoms, over half of patients exhibit persistent and widespread cognitive impairment (CI) [4–6], affecting core domains such as executive control, attention, working memory, verbal learning, and information processing speed [4, 7]. These deficits directly compromise academic, occupational, and social functioning while impeding clinical recovery [8–10]. Mounting evidence implicates dysregulation of glutamate, Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid (GABA), and monoaminergic neurotransmission within prefrontal-limbic and prefrontal-striatal circuits, particularly glutamatergic excitotoxicity, as the neurobiological substrate of CI in MDD [11–13]. Excessive activation of glutamate receptors (e.g.,N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor, α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptor) induces neuronal death via calcium overload preferentially damaging prefrontal-limbic pathways critical for executive control and working memory [14, 15].

Although MDD treatment guidelines emphasize the importance of cognitive assessment [16], current clinical evaluation tools face significant challenges in three domains [17]: subjective limitations due to reliance on insensitive instruments like dementia scales [18], ecological validity deficits from computerized tests that poorly simulate real-world demands [19, 20], and feasibility barriers arising from costly or time-intensive neuroimaging and neuropsychological assessments [21]. There is growing recognition that traditional diagnostic approaches are inadequate, and new initiatives are underway to develop objective biological markers to aid with diagnosis, early identification, treatment selection, and clinical monitoring [22–25]. Therefore, developing a method that can rapidly and objectively assess cognitive function in patients with MDD would be of significant value.

The Resting Motor Threshold (RMT) offers a promising solution to this impasse. As an objective, transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS)-derived metric [26], RMT bridges the cognitive pathology of MDD (MDD) with clinical assessment through three core properties. First, pathological specificity. RMT directly quantifies glutamatergic receptor function in the motor cortex [27]. Proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy demonstrates a robust positive correlation between primary motor-cortex glutamate metabolite levels and RMT [28]. Convergent in-vitro and in-vivo studies indicate that patients with MDD exhibit heightened intracortical facilitation and excessive glutamatergic activity [12, 15]. Glutamate is the brain’s principal excitatory neurotransmitter system [29], and its dysregulation is a central mechanism underlying cognitive impairment in MDD. Sustained over-activation of glutamate receptors precipitates neuronal death, especially under conditions of compromised energy supply and elevated oxidative stress [29]. This excitotoxic cascade—triggered by elevated extracellular glutamate, subsequent NMDA and AMPA receptor activation, and excessive calcium influx—selectively damages prefrontal–limbic circuits, compromising executive control, working memory, and attention [30, 31]. In Alzheimer’s disease, elevated RMT predicts global cognitive decline [32, 33], and certain TMS parameters have been proposed as markers of mild vascular cognitive impairment [34], underscoring the potential of motor-cortex excitability as a trans-diagnostic neurophysiological correlate of cognitive deterioration. Second, Cortico-Cognitive Connectivity as a Network Substrate. The motor cortex is not an isolated target for excitatory measurement. There is evidence that corticospinal excitability might be correlated with excitability in other cortical regions [31]. Evidence confirms that corticospinal excitability correlates with dynamic states in functionally coupled cortical regions(e.g., dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, DLPFC) [35]. This coupling is structurally anchored by long-range white-matter tracts, primarily the superior longitudinal fasciculus [35], which enables bidirectional propagation of excitability states along the motor-DLPFC axis [36]. Third, clinical feasibility. Repetitive TMS (rTMS) is a first-line physical treatment for MDD and shares neuroplastic mechanisms with RMT assessment, enabling bidirectional translation between evaluation and therapy [37]. Baseline RMT can guide individualized rTMS protocols (e.g., high-frequency left-sided stimulation to up-regulate hypo-excitable regions), while serial RMT monitoring allows dynamic optimization of treatment trajectories [38]. Standardized protocols endorsed by consensus guidelines ensure inter-rater reliability [39]. The motor cortex is a favoured target area for neuroscientific studies because changes in motor activation and excitability can be readily assessed by recording motor evoked potentials (MEPs) [30]. RMT requires minimal acquisition time (< 20 min), employs straightforward analysis pipelines, and is well tolerated by patients [40], allowing seamless integration into routine outpatient workflows.

Collectively, RMT anchors the excitotoxic glutamatergic cascade (the pivotal pathophysiology underlying cognitive impairment in MDD while leveraging motor-prefrontal network coupling. This dual foundation establishes RMT as an ideal translational bridge connecting neurophysiological metrics to multidimensional cognitive function. Furthermore, its mechanistic homology with rTMS enables a precision clinical loop integrating assessment, targeted intervention, and dynamic optimization.

Although prior studies have used RMT to examine the relationship between cortical excitability and depression severity in patients with MDD, findings have been inconsistent [12, 41–43], possibly due to medication-related confounding effects. Moreover, the association between motor-cortical excitability and cognitive performance in MDD remains largely unexplored. Building on evidence that RMT reflects glutamate-receptor-mediated cortical excitability and on demonstrations of correlations between cortical excitability and cognitive function in neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease [44], we hypothesized that, in drug-naïve MDD patients, RMT would be associated with cognitive performance. In addition, given the modulatory role of sex hormones on cortical excitability [45]and well-documented sex differences in brain structure and neurochemical systems [46], we pre-planned stratified analyses by sex. This design aimed to test the hypothesis that the relationship between RMT and cognitive function would differ between males and females.

Methods

Participants

This cross-sectional study consecutively enrolled 158 first-episode, drug-naïve patients with MDD at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University between August 2023 and April 2024. Diagnoses were established using the structured Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI 7.0.2), strictly adhering to ICD-10 criteria (code F32). Senior psychiatrists applied the depression module (Section A) to confirm core symptoms—depressed mood, anhedonia, and fatigue lasting at least two weeks—and to grade severity as mild, moderate, or severe. Prior manic or hypomanic episodes were actively excluded via the bipolar module (Section D). Untreated anxiety disorders classified under ICD-10 codes F40–F48 (e.g., generalized anxiety disorder F41.1, social phobia F40.1) were permitted, whereas obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD, F42) was excluded because of its distinct neuropathology and cognitive features that might confound neurophysiological findings in MDD research. Exclusion criteria encompassed intellectual disability, pervasive developmental disorders, schizophrenia, alcohol or substance dependence, severe chronic systemic diseases, and contraindications to transcranial magnetic stimulation or epilepsy risk. Written informed consent was obtained from adults (≥ 18 years); minors (< 18 years) provided written assent alongside guardian consent. This dual-consent protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (No. 2023 − 195) and implemented in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Assessment

Assessment methods for depression, anxiety, and sleep

The Self-rating Depression Scale (SDS) [47] was used to assess depressive symptoms, the self-rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) [48] was used to assess anxiety symptoms, and Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS-8) [49]was used to assess sleep status. The score [50] of SDS and SAS was the total score of 20 items (raw score), which was converted into standard score, and the formula was standard score = raw score×1.5. The higher the standard score, the more severe the depression and anxiety. The standard score less than 50 indicates the normal state, and the score between 50 and 60 indicates the mild condition. Scores of 60 to 70 indicated moderate status, and scores of more than 70 indicated severe status. The optimal cut-off value of AIS-8 for the diagnosis of insomnia is 6, and the higher the score, the more severe the insomnia [49, 51].

Cognitive assessment

A cognitive test battery (duration approximately 2 h) was administered by a trained investigator and included the following five domains: verbal learning, assessed by the Hopkins Verbal Learning Test-Revised (HVLT-R); visual learning, assessed by the Brief Visual Memory Test-Revised (BVMT-R); [41, 43] speed, assessed by the Brief Assessment of Cognition in Schizophrenia, Symbol Coding subtest (BACS-SC); reasoning and problem solving (RPS), assessed by the Neuropsychology Assessment Battery, Mazes (NAB-Mazes); and attention or vigilance, assessed by the Continuous Performance Test, Identical Pairs version (CPT-IP). The five domains were derived from the authorized Measurement and Treatment Research to Improve Cognition in Schizophrenia (MATRICS) Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB) (Chinese version), which was introduced to China by the Mental Health Institute of Peking University [52]. The MCCB has been used to test cognitive function in adolescent and adult MDD patients [21, 53–55]. Higher scores on all items reflect better neurocognitive function in the related domains. The scores after adjusting for age, sex, area of residence, and educational levels (T-score) for each item and domain were calculated in this study, and lower scores indicate more severe cognitive impairment [56].

TMS procedures

TMS as a non-invasive neurophysiological assessment technique, was initially employed to evaluate the excitability of the primary motor cortex (M1) and the functional integrity of the corticospinal tract [57]. In this study, the determination of the RMT in bilateral hemispheres was conducted under standardized experimental conditions: participants remained seated in a relaxed state in a sound-attenuated chamber using a dedicated armchair, and all assessments were performed by the same operator during a fixed time window (09:30–11:30 daily) to minimize inter-individual variability. Stimulation target localization strictly adhered to the International 10–20 System for electroencephalography (EEG) electrode placement: the Cz point (intersection of the nasion-inion line and the intertragal line) was determined by precise measurement of cranial midline landmarks (nasion, inion) and bilateral preauricular points. The target M1 hand area (left/right) corresponded to the standard EEG positions C3/C4, with initial coil placement approximately 6–7 cm lateral to the Cz reference point toward the target hemisphere. Surface electrodes were used to record motor evoked potentials (MEPs) from the bilateral abductor digiti minimi (ADM) muscles, with the active electrode placed over the muscle belly and the reference electrode positioned at the metacarpophalangeal joint of the little finger. Data acquisition, storage, and signal processing were performed using the Natus Keypoint 9033A07 electromyography/evoked potential system, while the MagPro R30 stimulator with a figure-of-eight coil precisely targeted the M1 region. RMT determination followed the standardized protocol of the International Federation of Clinical Neurophysiology (IFCN) [58]: single-pulse TMS was initially applied at subthreshold intensity (~ 30% of maximum stimulator output) over the contralateral M1, followed by incremental intensity adjustments (1–5% steps) until stable MEP elicitation was achieved. Subsequently, the intensity was decreased in 1% decrements until at least 5 out of 10 consecutive stimuli evoked MEPs with an amplitude ≥ 50µV. The final RMT was defined as the minimum stimulus intensity meeting this criterion, expressed as a percentage of the maximum stimulator output [59].

Hormone determination

The assessment of hormone variables was undertaken at a single point in time (8:00 am-9:00 am). The testing for estradiol, progesterone, and testosterone was undertaken by the staff of the Nuclear Medicine Department at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University using eletrochemiluminescence immunoassay kit (Cobas 6000) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [60].

Statistical analysis

Data were managed in Microsoft Excel and analyzed with SPSS Statistics (version 26.0). Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using an independent-samples t-test; non-normally distributed continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]) (M [P25, P75]). Categorical data are reported as counts (n) and percentages (%). Pearson correlation was used for bivariate analyses involving normally distributed variables; Spearman rank correlation was employed for analyses that included the non-normally distributed hormone measures (estradiol, progesterone, testosterone). Multivariable analyses were performed with multiple linear regression, constructing three models: Model 1 included only the independent variables—left and right RMT; Model 2 additionally adjusted for age, years of education, maternal years of education, and paternal years of education; Model 3 further adjusted for estradiol, progesterone, and testosterone levels. All tests were two-tailed, with statistical significance set at α = 0.05.

Results

Among the study participants, no adverse events or reactions were reported

A total of 138 subjects completed the entire testing protocol and were included in the analysis (20 subjects did not complete all tests). The participants’ clinical demographics, sex hormone levels, and neuropsychological characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 19.28 ± 6.84 years, with 41 males (29.7%) and 97 females (70.3%). Most participants were students (71.0%), with an average education of 11.62 ± 3.07 years. The mean disease duration was 6 months, with SDS, SAS, and Athens Insomnia Scale scores of 70.47 ± 12.03, 65.22 ± 11.91, and 13.45 ± 5.23, respectively. RMT values for the left and right hemispheres were 54.98 ± 9.98% and 52.83 ± 9.49%, respectively. No significant differences were found in demographic or clinical variables between male and female MDD patients. However, significant differences were observed in BVMT-R, BACS-SC, and NAB Mazes scores between sexes, with female MDD patients having higher BVMT-R and BACS-SC scores and male MDD patients having a higher NAB Mazes score (P < 0.05). Subgroup analysis of cognitive function in female patients with MDD

In separate multiple regression analyses conducted for HVLT-R, BVMT-R, BACS-SC, NAB Mazes, and CPT-IP, it was found that in Model 1, neither left nor right RMT had any correlation on patients’ cognitive function. Discussion

Sex-specific patterns of RMT-cognition association

This study sought to elucidate whether RMT, a TMS measure of motor cortical excitability, is associated with cognition in MDD patients and whether this association is impacted by sex, age, education or sex hormones. Our results reveal that greater right hemisphere RMT is associated with better attention or vigilance (CPT-IP) performance in the whole sample., an effect primarily accounted for by male subjects. The right hemisphere RMT of males shows a positive correlation with the CPT-IP score after running three models.That is, in male MDD patients, lower motor cortex excitability in the right hemisphere predicts better attention/alertness function. Multiple regression analysis of bilateral RMT and HVLT-R scores in male patients with MDD consistently showed that higher motor cortex excitability in the left hemisphere corresponded to higher HVLT-R scores, while lower motor cortex excitability in the right hemisphere was associated with higher HVLT-R scores. These results indicate that in male patients with MDD, the relationship between RMT of the right cerebral hemisphere and CPT-IP scores, as well as the relationship between bilateral RMT and HVLT-R scores, exist independently, unaffected by age, educational background, or hormone levels. However, age and educational background may play a moderating role in the relationship between left hemisphere RMT and CPT-IP scores in male MDD patients.Additionally, the correlation between left RMT and BACS-SC scores as well as NAB Mazes scores may be influenced by age, educational background, and hormonal levels. To our knowledge, these results demonstrate for the first time a direct correlation between motor cortical excitability and certain cognitive measures in patients with MDD. These findings suggest that RMT is not only associated with corticospinal excitability but may also be related to a broader state of cortical activity. Previous research [61]has found that changes in cerebral motor cortical plasticity are closely linked to alterations in cognitive function in MDD patients, indicating that the motor cortex may play a crucial role in cognitive disorders. However, we did not observe a significant correlation between bilateral RMT and the scores of the five cognitive tests administered in this study for female MDD patients.

Mechanistic homology of rTMS-RMT

Our findings reveal that, in male patients with MDD, increased left-motor-cortex excitability (lower RMT) or decreased right-motor-cortex excitability (higher RMT) is associated with cognitive improvement—a pattern that closely mirrors standard rTMS protocols for MDD [37–39]: high-frequency stimulation over the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) to potentiate cortical activity and ameliorate hypo-activation of the prefrontal cognitive-control network, thereby enhancing verbal learning and executive function [62, 63], and low-frequency stimulation over the right DLPFC to down-regulate hyperactive amygdala–anterior-cingulate circuitry, reduce anxiety and attentional interference, and optimize vigilance [64]. The observed RMT profile in the motor cortex likely reflects downstream DLPFC modulation conveyed via fronto-motor white-matter tracts such as the superior longitudinal fasciculus [65], accounting for why this excitability signature predicts cognitive gains comparable to those achieved with rTMS.

Supporting this relationship are multi-level neuroplastic mechanisms: at the molecular level [66], rTMS triggers neurochemical cascades encompassing neurotransmitter release, altered gene expression [67], neuroendocrine modulation, and shifts in AMPA/NMDA receptor expression [68], collectively conferring neuroprotection by attenuating oxidative stress and activating neurotrophic signaling [69]; at the immune–cellular level, rTMS-induced reductions in IL-1β correlate with cognitive improvement in treatment-resistant MDD [69], while peripheral cytokines influence central networks via bioactive effectors [70], and inflammatory cytokines impair astrocytic glutamate uptake and promote its release, producing excitotoxicity when glial clearance is compromised [71], with animal models further indicating that cytokines modulate long-term potentiation, neurogenesis, and synaptic plasticity to shape cognition [66]. Clinically, rTMS alleviates depressive symptoms and concurrently enhances cognition in MDD patients [72], and similar benefits emerge in healthy volunteers [73]. Crucially, RMT’s value lies in its glutamatergic specificity: as a direct read-out of ionotropic glutamate receptor function in the motor cortex, its fluctuations indirectly capture the integrated impact of these multi-level remodeling processes on cognitive networks [74], suggesting that the RMT–cognition association reported here represents the neurophysiological signature of a pathological cascade in which glutamatergic dysfunction drives cognitive impairment.

Biological mechanisms of sex differences and bias analysis

In this study, the correlations between estradiol, progesterone, testosterone, and RMT were analyzed in the overall cohort of MDD patients and after stratification by sex. A weak negative correlation was observed between right-sided RMT and estradiol levels in male MDD patients, while no other statistically significant associations were found. This may be attributed to the sample characteristics and disease-specific factors: abnormalities in neurotransmitter systems and neural network function in depressed patients may obscure the conventional relationship between hormones and cortical excitability [75]. For instance, dysfunction in the glutamate/GABA system in depression could interfere with the modulation of cortical excitability by estradiol, progesterone, and testosterone [76–78]. Future studies should further investigate the underlying mechanisms and expand sample sizes to enhance statistical power.

Another key finding of this study was the absence of significant correlations between bilateral RMT and all five assessed cognitive domains in female MDD patients, suggesting limited predictive value of RMT for cognitive function in this population. This observed gender disparity in RMT-cognition relationships may stem from the interactions of three biological factors: First, neuroendocrine regulation. The elevated testosterone levels in males (5.06 vs. 0.29 ng/mL) enhance NMDA receptor sensitivity, rendering motor cortex excitability more responsive to glutamate-dependent cognition [45]. In contrast, higher estradiol levels in females (78.43 vs. 31.06 pg/mL) attenuate glutamate-cognition coupling through γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-ergic inhibition, thereby reducing RMT’s predictive validity [79]. Second, functional connectivity differences [80, 81]. Stronger intrahemispheric connectivity in males enables direct cognitive modulation by local excitability changes, whereas females exhibit stronger interhemispheric connectivity that buffers the impact of excitability fluctuations in single brain regions [82]. Third, disease-modifying effects. Longitudinal neuroimaging studies of adolescent MDD reveal sexually dimorphic brain development patterns [83]: depressive symptoms correlate with accelerated amygdala growth in females but growth deceleration in males. Female depressive symptoms also associate with progressive nucleus accumbens volume reduction, a phenomenon absent in males. These differences may contribute to sex-specific motor-cognition network coupling.These findings suggest that inherent differences in endocrine profiles, functional connectivity, and brain structure during adolescence and early adulthood may underlie the gender-specific RMT-cognition relationships in MDD patients.

Furthermore, the observed gender differences in RMT-cognition correlations may partially reflect baseline cognitive disparities. Significant sex differences were found in cognitive performance: females demonstrated superior visual learning BVMT-R and processing speed BACS-SC (all p < 0.05), while males showed pronounced advantages in executive function (Neuropsychological Assessment Battery Mazes, NAB Mazes; 54.1 vs. 45.72, p < 0.001). These differences likely represent inherent sex-specific neurocognitive variations in depression pathophysiology, potentially related to divergent neurodevelopmental trajectories [84]. However, this introduces potential selection bias: the cohort’s predominance of students (71%) and hormonal profiles (e.g., higher testosterone in males, elevated estrogen/progesterone in females) may systematically influence baseline cognitive capacity [85, 86].

Thus, the observed significant correlation between RMT and cognitive function in male MDD patients, particularly the association of right-sided RMT with attention/verbal learning, likely stems from their executive function advantages (spatial working memory, attentional control). These advantages depend on glutamatergic excitability in the right prefrontal-parietal network [87], thereby amplifying the predictive validity of RMT for cognition. In females, however, superior visual memory and processing speed, mediated by efficient hippocampal-temporal white matter integration and estrogen-driven GABAergic inhibition, may attenuate the predictive association between cortical excitability (RMT) and cognition [88]. This suggests that our findings may exhibit cohort-specific dependencies. Nonetheless, multivariate regression models applied by our research team demonstrate that the associations of male RMT with HVLT-R and CPT-IP remain significant after controlling for education and hormonal factors. This supports the predominance of biological mechanisms over selection bias.

In summary, the correlation between RMT and word learning, attention, information processing speed, and reasoning may contribute to the understanding of human cognitive processes and has potentially important clinical implications.For instance, RMT determination can be utilized to explore and monitor pharmacological treatments known to affect cortical excitability and cognitive functions. Rapid clinical assessment of RMT may predict changes in word learning, attention, information processing speed, and reasoning abilities among male patients with MDD.

Limitations and future directions

First, while the observed correlations between RMT and cognitive domains are informative, causal inference constraints imposed by the cross-sectional design preclude definitive mechanistic conclusions; consequently, longitudinal studies tracking RMT dynamics across disease phases (e.g., depressive episodes versus remission) are essential to validate its stability as a biomarker for MDD-related cognitive impairment. Second, given the gender sample imbalance—driven by higher MDD incidence in adolescent females—subsequent investigations must rigorously match gender ratios and independently validate generalizability in occupationally diverse cohorts spanning multiple ethnicities and age groups. Furthermore, uncontrolled anatomical confounders (e.g., skull thickness, gray matter density) potentially confound RMT absolute values, necessitating the integration of MRI and neuronavigation for anatomical normalization through gray matter density correction or skull-cortex distance standardization to enhance measurement precision. Finally, to address insufficient mechanistic depth, future work should include gender-matched healthy controls to isolate MDD-specific pathways while concurrently exploring critical interactions between sex hormones (testosterone/estradiol) and glutamate receptor subtypes that modulate RMT-cognition associations.

Conclusion

This exploratory study is the first to demonstrate that RMT can predict verbal learning ability, attention, and vigilance in male patients with MDD, whereas no significant correlation was observed in female patients. This finding reveals RMT’s potential significance and highlights that sex differences necessitate consideration when developing rTMS-based treatment regimens targeting both antidepressant and cognitive outcomes. Further longitudinal studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate its clinical relevance.
