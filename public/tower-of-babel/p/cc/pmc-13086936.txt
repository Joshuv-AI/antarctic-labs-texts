Assessing farmers’ knowledge, attitudes, health risk perceptions, and practices toward pesticide use in Morocco
Authors: Aziza Menouni, Imane Berni, Hala Chetouani, Said Abousaid, Kaoutar Chbihi, Ibrahim El Ghazi, Amal Amellah, Radu-Corneliu Duca, Samir El Jaafari, Lode Godderis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Agriculture plays a vital role in Africa’s economy, yet farmworkers remain disproportionately exposed to pesticide-related health risks. Guided by the Theory of Planned Behavior, this study examined farmworkers’ knowledge, attitudes, practices, and perceptions regarding health risks associated with pesticide use and protective measures in Morocco. A cross-sectional study was conducted among 314 agricultural workers across seven major farming areas. Participants had a mean age of 40 ± 15.08 years, ranging from 18 to 77 years, were predominantly male (64.3%), and largely had no formal education or only basic literacy (74.2%). Among respondents with complete data (n = 300), 86.3% (n = 259) were unable to name the pesticides they used. Only 33.3% (n = 100) demonstrated good knowledge of pesticide safety, while 44.7% (n = 134) expressed positive attitudes toward safe pesticide application. Structural analysis showed that safety attitudes were the strongest determinant of reported pesticide safety practices, with health risk perception also influencing them. These findings suggest that improving knowledge alone is insufficient and that behavioral interventions should prioritize attitudinal change to promote safer pesticide use. By elucidating the behavioral and perceptual drivers of pesticide practices in low-resource farming communities, this study provides evidence to inform targeted public health and occupational safety interventions in Africa. Notably, the high willingness of farmers (93%) to participate in human biomonitoring initiatives highlights the feasibility of integrating biomonitoring and surveillance into prevention-oriented agricultural health strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42448-5.

Introduction

According to the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB), it is suggested that attitudes toward the behaviour, subjective norms, and perceived behavioural control influence a person’s intention to perform the behaviour1. A recent review highlighted the TPB’s effectiveness in identifying psychological determinants of intentions and behaviors in health contexts, emphasizing its utility in designing health-related interventions2. For farmers, this framework helps elucidate why they may adopt or resist certain practices, particularly in the context of pesticide application3. Farmworkers, who constitute a critical yet often vulnerable segment of the workforce, face unique occupational health risks ranging from exposure to pesticides, demanding physical labor, and potential lack of access to adequate safety and healthcare. Research suggests that improper pesticide use, poor knowledge, and weak awareness of the dangers of chemicals among farmers have become a major issue, threatening farmers’ health and being associated with the development of several serious diseases, including cancer, respiratory diseases, and infertility4. Hence, understanding farmworkers’ knowledge, attitudes, practices, and perceptions of health risks is crucial for designing targeted interventions to promote safer work practices and improve their overall well-being.

In many African contexts, agricultural intensification is accelerating without parallel investments in farmer education and safety infrastructure. In parallel, global demand and use of pesticides are still increasing, whereas the capacity to regulate pesticides and control exposures remains at its lowest levels5. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) pesticide use database, which compiles pesticide consumption (active ingredients) by country and year, the total global pesticide use in agriculture was approximately 3.70 million tons (Mt) of active ingredients in 2022, representing a 13% increase over the previous decade and about double the volume reported in 19906. Moreover, the intensity of pesticide use has increased by 94% per cropland between 1990 and 20226. Morocco is no exception. The country has a diverse agricultural sector that contributes significantly to its economy and produces a range of crops, including cereals, fruits, vegetables, and herbs. According to the World Bank, the agricultural sector employs about 40% of the country’s workforce, playing a crucial role in rural livelihoods and serving as a backbone for many communities7. Farming systems are predominantly small- to medium-scale and labor-intensive, with widespread reliance on chemical pesticides to protect crops and ensure productivity, particularly in cereal, vegetable, and fruit production. Pesticide application is often performed manually or with basic equipment, frequently under conditions of limited access to personal protective equipment and formal training. Agricultural pesticide application is estimated at 13,697 t per year over recent years, based on FAOSTAT data on active ingredients, putting Morocco among the highest consumers in Africa and reflecting sustained demand associated with crop protection needs and agricultural intensification8. The National Office for Health Safety of Food Products in Morocco (ONSSA) has implemented a comprehensive approval process to control the use of pesticides in agriculture better. Those allowed to circulate in the official market are listed under the phytosanitary index9. Surprisingly, certain pesticides banned in other countries, including in Europe, continue to be used in Moroccan agriculture. This is the case, for example, for Cyhalothrin, 1,3-Dichloropropène, Diquat, Glufosinate, Oxadiazon, Linuron, Paraquat, etc. In 2018, Greenpeace reported that Morocco is one of the top ten major importers of banned pesticides, with around 3900 t, making it the first in Africa10. A study conducted in 2020 by the Moroccan Association for Health, Environment, and Toxicovigilance found that 40 active substances classified by the International Pesticide Action Network as highly hazardous pesticides (HHPs) are still registered in Morocco for agricultural use11. While the legal market accounts for 85% of sales with 1,700 retailers, smuggling and counterfeiting constitute 15% of the pesticides sold12. More than 2,000 cases of acute pesticide poisoning were reported. Of those cases, 50% of the pesticides involved were classes I (extremely or highly hazardous) and II (moderately hazardous) according to WHO classifications11. Farmers are not necessarily aware of the risks that pose serious threats to their health.

Consequently, our research aims to address the geographical gap by assessing knowledge, attitude, health risk perceptions, and practices toward pesticide use and finding out the factors influencing farmers’ practices toward pesticide use, toward better policies for improving farmers’ practices and occupational health outcomes, as well as encouraging safe pesticide usage in agricultural low-resource settings.

Material and method

The study area

This study is part of the Parental Pesticide and Offspring Epigenome (PaPOE) cohort study, launched in Morocco in 2018 among farmers and their families, for which the protocol has previously been described13. In brief, an occupational-based cross-sectional study was conducted between June 2022 and July 2023. It was mainly conducted in the peri-urban and rural zones around the Meknes region, characterized by a mixed rainfed and irrigated agriculture influenced by the Mediterranean climate with semi-arid tendencies. Seven counties and peri-urban areas known for their intensive agricultural activity were included (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S1), mainly comprising small and medium-sized farms. The Fez-Meknes region is the second largest contributor to the national agricultural GDP14. The Region has become the third production basin in the country, with a functional agricultural area of about 1,3 M ha, of which 15% is irrigated, and an important dynamic agro-industry in sectors, such as olives and cereals.

Sample selection and data collection

The single population proportion formula was used to calculate the required sample size. The following assumptions were considered: a 73.3% proportion of farmers who used only scarves to protect against inhalation of pesticide odors15, a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and 5% estimated non-response rate ; the calculated sample size is 316. Participants were adult farmworkers living in the study area who had been official or seasonal farmers, farmworkers, and/or applicators for the last two years. Workers with a chronic disease or anemia, pregnant women, and participants under 18 were excluded, based on self-reporting and/or a quick clinical screening.

Questionnaire-based face-to-face interviews were used to collect data. A questionnaire pretest was conducted on a peri-urban farm in Meknes among 32 farmworkers, highlighting and, if necessary, rectifying errors, rephrasing unclear questions, and adding new ones. The questionnaire was reviewed by a panel of 3 experts in agronomy and epidemiology to check for consistency and relevance of each item. Six interviewers were selected from master’s and Ph.D. students at the university to collect the necessary information from the farmers. Prior to conducting the interviews, students were trained and instructed to probe information in the local language (Arabic dialect) while communicating with farmers, in a friendly discussion, and with field observations during farming and spraying activities, when possible. Contact was first established with the Agricultural Council Services in the Prefecture, which liaised the research team with professional farmers’ associations. A total of 316 farmworkers were initially approached and consented to participate in the study. For many of the farms visited (especially the medium ones), we were first introduced to the agricultural engineer or the agroecologist supervising the work on the farm. Two responses were dropped, because respondents did not complete critical sections of the questionnaire, resulting in a final enrolled sample size of 314. Item-level non-response was observed for some questionnaire sections. Consequently, analyses of knowledge, attitude, and practice constructs were conducted using complete-case data, yielding an effective analytical sample of 300 respondents. Percentages and statistical analyses were therefore calculated using variable-specific denominators, which are explicitly reported where applicable.

The questionnaire consisted of six sections. Section 1 included questions related to socio-demographic characteristics, including sex, age, residence, educational status, marital status, and work experience. Section 2 contained questions on the level of knowledge of pesticide use (referred to as KS). The third part of the questionnaire evaluated workers’ attitudes toward pesticide usage (AS). The fourth section focused on perceived health risks related to pesticide use (HR), while section 5 provided detailed information on practices toward pesticide use (PS). These domains were assessed using a five-range psychometric Likert scale anchored from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5). The variables for each construct are presented in Table S2 of the Supplementary Material. Section 6 assessed farmers’ predisposition to participate in a human biomonitoring campaign to assess cholinesterase levels and internal exposure to pesticides.

For all statements in sections 2 to 5, interviewers systematically probed responses by asking respondents to specify their answers and to provide concrete examples, allowing recognition or recall of relevant information, and differentiation between general beliefs, fact-based knowledge, and/or individual practices. Responses were considered valid only when participants were able to demonstrate recall or recognition of pesticide-related information. To improve response reliability and reduce social desirability bias, the survey statements were systematically complemented by probing questions (e.g., how the dose was determined, reference to label instructions or advice received, what pre-harvest interval PHI is, what is the re-entry interval REI after an application, who is the providing body of the training, and what was the content, etc.), allowing interviewers to assess the internal coherence of responses. To ensure contextual and regulatory relevance, all pesticides reported by participants were verified using the Moroccan phytosanitary index published by ONSSA. For each commercial product cited, the corresponding active molecule(s), chemical family, and authorization status were checked.

The raw data collected was reviewed after the interviews. Each farmer’s response was coded, entered in Microsoft Excel, and verified to eliminate the risk of error.

Data analysis

R was used to analyze data and determine correlations between variables. Cronbach’s alpha was employed to evaluate internal consistency. A correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships between demographic characteristics and scale variables. Validity tests were conducted. The Kaiser, Meyer, Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy suggests that the data seem appropriate for factor analysis (KMO = 0.86), and Bartlett’s test of sphericity suggests that there is a sufficiently significant correlation in the data for factor analysis (Chisq(378) = 3608.46, p < 0.001).

The path analysis was conducted to explore the effect of demographic and socioeconomic factors on KS, AS, HS, and PS, which are considered latent variables (LV). All categorical variables, such as sex, marital status, and smoking status, are treated as dummy variables. Ordinal variables, such as education, were used as continuous. The Direct Path Coefficient (DPC) was used as the standardized regression coefficient that quantifies the direct effect of an independent variable on a dependent latent variable. The higher the absolute value of DPC, the stronger the effect of the independent variable on the latent variable. To examine the influencing factors and assess the model’s adequacy, the entire measurement model was estimated using the Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). It validates the measurement model by assessing the relationships between observed variables (items) and latent constructs (KS, AS, HR, PS). This analysis provided standardized loadings, fit measures, and reliability estimates, which indicate how well the proposed model aligns with the observed data. Higher loadings (closer to 1) indicate stronger relationships, while lower loadings suggest weaker or less reliable contributions (Table S4 of the Supplementary Material).

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki and the requirements of the local ethical committee. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee at Moulay Ismail University of Meknes (Morocco) (approval number: CERB-UMI 201802). Participants provided written informed consent to participate in this study voluntarily.

Results

Descriptive socio-demographic and work-related characteristics of the sample

The demographic characteristics are given in Table 1. Respondents have an average age of 40 ± 15.08, ranging from 18 to 77 years. 64.3% of the participants are men, and 76.8% are married. 74.2% are not educated or have only a primary education, 67 (21.3%) have a high school education, and only 14 (4.5%) have higher education. 181 (57.6%) declared not having received any training. 39 (12.4%) are smokers, all men. The average farming experience is 12.25 years, with an average weekly working day of 5.88. The average working day lasts around 7.67 h, and the perceived average exposure to pesticide application while in the field is around 2.64 months per year.

Regarding pesticide use, there is substantial reliance on multiple classes of pesticides (insecticides, fungicides, herbicides). The pesticide profile reported by participants in the study area shows a coexistence of highly hazardous pesticides and substances with probable or possible carcinogenic potential, according to classifications by the World Health Organization and the International Agency for Research on Cancer (Table 2). All reported commercial names were cross-checked against the Moroccan phytosanitary index of the Moroccan Office for Health Safety of Food Products (ONSSA) to confirm their regulatory status and corresponding active ingredients. Actual use patterns include compounds from organophosphates, pyrethroids, dithiocarbamates, and chloronitriles. It is noteworthy that the regulatory situation in Morocco is evolving, with many commonly used compounds likely to be authorized at some point and subject to a periodic review. Hence, the actual field use includes obsolete or unregistered products due to stockpiles or informal distribution, especially in rural contexts. Among the substances identified, parathion (ethyl and methyl) (WHO class Ia) and endosulfan (WHO class II), both recognized for their high acute neurotoxicity16,17, were reported despite their restricted or withdrawn status from the national index. In addition, the use of paraquat (WHO class II), a herbicide associated with high fatality rates following acute exposure, was also reported.

Descriptive statistics for scale variables

The descriptive statistics for each item in each scale are computed, and the mean (SD) results are presented in Table 3. The Cronbach’s alpha on all scales is greater than 0.7, which is the general cutoff for reliability. This suggests that the questionnaire effectively measures the intended constructs and that responses are consistent across items within each category.

Regarding knowledge-related items (KS1-KS7), we found that KS items have mean values ranging between 2.39 (KS2) and 3.51 (KS7), with relatively high standard deviations (SDs), suggesting variability in knowledge levels among participants. 86.3% (n = 259) of workers were unaware of the names of the pesticides they used. KS1 has a mean of 2.77 (± 1.47), suggesting a moderate reliance on official sources for pesticide information, but variability confirms that some farmers may depend on informal sources. KS2 shows a limited understanding of pesticides’ active ingredients, suggesting a potential gap in fundamental knowledge. At the same time, KS3 suggests a moderate awareness of proper usage, with room for improvement in reading and following instructions, and a fair awareness of safe handling practices (KS4). Farmers’ answers showed they have a moderate understanding of appropriate pesticide application timing (KS5) and some knowledge about banned and dangerous pesticides. KS7 had the highest mean score, indicating strong awareness of the environmental and health hazards associated with pesticide use. This statement is later double-checked against detailed perceived risks of pesticide-related health effects. Globally, farmers recognize the risks of pesticides but may lack sufficient knowledge about their composition, proper handling, and regulatory restrictions.

The attitude-related items (AS1-AS6) show mean scores ranging from 2.71 to 3.39, with relatively high SDs, reflecting diverse perspectives. Farmers exhibit moderate agreement that pesticide safety training improves practices and reduces health risks (AS1). However, they recognize the importance of wearing personal protective equipment while handling pesticides (AS2), even though some workers may not strictly adhere to protective measures despite acknowledging their necessity. This could be influenced by accessibility or behavioral habits, especially among seasonal workers. Moreover, there is moderate to high agreement that pesticides are essential for maintaining crop yield and financial stability (AS3). This highlights farmers’ economic dependence on pesticides, which may pose a challenge to shift to alternative pest control methods. Surprisingly, there is a strong willingness to adopt less hazardous pest control methods if proven effective (AS5). However, the large SD indicates divergent views, possibly reflecting varying levels of trust in these alternatives, or more probably, financial affordability and the impact on crop yield, two reasons highlighted by farmers throughout the survey. Regarding community norms (AS4), peer influence seems to play a role. The deviation may suggest that some farmers may be willing to diverge from traditional practices based on personal beliefs or knowledge. The moderate AS6 score suggests that some farmers consume their own products, while others may rely on external sources, a pattern mostly linked to farm size and market dependency.

The health risk indicators (HR1-HR7) have mean scores ranging from 1.90 to 2.40, indicating a relatively low but notable perception of pesticide-related health risks. The relatively high standard deviation suggests a heterogeneous perception of occupational risks, possibly influenced by varying levels of direct exposure or by differences in protective measures taken by workers, and differences in training, safety awareness, or past exposure. Perceived risks related to mental health effects (anxiety, sleep disorders, cognitive dysfunction) are the lowest among the seven descriptors. This suggests that the psychological effects of pesticide exposure may be under-recognized, or workers may prioritize physical over mental health risks. HR4 is the highest perceived risk among the seven descriptors, indicating that food safety concerns are a major issue for farmers. This may stem from first-hand knowledge of pesticide use practices and potential consumer risks. The high variation suggests different levels of concern, possibly influenced by differences in farming practices and market orientation. Those producing for local markets might have less pressure to comply with international standards, leading to diverging attitudes toward pesticide risks. Export-oriented farmers may perceive greater risks due to compliance challenges, certification costs, and potential economic losses if their products do not meet safety standards.

Practice-related items (PS1-PS7) have mean scores between 3.02 and 3.25, indicating overall moderate compliance with safe pesticide handling practices, including moderate adherence to recommended pesticide dosages (PS1) and less moderate use of protective equipment (PS2). Surprisingly, there is reasonable compliance with the requirements to avoid food, drink, and smoking during pesticide handling (PS4 and PS5). Storage practices are relatively adequate (PS3), and post-spraying hygiene is practiced to a moderate extent (PS6). PS7 showed that farmers respect proper disposal of pesticide containers to some extent, which is still not in line with field observations, where, most of the time, many pesticide bottles were stored in the garage or near the houses.

Correlation analysis of scale variables with demographic data

A correlation analysis has been conducted to examine the relationships between demographic characteristics and scale variables. The results, as shown in Fig. 2, indicate that specific demographic and contextual factors influence knowledge of pesticide use (KS). Specifically, the significant positive relationship with gender (p < 0.05) suggests that knowledge about pesticides differs between men and women. This difference may be explained by gender disparities in access to training, risk perception, or engagement in pesticide-related activities, as observed in previous studies in sub-Saharan Africa22–24. Regarding age, the correlation appears weak to moderate. Older farmers may have more experience but not necessarily better knowledge or safer practices. When it comes to education, a moderate to strong positive correlation is observed, likely suggesting that higher education is associated with greater knowledge, more positive attitudes, and safer pesticide practices. This aligns with previous research suggesting that higher educational attainment is associated with improved comprehension of pesticide risks and safety measures25. The strong positive correlation (p < 0.05) between knowledge and prior training reinforces the importance of educational interventions in enhancing farmers’ understanding of pesticide risks and best practices26. For financial income, the positive correlation suggests that farmers with higher incomes may have better access to training and protective equipment.

Regarding farmers’ attitudes, a positive correlation exists with education levels and training (p < 0.05), suggesting that higher educational attainment and targeted training programs are associated with more positive attitudes. A significant negative correlation exists between the attitudes and working days (p < 0.05), indicating that longer working days are associated with less favorable attitudes. This may result from increased fatigue or reduced job satisfaction associated with extended working hours. Conversely, AS is not significantly related to the study area, age, marital status, smoking, or farming experience (p > 0.05), suggesting that these factors do not have a measurable impact on attitudes in our context.

HR is negatively and significantly correlated with age (p < 0.05), indicating that older individuals perceive fewer health risks than younger individuals. Risk habituation may explain this tendency, where individuals exposed to risks for extended periods may become desensitized. The strongest predictors of health risk perception are education and training, emphasizing the importance of educational interventions in health risk communication. The negative correlation with age suggests that older individuals may be less sensitive to health risks.

Validity tests

The suitability of the data for factor analysis was assessed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. The KMO statistic estimates the proportion of variance among variables and indicates the degree to which the data are appropriate for factor analysis. A KMO value close to 1.0 suggests that the data are highly suitable for factor analysis, while values below 0.5 indicate inadequacy. In our study, the KMO measure was 0.86, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.6 and falling within the “meritorious” range, indicating that the data are highly appropriate for factor analysis.

Additionally, Bartlett’s test of sphericity was conducted to examine whether the correlation matrix deviates significantly from an identity matrix, thereby confirming the presence of sufficient correlations among variables to proceed with factor analysis. The test yielded a statistically significant result (χ²(378) = 3608.46, p < 0.001), rejecting the null hypothesis that the correlation matrix is an identity matrix. This confirms that the variables are sufficiently correlated and suitable for factor analysis.

Path analysis

The results in Table 4 show that education is the strongest predictor across all variables (KS, AS, HR, PS), highlighting the crucial role of formal knowledge in shaping risk perception and safety behaviors. Moreover, training significantly boosts knowledge but does not strongly impact attitudes, risk perception, or safety practices, suggesting a gap between knowledge acquisition and behavioral change. When it comes to age, older farmers consistently score lower across KS, AS, and PS, suggesting that age-targeted interventions are needed to bridge generational gaps in pesticide safety practices. Farming experience improves attitudes and practices but does not enhance knowledge or risk perception, suggesting that long-term exposure leads to behavioral adjustments rather than deeper understanding of safety. This is likely due to economic pressures, risk normalization, and habitual practices that prioritize productivity over safety.

Influencing factors of farmers’ practices toward pesticide use

Measurement model

According to the CFA results (Supplementary Table S4), all construct standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.560 to 0.821. Thus, all loadings were above the cut-off value of 0.50 and significant at p < 0.001. For the knowledge construct, KS3 (0.821) and KS2 (0.763) show the highest loadings, indicating that these items contribute most to the knowledge score. KS6 (0.128) and KS7 (0.083) have very low loadings, suggesting that these items do not strongly contribute to the knowledge construct and may need to be reviewed to improve the model. Regarding attitudes, AS2 (0.607), AS3 (0.566), and AS5 (0.566) show moderate loadings, indicating acceptable contributions. AS4 (0.411) has a relatively lower loading, suggesting it may be less effective at defining the attitude construct. For health risk perceptions, HR4 (0.751), HR3 (0.699), and HR5 (0.687) have strong loadings, indicating that these items strongly define the perception. HR1 (0.581) and HR6 (0.59) have moderate contributions, suggesting acceptable but not strong indicators. All loadings for PS are high (> 0.64), with PS7 (0.804) being the strongest contributor. This suggests that the measurement of practices is highly reliable and well-structured.

Structural model

The structural equation model (SEM)analysis showed that all fit indices were within acceptable ranges, indicating that the model fit the data well (Supplementary Table S4). The dependent variable’s squared multiple correlation (R²) was 0.772, indicating that the investigated constructs explained 77.2% of the variable’s variance in the model.

The path diagram (Fig. 3) presents the relationships between latent variables influencing pesticide safety practices (PS). The standardized path coefficients (ranging from − 1 to + 1) represent the strength and direction of the relationships between the constructs Knowledge (KS), Attitudes (AS), Health Risk Perceptions (HR), and Practices (PS). Significant associations were identified between pesticide safety practices (PS) and the other latent constructs. Among these relationships, attitudes toward pesticide safety (AS) emerged as the most influential factor in predicting farmers’ pesticide safety behavior (Standardized Path Coefficient, SPC = 0.86). This finding suggests that farmers with positive safety attitudes are significantly more likely to adopt recommended pesticide practices, including proper handling, storage, and application. This association is consistent with behavioral frameworks such as the Theory of Planned Behavior, which emphasizes the role of attitudes in shaping behavioral intentions and ultimately self-reported actions.

Furthermore, health risk perception (HR) also had a notable positive influence on practices (SPC = 0.30), indicating that farmers who perceive pesticides as hazardous are more likely to adopt protective measures. However, the moderate strength of this relationship suggests that risk perception alone is insufficient to ensure proper safety practices, as other external factors, such as economic constraints, the availability of protective equipment, and peer influence, may also play a role in shaping behavior. In contrast, knowledge about pesticide use (KS) exerted the weakest direct influence on safety practices (SPC = 0.05), suggesting that knowledge alone does not necessarily translate into safer pesticide-handling behaviors. Indeed, while knowledge significantly influences attitudes (SPC = 0.44) and risk perception (SPC = 0.13), its direct effect on behavior remains minimal, reinforcing the idea that cognitive understanding alone does not guarantee action. This weak association suggests that knowledge-based interventions need to be complemented with attitude-shifting and risk-awareness programs to drive behavioral change.

Discussion

The present study explored the determinants of attitudes and practices regarding pesticide use among Moroccan farmers using the Theory of Planned Behavior. To the best of our knowledge, no study has been conducted to assess the knowledge, attitude, practices, and health risk perception among agricultural farmers in the target counties in Morocco.

Our findings indicated that numerous factors, such as education, age, and farming experience, strongly influenced pesticide safety practices. Combined with knowledge, attitudes, and health risk perceptions, these elements play a crucial role in shaping farmers’ decisions and capabilities to implement sustainable practices, as evidenced by studies across different regions.

Accordingly, this study found that literate farmers are more knowledgeable than illiterate ones about the effects of pesticides. Indeed, low levels of education contributed to a poor understanding and handling of pesticides. Similar results were found in other areas of Morocco27 and in Africa. In Egypt, most farmers have an illiteracy rate of 55% and hence are less likely to adhere to safety measures28. In Ethiopia, 55.2% of farmers had below-average knowledge of health risks associated with pesticides, primarily due to a lack of formal education29,30. In Cameroon, a similar trend was observed, where low education levels contributed to poor understanding and handling of pesticides31.

Our study reported a significant negative association between age and pesticide use practices: elderly farmers were less likely to adopt good and safe practices than younger farmers, leading to inappropriate behavior. This trend aligns with risk habituation theory, which posits that long-term exposure leads to lower perceived vulnerability. Similar studies in Tanzania32 and Nigeria33 also reported that older farmers exhibited less safety behavior. Even if holding valuable traditional knowledge, older farmers might have thought it was worthless to change their behavior after using pesticides for a long time34. In contrast, younger farmers tend to be more open to adopting innovative practices. This is also true when it comes to embracing new technologies, with younger farmers showing greater adaptability.

Regarding farming experience, most of respondents in our study had an average of 12 years of agricultural experience. It comes as no surprise that this variable was positively related to farmers’ practices. The perception of the ability to withstand pesticide risk after numerous exposures also led to decreased adoption of good pesticide practices toward pesticides amongst farmers35. Older farmers’ complacency may have decreased with their extensive farming experience, leading to poor adoption of good practices in pesticide handling34. The belief that farmers could handle the risk of pesticides after several exposures also led to lower adoption of new and healthy practices35,36, including the lowest level of protective equipment use37.

Despite the importance of knowledge as a significant predictor of safety practices toward pesticide use, the study’s results revealed that this component had a moderate effect on the practices, which aligns with the findings of other studies27,38,39. Elevated knowledge levels and a positive attitude are not always sufficient to change farmers’ safety practices. Appropriate interactive actions should be taken to address this gap. Besides the farmer-related behavioral factors, other African studies showed that external factors, such as political and economic issues5,25, technological use, agricultural environment and equipment conditions40 and difficulty of use41, can affect pesticide application. The occurrence of pesticide residues depends not only on farmers’ attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control, but also on external factors42 that should be considered in future studies.

Attitudes toward pesticide handling positively and significantly affected farmers’ practices. This finding is consistent with previous studies. Attitude is a strong predictor of any individual’s behaviour27,43,44. Consequently, farmers’ positive attitudes toward pesticide use can create the required framework for enhancing occupational safety behavior1. An agreement was observed in our study for AS5 (openness to alternative pest control methods) and AS3 (economic necessity of pesticide use), suggesting that while farmers acknowledge the financial benefits of pesticides, they are also receptive to safer alternatives, as demonstrated by a study conducted in Congo45. Moreover, farmers who regarded pesticides as associated with human illness and as capable of causing environmental problems were more likely to express favorable judgments and, consequently engage in favorable practices. From another angle, it can be argued that when people are highly aware of the health and environmental effects, they are more likely to adopt a positive attitude and feel social pressure to adopt good practices.

From a practical perspective, the results suggest that interventions aimed at improving pesticide safety should address not only technical knowledge but also farmers’ safety attitudes and risk perceptions. Training programs that integrate behavioral approaches, strengthen official information channels, and adapt communication to local literacy levels may enhance the adoption of safer pesticide practices and contribute to occupational health protection. Future interventions should focus on bridging the gap between knowledge acquisition and practical application through experiential learning, peer-based engagement, and incentive-driven safety compliance programs. Aligning these interventions with Morocco’s specific socio-economic landscape is crucial to ensuring their effectiveness, and can, therefore, enhance the adoption of safer practices among farmers and improve their socio-economic resilience.

While the Theory of Planned Behavior provides a robust framework for predicting behavior, it is important to consider additional factors, such as legal cognition and external support systems, that can enhance or hinder the translation of intention into action. In this regard, a few limitations need to be acknowledged. This study used questionnaires to assess farmers’ safety practices regarding pesticide use rather than structured and, systematic observation of their actual actions. This may not reflect the actual situation in each case due to possible under- or over-reporting.

Moreover, although the knowledge domain was designed to capture foundational knowledge, some items, particularly those related to access to information sources, may overlap conceptually with awareness. To mitigate this, interviewer-administered probing was used to ensure responses reflected recognition or recall of pesticide-related information; nevertheless, some degree of construct overlap cannot be entirely excluded. Also, certain practice items combined multiple behaviors (e.g., container reuse, disposal, and burning), which may reduce measurement precision. These items were therefore interpreted as composite indicators of safe or unsafe practices, and future surveys should disaggregate such behaviors. While we acknowledge limitations of relying on non-local prevalence data and data linked to practices, the adopted strategy reduced the risk of underpowering the study. Now that this baseline study has been conducted in Morocco, the data can serve as a locally valid reference for future research and more precise power calculations. Consequently, the study’s results should be generalized with caution. Although incorporating variables that affect farmers’ safety behavior and risk perception significantly improved the model’s explanatory power, a considerable portion of the variance remained unexplained. Despite these limitations, the study provides context-specific and policy-relevant insights into pesticide-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices among agricultural workers in the region, highlighting priority areas for targeted training and risk-reduction interventions.

Conclusion

Our study shed light on key factors affecting pesticide practices in Morocco. By applying TPB, researchers, labor force administrations, and policymakers can gain valuable insights into the drivers of farmers’ pesticide use behaviors and develop effective strategies to mitigate health and environmental risks. It comes without a doubt that economic considerations and openness to alternative methods are key drivers of pesticide-related attitudes, while social norms, personal protective behaviors, and daily habits exhibit more variability. Future interventions should focus on enhancing trust in alternative solutions, strengthening safety training, and addressing economic dependencies on pesticides. Future research could integrate other variables that have been less studied, such as threats, fears, and positive or negative feelings. Nonetheless, this study has achieved a critical goal by exploring and better understanding the determinants of safety practices regarding pesticide use in Morocco. Further investigations could delve into the long-term impacts of educational interventions and provide a monitoring and human biomonitoring roadmap to confront self-reported data and better understand the impacts of pesticide exposure in the Moroccan context. Also, a sentinel surveillance system for monitoring farmworkers’ health would be an important digitally enabled, cost-effective, evidence-based tool for gathering real-time data to support adaptative decision-making in low- and middle-income countries.
