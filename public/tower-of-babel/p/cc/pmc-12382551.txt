General practitioners’ perspective on timely interprofessional case conferences on non-cancer patients with palliative care needs: a qualitative study (KOPAL)
Authors: Gabriella Marx, Sven Schwabe, Sarah Kuba, Jan Philip Weber, Nadine Janis Pohontsch, Franziska Schade, Martin Scherer, Nils Schneider
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective Patients with progressive chronic non-malignant diseases (CNMD) such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, congestive heart failure and dementia could benefit from specialist palliative home care (SPHC). The two-arm, cluster randomised controlled KOPAL trial was conducted to test the effectiveness of a timely SPHC nurse-patient consultation followed by an interprofessional telephone case conference between general practitioners and SPHC teams. This study was a component of the KOPAL trial and aims to explore general practitioners’ (GP) experiences with treating CNMD patients before and with case conferences and with the following consequences for treatment and interprofessional collaboration with SPHC teams.DesignQualitative evaluation of the KOPAL trial focussing on GPs’ perspective. Open guided interviews using narrative techniques analysed with grounded theory.SettingThe KOPAL trial was conducted in Lower Saxony and the metropolitan region of Hamburg, Germany.Participants24 GPs who participated in the intervention group of the trial were interviewed.ResultsFindings show that GPs have practice-specific routines when caring for people with CNMD. Interprofessional case conferences may lead to more awareness regarding gaps in primary palliative care, one’s own care routines or a lack of psychosocial support. Although gaining new insights, GPs follow three different strategies following the case conferences: (1) Maintain routines of care and disregard added value, (2) extend routines of care according to SPHC advice and (3) integration and partial change of care routines caused by SPHC support. These strategies are influenced by GPs’ perception of SPHC teams (eg, ambivalence towards SPHC).ConclusionInterprofessional case conferences to timely discuss care options could improve healthcare for patients with advanced CNMD by revealing gaps in care. Promoting a regular process of exchange between GPs and SPHC teams (such as through round tables) may improve a trusting cooperation.Trial registration numberDRKSS00017795.Keywords: Primary Health Care, PALLIATIVE CARE, Chronic Disease, Interprofessional Relations, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH

Introduction

General practitioners (GPs) play a major role in treating patients diagnosed with progressive chronic non-malignant diseases (CNMD). The majority of such patients could benefit from general palliative care (PC) provided by GPs; however, sufficient PC is still lacking.1 2 Underlying barriers are lack of confidence in different areas such as managing specific symptoms including medication, dealing with psychological aspects or communicating end of life issues.3 Interprofessional collaboration may help to strengthen GPs' confidence. However, while specialist PC is commonly provided by interprofessional teams, this is not the case in the primary care setting. Considering the patients’ potential benefits of patient-centred collaboration, we designed the KOPAL trial, an interventional approach focussing on interprofessional collaboration for patients with advanced CNMD in primary care settings.4 To optimise this concept and better understand potential obstacles, GPs' perspectives and experiences were taken into account as part of the evaluation.

Background

Although non-oncological diseases are associated with high symptom burden similar to cancer5 and frequently report low quality of life,6,8 compared with cancer patients, and despite the recommendation of the European Association for PC in 2009,9 they are still less likely to receive (specialist) PC.1 10 Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), congestive heart failure (CHF) and dementia are among the most common CNMD seen by GPs in Germany.11 12 These conditions are characterised by prognostic uncertainty with intermittent crisis and, at an advanced stage, high rates of unexpected hospitalisation due to sudden deterioration of health.13 14

There is evidence that timely integration of PC can improve health-related quality of life, reduce physical symptoms and improve patients’ and relatives’ satisfaction.15 Unfortunately, existing tools to assess suitable onsets of PC for patients with CNMD are rare, inaccurate or inconsistently applied.16,19 Thus, we developed the KOPAL conversation guide that can serve as an aide memoire when assessing PC needs of those patients. In contrast to assessment tools, the conversations guide encompasses potential needs on a multidimensional level that might get overlooked due to the slow illness progression.20

In Germany, outpatient PC for adult patients is provided by GPs, nursing services and specialist palliative home care (SPHC) teams. GPs (without specialisation) provide basic support and fundamental medical consultation for patients and their relatives for moderate symptoms. Specialist PC in the outpatient setting is provided by SPHC teams, consisting of physicians (often GPs) and nurses with advanced qualifications in PC. They have capacity for frequent home visits and are available 24/7.21 In most cases, the integration of SPHC is prescribed by GPs. It is recommended that GPs remain integrated during the transition to specialist PC,22 23 since SPHC does not replace primary care, but provides additional specialist care concepts. Thus, the GP could continue medical treatment, remain as gatekeeper and take the role as a trusted person. However, in practice, the roles and tasks of healthcare providers often change so far as GPs withdraw from care for their patients although SPHC teams explicitly recommend GPs remain involved since their expertise is seen as a relevant part of PC.24,28 In order to provide optimal healthcare including palliative home care for patients with CNMD, close interprofessional collaboration and early joint care planning between primary care, in particular, GPs and SPHC teams is needed.29,31 Timely interprofessional collaboration can also be a viable solution to identify potential PC needs resulting in optimised general PC (provided by GP) or in inclusion of specialist PC. Mitchel et al32 found in their pilot study on interprofessional case conferences improved healthcare in the time following case conferences and positive feedback of participating healthcare providers.

The aim of the KOPAL trial was to develop, implement and evaluate a PC nurse home visit followed by a single patient centred interprofessional telephone case conference including GP and SPHC team in order to foster interprofessional collaboration and, as a consequence, enhance timely and appropriate PC provision for non-oncological patients.4 This qualitative study was embedded in the KOPAL main study with the aim to evaluate the GPs’ experiences with the intervention, as they play a pivotal role in providing end-of-life care. To date, GPs still need, among others, to improve their end of life skills and wish for enhancing interprofessional collaboration.33 Research questions were: How do GPs experience (1) usual care for patients with CNMD before the intervention, (2) interprofessional collaboration during the intervention (ie, PC nurse home visit followed by case conference) and (3) healthcare in the period after the intervention? Using a grounded theory approach, we aimed at developing a thick theory of relevant phenomena in this area and how they are associated from GPs’ perspective.

Methods

Study design

This is a qualitative evaluation study of the GPs’ perspective and experiences as part of the two-arm, cluster randomised controlled KOPAL trial with usual care in the control arm.4 Open guided narrative interviews were conducted with participating GPs and analysed using grounded theory to be able to gain indepth insights of their experiences.

Sampling

For participation in the KOPAL trial, first of all, SPHC teams were recruited in Northern Germany. As soon as a SPHC team was enrolled, all GPs within their area were invited to take part. Inclusion criteria for the KOPAL trial were: specialisation in primary care or internal medicine, focus on primary care medicine, use of computer-based documentation software (needed for identification of patients who participated in the trial); training as specialist PC provider or working in a SPHC team were exclusion criteria, as we expected a specialist perspective on PC patients. Our goal, however, was to gain knowledge on how primary care without a specialist focus on PC would benefit from interprofessional case conferences with SPHC teams. GPs who participated in the KOPAL trial also consented in interviews about their personal experiences at the end of the 48-week observation period (for further details see the study by Mallon et al34). As soon as the quantitative follow-up interviews were finished, GPs were reminded and the respective qualitative interviews were scheduled. As we aimed to interview all participating GPs of the intervention group, theoretical sampling, as an appropriate strategy within grounded theory methodology or another sampling strategy, has therefore not been applied. Participating GPs received an incentive for attendance in the randomised controlled trial and a further incentive for the qualitative interview. The amount of incentive is commonly used in Germany and covers the calculated time exposure.

Data collection

Open guided single telephone interviews were conducted from May 2021 to May 2022 by two researchers (JPW, PhD, male, public health researcher, and NJP, PhD, female, psychologist, senior researcher, both experienced in qualitative research) using the narrative interview technique in order to gain deep insights into GPs’ motives underlying their perspectives, attitudes and actions. No further person was involved in the interview. JPW and NJP were known to some of the interviewees from previous quantitative interviews and observations of the case conferences which were part of the KOPAL trial, but no personal information was given to the participants. Within each interview, three main topics were addressed and participants were encouraged to frankly narrate their experiences. The qualitative core research group, consisting of different disciplines and experienced in qualitative research (GM, PhD, sociologist, senior researcher, NJP, PhD, psychologist, senior researcher, JPW, PhD, public health researcher, CM, MD, senior researcher, FS, sociologist, PhD student) developed the interview guide and agreed on how to address the topics according to the research questions regarding GPs’ experiences with care for patients with CNMD before, during and after the intervention (for interview topics, see online supplemental appendix 1). After conducting the first interviews, the research group discussed the experiences and decided that no modifications of the interview guide were needed. Interviews were digitally recorded and transcribed verbatim using transcription convention as shown in online supplemental appendix 2. All transcripts were pseudonymised using the following scheme: Profession (GP), study site and number of interview (eg, HH003B), gender (m/f), section within the transcript (eg, pos. 56).

Data analysis

During data analysis, we applied all steps of the grounded theory methodology according to Strauss: open coding (defining sequences, analysing line-by-line, formulating codes, writing code memos), axial coding (grouping codes to categories) and selective coding (defining main categories and core phenomenon)35 in an iterative process conducted by GM, sociologist, SK, student of psychology (BA), MG, student of sociology (MA), and SS, PhD, sociologist. GM, MG and SS are experienced in qualitative research. SK was trained in qualitative research by GM. We used abductive reasoning,36 aiming to open-mindedly develop an empirical based theory of how GPs experience healthcare for patients with CNMD and interprofessional collaboration with SPHC teams in the course of the KOPAL trial. Following an interpretive paradigm, we assume that reality is subjective and based on individuals’ experiences and own meanings. Since action and attitudes are often affected by unconscious motives, we tried to understand and explain (rather than describe) them in using this approach. Analysis was conducted using the coding paradigm introduced by Glaser and Strauss: core phenomenon, context, causal condition, intervening conditions and consequences (see figure 1). Within this paradigm, we developed codes and categories which were repeatedly double-checked and discussed (especially questionable or unclear codes) in the coding team consisting of four team members to ensure plausibility. The coding process and team discussions were led according to the principles of qualitative research, including reflection of the researcher’s own background, trying to leave established position as this is a part of abductive reasoning. As we applied an interpretative approach trying to work out unconscious meaning instead of reporting attitudes, the interpretations were not proofed by the participants.

To understand experiences of GPs with interprofessional case conferences with SPHC team. GP, general practitioner; SPHC, specialist palliative home care.

Analysis was supported by using MAXQDA V.12. Steps of interpretative analysis were regularly presented and discussed in weekly team meetings.

Quotes were translated by Tina Mallon, who is a member of the KOPAL research team.

Patient and public involvement

Participants with COPD and CHF, patient representatives for patients with dementia and professional caregivers (ie, nurses, physicians) contributed to the development of the KOPAL conversation guide,37 which was used during the SPHC home visits in the KOPAL intervention. Professional caregivers and patient representatives were members of the KOPAL trial advisory board. No additional patients or public were involved in the qualitative evaluation.

Findings

Sample

Out of 26 GPs who participated in the intervention group of the KOPAL study and were invited to give an interview, 24 GPs agreed to take part in this qualitative substudy. The reason for non-response was lack of time in both cases. GPs cared for n=84 included patients (mean: 3.5) and participated in 78 case conferences (mean: 3.3), as sometimes two patients were discussed in one case conference. Categories

During analysis we developed a grounded theory on how GPs experience care of patients with CNMD before, during and after the intervention. The core phenomenon and the underlying categories are illustrated in figure 1. Details of all categories will be presented below.

Context

Progressive chronic diseases as a challenge in primary care

GPs experience primary care for patients with CNMD as a particular challenge. Besides the uncertainty of the illness trajectory, especially for CHF patients, care planning and appropriate medication is challenging.

Chronic heart failure yes that are (1) that is often well they often have long drug charts […] which always need adjusted again and again, more less and here a blood pressure prescription and THEY HAVE […] well also have a multi-medication where you always where you try to keep it within its limit that it doesn’t get out of hand (GP_HH013_m, pos. 33–41)

COPD, however, is more likely to be perceived in connection with limited healthcare options. In contrast, dementia is seen as a disease that transcends GP’s focus of care. Finally, the long and trusting relationship between GPs and their patients can lead to a strong personal involvement of the GPs, which they themselves consider problematic.

just because you know someone for ages and also the living environment it doesn’t mean/((laughs)) that you automatically treat him/ better (GP_HH020_m, pos 40)

Causal conditions

GPs’ self-conception of comprehensive care

GPs want to care for their patients until death and respect patients’ wish to stay at home as long as possible. This includes “everything [is] tried to make it work in outpatient care” (GP_GÖ011_m, pos. 60) and hospitalisation should be avoided as long as possible since this is associated with ‘giving up'. Care for patients with CNMD is daily practice for GPs, but compared with cancer patients, the focus of the treatment is different. While the primary aim in treating cancer patients is reduction of symptom burden, the focus of end-of-life care for patients with CNMD is more on psychosocial support which does not require medical expertise and is associated with higher effort.

and er er with these diseases it is rather that one has to has to (1) give social support (GP_HH009_m, pos. 10)

However, collaboration with SPHC teams is rarely considered an option. A major reason is that GPs are seldom aware of comprehensive SPHC care options covering not only medical but also multidimensional care. Instead, SPHC is often understood as a form of social emergency service, especially outside practice hours.

so and there it’s good if there is someone from SPHC () there. for people who can’t cope with psychological diseases at all so to speak, also one’s that […] need the [.] support at night (GP_GÖ008_f, pos. 330–334)

Also, in the final stage of life, SPHC competencies are mostly seen on a psychosocial level instead of providing symptom care and relief. Thus, it seems that a holistic view on these patients could be improved.

Limits of healthcare as part of everyday practice

GPs assess primary care for patients with CNMD as sufficient. Although perceiving limits in care, they do not always consider additional SPHC as an option for better supply.

as general practitioner one is a little a lone fighter . one has the patients in a longtime er care and er at some point the need for help increases noticeably […] where one gets a little insecure yes which is er necessary what is oversupply what is the right thing for my patient now and also the specialist’s level doesn’t always help (GP_OLD003_m, pos. 2)

It seems that GPs sometimes classify these limits as being an unchangeable part of primary care.

that are all patients who we treat alone. //mhm// in the outpatient care. […] and it works, also I think that’s our=to be honest that is our daily=our daily woork reality (GP_HH001B_f, pos. 8–10)

GPs’ main goal is to avoid exacerbations, hospitalisations and decompensations, which should be achieved by regular monitoring (physical, medication) during practice visits and by disease management programmes (for COPD). Regarding these aspects, contextualised by the mentioned accepted challenges when treating patients with CNMD, the benefit of interprofessional end-of-life care is not reflected as an option to share responsibility and to provide relevant support.

Core phenomenon

Complexity and challenges of care for CNMD (context) linked with GPs’ self-conception of comprehensive care and acceptance of their own limits (causal conditions) function as a frame for developing practice-specific routines.

Applying care according to practice-specific routines

Practice routine for participating GPs means to provide good healthcare using practice-specific, established, efficient and structured processes. These routines also exist with regard to the integration of specialist palliative care (SPC), as PC is primarily seen as an offer for cancer patients. This means, on the other hand, that GPs do not consider SPC for patients with progressed CNMD as a usual option.

sometime it’ll happen you know. er we have to simply wait what the diagnostics say. er, er, if that turns out to be a bronchial carcinoma, er then I’d definitely bring er SPC on board right (GP_HH003B_m, pos. 56)

SPC we have rather for our cancer patients, that we’ve got a collaboration there. with the other three diagnoses it was er rather uncommon, or I have never had it (GP_HH018B_f, Pos. 7–8)

For patients with CNMD, leading criteria for SPC is not the disease itself, but patients’ immobility, increased supply intensity or when the GP feels overloaded by patients’ psychosocial burden. For patients with dementia, the lack of informal support may also trigger the integration of SPC.

Patients and their relatives are assigned an important care-related role by wanting and respecting their decisions regarding different aspects of care. This can be interpreted as patient-centred care but also as an approach to reduce complexity of care.

well how often I want to see them the patients usually know and it is all- it’s their responsibility to do so and that’s handled very differently (GP_HH011A_f, pos. 4)

Sometimes, it’s ((laughs)) missing comprehension […] though it’s different with heart failure, errr, where the patients approach us (GP_HA003_f, Pos. 42)

Specialist experiences, in addition to experiences as a GP, seem to improve handling security and to strengthen primary care routine.

but that’s because for a long time I’ve been […] senior physician at n in a cardiological dominated clinic and with geriatric clients […] that is so to speak […] for me personally a bit yeah well AGAIN one. that doesn’t put me off right now (GP_GÖ008_f, pos. 50–56)

It appears that a practice routine can help to reduce daily challenges, but also involves the risk of overlooking individual needs, especially for long courses of illness.

Experiences with the intervention (interprofessional case conference)

Interprofessional case conference between GP and SPHC team

Some GPs perceived time load due to the case conferences, others did not and evaluated case conferences as workable. Regarding the interaction in the case conferences, GPs seemed to have prejudices against collaborating with SPHC teams and then were surprised about the low hierarchy, the friendly and constructive conversation and by the inherent potential of the discussion. The slight concern about being reviewed by the SPHC team has not come true.

well, I’ve had a positive experience and it was it was well no test but we had a talk well it was more like a proper dialogue […] well I was under the impression we can assess the patient well and in a respectful way (GP_HH018B_f, pos. 62)

I liked it that there waaas also empathy, that we didn’t just tick (1) box so that you could feel some good vibes which is particularly important in this team (GP_HA003_f, pos. 305)

Influence of case conference on level of perception

Become aware of gaps in primary care and PC dimension

During the case conferences, GPs became aware of the unmet needs of patients, especially on the level of psychosocial burden and nursing issues.

well it is generally the case that just like I said er I just realised what other problems the patient had or what else there was that is important to him (1) which was partly er not only medically relevant […] he himself had mentioned [during nurse visit] a lot more for example er er social contacts he misses he is he is lonely and the mobility is not ok (GP_OLD001A_f, Pos. 16)

Question one’s own routine of care

Some GPs reflected that they should raise awareness for the patient’s potential burden or complexity of needs and consider it as an essential part of primary care and their communication with patients.

one has to take it a little more seriously right? I think we as colleagues we are a little (challenged), we (brush) it off just chronic heart failure and that’s that. but it is rather the disease with the highest mortality rate which we have (GP_HH005_m, pos. 242–244)

Psychosocial support can enhance medical primary care

GPs recognised the need for psychosocial and care support for their patients. Since they do not see themselves as responsible, a person is needed who would regularly conduct home visits and would be able to decide about further steps. This could be a social service or just a person to talk to.

considering well (1) who [.] else have we got, who when is it also necessary to transfer to a care home and so on. well that’s obviously the gen- the general practitioners job, but also (1) the SPHC support for (1) life restricting diseases then also [.] (1) helpful if [.] there is an option (GP_GÖ011_m, pos. 178)

In particular for patients with dementia, additional support for relatives is needed, which could be addressed by SPHC teams. Some GPs admit that they are not always able to provide the necessary support for relatives.

Actions strategies

Maintain routines of care

GPs who maintain daily routine despite the intervention do not perceive gaps in patients’ home care or psychosocial healthcare or see them as unavoidable. According to their self-conception, these aspects are not necessarily part of primary care. Furthermore, they tend to interpret the recommendations of SPHC teams as a challenge for their decision-making authority.

I admit I would not like […] if other doctors would interfere […] which which medication I prescribe (GP_GÖ008_f, pos. 448–450)

Following this strategy, competencies of the SPHC team are sometimes neglected and no benefit is seen in the cooperation with SPHC teams even after the intervention. Non-cooperation with SPHC teams is often legitimated by external reasons: health insurances are impeding admission of patients to SPHC (in Germany, assumption of costs requires compliance to time limits), SPHC teams reject admission because of strong time limitation for cost recovery or SPHC teams are sometimes overburdened and do not have capacities.

er so that I honestly don’t have good access to this kind of support for severe chronic heart failure er severe COPD or else er severe advanced dementia or get co-treatment . so I have the patients we treated together and that were mainly oncological patients (GP_OLD003_m, pos. 4)

These reasons are only stated for patients with CNMD and therefore act as a legitimation for the non-collaboration with SPHC teams.

Extend routines of care

GPs recognise a partial benefit of SPHC, in the form of extending primary care. SPHC is seen as a service provider, consulted on specific issues as needed.

and there and th- sometimes the colleague is missing who could have a quick look and could assess the medication chart critically […] am I happy with these case conferences if there is someone really er and there I really see eye to eye with the nurse who takes a look and says right what can we optimise here (GP_OLD003_m, pos. 28)

Although GPs want to make their own medical decisions, they would accept regular collaboration for non-medical care that they do not (always) want to provide themselves (eg, nursing care, routine home care, time-consuming consultations). GPs would like to maintain their central role in interprofessional cooperation and sometimes seem to perceive competition with SPHC physicians, while the expertise of SPHC nurses is accepted and desired. GPs appreciate that SPHC teams could save their valuable time, organise communication with hospitals and support with advance directives. SPHC is characterised as an external service provider (eg, in case of time pressure), which supports the GPs in fulfilling their own tasks.

Integration and partial change of care routines

GPs attribute a high benefit to SPHC competency because it can improve the quality of primary care by integrating it into daily routine. GPs appreciate the holistic approach focusing on the individual patient’s needs, which leads to an increased health situation and allows patients to die at home.

just had a patient there now with final stage chronic heart failure […] he wanted to die badly and he wasn’t well at all and that is then=he received SPHC and was t=approximately two months in a hospice […] but then he improved so much so that he’s relatively well now and back at home (GP_HH007_m, pos. 3)

Collaboration at eye level is appreciated, but mainly desirable for non-medical care, for example, medical aids in the bathroom for mobile patients who are not visited at home. In such cases, GPs do not know the domestic situation and related needs.

I didn’t recognise that he wasn’t well because he still came to me right (GP_HH018B_f, pos. 28)

Some GPs realised that they had seen their patients far too rarely and changed their routine by increasing practice consultations.

well what I didn’t notice so much during the short contacts here as much as during the long interview Mrs A [SPHC nurse] conducted (GP_HH011A_f, pos. 12)

Although case conferences are quite time-consuming, they function as a possibility to strengthen the interprofessional collaboration to optimise care.

certain problem areas then er one can work out (GP_HH020_m, pos. 8)

Input given by the SPHC team is difficult to accept/to put into practice

Although collaboration is at least partially a conceivable option of action, analysis revealed GPs’ slightly defensive stance towards SPHC as part of primary care routine. One reason is that they do not assess CNMD such as CHF, COPD or dementia as eligible for SPHC analogous to cancer, which implies that they do not recognise the patients’ burden in general, but only in individual cases. Another reason is that end-of-life care requires modified care planning, particular patient-oriented communication and to address the end-of-life situation. GPs’ experience inhibits when talking about end-of-life as well as initiating SPC, since this is still a taboo subject. This means that talking and acting in PC settings require personal courage.

if one rather has the courage okay, we do it beforehand we pave the way for a faster transfer to palliative care right from the start so that he can in the short amount of time he’s got left er can stay at home I think we have to simply always be aware ourselves (GP_HA001B_f, pos. 52)

As a consequence, timely collaboration with SPHC teams is not a regular option; consideration might possibly be given in case of psychosocial needs or acute exacerbation.

the [patients] weren’t in such a situation where one would have said we need acute palliative care right away therefore the question wasn’t raised there really (GP_HA001B_f, item 28)

The analysis suggests that GPs are generally confident in the quality of care they provide. Additional SPHC could be perceived as a professional slight, with the consequence that some of them withdraw from the primary care responsibilities for the respective patient.

I generally make the referral meaning er if SPHC is necessary theeeeen er I rarely errrrrr that I go through with it but er then I say /((quickly)) okay then you have to take the patient fully on and I will only provide the minimal general practitioner’s part/what has to be done (GP_OLD004_m, pos. 8)

Discussion

The aim of the KOPAL trial was to evaluate the effectiveness of the interprofessional case conferences between GPs and SPHC teams on hospital admission (primary outcome), other secondary outcomes such as health-related quality of life or symptom burden. The findings presented in this article present the results of the qualitative evaluation of GPs’ perceptions of the intervention. Statistical findings on the effectiveness of the intervention in terms of patient-centred/reported outcomes and cost-effectiveness as well as qualitative perspectives of other actors involved are published elsewhere.28 34 38 39

We developed a grounded theory of GPs’ experiences with interdisciplinary and interprofessional case conferences on patients with advanced CNMD as part of the KOPAL trial.4 It reflects on how GPs perceive the case conferences and how they integrate the new experiences into their routines of care. Findings show that GPs have practice-specific routines when caring for people with CNMD. Interprofessional case conferences may lead to more awareness regarding gaps in primary PC, one’s own care routines or a lack of psychosocial support. Although gaining new insights, GPs follow three different strategies following the case conferences: (1) maintain routines of care and disregard added value, (2) extend routines of care according to SPHC advice and (3) integration and partial change of care routines caused by SPHC support. These strategies are influenced by GPs’ perception of SPHC teams (eg, ambivalence towards SPHC) and are less related to the content of the case conferences, but more to the experiences with and perspective on the SPHC and the persistence of GPs’ routines.

SPC is often associated with cancer patients and rarely offered to patients with CNMD, so there is a gap between both patient groups regarding the provision of SPC.1 10 Our study gives an explanation for this misbalance, focussing on how GPs frame potential benefits of SPC for both patients groups. From the perspective of GPs, cancer patients merely benefit through the competences of SPHC teams in the area of pain management and symptom control. The benefit for patients with CNMD is seen more on the psychosocial level; while the physical burden needs to be alleviated by medical experts (SPHC), the psychosocial burden could also be met by family caregivers or other social services.

The cooperation between GPs and SPHC teams is a crucial aspect of outpatient PC, including patients with CNMD as focused in this study. It improves the flow of information and a trustful communication between the healthcare professionals and thereby can improve continuity of care for patients40 and reduce hospital admissions.32 However, GPs’ perception of SPHC teams varies. On the one hand, they are sometimes reserved in sharing care with the specialist team, but on the other hand, they are more willing to cooperate and see the positive impacts for the patient and themselves, after they experienced shared management.41 42 At practice level, support for this collaboration, that is, by interprofessional case conferences with SPHC teams, has no significant impact on improving patient care as the quantitative evaluation of the KOPAL study shows.34 Our qualitative study provides a deeper explanation for the different perspectives of GPs on collaboration with SPHC teams. By case conferences, GPs become aware of unfulfilled care needs of their patients, which can be interpreted differently, either as a critic on their own incomplete care or as a valuable hint to improve care, which does not necessarily mean that patients have to be referred to SPC, but to question one’s own strategy. In the first case, GPs tend to devalue the work of SPHC teams and legitimise the care they provide, while in the second case, the competences and resources of the SPHC teams are mostly valued and the comprehensive approach is seen positively. Lacking interprofessional collaboration is, among others, caused by individual factors or professional culture.43

GPs value the long-lasting relationships with their patients and are willing to care for them also in the last phase of life.44 They see the provision of palliative home care to be part of their job responsibilities, which has a positive impact on job satisfaction.45 One reason for GPs’ reservations towards the integration of SPHC teams might be the assumption of patients that a GP’s significance decreases once an SPHC team gets involved. As SPHC takes over tasks in pain and symptom control, coordination of care and psychosocial and acute care, patients consider GPs’ role to be less central.26 Furthermore, after the integration of SPHC, GPs also criticise the missing transfer of information.44 Our study shows that GPs’ perception of SPHC also depends on the attitude and communication of SPHC team members. GPs tend to appreciate it if SPHC team members accept the GP’s sovereignty of treatment and give their feedback merely as proposals rather than as an order. Interestingly, participating SPHC physicians highly recommend communication with GPs on an equal level.28 The way of communication seems to have an impact on the cooperation between GPs and SPHC teams and could be used as an intervention on the micro-level.

PC is still mostly offered to and used by patients with cancer, while patients with CNMD, such as dementia, CHF or COPD rarely benefit from PC services.46 47 Barriers to integration of PC in these patient groups are, for example, difficulties of accurate prognosis, lack of resources of healthcare professionals, unwillingness to advance care planning discussions, lack of appropriate tools to guide GPs and difficulties in dealing with the PC taboo as well as breaking bad news.48 49 Our results confirm these findings and add further explanations from GPs’ perspective. According to the GPs interviewed, CNMD patients’ needs are predominantly in the domains of psychosocial care, everyday-life support and continuous nursing care that can be met by relatives or everyday companions rather than SPHC teams. The latter are considered too specialised on medical issues. As GPs feel responsible and capable to handle the medical treatments of these patients on their own, they see little need to integrate SPHC teams in the care of these patients. This one-dimensional thinking in PC with a focus on medical treatment, however, disregards the complexity of SPHC. Further, GPs often do not consider discussing the appropriateness of the medication regime. Since GPs feel a lack of professional consultation, case conferences might be an opportunity to close this gap.

The strength of our study lies in the development of complex indepth analysis. We provide a data-based theory that shows how the phenomena are interwoven. As we aimed to interview all participating GPs of the KOPAL intervention, theoretical sampling and data saturation did not guide data collection and data analysis. A broader sample might have revealed further aspects. A further limitation of our study might be the time lag of up to 1 year between the case conferences and the interviews, wherefore several GPs could hardly remember the content of the case conferences and its impact on their care routine. We chose this time period to avoid an influence on the intervention. On the other hand, GPs who had changed focus of care did remember details. Since the design of the study did not provide clear guidance on how the case conferences should be conducted or what topic should be discussed, not all case conferences ended with precise courses of action following the case conferences. Instead, it was the aim to analyse which strategies GPs generated by their own. This was a further strength of this study as we respected GPs’ decision making and could illustrate underlying mechanisms.

Our findings suggest that GPs could show more openness to cooperation with SPHC teams to face the challenges when treating patients with CNMD and be more willing to bundle respective competencies and resources. This implies thinking of working as partners and relying on counselling offers given by SPHC teams who have strong interest in strengthening collaboration with GPs at an equal level.28

Conclusion

Interprofessional or interdisciplinary collaboration between GPs and SPHC teams is characterised by structural conditions, daily routines and mutual knowledge of each other’s expertise and working realities. Broader knowledge about PC in general, including the perspective, competencies and networks of SPHC teams in primary care provision and timely discussion of care options, has great potential to improve patient care but also to facilitate the work of GPs and SPHC teams. However, this means that GPs should actively seek timely cooperation with SPHC to optimise end of life care. On the background of the increasing prevalence of non-malignant chronic conditions, awareness of the SPHC care concepts for these patients is required that also includes the psychosocial level. Promoting a regular process of exchange between GPs and SPHC teams (such as through round tables) may improve and establish a trusting cooperation.
