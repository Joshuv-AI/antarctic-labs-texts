Effect of Exercise Therapy on Non-Obese Women with Polycystic Ovary Syndrome: A Protocol for a Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Xiaodie Yang, Hong Yang, Jianting Lao, Qiang Zhang, Zhehao Li, Liping Bai, Chan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a prevalent endocrine disorder affecting up to 13% of women of reproductive age, with associated reproductive, metabolic, and psychological complications. While lifestyle interventions, particularly exercise therapy, are crucial in managing PCOS in obese individuals, their impact on nonobese patients remains inadequately explored. This study aims to assess the effects of exercise therapy on menstrual regularity, hormonal profiles, and metabolic parameters in nonobese women with PCOS.MethodsThis protocol outlines a single-blind, randomized controlled trial (RCT). Forty-eight nonobese women with PCOS (body mass index ≤25) meeting the inclusion criteria were enrolled and randomized into three groups: aerobic exercise with usual care (US), resistance exercise with UC, and UC alone. The interventions spanned 12 weeks, with assessments at baseline, 4, 8, and 12 weeks. Primary outcomes include menstrual regularity and anthropometric indices, while secondary outcomes encompass hormonal levels, insulin resistance, and ovarian morphology. Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.DiscussionThe study aims to provide robust evidence supporting the efficacy of exercise therapy in nonobese women with PCOS and to determine the most effective exercise modality for this population. The findings emphasize the importance of individualized exercise interventions and provide a foundation for larger scale RCTs to further substantiate these results.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a prevalent condition affecting up to 13% of women of reproductive age [1]. In addition to typical reproductive endocrinological abnormalities, such as oligo-ovulation and anovulation, hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology, metabolic disturbances are commonly observed in patients with PCOS [2]. Extensive evidence links PCOS to an increased risk of metabolic complications, including insulin resistance (IR), type 2 diabetes mellitus, metabolic syndrome (Mets), and cardiovascular disease [3].

The International Evidence-Based Guideline for the Assessment and Management of PCOS emphasizes lifestyle intervention as the primary approach for early management [4]. Traditionally, lifestyle interventions involve modifications to diet and physical activity, complemented by appropriate behavioral guidance. The 2018 PCOS guidelines advocate for lifestyle management, including exercise therapy, to enhance overall health, particularly for PCOS patients with excess weight [5]. In contrast, among women who engage in overtraining-such as high-intensity exercisers or individuals with low body weight-excessive energy expenditure, decreased leptin levels, and fluctuations in opioid neuropeptide levels may disrupt hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis function, leading to suppressed ovulation [6, 7]. The 2018 and 2023 Chinese guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of PCOS recommend that PCOS patients who need to lose weight should perform appropriate and regular physical activity (30 min daily, at least 5 times per week) and reduce sedentary behavior [8, 9]. However, these guidelines do not detail the timing, methods, intensity, or duration of exercise, nor do they address the potential benefits of exercise for nonobese PCOS patients. The 2023 International Evidence-Based Guidelines [10] offer more detailed exercise plans, but the recommended forms of exercise vary considerably and are largely based on single-center experiences. They also do not clearly identify which subgroups of PCOS patients would benefit more from exercise. Among these recommendations, the exercise guidance proposed by Australia’s Monash University Research Centre suggests that physical activity can be planned according to individual, family, and cultural preferences. Activity forms may include sports, daily household chores, active transportation (e.g., cycling), occupational activities, or recreational games. The ideal goal is to achieve 10,000 steps per day, encompassing both daily activities and planned exercise (equivalent to 30 min or 3,000 steps of walking). This approach aligns with the individualized program design emphasized in the Chinese expert consensus, offering greater practicality and adherence. Nevertheless, no studies to date have clearly answered whether nonobese PCOS patients should exercise or how they should do so. In summary, while exercise is recognized to benefit PCOS patients with metabolic disorders such as obesity, its role in restoring reproductive function in nonobese PCOS patients remains unclear. There is also a lack of research on suitable exercise modes, frequency, and volume for nonobese PCOS patients. Therefore, establishing standardized and scientifically sound exercise strategies is essential to effectively help PCOS patients improve ovulatory dysfunction.

PCOS is a multifaceted disorder with heterogeneous manifestations and a complex etiology. While IR is considered a central pathophysiological feature in PCOS, contributing to hyperandrogenism and exacerbating clinical symptoms [11], obesity is often regarded as a key factor in the development of metabolic complications. However, nonobese women with PCOS also experience disturbances in glucose and lipid metabolism, along with complications such as type 2 diabetes mellitus and Mets [12]. Although IR is strongly correlated with obesity, it is more prevalent in nonobese women with PCOS than in nonobese women without PCOS [13]. The mechanisms behind IR in lean women differ from those in obese individuals, but exercise therapy has proven effective for both groups [14].

In our previous study, standardized exercise therapy led to significant improvements in body mass index (BMI), hormonal profiles, and menstrual cycle regularity in women with PCOS, with minimal adverse effects and high patient acceptance [15]. Although obesity was not analyzed as an independent factor, the mean BMI of the primary treatment cohort was ≤25 kg/m2, indicating that most participants were nonobese and that exercise can yield therapeutic benefits in this population. Indeed, nonobese patients constitute a substantial proportion of individuals with PCOS. From our long-term clinical experience and observations, appropriately increased physical activity may also enhance sensitivity to ovulation induction agents and improve the likelihood of conception in this group. Building on these findings and clinical insights, we hypothesize that exercise may serve as an effective intervention for nonobese women with PCOS.

To investigate this, this study proposed a prospective, parallel, randomized, single-blind controlled trial to evaluate the efficacy and safety of exercise therapy in nonobese women with PCOS, comparing outcomes with a usual care (UC) control group. The study aims to assess the feasibility of conducting a large-scale randomized controlled trial (RCT), focusing on patient recruitment, retention, and treatment adherence.

Study Objectives

The primary objective of this study was to assess the impact of exercise therapy on nonobese women with PCOS. In addition, the study addresses the following secondary objectives:1.Evaluate the feasibility of conducting an RCT examining exercise training and increased physical activity in nonobese women with PCOS, including measurement of recruitment and retention rates.2.Conduct post-intervention interviews with both completers and dropouts to refine the interventions, identify barriers to physical activity, and understand how these factors may inform future exercise interventions for nonobese women with PCOS.3.Determine the standard deviation of menstrual cycle length to facilitate sample size calculation for a future large-scale RCT.

Methods

Study Design and Sample

This study follows a single-blind RCT design. The protocol has received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of West China Second University Hospital of Sichuan University (WCSUH; approval number 2024-328) and has been registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (ChiCTR2500097708). The protocol adheres to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines [16]. After obtaining participants’ consent and completing baseline assessments, eligible participants were randomly assigned to either the intervention group (exercise therapy) or the control group (UC). The study spanned 3 calendar months, from participant enrollment to follow-up assessments. Setting

Participants were recruited from WCSUH and its subcenter Hi-Tech Zone Hospital (HTH), both located in Chengdu, Sichuan Province, P.R. China. WCSUH, a leading women’s health institution in Sichuan Province, serves as a both specialist and referral center for hospitals across the province and surrounding regions. The hospital has a bed capacity of 1,580, with 46 wards and units, and employs a staff of over 5,000. In 2024, WCSUH recorded 4.128 million outpatient and emergency visits, 94,000 surgeries, and 27,800 deliveries. Similarly, HTH, a newly established 500-bed hospital, provides high-quality health services to women and children in Chengdu.

Recruitment of Participants

PCOS diagnosis was confirmed based on the Rotterdam criteria [17], requiring at least two of the following characteristics: oligo-/anovulation, clinical and/or biochemical signs of hyperandrogenism, and/or polycystic ovaries (as determined by ultrasound). Conditions with similar clinical features, such as congenital adrenal hyperplasia, Cushing's syndrome, or androgen-secreting tumors, were excluded prior to confirming the PCOS diagnosis. Women diagnosed with PCOS by clinicians were recruited through advertisements on WCSUH’s official accounts, posters, and WeChat. Eligibility screening was performed by the researcher and research assistants. Participants had to meet the following criteria: (1) aged 20–38 years, (2) BMI ≤25, and (3) provide written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) inability to perform basic activities or contraindications to exercise (e.g., heart failure, respiratory failure, bone and joint deformities), (2) cognitive disorders or severe emotional instability (e.g., Mini-Mental State Examination score <17) [18], and (3) use of drug therapies that may affect reproductive function in the past 3 months (e.g., letrozole, gonadotropins, estradiol/progesterone tablets).

Sample Size

The sample size for this study was determined based on a pilot study and referenced calculations [15]. Sample size estimation was conducted using the chi-square test formula via the “pwr” package in R software (www.r-project.org). The sample size was calculated assuming a 5% improvement in menstrual regularity for the control group [19] and a 40% improvement for the experimental group [20]. With a significance level (α) set at 0.05 and a power (1-β) of 80%, the initial estimation was increased to account for an anticipated 35% loss to follow-up and the demands of multiple procedures. This yielded a final requirement of 20 participants per group.

Data Collection Procedure

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of WCSUH. Assessments of all participants were carried out at four stages: baseline, 4 weeks, 8 weeks, and at the study’s conclusion. A comprehensive evaluation was conducted at baseline, collecting demographic data (name, age, menarche) and PCOS-specific information (anthropometric indices, menstrual regularity, hirsutism, acne, blood biomarkers, and ovarian ultrasound). Anthropometric indices and menstrual regularity were assessed every 4 weeks up to the 12-week mark, while all PCOS-specific parameters were assessed at the study’s conclusion.

Randomization

Following participant screening and baseline assessments, patients were randomized into three groups: aerobic exercise with UC group, resistance exercise with UC, and UC alone (UC group). Randomization occurred in a 1:1:1 ratio using a computer-generated random allocation sequence. To eliminate bias, the randomization process was conducted by a third party who remained unaware of the study groups. Outcome assessments were carried out by an experienced, trained researcher who was blinded to the interventions received by participants.

Interventions

The interventions in this study included aerobic and resistance exercise regimens, administered by the researcher and trained physiotherapists with postgraduate qualifications in women’s health. Different research assistants were assigned to each of the two training groups. Both exercise regimens were structured in accordance with the Consensus on Exercise Reporting Template (CERT) checklist [21]. Warm-up exercises were designed to elevate muscle temperature and enhance the elastic properties of collagen in muscles and tendons. A mild aerobic activity, such as slow running, brisk walking, or jumping jacks, was performed for 10 min until the body reached a light warmth. Stretching, which has been shown to reduce post-exercise discomfort and improve recovery, followed. The cool-down phase included self-myofascial release using a foam roller and static stretching.

Resistance Training

Resistance training focuses primarily on mat exercises or elastic bands, targeting 10 muscle groups, including the trunk, upper and lower limbs, and back (Fig. 3). The program prioritizes strengthening the chest, legs, arms, and abdomen, with alternating muscle groups each week (Table 2). Every week, 1–2 muscle groups will be emphasized. Training sessions will be scheduled 3 days, each lasting 30 min over a 12-week period. Each set consists of 8 repetitions, to be completed in 3 cycles with a 1-min rest between cycles. The load for each exercise is typically 70%–80% of the one-repetition maximum (1 RM), which is the maximum weight that can be lifted with proper form for a single repetition. For beginners, a muscular endurance regimen with lower loads and higher repetitions is recommended. Additionally, participants are advised to refrain from training during the first to fifth days of the menstrual cycle.

Aerobic Exercise

Aerobic exercises were performed for 40 min per session, with intensity set at 70%–80% of the maximum heart rate (HRmax), gradually increasing over time. The program spanned 12 weeks. In addition, other forms of regular aerobic activity, such as swimming or cycling, were recommended to complement the routine. Exercise training was advised against during the first to fifth days of the menstrual cycle.

Usual Care

Patients are advised to implement appropriate dietary modifications. For those presenting with IR, standard treatment is initiated with Metformin Hydrochloride tablets (Sino-American Shanghai Squibb Co., Ltd., H20023370) at a dose of 500 mg/day administered with meals, followed by a gradual increase to 1,500 mg/day. In cases of amenorrhea persisting for over 3 months, after pregnancy has been excluded, oral Progesterone Soft Capsules (Zhejiang Aisheng Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., H20031099) are prescribed at 200 mg/day for 14 consecutive days to induce withdrawal bleeding and provide endometrial protection.

Outcome

A proforma is developed by the researcher to document the baseline characteristics (such as age, weight, height, heart rate, previous menstrual history, and marital status) of all participants, as well as pre-, post-, and follow-up intervention outcomes. Additionally, participants’ personal exercise habits and preferences will be recorded, and appropriate considerations were made in the exercise prescriptions.

Primary Outcome Measures

The primary outcome measure will be the frequency of normal menstruation and anthropometric indices. Menstrual cycles will be evaluated throughout the study. For the purpose of this trial, an irregular menstrual cycle is defined as a cycle length >35 days, fewer than eight menstrual periods in a year, or amenorrhea (absence of menstruation for ≥6 months). Given the 3-month study duration, menstrual frequency will be assessed by comparing the number of periods in the 90 days prior to enrollment (baseline) with the number during the final 90 days of the intervention. Frequency will be categorized as 0, 1, 2, or 3 occurrences per 90-day period. Menstrual improvement is defined as an increase of one or more menstrual periods within a 90-day assessment period compared to baseline. The specific length of each menstrual cycle will also be recorded. Both the proportion of participants achieving menstrual improvement and the actual recorded cycle lengths will be included in the statistical analysis.

Anthropometric indices will be measured using a body composition analyzer (Inbody270, Biospace, Korea). Parameters will include body weight, height, body fat percentage, muscle mass, and basal metabolic rate. Measurements will be performed with participants wearing light clothing and standing barefoot, gazing straight ahead. Waist circumference will be measured at the level of the umbilicus using a non-stretchable tape measure applied directly to the skin. The hip circumference will be measured at the point of maximum circumference around the buttocks. Both measurements will be recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm. BMI and the waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) will be calculated from these measurements.

Secondary Outcome Measures

Secondary outcome measures will include sex hormone levels, fasting insulin levels, homeostatic model assessment for IR (HOMA-IR), and follicular counts. Clinical evaluation of the hyperandrogenic state will also be performed.

Blood samples will be collected between the 2nd and 5th days of the menstrual cycle (or following progesterone withdrawal bleeding if the menstrual cycle has been >2 months). Cubital venous blood will be collected and centrifuged at room temperature to obtain serum. Blood biomarkers will be analyzed using an automatic chemiluminescence immunoassay analyzer (ADVIA Centaur, Siemens, Berlin and Munich, Germany) and an automatic chemical analyzer (ADVIA2400, Siemens, Berlin and Munich, Germany) at WCSUH’s Laboratory Medicine Department. HOMA-IR will be calculated using the formula: fasting blood glucose level (mmol/L) × fasting insulin level (mU/L)/22.5. IR is defined as HOMA-IR ≥2.77.

Gynecological examinations, including transvaginal ultrasound (Resona 8; Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co, Shenzhen, China), will be performed to measure ovarian volume, antral follicle count, and endometrial thickness. For women with no sexual experience, transabdominal ultrasound will be used. These examinations will be conducted on the 10th to 14th day of the menstrual cycle.

The modified Ferriman-Gallwey scoring method [22] will be used to evaluate terminal hair growth across nine anatomical regions, including the upper lip, chin, chest, upper and lower back, upper and lower abdomen, arms, and thighs. A cumulative score exceeding 8 suggests hirsutism, with scores of 9–15 classified as mild and those above 15 indicating moderate to severe cases.

For acne assessment, the global acne grading system (GAGS) [23] will be applied. Severity is categorized into four levels: 0 (no acne/unchanged skin), 1–18 (mild), 31–38 (moderate), and scores surpassing 39 (severe).

Other Outcome Measures

Adverse events (AEs) will be continuously monitored and promptly reported throughout the study by a dedicated safety monitoring team using a comprehensive reporting system. The team will systematically track and documented all AEs to ensure timely intervention and participant safety. Patients who took continuous leave for more than 3 weeks or lost contact for over 2 weeks will be considered lost to follow-up. Every effort will be made to determine the reasons for patient dropout, which will be thoroughly documented for analysis.

Data Analysis

The per-protocol analysis approach will be employed, and data analysis will be conducted using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Normality tests will first be performed on quantitative data, including height, weight, muscle mass, sex hormone levels, fasting insulin levels, HOMA-IR index, menstrual cycle length, follicle count, and Ferriman-Gallwey score. Data conforming to a normal distribution will be presented as x¯±s, while non-normally distributed quantitative data will be presented as median (25th–75th percentile) [M (P25 ∼ P75)]. Qualitative data such as the number of oligomenorrhea cases and acne grading will be expressed as proportions and compared using the chi-square test. For hypothesis testing, normally distributed quantitative data will be analyzed using analysis of variance, non-normally distributed quantitative data will be analyzed using rank-sum tests, and qualitative data will be analyzed using the chi-square test. A significance level of 0.05 will be applied for all analyses.

Discussion

Exercise therapy is widely recommended for managing PCOS in individuals with obesity, primarily for reducing BMI and improving ovulation rates. However, its efficacy in women with nonobese PCOS remains unconfirmed, and evidence is lacking regarding the optimal exercise modality for alleviating symptoms in this population. Therefore, further investigation into exercise therapies tailored to benefit these women is crucial.

This study aims to evaluate the role of exercise therapy in nonobese PCOS and to identify the most effective exercise modality. The findings will be disseminated through relevant scientific platforms.
