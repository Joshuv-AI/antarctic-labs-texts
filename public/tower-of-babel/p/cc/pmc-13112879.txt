Patterns of blood pressure and factors associated with hypertension among young adults in Ibadan, Nigeria
Authors: Deborah Oke, Okechukwu Ogah, Yusuf Bello, Oluwatosin Lawal, Yusuf Kareem, Ololade Adeyemi, Oluwatoyin Olalemi, Babatunde Adedokun, Babatunde Salako, Isaac Adewole, Deborah Watson-Jones, Imran Morhason-Bello

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension is increasingly recognised among younger adults and contributes to early cardiovascular risk, yet population-level data in this age group remain limited in many low- and middle-income countries. This study examined the patterns of blood pressure, the prevalence of hypertension and factors associated with hypertension among adults aged 18–45 years in Nigeria.MethodsA secondary statistical analysis was performed on the Sexual Behaviour and HPV Infections in Nigerians (SHINI) study dataset, which included sexually active males and females between 18 and 45 years old in Ibadan. A face-to-face interview was conducted and participants’ socio-demographic, behavioural, lifestyle, and biological characteristics were recorded. Specifically, both systolic and diastolic blood pressure measurements were measured. Participants were classified as having stage 1 hypertension if the systolic blood pressure (SBP) was ≥ 130 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) was ≥ 80 mmHg, and as having stage 2 hypertension if SBP was ≥ 140 mmHg and DBP was ≥ 90 mmHg. Overall, 19.9% of participants had stage 1 hypertension and 9.0% had stage 2 hypertension. Both stage 1 and stage 2 hypertension were significantly more prevalent in females than in males. Age and BMI were significantly associated with higher odds of stage 1 hypertension while age was significantly associated with stage 2 hypertension.ConclusionStage 1 and stage 2 hypertension are more prevalent among females than males among sexually active young adults aged 18–45 years in Ibadan, Nigeria. Background

Globally, hypertension is the most significant, leading, and avoidable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and overall mortality [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that 1.28 billion people aged 30 to 79 years are affected worldwide, with the majority residing in low and middle income countries (LMICs) [2]. The global age-standardized prevalence of hypertension among populations aged 30–79 years is 32% in females and 34% in males [2]. The rising burden of hypertension has been linked to demographic transitions, particularly population ageing and increased unhealthy lifestyle behaviours such as excessive sodium intake, insufficient potassium consumption, and inadequate physical activity [2].

Over the past two decades, the prevalence of hypertension has increased significantly in LMICs, whereas, a decline was observed in most high-income countries (HICs) [2]. These contrasting trends indicate that health systems in developing countries may be confronting a rapidly expanding double burden of disease or even triple burden with the added challenges of HIV/AIDS and injuries in most parts of Africa [3, 4]. Africa currently bears a large portion of the growing global burden of hypertension [3, 4]. In Nigeria, hypertension is a prevalent risk factor for stroke, chronic kidney disease, heart failure, cognitive impairment and ischemic heart disease [5, 6].

Generally, hypertension is associated with high cardiovascular risk across age groups, race, or ethnicity [7, 8]. Every 20 mmHg increase in office systolic blood pressure (SBP) or 10 mmHg increase in office diastolic blood pressure (DBP) is associated with a 2-times higher risk of fatal coronary artery disease or stroke [9]. Emerging evidence indicates that hypertension are increasingly diagnosed among younger adults and are associated with higher cardiovascular (CV) event [9]. A large prospective cohort study reported that hypertension is associated with nonfatal CV events and mortality in young people [10]. For example, in the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) study that involved a follow-up of participants for nearly two decades showed a progressive increase in CV risk with rising BP [11]. Specifically, the study found that hypertensive young adults had a 8.4-times higher CV risk compared to normotensive peers [11] which is similar to another study by Son et al. in Korea [9]. A meta-analysis of 17 observational studies of more than 4.5 million young adults aged 18–45 years with a follow-up of 15 years, found that stage 1 and stage 2 hypertension were associated with a 1.92- and 3.15-fold higher risk of CV events, respectively, compared with optimal blood pressure [12]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the association between hypertension and adverse CV outcomes in young adults is similar to that observed in middle-aged and older populations.

Most population-based studies on hypertension in Nigeria were conducted on either older adults, broad adult age ranges or used traditional hypertension cut-offs (140/90 mmHg) [13, 17], resulting in limited data on blood pressure patterns, hypertension prevalence, and associated factors among young adults. In addition, few studies had examined hypertension within sexually active populations, a group that may have distinct behavioural, lifestyle, and biological risk profiles relevant to cardiovascular health. Ibadan, a large and socio-demographically diverse urban centre in southwestern Nigeria, provides an important setting to explore these patterns in a relatively young population.

This study therefore investigates the pattern of BP, prevalence of hypertension, and its risk factors among young sexually active adults aged 18–45 years in Nigeria using data from the Sexual Behaviours and HPV Infections in Nigerians in Ibadan (SHINI) Study. By applying the contemporary 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) hypertension classification criteria and focusing on a younger age group, this study addresses an important gap in Nigeria where hypertension poses a significant health challenge. Timely detection, effective treatment, and proper management of hypertension are essential for preventing severe complications such as stroke, chronic kidney disease and heart disease which are prevalent and debilitating in our population. By highlighting these issues, this study aims to generate evidence that can inform public health strategies and improve CV outcomes in Nigeria.

Methods

Study design, population, and study site

This was a secondary analysis of a cross-sectional study entitled sexual behaviour and HPV infection in Nigerians in Ibadan (SHINI), conducted in two local government areas (LGAs) in Ibadan among sexually active male and female aged 18 to 45 years [18, 19]. Data were collected from Moniya and Sasa in Akinyele LGA (peri-urban) and in the densely populated urban district of Mokola in Ibadan North LGA (urban). These locations were chosen because of their diverse socioeconomic and demographic composition, as well as their mix of cultures and religions. The detailed methodology and conceptual framework of the SHINI study have been described elsewhere [18, 21]. Participants were females from the general population (FGP) and female sex workers (FSWs), and males in general population without sex work experience.

Sampling and enrolment of study participants

A two-stage sampling procedure was used to select eligible male and FGP. The enumeration area (EA) list created for the 2006 National Population Commission Census was used to randomly select four EAs from Mokola and Moniya, and one from Sasa, during the first sampling stage. A unique identifying number was assigned to each household in the selected EAs. Research assistants then conducted a census in the selected EAs by visiting the households to generate a list of male and female aged 18 to 45 years. Using a systematic sampling approach, participants were randomly selected from a sampling frame generated through the house listing. The sampling frame included young individuals (18–24 years) and older adults (25–45 years) for the second stage of sampling.

Gender-matched research assistants invited eligible participants to attend the clinic, where the study objectives were further explained and an information leaflet was provided to support the discussion. Each consenting participant was reminded of their clinic appointment 72 h, 24 h, and on the day of participation. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants at the clinic prior to their enrolment. Thereafter, the research assistant conducted a 30-minute face-to-face interview to collect information on sociodemographic characteristics, sexual behaviours, and lifestyle characteristics, after which clinical assessments and biological sample collection were performed.

Specifically, each participant’s SBP and DBP was measured in a seated position after resting for at least five minutes using validated automated BP devices. The devices were configured to obtain multiple consecutive readings (typically three) during a single measurement session and automatically calculate the mean SBP and DBP values [22]. This automated averaging approach reduces the influence of single-reading variability and improves measurement reliability. The mean SBP and DBP values were reported and used for analysis. Participants’ diabetes and asthma status were also documented.

For the FSWs, trained field workers first conducted a mapping of brothels in five urban LGAs in Ibadan. Following discussions with the manager or leader of the FSWs, female research assistants generated a list of eligible participants and assigned each a unique identification number. In brothels with more than ten eligible participants, individuals were randomly selected while all eligible FSWs were recruited from brothels with ten or fewer participants. During visits to each brothel, female research assistants provided participants with informational pamphlets and discussed the study objectives privately with each participant, usually in their rooms. Data on sociodemographic characteristics, sexual behaviours, lifestyle, and SBP and DBP were collected in each participant’s room.

All participants provided written informed consent and underwent HIV testing using rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), following the National Guidelines for HIV Prevention, Treatment, and Care by the Federal Ministry of Health in Nigeria [23]. Detailed information regarding survey completion rates, sample collection, transportation, and storage procedures has been published elsewhere [18, 21]. Participants received toiletries and were reimbursed for transportation costs for their participation.

Ethical approval

The SHINI study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine Ethics Committee, London (LSHTM 9736-3); the University of Ibadan/University College Hospital Ethics Committee, Ibadan (UI/EC/16/005); and the Oyo State Government, Nigeria (AD13/479/712).

Data management

Data were analysed using STATA version 15.0 (StataCorp, 2019. Stata Statistical Software: Release 15. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC). Descriptive analysis was conducted for participants’ sociodemographic, behavioural, lifestyle, and biological characteristics. Frequency and percentage were used to summarize the categorical variables, while continuous variables were presented using the mean and standard deviation.

The hypertension was classified in accordance with the 2017 ACC/AHA guideline [24], which classifies it into stage 1 and stage 2 based on SBP and DBP thresholds. Participants were classified as having stage 1 hypertension if SBP was ≥ 130 mmHg and DBP was ≥ 80 mmHg, and as having stage 2 hypertension if SBP was ≥ 140 mmHg and DBP was ≥ 90 mmHg. Participants with discordant SBP and DBP values below the thresholds were not classified as hypertensive. This approach represents a conservative classification of SBP and DBP, reflects the graded increase in CV risk with rising BP, and allows differentiation between early and more severe hypertension when examining prevalence and associated factors. The 2017 ACC/AHA classification was adopted to allow for early identification of hypertension and facilitate comparability with contemporary global studies.

Mean SBP and DBP were compared between males and females with independent t-tests, while differences in the prevalence of hypertension stages were assessed using the chi-square test of association. Association between participants’ categorical characteristics and hypertension status were assessed using the chi-square test of association or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

To assess the significant risk factors for hypertension, both crude and adjusted multivariable hierarchical logistic regression analyses were performed. Model 1 comprised the crude (unadjusted) associations between the outcome variable and each explanatory variable. In the multivariate hierarchical models, variables were entered in conceptual blocks based on their theoretical proximity to the outcome. Sociodemographic variables were entered as distal determinant in the second model, followed by behavioural and lifestyle factors in the third model In the final model, biological and anthropometric variables were included as more proximal determinants. This approach allowed assessment of the independent effects of each block of variables while controlling for potential confounding by variables entered at earlier stages. The results of the crude and adjusted multivariable logistic regression analyses were reported as crude odds ratios (cOR) and adjusted odds ratios (aOR) respectively, together with their 95% confidence intervals (CI).

For all analyses pairwise deletion was used for descriptive analyses, allowing each analysis to include all available data for the relevant variables. For multivariable models and tests of association, listwise deletion was applied. That is, cases with missing values in any required variables were excluded [25]. The level of statistical significance was set at 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

A total of 941 participants, including 316 males and 625 females, were recruited for the SHINI study and analysed in this study. The mean age of the participants was 29.1 ± 7.4 years. The mean age was higher among females (29.5 ± 7.0 years) than males (28.3 ± 8.1 years). Majority of participants had at least a senior secondary education, and nearly half (47.7%) were single. Alcohol consumption was common among both males and females (65.2% vs. 52.2%), while cigarette smoking was reported by fewer than one-third of participants (28.2% vs. 17.1%) (Table 1).

The mean SBP was higher among males (121.1 ± 15.3 mmHg) than females (119.8 ± 19.2 mmHg), however this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.285). In contrast, the mean DBP was significantly higher among females (81.5 ± 14.4 mmHg) than males (75.0 ± 11.6 mmHg) (p < 0.001).

There was a significant association between sex and the distribution of SBP and DBP thresholds. While similar proportions of males (23.7%) and females (22.7%) had SBP ≥ 130 mmHg (p = 0.727), a significantly higher proportion of females had DBP ≥ 80 mmHg compared with males (49.4% vs. 30.4%, p < 0.001). Also, higher proportion of females than males had ≥ 140 mmHg (12.2% vs. 6.7%, p = 0.009) and DBP ≥ 90 mmHg (24.2% vs. 9.2%, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Similarly, the prevalence of stage 2 hypertension increased significantly with age among female participants. However, participants aged 35 to 39 years had a lower prevalence (15.8%) compared to those aged 30 to 34 years (17.5%) (p < 0.001). Hypertension was more prevalent among widowed participants (40.0%) and divorced/separated participants (16.2%) compared to single (12.2%) and married participants (6.0%) (p = 0.002). Hypertensive female participants had significantly higher body weight and height compared to non-hypertensive female participants. Obese female participants had the highest prevalence of hypertension (17.4%), followed by those who were overweight (12.1%), those with normal BMI (6.8%) and underweight (6.7%).

All variables significantly associated with stage 2 hypertension among both male and female participants (Table 3) were also associated with stage 1 hypertension (Supplementary Table 1). Ethnicity (p = 0.049), history of alcohol intake (p = 0.024), and body weight (p = 0.039) showed significant associations with stage 1 hypertension among males only (Supplementary Table 1).

In the crude model, the odds of hypertension increased significantly with age (p < 0.001). Female participants had 2.15 times higher odds of hypertension compared to male participants (95% CI: 1.24–3.72; p = 0.037). Underweight participants had lower odds of hypertension (crude odds ratio [cOR] = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.09–1.74) compared to those with normal BMI, while overweight (cOR = 2.20, 95% CI: 1.25–3.89) and obese participants (cOR = 3.56, 95% CI: 2.07–6.12) had significantly higher odds compared to participants with normal weight (Table 4).

Although being a female, marital status, and BMI were significantly associated with stage 2 hypertension in the crude models, these were no longer statistically significant after adjusting for sociodemographic, behavioural, and biological factors. In the fully adjusted model, age emerged as the only independent predictor of stage 2 hypertension. Participants aged 40 years and above had higher odds of hypertension compared with younger participants (aOR = 14.63, 95% CI: 4.58–46.72, p = 0.001).

In contrast to stage 2 hypertension, marital status and BMI were additional factors that were found to be significantly associated with stage 1 hypertension. Divorced or separated participants had higher odds of having stage 1 hypertension aOR = 1.85; 95% CI: 1.11–3.09) compared to single or married participants, while overweight participants also had increased odds (aOR = 1.37; 95% CI: 0.85–2.21) compared to those with normal BMI (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

This study uniquely focused on assessing the pattern of BP and factors associated with hypertension, among young adults in different population of male and female in Ibadan, Nigeria. The results showed a higher mean SBP among males than females, and a significantly higher mean DBP among females than males. Approximately two in ten young adults had stage 1 hypertension and nearly one in ten had stage 2 hypertension, with females having a higher burden of stage 1 and 2 than male participants. Sex of participants was significantly associated with higher odds of stage 1 and stage 2 hypertension, while BMI was associated with the odds of stage 1 hypertension only. Findings from this study align with emerging global and WHO evidence indicating that hypertension is increasingly recognised among young adults, prompting shifts toward earlier detection and prevention within contemporary hypertension frameworks [26, 27].

The prevalence of stage 2 hypertension in this study was lower than that reported in previous population-based studies and systematic reviews conducted in Nigeria among adults aged 18 years and above, many of which used a hypertension cut-off of 140/90 mmHg [13, 17]. This difference can be explained by variations in the age of study populations, as most previous studies included significant number of older adults, particularly those aged over 50 years, who are known to have a higher risk of secondary hypertension [28, 29]. In contrast, the current study was restricted to adults aged 18–45 years and therefore provides an estimate of hypertension burden specifically among younger adults.

Findings from this study is consistent with recent evidence from West Africa and sub-Saharan Africa, which highlights substantial variation in hypertension prevalence by age and sex, with higher risk among older adults and individuals with overweight or obesity [30, 32]. These regional patterns support the relatively lower prevalence observed in our young adults and underscore the importance of early-life hypertension surveillance.

This study shows a higher prevalence of both stage 1 and stage 2 hypertension among females compared to males. This finding contrasts with many previous studies, which generally found hypertension to be more prevalent in males than in females [14, 33, 34]. A systematic review of hypertension prevalence in Nigerian adults aged 18 years and above found that 22 studies reported higher prevalence in males, while 11 studies reported higher prevalence in females [13]. Our findings therefore add to the emerging evidence that hypertension may be equally or more prevalent among sexually active young adult females.

Several mechanisms may plausibly contribute to the higher prevalence observed among females in this study, particularly among sexually active women aged 18–45 years. One potential explanation is the use of hormonal contraceptives, especially those containing estrogen and progestin, which have been associated with elevated blood pressure in previous studies [35, 37]. The risk appears to be higher with long-term use and among women with additional risk factors such as obesity, a family history of hypertension, or smoking. In populations where contraceptive use is common among women of reproductive age, this may partly explain the higher prevalence of both stage 1 and stage 2 hypertension observed among females compared with males. However, contraceptive use was not assessed in the SHINI dataset, and this explanation should therefore be interpreted with caution rather than as a causal inference. Future studies incorporating detailed information on contraceptive use, duration, and formulation are needed to clarify the role of hormonal contraception in hypertension risk among young women in this setting.

This study corroborates the well-established association between hypertension and age. We found that the odds of both stage 1 and stage 2 hypertension significantly increase with age. This is likely due to age-related changes in the body, particularly in the vascular system, where arteries tend to stiffen and become less flexible [38]. Some studies from sub-Saharan Africa similarly identified aging as one of the strongest and most consistent predictors of hypertension across populations, after adjusting for behavioural and metabolic risk factors [30, 32]. The arterial stiffening due to atherosclerosis contributes to higher blood pressure readings, especially SBP [39]. Aging is also often accompanied by cumulative exposure to other risk factors such as poor diet, physical inactivity, and underlying metabolic changes, all of which may further contribute to elevated blood pressure levels.

Divorced or separated participants had higher odds stage 1 and stage 2 hypertension compared to those who were single, married, or widowed. This pattern aligns with findings from previous studies that reported lower BP levels among married individuals [40, 41]. The observed association may reflect profound impact of psychosocial stress and emotional well-being on cardiovascular health. However, in this study, the associations between marital status and stage 1 and stage 2 hypertension were not significant after full adjustment, suggesting that the relationship may be partly explained by confounding factors rather than an independent effect of marital status. Divorce or separation from a marital union is sometime associated with bouts of emotional distress [42], financial instability [43], social isolation [44], and disruption of daily routines, all of which can contribute to elevated BP or hypertension. In the context of sub-Saharan Africa, particularly in Nigeria, the social consequences of a broken marriage are more pronounced due to deeply rooted cultural and gender norms [45]. Divorced or separated individuals, especially women, may face stigma, reduced family support, and limited access to resources, which may increase their vulnerability to chronic stress and hypertension.

Numerous studies have shown a significant relationship between hypertension and body mass index (BMI) [46, 47]. Consistent with these findings, this study found that hypertension was significantly associated with BMI, with overweight and obese participants exhibiting higher odds of hypertension compared to those with normal or underweight BMI. Excess body fat contributes to elevated blood pressure through several physiological mechanisms, including increased sympathetic nervous system activity [48], insulin resistance [49], and the activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) [50], resulting in vasoconstriction and fluid retention. Furthermore, obesity is often accompanied by a pro-inflammatory state and endothelial dysfunction, which can further exacerbate vascular resistance and increase the risk of hypertension.

Although alcohol consumption and smoking are widely recognized as important risk factors for hypertension in both males and females, our study did not identify these factors as statistically significant predictors of stage 1 or stage 2 hypertension, either in the crude model or after adjusting for other covariates. The lack of statistically significant associations in this study may reflect the relatively low prevalence of heavy substance use, which limited the ability to detect clear associations within this population. Nevertheless, we observed a trend toward higher odds of hypertension among individuals who smoked or consumed alcohol. Notably, participants with heavy or frequent alcohol intake had elevated risk of hypertension compared to those who reported moderate consumption. This suggests a possible dose-response relationship, where the quantity and frequency of alcohol intake may influence BP levels. The mixed findings in our study are consistent with those reported in prior studies. While some studies reported that smokers had lower or similar odds of hypertension compared with non-smokers [51, 53], others found a higher risk of hypertension among smokers [54, 55]. These conflicting results highlight the complexity of the relationship between smoking, alcohol use, and BP, and underscore the need for further research that considers the duration, and intensity of substance use, as well as potential confounding lifestyle and genetic factors.

This study has some limitations. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between identified risk factors and hypertension. Data on dietary intake, physical activity, and family history of hypertension were not available and could not be accounted for in the analysis. The study population of sexually active young adults limits generalisability of findings among non-sexually active young adults.

However, there are some strengths in this study. Blood pressure was measured with a standardised protocol using validated automated devices configured to obtain multiple consecutive readings and report mean values, thereby enhancing measurement reliability and internal consistency [22]. Research staff were trained in BP measurement procedures to minimise measurement error. Overall, this study contributes contemporary population-level evidence on BP patterns and hypertension among young adults in Nigeria.

In conclusion, this study found that approximately two in every ten sexually active adults had stage 1 hypertension, while about one in every ten had stage 2 hypertension. The study highlighted factors such as being divorced or separated from their spouse and obesity as being associated with stage 1 hypertension. We recommend targeted hypertension screening among young adults particularly among females and those divorced or separated from their spouse. Public health interventions should prioritize lifestyle modifications such as weight management, regular physical activity, and healthy eating, especially for overweight and obese individuals. We advocate blood pressure measurement and risk reduction strategies especially among vulnerable young adults who may be at higher risk of having hypertension. Health education and awareness creation campaigns should emphasize that hypertension is not limited to older adults while early detection and prevention are essential for reducing long-term cardiovascular risks.
