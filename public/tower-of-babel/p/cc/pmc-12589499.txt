Study on ice condensation characteristics and deformation laws of two-dimensional orthopermafrost
Authors: Genglong Zhao, Zhilong Zhang, Jiangang Chen, Xuejun Liu, Bingbing Lei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With the further development of the permafrost area in the country, the research on geotechnical engineering is becoming more and more in-depth at this stage. Aiming at the two-dimensional freezing problem of frozen soil subgrade on slopes in alpine mountainous areas of Xinjiang, this paper studies the frost-heave segregation characteristics and deformation laws of silt and silty clay under two-dimensional freezing conditions through laboratory experiments, theoretical analysis and model calculations. The temperature field, moisture field and displacement field distribution of two-dimensional slope frozen soil under different working conditions are obtained from the laboratory test. On the basis of experiments, a hydrothermal coupling numerical calculation model for the growth criterion of two-dimensional ice permeable crystals is established. The results show that the temperature gradient will occur at the same soil layer during the two-dimensional freezing process, namely the temperature gradient generated by the initial temperature difference between the upper and lower roofs and the temperature gradient between the soil layers caused by the tilt of the upper roof. And due to the difference in soil properties, the temperature of silty clay at the same position is slightly higher than that of silt soil at the same upper roof inclination. In the case of two-dimensional freezing, water migrates from the unfrozen area in the lower part of the soil layer to the frozen area, and the water in the unfrozen area and the frozen area will also migrate horizontally to the right. The evolution of frost heave displacement can be divided into three stages: frost heave stage, frost heave rapid development stage, and stability stage. The frost heave displacement of the upper surface of the two-dimensional frozen soil sample is non-uniform, and the displacement of the middle position of the middle of the soil at the same height is greater than that of the two sides. The displacement of silt soil is slightly larger than that of silty clay, and different working conditions have the greatest influence on displacement at x = 0 m, and the influence of working conditions at x = 0.1 m is the smallest. The numerical simulation results are in good agreement with the experimental data, with a temperature field simulation accuracy of 90%, a water content error of less than 5% for the freezing front of the moisture field, and a maximum displacement error of 4.5% for the displacement field. The established model successfully reproduced the lateral migration of moisture and uneven frost heave deformation caused by the asymmetric temperature field, revealing that the two-dimensional coupling of heat, water, and force is the core disaster mechanism of slope frozen soil roadbed diseases. This model provides a reliable theoretical tool for predicting and preventing frost heave in cold region slope roadbeds. Analysis of the two-dimensional deformation mechanism of frozen soil subgrade in high-altitude mountainous areas and the provision of improved preventive measures.

Introduction

Frozen soil is a geological medium that is extremely sensitive to temperature changes, and its physical and mechanical properties will change significantly with temperature changes. Especially in road engineering in alpine mountainous areas, the frost heave problem of frozen soil subgrade on the slope is more prominent. Combined with the engineering practice of cold regions, it can be seen that there is a two-dimensional freezing effect caused by complex boundary constraints (i.e., phase change, heat transfer-deformation coupling phenomenon in the process of two-way freezing of soil) in engineering structures such as foundation pit support, slope stability, road foundation and channel seepage prevention. The temperature field gradient distribution, water migration path, and frost heave deformation characteristics in this type of engineering scenario are significantly different from the traditional one-dimensional freezing mode. Therefore, it is of great theoretical and practical significance to study the frost-heave segregation characteristics and deformation laws of frozen soil on slopes under two-dimensional freezing conditions. The field of frozen soil engineering has long focused on the frost heave behavior of soil and its interaction mechanism with structures1,2. In the slope directional frozen soil subgrade project represented by the alpine mountainous areas of Xinjiang, three core scientific problems are derived due to the influence of terrain asymmetry (geometry and slope anisotropy) and multi-field coupling effect3,4–6: (1) The asymmetric slope causes spatial differentiation of the thermal field, resulting in the difference in the propulsion rate of the freezing front and the heterogeneity of the spatial distribution of frost-heave segregation; (2) During the freeze-thaw cycle, the water in the active layer is driven by thermodynamic potential to migrate to the freeze-thaw interface, forming a dynamic water-rich soft zone, which constitutes a potential control surface for slope slippage. 3.The evolution of spatiotemporal asymmetry of hydrothermal field induces multi-scale diseases such as differential settlement of roadbed, structural cracking and slope instability, which seriously threatens the safety of road engineering operation. At this stage, it is urgent to construct a multi-field coupling theoretical system for frozen soil on slopes, and systematically reveal its freeze-thaw deformation dynamics and engineering disaster evolution laws. Aiming at the problem of frozen soil engineering in cold regions, the interaction mechanism between soil stress field and deformation field has important research value7. Through the integrated analysis of stress field and hydrothermal coupling model, the academic community has gradually constructed a multi-field coupling theory system. The Abouist team8delved into the water-thermal-force three-field coupling mechanism from the thermal expansion characteristics of porous media. Gatmiri et al.9 integrated the nonlinear deformation characteristics of soil particles into the coupling model, while Mu et al.10developed a quasi-three-field coupling model by equivalence the water field as an additional component of soil particles. Mao Xuesong’s group11 further constructed the correlation equation between the water field and the deformation field, forming a theoretical framework for indirect coupling. He et al.12 systematically established a theoretical system of hydrothermal coupling model based on the assumption of viscoelastic plastic materials, while Li et al.13,14 focused on the influence mechanism of frost heave deformation on heat and mass transfer, and improved the coupling model using continuous medium theory.

In terms of experimental research, Lin Xianchang et al.15 combined multi-field coupling analysis and crystallization kinetics theory to quantify the reinforcing effect of water migration effect driven by matrix suction on frost heave deformation through frost heave tests in unsaturated expansive soils. Zhou et al.16 revealed that the frost heave rate is positively correlated with the temperature gradient through the one-dimensional frost heave test of saturated silt, and the linear attenuation law shows with the increase of vertical pressure.

The one-dimensional frost heave test of silt soil revealed that the frost heave rate was positively correlated with the temperature gradient, and showed a linear decay law with the increase of vertical pressure. As a key scientific problem of frost heave model, the formation mechanism of ice lenses directly affects the prediction accuracy. The frozen edge theory proposed by Miller17 believes that there is a migration phenomenon of unfrozen water in the phase transition zone, and its hydrothermal coupling law dominates the rigid connection mechanism between the ice lens and the matrix, which lays the foundation for the rigid ice model. Gilpin et al.18,19 used the stress threshold as a criterion for ice lenses for the first time, while Sheng’s team20 improved the criterion based on the effective stress zeroing criterion. Zeng et al.21 used ice pressure as a key parameter to construct a frost heave model of saturated soil, and successfully simulated the evolution of frost-heave segregation under bottom hydration and top pressurization. The simplified rigid ice model developed by Sheng Daichao and Zhang et al.22 systematically reveals the regulatory mechanism of frost heave susceptibility by environmental factors such as overburden pressure and temperature gradient. In terms of engineering application research, numerical simulations of the slope subgrade of the Qinghai-Tibet Railway by Sheng Yu et al.23 showed that the slope foot area showed the characteristics of temperature and moisture synergistic enrichment, and the development direction of the melting interface was consistent with the natural slope trend. Zhang et al.24 revealed the difference in the lateral deformation of the subgrade caused by the yin-yang slope effect through the ground temperature-deformation coupling equation, and their research results provide a theoretical basis for engineering protection. Yang et al.25 found that the calculation of the heterogeneous temperature field can reduce the amount of deformation by about 20%, highlighting the significant effect of temperature gradient on the strength of frozen soil. Zhao et al.26 confirmed that the temperature gradient exacerbates the spatial heterogeneity of frozen soil failure and deformation, and the larger the gradient value, the more significant the material brittleness failure characteristics. This study focuses on the frozen soil roadbed on the slopes of alpine mountainous areas in Xinjiang, and uses a combination of laboratory experiments, theoretical analysis and model calculation to explore the development characteristics and deformation response of silt and silty clay in the two-dimensional freezing mode. Analysis shows that the combination of lateral and vertical thermal gradients in the two-dimensional freezing effect uniquely controls the origin, spatial distribution, and growth direction of ice lenses by altering the temperature field distribution, moisture migration paths, and phase change dynamics: there are mainly three changes: (1) The surface distribution of the temperature field leads to non-uniform advancement of the phase change front; (2) Water vapor migration shifts from purely vertical to a “vertical-horizontal” coupling mode; (3) The growth of ice lenses is dominated by the direction of heat conduction but modulated by the lateral gradient, resulting in tilted or wedge-shaped ice layers. By constructing a two-dimensional water-heat-force coupling numerical model, the frost heave mechanism of slope frozen soil under complex boundary conditions is systematically revealed.

Two-dimensional Frost heave test

The test uses a cylindrical plexiglass barrel as the specimen container, with a height of 400 mm and an inner diameter of 200 mm. By changing the inclination angle of the upper temperature control roof, the two-dimensional freezing conditions of frozen soil on different slopes are simulated. During the test, the changes in temperature, moisture and displacement are monitored in real time by the monitoring device and the data are recorded synchronously, and the instruments and monitoring devices are used in the test as shown in Figs. 1 and 2.

Fig. 2CXR6-XT hydrothermal dynamic monitoring system for saline soils.

CXR6-XT hydrothermal dynamic monitoring system for saline soils.

In the experiment, silt soil (A1, A2, A3, A4) and silty clay (B1, B2, B3, B4) were selected for two-dimensional freezing test, and the two-dimensional freezing distribution of frozen soil on different slopes was analyzed by changing the inclination angle of the roof (0°, 10°, 20°, 30°) respectively. During the test, the cold end temperature, warm end temperature, and ambient temperature were set to -5 °C, 3 °C, and 3 °C respectively.

Specific implementation steps: 1. Preparation of Remolded Soil: The soil sample was collected from a permafrost area traversed by the Nalati–Balguntay Highway, characterized by steep natural slopes, high ice content, and high porosity. Field-collected soil samples were tested for parameters such as water content and dry density. To accurately study the frost heave characteristics of the soil in this area, artificially remolded soil was used. The collected soil was dried, crushed, and sieved through a 0.5 mm sieve. A certain amount of deionized water was added and mixed with the soil to prepare samples with a water content of 30%, matching the water content and dry density of the field samples. The mixture was left to stand for 24 h. After 24 h, the prepared soil was divided into 10 equal parts and placed into the test container in layers using the layered compaction method. The total height of the soil sample was 20 cm. A displacement sensor was then fixed above the soil sample, completing the preparation process. It can also further reflect the special physical properties of silty soil in certain regions of Gansu Gannan and Xinjiang Badao.2. Specimen installation: After allowing the soil sample to stand, use the layered compaction method to fill the sample tube. Compress the upper surface of the sample at different angles and place it under the top temperature control plate. Then, insert four temperature sensors and three moisture sensors into the small holes left on the side of the sample tube, and place it into the frost heave testing machine. Connect the upper and lower cold and warm plates to the cooling bath, adjust the displacement gauge to touch the top temperature control plate, and set up the temperature, moisture, and displacement data collection. 3. Freezing process: The temperature at the cold end is set to -5 °C and the temperature at the warm end is set to 3 °C for 72 h, and data is collected in real time. 4. Post-freezing analysis: Determine the moisture content in layers and record the distribution pattern of ice condensation.

Temperature field analysis

The test results show that during the two-dimensional freezing process, the temperature change in the middle of the soil is more complex, the temperature reaches stability more slowly, and the temperature will continue to fluctuate around the freezing temperature for a period of time. After freezing, the temperature field distribution is basically curved, and the closer it is to the cold plate, the larger the tilt angle of the temperature field. There is a significant difference in the temperature field distribution between silty clay and silty clay, and the heat transfer rate of silty clay is faster and the temperature field distribution is more uniform. According to the classification method proposed by Lai27, the temperature field time history evolution with reference to this experiment can be divided into three stages: FC (0 ~ 10 h) in the rapid cooling stage, SC (10 ~ 30 h) in the slow cooling stage, and SS (30 ~ 72 h) in the stable freezing stage. In the rapid cooling stage, the temperature of the soil column decreases rapidly, and the closer it is to the cold end, the faster the temperature drop rate. In the slow cooling stage, the cooling time of the soil at different locations is different, and the closer it is to the cold end, the shorter the cooling time. In the stable freezing stage, the internal temperature of the soil gradually stabilizes with the change of time, and the time difference between the stable state is mainly controlled by the heat flow equilibrium conditions between the hot and cold interfaces. The formation mechanism of the above phenomenon is closely related to the equilibrium process of heat transfer between the hot and cold ends.

During the freezing process, the soil supercooling phenomenon occurred at the initial freezing stage 1 ~ 2 h at 0.175 m from the warm end of the specimen, and the analysis was that when the soil temperature dropped to the freezing point below 0 °C, the latent heat release of water phase change in the soil would lead to a jump increase in soil temperature and then a stable freezing temperature fluctuation zone, and the test results showed that the freezing temperature of silt and silty clay was − 0.24 °C and − 0.6 °C33, respectively. Observing the supercooling phenomenon of different soil samples, it was found that the silt was 0.175 m away from the warm end The temperature rebound (-0.24 °C→0 °C) occurred for 2 h, and no obvious supercooling was observed in the silty clay, which may be related to the high porosity of the silt, the large porosity between the particles, the abundance of air content, the good pore connectivity, and the relatively good overall heat transfer performance. Compared with silty clay, the rapid freezing stage of silt soil is shorter, about 9.5 h, and the slow freezing stage lasts about 20 h, which is 2 h earlier than silty clay and enters the stable freezing stage.

Migration law of freezing front

This study focuses on revealing the complex physical mechanisms and phenomenological laws of two-dimensional freezing. Due to the long trial period and high costs, the freezing curves shown in Figs. 6 and 7 are the results of a single trial. Figure 6 shows the curves of freezing depth and rate over time for silt and silty clay. Under the condition of constant cold end temperature, the freezing depth shows a nonlinear increase trend with the freezing duration, and finally approaches the limit value of 150 mm, and the freezing rate shows an exponential decay characteristic with the development of the phase transition zone. When the thermodynamic equilibrium is reached in the soil (after 70 h of freezing), the temperature field gradient distribution and the migration of the freezing front enter the steady state synchronously, showing the fixation of the freezing front in the spatial position. This phenomenon reveals the decisive role of the dynamic equilibrium relationship between the latent heat release rate of phase change and the external heat conduction rate on the evolution of freezing process. In the initial FC stage (0 ~ 10 h), as the temperature of the cold end gradually decreases, the freezing front develops rapidly downward, because the freezing front is equivalent to the freezing temperature isotherm, with the occurrence of supercooling, the latent heat of the pore water phase transition affects the temperature of the surrounding soil, resulting in a sharp fluctuation of the freezing rate, and even the freezing rate is negative, and the freezing front moves upward. The freezing rate fluctuation rate of silt soil fluctuates faster in the early stage of freezing, and the freezing rate fluctuation of silty clay is larger, and the freezing front of silt clay is faster than that of silty clay as a whole, and the final maximum freezing depth is also greater than that of silty clay. In stage 2 SC (10 ~ 25 h), the freezing depth under different soil types continued to increase, and the freezing front continued to migrate downward, but the development rate was obviously different, and the freezing rate of silty clay was more stable than that of silt, fluctuating at 5 mm/h, while the freezing rate of silt decreased from 7 mm/h to 3 mm/h. At the third stage of SS (25 ~ 72 h), the freezing rate of the two soil types gradually stabilized at the end of the inheritance stage 2.

The development trend of the frozen front with silt and silty clay under different inclinations of the upper ceiling can be summarized as follows: as the angle of inclination of the upper ceiling increases, the development trend of the frozen front becomes more complex and unstable. This is because the tilt of the upper ceiling not only maintains a vertical temperature gradient but also introduces significant horizontal temperature differences within the same horizontal soil layer. These horizontal temperature differences lead to an uneven distribution of unfrozen water content within the soil layer, which in turn triggers a soil-water potential gradient that drives the lateral migration of moisture. The dynamic redistribution process of moisture is coupled with the temperature field, ultimately resulting in an unstable characteristic of the development of the frozen front. With the increase of freezing time, the influence of the tilt angle of the upper roof will become smaller and smaller, and the temperature gradient difference of the same soil layer will become smaller and smaller, and the freezing front will gradually migrate downward steadily. The development of the two soil samples in stage 2 is slightly different, in which the freezing front development rate of silty clay will be faster than that of silty soil, and then the freezing rate of the two soil samples will gradually converge when entering stage 3.

Moisture field analysis

From Fig. 8, it can be seen that the distribution map of the moisture content of the two soil samples with height is roughly the same, taking 80 mm from the warm end as the freezing front, the closer to the freezing front, the higher the moisture content of the freezing front, the upper part of the freezing front is greater than the initial moisture content of 30%, and the lower part of the moisture content is less than the initial moisture content, and the overall gradient distribution trend is presented, the maximum moisture content in the soil is at 80 mm, and the soil sample is taken from the lower part of the frost-heave segregation soil, due to the occurrence of water migration and aggregation phenomenon in the soil. As a result, the moisture content here is significantly higher than 40%.

Fig. 8Distribution of moisture content of silt and silty clay after frost heave test.

Distribution of moisture content of silt and silty clay after frost heave test.

Due to the tilting of the upper plate, the frost heave test became a two-dimensional frost heave. Therefore, after the frost heave test was completed, the following method was used to determine the moisture content at the corresponding positions: quickly collect frozen soil samples from three longitudinal positions along the bottom of the upper plate, from the left side of the soil sample (x = 0 m) to the right side (x = 0.1 m, x = 0.2 m). Then, divide the small soil columns taken from the three locations into five layers and weigh them immediately (wet weight). After placing the soil samples in an oven until constant weight, weigh them again (dry weight), from which the total moisture content at each sampling point is calculated. The moisture content is the ratio of the sum of ice and unfrozen water to the weight of dry soil. Figure 8 shows that when the inclination angle of the roof is 0°, the moisture content of the frozen edge area of silt and silty clay is evenly distributed, which is about 40%. When the roof is tilted, as shown in Figs. 9 and 10, the moisture content in the frozen edge area is no longer evenly distributed, and the larger the inclination angle of the roof plate, the more uneven the distribution is, resulting in a larger moisture content difference, with a maximum difference of about 7%. Under the same temperature gradient (size and direction), the moisture content of the freezing margin zone of silt is high in the middle and low on the left and right sides, while the moisture content of silty clay gradually increases to the right along the frost-heave segregation layer. At an angle of 0°, this conclusion does not apply because the moisture content at the freezing front is equal.

The analysis shows that the silt contains more coarse particles and good permeability, so the moisture content will have obvious gradient changes, and the maximum moisture content at x = 0.1 m may be due to the redistribution of water and frost heaving there, which affects the further migration of water to the right end in the horizontal direction. However, the particles of silty clay are finer and the permeability is worse than that of silt, resulting in the water migration in the lower part of the freezing front mainly in the vertical direction, and the phenomenon of in-situ frost heave often occurs in the horizontal direction, so the moisture content gradient changes will show the law of moisture content gradient change from x = 0 m to x = 0.2 m.

The analysis indicates that during the two-dimensional freezing process, moisture from the unfrozen area accumulates towards the freezing front. Among them, the moisture content at the freezing front of silt increases from 30% to 50%, while that of silty clay increases to 45%. Observing the distribution of total mass moisture content under different soil types, it can be seen that there is a difference in moisture redistribution between silt and silty clay. The freezing front moisture content of silt is higher, while that of silty clay is lower. Furthermore, more unfrozen water from the unfrozen area beneath the silt migrates towards the frozen area, resulting in larger corresponding frost heave. After the freezing test, the sampling found that the ice lens formed a continuous ice layer in the silt, with a thickness of 2 ~ 4 mm. The silty clay ice layer is intermittently distributed, with a maximum thickness of 3 mm, which further supports the above view of the redistribution of moisture content under two-dimensional frost heave.

Displacement field analysis

Figures 11 and 12 show the change process of frost heave on the surface of silt soil and silty clay test specimens under different inclination angles, and the test data show that the freezing process presents a typical phase change latent heat and frost heave co-evolution characteristics, and the soil forms a freezing front propulsion zone from top to bottom, which simultaneously induces the accumulation of frost heave displacement. Two-dimensional hydrothermal coupling Frost heave model

Model overview

Based on Fourier’s law of heat conduction and the law of energy conservation, a two-dimensional temperature control equation applicable to slope permafrost was derived, with soil water potential acting as the driving force for moisture migration, resulting in the establishment of a two-dimensional moisture control equation. At the same time, considering the impact of temperature and moisture on the stress field, the criteria for ice crystal formation during two-dimensional freezing were derived, and a two-dimensional frost heave model incorporating hydro-thermal coupling effects was established. Combining Konrad & Shen’s28 description of the evolution path of ice crystals within a two-dimensional freezing system, this study refers to the research models of Zhou Yang32 and Li Ting33 to construct a theoretical framework for the coupling of the three fields of hydrothermal dynamics during the phase change process of slope permafrost; it also provides a quantitative description tool for the formation and development of ice crystals during the two-dimensional freezing process. A directional vector is introduced here: it quantitatively describes the anisotropy of ice lens growth through the decomposition of a mathematical vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d{\varepsilon _n}_{\tau }$$\end{document}, which is lacking in previous models. Additionally, by coupling with the direction of heat flow, the main axis of ice lens growth is bound to the main direction of heat conduction. Ultimately, this criterion provides a universal, physics-based theoretical framework for explaining the spatial heterogeneity distribution of ice lenses in sloped environments (such as wedge-shaped ice layers and asymmetric frost heaving), instead of merely relying on numerical fitting or empirical adjustments.

After the end of frost heave, the characteristics of frost heave at different locations are described based on the overall distribution changes of the soil moisture content. The calculated values at different locations are consistent with the moisture redistribution trend of the calculated values of 1D freezing, but there are differences from the measured value distribution. At x = 0 m, the moisture content of the soil under two-dimensional freezing is less than the one-dimensional freezing value, the freezing front is higher, and the maximum moisture content at the freezing front is smaller, and the difference in moisture content in the unfrozen area is large, indicating that the water migrates upward and to the right. At x = 0.1 m, except for the moisture content above the soil column height of about 130 mm and the one-dimensional freezing value, the moisture content in the other positions is relatively low, and the freezing front is higher, and the difference in moisture content in the unfrozen area is reduced. At x = 0.2 m, the overall moisture content under two-dimensional freezing is significantly higher than that of one-dimensional freezing, but the freezing front is lower and the moisture content in the unfrozen area is smaller. Under one-dimensional freezing, with the increase of the height of the soil column, the water migrating from the unfrozen area to the frozen area increases, the freezing front increases, and the maximum moisture content also increases. Under two-dimensional freezing, water migrates from the unfrozen area to the frozen area and horizontally to the right. As shown in Fig. 16, the measured values and simulated values of moisture content in the lower two layers gradually increase, but the model did not simulate the changes in moisture content of the unfrozen zone in the lower part, mainly because its lower boundary conditions failed to adequately reflect the shrinkage effect of the unfrozen soil after moisture migration. Additionally, the measured moisture content in the top layer is less than the calculated value, which may be due to: (a) the impedance factor used not fully simulating the hindrance effect of ice lenses on moisture migration; (b) the upper plate of the indoor experiment not tightly bonding with the soil sample, resulting in lower heat transfer efficiency than what the model would achieve under ideal conditions; (c) the layered sampling method may have introduced deviations in the calculated moisture content due to the inclusion of unevenly distributed ice crystals.

In Fig. 19, the freezing front at the initial freezing stage is an inclined straight line, and the angle is basically the same as the inclination angle of the upper roof plate, and the development of the freezing front of the two soils during the period of 0 ~ 10 h is basically the same, and the freezing front gradually migrates downward with the increase of freezing time. When the silt was frozen for 10 h, there were two bulges on the freezing front, and the formation rate of the freezing front on the right side was slightly smaller than that on the left. When freezing for 30 h, the middle of the freezing front oscillates up and down, indicating that the ice layer formed at this location is thicker than other locations, and water accumulates here. During 10 ~ 30 h period, the freezing front developed and gathered on the left side, and the right side of the freezing front continued to decline, and the left position had obvious bulges and rebound, indicating that the thickness of the ice layer on the left side was greater than that on the right side. After the two soils reached freezing stability (72 h), the freezing front basically showed a slightly inclined straight line.

In general. The development of the freezing front exhibits irregularity under two-dimensional freezing conditions. The development rate on the right side of the frozen silt front is significantly lower than that on the left side, and the inclination of the upper temperature control ceiling has a minor effect on the morphology distribution of the bottom layer’s separated ice, but it significantly impacts the distribution of separated ice in the middle section; during the freezing process of silty clay, a rebounding phenomenon occurs at the freezing front, with the development rate in the middle of the freezing front being higher than that on both sides. and it can be seen that the increase of the inclination angle of the upper roof will lead to the slower stable development of the freezing front, and its spatial distribution pattern is more complex.

Conclusion

We proposed an innovative guideline for the growth of two-dimensional ice lens bodies, breaking the limitations of traditional one-dimensional models, successfully simulating the asymmetric distribution characteristics of segregated ice under sloped terrain (where there are significant differences in the development rates of the freezing fronts in silty soil and silty clay; and the morphology of the segregated ice is influenced by the coupling of freezing rates and moisture migration: a continuous ice layer in silty soil and predominantly discontinuous lens bodies in silty clay).We discovered that the dominant path of moisture migration is not only vertical but also has a significant horizontal component. Moisture migrates and accumulates towards the relatively warmer side (simulating the sunny slope), leading to a maximum difference of 7% in water content, which is the inherent reason for uneven frost heave and lateral deformation in the roadbed.The change trend of frost heave displacement is divided into three parts according to the time course evolution: frost shrinkage, frost heave rapid development and stability stage; The frost heave displacement on the upper surface of the two-dimensional frozen soil sample is non-uniform, and the displacement of the middle position of the middle of the soil at the same height is greater than that of the two sides. This study clearly reveals the uneven frost heave patterns controlled by a two-dimensional thermal gradient at the laboratory scale (200 mm). However, the scale effect of actual engineering on frost heave needs to be further validated with long-term monitoring data from the field to calibrate the model, thus quantifying the conversion relationship from laboratory scale to field scale.
