Timing of immune checkpoint inhibitors infusion and prognosis in recurrent/metastatic nasopharyngeal carcinoma: a single-center, retrospective study
Authors: Lin Zhang, Xia Gan, Zhengzheng Yu, Wenji Xie, Xiaowen Sun, Yue Li, Jianing Fang, Yating Liu, Zhongjie Liu, Yaning Tang, Can Zhou, Zhuolong Xie, Shuyu Ouyang, Jing Zhang, Jingyu Wang, Hua Li, Feng Liu, Pan Chen, Yanxian Li, Hui Wang, Huai Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe timing of immunotherapy administration has been linked to variations in clinical outcome across multiple cancers, but its effect on clinical outcome in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) remains unstudied.MethodsIn this single-center retrospective cohort study, patients with recurrent/metastatic (R/M) NPC who received first-line anti-PD-1-based immunochemotherapy (June 2019–December 2024) were included. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS) was the secondary endpoint. Data regarding the time of day of administration (ToDA) during the initial four cycles were collected for each patient. Survival was estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with log-rank tests. Multivariable Cox models were adjusted for prespecified covariates. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed between patients from different ToDA groups. The ToDA cutoff was derived from the data without a prespecified hypothesis, using a data-driven approach.ResultsAmong the 334 screened patients, 312 met the eligibility criteria. The median follow-up was 24.9 months. The median PFS was 25.0 months, and the mOS was not reached. A data-driven optimal ToDA cutoff of 12:00 was identified. Patients who received immunotherapy after 12:00 had significantly longer PFS (31.2 vs. 18.3 months, p = 0.006) and OS (both not reached, p = 0.011). After PSM at a 1:2 ratio, PFS (36.1 vs. 18.3 months, p = 0.003) and OS (both not reached, p = 0.018) were still significantly improved in patients who received immunotherapy after 12:00. Multivariate analysis demonstrated that a ToDA after 12:00 was independently correlated with improved PFS (HR=0.63; 95% CI: 0.43-0.92; p = 0.017) and OS (HR=0.48; 95% CI: 0.27–0.84; p = 0.010).ConclusionsThese hypothesis-generating findings suggest that, in R/M NPC patients treated with first-line immunochemotherapy, afternoon administration during the initial four cycles may be associated with improved PFS and OS. However, because the ToDA threshold was selected post hoc, the results should be interpreted with caution. Randomized clinical trials are warranted to validate these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version of this article (https://doi.org/10.1007/s00262-026-04472-2) contains supplementary material, which is available to authorized users.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a head and neck malignancy closely associated with Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) infection in endemic areas. GLOBOCAN 2022 estimated 120,416 new cases and 73,476 deaths from NPC worldwide, and NPC ranks 23rd in incidence and 21st in cancer mortality [1]. Despite the relatively low global incidence, > 70% of new NPC cases occur in East and Southeast Asia[2], with the highest incidence in China [1]. Early-stage disease is highly curable, yet some patients present with distant metastasis at diagnosis or develop recurrence/metastasis (R/M) after definitive therapy. For R/M NPC, combined treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) antibodies, and chemotherapy has shown high efficacy in pivotal trials[3–5], with median progression-free survival (PFS) ranging from 9.7 to 21.4 months, reshaping first-line treatment.

Human circadian rhythms arise from a complex molecular clock that is coordinated by the suprachiasmatic nucleus, which generates ~24-hour cycles that regulate sleep–wake patterns, core temperature, and feeding behaviors [6, 7]. Emerging evidence indicates that antitumor immunity is tightly regulated by intrinsic circadian programs. Dendritic cells play a pacemaker-like role: Lymph node trafficking and CD80 expression by dendritic cells oscillate diurnally under direct transcriptional control of BMAL1, shaping the rhythmic activation and proliferation of CD8⁺ T cells [8]. In parallel, PD-1 expression on tumor-associated macrophages exhibits circadian oscillation that is regulated by the clock component DEC2 via NF-κB signaling inhibition [9]. Circadian control of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters further affects pharmacokinetics and therapeutic exposure [10].

Chronotherapy, the alignment of treatment with circadian rhythms, was proposed decades ago to enhance efficacy and reduce toxicity [7, 11]. In oncology, several retrospective studies in lung cancer, melanoma, and renal cell carcinoma have suggested that survival is longer with morning ICI infusions than with afternoon dosing [12–16]. Conversely, Guo et al. recently reported that among non-small cell lung cancer patients receiving ICI-based combinations, post-12:00 infusions were associated with longer PFS (16.5 vs. 9.8 months) [17]. These inconsistent findings highlight the complexity of ICI chronotherapy, which is potentially influenced by tumor type, regimen and drug combinations, and host circadian state. In NPC, a virus-related cancer with a distinctive immune microenvironment, related evidence remains lacking. Defining an optimal ICI dosing window in patients with R/M NPC could offer a low-cost, easily implementable optimization strategy.

The initial treatment phase may be pivotal for efficacy [17]. Classic pharmacokinetics indicate that ~five half-lives are required to reach steady state [18]. Anti-PD-1 antibodies such as pembrolizumab have elimination half-lives of ~2–3 weeks; therefore, reaching a steady state typically requires ~15–20 weeks [15, 19–21]. Before a steady state is reached (in approximately the first four cycles), the peak level, distribution volume, and clearance have not stabilized, and circadian differences in dosing time may modulate these parameters and downstream biology [22, 23]. We therefore aimed to systematically assess the relationship between ICI dosing time during the first four cycles and outcomes among patients with R/M NPC. We hypothesized that, owing to diurnal fluctuations in host immunity, administering ICIs within specific time windows could enhance antitumor responses and improve survival.

Materials and methods

Study objective, design, and ethics

This is a hypothesis-generating study, aims to investigate the clinical association between dosing time during the first four cycles of first-line anti-PD-1 immunotherapy and outcomes in R/M NPC patients. We performed a single-center retrospective cohort study including eligible patients treated at Hunan Cancer Hospital from June 2019 to December 2024. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and was approved by the relevant Institutional Ethics Committee (Expedited Review No. 219 in 2025). The study conformed to the STROBE guidelines [24].

Patient selection

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) age ≥18 years; (ii) histologically confirmed unresectable locally recurrent or metastatic NPC with at least one measurable lesion per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) v1.1; (iii) receipt of at least one cycle of first-line ICI plus chemotherapy; (iv) an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–2; and (v) traceable electronic medical records providing precise ICI infusion start times for the first four cycles, complete radiologic assessments, and survival follow-up. The exclusion criteria were missing ICI infusion time during the first four cycles, missing clinical data, locally recurrent disease that could be treated with curative re-irradiation or surgery, or daily systemic glucocorticoid use ≥ 10 mg (prednisone equivalent) for any reason.

Data collection and assessment

The prespecified baseline variables included ECOG performance status, disease status, metastatic sites, EBV DNA level, infusion start times for the first four ICI administrations, and a history of radiotherapy after recurrence/metastasis. Antitumor efficacy was routinely assessed using MRI of the head and neck region and CT of other body regions every 6 weeks for the first 6 months, every 9 weeks for the next 6 months, and every 12 weeks thereafter. Additional skeletal imaging (e.g., bone scan or PET/CT) was performed when clinically indicated. Imaging assessments were categorized as scheduled (performed within a one-week window of the planned interval), early (performed more than one week earlier than planned due to clinical indications), or delayed (performed more than one week later than planned). Follow-up was censored on June 13, 2025. In total, 2275 imaging assessments were performed before disease progression or death. In the Before 12:00 group, 10.0% of the assessments were early, 53.3% were scheduled, and 36.7% were delayed. In the After 12:00 group, the corresponding proportions were 5.6%, 61.9%, and 32.5%, respectively. Tumor response was categorized per RECIST v1.1 as complete response, partial response, stable disease, or progressive disease [25]. Two clinicians independently double-checked the data, with verification by the treating physician to ensure consistency and accuracy.

Immunotherapy and chemotherapy

All patients received combined ICI therapy and chemotherapy. The regimen consisted of tislelizumab (200 mg on Day 1) [5], camrelizumab (200 mg on Day 1) [3], or toripalimab (240 mg on Day 1) [4] in combination with gemcitabine (1000 mg/m2 on Days 1 and 8) and cisplatin (80 mg/m2 administered either as a single dose on Day 1 or divided over Days 1–3). This regimen was administered intravenously every 3 weeks for four to six cycles. Afterward, patients with nonprogressive disease continued receiving anti-PD-1 monotherapy (administered on Day 1 of each cycle) every 3 weeks until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, or patient refusal of further treatment.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was PFS, defined as the time from the first ICI administration to radiographically confirmed progression or death from any cause. Patients who were alive without progression at last contact were censored. The secondary endpoint was overall survival (OS), defined as the time from the first ICI administration to death from any cause; survivors were censored at the last follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are summarized as counts and percentages; continuous variables are presented as the mean ± SD or median (IQR) as appropriate. The median follow-up was estimated by the reverse Kaplan–Meier method. To evaluate the time of day of administration (ToDA), we examined hourly cutoffs from 11:00 to 18:00. On the basis of the infusion start time across the first four cycles, patients whose administration times all occurred before a given cutoff were assigned to the “Before” group; those with at least one administration after the cutoff were assigned to the “After” group, which is consistent with the methods of prior studies [19, 26]. Most existing studies have reported that compared with afternoon administration, morning administration of immunotherapy can significantly prolong patients’ PFS and OS. On this basis, we hypothesized that the same trend exists in R/M NPC-that is, that morning administration can significantly improve patient prognosis. To maximize the difference in prognosis between the two groups, we defined patients who received all of the first four cycles in the morning as the “Before” group. Group differences were tested with the χ2 test (or Fisher’s exact test) for nominal variables and the Cochran‒Armitage trend test for ordinal variables (ECOG performance status). PFS and OS were estimated using Kaplan‒Meier curves and compared with log-rank tests [15]. Cox models were used to assess the associations between ToDA cutoff values and both PFS and OS [27], adjusting for sex, age, ECOG performance status, disease status, baseline EBV DNA level, a history of radiotherapy, liver metastasis, lung metastasis, and bone metastasis. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model with the same set of covariates. Propensity score matching (PSM) was then performed using nearest-neighbor matching at a 1:2 ratio (without replacement, caliper = 0.2 × standard deviation of the propensity score). A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. SPSS v26.0 and R v4.4.2 were used for statistical analyses [28].

Results

Overall, 29 patients received fewer than four cycles of immunotherapy. In the Before 12:00 group, 7 patients (11.7%) received fewer than four cycles, including 1 patient (1.7%) who received fewer than four cycles because of severe adverse events; 3 patients (5.0%) because of disease progression; and 3 patients (5.0%) for other reasons, including COVID-19-related interruption and the patient’s own decision. In the After 12:00 group, 22 patients (8.7%) received fewer than four cycles, including 8 patients (3.2%) who received fewer than four cycles because of severe adverse events; 8 patients (3.2%) because of disease progression; 2 patients (0.8%) because of poor general condition after treatment; and 4 patients (1.6%) for other reasons, such as the patient’s own decision.

We plotted the distribution of each patient’s latest infusion start time across the first four cycles (Fig. S1), which formed the basis for classifying patients relative to prespecified ToDA cutoffs. Infusions clustered in two windows (10:00 to 13:00 and 16:00 to 18:00), with only sporadic administration before 10:00 and after 20:00. The median follow-up for this study was 24.9 months (95% CI: 22.1–27.6). The median PFS for the overall population was 25.0 months (95% CI: 19.1–31.0). OS data are currently immature at the data cutoff (not reached).

Identification of a daily ToDA cutoff for discriminating survival

Across cutoff times, the HRs for PFS ranged from 0.57–0.89 (unadjusted) and 0.55–0.90 (adjusted for covariates). Statistical significance was observed at the 11:00, 12:00, and 16:00 cutoff times, indicating that patients who received infusions after these times had significantly longer PFS than those who received infusions before.

Similarly, the HRs for OS ranged from 0.42–1.38 (unadjusted) and 0.34–2.24 (adjusted for covariates), with statistical significance at 11:00 and 12:00, respectively, further supporting a potential OS benefit with afternoon administration.

Notably, the lowest HRs for both PFS and OS occurred at the 11:00 cutoff time (PFS: unadjusted HR = 0.57, adjusted HR = 0.55; OS: unadjusted HR = 0.42, adjusted HR = 0.34). However, using 11:00 as the cutoff time resulted in substantial group imbalance (32 “Before” vs. 280 “After”), which could compromise statistical power and robustness. To enhance reliability, clinical interpretability, and comparability between groups, we selected 12:00, the cutoff time with the second-lowest HRs, for subsequent analyses.

Efficacy of immunochemotherapy administered before vs. after 12:00

Compared with the “Before 12:00” group, the “After 12:00” group had a higher rate of radiotherapy; the other baseline features were equivalent (Table 1). The median PFS was 18.3 months (95% CI: 11.4–25.1) in the “Before 12:00” group versus 31.2 months (95% CI: 23.7–38.6) in the “After 12:00” group (p = 0.006; Fig. 3A). To further explore the impact of infusion timing, we performed an additional analysis to compare patients whose first four cycles of immunotherapy were all administered before 12:00 (n = 60) versus all administered after 12:00 (n = 43). In this analysis, compared with the Before 12:00 group, the After 12:00 group continued to exhibit significantly longer PFS (34.2 months vs. 18.3 months, p = 0.033; Fig. S2A), whereas no significant difference was observed for OS (p = 0.463; Fig. S2B).

To evaluate whether the main findings were influenced by data from patients who received fewer than four cycles of ICI therapy, we conducted a sensitivity analysis in which the study population was restricted to those who completed at least four cycles. In this subgroup, administration after 12:00 significantly improved PFS (32.0 months vs. 19.2 months, p = 0.006; Fig. S3A) and OS (both NR, p = 0.009; Fig. S3B), which is consistent with the trend observed in the primary analysis.

Multivariable and subgroup analyses

On univariable analysis, the ToDA was associated with both PFS and OS; multivariable Cox models confirmed ToDA as an independent prognostic factor for PFS (HR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.43–0.92, p = 0.017; Fig. S4A) and OS (HR=0.48, 95% CI: 0.27–0.84, p = 0.010; Fig. S4B). Baseline plasma EBV DNA level and radiotherapy were also independent prognostic factors for PFS and OS, and patients with a lower EBV DNA level or who received radiotherapy had better PFS and OS. Furthermore, to formally evaluate the potential interaction between radiotherapy and ToDA, we added a “radiotherapy × ToDA” interaction term to the multivariate Cox regression model that included all the covariates. The interaction effect was not significant for PFS (p = 0.650; HR = 0.81; 95% CI: 0.32–2.04) or OS (p = 0.569; HR = 0.67; 95% CI: 0.17–2.67), indicating that radiotherapy did not significantly modify the effect of ToDA on survival.

Discussion

This study of 312 patients with R/M NPC receiving first-line immunochemotherapy revealed, for the first time in this disease setting, that ToDA during the initial four cycles may be significantly associated with long-term treatment outcomes. Our study revealed that post-12:00 dosing yielded significantly better PFS and OS than pre-12:00 dosing. In the unmatched cohort, the median PFS was 31.2 months versus 18.3 months for post-12:00 dosing vs. pre-12:00 dosing. After PSM was performed to account for known prognostic factors, median PFS was still significantly different between the two groups (36.1 versus 18.3 months). These findings indicate that the effect of the ToDA was not attributable to baseline differences. Multivariable models corroborated ToDA as an independent predictor of PFS and OS, and analyses in prespecified subgroups consistently favored afternoon dosing. Furthermore, although radiotherapy after R/M NPC was an important prognostic factor, no significant interaction between radiotherapy and ToDA was observed for either PFS or OS. Therefore, the analyses provided no clear evidence that radiotherapy status modified the association between ToDA and survival in this cohort, which is generally consistent with the results of the primary analysis. The median PFS in this cohort was 25.0 months, which is comparable to the 21.4 months reported for the immunochemotherapy group in the randomized, double-blind, phase 3 JUPITER-02 trial [4].

Circadian rhythms regulate key components of both innate and adaptive immunity, including immune effector functions, cell trafficking, antigen presentation by dendritic cells, and T-cell activation [8, 29, 30]. In cancer transcriptomes, clock genes are correlated with immune infiltration and proliferation [31, 32]. Preclinical studies further revealed that the efficacy of ICIs varies in a time-dependent manner [33, 34].

Several retrospective studies have reported that the ToDA of ICIs, either alone or in combination with chemotherapy, is associated with treatment outcomes across multiple cancer types [12–17].

In NPC, the core clock gene BMAL1 exhibits rhythmic expression. BMAL1 overexpression suppresses tumor cell proliferation, whereas BMAL1 knockdown promotes proliferation; these findings are further supported by findings in xenograft models [35]. Liu et al. demonstrated that TIMELESS, a mammalian homolog of a Drosophila circadian rhythm gene, was upregulated in NPC cells [36]. Furthermore, TIMELESS overexpression was significantly correlated with cisplatin resistance and poor survival outcomes. The BMAL1/CLOCK complex modulates dendritic cell metabolism and antigen presentation via HIF-1α and related pathways [37]; disruption of the core clock impairs CD8+ T-cell function and promotes an immunosuppressive microenvironment [38]. A phase II randomized controlled trial revealed a higher CD4+/CD8+ T-cell ratio in patients receiving cisplatin by chronomodulated infusion [39]. These data support a biologically plausible chronotherapeutic rationale in NPC.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to report that the ToDA of chemoimmunotherapy is significantly correlated with both PFS and OS in NPC. Patients who received treatment after 12:00 exhibited superior long-term survival. Notably, our results differed from the findings of several prior studies on different cancer types. A meta-analysis of 13 studies across melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and renal cell carcinoma reported better survival with morning ICI dosing [35]. This inconsistency is likely attributable to several factors, including the choice of cutoff time, the use of different treatment combinations, and variations in tumor biology. Several studies in which 16:30 was used as the cutoff time reported that patients who received fewer than 20% of ICI doses after 16:30 had prolonged survival [13, 40]. In our study, we observed that the HRs for both PFS and OS were lowest at approximately 11:00 and 12:00. After considering reliability, clinical interpretability, and comparability between groups, we selected 12:00 as the optimal cutoff time for our analysis. Second, most studies that support morning administration were based on ICI monotherapy [13, 35], whereas all patients in our cohort received chemoimmunotherapy. Notably, previous studies have indicated that administration of platinum-based chemotherapy in the afternoon is associated with a more favorable immune status [39] and improved treatment efficacy [41]. Guo et al. demonstrated that NSCLC patients who received chemoimmunotherapy after 12:00 PM had significantly prolonged PFS [17], a result that aligns with our findings. Furthermore, we propose that the viral etiology of NPC may have contributed to these differences in results. Circadian rhythms influence viral replication, and viruses can, in turn, reprogram host clock machinery [42]. HBV HBx disrupts circadian gene transcription [43]; in chronic HCV, the upregulation of miR-10a expression suppresses Bmal1 expression via RORα inhibition [44]. Moreover, influenza A and simian immunodeficiency virus perturb host circadian systems [45–47]; HIV alters circadian rhythms in mice and humans, resetting the phase and reducing activity amplitude [48–50]. Additionally, among herpesviruses, Bmal1 knockout models exhibit increased replication of MuHV-4 and HSV-1, but in wild-type animals, infection is strongly time dependent, with higher viral loads observed in response to inoculation at rest-phase onset [51]. Herpesvirus infection can sharply increase Bmal1 expression, and infection at specific circadian phases disrupts oscillations and augments replication, suggesting viral manipulation of the molecular clock [51]. The HSV protein ICP0 interacts with the CLOCK:BMAL1 complex; inhibiting CLOCK/BMAL1 dimerization reduces HSV replication, indicating direct crosstalk between herpesviruses and circadian pathways [52–54]. Given that EBV is a member of the Herpesviridae family, we hypothesize that EBV reprograms local circadian regulation within the NPC microenvironment, shifting immune functional phases such that the morning is relatively immunosuppressive while the afternoon provides a “functional window,” thereby enhancing responses to ICI therapy when the ICI is administered later in the day. Additional studies are needed in the future.

This study has several limitations. First, its single-center, retrospective design may have introduced selection bias and unmeasured confounders. Second, ToDA threshold was selected post hoc. Third, while clock time served as a pragmatic surrogate for circadian phase in this retrospective analysis, we acknowledge that circadian biology is highly individualized and that clock time alone cannot establish causality. Data on individual-level circadian biomarkers (e.g., core body temperature rhythms, peripheral clock gene expression, or melatonin profiles) were not available. Thus, our study is hypothesis-generating in nature. The results are not practice-changing and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Although our findings do not support a change in clinical practice, they suggest that the morning administration of immunotherapy, which is recommended by most studies, may not be universally suitable for patients with R/M NPC. Future prospective studies that incorporate direct measures of circadian phase are needed to precisely define the optimal individualized dosing window for immunotherapy. Nevertheless, shifting ICI administration from morning to afternoon represents a nearly zero-cost and readily implementable method of optimization, which is particularly relevant in regions with a high burden of NPC and uneven availability of health care resources. Our findings should be validated in a well-designed phase III randomized controlled trial prior to widespread implementation in clinical practice.

Conclusions

This study provides evidence that the time of ICI administration may be associated with survival outcomes in patients with R/M NPC, with dosing after 12:00 potentially correlated with improved PFS and OS. As this is a hypothesis‑generating study, the results are not practice‑changing and should be interpreted with caution. Future prospective studies to validate these findings and to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms are warranted.
