Reading the ECG in Time
Authors: Samid Ahmed, Konstantinos N. Aronis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

An electrocardiogram (ECG) read in isolation can mislead; read in series, it becomes a physiologic instrument rather than a confirmatory document. This is why every medical student is taught, before acting on an acute tracing, to ask for the prior. The case reported by Chu et al1 in this issue is a reminder of how much can be missed when a single tracing is read alone, and how much emerges when those tracings are interpreted across time. This is a lesson proved nearly 4 decades ago, showing across more than 5,000 patients that the availability of a prior tracing meaningfully refined diagnostic accuracy and triage decisions in acute chest pain.2 The case of Chu et al1 provides this lesson through 2 ECG findings that, though operating on different physiologic timescales, plausibly share a common substrate: Ta-wave pseudo-elevation, which vanishes the moment the atrial ectopic focus falls silent, and the Crochetage sign, which trails loading conditions and resolves with diuresis.

Chu et al1 describe a 25-year-old woman with uncorrected single-ventricle anatomy whose postcatheterization ECG demonstrated inferior Q waves and ST-segment elevations together with a notched R-wave in the inferior leads. The patient was prepared for an angiogram due to concerns of an evolving infarct, before a repeat ECG obtained 30 minutes later showed normalization of the ST-segments and disappearance of the apparent Q waves; the notch in the R-wave persisted. With these new data, the index ECG was reinterpreted as a low atrial ectopic rhythm with a prominent atrial repolarization (Ta) wave inscribed on the ST-segment, and the persistent notch was recognized as the Crochetage sign. An outpatient tracing following diuresis showed sinus rhythm and resolution of the Crochetage notch. Three serial ECGs; 1 patient; 2 findings; asymmetric resolution.

The Ta wave is the surface ECG manifestation of atrial repolarization and can be routinely overlooked in emergency settings. It is ordinarily invisible on the standard 12-lead ECG: its amplitude is low (typically 100-200 μV), its duration is long (mean 323 ± 56 ms), and its polarity is opposite to that of the preceding P wave, so that in sinus rhythm it is buried within the QRS complex and the ST-segment.3 The conditions in which Ta becomes visible are as follows: high-grade atrioventricular block, long QT syndromes, exercise-induced tachycardia, and as in this case, low atrial ectopic rhythms.4 In one of the earliest systematic descriptions, Ta waves were described in nearly half of patients with complete heart block, where the dissociation separates atrial repolarization from the QRST, rendering it visible on the surface ECG.5 Importantly, exaggerated Ta waves can result in false-positive exercise stress tests, mimicking ST-segment depression at peak heart rate.6 In low atrial ectopic rhythms, depolarization originates inferiorly and proceeds superiorly, generating an inverted P-wave in the inferior leads. Because the Ta is opposite in polarity to its preceding P, it is positive in those leads. Combined with a shortened or absent PR interval, which causes the QRS to arrive before atrial repolarization is complete, the Ta is pushed forward onto the ST-segment, where it produces apparent elevation.4,7 The apparent elevation reflects atrial repolarization inscribed on the ST-segment, not a ventricular current of injury. Ta visibility is rhythm-dependent and resolves on the timescale of a single beat: the moment the ectopic focus falls silent, the pseudo-elevation vanishes.

The Crochetage sign is a notch at the peak of the R-wave in the inferior leads and has a different physiologic anchor. First systematically characterized in 1996, it was identified in 73% of patients with secundum atrial septal defect compared with 7% of controls and correlates with shunt size; in combination with incomplete right bundle branch block, it becomes a highly specific ECG marker for the secundum atrial septal defect.8 Its early disappearance in approximately one-third of patients after surgical repair, despite persistence of right bundle branch block, suggested that the sign reflects right-sided hemodynamics rather than fixed conduction disease alone. Subsequent observations have refined this interpretation. The presence of the Crochetage sign after transcatheter atrial septal defect closure has been independently associated with late atrial arrhythmia occurrence, tying an ostensibly right ventricular ECG marker to atrial electrical remodeling produced by chronic right-sided volume overload.9 Furthermore, the Crochetage sign can be abolished by selective His-bundle pacing, implicating the conduction system rather than the ventricular myocardium as mechanistically associated with the sign, and indicating that the sign is modifiable by the pattern of ventricular activation.10 Taken together, the Crochetage sign can be reversed or modified: 1) slowly, through changes in right ventricular loading; and 2) instantly, through changes in the ventricular activation pattern. In the paper presented here, the Crochetage sign persisted on the 30-minute repeat ECG and resolved only at outpatient follow-up after diuresis. Thus, the Crochetage sign tracked loading conditions that resolved in parallel with hemodynamic improvement.

What makes the case uniquely instructive is that a single patient supplies the controlled comparison usually impossible at the bedside: each tracing varies one factor while holding the other fixed. The 30-minute repeat ECG holds loading constant while rhythm changes; the outpatient ECG holds rhythm constant while loading changes. The 2 manipulations dissociate the findings cleanly. Ta visibility tracks rhythm and changes instantaneously; Crochetage tracks loading and changes in parallel with hemodynamic improvement. The 2 plausibly share a substrate; chronic right-heart overload promotes atrial stretch, a recognized arrhythmogenic substrate for ectopic foci, and simultaneously produces the right ventricular pressure-volume loading and conduction-system remodeling that generate the notch.7,9 The same hemodynamic substrate amplifies both, but each has its own primary driver and its own timescale of resolution; that independence, despite a shared amplifier, is what makes the case physiologically instructive rather than merely a lesson in pattern recognition. A simple framework clarifies the relationship (Figure 1). Place rhythm (sinus vs low atrial ectopic) on one axis and right-sided loading (normal vs elevated) on the other. Four cells result. Sinus rhythm with normal loading gives the textbook normal inferior-lead tracing. Sinus rhythm with elevated loading hides Ta within the QRS but exposes the Crochetage notch: the patient of Chu et al1 at 30 minutes. Ectopic rhythm with normal loading unmasks Ta and produces pseudo-elevation without a notch: the isolated atrial repolarization signal, separated from any loading effect, and the one cell not represented in this patient. Ectopic rhythm with elevated loading produces the full pseudo-infarct pattern: inverted inferior P-waves mistaken for Q-waves, Ta-wave pseudo-elevation, and the Crochetage notch together: the patient of Chu et al1 at presentation. Three practical implications follow. First, serial ECGs in ambiguous presentations function as physiologic instruments, not merely as confirmatory documentation. A repeat tracing obtained minutes after the index ECG, or comparison with a prior asymptomatic tracing where one exists, costs little and can avert invasive workup in patients for whom procedural risk is meaningful. In a young woman with a large right-to-left intracardiac shunt, the risk of coronary angiography is not trivial; the decision to defer pending a second tracing was, on this account, the highest-value diagnostic maneuver in the case. Second, that value runs in both directions. Just as resolution of the ST-segment elevation excludes type 1 ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), persistence carries its own weight: once sinus rhythm is restored, residual elevation can no longer be ascribed to atrial repolarization, and with the rhythm-dependent contribution removed, the probability of a genuine ventricular cause, ischemic or otherwise, rises accordingly. The repeat tracing thus serves to escalate as readily as to reassure and should be read with that symmetry in mind. Third, this approach is especially valuable in adult congenital heart disease, where the 4-cell framework above sits beneath additional layered substrates that can each mimic ischemia.

The broader lesson extends well beyond this case. An ECG is most informative when it is read as 1 frame in a sequence rather than a fixed image, and never more so than in adult congenital heart disease, where baseline abnormalities are the rule and a single tracing rarely tells the whole story. In this population, layered substrates of abnormal looping, surgical scar, displaced conduction tissue, and chronic pressure or volume overload each write their own signature onto the tracing, and several can mimic ischemia. Pattern recognition applied to 1 ECG is therefore unreliable. A second tracing, or a prior one, reintroduces the dimension that a single image discards, which is time. Asking for the prior, or for the next, remains among the simplest and highest yield maneuvers in electrocardiography. It costs little, requires no additional technology, and in the right patient can avert an invasive procedure or, just as importantly, prompt one.
