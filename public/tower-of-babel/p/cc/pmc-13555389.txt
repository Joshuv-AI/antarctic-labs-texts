Does Fixation of Middle Lobe/Lingula During Right Upper Lobectomy/Left Apical Trisegmentectomy Reduce Postoperative Torsion/Atelectasis?
Authors: Devanish N. Kamtam, Douglas Z. Liou, Mark F. Berry, Satya Malla, Jake J. Kim, Natalie S. Lui, Irmina A. Elliott, Brandon A. Guenthart, Leah M. Backhus, Joseph B. Shrager
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrank torsion, or lesser atelectasis, of the right middle lobe/lingula may occur after right upper lobectomy/left apical trisegmentectomy. Although some surgeons fix/pex the right middle lobe/lingula to the lower lobe, the necessity for this is unclear.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed patients who underwent right upper lobectomy/left apical trisegmentectomy at our institution (2008-2023). We compared patients who underwent fixation vs those who did not. The primary outcomes were incidence of acute torsion within 6 months after surgery and atelectasis on ∼6-month postoperative computed tomography.ResultsOf the 497 patients, 438 (88.1%) underwent right upper lobectomy and 143 (28.8%) underwent fixation. Age, sex, race, comorbidities, and operative diagnosis were similar between the pexy and no-pexy groups (P = .20-.93). Thoracotomy approach was more common in the pexy group (64 [44.8%] vs 65 [18.4%], P < .001), whereas chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (69 [19.5%] vs 10 [7.0%], P < .001) was more prevalent in the no-pexy group. On the primary outcomes, there was no difference between the groups in frank torsion (0 [0%] vs 0 [0%]) or any atelectasis (23 [22.5%] vs 64 [22.9%], P = .94). These findings were unchanged in subgroups of patients without chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and with only right upper lobectomy patients. In a multivariate logistic regression model, undergoing fixation was not a significant predictor of atelectasis on 6-month postoperative computed tomography (odds ratio, 0.94; 95% CI, 0.48-1.86; P = .87).ConclusionsRoutine fixation/pexy of the right middle lobe/lingula during right upper lobectomy/left apical trisegmentectomy does not reduce rates of postoperative torsion or atelectasis. Although ensuring no malrotation of remaining lobes during lung reexpansion is likely important, the routine practice of pexy, which has downsides, should be abandoned.

In Short ▪In a retrospective study of 497 patients, fixation (pexy) of the middle lobe or lingula during upper lobectomy or apical trisegmentectomy did not reduce postoperative torsion or atelectasis.▪Multivariable analysis confirmed no independent benefit of pexy on radiologic or clinical outcomes.▪Findings suggest routine prophylactic pexy should be abandoned, emphasizing careful intraoperative lobar positioning instead.

Right upper and left upper lobectomies are the most commonly performed lung resections —31% and 23% of all lobectomies, respectively. Left apical trisegmentectomy (LATS) is procedurally analogous to the right upper lobectomy (RULobectomy), because it preserves the lingula, which is anatomically similar to the right middle lobe. RULobectomy and LATS are uniquely associated with the rare yet potentially fatal complication of middle lobe (or lingular) torsion, wherein a lobe axially rotates upon its bronchovascular pedicle.

After RULobectomy or LATS, the right middle lobe (RML) and lingula are susceptible to malrotation for several reasons. These include the small diameter and long length of their feeding bronchi, the frequent presence of a complete or nearly complete major fissure allowing a great deal of mobility after division of the horizontal fissure (on the right) or the intersegmental plane (on the left), and a frequently sizeable residual apical space after the resection.1 The incidence of frank lobar torsion after lobectomy characterized by malrotation >180° has been reported at 0.08% to 0.3%, with 70% to 85% of these cases involving RML torsion after RULobectomy.2 Less severe malrotation, also known as RML syndrome been reported in 8% to 11% of patients after RULobectomy.3 Lingular torsion or lesser malrotation after LATS clearly occurs, but its incidence remains unknown.4

To mitigate the risk of frank torsion and lesser malrotation after RULobectomy/LATS, routine pexy has been proposed and variably adopted.5,6 However, others argue that careful lobar repositioning after resection is sufficient to mitigate this risk and that one should avoid unnecessary pleural trauma and potential air leaks.1,3,5

This retrospective study was conducted to determine whether pexy of the middle lobe or lingula reduces the incidence of frank torsion or lesser degrees of atelectasis after RULobectomy and LATS. Secondary objectives included evaluating the impact of pexy on broader postoperative morbidity and mortality.

Patients and Methods

Study Design

This single-center, retrospective analysis was approved by the Stanford School of Medicine Institutional Review Board (IRB-70048), which waived the requirement for informed consent because the study involved only retrospective medical record review.

Patient Cohort

We retrospectively reviewed consecutive patients who underwent RULobectomy or LATS at the Stanford University Hospital Division of Thoracic Surgery from January 2008 through December 2023. Patients were categorized into 2 groups by whether “pexy” of the right middle lobe or lingula to the lower lobe was performed (pexy group) or not (no-pexy group). Outcomes

Outcomes were assessed during a 6-month postoperative period. The primary outcomes included incidence of frank RML (or lingular) torsion within 6 months postoperatively, any RML (or lingular) atelectasis on the 6-month postoperative computed tomographic (CT) scan, and grade ≥2 RML (or lingular) atelectasis on 6-month postoperative CT scan, as determined by a scoring of CT scans by 2 surgeons. Frank torsion was defined as anatomic evidence of malrotation with complete lobar atelectasis and substantial clinical deterioration.

Secondary outcomes included RML (or lingular) atelectasis on chest roentgenogram within 6 weeks postoperatively; a composite clinical outcome of mortality, prolonged length of stay (>14 days), unplanned readmission, reoperation, or bronchoscopy within 30 days and 6 months postoperatively; and the incidence of chronic cough at 6 months. Mortality, unplanned readmission, and reoperation were included only when attributable to pulmonary complications (eg, acute respiratory distress syndrome, pneumothorax, hydropneumothorax, pleural effusion, or subcutaneous emphysema).

Radiologic Evaluation

Chest roentgenograms obtained during the first 6 postoperative weeks were classified as showing atelectasis when noted in the radiology report.

RML (or lingular) atelectasis was assessed on the routine ∼6-month surveillance CT. Atelectasis was graded based on the extent of middle lobe/lingular parenchymal collapse observed on the scans upon review and agreement by 2 surgeons. A 5-point scale was used: no atelectasis, minimal, subsegmental, segmental, and lobar, modified from Newman and colleagues7 (Supplemental Figure 1). RML atelectasis on postoperative CTs was confirmed by deep learning–based volumetry.

Fissure completeness between the RML (or lingula) and the lower lobe (ie, the anterior half of the major fissure) was assessed based on the preoperative scan using a validated deep learning–based algorithm. A fissure was classified as complete if the fissure completeness score was ≥92%. All CT reviews were done blinded to clinical outcomes.

Statistical and power analysis is described in the Supplemental Material.

Results

Baseline Cohort Characteristics

The study included 497 patients: 438 (88.1%) underwent RULobectomy and 59 (11.9%) underwent LATS. Pexy was performed in 143 patients overall (28.8%), including 136 of 438 (31.1%) of RULobectomies and 7 of 59 (11.9%) of LATS. Baseline patient characteristics stratified by pexy status are presented in Supplemental Table 1. The overall incidence of frank lobar torsion was 0%. The overall incidence of any degree of atelectasis on CT at 6 months was 22.8% (87 of 381).

Independent Predictors of Atelectasis on CT at 6-Month Postoperative Follow-Up

Univariate logistic regression analysis (Supplemental Table 2) identified male sex (odds ratio [OR], 0.71; 95% CI, 0.44-1.15; P = .17), history of congestive heart failure (OR, 0.34; 95% CI, 0.08-1.49; P = .15), and complete fissures (OR, 1.73; 95% CI, 0.96-3.10; P = .07) as potential predictors of atelectasis on the 6-month postoperative CT. After adjusting for all factors with P values ≤.20, none remained independent predictors. Performing a pexy was not a significant predictor (OR, 0.94; 95% CI, 0.48-1.86; P = .87) of atelectasis on 6-month postoperative CT.

Independent predictors of 30-day composite clinical outcomes are available in Supplemental Table 3.

Subgroup Analyses

Among patients who underwent RULobectomy alone (excluding LATS) and those with no preoperative diagnosis of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), a reasonable proxy for more complete fissures, there was no significant difference in the primary or in all but 1 secondary outcome between the pexy and no-pexy groups (Supplemental Table 4, Table 5). Comment

In this large, single-institution, retrospective study, we found no difference in the incidence of frank lobar torsion or CT-graded atelectasis at ∼6 months after RULobectomy/LATS between patients who did and did not undergo pexy. This finding held true also in a subgroup that underwent only RULobectomy. Multivariable logistic regression did not identify having undergone pexy as an independent predictor of atelectasis. It must be noted that early postoperative chest roentgenograms at 30 days did show a higher incidence of upper lung field/RML/lingular atelectasis in the pexy than in the no-pexy group. However, these radiologic-only findings were unassociated with significant differences in 30-day composite clinical outcomes between the groups, and they were also not reflected in atelectasis that was present on the CT scan at 6 months.

Because frank torsion is a rare event (in fact 0% in our study), we focused more carefully on lesser degrees of malrotation that may cause RML syndrome (or its left-sided equivalent) with atelectasis and cough. Our findings indicate that routine pexy does not reduce even these problems. Certainly, our surgeons, in the absence of pexy, perform careful positioning of the middle lobe/lingula during reexpansion of the lung after resection, potentially explaining the lack of a demonstrable effect of pexy in our cohort. It is also possible that bronchial kinking due to upward displacement of the lower lobe (particularly after division of the inferior pulmonary ligament), as demonstrated by Ueda and colleagues,8 plays a larger role in atelectatic problems after RULobectomy, whereas only malrotation on the middle lobe or lingular axis may be preventable by pexy.

Given its doubtful benefits, we believe that “routine” prophylactic pexy should be abandoned, because it may prolong operative time by up to 10 minutes and, in our opinion, predispose to air leaks. However, our results do not rule out that there may be subgroups of patients in which pexy is helpful. For example, 1 study found a somewhat lower proportion of COPD in patients with RML atelectasis after RULobectomy (13.6% vs 22.6%, P = .16),1 likely reflecting the lower likelihood of complete fissures in COPD patients. We interpret the lower COPD prevalence in the pexy vs the no-pexy group in our study (7.0% vs 19.5%, P < .001) to reflect surgeon appreciation of less middle lobe/lingular mobility in patients with COPD.

Strengths and Limitations

The key strengths of this study include the large sample size, granular single-institution data, and radiologic outcomes graded by blinded and multiple independent reviewers.

However, several limitations must be considered. First, the single-center nature of the study may limit the generalizability of findings.

Second, the retrospective design introduces potential selection bias and unknown confounders. Surgeons may have selectively used fixation in patients with unknown high-risk factors of torsion; this could obscure a protective effect of pexy if such factors were not included in the regression model.

Although some might argue that the study is underpowered, the consistent lack of a significant difference across multiple outcomes—including on multivariate analysis—strengthen the validity of our findings. Certainly, a prospective multicenter study, stratified by fissure completeness, would be ideal to conclusively determine the role of pexy in various subgroups.

Conclusion

This study provides support for the abandonment of “routine” prophylactic pexy of the middle lobe after RULobectomy and of the lingula after LATS. These procedures do not substantially reduce the incidence of postoperative atelectasis or pulmonary morbidities. Given the lack of demonstrated benefits and the potential drawbacks of pexy, surgeons should prioritize meticulous intraoperative lobar positioning during reexpansion.
