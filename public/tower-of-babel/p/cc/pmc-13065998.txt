Validation of automated 5 mL thin liquid swallowing sound segmentation for estimating audio-derived pharyngeal clearance time
Authors: Dushyantha Jayatilake, Yohei Teramoto, Tomoyuki Ueno, Koichiro Matsuo, Kenji Suzuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluates the reliability of using automatically segmented audio events to detect pharyngeal swallowing activity by comparing their onset and offset timings with physiological event markers derived from videofluoroscopic swallowing studies (VFSS). Swallowing sounds were recorded from 45 patients with suspected dysphagia during VFSS using a neck-worn electronic stethoscope (NWES). A segmentation algorithm was used to detect the audio onset and offset of swallowing. These were compared to VFSS-annotated events: bolus contact with the epiglottis (P-Start), upper esophageal sphincter (UES) opening (E-Start), and UES closure (E-End). Timing offsets and duration measures were analysed, and subgroup comparisons were performed based on the presence or absence of oral containment. The algorithm detected 80 of 84 swallows. Audio onset occurred after P-Start in 96% of cases and after E-Start in 67.5%. Audio offset occurred after E-End in 82.5% of swallows. The mean audio-derived pharyngeal clearance time (PCT) was 706.5 ± 294.8 ms, closely aligned with the VFSS-based pharyngeal duration of 790.0 ± 310.0 ms. PCT did not differ significantly between swallows with and without oral containment, suggesting robustness against pre-pharyngeal activities. The findings confirm that audio-based segmentation reliably captures the pharyngeal phase and estimates durations that align with VFSS-derived physiological events, supporting its use in non-invasive, bedside screening for swallowing efficiency.Keywords: Automatic swallowing detection, Cervical auscultation, Pharyngeal clearance time, Audio segmentation, Digital healthSubject terms: Biomedical engineering, Geriatrics, Diagnosis

Introduction

Dysphagia, or difficulty in swallowing, affects a wide range of populations, particularly older adults and individuals with neurological disorders—such as strokes, Parkinson’s Disease, and Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)—or head and neck cancers. Left unmanaged, it can result in serious complications, including malnutrition, dehydration, and aspiration pneumonia—a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in older adults1–3.

Swallowing assessment is crucial for early detection and intervention. A variety of swallowing assessment methods are currently available in clinical practice, each with distinct advantages and limitations. Clinical bedside evaluations, such as the water swallow test or the repetitive saliva swallowing test, are widely used for screening due to their simplicity and low cost4. However, these tests rely heavily on subjective observation and qualitative assessment.

Instrumental methods enable more detailed evaluation of the biomechanics of swallowing. Among these, the videofluoroscopic swallowing study (VFSS), also known as the modified barium swallow, is considered the gold standard. VFSS offers dynamic, real-time visualisation of bolus flow and structural movements during swallowing, enabling more reliable analysis of timing, residue, and airway protection5. However, VFSS is constrained by radiation exposure, limited availability, and logistical burdens, especially in frail or home-bound patients. Fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES) provides visualisation of the pharynx and larynx without radiation, and is feasible at the bedside. However, the widespread usage of FEES too is constrained by complexity and the invasive nature of its usage.

Cervical auscultation (CA) is the technique of using audio and vibration information of swallows and airways for estimating the swallowing function and has a significant agreement with VFSS6,7. Historically performed via stethoscope and prone to subjective variability, CA has evolved with the emergence of digital electronics and signal processing techniques. Prior studies have examined the temporal alignment between human-coded audio events and physiological events visible in VFSS8. The studies have shown that acoustic features of swallowing differ between healthy and dysphagic individuals9, and that post-swallow breath sounds may aid in aspiration detection10–15.

Previous VFSS studies have shown that extended timing values have associations with aspiration risk, residue, and impaired bolus flow. Oral Transit Time (OTT) reflects the time taken for the bolus to travel from the oral cavity to the pharynx, and tends to be prolonged in individuals with neurological impairments or age-related motor decline. Pharyngeal Delay Time (PDT), the interval between bolus arrival at the pharynx and initiation of the swallow reflex, is strongly associated with aspiration risk in patients with stroke or sensory deficits16,17. Pharyngeal Transit Time (PTT) captures the duration of bolus passage through the pharynx; longer PTT often correlates with pharyngeal residue and post-swallow aspiration. Swallow Apnea Duration (SAD), representing the breathing pause during swallowing, is altered in populations with respiratory dysfunction or discoordination. Meanwhile, Upper Esophageal Sphincter (UES) opening duration and total swallow duration further characterize the temporal mechanics of bolus clearance and can indicate inefficient or compensatory swallowing patterns18,19. However, many of these measures require frame-by-frame VFSS analysis and are not practical for routine bedside evaluation.

Recent advances in digital health technologies have enabled a broader transformation in the assessment of physiological functions, commonly referred to as digital transformation (DT or DX) in healthcare. This consist of the systematic integration of digital technologies—such as wearable sensors, mobile computing, artificial intelligence (AI), cloud infrastructure, and interoperable data systems—into healthcare delivery and decision-making processes. Its aim is to improve accessibility, efficiency, quality of care, and patient outcomes by shifting from episodic, reactive care to continuous, preventive, and data-driven management. Such systems allow for bedside or home-based screening, generate high-resolution datasets, and facilitate early detection of functional decline, contributing to more accessible and scalable dysphagia care.

In this context, recent work has shown that the cervical auscultation-based Pharyngeal Clearance Time (PCT)—defined as the time to clear the pharynx from the initiation of the swallow, and measured as the duration between the audio onset and offset of pharyngeal swallowing activity—is a clinically meaningful and accessible biomarker of pharyngeal efficiency and timing abnormalities in swallowing20. That study showed that PCT increases with age, with a significant difference between young and elderly groups, suggesting its relevance in assessing swallowing dysfunction related to oral hypofunction. In this paper, we examine the physiological correspondence of these automatically segmented events—specifically, the start and end points identified by our real-time audio segmentation algorithm—with VFSS-derived ground truth markers, in order to evaluate their reliability for objective detection of pharyngeal swallowing activity.

Materials and methodology

Participants and data collection

As per the institutional protocol of the University of Tsukuba Hospital, patients suspected of having dysphagia or considered at high risk are referred to the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine for swallowing assessment. A speech-language pathologist (SLP) initially performs a standardized bedside screening using a water swallow test. Based on the SLP’s findings and the clinical judgement of the rehabilitation physician, patients requiring more detailed evaluation undergo VFSS.

This study was conducted from November 2013 to March 2015. During this period, all 108 patients referred for VFSS were considered for inclusion. Each patient was administered between 1 and 5 boluses of varying consistencies—including thin liquids, thickened liquids, and solids—and in volumes of 1 mL, 3 mL, or 5 mL. Bolus type and volume were selected in a stepwise fashion starting with safer options, as informed by the bedside screening, and increased under the supervision of the attending clinician or SLP based on observed safety.

Swallowing examinations were conducted according to the standardized VFSS protocol of the Japanese Society of Dysphagia Rehabilitation (JSDR)21. All bolus materials were prepared in accordance with these guidelines. In this protocol, ‘thin liquids’ consisted of a 20% w/v barium sulfate suspension (Baritester®, Kaigen Pharma Co., Ltd., Japan) used as the standard liquid contrast medium for VFSS at our institution, which correspond to IDDSI Level 0 (‘thin’). The viscosity of this suspension is approximately 10–25 mPa·s at room temperature, consistent with previously reported values for thin barium preparations used in dysphagia assessment22. ‘Thick liquids’ were prepared by adding a thickening agent (Neo-High Toromeal Slim, Foodcare Co., Ltd., Japan) to the same barium suspension. The slightly, moderately or extremely thick liquids were prepared by adding 3, 6 or 9 g of the thickener powder respectively to 500 mL of the barium suspension, and all test liquids were administered within 5 minutes after preparation. The VFSS protocol included three levels of thick liquid consistency (slightly, moderately, and extremely thick) as well as semi-solid foods such as jelly, in order to comprehensively evaluate swallowing safety and efficiency.

From this cohort, all 45 patients (31 male, 14 female; mean age 56.6 ± 12.0 years) who were able to safely swallow 5 ml of thin liquid without aspiration (corresponding to a penetration aspiration scale score of 0) were included in the analysis. The 5 ml thin liquid task was selected as a standardised benchmark condition, as the bolus volume and the consistency could affect the swallowing timing parameters. Of the 45 patients, 39 were administered two 5 ml boluses, while the remaining 6 were tested only once, resulting in a total of 84 swallowing samples. The underlying medical conditions among these patients included head and neck cancers, cerebral haemorrhage, stroke, and ALS. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Tsukuba Hospital (IRB No. H24-050IRB), and written consent has been obtained by all the participants or their guardians. All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institution and with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.

Swallowing sounds were collected using a NWES (Voice Touch Co. Ltd., Taiwan; sensitivity 40 – 45 dB, frequency range 200 Hz – 8 kHz) positioned on the anterior neck, approximately between the C2 and C5 vertebral levels (Fig. 1). VFSS imaging was performed using Toshiba fluoroscopy equipment at the hospital’s VF facility, and recordings were captured using a Canon iVIS HF R31 video camera (Canon Inc., Japan), which simultaneously recorded both the fluoroscopic monitor and the NWES audio waveform via the camera’s microphone input.

Setup for simultaneous VFSS-NWES data acquisition. Participants remained seated during VFSS and wore the NWES at the level of the C5 vertebra to avoid obstructing the fluoroscopic view. NWES audio was routed to the video camera via its microphone input, which recorded the VFSS monitor display. An audiovisual trigger signal (tapping the NWES with a key) enabled synchronisation of NWES audio with VFSS video during post-processing.

To synchronise VFSS video and audio data, an audiovisual trigger signal was introduced at the beginning of each recording, enabling precise alignment of the two modalities. Videos were recorded in MPEG-4 AVC/H.264 format with audio sampled at 44.1 kHz, which was later downsampled to 11,025 Hz for analysis. This downsampling was performed to reduce computational cost—an important consideration for real-time implementation in DX platforms—and is not expected to affect performance, as prior studies have shown that the key audio features of swallowing sounds lie well within this frequency range23.

Swallow event segmentation from audio

To estimate pharyngeal activity timing, incoming audio signals were segmented into individual swallowing events in real time. The segmentation algorithm monitors the amplitude of raw PCM audio, down-sampled to 11.025 kHz and buffered in 484-sample packets (≈44 ms). Based on threshold crossings and silence duration, each swallowing activity is framed with a start and end timestamp as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Overview of the audio segmentation algorithm. Incoming audio is processed in packets, each analysed in real time. When the amplitude of a packet exceeds a predefined threshold, a start or end segment is set accordingly. Detected segments are then passed downstream for classification or visualisation.

The segmentation follows a rule-based algorithm that detects the onset and offset of audio activity using fixed thresholds, silence counters, and timing constraints. The following subsection provides a step-by-step breakdown of this algorithm.

Signal monitoring and activity onset marking: each incoming audio frame (n samples at a sampling rate of Fs) is examined for amplitude values exceeding a predefined activity threshold β. In this study, n=484, Fs=11,025 Hz and β=0.17 (normalised) are predefined. If any such values are found, the algorithm marks the onset of segment to the time-stamp τs of the first amplitude>β sample index in the frame (sfirst) and sets the segment frames counter Nseg(f) to zero.

Ongoing activity and silence tracking: while audio frames continue to show activity, the segment remains open. If no sample in a frame exceed β for γ or more number of consecutive frames (where γ=5 in this study), the segment is considered to have ended. For each frame analysed, the counter Nseg(f) is incremented by one.

The parameters β=0.17 and γ=5 were determined empirically through analysis of swallowing events collected from a wide range of patient and healthy elderly cohorts across multiple recording environments. These values were found to provide robust and consistent detection performance under diverse conditions20 and are therefore treated as universal across datasets. A smaller β increases sensitivity to baseline fluctuations and noise, while a larger β can lead to truncation of swallowing segments. The γ=5 threshold ensures that a silence period of approximately 220 ms (computed as 5×484/11,025), which exceeds the 200 ms minimum post-swallow silence established in previous work 23.

VFSS annotation and audio synchronisation

A trained and certified SLP from the University of Tsukuba Hospital, with over 15 years of clinical experience in dysphagia management, manually annotated timestamps for three key physiological swallowing events related to the pharyngeal stage from the VFSS recordings:

P-Start: bolus contacts the epiglottis

E-Start: upper esophageal sphincter (UES) opens

E-End: UES closes

During a normal swallow, a series of tightly coordinated movements ensures safe and efficient bolus transit through the pharynx, and the three annotated VFSS events in this study correspond to physiologically significant moments within this sequence.

Bolus contacting the epiglottis marks the point at which the bolus passes from the oral cavity into the pharynx and begins to approach the laryngeal vestibule. It typically occurs just before or in conjunction with the initiation of the pharyngeal swallow. This event reflects the effectiveness of oral containment and the timeliness of swallow triggering. If the swallow is not initiated promptly after the bolus reaches this point, there is an increased risk of premature spillage into the airway, especially in patients with sensory or neuromuscular deficits. For the present study using 5 ml command water swallows, this event—which is relatively easier to identify on VFSS—can be considered to mark the start of the pharyngeal phase in cases where there is no evident delay in swallow initiation.

UES opening is the result of a complex interplay of muscular and mechanical actions. As the swallow progresses, the suprahyoid muscles contract, causing the hyoid bone and larynx to elevate and move anteriorly. This movement mechanically pulls open the UES while pharyngeal constrictors contract to propel the bolus downward.

Closure of the UES marks the end of bolus passage through the pharynx and the transition to esophageal transport. It occurs as the hyolaryngeal complex begins to descend and the cricopharyngeus muscle resumes tonic contraction. The total interval from P-Start to E-End represents the pharyngeal clearance duration and is a meaningful measure of swallowing efficiency24–26.

P-Start, while a practical and visually identifiable landmark in VFSS, may substantially precede the actual initiation of the pharyngeal phase in cases of premature bolus spillage or delayed swallow triggering. In such situations, although the bolus is already at the level of the epiglottis or valleculae, the pharyngeal swallow has not yet commenced25, resulting in an extended P-Start to E-Start interval and, consequently, an overestimation of the actual pharyngeal clearance time when using the P-Start to E-End duration as a proxy.

To evaluate whether this premature bolus spillage—arising from impaired oral containment in this case—may lead to altered pre-swallow audio activity that affects the timing of audio onset detection, we also assessed the presence or absence of oral containment in each VFSS trial.

To determine the audio-video offset in each recording, we used AudioDirector 4 (CyberLink Corp., Taiwan) to inspect the alignment between the audiovisual trigger signal and the corresponding audio onset. Frame-by-frame analysis of both the VFSS video and the overlaid audio waveform enabled precise estimation of the synchronisation delay, which was then compensated for in the comparison between VFSS and NWES-based timings.

Audio auto segmentation-based onset/offset were validated against VFSS landmarks by analysing the timestamp offsets.

Results

The audio segmentation algorithm successfully detected 80 out of 84 swallows. Of the four events that were not detected, one had abnormally low audio amplitude likely due to device malfunction and therefore the activity was not detected. The remaining three were automatically rejected by the algorithm for exceeding 40 packets in duration due to: multiple attempts of swallowing, immediate post-swallow speech, and immediate throat clearing, respectively.

For the 80 events that were successfully detected, the mean (SD) and median of the overlap percentage (OLP) were 64.0 (25.4) and 64.4, respectively. Figure 3 illustrates an example of an audio waveform with the corresponding VFSS event annotations.

Figure 4 show the distribution of temporal offsets between audio-based detection and VFSS-based events.

Boxplots of the time offsets between P-Start (bolus contacts the epiglottis) and audio-based onset, E-Start (UES opens) and audio-based onset and E-End (UES closes) and audio-based offset. Negative values indicate that the audio event occurred before the VFSS-based event.

In 77 of the 80 detected swallows (96%), the audio onset occurred after the VFSS-defined P-Start (i.e., when the bolus contacted the epiglottis). In the remaining three cases, the onset was within 100 ms before the P-Start. While a few outliers were present, 80% of the audio onsets occurred within 500 ms after P-Start. The average P-Start to audio start delay was found to be 354.2 ± 322.3 ms with a median value of 279.0 ms.

When compared to E-Start (UES opening), audio onset trailed it in 54 out of 80 cases (67.5%), with a median lag time of 58.5 ms. This suggests that audio-based detection generally falls within the pharyngeal transit period, closer to the UES opening.

The audio offset occurred after the VFSS-defined E-End (UES closure) in 66 cases (82.5%). In 12 instances (15%), the audio offset exceeded E-End by more than 500 ms. This likely reflects trailing audio activity—such as the sound of exhale—at the end of the swallow. On average, audio offset occurred 271.2 ± 293.9 ms after the UES closure with an interquartile rage (IQR) of 88.5–389.25 ms.

Figure 5 compares different audio and VFSS-based duration values. The average audio-based PCT (Audio Duration) was 706.5 ± 294.8 ms, while the VFSS-based P-Start to E-End had a mean duration of 790.0 ± 310.0 ms. These values were generally aligned, though the VFSS-based estimate exhibited a slightly higher mean and broader spread. The UES opening duration (E-End–E-Start) showed a more consistent pattern, with a mean of 482.9 ± 99.5 ms, reflecting the more physiologically constrained nature of this event. In contrast, the UES start delay (E-Start–P-Start) had the highest variability, with a mean of 307.1 ± 308.7 ms, highlighting substantial differences in swallow initiation timing among patients in this dysphagia-suspected cohort.

Discussion

This study investigated the correspondence between automatically segmented swallowing events from neck-worn audio recordings and physiologically defined events identified via VFSS. Specifically, we evaluated whether the onset and offset of pharyngeal activity, as estimated by a rule-based audio segmentation algorithm, align meaningfully with established VFSS landmarks and whether the derived durations reflect relevant functional differences across swallowing conditions.

Our findings indicate that the audio-based segmentation consistently captured the pharyngeal swallowing phase. In 96% of cases, the audio onset occurred after the bolus contacted the epiglottis (P-Start), and in 82.5% of cases, the audio offset occurred after UES closure (E-End). These results confirm that the detected acoustic segment generally spans the VFSS-defined pharyngeal bolus transit interval (from P-Start to E-End). When aligned with UES opening (E-Start), the audio onset trailed E-Start in 54 of 80 detected swallows (67.5%) and preceded it in the remaining 26 swallows (32.5%), with a median lag of 58.5 ms. These timing relationships indicate that the segmentation algorithm detects an acoustic event that is tightly time-locked to UES opening and consistently occurs within the temporal interval during which the bolus transits the pharynx (between P-Start and E-End). This is similar to the findings by Selley et al.27, where they found the first swallow sound occurs as the bolus approach the UES.

The comparison of total swallowing duration values further supports the potential of the audio-derived PCT where the mean audio-based PCT was closely aligned with the VFSS-based epiglottis to UES closing duration (706.5 vs. 790.0 ms). As this is a measure that is based on a set of rules and therefore can be automated, it is consistent and a viable parameter to represent the swallowing ability.

While the mean audio-derived PCT (706.5 ± 294.8 ms) was slightly (10.6%) shorter than the VFSS-defined P-Start to E-End duration (790.0 ± 310.0 ms), subgroup analysis shows this difference is primarily driven by cases with oral containment failure: in the 63 swallows with oral containment, the audio-derived PCT remained stable (705 ± 310 ms) while the VFSS duration decreased to a similar value of 704 ± 136 ms. This may be explained by Cichero and Murdoch28 who proposed that swallowing sounds are primarily generated by the vibration of valves, and premature spillage is often a passive event (gravity-driven flow) occurring before the structural “valving”. This audio-derived PCT closely aligns with the pharyngeal sound duration of 690 ± 162 ms reported by8. Previous studies have shown that pharyngeal transit times can vary naturally by over 100 ms between healthy individuals across age groups and bolus types 29, and oral hypofunction is typically associated with a prolongation of the pharyngeal clearance duration, often exceeding 1000 ms 20. Given this context, the automatically derived audio-based PCT, which closely matches the VFSS-derived P-Start to E-End interval from the initiation of the swallow, can serve as a clinically useful parameter that robustly measures the speed of the pharyngeal motor response for bedside assessment of pharyngeal-phase swallowing efficiency.

The audio-based durations in this cohort—none of whom exhibited UES opening or transit impairments—did not differ significantly between swallows with and without oral containment, whereas VFSS-derived durations showed significant variation. This suggests that the detected audio onset is not influenced by pre-pharyngeal activities and predominantly captures the pharyngeal swallowing phase. Such insensitivity to pre-pharyngeal variation implies that the segmentation algorithm is detecting a core, consistent physiological trigger—rather than being mistriggered by oral-phase variability—which is plausibly the laryngeal ascension sound (LAS), as also indicated in previous work 8. This robustness is a key advantage for using audio-derived duration—as defined by PCT—to assess pharyngeal-stage swallowing efficiency in both clinical and remote monitoring contexts.

However, the audio-derived PCT may underestimate total swallowing duration in individuals with reduced oral bolus control and should be interpreted alongside complementary parameters—such as the delay between bolus administration and audio onset—to more comprehensively assess oral-phase dysfunction.

Using power analysis of the overall VFSS duration (790.0 ± 310.0 ms) across 84 swallows, the minimum detectable difference in means at 80% power and 95% confidence was approximately 94.7 ms. For the 63 swallows with oral containment, where VFSS durations showed lower variability (SD = 136 ms), the detectable difference reduced to 48 ms. This is below the value of clinically useful pharyngeal timing deviations, which often exceed 100 ms29,30.

The findings of this study support the integration of non-invasive, sensor-based tools into clinical workflows and remote care pathways. By demonstrating that PCT can be estimated reliably through automated audio segmentation, our work offers a pathway towards objective, scalable, and accessible dysphagia screening. In contrast to VFSS, which is resource-intensive and logistically constrained, audio-based assessment using wearable sensors and automatic algorithms can be deployed in bedside, community, or home settings, facilitating earlier identification of swallowing dysfunction and longitudinal monitoring of swallowing recovery. These capabilities reduce reliance on episodic, specialist-driven evaluations and enable data-driven, preventive dysphagia management. In clinical practice, this approach can support routine dysphagia screening in hospital wards, nursing homes, and home-based care without requiring radiographic imaging or specialist interpretation. It can also enable repeated monitoring of swallowing function over time, aiding early detection of decline, assessing therapy response, and preventing complications such as aspiration pneumonia.

Limitations and future work

This study has several limitations. All participants in this study were patients undergoing VFSS during the acute stage of illness. Swallowing characteristics at this stage may not reflect stable or chronic patterns, and may include transient compensations or recovery effects. As such, the generalisability of findings to healthy, long-term dysphagia populations or to individuals undergoing rehabilitation remains to be established.

Moreover, the study cohort consisted exclusively of Japanese patients, which may limit applicability to populations with different anatomical characteristics. Factors such as neck size, laryngeal prominence, or tissue density could influence acoustic transmission and sensor coupling, potentially affecting signal quality and segmentation performance. Cross-cultural and anatomical validation studies are needed to assess robustness across diverse groups.

In addition, analysis was limited to 5 mL thin liquid as the bolus volume and the consistency could affect audio characteristics. Further, the VFSS landmarks were annotated by only one trained rater, and the inter-rater variability that may contribute to timing variability was not quantified.

While the UES transit duration was relatively stable across swallows (mean: 480 ms), the UES opening delay varied substantially, particularly in patients with oral containment difficulties. These findings reinforce the clinical importance of temporally resolving subcomponents of the swallow and suggest that automatic audio segmentation, while effective for capturing pharyngeal transit, may require complementary tools to fully realize its potential.

Finally, participants were not stratified by underlying disease etiology. As dysphagia can manifest differently in conditions such as stroke, ALS, or head and neck cancer, future work should investigate whether segmentation accuracy and timing correspondence vary across diagnostic subgroups.

Conclusions

The results demonstrate that automatic audio-based segmentation reliably captures the pharyngeal phase of swallowing and produces duration estimates that closely correspond with VFSS-derived metrics. While not sensitive to pre-pharyngeal impairments, the method shows strong promise as a non-invasive tool for estimating pharyngeal clearance during 5 mL thin liquid swallows in both clinical and home settings. These findings support the role of cervical auscultation with automatic swallowing segmentation in digital health-enabled dysphagia screening and monitoring.
