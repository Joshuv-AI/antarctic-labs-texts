Socioeconomic determinants of male contraceptive use in South Africa: a secondary analysis of the 2016 SADHS data
Authors: Yulisha Naidoo, Liezl Joubert, Karen Nhakaniso, Emmanuella Nzeribe, Folahanmi Tomiwa Akinsolu, Denis Okova, Plaxcedes Chiwire, Akim Tafadzwa Lukwa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHistorically, family planning discussions have predominantly centred on women, overlooking the crucial role men play in contraception. This is particularly concerning in regions like Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), where reproductive health challenges are pronounced. South Africa, a key country within SSA, continues to grapple with issues such as high unintended pregnancy rates and increasing pregnancy-related mortality. Understanding the factors that deter male contraceptive use is essential for achieving universal access to sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services. This study investigates the socioeconomic disparities influencing male contraceptive use in South Africa, drawing on data from the 2016 South Africa Demographic Health Survey.MethodsThe study utilised data from the 2016 South African Demographic and Health Survey, which employed a stratified, two-stage sampling design across all provinces based on the 2011 Census. The survey focused on men aged 15–59 using the “Man’s Questionnaire.” Various socio-demographic and attitudinal variables were analysed using STATA 17. The analysis included multinomial logistic regression to identify determinants of male contraceptive use and the Erreygers Normalized Concentration Index to assess socioeconomic inequalities in contraceptive uptake.ResultsAmong the surveyed men, 47% reported not using any contraception, 40% used male methods, and 13% relied on their partners’ contraceptive use. A majority began sexual activity between the ages of 14–17 (54%), resided in urban areas (56%), were unmarried (67%), and had attained secondary education (70%). Notably, 78% disagreed that contraception is solely a woman’s responsibility, and 64% expressed positive attitudes toward contraception. Age, marital status, and education level emerged as significant factors influencing contraceptive use. The study revealed a slight pro-rich inequality in male contraception use, with socioeconomic status (SES) being the most significant contributor to this disparity (223.22%). This suggests that higher SES is associated with increased inequality in the adoption of modern male contraceptive methods.ConclusionThese findings highlight the impact of socioeconomic disparities and entrenched cultural beliefs on male contraceptive use in South Africa. To address these challenges, comprehensive strategies that prioritise education, expand family planning media campaigns and ensure equitable access to contraception are urgently needed.

Introduction

Globally, family planning remains a cornerstone of reproductive health with substantial social and economic benefits. Research highlights that every dollar spent on family planning will generate 660 million USD in economic returns by 2050 [1]. Additionally, family planning offers cost-saving benefits by reducing reproductive, maternal, and newborn healthcare expenses [2]. As of 2023, 377 million women and adolescent girls in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) were using modern contraception, marking a 20% increase since 2012 [3]. Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) has contributed 32 million additional female users of modern contraception, representing the highest global increase (6.4%) since 2012 [3]. Despite these achievements, 218 million women in LMICs still have unmet contraceptive needs, leading to approximately 111 million unplanned pregnancies annually, accounting for nearly half of all pregnancies in these regions [2]. Furthermore, 800 women die each day from childbirth-related causes, with most of these deaths occurring in LMICs [4, 5].

Historically, the focus of family planning has been disproportionately placed on women. Stakeholders are now advocating for gender-transformative approaches that challenge traditional gender norms, promote power equity between men and women, and encourage women’s empowerment [3]. While female contraceptive methods dominate, with at least 14 options available, men are limited to condoms and vasectomy [6]. This gender disparity has led to family planning being viewed primarily as a female responsibility, contributing to a lack of development, availability, and adoption of male contraceptive methods, especially in regions like SSA, where fertility rates remain high and access to reproductive health services is constrained [7–10].

This imbalance is particularly pronounced in SSA, where unmet contraception needs exacerbate existing social and health challenges [7]. Despite having lower fertility rates than other SSA countries, South Africa faces substantial sexual and reproductive health (SRH) challenges. These include unintended pregnancies, significant unmet family planning needs among unmarried young women, and high adolescent pregnancy rates [11]. These challenges are compounded by a rising pregnancy-related mortality ratio [12, 13]. The 2016 South African Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) reported low vasectomy uptake and inconsistent condom use among men, revealing a gap in male participation in family planning [14]. Recognising men as active participants in SRH is vital for expanding access to modern family planning services [14, 15]. However, male involvement is hindered by socio-cultural, economic, political, and gendered factors [16]. African societies often reinforce traditional views that place family planning responsibility on women, perpetuating gender inequality and limiting men’s active participation [11]. Additionally, misinformation about contraception and male dominance in relationships further frustrates efforts to engage men [14, 17].

There has been a growing recognition of the need to actively involve men in family planning programs to achieve more equitable reproductive health outcomes [18, 19]. This shift has been driven by the understanding that family planning is not solely a women’s issue but a shared responsibility that benefits the entire household and society. Male involvement in contraception is particularly important in addressing gaps in contraceptive use and reducing the burden placed on women. Studies suggest that increasing male participation in family planning decisions could lead to more consistent contraceptive use, reduced unintended pregnancies, and improved communication between partners about reproductive health choices [14, 20, 21]. Furthermore, promoting shared responsibility in contraception could help challenge deeply rooted gender norms that perpetuate unequal power dynamics and hinder progress towards gender equality in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [16].

However, despite these efforts, men’s participation in family planning remains low in many regions, particularly in SSA [6, 9, 22–24]. Barriers such as limited access to male-focused reproductive health services, cultural expectations, and misinformation about male contraceptive options continue to impede progress. Male contraceptive methods, beyond condoms and vasectomy, remain under-researched and underdeveloped, contributing to a lack of options for men and reinforcing the perception that family planning is primarily a woman’s responsibility [15]. Addressing these barriers requires a multifaceted approach that includes improving access to male contraceptive methods, raising awareness about the benefits of male involvement, and promoting gender-equitable policies that support shared reproductive health responsibilities. Without concerted efforts to engage men and address these challenges, the progress made in advancing family planning services may remain uneven and insufficient to meet global targets for reproductive health [21].

Despite the growing interest in male contraceptive involvement, research remains limited on how these barriers vary across socioeconomic groups. Studies across Southern Africa, including South Africa, have identified factors such as age, education, employment, marital status, media access, and being from a female-headed household as determinants of male involvement in family planning [20, 21]. However, evidence on the extent of socioeconomic inequality in male contraception uptake remains scarce. Understanding these disparities is essential for developing inclusive policies that promote gender equality, enhance SRH outcomes, and address health inequities. This study addresses these gaps by analysing data from South Africa’s 2016 Demographic Health Survey to (1) Assess the socioeconomic determinants of male contraceptive use, (2) Determine the extent of socioeconomic inequality in the uptake of male contraception, and (3) Examine the drivers of such inequality. These findings will provide essential insights into the barriers men face in accessing contraception across diverse socioeconomic backgrounds, contributing to evidence-based recommendations for policy and practice. This is crucial for advancing South Africa’s progress toward universal SRH access and achieving Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) and SDG 5 (Gender Equality) [1].

Methods

Data source and study population

This cross-sectional study used South Africa’s 2016 Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) [25]. The survey, conducted between June and November 2016, was a collaborative effort by the National Department of Health (NDoH), the South African Medical Research Council (SAMRC), and Statistics South Africa (StatsSA) [26]. South Africa has nine provinces: Eastern Cape, Free State, Gauteng, KwaZulu-Natal, Limpopo, Mpumalanga, Northern Cape, North-West, and Western Cape. South Africa’s DHS was selected due to the country’s historical inequalities and the critical need to address health disparities, particularly those related to male contraceptive use.

The SADHS 2016 employed a stratified, two-stage sampling design based on the Statistics South Africa Master Sample Frame (MSF). This design involved selecting Primary Sampling Units (PSUs) from Census 2011 enumeration areas stratified by geography (urban, traditional, or farm) [26]. Seven hundred and fifty PSUs were selected across 26 strata, with a systematic sampling of residential units (DUs) within each PSU. Data were collected using the “Man’s Questionnaire,” which targeted men aged 15 to 59 from selected households. The survey captured data on various socio-demographic and health-related aspects, including education, media exposure, family planning, marital and sexual history, employment, HIV/AIDS awareness, and health-seeking behaviours [26]. The survey targeted 3 618 men aged 15 to 59 from selected households, using the “Man’s Questionnaire” to gather data. The questionnaire captured information on a wide range of socio-demographic and health-related aspects: age, education, media exposure, family planning, marital and sexual history, fertility inclinations, employment, HIV/AIDS awareness, and health-seeking behaviours such as tobacco and alcohol consumption [26].

Outcome variable

The outcome variable for this study was the current contraceptive method used by males. This variable was derived from the question, “Are you currently doing something or using any method with any partner to delay or avoid a pregnancy?” Respondents who answered “yes” were further asked to specify the methods used. Based on their responses, three categories were established: males who reported not using any modern contraceptive method or relying on ineffective traditional/natural methods (e.g., periodic abstinence, lactational amenorrhea, and withdrawal) were classified as ‘0 = Not using.’ Those who reported using modern contraception through their partners (e.g., pill, IUD, injections, female sterilisation, and Norplant) were categorised as ‘1 = Using Partner Contraceptive.’ The final category, ‘2 = Using Male Contraceptive,’ included males who reported using male-only methods such as male condoms and male sterilisation.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using STATA 17, weighted to adjust for differences in the probability of selection and non-response. The study excluded males with missing values from the analysis. The analysis began with descriptive statistics, including proportions and frequencies, to report sample characteristics. Ethical considerations

This study involved secondary analysis based on a publicly available de-identified DHS dataset, eliminating the need to seek ethics approval. Even so, it is important to note that Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) comply with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki: the importance of informed consent, confidentiality, and the right to withdraw from participation at any time without penalty.

Descriptive statistics of male contraceptive use in South Africa

Regarding contraceptive methods, 47% (95% CI [45.72%, 48.98%]) of the respondents reported not using any method, while 40% (95% CI [38.49%, 41.69%]) used male contraceptives, and 13% (95% CI [11.54%, 13.70%]) indicated that their partners were the ones using contraceptives. The age at first sexual experience varied among participants: 8% (95% CI [7.14%, 9.03%]) initiated sexual activity before the age of 14, 54% (95% CI [52.55%, 56.01%]) between 14 and 17 years, 34% (95% CI [32.69%, 35.99%]) between 18 and 24 years, and 4% (95% CI [2.79%, 4.05%]) after the age of 24 (Table 1).

In terms of residential status, 56% (95% CI [54.24%, 57.47%]) of the participants resided in urban areas, while 44% (95% CI [42.53%, 45.77%]) lived in rural areas (Table 1). The majority of the participants, 67% (95% CI [65.45%, 68.51%]), were not married, with 33% (95% CI [31.49%, 34.55%]) being married. Educational attainment varied, with 4% (95% CI [3.14%, 4.37%]) having no formal education, 17% (95% CI [15.54%, 17.97%]) completing primary education, 70% (95% CI [68.92%, 71.89%]) having secondary education, and 9% (95% CI [8.25%, 10.13%]) attaining higher education. Socioeconomic status among participants was distributed across wealth quintiles, with 21% (95% CI [19.63%, 22.28%]) in the poorest quintile, 23% (95% CI [22.00%, 24.76%]) in the poorer quintile, 24% (95% CI [21.98%, 24.74%]) in the middle quintile, 19% (95% CI [17.88%, 20.44%]) in the richer quintile, and 13% (95% CI [12.20%, 14.41%]) in the richest quintile (Table 1).

Sexual behaviour patterns revealed that the majority of participants, 80% (95% CI [78.96%, 81.86%]), had only one sexual partner, 19% (95% CI [17.20%, 20.04%]) had between two to five partners, and 1% (95% CI [0.67%, 1.41%]) reported having more than six partners. Regarding fertility preferences, 37% (95% CI [34.31%, 39.87%]) of the participants desired children, 54% (95% CI [51.52%, 57.26%]) did not want children, and 9% (95% CI [7.07%, 10.31%]) were undecided (Table 1). Attitudinal factors showed that 78% (95% CI [76.71%, 79.58%]) of participants disagreed with the notion that contraception is solely a woman’s responsibility, while 22% (95% CI [20.42%, 23.29%]) agreed. Additionally, 69% (95% CI [67.43%, 70.69%]) disagreed with the statement that women who use contraception are promiscuous, while 31% (95% CI [29.31%, 32.57%]) agreed. Overall, 64% (95% CI [62.30%, 65.58%]) of participants had a positive attitude towards contraception, while 36% (95% CI [34.42%, 37.70%]) held negative views (Table 1).

Regarding media exposure, 53% (95% CI [51.72%, 54.97%]) of participants had not obtained family planning information from television or radio, while 47% (95% CI [45.03%, 48.28%]) had (Table 1). Health system factors indicated that 29% (95% CI [27.67%, 30.64%]) of participants had never been tested for HIV, whereas 71% (95% CI [69.37%, 72.33%]) had undergone testing. A noTable 86% (95% CI [84.90%, 87.16%]) of participants did not have health insurance, with only 14% (95% CI [12.84%, 15.10%]) being covered. Furthermore, a significant 93% (95% CI [91.63%, 93.35%]) had never discussed contraception with a health professional, while only 7% (95% CI [6.65%, 8.37%]) reported having done so (Table 1).

Discussion

The findings from the 2016 South Africa Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) provide nuanced insights into the multifaceted determinants influencing male contraceptive use in South Africa. This study sought to understand how demographic, socioeconomic, and behavioural factors shape men’s reproductive health choices, and the results reveal critical areas of concern that align with and sometimes diverge from existing literature. The age distribution of the participants, while within expected ranges, revealed a substantial number of early sexual initiations, with a significant portion of respondents beginning sexual activity before adulthood. This finding is particularly concerning as it highlights the vulnerability of adolescents to early sexual activity without adequate reproductive health education. Kriel et al. underscores the importance of early and comprehensive sexual education, which can empower adolescents to make informed decisions about their reproductive health, reducing the risks associated with early sexual activity [31].

The study also uncovered a gender imbalance in contraceptive responsibility. A smaller proportion of men reported using male contraceptives, while a significant number relied on their partners or did not use any method at all. This finding is consistent with the gendered dynamics in reproductive health highlighted by Namasivayam et al., who argue that entrenched cultural norms often place the burden of contraception on women [32]. The persistent cultural expectations around gender roles in contraception necessitate targeted interventions to promote equitable contraceptive responsibility. Demographic factors such as urban versus rural residency also emerged as significant influencers of contraceptive behaviour. Urban dwellers, who generally have better access to health services and are potentially more exposed to changing gender norms, were more likely to use modern contraceptive methods compared to their rural counterparts. This urban-rural divide is consistent with the findings of Roudsari et al. [16], who documented how disparities in infrastructure and access to health services between urban and rural areas contribute to differences in health outcomes, including contraceptive use.

Education was another critical determinant of contraceptive use. The positive association between higher education levels and the use of both male and female contraceptive methods is well-supported by the literature. Kabagenyi et al. (2014) emphasise that education enhances individuals’ ability to access and critically evaluate health information, leading to better health outcomes [7]. However, the presence of a significant portion of the population with limited or no formal education is concerning, as this group may be more susceptible to misconceptions about contraception, which could hinder informed decision-making. Socioeconomic status also played a crucial role in shaping contraceptive choices. The data showed that wealthier individuals were more likely to use male contraceptives, which aligns with global findings on health inequalities. Economic barriers are a significant impediment to accessing modern contraceptive methods, as noted by Nketiah-Amponsah et al. [33]. The association between wealth and contraceptive use underscores the need for policies that reduce economic barriers and promote equitable access to reproductive health services across all socioeconomic groups.

Interestingly, a substantial majority of participants disagreed with the notion that contraception is solely a woman’s responsibility, indicating a shift in societal attitudes towards more progressive views on gender roles in reproductive health. This shift, while positive, is still nascent, as entrenched cultural norms continue to influence perceptions, as noted by Pearson and Makadzange [34]. There is a clear need for continued efforts to promote gender equality in contraceptive responsibility. Media exposure emerged as a significant factor influencing contraceptive behaviour, with those exposed to family planning messages on television or radio being more likely to use contraceptives. This finding is consistent with the literature on the role of media in shaping health behaviours. Ndinda, Ndhlovu, and Khalema (2017) highlighted the potential of media campaigns to promote public health awareness effectively [35]. The underutilisation of media for disseminating family planning information represents a missed opportunity to reach wider audiences, particularly in rural and underserved areas.

The multivariate regression analysis further revealed that older men were less likely to use male contraceptives, a trend consistent with previous research [7, 36]. This may reflect generational differences in attitudes toward family planning, with older cohorts being more influenced by traditional gender roles that place less emphasis on male participation in contraceptive use. On the other hand, those who initiated sexual activity at a younger age were more likely to use male contraceptives, which may suggest that early sexual experiences could lead to a greater awareness of the importance of contraception in preventing unintended pregnancies and sexually transmitted infections.

The results from the Erreygers concentration index and its decomposition analysis provide further insights into the socioeconomic inequalities in male contraceptive use. While age and marital status appeared to reduce inequality, reflecting their role in balancing contraceptive use across different socioeconomic groups, education and wealth significantly contributed to the observed disparities. The strong association between socioeconomic status and contraceptive use, as highlighted by the World Health Organization and Darin-Mattsson et al., underscores the deep-rooted structural inequalities that persist in access to reproductive health services [37–40].

In conclusion, this study highlights the complex interplay of demographic, socioeconomic, and behavioural factors in shaping male contraceptive use in South Africa. The findings underscore the need for targeted interventions that address the specific needs of different demographic groups, promote gender equality in contraceptive responsibility, and reduce socioeconomic barriers to access. Addressing these challenges will be critical to achieving more equitable and effective reproductive health outcomes in South Africa.

Strength and limitations

This study makes significant contributions to understanding male contraceptive use in South Africa, particularly in its focus on socioeconomic disparities, an area that has been underexplored in previous research. By utilising data from the most recent South Africa Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) conducted in 2016, our analysis provides up-to-date insights into male contraceptive use, highlighting critical determinants influencing this behaviour across different socioeconomic groups.

A key strength of this study is its novel focus. Unlike many existing studies that examine general determinants of contraceptive use, our research specifically investigates the socioeconomic disparities that underpin these determinants. This approach allows for a better understanding of the inequalities in male contraceptive use, offering a foundation for targeted interventions that could address these disparities. Additionally, the use of recent and comprehensive data from the 2016 DHS allows us to evaluate current trends and disparities in male contraceptive use, offering insights that are relevant to the present-day context of reproductive health in South Africa. Furthermore, the study employs the Normalised Erreygers Concentration Index and its decomposition, quantitatively assessing socioeconomic inequalities in male contraceptive use. This method quantifies the extent of inequality and identifies specific factors contributing to these disparities, offering clear directions for policy and programmatic interventions.

However, the study also has several limitations. A primary limitation is its cross-sectional design, which restricts our ability to infer causal relationships between the determinants identified and the use of male contraceptives. While our findings highlight associations between various factors and contraceptive use, longitudinal studies would be necessary to establish causality and track changes in behaviours and attitudes over time. Additionally, the study relies on self-reported data, subject to recall and social desirability biases. Participants may have underreported or overreported their contraceptive use and other behaviours based on what they perceive to be socially acceptable, potentially leading to misclassification and affecting the accuracy of our findings.

The absence of qualitative data is another limitation, as it restricts our understanding of the complex, subjective factors that influence male contraceptive use. Cultural norms, personal experiences, and interpersonal dynamics are deeply intertwined with contraceptive behaviours, yet these elements are challenging to capture through quantitative methods alone. A mixed-methods approach that includes qualitative interviews or focus groups could provide richer insights into the motivations and barriers related to contraceptive use. Furthermore, while our study considers some health system factors, such as HIV testing and health insurance coverage, it does not delve into other critical aspects like healthcare accessibility, the attitudes and practices of healthcare providers, or the influence of intimate partner dynamics on contraceptive choices. These factors are pivotal in shaping contraceptive behaviour and deserve further investigation in future studies. Lastly, there may be other confounding variables not included in our analysis, such as regional variations in healthcare infrastructure, education quality, or the availability of male contraceptive options, which could influence the observed disparities. The omission of these factors may have led to an incomplete understanding of the full range of influences on male contraceptive use.

Conclusion and recommendations

To advance the understanding of contraceptive decisions and to develop more effective interventions, future research should delve deeper into the complex dynamics that influence these choices [41–43]. A mixed-methods approach is recommended, as it would allow for a comprehensive understanding by integrating quantitative data with qualitative insights. This approach can capture not only the patterns and determinants of contraceptive use but also the personal, cultural, and contextual factors that shape these behaviours. Additionally, designing a longitudinal study would be invaluable in tracking changes in contraceptive behaviours and preferences over time, providing a clearer picture of how these evolve in response to various influences, including policy changes and social shifts.

Engaging with local communities and stakeholders is also crucial [44–47]. Their involvement can provide context-specific insights essential for designing culturally appropriate interventions that are more likely to be effective. Such engagement can help ensure that policies and programs are tailored to different communities’ unique needs and circumstances, thereby enhancing their impact.

Moreover, leveraging technology offers a promising avenue for research and intervention [48–51]. For instance, mobile applications can gather real-time data on contraceptive use, providing more accurate and timely information. These platforms can also serve as effective tools for disseminating credible family planning information, helping to bridge the gap between knowledge and practical application. By making information more accessible and engaging, technology can play a key role in promoting informed contraceptive choices and improving reproductive health outcomes.

In conclusion, despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the socioeconomic factors affecting male contraceptive use in South Africa. Identifying key inequality areas offers a basis for more equitable and effective reproductive health policies and interventions. Future research should aim to address the limitations identified, particularly through longitudinal studies and mixed-method approaches, to deepen our understanding of the determinants of male contraceptive use and to inform more comprehensive strategies for addressing these disparities.
