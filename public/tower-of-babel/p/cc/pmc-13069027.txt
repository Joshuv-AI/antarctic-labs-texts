Multidimensional Trends and AI-Based Forecasts of HIV Incidence in Korea, 1985–2024
Authors: Seungho Baek, Haneol Cho, Chansoo Kim, Yeonju Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSince the first reported HIV case in Korea in 1985, the incidence of HIV/AIDS has continued to rise, emphasizing the need for effective prevention and management strategies. While global initiatives aim to end the epidemic by 2030, Korea has observed demographic shifts, particularly among young men and foreign nationals, that warrant further investigation.MethodsWe analyzed national HIV incidence data from 1985 to 2024, stratified by nationality, age, transmission route, and testing motivation. To forecast future trends, we employed a stacking ensemble model combining ARIMA, Random Forest, and LSTM, each trained to capture different aspects of temporal and demographic patterns. Model performance was evaluated using RMSE and MAE.ResultsAnnual new HIV infections rose steadily until the mid-2010s, peaking at 1,223 cases in 2019, before stabilizing around 975–1,000 cases by 2024. Korean men showed a recent decline, while foreign nationals, especially foreign women, displayed continuous growth. Young men aged 25–34 emerged as the most affected group, comprising nearly half of male cases in 2023. The ensemble model outperformed individual approaches, projecting that new HIV cases will stabilize at approximately 1,024 annually through 2030.ConclusionAnalysis of HIV incidence in Korea using 40-year trend data reveals a historically rising trend that has recently begun to stabilize, with projections suggesting this plateau will continue through 2030. Ensemble-based forecasting models leveraging four decades of surveillance data may enhance predictive accuracy and serve as a framework for other settings facing shifts in HIV epidemiology.

Introduction

Globally, an estimated 40 million people lived with HIV in 2024, with about 1.3 million new infections recorded in the same year [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) and the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) have a shared goal of ending the HIV/AIDS epidemic as a public health threat by 2030 [2]. In terms of incidence, UNAIDS and WHO advocate for reducing new HIV infections by 90 % from 2010 levels and a continued 5 % decline per year after 2030 [2]. Based on temporal trends of several countries, despite substantial progress in antiretroviral therapy (ART), the UNAIDS-endorsed goal to end the AIDS epidemic by 2025 - the 95-95-95 treatment cascade (95 % of people living with HIV to know their status, 95 % of those diagnosed to be on ART, and 95 % of those on ART to achieve viral suppression) - is challenging to achieve [2–8].

In Korea, the first HIV case was reported in 1985, and by 2024, the number of people living with HIV was approximately 20,450 men and women [9]. The “Prevention of Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome Act” of Korea was enacted on November 28, 1987, and came into effect on January 28, 1988 [10]. Building on this legislative foundation and subsequent developments in treatment, along with increasing public support for people living with HIV (PLWH) and their caregivers, the government has progressively expanded public services and welfare support. These initiatives include comprehensive coverage of medical expenses, hospital-based counseling programs, and enhanced long-term care services [10].

In order to monitor the HIV incidence to design a more effective strategy for HIV prevention and HIV management, many researchers make an effort to produce a precise estimation of the future trend of HIV in each country or region. Several studies have employed a variety of time-series models on HIV/AIDS in Korea [11] and in China [12] to estimate the burden of HIV in the future in each region. Yu et al. applied ARIMA (AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average) models to Korean national HIV incidence data (1985–2012) and successfully predicted the incremental rise in annual cases [11]. Similarly, a research in Guangxi, China has demonstrated that HIV will not decrease using deep learning methods such as LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) [12].

In Korea, extended back-calculation and dynamic transmission models have also been applied: Lee et al. estimated the number of undiagnosed HIV cases and time to diagnosis using the ECDC HIV Modeling Tool [13], while Choi et al. evaluated the long-term impact and cost-effectiveness of PrEP implementation among MSM through a compartmental dynamic model [14]. Deep learning methods have been shown to yield accurate forecasts of monthly HIV occurrence and often outperform traditional statistical approaches. However, there are limitations of these studies such as short data spans, a lack of covariates to capture behavioral or demographic factors, and assumptions that past trends will continue unchanged [11, 12].

The present study seeks to address some of these gaps by consolidating and analyzing annual HIV incidence data of Korea from 1985 to 2024, encompassing a broader temporal scope in further prediction. We have employed an ensemble of time-series models—such as LSTM, Random Forest, and ARIMA—and combine them in a stacking framework for robust forecasts.

Methods

We collected annual HIV incidence data from 1985 to 2024 from official Korean sources, including the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) and Statistics Korea, referencing the 2024 Annual Report on the Notified HIV/AIDS in Korea published by KDCA [15]. All datasets were merged based on the year variable. From the raw data, we engineered features to better capture trends. Specifically, we calculated the proportion of cases attributed to foreigners and to men who have sex with men (MSM) relative to the total annual incidence. For age, raw data categorized in five-year brackets were aggregated into four broader age groups: under 25 years, 25–34 years, 35–59 years, and 60 years and above.

We employed a stacking ensemble approach to leverage the complementary strengths of different modeling paradigms and to improve prediction robustness relative to individual models [16]. The ensemble integrates three base models, each capturing distinct aspects of HIV incidence patterns. First, an ARIMA model was fitted to the univariate time series with automatic parameter selection based on AIC minimization, effectively capturing temporal dependencies and linear trends [17]. Second, a Random Forest regressor with 100 trees was trained on seven input features — year, foreign_ratio, msm_ratio, and four age group variables — to model complex non-linear relationships and feature interactions [18]. Third, an LSTM network with a single hidden layer of 16 units was trained for 50 epochs using a sliding window of size 3, enabling it to learn sequential patterns from historical observations [19].

The predictions from these base learners were then combined using a linear meta-learner trained via ordinary least squares on in-sample predictions, allowing the ensemble to assign optimal weights to each model’s output and generate the final forecast [20]. Model performance was assessed using Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) to quantify prediction accuracy, with both metrics expressed in case counts for direct interpretation [21].

Results

From 1985 to the mid-2010s, the total annual number of newly diagnosed HIV cases in Korea exhibited a steady rise, peaking at 1,223 cases in 2019 (Fig. 1). The incidence increased rapidly until the mid-2010s, then showed a notable decline in 2020 (1,014 cases), likely influenced by the COVID-19 pandemic and associated public health measures. By 2024, cases remained relatively stable at 975, indicating a plateauing trend. Korean men constituted the majority of cases throughout the study period, peaking at 1,016 cases in 2014 before declining to 683 cases by 2024. In contrast, foreign populations demonstrated sustained growth: foreign men increased from 70 cases in 2013 to 182 cases in 2024, while foreign women cases rose from 31 to 79 cases over the same period. Notably, foreign women surpassed Korean women in case numbers from 2017 onward, with foreign women reaching 79 cases compared to 31 cases among Korean women by 2024.

Age-specific analyses revealed a substantial increase in the proportion of men aged 25-34 years since 2011 (Fig. 2). This age group represented 22.1 % of male HIV cases in 2011, rising progressively to 45.4 % by 2023, indicating more than a two-fold increase over the study period. The 25-29 age group showed particularly pronounced growth, peaking at 247 cases in 2019 before declining to 156 cases in 2023 and further to 134 cases in 2024. By contrast, female patients demonstrated a more dispersed age distribution across all age groups. The smaller absolute numbers in female cases (31 total in 2023) limit the interpretability of age-specific patterns in this population.

Examination of transmission routes (Fig. 3) showed that male cases were predominantly attributed to sexual contact, with homosexual transmission showing substantial growth from 29.6 % in 2015 to 46.9 % in 2024. Concurrently, the proportion of male cases classified as ‘unknown’ increased from 18.9 % in 2018 to 28.8 % in 2024, representing a notable rise in unidentified transmission routes. Female cases remained predominantly linked to heterosexual transmission (averaging over 65 %), although this proportion showed considerable year-to-year variation, ranging from 35.5 % to 83.0 % during the study period, likely reflecting the small sample sizes involved.

Analysis of HIV testing reasons (Fig. 4) presents the cumulative number and proportion of HIV cases among men and women in Korea by testing category. Categories include disease investigation, surgical/hospitalization screening, health check-ups, voluntary testing, and others. Among male cases, disease investigation and voluntary testing constitute the largest proportions, with voluntary testing showing notable growth from 19.1 % in 2015 to 30.7 % in 2024. Importantly, the proportion of cases with unknown testing reasons decreased dramatically from 26.1 % in 2015 to 3.2 % in 2024, indicating improved data collection over time. For women, while absolute numbers remain small (averaging 40 cases annually), disease investigation and routine medical examinations represent the primary detection methods. To evaluate predictive performance, observed data from 2015 to 2024 were compared with forecasts generated by three base models (ARIMA, Random Forest, and LSTM) and their stacking ensemble (Fig. 5). The stacking ensemble demonstrated superior predictive accuracy (RMSE = 88.56, MAE = 75.96) compared to individual models: Random Forest (RMSE = 142.03, MAE = 129.71), ARIMA (RMSE = 376.53, MAE = 316.30), and LSTM (RMSE = 823.22, MAE = 689.53). Based on this superior performance, the ensemble model was used for future projections. The stacking ensemble forecasted HIV incidence to stabilize at approximately 1,024 cases annually from 2025 through 2030, suggesting a plateau pattern rather than continued growth. Discussion

Our findings reveal a steady increase in HIV incidence in Korea until the early to mid-2010s, followed by a slight decline or stabilization in more recent years. Distinction between men and women was the distribution of age groups: increase in cases was notable among young men while age groups among Korean women were evenly distributed. In terms of transmission route, reports of homosexual contact have increased over years, which is relevant to the increasing young cases among Korean men. Difference between sex was also observed in the reason for HIV testing. While reasons for tests among men were mostly routine checkup or voluntary test, many reasons among women were preoperative/inpatient test and diagnostic clarification.

The temporary decline observed in 2020 (1,014 cases from 1,223 in 2019) likely reflects COVID-19 pandemic disruptions, including reduced social interactions, delayed healthcare visits, and decreased routine testing [22]. This was followed by stabilization around 975-1,000 cases in recent years. The recent trend may reflect a decrease in HIV testing [23]; therefore, careful monitoring will be warranted in the coming years. Increasing cases among young men was reported in previous reports [24]. Our analysis revealed that men aged 25-34 years represented 45.4 % of all male HIV cases by 2023, compared to 22.1 % in 2011, indicating a dramatic demographic concentration among this age group. This trend reflects global patterns where new infections predominantly occur among younger populations, while older age groups represent long-term survivors benefiting from improved antiretroviral therapies. As prevention strategies become more effective, the proportion of younger patients may eventually decline, with an aging HIV population becoming more prominent.

Another notable outcome is the ongoing predominance of sexual transmission among men, where homosexual contact shows a gradual increase from 29.6 % in 2015 to 46.9 % in 2024. However, stigma and cultural barriers can lead some men to report heterosexual or unknown routes, reflecting potential under-reporting in a very conservative society like Korea [25]. Despite significant improvements in HIV treatment and the health status of people living with HIV both domestically and internationally, negative perceptions about the disease remain a major obstacle to AIDS prevention in Korea. According to the 2018 National Multicultural Acceptance Survey of 1,245 Korean citizens, AIDS patients (92.9 %) and homosexuals (79.6 %) were identified as people they would not want as neighbors, far exceeding rates for alcoholics (62.3 %), other races (15.2 %), or other religions (5.5 %) [26]. The 2021 survey of 10,000 people showed similar results, with AIDS patients (91.5 %) and homosexuals (77.1 %) maintaining high rejection rates, indicating that negative perceptions have barely improved [27]. Despite the fact that HIV cannot be transmitted through air, handshakes, hugs, or other casual physical contact, nor through droplets, saliva, sweat, utensils, bedding, or clothing, respondents expressed reluctance even to live in the same neighborhood. Nevertheless, the increasing honest reporting of homosexual contact is encouraging and suggests that future epidemiological investigations should employ more advanced methods in Korea, such as self-administered questionnaires, to facilitate truthful responses.

Voluntary testing among women remains relatively low, potentially reflecting lower perceived risk or fewer public awareness campaigns targeted at female populations [28]. In addition, women in Korea may have a lower level of HIV awareness compared to men, partly due to societal norms that tend to associate HIV risk primarily with men who have sex with men (MSM) or intravenous drug users. As a result, some women may underestimate their own vulnerability or believe they are not in a high-risk group, thereby delaying or foregoing voluntary testing. In cases where male partners are tested first—or where healthcare outreach focuses on male populations—women might also miss early detection opportunities. These observations highlight the importance of tailoring educational and screening interventions to specific demographic groups, emphasizing that both men and women can be at risk and require timely testing and treatment.

The incidence among foreign nationals—especially foreign men—has exhibited sustained growth since the mid-2010s. Several factors may contribute to this trend: increased international mobility, changes in visa policies, and improved medical access for non-Korean residents. In early 2010, the Korean government abolished entry restrictions on foreigners with HIV [29]. From 2018, HIV test results are not required for all types of visa issuance to enter Korea. In 2023, the foreign population reached 2.5 million, with 256 newly reported HIV cases among foreign nationals [30]. Official surveillance data used in our analysis did not include nationality information, precluding a detailed examination of countries of origin among foreign nationals. A previous analysis by the KDCA, based on unofficial records, reported that Thailand, Korean-Chinese, Uzbekistan, Vietnam, Russia, the Philippines, and Cambodia each accounted for more than 10 newly diagnosed HIV cases among foreign nationals in 2023 [30]. Regarding visa status, the most common category was Overseas Korean (F-4), followed by Miscellaneous (G-1), Student (D-2), and others [30]. Although pinpointing the exact cause requires further qualitative or policy-specific data, the results suggest that targeted public health measures should account for the rising foreign population at risk.

Based on national annual data spanning 40 years, we were able to develop several models for estimating future trends through 2030. In this study, we projected that new cases will stabilize around 1,024 annually through 2030, which contrasts with the WHO and UNAIDS goals of reducing new infections. This projection underscores the need for enhanced prevention education, expanded testing initiatives, and increased participation in pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) programs. Encouragingly, since April 17, 2024, the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency has significantly expanded the accreditation of confirmatory HIV testing institutions, enabling more patients to receive earlier treatment [31].

From a methodological standpoint, the stacking ensemble approach outperformed individual models (ARIMA, Random Forest, and LSTM) in both historical validation (2015–2024) and future projections (2025–2030), achieving superior accuracy (MAE = 75.96 cases). Each base model captures different data characteristics: ARIMA accounts for linearity and lag structures, tree-based methods capture nonlinear interactions between covariates, and LSTM learns longer temporal dependencies. By combining these strengths through a meta-learner, the ensemble produced forecasts that were more robust and accurate. Nonetheless, abrupt policy shifts or behavioral changes may limit its generalizability, underscoring the need to account for forecast uncertainty in public health planning. To address such challenges, some studies have incorporated behavioral or policy change scenarios directly into network- or agent-based models [32, 33]. Our study has several limitations. First, the surveillance data includes only individuals who have undergone testing; therefore, it likely underestimates true HIV incidence by excluding undiagnosed cases. Previous research utilizing Korean National Health Insurance (KNHI) data and the ECDC HIV Modeling Tool estimated that undiagnosed cases accounted for approximately 37.5 % of the total HIV population in Korea in 2020 [13]. In contrast, our own back-calculation model using National Surveillance data estimated the undiagnosed proportion to be between 14 % and 20 % (data not shown). Furthermore, the prediction model did not account for untreated or non-virally suppressed cases. Notably, 97.9 % of people living with HIV in Korea were receiving Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) in 2022, according to KNHI data analysis [34]. Viral suppression rate was 90.2 % among 4,962 PLWH from 17 hospitals in Korea in 2019 [35]. We rely on annual aggregates, which obscure short-term fluctuations. While social barriers and behavioral variables likely contribute to underreporting, we were unable to incorporate these factors into the current models.

Forty years of trend data indicate that while HIV incidence in Korea rose consistently for several decades, this growth has recently plateaued. The stacking ensemble model yielded accurate forecasts suggesting that integrating multiple algorithms can offer robust support for public health decision-making. Nonetheless, underreporting, annual data aggregation, and evolving policy contexts remain critical factors that warrant careful consideration.
