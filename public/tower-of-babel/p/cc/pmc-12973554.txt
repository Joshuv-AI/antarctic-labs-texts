Ethnobotanical documentation and conservation assessment of medicinal plants used by the Somali community in Karamara forest patches, Eastern Ethiopia
Authors: Getu Alemayehu, Ashebir Awoke, Zewdie Kassa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional medicinal knowledge is central to healthcare and cultural identity in Ethiopia, particularly in the Somali Region, where plant-based remedies are widely used. Despite its importance, this knowledge remains underdocumented. This study aims to fill this gap by documenting the medicinal plants (MPs) used by the Somali community in the Karamara forest patches of eastern Ethiopia, focusing on their cultural significance, conservation status, and role in local healthcare. It also highlights the need to preserve both the knowledge and the threatened ecosystems that support it.MethodsData were collected from 64 informants (40 men and 24 women) through semi-structured interviews, field walks, and focus group discussions conducted from February to June 2023. To evaluate the cultural importance and reliability of plant use, several ethnobotanical indices, including Use Value (UV), Informant Consensus Factor (ICF), and Fidelity Level (FL), were applied. Voucher plant specimens were gathered, identified using the Flora of Ethiopia and Eritrea, and authenticated at the National Herbarium, Addis Ababa University. The similarity of plant usage across different Ethiopian regions was assessed using Jaccard’s index. Statistical analyses, such as t-tests, ANOVA, and correlation tests, were performed using R software to examine variations in knowledge across different demographic groups.ResultsA total of 85 medicinal plant species belonging to 70 genera and 40 families were documented. Fabaceae, Solanaceae, and Asteraceae were the dominant families. Shrubs were the most frequently used growth form. The majority of remedies were prepared from leaves and administered orally. Wounds, malaria, stomach disorders, and snake bites were the most commonly treated ailments, showing high informant consensus. High ICF values were observed for dermatological (0.85) and gastrointestinal (0.83). Euphorbia abyssinica J.F.Gmel., Cyphostemma adenocaule (Steud. ex A.Rich.), and Moringa stenopetala (Baker f.) Cufod. exhibited high FL, while Cleome kelleriana (Schinz) Gilg & Gilg-Ben. and Aloe megalacantha Baker were prioritized for malaria treatment. Major threats to MPs included agricultural expansion, overharvesting, and firewood collection. Ethnobotanical knowledge varied significantly by informant groups (P < 0.05).ConclusionThe Somali community around Karamara forest possesses rich ethnomedicinal knowledge vital for local healthcare and biodiversity conservation. Sustainable management, pharmacological validation, and intergenerational knowledge transfer are urgently needed to safeguard this cultural and biological heritage.Keywords: Biodiversity conservation, Eastern ethiopia, Ethnobotany, Karamara forest, Medicinal plants, Somali community, Traditional medicine

Background

Ethnobotany explores the long-standing and often intimate relationships between people and plants, focusing on how local knowledge, cultural values, and ecological experience shape plant use, management, and conservation [1, 2]. Across generations, indigenous societies have developed sophisticated systems for recognizing, classifying, and utilizing plants based on their habitat, form, availability, and practical value [3]. Such knowledge is not static; rather, it evolves through continuous interaction with changing environments and social conditions, forming a living cultural heritage closely tied to local ecosystems [4].

Traditional medicine represents one of the most visible and enduring expressions of ethnobotanical knowledge. Through ethnomedicine, communities interpret illness and health in ways that integrate physical, social, and spiritual dimensions, drawing on locally available biological resources [5, 6]. In many parts of the developing world, traditional medicine continues to serve as the primary and, in some cases, the only accessible form of healthcare [6]. This is particularly true in Ethiopia, where limited access to modern medical facilities, especially in rural and pastoral areas, has sustained a strong reliance on plant-based remedies. It is estimated that 80–90% of the population depends on traditional MPs for primary healthcare needs [7–9]. Ethiopian healing traditions are deeply embedded in cultural belief systems and extend beyond the treatment of disease to encompass mental well-being, social harmony, and spiritual balance [10, 11].

Despite its continued importance, traditional medicinal knowledge in Ethiopia is facing increasing pressure from rapid socio-economic and environmental change [12]. Expanding agriculture, urbanization, deforestation, and shifts in lifestyle and education have disrupted customary systems of knowledge transmission, leading to a gradual decline in ethnomedicinal expertise, particularly among younger generations [13]. From an ecological perspective, this erosion has broader consequences. Indigenous knowledge often guides sustainable harvesting, seasonal use, and species selection, and its loss can accelerate biodiversity decline and weaken community-based conservation practices [14, 15].

In eastern Ethiopia, remnant forest ecosystems such as the Karamara forest patches play a vital role in sustaining both biodiversity and traditional livelihoods. These forests support the health systems of the Somali community, whose ethnomedicinal practices reflect long-term adaptation to dryland and semi-arid environments. However, the Karamara forest patches are increasingly threatened by agricultural expansion, overgrazing, charcoal production, and recurrent droughts linked to climate variability. The growing demand for medicinal plants, particularly those harvested for roots, bark, or whole plants, poses additional pressure on already fragmented habitats and places several culturally important species at risk.

Although Ethiopia is widely recognized for its ethnobotanical richness, research efforts have been unevenly distributed across regions. Most ethnomedicinal studies have focused on Oromia, Southwest Ethiopia, and the Amhara regions [16–19], while Somali communities remain poorly represented in the national ethnobotanical literature. This imbalance limits understanding of how medicinal plant knowledge varies across ecological zones and cultural settings, particularly in dryland systems where environmental constraints strongly influence plant availability and use. The Karamara forest therefore offers a valuable opportunity to document and interpret ethnomedicinal knowledge within a Somali cultural and ecological context that has received little scientific attention.

Beyond documentation, contemporary ethnobotanical research increasingly emphasizes the need to identify culturally important and ecologically vulnerable species to support ethnopharmacological research and conservation planning. Quantitative tools such as use value (UV), informant consensus factor (ICF), and fidelity level (FL) have become widely used for evaluating the relative importance, reliability, and therapeutic consistency of medicinal plant use [4, 17, 20, 21]. When combined with careful voucher specimen collection and taxonomic verification, these approaches allow ethnobotanical data to contribute meaningfully to biodiversity conservation and medicinal plant prioritization.

The documentation and analysis of ethnomedicinal knowledge also align closely with global development priorities. Traditional medicine supports affordable and culturally appropriate healthcare, contributing to Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3 on good health and well-being. At the same time, conserving medicinal plant resources and their habitats advances SDG 15 on life on land, while plant-based livelihoods can enhance household resilience and support poverty reduction under SDG 1 [11, 22, 23]. In dryland regions such as eastern Ethiopia, where ecological vulnerability and health challenges often intersect, these linkages are particularly important.

Within this broader ethnobotanical and ethnoecological context, the present study seeks to address clear gaps in knowledge concerning Somali ethnomedicine in eastern Ethiopia. By combining qualitative documentation with quantitative analysis, the study aims to generate evidence that is relevant not only for cultural preservation but also for ethnopharmacological prioritization and conservation planning.

Specifically, this study investigates the medicinal plant knowledge of the Somali community inhabiting the Karamara forest patches of eastern Ethiopia. It aims to: (i) document a vouchered and taxonomically verified inventory of medicinal plant species and examine how ethnomedicinal knowledge is distributed across socio-demographic groups; (ii) analyze patterns of plant part use, preparation methods, and routes of administration, and compare these findings with reports from other Somali-inhabited areas of Ethiopia and the Horn of Africa; (iii) evaluate the cultural importance and consensus of medicinal plant use using standard quantitative ethnobotanical indices; and (iv) assess the implications of this knowledge for strengthening primary healthcare systems, guiding conservation priorities, and supporting sustainable management of MPs in dryland forest ecosystems.

The study is guided by the following hypotheses: (i) medicinal plant knowledge is unevenly distributed across age groups and healing specialization, with elders and traditional healers possessing significantly greater knowledge than younger community members; (ii) medicinal species harvested for destructive parts such as roots, bark, and whole plants face significantly higher conservation risk than species harvested for renewable parts such as leaves and fruits; (iii) a core group of medicinal species and therapeutic applications in Karamara shows strong cross-regional similarity with other Somali communities, while forest-dependent uses reflect local ecological adaptation; and (iv) high reliance on locally available MPs is positively associated with household health resilience and pro-conservation attitudes, strengthening the link between indigenous medical systems, biodiversity stewardship, and sustainable development goals related to health and ecosystems.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The study was conducted in the Karamara forest patches, situated in the Jijiga District of the Somali Regional State, eastern Ethiopia (Fig. 1). The area lies between 9°21′–9°23′ N latitude and 42°42′–42°43′ E longitude, at elevations of 1,921–2,255 m above sea level, representing a dryland highland–lowland transition zone with marked seasonal rainfall variability and recurrent droughts. Rugged terrain and rocky substrates have allowed fragments of natural vegetation to persist, despite widespread land-use change in the surrounding landscape [24].

Ecologically, the Karamara forest patches represent a mosaic of dry evergreen Afromontane remnants that gradually transition into woodland and shrubland formations. The vegetation is largely composed of drought-tolerant woody species, shrubs, climbers, and herbaceous plants that are well adapted to shallow soils and pronounced seasonal water stress. Floristically, the area is dominated by plant families such as Fabaceae, Solanaceae, Asteraceae, Euphorbiaceae, Lamiaceae, and Burseraceae, reflecting adaptation to semi-arid environmental conditions.

The most widespread vegetation type in Karamara Forest is Acacia–Commiphora woodland and bushland, characterized by resilient tree and shrub species, particularly Acacia and Commiphora. These taxa are well suited to the region’s semi-arid climate and play a key role in maintaining ecosystem functions. The woodland supports a rich diversity of ethnomedicinal plants that are widely used by local communities for healthcare and livelihood needs [11].

Extensive shrubland areas occur throughout the forest, especially in sites influenced by grazing and other human activities. These habitats are dominated by woody shrubs and small trees and provide important resources such as fuelwood, fodder, and medicinal plants. Despite their relatively open structure, shrublands contribute substantially to the overall biodiversity of the area.

Grasslands with scattered trees are also present in open patches and seasonally grazed zones. These areas are dominated by herbaceous species and are particularly important for livestock grazing, especially during the rainy season when forage availability is high.

Vegetation associated with riverine and seasonal stream habitats is found along temporary watercourses and in low-lying areas. These sites support relatively dense and diverse plant growth, including moisture-dependent herbs, shrubs, and trees that are less common in the surrounding dryland habitats. Thus, the vegetation of Karamara Forest reflects the combined influence of semi-arid climatic conditions, grazing pressure, and human use, while retaining considerable ecological and ethnobotanical significance.

Many species serve multipurpose roles, providing medicinal, nutritional, and material resources. However, ongoing pressures such as agricultural expansion, overgrazing, firewood collection, and selective harvesting of roots and bark have led to fragmentation and ecological degradation, making the patches conservation-sensitive and several medicinally important species increasingly threatened [11].

The local population is predominantly Somali, organized into subclans sharing language, culture, and Islamic faith. Customary institutions guide social interactions, resource access, and land management. Livelihoods are mainly agro-pastoral, combining crop cultivation with livestock rearing, supplemented by small-scale trading, charcoal production, and fuelwood collection. This dependence on forest resources directly shapes the use and management of medicinal plants.

Healthcare in the region relies heavily on traditional medicine due to limited access to biomedical services, which are constrained by distance, cost, and personnel shortages. MPs harvested from the Karamara forest serve as primary remedies for a wide range of human ailments and are occasionally used alongside biomedical treatments when available. Ethnomedicinal knowledge is transmitted orally, primarily through elders, traditional healers, and knowledgeable family members, with apprenticeship and observational learning central to its maintenance. Knowledge transfer typically follows social norms and familial lines, though it is increasingly challenged by formal education, urbanization, and reduced engagement of younger generations with forest resources.

By linking ecological characteristics with sociocultural reliance, the Karamara forest patches represent a critical biocultural landscape. They provide a valuable setting for documenting medicinal plant diversity, understanding traditional healthcare practices, and informing conservation strategies that integrate biodiversity preservation with sustainable community use.

Reconnaissance Survey, study Site, and informant selection

A reconnaissance survey was conducted from 12 to 29 February 2023 to gather baseline information and guide site selection within the Karamara forest patches. Data on the district and forest ecosystem were obtained from the Jigjiga District Office of Agriculture, supplemented by consultations with local elders, kebele leaders, forest guards, and other knowledgeable community members. Jijiga district was purposively selected due to its relatively rich vegetation, high reliance on MPs, and the absence of formal conservation initiatives for these resources.

A total of 64 informants (40 men and 24 women), aged 20–80 years, participated in the study. Of these, 40 general informants were recruited through snowball sampling, targeting long-term residents recognized by their communities for extensive indigenous knowledge of MPs. An additional 24 key informants were purposively selected based on recommendations from community leaders and local experts. Key informants included traditional healers, vendors, elders, and other individuals with specialized knowledge. This approach ensured coverage of both general community knowledge and expert insights.

Informants were categorized into three age groups: young adults (18–30 years, 18.7%), middle-aged adults (31–49 years, 29.7%), and elders (50–80 years, 51.6%), following common ethnobotanical research classifications (Table 1). The sample included 62.5% males and 37.5% females, reflecting gender roles in plant collection, preparation, and use. Marital status was 62.5% married, 28.1% single, and 9.4% divorced. Educational backgrounds varied, with 70.3% illiterate and 29.7% literate. Livelihoods were diverse: 38.6% farming, 31.4% livestock keeping, 18.3% merchants, 11.7% charcoal or firewood production, and the remainder engaged in other activities.

While this sampling strategy captured a broad range of knowledge, several limitations must be acknowledged. The total sample size (N = 64) is relatively small, limiting the statistical power of demographic comparisons. Gender representation is uneven, reflecting local roles but potentially biasing findings toward male knowledge. Reliance on purposive and snowball sampling may also introduce selection bias, emphasizing individuals known for their expertise while potentially overlooking less prominent knowledge holders. These factors are considered when interpreting the results and framing conclusions.

Ethnobotanical data collection and plant identification

Ethnobotanical data in the Karamara forest patches were collected through semi-structured interviews, guided field walks, direct observations, and focus group discussions (FGDs). This integrated approach allowed thorough documentation of local knowledge on medicinal plants, including identification, preparation methods, therapeutic applications, and conservation practices, providing rich, contextually grounded information.

Semi-structured interviews targeted informant’s native to, or long-term residents of, the Karamara region, ensuring authentic and experienced perspectives. Interviews were conducted in Somali with the support of local translators, and responses were recorded in English for analysis (Fig. 2). A guiding questionnaire captured demographic data (age, sex, residence) and detailed ethnobotanical information, including vernacular names, ailments treated, plant parts used, sources (wild or cultivated), relative abundance, preparation methods, administration routes, seasonal availability, and conservation practices. The semi-structured format allowed probing for nuanced knowledge, individual experiences, and local variations, following established ethnobotanical protocols [1, 2].

Guided field walk with a key informant and on-site observations (Photo credit: Getu Alemayehu) Eight focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted with key informants to document collective indigenous knowledge and practices related to MPs. Participants engaged in open dialogue, sharing diverse experiences and perspectives

Plant specimens were collected with prior informed consent from landowners and in coordination with district administrative and agricultural offices, adhering to ethical research and conservation guidelines. Guided field walks with key informants enabled the identification and documentation of MPs in their natural habitats, with observations on morphological characteristics, growth habits, ecological context, and plant distribution. Voucher specimens were labeled, preserved via pressing, air-drying, or deep-freezing, and photographed to support taxonomic verification and create visual references (Fig. 2).

Field walks also facilitated the collection of supplementary ethnobotanical data, including preparation techniques, alternative uses, and local conservation practices. Observations highlighted threats to MPs and patterns of knowledge transfer across generations, providing an integrated understanding of both ecological and cultural dimensions of plant use.

Data collection relied primarily on self-reported information, which may be affected by recall bias or personal perception. Seasonal constraints limited access to some plant species, potentially underrepresenting seasonal or ephemeral taxa. Additionally, the oral transmission of traditional knowledge may have introduced variability in information accuracy. These limitations were mitigated through cross-validation across multiple informants, repeated field visits, and triangulation with guided observations, but they should be considered when interpreting the findings.

The discussions addressed preparation methods, dosage forms, additive ingredients, potential side effects, administration routes, and traditional systems of knowledge transfer. Participants also provided insights into management and conservation of MPs and their surrounding vegetation, offering a holistic understanding of both the cultural and ecological dimensions of plant use (Fig. 3).

Photographs illustrating a group discussion with informants (Photo credit: Getu Alemayehu)

Voucher specimens of MPs were systematically collected in collaboration with local informants and field assistants, ensuring that indigenous knowledge and ecological context were accurately captured. During fieldwork, detailed notes and photographs documented each species’ habitat, growth form, and traditional uses. Specimens were transported to the Biology Department Laboratory, College of Natural and Computational Sciences, Jigjiga University, where they were air-dried, pressed, labeled, numbered, and initially identified to the family or, when possible, species level.

Comprehensive taxonomic identification was conducted using authoritative references, including the Flora of Ethiopia and Eritrea and standard taxonomic keys. Specimens were verified at the National Herbarium (ETH), Addis Ababa University, by comparison with authenticated herbarium samples and published references [25, 26]. Scientific nomenclature followed the accepted standards of the Flora of Ethiopia and Eritrea, ensuring consistency and reliability.

To resolve potential taxonomic ambiguities, digital resources were also employed, including PlantNet, Flora Finder, PlantSnap, Google Image Search, the African Plant Database, the World Checklist of Selected Plant Families, JSTOR Global Plants, and Plants of the World Online (Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew) [3, 27]. This integrative approach combining field observation, herbarium verification, and digital tools ensured precise identification and thorough documentation of MPs in the Karamara forest patches.

All identifications were formally confirmed by Dr. Getu Alemayehu, and voucher specimens were deposited in the Mini Herbarium, Department of Biology, Jigjiga University (deposition number JJU004107). These specimens provide a permanent reference for future taxonomic studies, biodiversity assessments, and conservation planning, establishing a reliable foundation for ethnobotanical research and sustainable management of medicinal plant resources in the region.

Quantitative ethnobotanical analysis

To evaluate the reliability, depth, and consensus of traditional medicinal plant knowledge in the Karamara forest patches, several established quantitative ethnobotanical indices were applied. These included the Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC), Fidelity Level (FL), Cultural Value Index, and Informant Consensus Factor (ICF).

Participatory ranking methods, including preference ranking and direct matrix ranking, were employed to capture local perceptions of plant efficacy and prioritize species based on cultural and therapeutic importance. Additionally, similarity analyses using the Jaccard Similarity Index were conducted to compare species composition and usage patterns within the study area and with findings from other ethnobotanical studies. This integrative approach allowed for objective assessment of species significance, consensus among informants, and identification of culturally and ecologically valuable medicinal plants.

The Relative Frequency of Citation (RFC) was calculated for each medicinal plant species to evaluate its local prominence and cultural importance within the study community [28, 29]. RFC reflects the proportion of informants who mentioned a given species for the treatment of one or more human ailments and serves as an indicator of how widely known and commonly used the species is in traditional healthcare practices.

The Cultural Value Index (CVI) was calculated to quantify the overall cultural importance of each medicinal plant species within the study community [30]. The index integrates three key dimensions of ethnobotanical relevance: the frequency with which a species is cited by informants, the diversity of medicinal uses attributed to it, and the number of distinct use categories in which it is employed. By combining these elements, CVI provides a holistic measure of how deeply a plant is embedded in local traditional knowledge and everyday healthcare practices.

Preference ranking was used to evaluate the relative importance and perceived efficacy of selected MPs for specific ailments. Following the methodology of [2], ten key informants ranked five species based on their effectiveness against intestinal parasites, using a scale from 1 (least effective) to 5 (most effective). Cumulative scores established the overall ranking, highlighting species considered most efficacious by the local community [1]. This approach provided insight into community treatment priorities and perceptions of plant efficacy.

To assess multipurpose utility beyond medicinal use, direct matrix ranking was conducted. Five commonly cited tree species were evaluated by ten key informants across seven categories: medicinal value, construction material, firewood, charcoal, furniture, agricultural tools, and food. Informants scored each category from 1 (least important) to 5 (most important). Summed scores identified species valued for both therapeutic and socio-economic roles, informing conservation priorities and highlighting the broader importance of MPs in rural livelihoods [1, 2].

Data analysis

A mixed-methods approach combining qualitative and quantitative analyses was used. Qualitative data local plant names, ailments, and preparation methods were coded, categorized according to standard ethnomedicinal classifications, and validated through cross-checking with multiple informants. Thematic analysis identified patterns of plant use and cultural knowledge.

Quantitative data were summarized using descriptive statistics and ethnobotanical indices (e.g., Informant Consensus Factor, Use Value) [4, 6]. Statistical analyses were conducted in R (v4.4.3), with normality assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test. Differences in indices across age, gender, and informant type were evaluated using ANOVA or non-parametric alternatives as appropriate. Pearson correlation and linear regression examined relationships between age and the number of MPs reported [4, 6]. This integrated framework provided a robust understanding of medicinal plant knowledge, its demographic determinants, and regional patterns of use.

Ethical considerations

The study received official approval from the Department of Biology, Jigjiga University, and the administrative offices of Jigjiga district. Research was conducted in accordance with established ethnobotanical ethical guidelines. Prior informed consent was obtained from all participants after explaining the study’s objectives, methods, and voluntary nature. Confidentiality was maintained, and personal identifiers were removed from the dataset. Special care was taken to respect cultural sensitivities, particularly regarding sacred or ritually significant plants. The study adhered to international ethical standards, including the Code of Ethics of the International Society of Ethnobiology (ISE), ensuring the protection and respectful use of indigenous knowledge.

Results

Diversity and distribution of medicinal plants in Karamara forest patches

A total of 85 medicinal plant species belonging to 70 genera and 40 families were documented in the Karamara forest patches (Table 2). Among these, Fabaceae was the most dominant family, followed by Solanaceae, Asteraceae, Euphorbiaceae, Lamiaceae and Burseraceae. Several other families were represented by a single species, highlighting the heterogeneous composition of the forest’s medicinal flora (Fig. 4).

Major human ailments and associated medicinal plants

In the Karamara forest patches, 85 medicinal plant species were used to treat 54 human ailments (Table 2). The most commonly addressed health problems included wounds, malaria, toothache, snake bites, abdominal pain, and kidney disorders, with wounds treated by the highest number of species, followed by malaria, digestive disorders, snake bites, and eye problems (Fig. 6).

Major human ailments and the plant species used by local people

MPs were employed across a wide range of conditions, including gastrointestinal, respiratory, dermatological, urogenital, parasitic, musculoskeletal, and neurological disorders.

Gastrointestinal and digestive ailments were the most frequently treated. Species such as Acacia nilotica, Acacia nubica, Acacia senegal, Crotalaria jijigensis, Ehretia cymosa, Ocimum lamiifolium, and Ziziphus spina-christi were used for gastritis, abdominal pain, constipation, diarrhea, and stomachache.

Dermatological disorders and wounds were managed with Abutilon fruticosum, Cadaba glandulosa, Cynoglossum amabile, Euphorbia abyssinica, Jasminum grandiflorum, Solanum coagulans, and Tagetes minuta, typically applied topically for wounds, rashes, ringworm, and insect bites.

Respiratory ailments, including coughs and colds, were treated with Aloe megalacantha, Jatropha aceroides, Ocimum lamiifolium, and Withania somnifera.

Urogenital and reproductive issues were addressed using Vachellia bussei, Otostegia modesta, and Maytenus arbutifolia, for urogenital infections, menstrual disorders, and kidney problems.

Parasitic and microbial infections were managed with Carica papaya, Dovyalis abyssinica, Psidium guajava, and Opuntia ficus-indica, targeting intestinal worms, amoebiasis, and ectoparasites.

Musculoskeletal and neurological conditions were treated with Amaranthus dubius for swollen joints, Commiphora myrrha for brittle bones, and Withania somnifera for epilepsy, highlighting remedies for chronic or systemic ailments.

Additional common treatments included malaria, using Aloe megalacantha, Aloe pirottae, Cleome kelleriana, Eucalyptus camaldulensis, Tamarindus indica, and Lawsonia inermis; toothache, with Acacia nilotica, Clerodendrum myricoides, and Solanum spina-christi; headache, with Commiphora hodai and Kleinia neriifolia; and poisoning or spiritually attributed conditions, with Calotropis procera, Punica granatum, and Datura stramonium.

Plant parts used for medicinal purposes

Traditional remedies in the Karamara forest patches employed a variety of plant parts, including leaves, roots, bark, latex, seeds, fruits, stems, flowers, and whole plants. Leaves were the most commonly used, followed by roots and latex, reflecting their accessibility, ease of processing, and high concentration of bioactive compounds. Other parts fruits, seeds, stems, whole plants, and bark were used less frequently (Fig. 7), highlighting a preference for plant tissues that balance therapeutic efficacy with sustainable harvesting practices.

Methods of medicinal plant Preparation

The local community of the Karamara forest employs a diverse array of preparation techniques tailored to the plant part, therapeutic target, and species characteristics.

The most frequently used method is crushing or homogenizing plant parts with water, applied to leaves, roots, or fruits of species such as Aloe megalacantha, Carica papaya, Cissus quadrangularis, Leucas jamesii, Moringa stenopetala, and Ricinus communis. This approach is commonly used to prepare decoctions, infusions, or juices for oral administration, primarily targeting gastrointestinal, respiratory, and febrile ailments.

Topical application of crushed or pounded material is widely practiced for species including Abutilon fruticosum, Amaranthus dubius, Tagetes minuta, Cynoglossum amabile, and Verbascum sinaiticum. The resulting paste is applied directly to wounds, insect bites, or skin infections, underscoring the importance of external treatments in local ethnomedicine.

Direct consumption or chewing of plant parts, such as gums, seeds, fruits, or leaves, is common for species like Acacia nilotica, Acacia nubica, Cordeauxia edulis, Dovyalis abyssinica, Myrsine africana, Opuntia ficus-indica, and Psidium guajava. Chewing is used for digestive support, musculoskeletal relief, or nutritional supplementation.

Boiling and drinking decoctions is another prevalent method, applied to Argemone mexicana, Brassica oleracea, Eucalyptus camaldulensis, Ruta chalepensis, Withania somnifera, and Ziziphus spina-christi. This technique extracts bioactive compounds from leaves, roots, or fruits, targeting internal conditions such as fever, gastrointestinal disorders, and systemic infections.

Latex, sap, or gum extraction is employed for species such as Vachellia bussei, Acacia senegal, Boswellia rivae, Euphorbia tirucalli, Opuntia ficus-indica, and Ziziphus mauritiana. The extracted material is applied directly or combined with other substances for topical treatments, including wound healing, skin infections, and inflammation.

Inhalation of smoke or steam from crushed, boiled, or burned plant parts is practiced with species such as Calotropis procera, Cleome kelleriana, Cyphostemma adenocaule, and Eucalyptus globulus, primarily for respiratory relief and alleviation of nasal or chest congestion.

Other preparation techniques include mixing with milk (Punica granatum), tying crushed powder on wounds (Solanum jubae), concoctions for oral ingestion (Foeniculum vulgare, Vachellia tortilis), and gradual release through chewing or holding plant parts in the mouth (Acacia nubica, Melia azedarach). These diverse methods reflect a sophisticated understanding of plant properties and local health needs.

Routes and methods of administration

Medicinal plant remedies in the Karamara forest patches were applied through multiple routes, reflecting their diverse therapeutic uses. Oral administration was the most common, followed by dermal applications, with less frequent use via nasal, ocular, anal, and urogenital routes (Fig. 8). This pattern underlines the community’s practical approach, selecting administration routes based on ailment type, plant preparation, and desired therapeutic effect.

Route of medicinal plants administration

Oral administration was primarily used for treating gastrointestinal disorders, parasitic infections, malaria, and systemic ailments. Species frequently administered orally included Aloe pirottae, Asepalum eriantherum, Boswellia rivae, Commiphora kua, Crotalaria jijigensis, Indigastrum costatum, Iphionopsis rotundifolia, and Pistacia aethiopica. Remedies were prepared as decoctions, infusions, crushed materials, or plant juices.

Dermal application was the second most common route, targeting wounds, skin infections, swelling, and insect bites. Plants such as Abutilon fruticosum, Cucumis pustulatus, Dodonaea angustifolia, Euphorbia abyssinica, Ficus bussei, Ocimum lamiifolium, and Solanum somalense were applied topically as crushed leaves, latex, sap, powders, or poultices.

Ocular administration was reported for species like Aloe pirottae, Leucas jamesii, and Solanum coagulans, mainly for eye infections and pain, using freshly extracted juices or squeezed leaves.

Urogenital administration was limited to species such as Vachellia bussei, used to treat urogenital infections, while anal administration of Heliotropium linariifolium addressed constipation and rectal disorders.

Several species were employed via multiple routes, depending on the target ailment or plant part used. For instance, Aloe pirottae was administered both orally and ocularly, whereas species like Solanum coagulans and Asepalum eriantherum could be applied either orally or dermally. These practices highlight the community’s nuanced understanding of plant properties and their versatile applications in traditional healthcare.

Use of fresh versus dried plant materials

In the Karamara forest patches, medicinal plant species were utilized either fresh, dried, or in both forms, depending on plant part, availability, and intended application. The majority of plant preparations (87 species, 76.9%) relied on fresh materials, while 23 species (20.3%) were used in dried form, and 2 species (1.7%) were employed in both fresh and dried forms.

Species such as Abutilon fruticosum, Acacia nubica, Vachellia tortilis, Aloe megalacantha, Dodonaea angustifolia, Eucalyptus globulus, Eucalyptus camaldulensis, Ocimum lamiifolium, Solanum coagulans, and Ziziphus spina-christi were predominantly used fresh. Fresh materials, especially leaves, fruits, and latex, were preferred for treating wounds, digestive disorders, malaria, eye problems, and respiratory ailments, as they retain higher bioactive potency and are readily available for immediate use.

Conversely, species such as Clerodendrum myricoides, Coffea arabica, Datura stramonium, Myrsine africana, Ricinus communis, Solanum incanum, Tamarindus indica, and Withania somnifera were primarily used dried. Drying was mostly applied to roots, seeds, stems, and bark, which were subsequently boiled, crushed, or powdered for long-term storage, decoctions, or oral administration.

Several species demonstrated dual usage, being applied either fresh or dried depending on seasonal availability and therapeutic purpose. Examples include Acacia nilotica, Argemone mexicana, Calotropis procera, Solanum coagulans, Solanum incanum, Aloe pirottae, and Ziziphus spina-christi. This flexibility reflects the community’s adaptive knowledge in maximizing the efficacy and accessibility of MPs throughout the year.

Dosage, Additives, and diagnostic practices in medicinal plant use

In the Karamara forest patches, the dosage of herbal medicines varied according to the type of disease, the patient’s age, and overall health condition. Traditional practitioners determined doses using locally available measures, including liters, spoons, tea cups, coffee cups, water glasses, or even the palm of the hand, adapting quantities to the patient and the ailment’s severity.

Diagnosis was primarily based on careful observation and patient consultation. Healers assessed symptoms through interviews with patients or their attendants, while visually examining changes in eyes, urine, skin color, tongue, and throat. Other indicators included body temperature, swelling, edema, coughing, bleeding, diarrhea, vomiting, presence of parasites, and sores, allowing practitioners to prescribe appropriate treatments and adjust dosages according to the duration and intensity of the illness.

Herbal remedies were used with or without additives, depending on the preparation and desired effect. Most plants were administered without additional ingredients. However, certain remedies incorporated water, milk, coffee, honey, or meat to enhance therapeutic efficacy. Cold water was the most common solvent, used for homogenizing, preparing decoctions, or drinking, as seen with species such as Aloe megalacantha, Croton macrostachyus, and Withania somnifera. Milk was occasionally employed, for instance with Punica granatum, particularly for spiritual ailments.

Topical applications frequently involved plant oils, juices, or latex, directly applied to affected areas, as observed with Ziziphus mauritiana, Jasminum grandiflorum, and Boscia angustifolia. Additionally, some species, including Calotropis procera, Cleome kelleriana, and Eucalyptus globulus, were burned or heated to produce smoke for inhalation, primarily for respiratory conditions, spiritual purposes, or pain relief.

Traditional healers also emphasized the use of antidotes to mitigate potential adverse effects of medicinal preparations, reflecting a sophisticated understanding of safety and efficacy in local ethnomedicine.

Marketability of medicinal plants

MPs in the Karamara forest patches demonstrate substantial market potential, reflecting both their therapeutic importance and cultural acceptance. Informants reported that numerous species are actively traded in local markets. Commonly sold MPs include Senna italica, Eucalyptus globulus, Pistacia aethiopica, Crotalaria jijigensis, Commiphora myrrha, Lippia savoryi, Clerodendrum myricoides, and Dodonaea angustifolia. Other species, such as Zingiber officinale, Capsicum annuum, Carica papaya, Dovyalis abyssinica, Moringa stenopetala, and Ruta chalepensis, are marketed primarily for their food and nutritional value, highlighting the multifunctional utility of local plants.

Certain species Aloe megalacantha, Calotropis procera, Ocimum lamiifolium, and Ziziphus spina-christi showed high citation frequencies, indicating both frequent use and strong local demand. These species are prized for treating common ailments, including hemorrhoids, malaria, cough, common cold, and stomachache, making them commercially viable for traditional healers and small-scale herbal traders.

The marketability of plants is influenced by the plant parts used and preparation methods. Leaves, roots, latex, and fruits are the most frequently traded materials, commonly processed through crushing, decoction, infusion, or extraction. For example, Cucumis prophetarum and Cucumis pustulatus are sold as dried or powdered preparations for wound and hemorrhoid treatment, facilitating storage and transport. Similarly, Commiphora myrrha and Ficus bussei are marketed either for topical application or direct chewing, demonstrating adaptability for both raw and processed forms.

Marketable species are predominantly trees and shrubs, owing to their larger biomass and year-round availability. Species such as Acacia nilotica, Moringa stenopetala, and Boswellia rivae hold particular promise as sustainable economic resources. The potential for commercialization is also influenced by ease of cultivation and regeneration. Fast-growing herbs with high turnover, including Argemone mexicana, Bidens pilosa, and Lippia savoryi, provide sustainable trade options, whereas slower-growing or seasonally available species, such as Acacia senegal and Commiphora myrrha, require regulated harvesting to maintain ecological balance and continuous market supply.

Informant consensus factor (ICF) and fidelity level (FL) of medicinal plants used for human ailments

The Informant Consensus Factor (ICF) was employed to explore patterns of agreement among local informants regarding the use of medicinal plants for different categories of human ailments. While ICF is widely applied in ethnobotanical studies, several authors, including Leonti, have emphasized that this index has important conceptual and methodological limitations. In particular, ICF does not measure pharmacological efficacy and may be influenced by sample size, informant selection, and cultural transmission processes rather than therapeutic effectiveness. Accordingly, in this study ICF is interpreted cautiously as a descriptive indicator of shared cultural knowledge and local agreement, rather than as evidence of medicinal efficacy.

Novel ethnobotanical findings from Karamara forest patches

The ethnobotanical survey of the Somali community in Karamara forest patches revealed several novel medicinal uses, plant-part applications, and preparation methods, expanding the knowledge of Ethiopia’s traditional medicinal flora. Several species exhibited therapeutic applications not previously reported in the region. For instance, Withania somnifera roots are used to manage common cold, while leaves are administered for epilepsy, demonstrating a dual-purpose therapeutic role. Similarly, Opuntia ficus-indica is ingested to treat amoebic infections, Cadaba glandulosa leaves are applied topically for skin infections, and Iphionopsis rotundifolia roots are chewed for gastric disorders, all representing new ethnomedicinal uses for the region.

Several species showed novel combinations of plant parts and preparation methods. Moringa stenopetala leaves are consumed to manage hypertension in addition to their nutritional role, reflecting an integrated food-medicine function. Ziziphus mauritiana leaves and latex are applied topically for ringworm, while fruits are ingested for gastrointestinal complaints, highlighting dual-use practices previously unrecorded in eastern Ethiopia. Ocimum lamiifolium leaves are inhaled to alleviate cough, common cold, and headache, illustrating a novel route of administration. Additionally, Clerodendrum myricoides roots are chewed to relieve toothache, showing a localized adaptation of known therapeutic applications.

Several under-studied taxa were documented with new medicinal applications. Cadaba glandulosa, Iphionopsis rotundifolia, Opilia campestris, and Asepalum eriantherum are used for ailments ranging from gastrointestinal disorders to wound healing and snakebite management. Resinous species such as Boswellia rivae, Commiphora myrrha, and Commiphora hodai are applied for snakebites, headaches, and gastrointestinal issues, revealing specific therapeutic roles not previously highlighted in Ethiopian literature.

The survey also highlighted dual-use species, demonstrating an important interface between nutrition and healthcare. Plants such as Opuntia ficus-indica, Punica granatum, Psidium guajava, Moringa stenopetala, and Ziziphus mauritiana serve both as food and medicine, supporting household nutritional security while contributing to traditional healthcare. Such integrated use has not been previously documented in dryland forest systems of eastern Ethiopia, emphasizing the multifunctional role of medicinal flora in local livelihoods.

Finally, several taxa are used for culturally specific and ritualistic purposes. Calotropis procera is burned or inhaled to ward off evil spirits, and Punica granatum is ingested to counteract culturally recognized spiritual afflictions. These practices highlight the cultural and psychosocial importance of medicinal plants, reflecting their role in maintaining social identity and traditional knowledge systems in Karamara.

Indigenous knowledge transfer and medicinal plant collection

The majority of informants reported that most ailments are treated at the family level using self-prepared remedies. When necessary, community members may consult other knowledgeable individuals with minimal or no cost. Notably, a substantial proportion of informants approximately 80% and 75% in different parts of the district indicated that they kept their medicinal plant knowledge secret, and its transfer was largely restricted to family members. Typically, the knowledge holder (often a father or mother) selects a child usually a son based on specific personal criteria for knowledge inheritance. Transfer of knowledge to individuals outside the family generally required substantial compensation. Only a small number of practitioners openly taught medicinal plant use to all family members, while some were unwilling to pass on their knowledge even within the household. Interestingly, about 10% of informants reported that their knowledge was revealed through dreams, reflecting spiritual dimensions of ethnomedicine.

Most informants (80%) expressed concern that vertical transmission of traditional medicinal knowledge is declining, primarily due to the younger generation’s reduced interest, influenced by acculturation and modernization. According to 90% of respondents, the transfer of indigenous plant knowledge occurs orally rather than through written documentation, underscoring the fragility of this knowledge system.

The timing of medicinal plant collection varied considerably among practitioners and was largely guided by individual beliefs. Approximately 30% preferred collecting plants before 4:00 p.m., while 70% favored early morning (up to 9:00 a.m.) or late afternoon (after 4:00 p.m.). Some practitioners considered nighttime collection suitable, whereas others emphasized early morning or early afternoon.

Local beliefs also shaped the collection and application of medicinal plants. For instance, some remedies were to be collected only by single females or by males, based on the conviction that deviation from these practices would reduce the efficacy of the herbal medicine. Such practices reflect the integration of cultural norms and spiritual considerations into traditional healthcare in the Karamara forest patches.

Management and conservation of medicinal plants

The Somali community in the Karamara forest patches has developed various strategies to manage and conserve medicinal plants, ensuring their sustainable use. Some species, such as Ocimum lamiifolium, Aloe megalacantha, Moringa stenopetala, and Calotropis procera, are actively cultivated in home gardens because of their frequent use, multiple applications, and ease of propagation. Community members reported practicing careful harvesting to avoid harming plant populations: leaves and fruits are collected without damage, roots and bark are taken only from mature plants to allow regrowth, and latex or resins are tapped without uprooting the species. These practices are especially emphasized for high-value plants like Commiphora myrrha, Aloe megalacantha, and Vachellia bussei.

Community-based conservation measures include restricting grazing in areas rich in medicinal plants, protecting culturally important or sacred sites from clearing, and replanting harvested species such as Moringa stenopetala and Calotropis procera. The transfer of ethnomedicinal knowledge from elders to younger generations is crucial, teaching proper plant identification, preparation methods, and sustainable collection techniques. Cultural norms further reinforce conservation by discouraging the harvesting of rare or slow-growing species. Based on informant consensus and frequency of use, priority species for conservation include Calotropis procera, Aloe megalacantha, Commiphora myrrha, Vachellia bussei, and Ocimum lamiifolium. These plants are protected both within the forest (in situ) and cultivated in home gardens (ex situ), reflecting an integration of traditional knowledge with ecological stewardship.

Implications of medicinal plant use on health, food security, environmental protection, and cultural value

The medicinal flora of the Karamara forest patches plays a central role in local primary healthcare, addressing most of the commonly reported health problems in the community. The majority of recorded species are used to treat infectious diseases, gastrointestinal disorders, respiratory illnesses, skin conditions, musculoskeletal problems, and febrile ailments. Widely used species such as Aloe spp., Withania somnifera, Ocimum lamiifolium, Clerodendrum myricoides, Ruta chalepensis, and Ziziphus spp. show high informant agreement, indicating their strong therapeutic relevance. The continued dependence on plant-based remedies reflects both the accessibility of these resources and the limited availability and affordability of formal healthcare services. At the same time, the use of biologically potent and potentially toxic species such as Datura stramonium, Calotropis procera, Euphorbia spp., and Ricinus communis demonstrates the importance of specialized indigenous knowledge for safe dosage and preparation, highlighting both the strength and vulnerability of the traditional medical system.

MPs also make an important contribution to household food security and nutrition. Several species provide edible fruits, leaves, seeds, or pods that supplement daily diets, especially during periods of drought and food scarcity. Species such as Ziziphus mauritiana, Ziziphus spina-christi, Opuntia ficus-indica, Tamarindus indica, Punica granatum, Psidium guajava, Amaranthus dubius, and Moringa stenopetala were frequently cited as both food and medicine, reflecting their dual nutritional and therapeutic value. In addition, fodder-providing trees such as Vachellia tortilis, Acacia nilotica, and Acacia senegal indirectly strengthen food security by sustaining livestock, which forms the backbone of local livelihoods. These findings indicate that MPs are not used solely for healthcare but are deeply integrated into broader household survival and nutritional strategies.

From an environmental perspective, most medicinal species are harvested from wild forest patches, shrublands, and riverine habitats, showing a strong dependence on natural ecosystems. Multipurpose species such as Boswellia rivae, Commiphora myrrha, Vachellia tortilis, Ficus spp., and Pistacia aethiopica provide overlapping medicinal, ecological, and economic functions, including soil stabilization, microclimate regulation, wildlife habitat, and resin or fuel production. However, species harvested for roots, bark, and resin were consistently perceived as being under higher harvesting pressure and declining availability, particularly in fragmented forest patches. In contrast, species harvested mainly for renewable parts such as leaves, fruits, and flowers showed greater local sustainability. These results demonstrate a clear link between harvesting strategy, ecosystem degradation, and conservation risk.

Medicinal plant use in Karamara is also strongly embedded in cultural identity and social organization. Many species carry ritual, spiritual, and symbolic significance alongside their therapeutic functions, particularly incense-producing taxa such as Boswellia and Commiphora, cosmetic plants such as Lawsonia inermis, and culturally protected trees such as Ficus and Ziziphus. Ethnomedicinal knowledge is predominantly held by elders and traditional healers and is transmitted orally across generations. However, informants widely reported a decline in youth participation in traditional healing practices due to formal education, urban influence, and increased reliance on pharmaceutical medicine. Together, these results show that MPs in the Karamara forest patches simultaneously support public health, food security, environmental stability, and cultural continuity, while also facing growing pressure from ecological degradation and weakening knowledge transmission systems.

Discussion

Diversity and growth forms of medicinal plants in Karamara forest patches

The Karamara forest patches harbor a remarkable diversity of ethnomedicinal plants, comprising 85 species across 40 families. Fabaceae was the most dominant family, followed by Solanaceae, Asteraceae, Euphorbiaceae, Lamiaceae, and Burseraceae, consistent with ethnobotanical surveys across Ethiopia and East Africa [12, 14, 35]. Fabaceae’s prevalence likely reflects both ecological abundance and pharmacological potential, making it accessible and culturally significant. The prominence of these families may also reflect shared ethnobotanical knowledge and chemical diversity, highlighting their potential for pharmacological screening and novel therapeutic discovery [48, 49, 57].

Tropical forests like Karamara serve as reservoirs of traditional knowledge and natural resources, contributing to roughly one-quarter of modern pharmaceuticals [4, 8, 26]. The high representation of Fabaceae and Asteraceae aligns with their prominence in Ethiopian flora [61], while studies elsewhere emphasize the ecological and medicinal importance of Asteraceae [62, 63]. This dual ecological and therapeutic value suggests that local plant selection is guided by both availability and proven efficacy.

Comparative studies show convergence and regional variation in medicinal plant use. Across Africa and Asia, Fabaceae and Asteraceae rank among the top medicinal families, reflecting ecological dominance and integration into traditional healthcare [64, 65]. In Europe, aromatic families such as Asteraceae and Lamiaceae are emphasized [66], whereas Latin American inventories highlight Asteraceae, Fabaceae, and Solanaceae, influenced by ecosystem and cultural preferences [66]. Thus, medicinal plant use is shaped by both biodiversity and cultural knowledge.

Ecological and methodological factors can influence reported diversity. Habitat type, forest fragmentation, sampling intensity, and inclusion of cultivated species affect species richness [67, 68]. Studies that include cultivated taxa report higher diversity, while those limited to forest patches often emphasize woody and resinous species, explaining the prominence of Burseraceae in Karamara.

These findings have implications for conservation, pharmacology, and public health. Dominant families such as Fabaceae, Asteraceae, Solanaceae, Lamiaceae, and Euphorbiaceae are promising targets for phytochemical research. Conservation strategies should prioritize ecologically restricted and culturally important taxa, particularly resinous Burseraceae species. Standardized ethnopharmacological protocols can improve study comparability, strengthen sustainable utilization, and support evidence-based integration of MPs into local healthcare systems [33, 35, 64].

Policy recognition of these plant families can inform environmental management strategies that protect biodiversity and ecosystem services. Community-based cultivation, habitat restoration, and regulated harvesting can maintain access to culturally relevant medicines while preserving ecological integrity. Linking ethnobotanical knowledge with policy and management fosters resilient healthcare systems, biodiversity conservation, and cultural heritage preservation in semi-arid forests.

Analysis of growth forms showed shrubs were most dominant, followed by trees, herbs, and climbers, reflecting patterns in other Ethiopian studies [37, 38, 41]. For example, in Addi Arkay District, shrubs accounted for 37.5% of medicinal species, herbs 36.6%, trees 17%, and climbers 9% [14], while in Sheka Zone, herbs were most prevalent [56]. Shrubs and herbs are accessible, abundant, fast-growing, and species-rich, making them practical for frequent use [6, 27, 55, 69].

Similar trends occur beyond Ethiopia. In Sulaymaniyah Province, Iraq, herbs made up 75% of medicinal species [70], while in Mana Angetu District [71] and Tigray [72], shrubs dominated. Continental comparisons show herbs are commonly used in Uganda (41% of species) [73], shrubs and herbs in South China [64], and herbaceous/shrubby species in Latin America [66]. Differences in dominant growth forms reflect vegetation type, resource availability, and cultural adaptation [74, 75].

In Karamara, shrub dominance may also result from ecological disturbance and forest fragmentation, as canopy openings favor shrub proliferation, contrasting with herb-dominated floras in less disturbed sites [62, 75]. Herbs and shrubs dominate across Africa and Asia due to ecological flexibility, accessibility, and regenerative capacity. Shorter life cycles and ease of propagation make them practical for traditional use, whereas trees and climbers are less accessible and slower to regenerate [6, 7, 14, 56, 61].

Understanding growth form distribution aids conservation and sustainable use. Identifying heavily exploited growth forms can guide strategies to prevent overharvesting and maintain species diversity, safeguarding the ethnomedicinal heritage of the Somali community and other indigenous populations reliant on forest resources for primary healthcare [64, 65].

Major human ailments and medicinal plant species used by local people

In the Karamara forest patches, the Somali ethnic community uses 85 medicinal plant species to treat 54 human ailments. The most frequently addressed conditions include wounds, malaria, gastrointestinal disorders, snakebites, eye pain, and toothaches. This pattern aligns with findings from other parts of Ethiopia, where gastrointestinal and dermatological problems are commonly managed through traditional medicine. For instance, studies from the Sheka Zone reported similar plant use for digestive and skin-related conditions, highlighting the enduring role of traditional medicine in Ethiopian healthcare systems [6, 33, 56].

The diversity of ailments treated in Karamara is relatively high compared to other Ethiopian studies. For example [76], documented 48 plant species for 47 ailments [77], recorded 77 species for 49 ailments, and [78] reported 78 species for 50 diseases. The higher number of ailments addressed in Karamara reflects both the rich medicinal plant diversity and the extensive ethnomedicinal knowledge of the Somali community.

Similar therapeutic patterns are observed across Africa. In Uganda [79], documented plant species used for gastrointestinal disorders and skin diseases, echoing the focus in Karamara. In Nigeria and neighboring countries, MPs are commonly employed to manage wounds, malaria, and digestive ailments [80], suggesting a shared ethnobotanical heritage and convergent adaptation of plant remedies across African communities.

Comparable trends extend to Asia. In Bangladesh [81], recorded numerous plants traditionally used for dermatological disorders, while in China [64, 82, 83], reported plants employed against gastrointestinal and respiratory conditions. European studies mirror these patterns; in Spain [65], noted MPs frequently used for gastrointestinal and dermatological conditions. Similarly, in the Americas, indigenous groups have long utilized plants to treat wounds, digestive disorders, and respiratory problems [84].

The recurring use of plants for common ailments worldwide highlights a universal reliance on traditional remedies, reflecting both empirical effectiveness and the shared biocultural evolution of medicinal knowledge. Preserving such ethnobotanical wisdom is essential for maintaining cultural heritage and for the discovery of novel pharmacologically active compounds.

Within Karamara, the Somali community identifies A. megalacantha and C. procera as key medicinal species, each used to treat four distinct ailments [85, 86]. A. megalacantha is traditionally employed in Ethiopia for wounds, dandruff, malaria, diabetes, impotence, and colon cleansing [87], while C. procera is commonly used for warts and possesses notable antibacterial and antifungal properties [88].

The use of these species in Karamara is consistent with ethnobotanical reports across Africa, where Aloe species are frequently applied to manage diabetes mellitus, malaria, gastrointestinal disorders, pain, inflammation, and snakebites [87, 89–91]. Their versatility and accessibility make Aloe among the most cited plants in African traditional medicine. Beyond Africa, Aloe species are widely used in Asia to treat amoebiasis, cough, earache, fever, hernia, malaria, ringworm, stomachache, and swellings [89, 90]. European practices similarly employ Aloe for wound healing and gastrointestinal problems [89], and in the Americas, indigenous communities have long used A. vera for skin treatment and general healing [89, 91].

The consistent use of A. megalacantha and C. procera across diverse ecological and cultural contexts emphasizes their pharmacological potential and ethnobotanical significance. Their widespread application for similar ailments worldwide suggests convergent medicinal knowledge shaped by empirical observation and cultural exchange. Future pharmacological investigations are warranted to validate their traditional uses, identify active compounds, and support their integration into evidence-based healthcare systems [85, 86].

Plant parts Used, methods of Preparation, and routes of administration

In the Karamara forest patches, the Somali ethnic community predominantly uses leaves for medicinal preparations, followed by roots and latex. This trend aligns with ethnobotanical studies across Ethiopia and beyond, where leaves are most frequently utilized [3, 12, 14, 16, 33, 39]. Similar patterns have been reported in Nigeria [91, 92], India [93–95], Tanzania, and Uganda [79, 96], reflecting a global preference for leaves and roots.

Leaves are favored because they are easily accessible, require minimal processing, and are rich in bioactive compounds such as alkaloids, flavonoids, and phenolics. Leaf harvesting is also more sustainable than root or bark extraction, allowing plants to regenerate and reducing long-term ecological impact. However, in some Ethiopian and Indian studies [71, 76, 95, 97–100], roots were more commonly used, highlighting how local flora, disease prevalence, and cultural practices shape plant part selection.

Certain species, such as C. gigantea, are valued for multiple parts leaves, roots, and latex which are incorporated into remedies [93, 101]. Similarly, in Spain [102] and across the Americas [103, 104], leaves and roots dominate traditional preparations, illustrating a universal ethnomedicinal preference for pharmacologically potent and accessible plant parts.

The Somali community in Karamara employs diverse preparation methods. Crushing and homogenizing with water is the most common, producing extracts administered orally as decoctions, infusions, or juices. Species prepared this way include A. megalacantha, C. papaya, C. quadrangularis, L. jamesii, M. stenopetala, and R. communis, primarily for gastrointestinal, respiratory, and febrile conditions [21, 27, 64].

Most remedies (77.33%) involve single plant species, while 22.66% use mixtures. This predominance of single-species preparations mirrors other Ethiopian studies [3, 17, 27, 45, 56, 105], reflecting confidence in the efficacy of specific taxa.

Topical applications involve crushing, pounding, or grinding plant materials into a paste. Species such as A. fruticosum, A. dubius, T. minuta, C. amabile, and V. sinaiticum are applied to wounds, skin infections, or insect bites [106]. Chewing or direct oral consumption is also common, particularly for A. nilotica, A. nubica, C. edulis, D. abyssinica, M. africana, O. ficus-indica, and P. guajava, mainly for digestive issues, musculoskeletal pain, and nutritional deficiencies [94].

Decoctions are widely used for species such as (A) mexicana, (B) oleracea, E. camaldulensis, R. chalepensis, W. somnifera, and Z. spina-christi, as boiling enhances extraction of bioactive compounds for internal ailments [107]. Latex, sap, and gum preparations from species like V. bussei, (A) senegal, (B) rivae, E. tirucalli, O. ficus-indica, and Z. mauritiana are applied topically for wound healing, skin infections, and inflammation [108]. Inhalation therapies, using crushed, boiled, or burned plant parts (e.g., (C) procera, C. kelleriana, C. adenocaule, E. globulus), target respiratory ailments, reflecting practices common in Asian ethnomedicine [70].

Additional methods include mixing extracts with milk (P. granatum), tying crushed powders to wounds (S. jubae), and chewing plant parts (A. nubica, M. azedarach) to gradually release bioactive compounds. These diverse preparation techniques demonstrate the community’s deep empirical understanding of plant chemistry and therapeutic efficacy.

Medicinal preparations are administered through multiple routes. Oral administration is dominant, mainly for gastrointestinal disorders, malaria, parasitic infections, and fever, using decoctions, infusions, or juices [4, 6, 27, 54, 55, 58, 71, 73, 109]. Topical application is the second most common, primarily for wounds, skin infections, inflammation, and bites, using species such as A. fruticosum, D. angustifolia, and O. lamiifolium [92, 110–112].

Less frequent routes include ocular (A. pirottae, S. coagulans) [21, 101] and urogenital or anal applications (V. bussei, H. linariifolium) [107, 108]. Some species are versatile, administered through multiple routes depending on the ailment and plant part, such as A. pirottae (oral and ocular) and S. coagulans (oral and dermal).

Thus, oral and dermal routes predominate in Somali traditional medicine, reflecting both therapeutic efficacy and cultural significance. The consistency of these administration patterns across global ethnomedicinal systems emphasizes a shared empirical reasoning in traditional healthcare. Understanding these practices preserves indigenous knowledge and informs future pharmacological validation and drug development.

Use of fresh versus dried plant materials in Karamara forest patches

In the Karamara forest patches, the majority of MPs were used in fresh form, while 20.3% were used dried, and a small proportion (1.7%) was employed in both forms. Fresh materials particularly leaves, fruits, and latex were preferred for treating wounds, digestive ailments, malaria, eye problems, and respiratory disorders. Commonly utilized species included A. fruticosum, A. nubica, V. tortilis, A. megalacantha, D. angustifolia, E. globulus, O. lamiifolium, and Z. spina-christi.

The preference for fresh plant parts reflects a widespread traditional belief that freshly harvested materials are more potent due to their higher content of active secondary metabolites, which may degrade during drying. Similar patterns have been documented across Ethiopia, where fresh leaves and latex are commonly used for both topical and internal treatments because of their perceived superior efficacy and immediate availability [3, 17, 33, 71, 76].

Dried plant materials were primarily used for roots, seeds, stems, and bark, which are often powdered, boiled, or crushed before administration. Drying facilitates storage, transportation, and preparation of decoctions or powders for long-term use. Species such as C. myricoides, C. arabica, D. stramonium, R. communis, T. indica, and W. somnifera were typically used in dried form. Comparable practices are reported in Kenya and Nigeria, where dried roots and bark are preferred for oral remedies targeting gastrointestinal and systemic disorders [92, 110].

Certain species were used both fresh and dried, depending on seasonal availability and intended therapeutic purpose. For example, A. nilotica, A. mexicana, C. procera, S. coagulans, and A. pirottae were administered interchangeably in either form. Similar flexibility has been observed in traditional medical systems of India, China, and Pakistan, where both fresh and dried materials are used to ensure continuous access to medicinal resources throughout the year [101, 108].

Globally, this dual approach reflects consistent ethnomedicinal logic: fresh materials are favored for topical or immediate treatments, while dried materials are preferred for storage, standardization, and oral administration [3, 27, 70, 106]. In Europe and the Americas, similar practices are reported, where fresh leaves or fruits are used for direct application, and roots, seeds, and bark are dried to extend shelf life and maintain consistent dosage in traditional pharmacopoeias [89, 90].

The predominance of fresh plant use in the Karamara forest patches highlights the community’s close connection to its natural environment and reliance on readily accessible resources. Conversely, the use of dried materials demonstrates adaptive strategies for resource preservation and year-round medicinal availability. Together, these practices reflect a well-balanced ethnobotanical system that supports both cultural continuity and ecological sustainability.

Dosage of Administration, Additives, and diagnostic features for medicinal plants

In the Karamara forest patches, the dosage of medicinal plant remedies varies according to the type of ailment, patient age, and overall health condition. Traditional healers determine dosages using practical, locally available tools such as cups, spoons, liters, or the palm of the hand. This flexible, experience-based approach reflects the adaptive nature of traditional medicine, where individualized treatment is central to healing practices. Similar dosage variability has been documented in other parts of Ethiopia and across Africa, emphasizing the personalized nature of ethnomedical systems [27, 92, 93, 106].

Diagnosis within the Somali community of Karamara relies primarily on a combination of patient interviews and visual observation of symptoms. Healers assess indicators such as changes in urine color, skin and eye tone, tongue appearance, and the presence of swelling, bleeding, or visible parasites. This empirical and symptom-based diagnostic method parallels ethnomedical traditions elsewhere, including India, Pakistan, and Sudan, where healers integrate visual inspection and patient-reported experiences to guide diagnosis and treatment [21, 101, 107]. In traditional Chinese medicine, similar diagnostic techniques such as visual and pulse examination are also employed to determine the most appropriate dosage and remedy [108].

Additives play an important role in the preparation and administration of herbal remedies. While most treatments in Karamara are administered without additional substances, some incorporate water, milk, coffee, honey, or meat to enhance taste, efficacy, or spiritual potency. Water, particularly cold water, serves as the principal solvent for decoctions and infusions, while milk and oils are used for spiritual cleansing or topical application, as observed with P. granatum and Z. mauritiana. Comparable use of solvents and additives has been reported in traditional medicine systems across Africa, Asia, and the Americas, where such ingredients aid in extracting active compounds, improving palatability, and enhancing therapeutic effectiveness [55, 70, 89, 90].

Certain treatments involve heating, burning, or smoking plant materials, particularly for managing respiratory problems, pain, or spiritual ailments. Species such as C. procera, C. kelleriana, and E. globulus are commonly used in these thermal preparations. Similar practices have been recorded in Ethiopia, India, and Pakistan, reflecting the global use of heat and smoke-based remedies to release volatile compounds and deliver inhalable therapies [3, 27, 101, 108].

Hence, the dosage determination, diagnostic processes, and use of additives in Karamara demonstrate a sophisticated ethnomedical system that integrates patient-centered assessment, knowledge of bioactive plant chemistry, and adaptive modes of preparation. These practices mirror broader ethnobotanical patterns observed globally, revealing both cultural specificity and a shared human logic in the use of MPs for healing.

Marketability of medicinal plants

The present study reveals that MPs from the Karamara forest patches possess considerable market potential, shaped by their proven therapeutic value, cultural acceptance, and high local demand. Frequently cited species such as A. megalacantha, C. procera, O. lamiifolium, and Z. spina-christi are among the most sought-after, reflecting both their medicinal efficacy and commercial significance. Similar patterns have been documented in other parts of Ethiopia, where highly utilized species are also the most marketable due to their effectiveness against common ailments and their wide recognition in traditional healthcare systems [4, 17, 56].

Across Africa, market trends show comparable dynamics. In Kenya and Nigeria, marketable MPs include both high-biomass trees and rapidly regenerating herbs, suggesting that trade value is influenced not only by therapeutic efficacy but also by ecological abundance and ease of regeneration [92, 110–112]. In Asia particularly India, Pakistan, and China market demand similarly correlate with citation frequency and the plants’ effectiveness in treating widespread diseases such as malaria, gastrointestinal infections, and respiratory disorders [67, 92, 108].

The specific plant parts used and methods of preparation play a central role in determining marketability. Leaves, roots, latex, and fruits are the most frequently traded components, often processed into powders, decoctions, or infusions for ease of transport, storage, and use. Comparable practices are reported in Sudan and other African regions, where powdered leaves, dried roots, and latex constitute the principal forms of trade in traditional herbal markets [106, 107]. Likewise, in Europe and the Americas, the herbal trade is dominated by processed plant products typically derived from leaves, bark, and seeds underscoring a global preference for materials that are stable, portable, and easily standardized [89, 91].

In the Karamara region, marketable species are predominantly trees and shrubs, such as (A) nilotica, M. stenopetala, and (B) rivae, valued for their large biomass and year-round availability. At the same time, fast-regenerating herbs like (A) mexicana, (B) pilosa, and L. savoryi present sustainable options for trade, given their rapid growth and resilience. Conversely, slow-growing woody species, including A. senegal and (C) myrrha, require careful management and harvesting regulation to prevent overexploitation and ensure long-term ecological stability. Similar sustainability concerns have been noted globally, where balancing commercial demand with conservation remains a critical challenge [51, 53, 113, 114].

Hence, the marketability of MPs in the Karamara forest patches is shaped by an interplay of therapeutic efficacy, accessibility, growth form, and sustainable harvest potential. These dynamics mirror global trends in the medicinal plant trade, where high-demand species often occupy both cultural and economic importance. Promoting sustainable harvesting, community-based cultivation, and value-added processing could therefore enhance both the conservation of medicinal plant biodiversity and the livelihoods of local communities.

Informant consensus factor (ICF) and fidelity level (FL) of medicinal plants

The Informant Consensus Factor (ICF) is widely used in ethnobotanical research to explore the degree of agreement among informants regarding the use of medicinal plants for particular ailment categories. However, several authors, including Leonti, have highlighted important conceptual and methodological limitations of this index, emphasizing that ICF does not assess pharmacological efficacy and may be strongly influenced by sample size, informant selection, and patterns of cultural knowledge transmission rather than therapeutic effectiveness [103]. Consequently, its application requires careful and cautious interpretation. In this study, ICF is therefore used as a heuristic tool to examine patterns of shared cultural knowledge and local agreement rather than as a direct indicator of therapeutic effectiveness.

In the Karamara forest patches, ICF values ranged from 0.60 to 0.85. Dermatological disorders showed the highest consensus, indicating strong agreement among informants on remedies for wounds, ringworm, and other skin conditions. Similar patterns have been reported in other Ethiopian studies, where visible and frequently encountered ailments tend to generate higher levels of shared knowledge and agreement [53, 56]. High ICF values in such categories may also reflect the accessibility of the plant species involved and the collective learning processes within the community rather than superior medicinal efficacy.

Gastrointestinal disorders ranked second in terms of consensus, reflecting widespread recognition of herbal treatments for stomachache, diarrhea, constipation, and intestinal parasites. Comparable trends have been reported in ethnobotanical studies from Kenya, Nigeria, and Sudan, where digestive ailments are commonly treated using a relatively small number of well-known plant species [92, 107, 110, 111]. Respiratory illnesses and malaria-related conditions exhibited moderate consensus, suggesting a broader range of species and treatment options, a pattern similarly observed in India, Pakistan, and China [93, 94, 101, 108].

The lowest ICF values were recorded for urogenital and reproductive disorders, indicating limited agreement among informants. This may reflect fewer reported cases, the involvement of specialized or sensitive knowledge, or restricted modes of cultural transmission. Comparable observations have been made in Africa and Asia, where ethnomedicinal knowledge related to reproductive health is often confined to a smaller group of practitioners [70, 106].

By definition, high ICF values (approaching 1) indicate that relatively few species are repeatedly cited by many informants for a given ailment category, whereas low values reflect greater variability or individualized knowledge [17, 56]. While ICF has frequently been used to highlight plants of potential interest for further investigation [6, 45, 53, 56, 77, 99], its results should be interpreted cautiously. In the Karamara context, high-consensus categories such as dermatological and gastrointestinal disorders are best understood as culturally validated domains of shared practice, whereas low-consensus categories may warrant further ethnographic exploration rather than immediate pharmacological prioritization [80, 89].

Fidelity Level (FL) similarly serves as a quantitative indicator of how consistently a given plant species is associated with a specific ailment within a community. Like ICF, FL has recognized limitations, including its sensitivity to informant number and its inability to distinguish between cultural preference and biomedical efficacy. In this study, FL is therefore applied as an interpretive measure of therapeutic specificity within local knowledge systems, rather than as proof of medicinal effectiveness.

In Karamara, FL values ranged from 0.434 to 1.0, reflecting varying degrees of use specificity. Three species Euphorbia abyssinica, Moringa stenopetala, and Cyphostemma adenocaule achieved maximum FL values, as they were consistently cited for wound healing, hypertension, and malaria, respectively [17, 41, 53, 56, 60]. High FL values for these species indicate strong cultural agreement on their primary uses, though such agreement should be interpreted as cultural reliability rather than clinical validation.

Other species, such as Datura stramonium and Jatropha aceroides, also exhibited high FL values for specific conditions such as “evil eye” and cough. Moderate FL values were recorded for Ziziphus spina-christi and Commiphora myrrha, which were primarily associated with stomachache and snakebite but also had secondary applications. This pattern reflects the multifunctional use of medicinal plants, a strategy widely reported in ethnobotanical studies from India, Pakistan, and China [93, 94, 101, 108].

Lower FL values were observed for Aloe megalacantha and Calotropis procera, reflecting their use across multiple ailment categories. Similar trends have been documented in Europe and the Americas, where culturally prominent or readily available species often display lower FL values due to their broad therapeutic application [80, 89]. Such multipurpose use does not diminish their cultural importance but instead highlights adaptive and flexible healthcare strategies within traditional medical systems.

Thus, FL analysis in Karamara reveals a dual pattern: certain species are highly ailment-specific and culturally emphasized, while others serve multiple therapeutic roles. While high-FL species are often considered candidates for further scientific investigation [17], this study emphasizes that FL primarily reflects patterns of cultural emphasis and shared practice. Ethiopian ethnobotanical studies have similarly employed FL to explore medicinal plant prioritization while acknowledging its interpretive limitations [3, 12, 14, 33, 37, 59, 60]. When applied cautiously, FL contributes to understanding how traditional communities structure and transmit medicinal plant knowledge across diverse health needs [70, 106].

Preference ranking and direct matrix ranking of medicinal plants

The preference ranking exercise in the Karamara forest patches revealed community perceptions of medicinal plant efficacy for malaria treatment. Among seven commonly cited antimalarial species, C. kelleriana was the most preferred, followed by A. megalacantha, L. inermis, and E. camaldulensis. Lower-ranked species included J. aceroides, T. indica, and C. adenocaule. These rankings reflect local knowledge and perceived therapeutic effectiveness, consistent with ethnobotanical principles linking preference to efficacy, accessibility, and familiarity [48, 49, 53, 56, 59, 60].

The high preference for C. kelleriana aligns with studies in Ethiopia and Kenya, where potent antimalarial plants are consistently prioritized due to perceived effectiveness [107, 111, 112]. Aloe species are widely recognized for treating malaria and febrile disorders across Africa, Asia, and the Americas, emphasizing their bioactive compounds and therapeutic reliability [89, 90]. Moderate preference for L. inermis and E. camaldulensis suggests that while culturally validated, efficacy or availability may vary, a pattern observed in Nigeria, Sudan, and Pakistan [92, 101, 110, 113, 114]. Lower preference for J. aceroides, T. indica, and C. adenocaule likely reflects limited local knowledge, reduced accessibility, or lower perceived effectiveness, consistent with ethnobotanical findings in India, China, and Europe [80, 108].

Preference ranking provides critical insights for identifying priority species for pharmacological validation and sustainable use. Globally, highly ranked antimalarial plants often represent the most reliable and culturally significant remedies, guiding conservation and scientific research [14, 17, 33, 48, 54, 70, 106]. In Karamara, these rankings underline the interplay of cultural knowledge, perceived efficacy, and accessibility in shaping community preferences.

The direct matrix ranking of multipurpose MPs highlights their socio-economic and ethnobotanical significance beyond therapeutic use. A. nilotica emerged as the most valuable species, primarily for construction and furniture, followed by P. guajava and C. myrrha, reflecting broad utility across multiple categories. Similar patterns are reported in Ethiopia and Kenya, where Acacia and Commiphora species are valued for timber, fodder, and medicinal purposes [56, 111, 112].

The prominence of fence and construction uses highlights the community’s reliance on woody species for household and communal infrastructure, a trend observed in Sudan, Nigeria, and other African regions [107, 110]. Beyond Africa, species such as T. indica and L. inermis in India, Pakistan, and China are valued for medicinal, nutritional, dye, and fuel uses, illustrating their integral role in rural livelihoods [93, 101]. Lower-ranking species, including V. bussei and E. camaldulensis, have more specialized or limited uses, reflecting their narrower socio-economic contributions. Comparable findings exist in Europe and the Americas, demonstrating the global utility of matrix ranking in ethnobotanical prioritization [80, 89].

Direct matrix ranking informs conservation and sustainable utilization. Species with high cumulative scores, such as A. nilotica and P. guajava, should be prioritized for sustainable harvesting and habitat protection, while lower-ranked species may require targeted measures to prevent overexploitation in specific use niches. Globally, such assessments emphasize integrating ethnobotanical knowledge with livelihood strategies and ecological management to ensure sustainable use of medicinal and multipurpose plants [70, 106].

Comparison with previous ethnobotanical studies using the Jaccard similarity index

Comparing the MPs of the Karamara forest patches with previous ethnobotanical studies using the Jaccard Similarity Index (JI) highlights both shared and unique aspects of traditional knowledge. The highest similarity was observed with studies from Jigjiga town and Jigjiga district, reflecting strong concordance in species composition among geographically and culturally proximate Somali communities. This overlap likely results from shared ecological conditions, plant availability, and cultural practices, consistent with other studies of ethnic-specific medicinal plant use in Ethiopia [53, 56].

Moderate similarity with regions such as Guraferda, Yeki, Armachiho, Bita, and Asagirt indicates partial overlap in plant use. These variations may arise from differences in ecology, accessibility, and cultural practices. Similar trends are reported in Kenya and Nigeria, where species overlap is shaped by local flora, cultural preferences, and traditional healthcare practices [92, 110–112].

Lower similarity indices (< 20%) with areas including Quarit, Ticho, Sodo, and Dawuro highlight the distinctiveness of the Karamara flora and the specialized ethnobotanical knowledge of the Somali ethnic group. Globally, similar patterns emerge where ecological heterogeneity, floristic diversity, and cultural traditions generate unique medicinal plant assemblages, as documented in India, Pakistan, China, Europe, and the Americas [93, 94, 108, 109].

The Jaccard analysis highlights the influence of geographic proximity, cultural affiliation, and ecological similarity on medicinal plant knowledge. High similarity among neighboring Somali communities supports targeted pharmacological research and conservation planning, while low similarity with distant regions points to opportunities for discovering underutilized or novel species. This approach aligns with global ethnobotanical practices, where similarity indices are valuable for assessing cross-cultural and ecological patterns in medicinal plant use [70, 106].

Indigenous knowledge transfer and medicinal plant knowledge among informant groups

Independent t-tests revealed significant differences in medicinal plant knowledge among informant groups in the Karamara forest patches. Male participants demonstrated higher knowledge than females (p < 0.05), reflecting gender-specific roles in resource use. Men typically engage in harvesting trees, shrubs, and multipurpose plants, increasing familiarity with species used for construction, fuel, and medicine [4, 6, 48, 49, 59, 111, 112]. In contrast, females tend to specialize in herbs and domestic remedies, a pattern also documented in Nigeria, India, and China [21, 64, 82, 83], 110– [94, 101].

Illiterate participants had significantly greater knowledge than literate individuals (p < 0.05), suggesting that formal education may reduce engagement with traditional medicinal practices. Similar trends appear in Tanzania, Pakistan, and Latin America, where elders and less formally educated individuals retain deeper ethnobotanical knowledge due to reliance on traditional remedies [30, 70, 96, 101, 113, 114].

Key informants, including traditional healers and community elders, showed the highest knowledge, significantly exceeding general informants (p < 0.05). Specialized experience, social roles, and prolonged exposure to local healthcare practices are strong predictors of ethnobotanical expertise. Globally, healers and elders function as custodians of medicinal plant knowledge, emphasizing their role in documentation, conservation, and sustainable use [30, 51, 84, 89, 106, 108].

Age also strongly influenced knowledge. One-way ANOVA indicated a significant effect of age (F = 50.54, p < 0.05), with older participants demonstrating the greatest expertise. Correlation (r = 0.653, p < 0.001) and regression analysis (R² = 0.575) suggest that age accounts for over half the variation in knowledge, reflecting accumulation through prolonged experience and engagement with traditional healthcare. Similar age-related patterns are reported in Ethiopia, Kenya, Nigeria, India, China, and Pakistan, where elders serve as primary knowledge holders while younger generations show reduced familiarity due to urbanization and formal education [3, 4, 12, 27, 56, 70, 101, 110, 111]. These trends are also observed in Europe and the Americas, highlighting knowledge erosion among youth in modernized contexts [89, 108].

Knowledge transfer in Karamara is predominantly vertical, passing within families to selected heirs, typically male children. This selective transmission preserves community-specific therapeutic practices and social prestige, a pattern noted in Ethiopia, Kenya, Nigeria, India, China, and Pakistan [53, 55, 59, 60, 64, 101, 110, 112, 113]. Sharing with outsiders is infrequent and often contingent on compensation, reflecting the cultural and economic value of traditional medicinal knowledge. Similar restrictions occur in Sudan, other African regions, and South America [70, 108, 113, 114]. Transmission occurs primarily orally, with occasional knowledge reported from dreams or divine inspiration, a mode common in Ethiopia, Asia, and Latin America [89, 106].

Medicinal plant collection practices are guided by cultural beliefs and healer-specific customs, including timing, method, and collector identity. Some informants prefer morning or afternoon harvests, while others follow gender-specific rules. These practices resonate globally, where lunar cycles, spiritual status, and social norms are believed to influence the potency of remedies [10, 25, 93, 94, 113].

Thus, these findings emphasize the intricate linkages among ethnobotanical knowledge, cultural practices, and spiritual beliefs. The predominance of familial transfer, coupled with generational disengagement, highlights the urgent need for systematic documentation and community-based programs to safeguard traditional knowledge—a concern shared globally in both developing and developed contexts [89, 106, 113, 114].

Threats and conservation of Indigenous knowledge associated with medicinal plants

MPs in the Karamara forest patches face multiple threats, with agricultural expansion identified as the primary driver of species loss. Additional pressures include firewood collection, charcoal production, timber extraction, and construction activities. Similar patterns have been reported across Ethiopia, where population growth and the need for agricultural land significantly threaten both general flora and medicinal plant species [7, 33, 41, 46, 48]. In the Eastern Nuba Mountains of Sudan, overharvesting for timber, agricultural expansion, and overgrazing were also highlighted as principal threats to wild edible and medicinal trees [115]. Comparable pressures are documented in Kenya, Nigeria, India, China, and Pakistan, where habitat degradation, urbanization, and unsustainable harvesting drive declines in medicinal plant populations [110– [93, 101].

Indigenous knowledge associated with MPs is primarily transmitted orally, often within family lines and restricted to selected heirs, typically male children. The death of elder healers, combined with modernization, formal education, and cultural shifts, accelerates the erosion of authentic ethnomedicinal knowledge [17, 47, 50, 59]. Secrecy in knowledge transmission, practiced to maintain social respect and ensure compensation, further limits dissemination beyond the family, posing additional challenges to intergenerational continuity [6, 54, 59, 60].

Conservation of MPs in the Karamara forest is largely informal. Most species are harvested from the wild, with few structured protection measures. However, some traditional practitioners have initiated cultivation of high-use species, such as O. lamiifolium, A. megalacantha, M. stenopetala, and C. procera, in home gardens, reflecting a proactive approach to ensure year-round availability and reduce pressure on wild populations [77, 105]. Cultural beliefs and traditional practices also contribute to in situ conservation, including the protection of sacred groves and selective harvesting methods that preserve roots and mature trees [6, 33, 53].

The preference ranking conducted in this study confirms agricultural expansion as the most critical threat, followed by firewood collection, charcoal production, and construction activities. Moderate threats include fodder collection and the impacts of modernization, while drought was perceived as the least severe threat due to the adaptation of many species to semi-arid conditions. Globally, similar patterns are observed, with anthropogenic pressures and habitat degradation posing major risks to medicinal plant diversity across Africa, Asia, and the Americas [89, 106, 113].

Community-based strategies in Karamara emphasize sustainable management of medicinal resources. Practices include cultivation of high-use species, controlled harvesting, protection of grazing areas, and replanting of harvested plants. Species are prioritized for conservation based on frequency of use, multipurpose applications, or ecological vulnerability, including C. procera, A. megalacantha, C. myrrha, V. bussei, and O. lamiifolium. Intergenerational transfer of knowledge, through mentorship by elders, ensures that sustainable collection techniques, plant identification, and preparation methods are preserved for future generations [89, 106, 113]. Thus, the Karamara forest patches exemplify community-driven conservation, where traditional ecological knowledge, cultural norms, and practical management strategies collectively contribute to the sustainable use and preservation of medicinal plants. These findings highlight the importance of integrating indigenous knowledge with formal conservation initiatives to safeguard ethnomedicinal biodiversity both regionally and globally.

Implications of medicinal plant use on Health, food Security, environmental Protection, and cultural value

The use of MPs by the Somali ethnic community in the Karamara forest patches carries profound significance for healthcare, food security, environmental conservation, and cultural heritage.

In terms of healthcare, the community heavily relies on ethnomedicinal knowledge to address a wide range of conditions, including gastrointestinal disorders, malaria, respiratory infections, hypertension, wounds, and skin diseases. High Informant Consensus Factor (ICF) and Fidelity Level (FL) values for species such as E. abyssinica, M. stenopetala, and C. adenocaule indicate strong agreement among informants about their effectiveness. These findings align with studies in Ethiopia, Kenya, Nigeria, India, and Pakistan, where certain plants are consistently favored for specific ailments due to their perceived efficacy [34, 35, 38, 101, 112]. Preference ranking exercises further highlight the targeted use of species like C. kelleriana, A. megalacantha, and L. inermis for malaria and other priority health conditions, echoing patterns observed in Asia and South America, where community preferences strongly influence medicinal plant selection [70, 84, 93, 94]. Together, these observations emphasize the central role of MPs as primary healthcare resources and emphasize the need to integrate indigenous knowledge into broader health strategies.

MPs also play a critical role in food security. Several species, including C. edulis, M. stenopetala, and T. indica, serve both nutritional and therapeutic functions. This dual purpose enhances dietary diversity, particularly during periods of scarcity, and strengthens community resilience a pattern documented in Ethiopia, India, China, and Brazil [5, 106, 116–118]. The nutritional and medicinal multifunctionality of these plants illustrates the interdependence of healthcare and food security in semi-arid forest ecosystems and highlights opportunities for policies that simultaneously promote nutrition and health outcomes [119–122].

From an environmental perspective, the community demonstrates adaptive and sustainable management practices, including selective harvesting, home garden cultivation, and the protection of high-value species [119–123]. Species such as C. procera, A. megalacantha, and C. myrrha are harvested in ways that preserve leaves, fruits, or latex while avoiding destructive extraction of roots or bark. Such practices align with strategies observed in Kenya, Nigeria, and Sudan, where traditional ecological knowledge mitigates threats like agricultural expansion, overgrazing, and fuelwood collection [53, 92, 110, 114]. Home garden cultivation, in particular, mirrors global approaches in Asia, Europe, and the Americas that aim to maintain plant availability while conserving wild populations [93, 94, 101, 108]. These findings highlight the vital role of ethnobotanical knowledge in shaping adaptive environmental management strategies that can inform conservation and ecosystem planning in dryland forests.

Culturally, MPs in Karamara are deeply woven into spiritual practices, rituals, and social cohesion. Species such as D. stramonium, C. procera, and P. granatum are used in traditional ceremonies and protective rituals, reflecting the interconnection between ethnomedicine and cultural identity. The transfer of knowledge within families and selective sharing practices ensures the preservation of both medicinal efficacy and cultural heritage, a pattern also reported in Ethiopia, Pakistan, China, and South America [89, 93, 106, 113, 119, 123]. Maintaining these cultural practices safeguards indigenous knowledge systems while reinforcing communal health and social networks.

These findings have important implications for public health and environmental policy in the Karamara forest region. Incorporating ethnomedicinal knowledge into primary healthcare programs can increase access to culturally appropriate treatments, reduce reliance on limited pharmaceutical resources, and strengthen community resilience. Simultaneously, recognizing the conservation value of MPs can guide sustainable harvesting practices, habitat protection, and restoration efforts, particularly for species under high extraction pressure. Policymakers should also promote initiatives that encourage intergenerational knowledge transfer and community-based management, ensuring that cultural heritage and ecosystem services are both maintained. By integrating healthcare, nutrition, and biodiversity strategies, these approaches can support sustainable development in semi-arid forest landscapes while safeguarding the livelihoods, health, and cultural identity of the Somali communities.

Limitations of the study

While this study provides important insights into the ethnobotanical knowledge of the Somali community in the Karamara forest patches, several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. The sample size was relatively small, and gender representation was uneven, reflecting local sociocultural roles but potentially limiting the generalizability of the results to the wider community. Participation was also influenced by ethical requirements and informed consent procedures, which may have constrained informants’ willingness to disclose certain types of knowledge, particularly information considered sensitive, specialized, or culturally restricted.

The study relied primarily on self-reported data obtained through interviews and discussions, which are inherently subject to recall bias, selective memory, and personal interpretation. Seasonal variation in plant availability and use was not fully captured due to time constraints, potentially leading to the omission or underrepresentation of seasonally important or ephemeral species. The researcher’s outsider status and the need for translation may have introduced cultural and linguistic biases, affecting the accurate recording and interpretation of local plant names, preparation methods, and illness concepts. Additionally, the exclusively oral mode of knowledge transmission within the community posed challenges for precise documentation and cross-verification.

Methodologically, the quantitative ethnobotanical indices applied in this study, including Informant Consensus Factor (ICF) and Fidelity Level (FL), have well-recognized conceptual and analytical limitations. These indices do not measure pharmacological efficacy and may be influenced by sample size, informant selection, and patterns of cultural knowledge transmission rather than therapeutic effectiveness. Consequently, the values reported in this study should be interpreted cautiously as indicators of shared cultural agreement and knowledge emphasis, rather than as evidence of clinical validity.

Furthermore, this study focused on documenting traditional medicinal plant uses and did not include laboratory-based validation of efficacy, safety, dosage, or potential toxicity. As a result, the applicability of the findings beyond the local cultural context remains limited. Despite these constraints, efforts were made to mitigate methodological challenges by encouraging broad participation, triangulating information across multiple informants, using local translators, and conducting guided field walks to enhance accuracy.

Recognizing these limitations is essential for contextualizing the results and avoiding overinterpretation. The findings highlight the need for future research that integrates ethnobotanical documentation with phytochemical, pharmacological, and toxicological analyses, as well as longitudinal and seasonally comprehensive studies, to strengthen understanding of medicinal plant use and support evidence-based conservation and healthcare initiatives.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study highlights the rich ethnobotanical knowledge of the Somali community in the Karamara forest patches, revealing a deeply embedded system of medicinal plant use that supports holistic healthcare and contributes to biodiversity conservation. Traditional healers and elders serve as the main custodians of this knowledge, which is predominantly transmitted orally within families, reflecting a strong cultural framework for health management and ecological stewardship. The findings emphasize the intricate connections between cultural resilience, sustainable resource use, and ecological integrity in semi-arid forest landscapes.

The Karamara forest serves not only as a reservoir of biodiversity but also as a living archive of Somali ethnomedicine. Integrating this knowledge into broader health and conservation frameworks can enhance community wellbeing while promoting sustainable management of forest resources. Efforts to document and safeguard indigenous medicinal knowledge are urgent, particularly through participatory education, intergenerational transfer programs, and cultural heritage initiatives within local schools and community organizations.

Species identified as culturally significant warrant pharmacological evaluation to validate bioactivity and safety, enabling collaboration between ethnobotanists, pharmacologists, and traditional healers to bridge traditional wisdom with modern medicine. Conservation strategies, including community-managed reserves, sustainable harvesting guidelines, and home-garden cultivation, should align with local ecological practices and cultural norms to reduce pressure on wild populations.

Policy frameworks should recognize and support traditional medicine, ensuring equitable benefit-sharing, institutional backing for knowledge protection, and integration into primary healthcare. Future research should adopt longitudinal and ecological approaches to monitor key species, assess market value chains, and examine the socio-economic dimensions of traditional medicine in pastoral and semi-pastoral communities.

By highlighting the interplay of cultural knowledge, medicinal plant diversity, and ecological stewardship, this study highlights the importance of biocultural conservation as a foundation for sustainable healthcare and the preservation of indigenous knowledge systems.
