Sinus of Valsalva Aneurysm Rupture Causing Ventricular Septal Dissection With Left Ventricular Penetration
Authors: Lei Liu, Xiuling Ma, Hui Chen, Yanfei Mo, Ning Zhang, Jing Yao, Xuelin Yang, Aijuan Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRupture of a sinus of Valsalva aneurysm is most often congenital, resulting from inherent weakness at the aortic-annular junction. Rupture into the interventricular septum is a rare complication.Case SummaryA 58-year-old man presented with recurrent chest tightness consistent with NYHA functional class III symptoms and hypotension (blood pressure: 103/43 mm Hg). Transthoracic echocardiography, transesophageal echocardiography, and computed tomography angiography demonstrated rupture of a right coronary sinus aneurysm into the interventricular septum, forming a dissection that communicated with the left ventricle via 2 defects. Emergent surgical repair led to complete resolution of the dissection.DiscussionThis case illustrates the diagnostic challenges associated with rupture of a sinus of Valsalva aneurysm complicated by ventricular septal dissection. It emphasizes the critical role of multimodal imaging in detecting left ventricular involvement, and it underscores the necessity of prompt surgical intervention to reduce mortality.Take-Home MessageEarly identification using multimodal imaging and immediate surgical repair are essential for favorable outcomes in rare cases of sinus of Valsalva aneurysm rupture complicated by ventricular septal dissection and left ventricular penetration.

History of Presentation

A 58-year-old man was admitted chiefly because of recurrent chest tightness lasting over 10 days, with worsening symptoms during the past 2 days (NYHA functional class III). On admission, his body temperature was 36.5 °C, pulse rate 72 beats/min, respiratory rate 20 beats/min, blood pressure 103/43 mm Hg, and body mass index 28.91 kg/m2. Cardiac auscultation revealed a diastolic murmur at the aortic area with diminished intensity of the second heart sound (S2). No audible systolic murmur, third heart sound (S3), or fourth heart sound (S4) was detected. Neither jugular venous distension nor lower extremity edema was observed.Take-Home Message•Early identification using multimodal imaging and immediate surgical repair are essential for favorable outcomes in rare cases of sinus of Valsalva aneurysm rupture complicated by ventricular septal dissection and left ventricular penetration.

Past Medical History

Eight months ago, he received a cardiac pacemaker implantation (VAT mode) to address atrioventricular conduction block. Preoperative transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed normal left heart function and an aortic root diameter of approximately 43 mm, with no evidence of ventricular septal dissection. The patient did not attend follow-up appointments, and there were no recorded postoperative TTE results. He denied any history of hypertension or diabetes, and reported no family history of cardiac disease. He also denied smoking or heavy alcohol intake.

Investigations

Electrocardiography showed atrial sensing and ventricular pacing rhythm. Chest x-ray revealed an enlarged cardiac silhouette. Laboratory tests showed elevated serum B-type natriuretic peptide at 1680 pg/mL (reference range: 5-100 pg/mL) and C-reactive protein at 25.7 mg/L (reference range: 0-6 mg/L). Complete blood count, troponin T, and creatine kinase-MB were within normal limits.

TTE in the parasternal long-axis view (Figure 1A, Video 1) demonstrated rupture of the right coronary sinus of Valsalva aneurysm into the interventricular septum, resulting in a ventricular septal dissection. A defect on the left ventricular aspect of the septum communicated with the left ventricle. In the short-axis view of the great arteries (Figure 1B, Video 2), disruption of echogenicity in the right coronary sinus was noted, with aliased turbulent flow showing a multicolored mosaic flow at the rupture site. Modified short-axis view illustrated the extent of the ventricular septal dissection and the entry site (Figure 1C, Video 3). The apical 5-chamber view showed that the right coronary sinus aneurysm had ruptured into the interventricular septum, forming a dissection. The dissection ballooned toward the left ventricular outflow tract, and 2 defects were present on the left ventricular side (Figure 1D, Video 4). Spectral Doppler demonstrated predominantly diastolic, high-velocity turbulent flow at the left ventricular outflow tract rupture site (peak velocity: 3.05 m/s; peak pressure gradient: 37 mm Hg) (Figure 1E). Management

After diagnostic confirmation through multimodal imaging, we promptly scheduled the patient for surgical intervention. Intraoperative exploration (Figure 4A) showed a rupture measuring approximately 15 × 15 mm within the right coronary sinus of the aorta, which had penetrated the interventricular septum. Two additional defects (10 × 10 mm and 5 × 5 mm) were identified on the left ventricular aspect of the interventricular septum, each communicating with the left ventricle. The number of aortic valve leaflets was normal. However, the right coronary leaflet showed contracture, and perforation was noted at the commissural leaflet, resulting in moderate regurgitation. Moderate mitral regurgitation and severe tricuspid regurgitation were also observed. The patient underwent aortic valve replacement, aortic sinus repair, interventricular septal repair, and tricuspid valvuloplasty. Immediate postoperative TEE (Figure 4B, Video 7) revealed normal function of the bioprosthetic aortic valve, without significant regurgitation or perivalvular leak. Outcome and Follow-Up

The postoperative course was uneventful. At the 1-week postoperative follow-up, TTE demonstrated complete resolution of the ventricular septal dissection and absence of septal–left ventricular communication (Figure 5, Videos 8 and 9). The patient reported marked alleviation of precordial discomfort (NYHA functional class II). Discussion

Rupture of a sinus of Valsalva aneurysm, accounting for 0.1% to 3.5% of congenital heart diseases,1,2 results from congenital weakness in the elastic lamina at the aortic-annular junction. Sinus of Valsalva aneurysms can be classified as congenital or acquired. Acquired causes include trauma, endocarditis, syphilis, Behçet disease, and tuberculosis.3,4 Rupture of the right coronary sinus is more commonly observed and typically involves the right heart chambers. In contrast, rupture into the interventricular septum is rare (occurring in <2% of cases).5 This rarity is attributed to the specific anatomical relationship between the right coronary sinus and the interventricular septum. When a sinus of Valsalva aneurysm ruptures with dissection extending into the interventricular septum, the dissection's right border abuts the myocardium of the right ventricular outflow tract, whereas its left border is defined by the aortic annulus and muscular ventricular septum. Such lesions are more likely to rupture into the left ventricle, and the defects may be single or multiple. Extensive dissection cavities and associated inflammatory responses may result in the development of multiple channels.6

In this case, the patient was a 58-year-old man with a rupture at the base of the right coronary sinus, which extended inferiorly into a dissection of the interventricular septum bordered almost entirely by muscular tissue. Blood flow ultimately entered the left ventricular outflow tract via a defect on the left ventricular side. Notably, the rupture site was distant from the ostium of the right coronary artery, and the coronary artery was not involved. No evidence of valvular vegetations was found on either echocardiography or intraoperative exploration. The patient had no recent history of intensive physical activity or infections. However, he had undergone cardiac pacemaker implantation 8 months ago. Preoperative echocardiography revealed no structural abnormalities of the heart. As there were no postoperative follow-up echocardiography records available, it is uncertain whether the current rupture of the sinus of Valsalva aneurysm is a complication of the pacemaker implantation.

Rupture of a sinus of Valsalva aneurysm into the interventricular septum is associated with a progressive clinical course and poor prognosis, with a perioperative mortality rate of up to 3.9%.7 Prompt surgical intervention is recommended once rupture occurs. In this case, the patient received a timely diagnosis and underwent surgery while hemodynamic status remained stable, resulting in a favorable recovery. Long-term echocardiographic follow-up is advised to monitor ventricular function and detect recurrence of sinus of Valsalva aneurysm rupture.

Conclusions

This rare case of right coronary sinus of Valsalva aneurysm rupture resulting in ventricular septal dissection, with dual perforations into the left ventricle, underscores the critical importance of multimodal imaging in diagnosing complex cardiac pathologies. TTE initially identified the dissection tract and turbulent diastolic flow, whereas TEE and computed tomography angiography provided detailed characterization of the rupture site and communication pathways. Emergent surgical repair was necessary to address the resulting hemodynamic instability. Long-term surveillance with serial echocardiography is essential to monitor for recurrence or delayed complications. This report highlights the importance of early recognition of diastolic murmurs, prompt use of integrated imaging modalities, and timely surgical intervention to achieve favorable long-term outcomes in these high-risk clinical scenarios.
