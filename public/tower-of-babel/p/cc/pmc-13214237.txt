Effectiveness of bariatric surgery in improving sleep-related symptoms of obstructive sleep apnoea in obese patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Madiha Masood Khan, Surrendar Dawani, Rashid Ali, Tashaba Qaiser Faizi, Sanum Ali, Adeela Zuhair Siddiqui, Aruna Dawani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to assess the effectiveness of bariatric surgery in improving symptoms of sleep-related outcomes among obese patients suffering from obstructive sleep apnoea. MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analysed prospectively collected clinical records of patients with a symptomatic diagnosis of obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), who later underwent bariatric surgery (sleeve gastrectomy or mini-gastric bypass) at the Department of General Surgery at Jinnah Postgraduate Medical Centre (JPMC), Karachi, between 2018 and 2023. Preoperative and postoperative (6 weeks and 3 months) follow-up records were reviewed to extract information on OSA-related clinical features, including apnoea status (yes/no), loud snoring (yes/no), daytime sleep bouts (yes/no), and the number of nighttime waking events. Data were analysed using SPSS version 23, and paired-sample t-tests and McNemar’s test were applied. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.Results A total of 150 patients were included with equal gender distribution (50% male, 50% female) and a mean body mass index (BMI) of 47.8 ± 8.5 kg/m². At baseline, all patients (100%) were diagnosed as having obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) based on symptomology. All patients showed complete clinical improvement of OSA-related symptoms by 6 weeks, which persisted at the 3-month follow-up, as assessed by the study questionnaire. Marked reductions were also noted in loud snoring and daytime sleepiness. Furthermore, the average number of waking up events at night decreased significantly by 0.66 ± 2.12 events at six weeks and by 3.09 ± 1.89 events at three months postoperatively (p < 0.001).ConclusionsBariatric surgery showed significant improvement in sleep quality and led to marked clinical improvement and symptom-based remission of OSA-related manifestations within 3 months of follow-upSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03704-7.Keywords: Sleep apnoea, Obstructive, Body mass index, Bariatric surgery, Snoring, Hypoxia, Sleep

Background

Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is a condition in which the upper airway gets partially or completely blocked during sleep, causing disordered breathing and various physiological disturbances [1]. The alternate apnoea and hypopnea episodes result in disrupted sleep and fluctuations in oxygen levels [2]. When these breathing interruptions are accompanied by clinical symptoms and comorbidities, they cause a complex condition termed obstructive sleep apnoea syndrome (OSAS), which affects 3–7% of adult men and 2–5% of adult women in the general population [3]. These numbers are expected to rise further with the ongoing increase in obesity rates. Several factors increase the risk of OSA, including obesity, male gender, advancing age, certain ethnic backgrounds, smoking, alcohol use, genetic susceptibility, and structural abnormalities of the upper airway [4]. Among these, obesity remains the most significant and well-established contributor to the development of OSA [5].

Obesity has become a major global health challenge and is one of the most important modifiable determinants of OSA [6]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), nearly half of the world’s population is expected to be overweight by 2030 [7]. Individuals with a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m² are at a considerably higher risk of developing OSA. A 10% increase in body weight has been shown to raise the apnoea–hypopnea index (AHI) by approximately 32%, whereas weight reduction can lower it by about 26% [8, 9]. Consequently, weight-loss interventions, including bariatric surgery, have gained prominence as integral components of OSA management in obese patients [10].

Weight reduction is one of the effective therapeutic options available for managing OSA; however, patients find it challenging and time-consuming to achieve and maintain significant weight loss just through lifestyle changes [11]. Such lifestyle modifications are effective only in patients with mild to moderate obesity. Bariatric surgery has therefore emerged as a preferred approach to achieve substantial and sustained weight loss in obese individuals [8]. Among the various available procedures, sleeve gastrectomy has particularly demonstrated more durable outcomes and notable improvement in obesity-related comorbidities, including OSA [1, 10].

The rising prevalence of obesity and its strong correlation with OSA highlight the need to outline the effective management options. Bariatric surgery has emerged as a promising therapeutic option; however, while long-term outcomes remain insufficiently explored, early postoperative improvements in OSA-related symptoms are clinically meaningful, particularly in terms of sleep quality, daytime functioning, and patient well-being [2]. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no such study has been conducted in Pakistan before, and there is a lack of evidence from low-income countries where patient characteristics, healthcare access, and follow-up practices differ considerably [12]. The current study aims to address the gap by evaluating the effectiveness of bariatric surgery on improving clinical symptoms related to OSA. This will contribute valuable regional data and enhance the global understanding of surgical outcomes in high-risk obese populations.

Methods

Study design, duration, and setting

This is a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected clinical data, in which bariatric surgery preceded outcome assessment, and postoperative outcomes were evaluated during routine short-term follow-up at the Department of General Surgery at Jinnah Postgraduate Medical Centre (JPMC), Karachi, Pakistan. The study included all patients who underwent bariatric surgery between January 2018 and December 2023. The data were analysed retrospectively in 2025 to assess the impact of bariatric surgery on OSA symptom improvement.

Ethical consideration

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Jinnah Postgraduate Medical Centre (JPMC) provided ethical approval for the study (Approval number: NO, F.2–81/2024-GENL/173/JPMC) and granted a waiver of additional study-specific consent. The confidentiality of participants’ information was strictly maintained throughout the study.

Study population

Patients aged 20 to 60 years of both genders, with a body mass index (BMI) greater than 30 and clinically suspected obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) evaluated by a sleep specialist based on symptomatology, were included in the study. Patients with an ASA physical status of IV or above and those with other sleep disorders that could affect the assessment of OSA were excluded.

The diagnosis of OSA was made clinically, as polysomnography and apnoea-hypopnea index assessments were not performed due to limited resources in the public setup. Patients who experienced more than five awakenings per hour during sleep, each lasting at least 10 s, were classified as having clinically suspected OSA based on symptomatology, in the absence of polysomnography. This threshold reflects patient-reported symptom frequency and is distinct from polysomnography-derived apnoea–hypopnea index (AHI). Excessive daytime sleepiness, defined as three to five sleep episodes or “sleep bouts” during routine daytime activity, was also considered supportive of the diagnosis.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using the WHO sample size estimation software, based on a previously reported 65% remission rate of OSA after bariatric surgery [6], with a 7% margin of error and a 95% confidence interval. The final sample size was 177 participants. Non-probability consecutive sampling was used for patient selection. Although the calculated sample size was 177, data were available for 155 patients initially, which was later reduced to 150 owing to loss of follow-up and lack of patient cooperation. The reason for this difference is that our study took place in a resource-constrained public sector hospital where bariatric surgery is performed less frequently as compared to private hospitals. As a result, we included all eligible cases that met the inclusion criteria during the time frame of 5 years.

Data collection

The medical records of patients who had undergone bariatric surgery were reviewed after ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of JPMC. Patients who met the inclusion criteria completed a structured questionnaire that was specifically designed to examine the clinical features of OSA during routine clinical care. The questionnaire included sections on demographic information (name, age, gender, BMI, neck circumference), and symptom-related variables such as frequency of nighttime awakenings, presence of loud snoring, and episodes of excessive daytime sleep bouts. Improvement was defined as the complete absence of the symptom at follow-up compared to baseline. This questionnaire was administered during preoperative evaluation and at 6-week and 3-month postoperative follow-ups in person by the research team. Although the questionnaire used in this study was not formally validated, it was developed based on commonly reported clinical features of OSA and included items conceptually aligned with established screening tools such as the STOP-BANG, Berlin Questionnaire, and Athens Insomnia Scale. The questionnaire is provided as supplementary material.

Further, the hospital’s records provided comprehensive information, including each patient’s history, clinical findings, investigations, surgical notes, and postoperative follow-up. Before surgery, patients underwent routine preoperative preparation, which included an anaesthesia fitness assessment, cardiopulmonary evaluation, BMI measurement, and a gastrografin meal study. All surgical procedures, both sleeve gastrectomy and mini-gastric bypass, were carried out following standard, well-established protocol.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 23. Patients without complete 3-month follow-up data were excluded. Quantitative variables (age, height, weight, BMI, neck circumference) are presented as mean ± SD, and qualitative variables as frequencies and percentages. Baseline symptom frequency was recorded as continuous data for description, while postoperative outcomes were analyzed as binary (present/absent) variables.

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the data were normally distributed, paired-sample t-tests were conducted to compare mean nighttime awakenings before surgery with those at 6 weeks and 3 months post-surgery. For paired categorical variables, such as OSA status, loud snoring, and daytime sleep episodes, McNemar’s test was applied to assess changes from baseline to postoperative follow-up. When all patients showed a change in the same direction (resulting in no discordant pairs), the descriptive findings were reported instead. Effect size estimation was performed for continuous outcomes only, as effect size measures are not informative for binary variables demonstrating uniform change. A p-value of < 0.05 was taken as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Of the 155 patients assessed for eligibility, 2 patients declined participation, 3 patients were lost to follow-up despite reminders, and the remaining 150 patients were included in the study as shown in Fig. 1. The cohort comprised of 75 males (75/150, 50%) and 75 females (75/150, 50%). The mean age was 44.2 ± 13.8 years, the mean height was 1.77 ± 0.13 m, the mean weight was 152.65 ± 28.6 kg, and the mean neck circumference was 40.5 ± 2.7 cm.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the impact of bariatric surgery on the improvement of OSA-related sleep parameters among obese patients reported at JPMC, Karachi, Pakistan. While the long-term benefits of bariatric surgery on OSA are well established, this study focused on early postoperative symptom dynamics in a public-sector resource-limited setting, thereby addressing a gap in short-term, real-world clinical data.

A total of 150 participants with baseline OSA were included, which was slightly below the calculated sample size due to limited case availability within the study duration, with an equal gender distribution and a mean BMI of 47.8 ± 8.5 kg/m². Following surgery, all patients showed significant improvement in sleep-related symptoms of OSA within six weeks, which persisted at the three-month follow-up. This finding highlights a strong therapeutic effect and aligns with evidence that substantial weight loss improves OSA after bariatric surgery [13, 14]. This improvement refers to clinical improvement rather than objective confirmation via polysomnography, which was not possible in our setting. It’s worth noting that variability in symptom reporting and lack of objective measures may limit comparisons with other studies that often detect residual OSA.

Additionally, loud snoring and daytime sleep bouts decreased significantly in the entire group, suggesting better overall sleep quality and reduced upper airway obstruction. The mean number of nighttime awakenings decreased significantly after surgery (p < 0.001), demonstrating progressive improvement in sleep continuity, with a moderate effect at six weeks and a large effect at three months. This progressive improvement suggests that the physiological benefits of bariatric surgery on airway stability continue to evolve during postoperative recovery, as seen in literature [1, 4]. However, early postoperative symptom improvement may also be influenced by non-specific factors related to postoperative care. These include the use of analgesics, reduced physical activity during recovery, enhanced sleep hygiene, and temporary modifications in sleep posture, all of which may independently reduce symptom perception in the early postoperative period. Such factors could partly account for the symptom relief observed at the six-week follow-up, while the more pronounced improvements at three months are likely to reflect sustained physiological changes associated with weight loss and airway dynamics.

OSA is primarily suspected based on a characteristic history of loud snoring, daytime sleepiness, and increased neck circumference, while overnight polysomnography remains the gold standard diagnostic test [15]. However, because polysomnography is costly, resource-intensive, and not widely accessible, several alternative screening tools and questionnaires have been developed to evaluate OSA severity and monitor postoperative improvement. Reported methods in the literature include the Athens Insomnia Scale, Berlin Questionnaire, and STOP-BANG Questionnaire, each focusing on sleep quality, snoring intensity, and fatigue-related symptoms [1, 16, 17].

Due to the limited resources at our hospital, polysomnography could not be performed. Instead, we assessed postoperative outcomes through a questionnaire specifically designed for this study. It included variables similar to those used in the validated tools mentioned above. This allowed us to correlate our findings with previously published research. These comparisons are intended to demonstrate consistency in the direction of clinical improvement rather than direct equivalence of diagnostic methods or outcome measures.

An earlier Indian study by Priyadarshini et al. assessed the outcome of bariatric surgery in improving sleep quality among patients with OSA. The mean preoperative BMI of their study population was 48.4 ± 8.2 kg/m², similar to the mean preoperative BMI in our study (47.8 ± 8.5 kg/m²) [18]. In their cohort, patients underwent either laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy or laparoscopic Roux-en-Y gastric bypass procedures, similar to the procedures performed in our study. After surgery they found significant reduction in BMI and weight of patients (BMI = 41.2 ± 8.2 kg/m2, p < 0.001) along with improvement in OSA severity and daytime sleepiness confirmed by polysomnography on fifth month of follow-up. These results align closely with our findings, as all patients in our cohort showed complete improvement of OSA related symptoms, including loud snoring and daytime sleep bouts, within three months after surgery and BMI reduced to 40.5 ± 6.4 kg/m2. The consistency between the two studies highlights the strong link between postoperative weight loss and better sleep outcomes following bariatric procedures. However, unlike their study, which used objective polysomnographic assessment, our study relied on subjective, questionnaire-based evaluation. Despite this methodological difference, both studies demonstrated a similar pattern of improvement, reinforcing the positive impact of bariatric surgery on sleep quality and continuity.

The number of nighttime awakenings in patients with OSA is a useful marker in noting the differences after surgery. Our study noted a significant reduction in the mean number of night-time waking events both at 6 weeks and 3 months postoperatively (p < 0.001) and a marked reduction in loud snoring after bariatric surgery. Our results are consistent with findings of Wyszomirski et al., where they reported a significant reduction in the incidence of night awakenings following laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy, with 40% of participants experiencing nighttime awakenings before surgery, which decreased to 25% after six months (p-0.03) [16]. Similarly, the rate of patients reporting loud snoring also declined notably from 60% preoperatively to 38.75% postoperatively (p < 0.05) in their study. Both studies show compelling evidence that bariatric surgery significantly enhances sleep quality and reduces the intensity of snoring. This, in turn, helps relieve symptoms of OSA, leading to better overall rest and well-being.

A recent prospective study by Musleh et al. assessed the impact of laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy in relieving the symptoms of OSA by using STOP-BANG questionnaire. They found that their STOP-BANG score, which correlates well with the Apnoea-Hypopnea Index (AHI) and reliably predicts OSA severity, decreased significantly after bariatric surgery (p < 0.001) [1]. Their results showed improvement in almost all components of the STOP-BANG questionnaire, particularly in loud snoring, daytime fatigue, and sleepiness (p < 0.001 and 0.008). Their results suggest a meaningful resolution of sleep-related symptoms after weight reduction [19]. Our findings showed a similar trend, where daytime sleep bouts and loud snoring improved completely within three months of surgery, indicating a comparable improvement in sleep quality. The consistency among studies indicates that bariatric surgery significantly alleviates symptoms of OSA. It’s also worth mentioning that the results from our questionnaire align well with the validated STOP-BANG screening tool, further strengthening our findings.

An interesting finding observed in our study was that OSA symptoms improved quite early after bariatric surgery, even before substantial weight loss was achieved. This early improvement could be attributed to decreased systemic inflammation and improved airway dynamics, apart from weight loss [20]. Similar early postoperative improvements have been reported in other studies, supporting the idea that bariatric surgery provides metabolic and anti-inflammatory benefits in addition to weight loss [15, 19]. Bariatric surgery has been shown to reduce the chronic low-grade inflammation marked by elevated IL-6 and TNF-α levels by lowering it while increasing anti-inflammatory cytokines like IL-8 [18]. Additionally, a postoperative reduction in soluble TNF-α receptor-2, a known independent predictor of OSA improvement, has also been observed, suggesting a direct link between inflammation modulation and early improvements in sleep-related symptoms [21].

The structural changes in the upper airway after bariatric surgery have been shown to significantly improve the OSA symptoms. A recent interesting study by Zhao et al. assessed the impact of bariatric surgery not only on OSA severity but also on upper airway structural changes, with a particular emphasis on the pharyngeal cavity [4]. Their study showed significant reductions in BMI and AHI values three months after surgery in patients with mild OSA. Beyond symptom relief, they reported anatomical enlargement of the upper airway, including increases in pharyngeal volume, oropharyngeal junction (OPJ) area, and glottal area within six months postoperatively. These structural changes were closely associated with improvements in OSA, suggesting that bariatric surgery alleviates OSA symptoms not only through weight loss but also by enhancing airway patency. Our findings support these observations, showing significant improvements in snoring, daytime sleepiness, and sleep continuity following surgery, which indicates that functional recovery aligns with the airway modifications.

This study has some limitations. Although the calculated sample size was 177, only 150 participants were included due to the limited number of bariatric surgeries performed in the public sector. The relatively small sample size and short postoperative follow-up period may limit the generalizability of the findings, even though the internal validity remains intact. Another limitation of this study is that the questionnaire used to assess sleep-related outcomes was not formally validated. However, it was designed to capture key clinical features of OSA, including loud snoring, daytime sleepiness, and nocturnal awakenings, which align with core components of established screening tools such as the STOP-BANG and Berlin questionnaires. This lack of validated tools restricts standardization and direct comparability with studies using internationally accepted OSA assessment frameworks.

Furthermore, postoperative assessments were conducted before patients achieved their optimal weight loss, which typically occurs between 12 and 24 months after bariatric surgery. As a result, the full extent of improvement in OSA symptoms may have been underestimated. Our study did not control for postoperative factors such as analgesic use, postoperative counseling, or sleep positioning, which may have contributed to early symptom improvement. However, the sustained improvement observed at three months suggests that these factors alone are unlikely to explain the findings fully.

Another limitation is that the study relied on patient-reported symptoms recorded during routine clinical visits, which introduces the potential for recall bias and subjective interpretation. The use of symptom-based assessments may overestimate the apparent improvement of OSA, as residual disease could be present but undetected in the absence of objective testing. Since outcomes were assessed using symptom-based clinical measures in a cohort with uniform postoperative improvement, adjusted analyses were not feasible.

Although the type of surgery (sleeve gastrectomy vs. mini-gastric bypass), baseline BMI severity, comorbidities, and CPAP use may influence OSA outcomes, these variables were not analyzed separately due to the limited sample size and the retrospective, symptom-based nature of our study. Future studies with larger cohorts, longer follow-up durations, formal validation of the symptom-based questionnaire, and incorporation of objective assessments such as polysomnography would help further strengthen these findings.

Conclusion

Bariatric surgery improves the symptoms of OSA in obese patients even before significant weight loss is achieved. The early postoperative benefits include a reduction in snoring, daytime sleepiness, and night awakenings, highlighting the positive impact of weight loss surgery on sleep quality and overall well-being. Our study concludes that bariatric surgery is an effective therapeutic option for clinically improving OSA-related symptoms in obese individuals, particularly in resource-limited settings.
