ATACompass enables cross-species cell identity mapping for scATAC-seq without gene annotations
Authors: Jie Jiang, Yali Ding, Bu Jin, Guole Liu, Siqi Wang, Ying Zhang, Yong Wang, Xin Li, Jing Liu, Wei Li, Guihai Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySingle-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (scATAC-seq) provides insights into transcriptional regulation, but there remain challenges in cell identity annotation due to data sparsity and limited gene regulatory annotations, particularly in non-model organisms. To address these limitations, we present ATACompass, a sequence-based framework for cell annotation that directly encodes chromatin accessibility peak sequences using the DNA foundation model HyenaDNA and large language models. By incorporating controlled sequence mutations for data augmentation, ATACompass achieves performance competitive with or superior to existing state-of-the-art methods in intra-species tasks. Critically, ATACompass enables zero-shot cross-species cell annotation by restricting input data to conserved sequences shared between species, allowing non-model organism data to be annotated without species-specific prior knowledge. Furthermore, the integration of datasets from representative evolutionary lineages enhances predictive accuracy, enabling comprehensive annotation across species. In summary, ATACompass provides a gene annotation-independent framework for studying cell types and regulatory landscapes, especially in non-model organisms.

Introduction

Single-cell sequencing technologies have profoundly advanced our understanding of cellular heterogeneity and diversity across tissues and organisms.1 Compared to single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), which directly quantifies gene expression levels, single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (scATAC-seq) profiles open chromatin regions, enabling to identify putative regulatory elements and explore cell type-specific transcriptional regulatory mechanisms.2,3 By capturing chromatin accessibility, scATAC-seq provides a unique epigenetic perspective on cell identity, state, and regulatory networks at single-cell resolution.4 However, interpreting scATAC-seq data is particularly challenged, primarily due to the intrinsic sparsity of single-cell measurements and the dispersion of functional genomic elements across the genome.5,6 These characteristics complicate the accurate delineation of cell identities from chromatin accessibility profiles.

To address this challenge of annotating cell identities from single-cell or single-nucleus ATAC-seq data, a range of tools primarily based on two distinct strategies have been developed.7 A common approach involves mapping chromatin accessibility fragments to proximal genes to infer gene activity, which is subsequently integrated with prior biological knowledge to facilitate cell type annotation, as implemented in tools such as Signac8 and SingleR.9 Another widely used strategy integrates scATAC-seq or snATAC-seq data with scRNA-seq data from the same tissue or organ, enabling the transfer of cell type annotations between data modalities, as achieved by tools like Seurat,10 scJoint,11 and scDART.12 More recently, deep learning-based models have shown promise by directly annotating cell types using peak-by-cell matrix data from ATAC-seq experiments. Methods such as EpiAnno,13 scATAnno,14 and the emerging SANGO15 have demonstrated improved annotation performance within intraspecies datasets. Nevertheless, the generalizability of these approaches remains constrained by their reliance on prior knowledge or existing datasets. This limitation becomes even especially pronounced in non-model organisms, where high-quality reference datasets are scarce, gene regulatory information remain poorly annotated and relatively understudied.16 Consequently, current methods are unable to perform robust cross-species, zero-shot annotation.

Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs), such as GPT17 and LLama,18 have demonstrated remarkable potential across a wide range of applications. In the biological sciences, these models have already been employed to decode the encoded information and functional elements of DNA, RNA, and protein sequences.19 LLMs have even been applied to annotate cell types from scRNA-seq data.20,21,22 Additionally, various large-scale pretrained models specific to genomic DNA sequences, such as HyenaDNA,23 EVO2,24 DNABERT,25 and GENA-LM,26 have been developed. These models have shown impressive performance on learning the underlying logic of sequence composition and enabling functional characterization.27 Building on the representation power of pretrained sequence models, recent studies have begun exploring sequence-to-function prediction directly from genomic sequences. For example, models such as Scooby28 and UNICORN29 demonstrate the potential to predict gene expression directly from DNA sequences. Considering the rich transcriptional regulatory information encoded in scATAC-seq sequences,2 we hypothesized that coupling these foundational DNA sequence models with an LLM framework could capture the underlying determinants and biological features of chromatin accessibility, support the development of a robust and generalizable approach for cell identity annotation from scATAC-seq data, capable of zero-shot transfer across species without reliance on prior annotations.

Herein, we propose ATACompass, a sequence-based framework for cross-species scATAC-seq cell type annotation. The model directly inputs the most abundant peak sequences detected in scATAC-seq data and pairs them with cell type labels to construct the training dataset. The DNA foundation model, HyenaDNA, was used to encode these sequences into embeddings, which were then combined with text and fed into a LLM for sequence relationship modeling and analysis. To improve the model robustness and performance, we implemented data augmentation by introducing a controlled proportion of random nucleotide mutations during training. ATACompass achieves performance comparable to or surpassing state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods for scATAC-seq annotation. Furthermore, by focusing on conserved sequences shared between training and test species, the model enables zero-shot cross-species annotation, which is a practical capability for annotating scATAC-seq data from non-model organisms. Finally, we integrated datasets from species in representative evolutionary nodes, enabling comprehensive full-branch species scATAC-seq annotation. Overall, ATACompass represents a major advance in scATAC-seq cell type annotation, providing a scalable and versatile solution for characterizing cell types across diverse species.

Results

Overview of the ATACompass workflow

As shown in Figure 1, ATACompass is a sequence-based framework for cell type annotation of scATAC-seq data that leverages a combination of a DNA foundation model and LLMs, using top-ranked peaks from each cell as input without relying on peak-to-gene linkage or high-quality scRNA-seq references. Compared to other DNA foundation models (MegaDNA,30 DNABERT,25 and DNABERT-231), HyenaDNA showed the strongest performance and was consequently adopted in ATACompass (Figures S1A–S1D).Figure 1Schematic overview of the ATACompass framework(A) During pretraining data preprocessing, peak regions from scATAC-seq data are ranked by accessibility, and the top N peaks are selected. During the data preprocessing stage, we addressed the potential issue of peak shifting, which can occur during peak calling across datasets from different sources. To mitigate this, we applied data augmentation by introducing controlled shifts around the detected peaks, enhancing the diversity of the training data and ensuring the model’s robustness across datasets. Furthermore, we augmented the peak sequences by introducing random mutations within a defined proportion M, increasing the variability of the training data (Figure 1A). In the training phase, scATAC-seq samples were processed at the single-cell level. Peaks were ranked by detection frequency, and the most frequently N detected peaks were encoded using a Hyena Operator-based encoder to extract features. These features, combined with cell type labels as text inputs, were fed into the ATAC decoder, a generative language model trained to predict the next token in the sequence. The ATAC decoder ultimately outputted the predicted cell type for the input scATAC-seq data (Figure 1B). The entire training process consists of two stages. In the first stage (pre-training), the ATAC encoder and decoder were trained using a next-token prediction approach, with both regression loss and cross-entropy (CE) loss jointly used to help the model capture relationships both among peak sequences and between peak sequences and cell types. In the second stage (fine-tuning), the model was trained again on the same data using CE loss, further enhancing its capability for cell type annotation tasks. Finally, we froze the model parameters of ATACompass and proceed with testing (Figure 1C).

To evaluate the performance and applicability of ATACompass, we tested the model on cell annotation tasks across three scenarios: datasets from the same source, datasets from different sources, and most importantly, cross-species datasets (Figure 1D).

High performance in intra-species cell type annotation independent of gene annotation

Across the same batch of tasks, we first evaluated the effectiveness of hyperparameter settings and data augmentation strategies using human retinal scATAC-seq data.32 Ablation experiments revealed that optimal performance in cell type annotation accuracy could be achieved by selecting the top 100 most frequently detected peaks, a 10-fold data augmentation and a nucleotide mutation probability of 15% as input (Figures 2A and S2A). And we selected 10 epochs, a batch size of 16, and a learning rate of 5 × 10−5 as the final configuration through analyses (Figures S2B–S2D). Based on these optimized parameters, we further tested ATACompass on scATAC-seq datasets from different organs, including the mouse brain (Mos)33 and human liver.34 ATACompass achieved an average prediction accuracy of 95.98%, demonstrating robust performance with no systematic bias across cell types (Figures 2B, S3A, and S3B). Cell type annotation tasks often rely on domain expertise and curated reference datasets, making cross-batch annotation a common strategy for scATAC-seq data. Similar to intra-dataset annotation, we conducted experiments to assess the impact of hyperparameters and ablation parameters on inter-dataset annotation performance using scATAC-seq data from heart samples35,36 sequenced in different batches. The results mirrored those of the intra-dataset experiments, with the best performance achieved using a 15% mutation rate, 10-fold data augmentation, and the top 100 peak sequences as input and the same training configuration of 10 epochs, a batch size of 16, and a learning rate of 5 × 10−5 (Figures S4A–S4F).

Using this configuration, we tested ATACompass on cross-batch datasets from other organs, including the intestine, pancreas,34,35 and kidney,37,38 achieving an average prediction accuracy of approximately 80% for cell type classification (Figure 2D). Moreover, the predictions exhibited no systematic bias across different cell types within each dataset, further highlighting the robustness of ATACompass (Figures S5A–S5H). To further evaluate the model, we tested it on 7 benchmark datasets33,39comprising 12 evaluated tasks. ATACompass achieved optimal performance on 8 of 12, with an average accuracy of 80.17%, representing a 6.18% improvement over the next best method, SANGO (Figure 2E). For example, on the MosP1-cerebellum dataset, our method effectively distinguished different cell types, whereas most other methods failed to separate endothelial cells from astrocytes (Figure 2F).

In summary, ATACompass encodes enriched peak sequences from scATAC-seq samples using a foundational model and constructs a generative model based on their detection frequency for cell type annotation. By incorporating data augmentation and hyperparameter optimization, ATACompass demonstrated SOTA or near-optimal performance for both intra-dataset and inter-dataset cell type annotation tasks, achieving these results without relying on gene annotations.

Cross-species cell type annotation leveraging conserved peak sequences

By introducing a certain proportion of random sequence mutations during model training for data augmentation, we aimed not only to improve model stability but also to enable the model to perform cross-species annotation of scATAC-seq data. To better adapt ATACompass for cross-species annotation tasks, we adjusted its framework for scATAC-seq cell annotation. We introduced a key modification by aligning detected peak sequences from the test species to the training species and retaining only peaks with a sequence similarity above 60%, which corresponded to the optimal cutoff identified through threshold ablation experiment (Figure S6A). Similar to intra-dataset and inter-dataset annotation tasks, we performed parameter ablation to optimize our approach. Using human intestine scATAC-seq data for training, we applied zero-shot annotation to mouse intestine samples.39 Consistent with earlier findings, the model performed best when using a 15% mutation rate, 10-fold data augmentation, and the top 100 peak sequences as input (S6B and S6C) with the optimal hyperparameter configuration (Figures S7A–S7C). Beyond the intestine samples, we trained our model on scATAC-seq datasets from five additional human organs34,35 and tested corresponding zero-shot annotations in mouse samples39 from corresponding organs. Across all tasks, ATACompass achieved an average accuracy of 72.95% in cross-species prediction (Figure 3B).

Confusion matrix results showed that ATACompass annotated the major cell types of each organ with accuracies exceeding 70% in most cases (Figures 3C and S7D). In the heart, kidney, and brain, ATACompass achieved consistently high accuracy in cell type annotation, with values exceeding 70% for most major cell types, such as cardiomyocytes, podocytes, and astrocytes. Slightly reduced accuracy for some kidney tubule-associated cell types was likely due to similar chromatin accessibility profiles. Across multiple organs, the model effectively classified diverse cell types, including challenging ones like inhibitory neurons and oligodendrocytes, demonstrating robust performance and minimal misclassification (Figure 3D).

By integrating sequence augmentation with random mutations and optimizing its framework, ATACompass successfully enabled cross-species zero-shot scATAC-seq annotation. Trained on human data and tested on mouse organs, the model achieved over 70% accuracy across diverse cell types, demonstrating robust performance and reliability in cross-species annotation tasks.

Using goat ovarian cell types at embryonic day 34 (E34), annotated by experts as the gold standard,41 our model achieved 70.8% accuracy in zero-shot annotation of goat scATAC-seq data by training human ovarian samples40 (Figure 4B). Further analysis revealed that chromatin accessibility signals for PECAM1 (CD31), a marker of endothelial cells, and NANOG, a marker of primordial germ cells (PGCs), were also detected in immune cells (Figures 4C, S8A, and S8B). These overlapping signals likely interfered with cell classification, which may explain the model’s reduced performance for these specific cell types.

Our model leverages sequence-based inputs, enabling the integration of peak sequences from multiple species for joint training. We attempted to incorporate samples from additional species to enhance the model’s cross-species prediction performance. Compared to training solely on human samples, incorporating additional species into the training set led to a remarkable improvement in cross-species prediction accuracy. In both testis and ovary datasets, models trained exclusively on human data exhibited lower accuracy, while progressively adding more species (up to four in the ovary dataset) consistently enhanced performance (Figure 4D). These results highlight the enhanced effectiveness of ATACompass on cross-species cell annotation by leveraging multi-species information.

Confusion matrix analysis showed that integrating multi-species data improved model accuracy across all tested cell types, providing balanced predictions without significant biases (Figure 4E). Importantly, the inclusion of additional species effectively reduced off-target WNT6 peak signals, a key marker for granulosa cells, highlighting the robustness of this approach (Figures 4F and S8C).

These findings prove ATACompass as a powerful tool for universal cross-species zero-shot scATAC-seq annotation without relying on gene annotations. Expanding the training dataset with more representative species consistently enhances prediction accuracy, advancing the study of non-model organisms with limited genomic resources and expanding scATAC-seq applications in comparative and evolutionary biology.

Discussion

Through the development of ATACompass, we provide a sequence-based framework for cross-species zero-shot scATAC-seq cell type annotation, marking a significant advancement in this field. Unlike previous methods that rely on mapping chromatin accessibility information to target genes and inferring cell types based on prior knowledge-based gene annotations,8,9 ATACompass eliminates the dependence on gene annotations while achieving SOTA and even superior performance in intra-species cell type annotation. Furthermore, by introducing a controlled proportion of random mutations during data augmentation, our model demonstrates robust cross-species annotation capability. By incorporating representative species across key evolutionary branches, ATACompass establishes a versatile framework that can theoretically be applied to annotating data from non-model organisms, broadening its utility and significance for diverse biological research.

The ATACompass strategy possesses inherent advantages, as it employs an approach that directly encodes sequences detected by ATAC-seq and leverages a language model to generate cell type annotations. This strategy not only reduces dependency on gene annotations and prior knowledge but also minimizes the influence of uncontrollable data processing steps, such as batch effect correction and data integration. Moreover, the direct sequence-input framework enables highly versatile data augmentation approaches, including adjusting peak shifting positions, introducing controlled proportions of mutations, and modifying mutation site distributions. These augmentation strategies notably enhance the model’s robustness and ensure consistently high performance across datasets. Importantly, by fine-tuning the proportion of introduced mutations, ATACompass exhibits extensive applicability and scalability to diverse or evolutionarily distant species with satisfactory performance.

While ATACompass presents a robust and versatile framework, several avenues for further development remain to be explored. A reliable reference dataset is critical for model training under the current framework; however, identifying high-quality scATAC-seq datasets is substantially more challenging compared to scRNA-seq.5 This raises the possibility of integrating scRNA-seq datasets directly as model references, a strategy that may enhance user accessibility and facilitate broader adoption. In this study, we focused on selecting representative species from key evolutionary branches, enabling the application of the model to a wide range of mammals. Although large-scale pretraining on extensive scATAC-seq datasets could further improve representation quality, the accuracy and reliability of the reference datasets are critical for obtaining biologically meaningful annotations. Meanwhile, generating high-quality cell-type annotations across datasets requires substantial effort. Accordingly, in this work we focused on establishing the ATACompass framework using curated reference datasets, while large-scale scATAC-seq pretraining remains a promising direction as more standardized datasets become available.

Nevertheless, the model’s applicability to more distantly related species requires further investigation. As sequence conservation decreases across species,43,44 differences in gene structure and regulatory elements may reduce the transferability of sequence representations learned from reference datasets. Within ATACompass, such effects can be mitigated by filtering repetitive or low-conservation regions, focusing on accessible peaks with stronger signals and incorporating multiple reference species to broaden the learned regulatory space and reduce lineage-specific bias. In addition, ATACompass may also be applicable to species with limited genomic annotation. When genomic resources are limited, peak sequences may be derived through de novo peak-calling approached,45 and reference datasets from phylogenetically related organisms can then support cross-species prediction. Thus, although cross-species annotation becomes more challenging with increasing divergence, ATACompass remains a practical and extensible framework as genomic resources continue to expand.

In its present design, ATACompass transfers cell identity labels from a reference species through supervised learning. Because of this setup, predictions are restricted to cell types already included in the training set. The method does not directly detect cell types or subtypes that are absent from the reference. Still, cells with weak or uncertain assignments may represent divergent or previously uncharacterized populations. Extending ATACompass to detect new cell types remains for future investigation.

While ATACompass focuses on cross-species cell-type annotation, its sequence-level representations and prediction confidence may also inform regulatory analysis. In cross-species settings, high-confidence predictions likely reflect conserved regulatory programs, on the contrary may indicate regulatory divergence.46 These signals can be used to help rank candidate regulatory regions from scATAC-seq and support comparisons between conserved and species-specific regulation. Exploring this direction in a more direct way would be a useful topic for future work. We also explored the possibility of extending ATACompass to cross-modality prediction tasks such as scRNA-seq. However, preliminary tests showed unstable performance, which likely reflecting the fundamental differences between chromatin accessibility and transcriptional output. Addressing this modality gap will require further methodological development.

Additionally, in our study, we observed that data augmentation by a factor of 10 yielded optimal model performance; however, further expansion could potentially enhance results, though it would impose greater demands on training time and computational efficiency. Optimizing these aspects will be essential for unleashing the full potential of ATACompass in future applications.

In summary, ATACompass establishes a robust and versatile framework for scATAC-seq cell type annotation in both intra-species and cross-species contexts. By eliminating dependence on gene annotations, integrating multi-species datasets, and enabling direct sequence-based annotation, the model offers transformative potential, particularly for studies involving non-model organisms. Furthermore, its approach serves as a valuable reference for advancing methodologies in other omics-related research domains, paving the way for broader applications across diverse areas of biological inquiry.

Limitations of the study

While ATACompass provides a robust and comprehensive framework for cross-species zero-shot scATAC-seq cell type annotation, its performance may become increasingly challenged as evolutionary divergence between species grows. Although we evaluated the framework across several vertebrate species, including chicken and zebrafish (Figure S7A), larger evolutionary distances are often accompanied by substantial differences in genome organization and regulatory architecture. Such divergence may limit the transferability of sequence representations learned from reference species.

Systematic benchmarking across species spanning broader evolutionary distances would therefore be valuable for further assessing the robustness of ATACompass across evolutionary scales and for better understanding how regulatory divergence affects the cross-species transferability of sequence-based representations.

Importantly, ATACompass does not impose intrinsic limitations on the expansion of reference datasets. In principle, this challenge could be mitigated by incorporating well-annotated reference datasets from representative species positioned at key nodes of the phylogenetic tree. By constructing a diverse reference panel covering major evolutionary branches, one could select phylogenetically closer species as references when annotating a target species, thereby improving annotation accuracy despite large evolutionary distances.

Method details

scATAC-seq preprocessing

We downloaded scATAC-seq datasets in various formats from the literature and processed them using Seurat (v4.3.0.1)10 and its companion package, Signac (v1.11.0)8. Peak calling was performed using the CallPeaks() function (MACS256), which utilizes data from all cells and peaks overlapping with ENCODE blacklists59 were removed. Following the quality control criteria outlined in the original publications and applying a blacklist ratio threshold of <0.001, low-quality cells were removed. Normalization was performed using the term-frequency inverse-document-frequency (TF-IDF) method. We then applied PCA for dimensionality reduction, constructed a nearest-neighbor graph, and generated two-dimensional visualizations using UMAP. For both intra-batch and inter-batch datasets, the normalized peak count for each cell was directly extracted for subsequent analysis. Notably, for the adult single-cell transcriptomic atlas dataset, we adhered to the SnapATAC260 analytical workflow, selecting a bin size of 1000 for preprocessing and analysis.

For cross-species datasets, dimensionality reduction was conducted on the predicted datasets using a combination of TF-IDF matrices and singular value decomposition (SVD) on UMAP embeddings. The Louvain algorithm was employed to construct a k-nearest neighbor (KNN) graph for clustering cells, yielding cluster labels for each cell. Given the challenges posed by sequence homology in cross-species datasets, we computed homology between sequences from different species and retained sequences using the optimal similarity threshold identified through ablation analysis for further analysis. Specifically, peaks from the predicted datasets were extracted, and their corresponding DNA sequences were retrieved using BEDTools (v2.31.1),55 followed by saving the sequences in FASTA format. Homology analysis was conducted using BLAT (v.35)57 by comparing the FASTA file of the predicted dataset with the corresponding reference genome of the training dataset. The same process was applied to the training dataset, and peaks with homology exceeding the optimal similarity threshold were retained for subsequent analyses.

Finally, for fair model evaluation across reference–query paired datasets in multiple tasks, cell types present in the query dataset but absent in the reference dataset were removed before prediction.

The architecture of ATACompass

ATACompass aimed at annotating cross-species cell types in a scalable and zero-shot way, shown in Figure 1. In Figure 1A, we provided the data preprocessing and augmentation workflow. In Figures 1B and 1C, we presented the overview of the proposed ATACompass and the training and testing pipeline. In Figure 1D, we demonstrated the tasks that our model could perform.

Input data preprocessing and augmentation

During the data preprocessing stage, we addressed the potential issue of peak shifting by introducing controlled shifts around the detected peaks, while enhancing the diversity of the training data and ensuring the model’s robustness across datasets. Specifically, ATACompass employed a multi-step augmentation workflow, shown in Figure 1A. For each cell, scATAC-seq peaks were ranked according to their counts, reflecting the level of chromatin accessibility at each region. The top N most accessible regions from the ranked list were then selected as input to ATACompass, ensuring that the most biologically relevant and informative sequences were utilized for data analysis. To enhance the diversity and robustness of the input data, we applied two augmentation strategies: Controlled shifts and Random mutations. Controlled shifts involved systematically shifting the detected peak regions within a predefined range to account for potential variability in peak calling across datasets. During this process, we randomly selected a base position within the given chromosomal start-end range as the cropping start point. Using this selected position, we extracted scATAC-seq sequences of a consistent, fixed length L∈R, ensuring uniformity in input length. For sequences shorter than L, we applied padding to extend them to the required length, maintaining consistency across all samples. Following this, we introduced Random mutations into a subset of bases within each sequence. Random mutations introduced stochastic alterations to a subset of bases within each sequence, increasing the variability of the training data and enhancing the model’s generalization ability across species. For each scATAC-seq, we randomly mutated L×k bases with a mutated rate of k∈R. This mutation process was performed by randomly selecting a proportion k of the bases across the sequence and altering them with a mutation operation that randomly replaces them with other bases. Through the above operations, we augmented a single data into multiple instances, effectively addressing the issue of limited training data and the difficulties to handle scATAC-seqs from unseen species.

Model structure

ATACompass is a robust and efficient framework for annotating cell types in scATAC-seq data by directly leveraging DNA sequence information. Figure 1B illustrates its architecture that integrated an ATAC Encoder with an ATAC Decoder.

ATAC encoder

The ATAC Encoder transformed scATAC-seq data into high-dimensional feature representations, which captured key biological information from DNA sequences and supported cell type prediction. Given an input scATAC-seq as x∈RL (L denoted sequence length and was set to 512), the ATAC encoder obtained the feature representation e of input sequence, in the latent space with a HyenaDNA23 based architecture. The ATAC Encoder comprised 2 Hyena blocks, each combining a Hyena Operator and a multi-layer perceptron (MLP). The Hyena Operator can be defined as:x1=Wx1x,x2=Wx2x,v=Wvx(x1,x2,v)↦ee=H(x1,x2)vH(x1,x2)v=Dx2ThDx1where x1, x2, v were transformed vectors of the input x, Wx1,Wx2andWv were learned weight matrices. Th∈RL×L represented a Toeplitz matrix, which was constructed from a learnable long convolution filter h. The filter was produced as the output of a neural network, and its elements were defined as: (Th)ij=hi−j. Dx1,Dx2∈RL×L were matrices with x1 and x2 values placed diagonally and their values were modified using a gating mechanism, which selectively allowed information through based on the matrix values. The MLP layer following the Hyena Operator served as an additional layer of processing to further refine the features extracted by the Hyena Operator.

ATAC decoder

ATAC Decoder was essential for the prediction aspect of ATACompass, transforming the embeddings from the input sequences into meaningful predictions of cell types, and it did so in a way that can handle complex, high-dimensional biological data. The ATAC Decoder took the encoded features from the ATAC Encoder as input. The number of peaks was set to N = 100. Then, the encoded scATAC-seq features (e1, e2, e3, … , e100) were concatenated with structured text prompts like “Providing the top 100 ATAC sequence as: e1e2e3 … e100, the cell type is Adrenocortical Cells” and processed by ATAC Decoder.

The ATAC Decoder was built upon a generative language model framework and performed cell-type prediction. The decoder contained several blocks, each consisting of masked multi-head self-attention, layer normalization, and feedforward layers. The masked multi-head self-attention mechanism ensured that the model only attended to previously generated tokens during decoding, preserving the causal structure necessary for autoregressive generation. Layer normalization was applied to stabilize the learning process and ensured effective gradient flow throughout the network. The feedforward layer, typically composed of fully connected layers with activation functions, allowed the model to learn complex transformations and relationships in the data. Additionally, the decoder utilized residual connections between these components, enabling the model to efficiently learn from the input sequence while maintaining the integrity of the learned representations across multiple layers. These blocks were stacked together to form a deep architecture, which improved the model’s ability to capture long-range dependencies and intricate patterns in the data. The decoder could be replaced with any generative model, and in this paper, GPT-261 was exploited. The GPT-2 consisted of 3 Transformer layers with feature dimension set to 768. During testing, it generated a prediction of the cell type based on the input sequence.

The decoder was trained by predicting the next token of the sequence in an auto-regressive manner. The core of these approaches were language modeling, which was usually framed as unsupervised distribution estimation from a set of examples(x1, x2, …,xn), each composed of variable-length sequences of symbols (s1,s2, …,sn). The process can be represented as:p(x)=∏i=1np(sn|s1,...,sn−1)

Training strategy

ATACompass adopted a two-stage training pipeline to balance generalizability and task specificity.

In stage one, we pre-trained the model for constructing comprehensive understanding of scATAC-seq. The model was optimized with a regression loss LR and a cross-entropy loss LC:LP=LR+λLC,where λ was a weight to balance the two losses and was set to 1 in our implementation. LC was the cross-entropy loss that mapped the sequences to cell types. LR represented the regression loss which helped learn the relationships within the scATAC-seq. We denoted the generated embedding as eˆ1eˆ2eˆ3…eˆN and the regression loss could be formulated as:LR=1N∑t=1N‖et−eˆt‖22

We trained the model for 10 epochs with batch size set to 16 and learning rate set to 5 × 10−5. By pre-training the model with two losses, ATACompass could learn to construct a comprehensive understanding of scATAC-seq. This allowed the model to capture complex patterns and dependencies within the sequences, helping the model build generalized knowledge that can be effectively transferred to downstream tasks.

In stage two, we fine-tuned the model for achieving better performance on cell type annotation task. We employed cross-entropy (CE) loss in this stage. Fine-tuning enabled the model to leverage the general knowledge gained during pre-training while adapting to the specific characteristics of the task at hand, resulting in improved accuracy and efficiency. We fine-tuned the model for 10 epochs with batch size set to 16 and learning rate set to 5 × 10−5.

Training and implementation details

The model was initialized from the pretrained gpt2 backbone. Gradient accumulation steps were set to 1, and mixed-precision (fp16) training was enabled to improve computational efficiency. Evaluation and checkpointing were performed at each step to monitor training stability and performance, and the reported configuration corresponds to the best-performing setting identified during hyperparameter exploration.

For baseline methods, we adopted their officially reported results on the same datasets using the hyperparameter settings described in their original publications.

Inference and cross-species generalization

During inference, ATACompass predicted cell types for the given input scATAC-seq sequences. The parameters of the ATAC Encoder and Decoder were completely frozen, meaning no further updates occur, and the model was applied in a zero-shot way.

Given an input scATAC-seq dataset, the model performed cell-type annotation based on the learned representations of peak sequences. Specifically, peak sequences were ranked by accessibility, and the top-N sequences were selected as input. These sequences passed through the ATAC encoder to obtain latent representations, which were then combined with a text prompt as above mentioned and fed into the ATAC decoder. The decoder predicted the cell type in an autoregressive manner by sequentially generating tokens that represent the most likely cell-type label. The predicted cell type was determined by comparing the similarity between the model’s learned representations and the textual representations of known cell types. The model generated a probability distribution over known cell types based on their similarity to the input representation. The final predicted cell type was assigned as the one with the highest similarity score.

For cross-species cell type prediction, we first obtained the cell types and clusters for each cell. Using these samples as input to the model, we obtained the probability of each sample belonging to a known cell type. Suppose the predicted probability for each cell within one cluster was k1,k2,…,kM∈R1×C (C represented the number of known cell types). We calculated the total probability k0∈R1×C for the cluster by summing up the values of all cells within the same cluster and taking the average. By calculating ki∘k0 and applying Softmax for normalization, we obtained the final predicted probability kˆi for the i-th cell. This approach enhances robustness by integrating individual probabilities with cluster-level information, reducing the impact of technical noise and sequencing biases. Additionally, it improves cross-species generalization by leveraging shared characteristics within clusters, leading to more reliable and stable cell type predictions. The cell type with the highest probability in kˆi was selected as the predicted result for that cell. Through this approach, we successfully enabled cross-species cell type prediction, allowing the model to generalize across different species and accurately annotate cell types in previously unobserved organisms.

The inference process allowed the model to utilize the learned representations without further updates to the parameters, providing efficient and accurate predictions based on the pretrained knowledge. Further, since the model used scATAC-seq as input, it could predict cell types for scATAC-seq from previously unseen species, organs, or tissues. This ability arose from the model’s generalization of learned features during pre-training and fine-tuning, allowing it to handle diverse and novel biological data. This flexibility significantly enhanced the model’s applicability across various research areas, making it a powerful tool for cross-tissue, cross-organ, and cross-species cell type annotation.

Workflow for cross-species annotation in non-model organisms

For a novel non-model organism, ATACompass requires only a reference genome assembly (FASTA) and scATAC-seq peak sets. Peaks are aggregated across cells and ranked by accessibility frequency to define candidate regulatory regions. The ATAC encoder and decoder are pretrained on annotated scATAC-seq datasets from model organisms (e.g., human or mouse). After training, all parameters are fixed and directly transferred to the target species without further fine-tuning. After training, all parameters are fixed and directly transferred to the target species without further fine-tuning. Peak sequences from the target species are encoded using the pretrained ATAC encoder, and conserved regions are selected based on the sequence similarity threshold determined through ablation analysis. The resulting representations are combined with textual prompts of candidate cell types for zero-shot decoding. Per-cell predictions are further refined by cluster-level aggregation to enhance robustness.

Selection of reference species

Reference species were selected based on evolutionary proximity to the target species and the availability of high-quality genomic resources. Well-annotated model organisms with high-quality genome assemblies and scATAC-seq datasets (e.g., human or mouse) were preferentially used, as they provide reliable regulatory annotations and stable training signals. When multiple candidate reference species were available, those with closer evolutionary relationships to the target species were prioritized. In addition, combining datasets from multiple model organisms was used as an alternative strategy to improve cross-species generalization and broaden the learned regulatory space.

Benchmarking against existing methods

To evaluate the performance of ATACompass, we conducted a comparative analysis against Seurat,10 SingleR,9 EpiAnno,13 Cellcano,62 scNym,63 scJoint,11 and SANGO15 in both same-batch and cross-batch cell annotation tasks. For same-batch comparisons, we directly used the prediction results from the SANGO study, which included datasets from different regions of the mouse brain’s secondary motor cortex. Similarly, for cross-batch evaluation, we leveraged SANGO-reported predictions on mouse brain datasets.

Phylogenetic tree construction and visualization

To reconstruct the evolutionary relationships among 81 vertebrate species, we first compiled a list of scientific species names representing major mammalian clades. The species list was submitted to TimeTree58 (https://timetree.org/), a resource for divergence time estimation based on published molecular clock analyses. Pairwise divergence times were retrieved and used to generate a Newick-formatted tree file (.nwk), which encodes the phylogenetic relationships along with estimated divergence times in millions of years ago (MYA).

The resulting tree was visualized using the Interactive tree of life (iTOL, https://itol.embl.de/) web-based tool.64 Clade-specific annotations were applied to distinguish major taxonomic orders, including Artiodactyla, Carnivora, Cetartiodactyla, Chiroptera, Perissodactyla, Pinnipedia, Proboscidea, Rodentia, and Primates. Each clade was assigned a distinct color, and representative species were illustrated using silhouette icons for enhanced interpretability. A scale bar representing divergence time (in MYA) was included to indicate branch lengths.

Chromatin accessibility visualization

Coverage plots were generated using the Signac package8 (v1.11.0). Fragment files from the query dataset were first processed to create a fragment index, which was then integrated into the Signac framework. Genomic accessibility across regions of interest was visualized using the CoveragePlot() function, enabling high-resolution inspection of chromatin accessibility at single-cell resolution.

Evaluation metrics

For evaluation, the model’s predictions were compared against ground-truth cell-type labels using accuracy (ACC) and F1-score. Accuracy was a fundamental metric used to evaluate the overall correctness of a classification model. It was defined as the ratio of correctly predicted instances (both true positives and true negatives) to the total number of instances. It was formulated as:Accuracy=TP+TNTP+TN+FP+FNwhere TP (True Positives) denoted correctly predicted positive instances, TN (True Negatives) denoted correctly predicted negative instances, FP (False Positives) denoted incorrectly predicted positive instances and FN (False Negatives) denoted incorrectly predicted negative instances. F1-score was a widely used metric for classification tasks, especially when dealing with imbalanced datasets. It was the harmonic mean of precision and recall, providing a balance between the two metrics. It was formulated as following:Precision=TPTP+FPRecall=TPTP+FNF1-score=2×Precision×RecallPrecision+Recall

Datasets used in this study

We collected multiple publicly available scATAC-seq datasets to evaluate ATACompass across intra-species, cross-batch, and cross-species annotation tasks. The human retina dataset32 was generated using 10× Genomics snATAC-seq technology65 and annotated with the hg19 reference genome. Mouse motor cortex datasets33 represent anterior, middle, and posterior sections of the secondary motor cortex and were aligned to the GRCm38 reference genome. Human fetal atlas datasets (liver, heart, intestine, and pancreas) were generated using sci-ATAC-seq334 and annotated based on the hg19 reference genome. Additional adult human atlas datasets (heart, intestine, pancreas, cerebellum, lung, and liver) were generated using sci-ATAC-seq35 and annotated with the hg38 reference genome. Human heart dataset36 and human kidney datasets37,38 used in the cross-batch and cross-species tasks were generated using 10× Genomics snATAC-seq and aligned to the hg38 reference genome. For cross-species annotation, mouse organ datasets including lung, kidney, liver, heart, large intestine, small intestine, whole brain, cerebellum, and prefrontal cortex were obtained from the adult mouse atlas39 and aligned to the mm9 reference genome. Gonadal datasets from human,40 mouse, macaque, pig, and goat41 were also included for cross-species evaluation. In addition, chicken and zebrafish scATAC-seq datasets from Han et al.47 were used in the similarity-threshold ablation analysis.

Quantification and Statistical analysis

For intra-batch analyses, prediction accuracy was calculated for each cell type. Each point in the boxplots represents the accuracy of an individual cell type within a given dataset. Boxplots summarize the distribution of accuracies, where the center line indicates the median, boxes represent the interquartile range (IQR), and whiskers extend to 1.5 × IQR. For cross-species analyses, prediction performance was evaluated across different training species compositions. For testis and ovary evaluation, each point represents the accuracy for an individual sample (n = 5 samples per group). Statistical significance between groups was assessed using a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, as indicated in the figures. No multiple testing correction was applied unless otherwise specified. All analyses were performed in Python (version 3.8) using standard scientific computing libraries.
