China’s actions for post-political commitment to global strategy to accelerate cervical cancer elimination
Authors: Hanying Huang, Chaobo Du, Guangqiong Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChina is among the largest contributors to the global cervical cancer burden. Problems such as insufficient vaccine supply, high vaccine prices, and insufficient policy support hindering vaccine popularization need to be solved.MethodsIn the present study, we employed a qualitative research methodology to retrieve and organize policy documents on cervical cancer from 31 provincial and central governments by the documentation method.ResultsThe current relevant action program of the Chinese government places strong emphasis on screening and treatment; however, the absence of a consistent national HPV vaccination policy remains the most critical gap in China’s strategy to achieve elimination.DiscussionBased on the findings of this study, several key recommendations emerge to advance China’s political commitment to the Global Strategy to Accelerate the Elimination of Cervical Cancer.

Background

Cervical cancer ranks as the fourth most prevalent cancer among women globally [1]. Acknowledging this public health concern, the World Health Organization (WHO) has recommended adopting and implementing a national immunization program [2]. In 2020, the WHO launched a global strategy for eliminating cervical cancer, establishing specific targets for attainment by 2030 [3]. To achieve these targets set by the Global Strategy until 2030, measures have been specified, including full human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination of 90% of girls by the age of 15, high-performance testing for cervical cancer screening of 70% of women by 35 years of age and again by 45 years of age, and guaranteeing that 90% of the women diagnosed with cervical disease have received appropriate treatment [3].

The HPV vaccine is a main weapons for cervical cancer [4]. Persistent HPV infection stands out as the leading cause of cervical cancer [5, 6]. Vaccines constitute primary prevention that can better prevent the occurrence of cervical cancer and more effectively reduce health care system burden than secondary prevention [7, 8]. The HPV vaccine is the cornerstone of cervical cancer prevention, providing primary protection and preventing over 90% of cases when nine-valent vaccines are used [2]. Hence, vaccination is a powerful and effective way to prevent cervical cancer. From 2006, when the first HPV vaccine was invented, to June 2020, the 107 WHO member countries included HPV vaccination in their national immunization programs [9].

Albeit with the proven efficacy of vaccination, limited production and high vaccine prices have contributed to low and disparate cervical cancer vaccination rates, especially in affluent and less privileged populations [10]. Only 13% of 9–14-aged girls worldwide received the HPV vaccine in 2020 [10]. Australia is the birthplace of the HPV vaccine; in 2020, the well-implemented vaccination program in this country contributed to an age-standardized incidence rate of 5.6 (per 100,0000 women) of cervical cancer cases attributable to HPV [1, 11, 12]. This rate in the United States, which is the largest HPV vaccine exporter, is 6.2 (per 100,0000 women), which is higher than that in Australia [1]. Although the United States introduced the HPV vaccine in 2006, inadequate policies have resulted in uneven vaccination rates across different ages, regions, genders, and subpopulations in the country [8].

As one of the largest contributors to the global burden of cervical cancer, China faces unique challenges. In 2020, China recorded an age-standardized incidence rate of 10.7 (per 100,0000 women) for HPV-attributable cervical cancer cases, a figure nearly double that of the United States and Australia [1, 13]. In 2021, an estimated 109,741 new cases and 59,060 deaths from cervical cancer were recorded [14]. One of the primary factors contributing to this phenomenon is that China is quite rigorous in introducing vaccines. Due to the absence of local clinical trial data and different approval criteria, China experienced a delay of approximately a decade in introducing the HPV vaccine [15, 16]. The introduction of foreign bivalent vaccines (Cervarix) occurred in 2016, followed by foreign tetravalent vaccines (Gardasil 4) in 2017, and foreign nine-valent vaccines (Gardasil 9) in 2018 [17]. The major reasons for this status quo in China are the following: (1) inadequate publicity for HPV vaccination; (2) restrictions on the eligible age for HPV vaccination; (3) an insufficient supply of imported nine-valent vaccines that cannot meet the demand due to China’s large population base; and (4) unwillingness and unaffordability of ordinary families for the imported high-cost nine-valent vaccine [18].

In response to these challenges, China has been taking steps to align with the WHO’s Global Strategy to Accelerate Cervical Cancer Elimination [19]. The National Health Commission stated that to protect women and girls from cervical cancer, China would gradually roll out free HPV vaccination nationwide, starting with pilot areas [19]. A domestic bivalent vaccine was launched in 2019 with sufficient supplies [20]. Additionally, domestic quadrivalent and nine-valent vaccines are under development [21]. Some provinces in China began to promote free HPV vaccination in 2020 [17], and the government expanded the vaccination age range for the nine-valent HPV vaccine from 16–26-year-old to 9–45-year-old females to be achieved by August 31, 2022 [22].

Notwithstanding these endeavors, the current state of cervical cancer prevention and treatment in China remains significantly distant from fulfilling the objectives set forth by the WHO. As a country committed to international obligations, China aims to accelerate cervical cancer elimination by 2030 [23]. To align with the 2030 WHO target, the Chinese government has initiated measures subsequent to making a robust political commitment to the Global Strategy to Accelerate Cervical Cancer Elimination. In this study, we analyzed 31* provincial and central government policy documents on cervical cancer, offering a comprehensive examination of the actions undertaken to achieve the 2030 Cervical Cancer Elimination target. In addition, several practical recommendations are proposed.

* China has 34 provincial governments, but Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Macau have not been included in this study because of the policy of “One Country, Two Systems.”

Methods

In this study, we employed a qualitative research methodology to examine policies related to cervical cancer. Using the documentation method, we systematically retrieved and organized policy documents on cervical cancer issued by 31 provincial and central governments. The primary data were then coded for policy instruments, following the methods outlined by McDonnell & Elmore and Schneider & Ingram [24, 25]. Two independent researchers performed these processes concurrently. In cases of disagreement, both researchers engaged in discussions to reconcile differences until a consensus was reached. Most provincial and municipal materials are internal notices or government website postings that lack DOIs/ISBNs and are not publicly archivable. Per journal policy, these cannot be listed as references nor posted as supplementary files. We therefore cite only national-level, stable policy documents in the references and provide full transparency via: (i) a reproducible search protocol (platforms, terms, and dates), (ii) an editor-only Document Inventory with metadata for all items, and (iii) province-level aggregates in the main text.

Search strategy

Searches were conducted using the official websites of the Central People’s Government, the National Health Commission, the National Working Committee on Children and Women under the State Council, and provincial people’s governments and women’s federations from January 4, 2023 to February 6, 2023. In consultation with university professors trained in policy management, key search terms for cervical cancer and related policies were used in the search strategy, including “cervical cancer,” “HPV,” “HPV vaccine,” “women’s development plan,” and “healthy initiative.” Finally, 82 policy texts were retrieved (Fig. 1).

Data collection

Two major categories of literature sources were included. The first category was a collection of central and provincial government policy texts on cervical cancer prevention and treatment, including material from three sources: the Accelerated Cervical Cancer Elimination Initiative, the Outline for Women’s Development, and the Healthy China Initiative. The selection of these three materials was driven by their alignment with the WHO’s cervical cancer elimination cycle. They serve as manifestations of China’s commitment to WHO’s initiative for cervical cancer elimination. Moreover, these three text profiles originated from high-hierarchy institutions of China’s policy system, distinguished by a top-level design and consistency over time, thereby mirroring the steadfast determination of the government.

The second type of literature source consisted of information on cervical cancer control pilots. To conduct relevant case studies, we selected six pilots from the 15 pilot cities promoting cervical cancer prevention and treatment based on their economic level, including highly economically developed, middle-class, and backward areas. These sources were searched through the official websites of the local governments and the local health commissions.

For the present analysis, we restricted our coding and interpretation to policy and strategy documents directly addressing prophylactic HPV vaccination. Screening and treatment-related documents were excluded from detailed discussion.

Data analysis

An inductive analysis approach was used in the present study. Bottom-up induction, comparison, and thematic analysis of the texts were performed, and critical codes were defined. The data analysis process comprised two primary steps.

In the first (transcription) step, information was extracted from the original text and transcribed into tables. We organized the data according to the issuing unit, policy documents, and three levels of prevention (vaccine, screening, and treatment). Then, we sequentially extracted the content of the relevant documents collected from the central and provincial governments for inclusion in the tables. Two scientists entered the data. Then, an overall analysis of the Chinese government’s action strategy was conducted based on the extracted raw data.

In the second step, the raw data obtained were used to define and code the policy instruments using the methodology proposed by McDonnell & Elmore and Schneider & Ingram for policy literature analyses [24, 25]. We next created six policy tools based on the delineation of policy tools suggested by these authors: authority tools, inducements, capacity-building, system-changing, symbolic and hortatory tools, and learning tools (Table 1). The coding was done by two specialists. The completed coding was further employed to analyze the proportion of various policy instruments used by the Chinese government.

Authority tools refer to the degree of people’s compliance with policy [25]. The use of other instruments, such as monetary incentives or penalties, is not required; the implementation of these tools is based on the government’s authority. For example, a government notification requiring the implementation of domestic bivalent HPV vaccination for school-age girls is a firm order. Inducements are the use of short-term monetary rewards or other incentives to get people to comply with the policy [24]. While inducement tools focus on the present, capacity-building tools, such as training, organizing learning, and improving competence in a particular area, are used for their longer-term impact [24]. System-changing tools indicate the changes in the distribution of authority throughout the system [24]. For example, in the past, all HPV vaccines were imported, whereas now domestic HPV vaccines are to be introduced, which is a system change. Symbolic and hortatory tools are high-profile oaths emphasizing the benefits, such as raising women’s awareness of cervical cancer prevention and control [25]. Learning tools are typically pilots. Different policy approaches were tried on a small scale in selected regions to explore feasible policies [25].

Results

Chinese overall policy strategy includes vaccination, screening, and treatment; however, this analysis focuses specifically on HPV vaccination policies [3]. The current relevant action program of the Chinese government, factoring in these three objectives (vaccine, screening, and treatment), demonstrates a greater emphasis on screening and treatment, with some mandatory objectives. However, the Chinese government’s policy regarding vaccination is vague. Notably, economically backward regions and areas populated by ethnic minorities seem to have made more efforts to promote HPV vaccination. On the contrary, of the economically developed regions, Zhejiang shows no advancements in this domain, whereas weak positive trends are evident in Beijing and Shanghai. In our view, the best explanation for this situation is that the incidence of cervical cancer has already been controlled well in economically developed areas. More explanations and detailed elaboration are given in the Discussion section.

Here, we provide a two-part response to the research question of how China has acted after committing to eliminating cervical cancer. In the first part, we provide evidence that the Chinese government used three strategies for action:

Symbolic declaration: This involves a political gesture to make such a statement above the highest level of government. Use of specific policy tools in 31 provinces and municipalities

Additionally, 93.5% (29/31) of the provinces and municipalities used symbolic and hortatory tools to raise women’s awareness and ability to prevent and control cervical cancer, of which 17 provinces and municipalities required that the knowledge rate of cervical cancer prevention and control should reach more than 90% (Fig. 2). In pursuit of the objective of ensuring that 90% of girls complete HPV vaccination by the age of 15, 45.2% (14/31) of the Chinese governments have used learning tools. Nine of the 15 Healthy China pilot cities have taken the lead in offering free or subsidized HPV vaccination. Finally, 48.4% (15/31) of the provinces and cities have used inducements for vaccination. Five of these provincial governments (Guangdong, Hainan, Fujian, Jiangsu, and Jiangxi) have included free HPV vaccination for women of appropriate age throughout the province (Fig. 3).

In addition, all provinces and municipalities have leveraged capacity-building tools to bolster the targeted capacity for cervical cancer prevention and control to address their shortcomings. These capacity-building efforts encompass the implementation of regular screenings for female workers, harnessing the role of commercial insurance as a safeguard, integrating artificial intelligence technology, ensuring sustained and stable funding, training technical cadres, and refining the linkage mechanism and the supervision mechanism for screening, diagnosis, and treatment. As of now, the Chinese government has not implemented any policies related to the use of systemic change tools. However, high-valent (9-, 11-, and 14-valent) domestically produced vaccines are currently under development and are anticipated to be included in the Chinese National Immunization Program [28]. The domestically produced nine-valent HPV vaccine is projected to be widely available by 2025.

Discussion

Reflections on the single-dose vaccine policy

According to the results we obtained, each province has its screening indicator requirements. However, some provinces and cities still need improvement in terms of treatment. Despite the burgeoning availability of HPV vaccines in China, numerous technical hurdles remain to be addressed. For example, the research and development of domestic vaccines are still underway and imperfect; the supply of imported vaccines is insufficient, the queue for appointments is long, and the price of imported nine-valent vaccines is high, hindering their popularization. Additionally, inadequate policy support has been provided. Some cities have begun the implementation of free vaccination. However, only five cities have fully committed to implementing free province-wide vaccination, accounting for 16.1% (5/31) of mainland China’s total provinces and cities.

Meanwhile, foreign countries like Australia and the United Kingdom began implementing a single dose of the HPV vaccine [29, 30]. A recent study showed that a single dose of HPV vaccine is effective in preventing infection with HPV types 16 and 18, which are responsible for 70% of cervical cancer cases [31]. Therefore, the implementation of a single-dose HPV vaccine would markedly enhance the vaccination situation in China. Unfortunately, China has not yet enacted a relevant policy for its widespread administration.

Hence, it is essential for the Chinese government to bolster its support for domestic HPV vaccine research and development initiatives, surmount technical barriers through scientific and technological innovation, and expedite the resolution of the issue regarding domestic HPV vaccine coverage. Given the effectiveness of single-dose HPV vaccines, China could introduce this new vaccination program and formulate policies accordingly. Simultaneously, the media should strengthen the publicity about the single-dose vaccine to raise public awareness and increase the motivation for the implementation of this program.

Most effective strategy – the combination of vaccination and screening

According to our present results, some provinces and cities focus on vaccination while others focus on screening. First, economically disadvantaged regions like autonomous areas implemented more vaccination policies than developed regions. However, in autonomous areas such as Inner Mongolia and Xizang, these vaccination policies were only implemented in individual urban areas when they were first enacted. These policies were not advanced to perfection and disappeared soon. In contrast, vaccination were planned and programmed to be included in the local livelihood programs in the better-off regions. This process was supported by long-term subsidies and the construction of ever-expanding vaccination sites.

Although the focus of policies varies between provinces and municipalities, numerous studies have showcased that screening combined with HPV vaccination is the most cost-effective strategy [20]. The nine-valent HPV vaccine is expected to be more effective than the four- and two-valent ones for all HPV-associated diseases (cervical and non-cervical). The nine-valent vaccine also reduces the incidence of HPV-associated disease in a shorter period than other vaccines [32]. Furthermore, it is crucial to recognize that preventing cervical cancer isn’t solely reliant on vaccination; regular screening every five years thereafter is equally vital [20]. After all, the Gardasil 9® vaccine prevents only around 90% of the disease. The newly developed 14-valent vaccine offers broader cervical cancer protection (above 95%) than Gardasil 9® but still does not contain all disease-causing HPV types [28]. However, the vaccine can quickly control the incidence of cervical cancer and can considerably reduce treatment costs [20, 33, 34]. Therefore, some developed areas can not only focus on screening, it is continuously imperative to popularize vaccination in China, a country with a high incidence of cervical cancer.

Therefore, to ensure full vaccination policy implementation, China’s provincial and municipal governments should closely scrutinize the promotion and implementation of cervical cancer prevention and control policies, especially in economically disadvantaged areas. Moreover, regular screening and education campaigns should emphasize that vaccines are not a substitute for routine cervical cancer screening to raise women’s awareness of cervical cancer prevention and encourage regular health checkups.

Future policy perspectives

The results obtained showed that a big gap persists between China’s current HPV vaccination rate and the WHO target of 90% coverage for 15-year-old girls. A few schools have already begun to perform uniform vaccination, which is worth emulating. Overseas studies in the United States and Canada also demonstrated that HPV vaccination in schools can be a quick and effective tool for preventing cervical cancer [35, 36]. Additionally, previous investigations have indicated the necessity for HPV vaccination among men as well [37, 38]. This need arises not only from men’s poor hygiene habits, which can increase the risk of cervical cancer in women, but also from the fact that vaccination in men can offer protection against penile, anal, and oropharyngeal cancers [37, 39].

Therefore, China should learn from successful cases abroad and further promote uniform vaccination in schools to ensure that more school-age girls can promptly receive the free HPV vaccine. China can also expand the target group of HPV vaccination to include males. Furthermore, the promotion of HPV vaccination for men would reduce the risk of cervical cancer transmission and prevent other related cancers.

Future prospects

This policy study analyzed government policies to identify policy gaps and implementation shortcomings and propose solutions. Future research could further explore the possibility of implementing single-dose immunization based on the results of this qualitative study of cervical cancer policy. Given the current scarcity of the nine-valent HPV vaccine, the Chinese government should implement single-dose immunization. In addition, the bivalent and quadrivalent HPV vaccines could be included in China’s National Vaccine Immunization Programme (NVIP) due to their widespread availability, especially since the bivalent vaccine has been mass-produced domestically in China.

Conclusion

In congruence with the WHO’s requirements, our analysis highlights that while China has made progress in screening and treatment, the absence of a robust, consistent HPV vaccination policy is the major barrier to achieving cervical cancer elimination. Strengthening HPV vaccination strategies, ensuring equitable access, and integrating vaccination into national programs are urgent priorities.
