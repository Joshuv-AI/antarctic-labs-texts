Waist-to-height ratio and the incidence of rapid kidney function decline and chronic kidney disease: insights from the China health and retirement longitudinal study
Authors: Xinzhong Zhang, Xiaoying Ren, Xiaona Chang, Jia Liu, Guang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to assess the association between waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) and the risk of rapid kidney function decline (RKFD) and chronic kidney disease (CKD).MethodsThis study analyzed 4374 participants from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). WHtR was calculated as waist circumference (cm) divided by height (cm). RKFD was defined as an annualized estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) decline ≥ 5 mL/min/1.73 m2, and CKD as RKFD with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 at the final follow-up. Linear mixed-effects models were used to assess the association between baseline WHtR and annual changes of eGFR. Cox proportional hazards models were employed to estimate the association between baseline WHtR and the risks of RKFD and CKD. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was conducted explored the dose-response relationships.ResultsOver a mean 3.99-year follow-up, 200 participants (4.57%) developed RKFD, and 66 (1.51%) developed CKD. Each 1-SD increase in WHtR was associated with a greater decline in eGFR (β = -0.242, 95%CI: -0.355 to -0.130) and increased risks of RKFD (HR = 1.298, 95% CI: 1.111 to 1.517) and CKD (HR = 1.518, 95% CI: 1.184 to 1.945) after full adjustment. Compared with the first WHtR quartile group (Q1), the risk of RKFD was significantly increased in Q3 (HR = 1.969, 95%CI: 1.234 to 3.142) and Q4 groups (HR = 2.203, 95%CI: 1.360 to 3.568), whereas the risk of CKD was increased in Q4 group (HR = 3.931, 95%CI: 1.618 to 9.549). RCS analysis demonstrated a positive linear association between baseline WHtR and the incidence of RKFD and CKD (P for nonlinear = 0.403 and 0.492, respectively).ConclusionsElevated WHtR is significantly associated with accelerated eGFR decline and higher risks of RKFD and CKD in middle-aged and older Chinese adults.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04474-9.Keywords: Waist-to-height ratio, Rapid kidney function decline, Chronic kidney disease, Obesity

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is characterized by persistently reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and/or elevated urinary albumin, affecting approximately 9.1% of the global population, with nearly a third of patients living in China and India [1]. CKD progresses insidiously, with symptoms such as nausea and pruritus appear in advanced stages when structural kidney damage is largely irreversible and kidney failure imminent [2]. Rapid kidney function decline (RKFD) is defined by an accelerated annual eGFR decline, frequently precedes the incidence of CKD and is a strong predictor of mortality [3, 4]. Unlike static kidney function measures, identifying individuals at risk of RKFD enables earlier detection and provides a critical window for preventive intervention before irreversible damage occurs.

Obesity has been recognized as a risk factor for kidney dysfunction, mediated through altered renal hemodynamics, insulin resistance, hypertension and chronic inflammatory [5]. Abdominal obesity may exert mechanical pressure on the kidneys, reduce renal blood flow, activate the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and trigger oxidative injury that promotes tubular injury and apoptosis [6]. Our group demonstrated that insulin resistance plays a significant role in the incidence of RKFD [7]. Recent studies have demonstrated a causal link between a higher body mass index (BMI) and CKD [8]. However, BMI fails to distinguish between adipose and muscle mass and the distribution of adipose tissue [9]. By contrast, visceral adiposity is more closely linked to metabolic syndrome and adverse renal outcomes than general obesity [10]. Waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) is simple, inexpensive and increasingly recognized as a reliable marker of central obesity than BMI in predicting metabolic and cardiovascular risk [11–13].

Importantly, middle-aged and older adults face not only age-related decline in kidney function but also a high prevalence of metabolic and cardiovascular comorbidities that may accelerate renal deterioration. China, bearing a heavy global burden of CKD, urgently requires evidence to identify high-risk individuals and inform preventive strategies. Yet, prospective data on the association between WHtR and RKFD or CKD in this population remain scarce.

Therefore, we conducted a 3.99-year prospective analysis among middle-aged and older Chinese adults in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to examine the association between WHtR and the risks of RKFD and CKD.

Methods

Study population

This study utilized data from CHARLS, a nationally representative longitudinal survey of Chinese adults aged 45 years and older. CHARLS covered 150 counties or districts and 450 villages or communities across 28 provinces. The first survey was collected in 2011, with follow-up surveys conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018 and 2020. CHARLS was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

For this study, data from the 2011 baseline and 2015 follow-up were used. Of the 17,705 participants enrolled at baseline, we excluded those with: [1] missing data on gender, age or age < 45 years at baseline (n = 408); [2] missing data on eGFR at baseline or follow-up (n = 11790); [3] missing data on WHtR at baseline (n = 700); [4] extreme WHtR values [14] (n = 75); and [5] baseline eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (n = 358). After these exclusions, 4374 participants were included (Fig. 1).

Data collection and measurements

Trained professionals collected data on participants’ basic demographic characteristics, health-related behaviors, medical history and fasting blood samples. Blood sample analyses only collected in 2011 and 2015, included fasting plasma glucose (FPG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), serum cystatin C and serum creatinine. All blood analyses were conducted at the Youanmen Centre for Clinical Laboratory, Capital Medical University.

Variable definitions

The WHtR was calculated as waist circumference (WC) (cm) divided by height (cm). BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Hypertension was diagnosed based on self-reported history, an average systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg or use of antihypertensive medication [15]. Diabetes was diagnosed based on self-reported history, FPG ≥ 126 mg/dl, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5% or use of antidiabetic medication [16]. The eGFRcr-cys (hereafter referred to as eGFR) was calculated using the combined creatinine-cystatin C Eq. [17], as shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of RKFD, defined as an annualized eGFR decline ≥ 5 ml/min/1.73 m2 [7]. The secondary outcome was the incidence of CKD, defined as RKFD with eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73 m2 at the final follow-up [7]. Annualized eGFR decline was calculated as (eGFR at baseline - eGFR at follow-up) / follow-up time (years).

Statistical analyses

Participants were categorized into four groups according to the quartiles of baseline WHtR (Q1 ≤ 0.496, 0.496 < Q2 ≤ 0.538, 0.538 < Q3 ≤ 0.587, Q4 > 0.587), with Q1 as the reference. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as numbers (percentages).

Linear mixed-effects models with a random intercept were used to assess the association between baseline WHtR and annual change in eGFR. Fixed effects included WHtR, time (years) and their interaction (WHtR × time). Covariates included age, gender, SBP, DBP, TC, HbA1c, smoking, drinking, marital status, residence, education and baseline kidney disease, along with their interactions with time. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between baseline WHtR and the risks of RKFD and CKD. Three models were constructed based on prior studies [7, 18]: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, gender, SBP, DBP, TC, HbA1c and baseline eGFR; Model 3 was further adjusted for smoking, drinking, marital status, residence, education and baseline kidney disease. Restricted cubic spline regression (RCS) was used to assess potential nonlinear associations, using the WHtR median (WHtR = 0.538) as the reference. RCS models with 3–6 knots were compared, and the optimal number of knots was selected by the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) to minimize overfitting and improve interpretability (Supplementary Table 2). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC) were used to assess predictive efficacy of WHtR.

Subgroup analyses were stratified by age, gender, residence, drinking, smoking, marital status, diabetes and hypertension. Sensitivity analyses included reclassifying participants into high (WHtR ≥ 0.5) and low (WHtR < 0.5) groups and excluding individuals with baseline kidney disease. All statistical analyses were two-tailed tests, with a P value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed using R software (version 4.4.2) and GraphPad Prism software (version 10.4.0).

Results

A total of 4374 participants were included, comprising 2001 male (45.75%) and 2373 female (54.25%), with a mean age of 59.25 ± 8.83 years. The mean baseline eGFR was 87.76 ± 14.41 mL/min/1.73 m2. Participants with higher WHtR levels were more likely to be female and to have hypertension and diabetes. Table 1 presents the characteristics of participants stratified by WHtR quartiles. Association between WHtR and annual change in eGFR

In linear mixed-effects models (Table 2), WHtR was inversely associated with annual change in eGFR. Each 1-SD increase in WHtR was associated with a greater decline in eGFR (β = -0.242, 95%CI: -0.355 to -0.130) after adjusting the age, gender, SBP, DBP, TC, HbA1c, smoking, drinking, marital status, residence, education and baseline kidney disease. In quartile analyses, compared with the first WHtR quartile group (Q1), the annual decline in eGFR was significantly greater in Q3 (β = -0.419, 95%CI: -0.712 to -0.125) and Q4 groups (β = -0.656, 95%CI: -0.969 to -0.342).

Association between WHtR and the risks of RKFD and CKD

Over a mean 3.99-year follow-up, 200 participants (4.57%) developed RKFD, and 66 participants (1.51%) developed CKD. Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the relationship between baseline WHtR and the risk of RKFD and CKD (Tables 3 and 4). Each 1-SD increase in WHtR was associated with a 29.8% higher risk of RKFD (HR = 1.298, 95%CI: 1.111 to 1.517) and a 51.8% higher risk of CKD (HR = 1.518, 95%CI: 1.184 to 1.945) after adjusting for age, gender, SBP, DBP, TC, HbA1c, baseline eGFR, smoking, drinking, marital status, residence, education and baseline kidney disease. Similar results were also seen in the analysis stratified by WHtR quartiles. Compared with the first WHtR quartile group (Q1), the risk of RKFD was significantly increased in Q3 (HR = 1.969, 95%CI: 1.234 to 3.142) and Q4 groups (HR = 2.203, 95%CI: 1.360 to 3.568), whereas the risk of CKD was increased in Q4 group (HR = 3.931, 95%CI: 1.618 to 9.549).

In RCS analysis with 3 knots, WHtR showed a linear association with the risks of both RKFD and CKD after full adjustment (P for nonlinear = 0.403 and 0.492, respectively), as shown in Fig. 2. Furthermore, in ROC analysis, WHtR demonstrated better predictive ability than BMI for both RKFD and CKD (Supplementary Fig. 2). Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses stratified by age, gender, residence, smoking, drinking, marital status, diabetes, and hypertension revealed no significant interactions (Supplementary Figs. 3–4). Sensitivity analyses had consistent results. Participants with WHtR ≥ 0.5 had higher risks of RKFD (HR = 1.659, 95% CI: 1.114 to 2.472) and CKD (HR = 2.384, 95% CI: 1.108 to 5.128) compared with those with WHtR < 0.5 (Supplementary Tables 5–6). Meanwhile, the results remained robust after excluding participants with baseline kidney disease (Supplementary Tables 7–8).

Discussion

In this 3.99-year prospective cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, elevated WHtR was independently associated with a greater decline in eGFR and higher risks of RKFD and CKD after full adjustment. The association between WHtR and the risks of RKFD and CKD was linear. Moreover, ROC analyses indicated that WHtR thresholds of 0.559 and 0.567 could identify individuals at elevated risk for RKFD and CKD, respectively.

Our findings were consistent with prior studies associating central obesity with adverse renal outcomes. Cross-sectional analyses from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey reported positive associations between WHtR and CKD prevalence and albuminuria [18], while a Swedish 18-year cohort demonstrated that higher WHtR predicted CKD incidence [19]. Moreover, Shen et al. showed WHtR was a strong predictor of all-cause mortality among patients with CKD [20]. Our study extends these observations across Chinese population, reinforcing WHtR as a robust marker for kidney function decline.

Compared with traditional obesity indices such as BMI, WHtR better reflects central adiposity and its metabolic consequences, including hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and metabolic syndrome, all of which contribute to renal dysfunction [21–23]. Obesity has been implicated in the development of focal segmental glomerulosclerosis and glomerulomegaly [24]. Visceral adiposity has a greater risk of metabolic syndrome than subcutaneous adiposity [10] and acts as a metabolically active organ, releasing free fatty acids, triglycerides and ceramides. This lipid overload triggers low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress, thereby disrupting renal homeostasis and initiating tubular cell injury [25–27]. These fat depots also secrete excess leptin and reduced adiponectin, promoting endothelial dysfunction and progressive glomerulosclerosis [28–30]. Obesity-related insulin resistance further amplifies activation of the RAAS, resulting in hemodynamic imbalance and structural damage [31]. Additionally, fat accumulation in the perirenal and renal-sinus regions imposes physical compression on intrarenal vessels and tubules, impairing venous outflow [32]. This combination of metabolic stress, hemodynamic overload and physical compression creates a sustained injurious environment, which may explain the graded associations observed between WHtR and both RKFD and CKD risk in our study.

RKFD has been increasingly recognized as a suitable alternative endpoint for kidney disease, given its strong association with adverse outcomes such as heart failure, myocardial infarction, and stroke [33, 34]. RKFD often precedes overt CKD and may remain undetected in routine clinical practice, early identification of high-risk individuals is critical for timely prevention and intervention. In our study, we found a linear association between baseline WHtR and the risks of both RKFD and CKD. ROC analyses identified optimal WHtR thresholds of 0.559 for RKFD and 0.567 for CKD, indicating that risks increasing when WHtR exceeds these values. These thresholds are broadly consistent with the widely accepted 0.50 cut-off used to define central obesity across different sexes and ethnicities [35, 36]. Sensitivity analyses further confirmed that participants with WHtR ≥ 0.5 had higher risks of RKFD and CKD compared with those below this level. In addition, we found that WHtR was a better predictor of RKFD and CKD than BMI, aligning with previous studies that WHtR provides better predictive value for metabolic diseases. For example, a study in Jilin Province identified 0.5337 as the optimal WHtR threshold for predicting diabetes [37], while a recent meta-analysis demonstrated that WHtR had significantly greater discriminatory power for cardiometabolic risk factors than BMI [21]. Our results reinforce WHtR as a practical and robust anthropometric index for detecting early kidney risk and offering clinically meaningful thresholds for risk stratification.

Utilizing a large-scale, nationally representative dataset with a mean follow-up of 3.99 years, this study demonstrated that higher WHtR was significantly associated with faster eGFR decline and increased risks of RKFD and CKD. The use of both serum creatinine and cystatin C to estimate eGFR enhanced the reliability of kidney function assessment. These findings underscore the clinical value of WHtR as a simple anthropometric measure for identifying individuals at elevated risk, thereby offering opportunities for earlier intervention to prevent kidney function deterioration. Nevertheless, several limitations should be noted. First, participants were limited to middle-aged and older Chinese adults, which may restrict the generalizability of our findings to younger populations or other ethnic groups. Second, other renal injury markers, such as urinary albumin, were not available in CHARLS, preventing a more comprehensive evaluation of kidney damage. Third, eGFR was measured at baseline and 2015 follow-up, which reduced the precision of estimating the rate of eGFR decline and made it impossible to identify participants who had already experienced RKFD at baseline. Finally, the mean follow-up duration was 3.99 years, which is relatively short to capture longer-term kidney function changes. Future studies with longer follow-up periods, more frequent biomarker assessments, and inclusion of additional renal injury indicators are needed to validate and extend our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, higher WHtR is significantly associated with accelerated decline in eGFR and elevated risks of RKFD and CKD in middle-aged and older Chinese adults. WHtR, as a simple anthropometric measure, may serve as a practical screening tool to identify high-risk individuals for early intervention to prevent kidney function deterioration.
