In silico assessment of cellular damage from Lu‐177, Ac‐225, and Pb‐212 therapeutic radionuclides
Authors: Konstantinos P Chatzipapas, Konstantinos Papachristou, Dimitris Visvikis, Sebastien Incerti, John D Hazle, George C Kagadis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTargeted radionuclide therapy (TRT) has emerged as a unique and effective treatment modality for cancer. Monte Carlo simulations have greatly advanced investigations into radiation‐caused DNA damage, including the complexity of this damage.PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate DNA damage induced by high‐linear energy transfer therapeutic radionuclides used in TRT, specifically 225Ac, 177Lu, and 212Pb, using Geant4‐DNA Monte Carlo simulations.MethodsThe Geant4‐DNA toolkit, incorporating the “molecularDNA” example, was employed to simulate radiation interactions within a human fibroblast cell model featuring a fractal chromatin fiber geometry within an ellipsoidal nucleus. Three source geometries (membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus) were modeled to assess the impact of radionuclide localization. Key metrics, including absorbed dose, double‐strand break (DSB) yield, single‐strand break/DSB ratio, and DSB/Gbp/decay, were calculated for 225Ac (alpha emitter), 177Lu (beta emitter), and 212Pb (mixed alpha/beta emitter). Simulations accounted for physical, physicochemical, and chemical stages, with validation against published data for 177Lu and 225Ac.ResultsAlpha emitter 225Ac exhibited the highest DSB/Gbp/decay (1.646 in nucleus geometry) and absorbed dose (0.256 Gy/decay), followed by 212Pb (0.455 DSB/Gbp/decay, 0.0684 Gy/decay), and 177Lu (0.0058 DSB/Gbp/decay, 0.0007 Gy/decay). DSB yields increased with proximity to the nucleus, with 225Ac showing up to 284 times greater DSB/Gbp/decay than 177Lu. Validation showed < 10% divergence from reference studies.ConclusionsGeant4‐DNA simulations highlight the superior radiobiological effectiveness of alpha emitters, particularly 225Ac, for inducing DNA damage and emphasize the importance of source localization. These findings enhance our understanding of TRT's radiobiological effects and can be used to support development of refined therapeutic strategies.Keywords: dosimetry, Geant4‐DNA, molecular radiotherapy, Monte Carlo simulations, radionuclide therapy

INTRODUCTION

Targeted radionuclide therapy (TRT), or simply radionuclide therapy, has emerged as a unique and notably effective treatment modality for cancer. By combining the principles of molecular biology with the cytotoxic effects of radiation, TRT can deliver a highly concentrated radiation dose to tumor cells while minimizing adverse effects on surrounding healthy tissues. 1 This selectivity has made TRT especially appealing for the treatment of malignant systemic diseases like bone metastases, where the whole‐body exposure of external beam radiotherapy is not the optimal choice. 2 TRT targets tumors via tumor‐specific carrier molecules—such as monoclonal antibodies, peptides, and other specific ligands—conjugated with appropriate therapeutic radionuclides. These molecules generally bear a high affinity for tumor cells, so their radioactive payload accumulates in the tumor site, sparing normal tissues. 1 The three components on which the success of TRT depends are the radionuclide, the transporter or carrier molecule, and the tumor target. A typical radiopharmaceutical consists of these three components linked together through a chelator, which binds the radionuclide to the transporter (Figure 1).

‘Typical’ Radiopharmaceutical product.

Unlike external beam radiotherapy, which allows for dose fractionation in scheduled sessions, TRT emits a continuous dose to the tumor over time to limit tumor cells’ DNA damage repair mechanisms and, thus, their ability to proliferate. The selection of the radionuclide is based on the physical and biochemical properties of the radionuclide, which determine the radionuclide's behavior in the body and its ability to target tumors effectively. TRT is applied in treating various types of cancer, such as hepatocellular carcinoma with ⁹⁰Y in transarterial radioembolization, 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 neuroendocrine tumors with 177Lu‐DOTATATE, 3 , 7 , 8 , 9 and metastatic prostate cancer using 177Lu‐PSMA. 10 , 11 TRT strategies with 2 2 3Ra and 153Sm are also effective for pain palliation in patients with bone metastases. 7 , 12

Monte Carlo (MC) simulations have greatly advanced studies into the cellular damage caused by radiation, especially at the DNA level. MC simulations are extremely valuable in modeling not only the physical interactions of ionizing particles with biological media, but also the additional stages of action in which free radicals produced through radiolysis interact with DNA and other macromolecules. Furthermore, these models allow a detailed and complex quantitative description of the interactions of macromolecules such as DNA with charged particles, including heavy ions, and the damage caused by these interactions. Because MC simulations allow an understanding of each of these damage stages during tissue irradiation, they offer an incredibly wide range of applications extending from medical imaging and therapy to space exploration.

A distinctive example of MC's medical applications is the Geant4‐DNA toolkit, which extends the capabilities of the Geant4 toolkit to simulate particles’ track structures, water radiolysis, and the biological damage induced by ionizing radiation in liquid water, the primary component of biological systems. 13 The Geant4‐DNA extension includes a detailed set of physics processes for electrons, protons, neutral hydrogen, alpha particles, and various ions. 14 Processes such as ionization, excitation, elastic scattering, and charge exchange are modeled with cross‐sectional data down to the electron‐volt scale. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 MC track structure models enable high‐resolution simulations at the nanometer scale, a level of resolution that is essential for microdosimetry studies. 18 , 19 , 20 However, as MC track structure codes are computationally intensive, studies tend to propose fast approaches, where precalculated track structures are used to estimate both the dose and the damage, using 177Lu. 21 , 22 In addition, other studies are focused on dosimetry and S‐values calculation, for b‐emitters. 23 , 24

Alpha particles, due to their short range in tissues—comparable to the size of individual cells—are ideal for treating small tumors and micrometastases. 25 , 26 It should be noted that alpha emitters such as 225Ac have a complex decay chain, which introduces challenges such as the migration of daughter radionuclides after recoiling, which in turn can reduce treatment efficacy and cause off‐target tissue toxicity. Therefore, understanding the absorbed dose contributions from each decay product and the effect of the radiopharmaceuticals’ behavior inside and between cells is crucial for optimizing TRT. 27 To this end, advanced simulation tools such as Geant4‐DNA are increasingly used to accurately model these processes at the cellular level and support the development of safer and more effective alpha particle‐based treatments. 28 , 29 , 30 However, existing studies mainly emphasize dosimetric modeling and physical calculations, 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 but relatively limited data are available on biological effects such as DNA damage, especially for 225Ac and 212Pb. Therefore, in the present study, we investigated the cellular damage induced by the alpha‐ and beta‐emitting radionuclides 225Ac and 177Lu, at both dosimetric and biological levels.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We used 177Lu for validation because it is therapy‐relevant and has been extensively studied. Numerous experimental and computational works have reported calculations for cellular uptake, S‐values, and DNA damage with 177Lu, 35 , 36 , 37 giving us the necessary background information to validate our simulation methodology and biological damage models. Following validation of the method with 177Lu, the same methodology was applied for the simulation and analysis of the biological impact of 225Ac and 212Pb. As a result, direct comparison between these radionuclides was feasible in terms of energy deposition and DNA DSB induction.

MolecularDNA

This study used molecularDNA, 30 , 31 , 32 a Geant4‐DNA advanced example, which is a user‐friendly tool for simulating radiation‐induced DNA damage. The molecularDNA includes DNA models of different size, from simple linear DNA and plasmids, up to bacterial cell and human cell, including chromosomal geometries. It simulates both the direct radiation‐matter interaction and the indirect, chemical interactions induced by water radiolysis. The synchronous independent reaction time (IRT‐sync) chemistry model and the G4EmDNAChemistry_option3 list are utilized, based on methodologies developed by Plante and Devroye. 38 These functionalities allow for advanced simulations of DNA damage mechanisms, including both the damage complexity (i.e., SSB, DSB, DSB+, etc.) and the source of damage, either direct or indirect.

Physics (Geant4‐DNA option 4)

The electromagnetic Geant4‐DNA physics list option 4 is designed to enhance the accuracy of Geant4‐DNA simulations, particularly for electron interactions with liquid water. This list adopts Emfietzoglou's optical‐data model 39 for the dielectric function of liquid water, as implemented by the algorithm developed by Kyriakou and co‐workers. 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 This model allows electron transport from 10 eV up to 10 MeV in liquid water medium accounting for both condensed‐phase and molecular effects in a physically consistent manner. DNA option 4 currently incorporates the most accurate EM physics model for electrons in Geant4‐DNA. The maximum distance from the DNA, where deposited energy is considered to cause DNA damage, is known as the range for direct interaction (Rdir), and was set at 2 Å, following literature recommendations. 44 , 45 In addition, the minimum (Ebreakmin) and maximum (Ebreakmax) energy thresholds, which relate to the total energy in a particular region of the DNA, were set at 5 and 37.5 eV, respectively.

Chemistry

For the chemical stage, the Geant4‐DNA chemistry module was implemented; this module uses the independent reaction time (IRT) method. In the IRT method, events are defined as reactions between pairs of reactants, improving computational efficiency by reducing the number of time steps required. For this study, the optimized IRT‐sync was used, and damage production was limited to molecules within a specified distance of 9 nm (rkill) of the DNA chain, reflecting the likelihood of scavenging for radicals at greater distances. The chemical stage was limited to 9 ns, following recommendation of the literature. 45 , 46 Most reactions occurred between OH• and the sugar‐phosphate groups or bases. The efficiency of OH•‐induced DNA single‐strand breaks (SSBs) was set to 40%, which led to about 13% of these reactions resulting in double‐strand breaks (DSBs), consistent with previously published data. 44 , 47 Reaction rates and diffusion constants for other radicals remained unchanged from Geant4‐DNA defaults.

DNA geometry

In Geant4‐DNA simulations, a fractal structure such as the Hilbert curve is used to represent chromatin fibers in a continuous and space‐filling manner. The fractal chromatin fiber geometry incorporates histones to model DNA folding, which is confined within a cubic volume of 75 nm per side. This allows for highly compact and realistic DNA configurations, enabling the study of DNA interactions, damage, and repair mechanisms in simulations. The fractal approach also facilitates integration into larger cellular geometries, providing a foundation for more comprehensive biological modeling. 48

Human cell geometry

The human cell geometry in molecularDNA extends the fractal DNA models 49 to a full cell nucleus, enabling detailed simulations of subcellular processes. The nucleus is represented as an ellipsoid with dimensions of 14.2 µm × 14.2 µm × 5.0 µm containing approximately 6.4 Gbp of DNA organized in a continuous Hilbert curve‐based chromatin fiber, as seen in Figure 2. 47 This arrangement results in a base pair density of about 0.015 bp/nm3, reflecting typical nuclear densities. The ellipsoidal mask defines the nucleus's shape, ensuring that only chromatin segments completely within the ellipsoid are included in the model. Beyond the nucleus, the cytoplasm is represented as an ellipsoid of water with dimensions of 28.0 µm × 28.0 µm × 5.0 µm, corresponding to a total cellular volume of approximately 2052 µm3, which is the typical volume of a human fibroblast. This setup accommodates simulation of a wide range of biological phenomena, from DNA damage and repair to radiation interactions. The model's adaptability ensures simulation of different cell types and configurations.

Source description

In our study, an ellipsoid surface source was used in three different setups as seen in Figure 3. This methodology was followed since radionuclides are either attached to the nucleus, the cytoplasm or the cellular membrane. In addition, they can travel to neighboring cells causing harm. To this purpose we adjusted the default human cell‐case that includes an outside source to this more specific approach, to cover these different potential situations. Both the human cell and its DNA were unaffected.

□ Membrane: The source was homogeneously distributed around the outer boundary of the cell, mimicking radionuclides located on or near the cell membrane. The corresponding ellipsoid with dimensions of 28.0 µm × 28.0 µm × 5.0 µm fully enclosed the nucleus and cytoplasm (Figure 3a).

Membrane: The source was homogeneously distributed around the outer boundary of the cell, mimicking radionuclides located on or near the cell membrane. The corresponding ellipsoid with dimensions of 28.0 µm × 28.0 µm × 5.0 µm fully enclosed the nucleus and cytoplasm (Figure 3a).

□Cytoplasm: The source distribution was positioned in a 21.0 µm × 21.0 µm × 5.0 µm ellipsoidal region centred between the nuclear envelope and the cell membrane. This configuration simulates radionuclides located within the cytoplasm (Figure 3b).

Cytoplasm: The source distribution was positioned in a 21.0 µm × 21.0 µm × 5.0 µm ellipsoidal region centred between the nuclear envelope and the cell membrane. This configuration simulates radionuclides located within the cytoplasm (Figure 3b).

□Nucleus: The source was placed directly around the cell nucleus, simulating radionuclides interacting locally with nuclear material. The ellipsoid defining this setup had dimensions of 14.2 µm × 14.2 µm × 5.0 µm, matching the shape of the modeled nucleus (Figure 3c).

Nucleus: The source was placed directly around the cell nucleus, simulating radionuclides interacting locally with nuclear material. The ellipsoid defining this setup had dimensions of 14.2 µm × 14.2 µm × 5.0 µm, matching the shape of the modeled nucleus (Figure 3c).

In every geometry shown in Figure 3, light blue indicates the source distribution relative to the nucleus (in darker blue) and the whole cell body (in gray). These configurations were designed to capture different biological scenarios, such as membrane‐bound targeting agents, cytoplasmic localization, and direct nuclear delivery.

An important consideration for the source is that each radionuclide represented in the respective decay chain was simulated individually. In addition, with regard to the number of simulated radionuclide decays, initial numbers of 10 nucleus decays for alpha emitters and 2000 nucleus decays for beta emitters were used. Regarding this point, it should be considered that, due to the complex physics of these simulations—and most importantly their chemistry—emissions of 10 high‐LET alpha and 2000 beta particles produce high numbers of secondary particles and chemical reactants, leading to sufficient energy deposition (i.e., ∼5 Gy) and DNA damage to obtain accurate, meaningful, and unbiased results. In addition, the simulation of this number of particles requires extensive computational resources, and the augmentation of primaries would lead to extremities.

177Lu simulations

After the validation process, numerous simulations were performed with different geometries for 177Lu decay. Figure 3 indicates the differences between these geometries. The parameters calculated were Gy/decay and DSB/Gbp/decay, for 10,000 initial 177Lu nuclei.

225Ac simulations

Continuing with the same simulation philosophy, for the 225Ac decay, simulations were performed for 10 alpha‐emitting and 2000 beta‐emitting nuclei. The quantities calculated were again DSB/Gbp/decay and Gy/decay. Three different geometries were used for the 225Ac investigation, as for 177Lu. All the simulations were conducted with the step‐by‐step framework, meaning that each decay product was simulated individually, taking into consideration all the radionuclides in the main 225Ac decay chain.

212Pb simulations

The final radionuclide examined in the present study was 212Pb. The simulations had the same characteristics as those for 225Ac and were performed for the same geometries. The number of nuclei was 10 for alpha emitters, 2000 for beta emitters, and 50 in the case of 212Bi because of its hybrid nature, emitting both alpha and beta particles.

Validation

A two‐step validation process was followed. First, this study's models were compared in terms of 177Lu irradiation induced DNA damage, to validate cell geometry, physical and chemical interactions against published data. The second step was to validate the physics model and the cell geometry, regarding the irradiation with alpha emitters and their dosimetric effect when compared to the literature. The ultimate purpose of these two steps is to introduce the chemical interactions and damage quantification by radionuclides emitting alpha and/or beta particles in the modeling, and share the integrated procedure with our scientific community.

The radiobiological part of this study was validated through a review of the literature on 177Lu to quantify and compare the quantity DSB/Gbp/decay. Specifically, we compared the Cytoplasm geometry in a study by Tamborino et al. 37 with the Cytoplasm geometry in our study. Three cellular models from the reference study were investigated, named Cell 1, Cell 2, and Cell 3. Their geometric characteristics are described in Table 1.

DISCUSSION

The results show a clear dependence of DNA damage on absorbed dose based on both the radionuclide type and the location of the plane source. 225Ac, a high‐energy alpha emitter, consistently produced the highest values for both DSB yield and dose per decay across all geometries. This is expected because alpha particles deposit energy in a highly localized manner, causing more clustered DNA damage and higher absorbed doses than beta particles. The DSB yield for 225Ac was highest when the source was in the nucleus (1.646 DSB/Gbp/decay), followed by the cytoplasm (1.115 DSB/Gbp/decay) and the membrane (0.785 DSB/Gbp/decay). The same trend held for the dose per decay, which was highest in the nucleus (0.256 Gy/decay) and lowest in the membrane (0.0861 Gy/decay). This reflects the short range of alpha particles in biological tissues, meaning that their effects are most pronounced when the source is close to the target.

212Pb, a beta‐emitting radionuclide with some alpha‐emitting daughters, exhibited an intermediate radiobiological effect, between those of 225Ac and 177Lu. Its DSB yield and dose per decay values were consistently lower than those of 225Ac but substantially higher than those of 177Lu. When the source was placed in the nucleus, 212Pb produced a DSB yield of 0.455 DSB/Gbp/decay and a dose per decay of 0.0684 Gy/decay. In the cytoplasm and membrane, these values decreased accordingly. The presence of some alpha contributions by the decay chain of 212Pb may explain why it still causes considerable DNA damage, but with a lower impact than that of 225Ac.

177Lu, as a pure beta emitter, showed the lowest values for both DNA damage and absorbed dose. Beta particles have a longer range but a lower LET than alpha particles, leading to more sparsely distributed damage that is less effective at causing DSBs. The DSB/Gbp/decay for 177Lu remained extremely low across all geometries, with values of 0.00580 in the nucleus, 0.00460 in the cytoplasm, and 0.000361 in the membrane. Similarly, the absorbed dose per decay was markedly lower, with 0.000679 Gy/decay in the nucleus, and even lower in the cytoplasm and membrane. This confirms that beta emitters are less effective in producing clustered DNA damage than are alpha emitters, making them less threatening to the nucleus when the source is placed outside of the nucleus.

The ratios of damage yield between radionuclides (Table 9 ) show clear variations in radiobiological effectiveness in accordance with the radionuclides’ physical decay, a finding also supported by the literature. 50 , 51 Τhe DSB/Gbp/decay ratio of 225Ac to 177Lu was more than 240 in all calculations conducted in the present study, and more than 60.5 DSB/Gbp/decay per emitted alpha particle, illustrating the documented high relative biological effectiveness (RBE) of alpha particles compared to beta particles. Experimental studies have shown that the most frequent RBE values for alpha emitters are 3 to 7 times higher than those of beta emitters, depending on the type of irradiated cell and the biological endpoint. 50 , 51 The high ratios presented here, exceeding 300 in terms of absorbed dose, can be reasonably explained by considering that each 225Ac decay event produces multiple high‐LET alpha particles along its decay chain. In comparison, the ratio of 212Pb and 177Lu were lower (44.8 for DSB/Gbp/decay in the nucleus geometry), as only a fraction of 212Pb decay results in alpha emissions through its daughters such as 212Bi and 212Po. The same pattern is evident in 212Pb /225Ac ratios, as 225Ac provides a higher total alpha energy output per decay. It must be stated that alphas (as independent particles) produce similar DNA damage in both cases. However, alphas emitted by 225Ac are more penetrating, resulting in a larger ratio for the membrane and cytoplasm geometries (∼0.73), whereas if the source was in the nucleus the ratio was ∼1.1, meaning that the alphas emitted by 212Pb have a shorter range and produce more damage closer to the point where they are produced.

Overall, these findings demonstrate how important the type of radionuclide and its spatial distribution are in determining biological outcomes. Alpha emitters like 225Ac deliver highly localized, high‐LET radiation that maximizes DNA damage when placed in direct proximity to the nucleus. In contrast, beta emitters like 177Lu provide a more uniform but lower‐intensity dose, making them less effective for causing direct DSBs but potentially more suitable for broad irradiation of surrounding tissues. 212Pb, with its mixed emissions and fewer decay products, falls between these two extremes, showing a moderate level of damage and dose deposition. The extent of the observed differences, including the DSB and absorbed dose ratios, come with remarkable alignment with the established radiobiological principles and reported RBE values in the literature. However, it needs be stated that such simulations need extensive resources and the availability of a private cluster with large amount of RAM (i.e., 5 GB per core) and number of cores, as well as available time, because each high‐LET particle needs about 5 days of simulating when chemistry is involved.

CONCLUSION

This study aimed to improve our scientific knowledge about these effects specifically for 225Ac, as there is a lack of radiobiological research for this radionuclide. The simulation results confirmed that alpha emitters deposit energy densely, compared to b‐emitters such as the 177Lu, causing higher DSB yields and absorbed doses, while beta emitters produce more dispersed DNA damage, with a higher SSB/DSB ratio. More precisely, the values for the nucleus geometry, in which the source was enclosed by the nucleus, were 1.646, 0.563, and 0.0058 for 225Ac, 212Pb, and 177Lu, respectively for the DSB/Gbp/decay metric, and 0.256, 0.0906, and 0.0006792 for the Gy/decay metric.

Another innovative aspect is the placement of the source, as DNA damage and dose deposition increases as the source moves closer to the nucleus, which was evident through our simulations of the three different geometries. Furthermore, the analysis of radionuclide ratios showed consistent alignment with known energy outputs and literature‐based RBE values, 50 , 51 supporting the accuracy of the simulation results. Such ratio‐based comparisons reinforce the interpretation of radiobiological effectiveness across radionuclides, and further validate the model's biological relevance.

In conclusion, the use of Geant4‐DNA, including the molecularDNA example, allowed us to perform precise and accurate modeling of DNA damage. Use of this method will help to enhance and refine computational radiobiology. To our knowledge, no other study has directly and indirectly quantified DNA damage across multiple geometries and varied simulation parameters. The integration of comparative dose and DSB ratios, supported by literature‐derived RBE values, further strengthens the biological interpretation of the results. Future studies can build on this study's radiobiological insights, especially for 225Ac, for which radiobiological knowledge remains limited, to expand its potential applications in cancer treatment. Incorporating additional features such as DNA repair modeling and long‐term cellular responses can lead to more definitive results, improving personalized treatment strategies and enhancing the effectiveness of TRT.
