The mediating role of fasting plasma glucose and systolic blood pressure in the association between Chinese visceral adiposity index and carotid atherosclerosis: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mingyue Qu, Yang Qu, Yang Luo, Haixia Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVisceral adipose tissue dysfunction is a key driver of cardiometabolic disease, yet the pathways linking it to atherosclerosis remain incompletely understood. This cross-sectional study included 6767 adults from a general health check-up population in Shanghai and investigated the association between the Chinese Visceral Adiposity Index (CVAI) and carotid atherosclerosis (CAS), as well as the mediating roles of systolic blood pressure (SBP) and fasting plasma glucose (FPG) using parallel mediation analysis. After full adjustment for confounders, each standard deviation (SD) increase in CVAI was associated with 37% higher odds of CAS (OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.26–1.49), demonstrating a significant linear dose-response relationship (P-overall < 0.001). Parallel mediation analysis revealed that SBP and FPG jointly mediated 59.2% of the total effect of CVAI on CAS, with SBP and FPG accounting for 36.0% and 23.2%, respectively, while a significant direct effect persisted (40.8%, P = 0.025). The association was consistent across all subgroups without significant interaction (all P-interaction > 0.05). Introduction

Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases remain the leading causes of global mortality and morbidity, with atherosclerosis as their primary pathological foundation1,2. Carotid atherosclerosis (CAS), due to its non-invasive assessment by carotid ultrasound and its ability to reflect systemic atherosclerotic burden, is widely regarded as a valuable surrogate marker for cardiovascular risk stratification3,4. Traditional measures of overall adiposity such as body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC) are limited in their ability to capture differences in adipose tissue distribution and metabolic activity, particularly visceral adipose tissue5, which exhibits higher endocrine and inflammatory activity and is more strongly linked to cardiometabolic outcomes6,7.

To address the limitations of simple anthropometric measures, composite indices such as the Visceral Adiposity Index (VAI) were developed, integrating metabolic and anthropometric parameters to better estimate adipose tissue dysfunction and cardiometabolic risk8,9. However, VAI was derived from Caucasian populations and may not optimally capture the characteristics of visceral adiposity in Chinese individuals. In contrast, the Chinese Visceral Adiposity Index (CVAI) was specifically developed and validated for the Chinese population10, incorporating age, WC, BMI, triglycerides, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol to provide a more comprehensive assessment of visceral adipose dysfunction. Compared with traditional indices and VAI, CVAI has demonstrated superior performance in predicting cardiometabolic risk and incident cardiovascular events in Chinese cohorts11–13. In large population-based studies, higher CVAI levels have been associated with increased risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases14, supporting its utility for risk stratification in both clinical and population settings.

Despite these advances, the mechanisms through which visceral adiposity contributes to atherosclerosis remain incompletely understood. Blood pressure and glucose metabolism are key determinants of vascular health; elevated systolic blood pressure (SBP) induces mechanical stress and endothelial dysfunction15, while hyperglycemia promotes oxidative stress, inflammation, and impaired vasodilatory responses16,17. Investigations have shown that the strength of the association between traditional obesity indices (e.g., BMI, WC) and CAS is significantly attenuated when blood pressure or blood glucose variables are included in statistical models18,19. This phenomenon suggests that metabolic dysregulation may play key mediating roles in the association between visceral adiposity and vascular pathology. However, conventional regression models provide only indirect evidence and cannot quantify the relative contributions of intermediary factors such as SBP and fasting plasma glucose (FPG) in the visceral adiposity–CAS pathway. Therefore, elucidating the potential pathways in this link necessitates the application of statistical methods like mediation analysis20, which can decompose total effects into direct and indirect effects and quantify their respective contributions.

In the present cross-sectional study of 6767 adults, we examined the association between CVAI and CAS and quantified the mediating roles of SBP and FPG. We hypothesized that SBP and FPG would significantly mediate the CVAI-CAS association, providing mechanistic insight into how visceral adipose dysfunction contributes to atherosclerotic processes and offering potential targets for integrated risk management.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study participants

This study included a total of 6767 participants, with an age range of 18–90 years (median age: 47 years). Among them, 4656 (68.8%) were male and 2111 (31.2%) were female. Participants were divided into three groups according to CVAI tertiles: Q1 (CVAI ≤ 67.35, n = 2256), Q2 (67.35 < CVAI ≤ 105.43, n = 2255), and Q3 (CVAI > 105.43, n = 2256).

As shown in Table 1, compared to the Q1 group, participants in the Q3 group were older and had significantly higher anthropometric measures (height, weight, BMI, WC, hip circumference [HC]). Simultaneously, they exhibited higher levels of blood pressure (SBP and diastolic blood pressure [DBP]), blood lipids (triglycerides [TG], total cholesterol [TC], low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C]), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), serum creatinine(Scr) and inflammatory markers (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein [hsCRP], and systemic immune-inflammation index [SII]), while high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels were significantly lower. Furthermore, the proportions of males, current smokers, current alcohol drinkers, and individuals with abdominal obesity, hypertension, diabetes, or metabolic syndrome were higher in the Q3 group. All differences between the two groups were statistically significant (P < 0.001).

Association between CVAI and CAS risk

Multivariable logistic regression analyses demonstrated a significant positive association between CVAI and the risk of CAS across all models (see Table 2). For each standard deviation increase in CVAI, the risk of CAS was significantly elevated: the odds ratio (OR) was 2.32 (95% confidence interval [CI] 2.18–2.47; P < 0.001) in the unadjusted Model 1; 1.44 (95% CI 1.32–1.56; P < 0.001) in Model 2 after adjusting for age, sex, smoking, and alcohol consumption; and 1.37 (95% CI 1.26–1.49; P < 0.001) in the fully adjusted Model 3, which further included FPG, LDL-C, Scr. When analyzed by CVAI tertiles, using the Q1 group as the reference, both the Q2 and Q3 groups showed a significantly higher risk of CAS in the fully adjusted Model 3. The ORs were 1.32 (95% CI 1.10–1.58; P < 0.001) for the Q2 group and 1.71 (95% CI 1.41–2.07; P < 0.001) for the Q3 group, revealing a significant dose-response trend (P-trend < 0.001).

The mediating roles of SBP and FPG in the association between CVAI and CAS

To explore the potential mechanisms linking CVAI to CAS, a mediation analysis was employed. After adjusting for age, sex, alcohol consumption, smoking, LDL-C, and Scr, parallel mediation analysis based on structural equation modeling revealed that both FPG and SBP played significant mediating roles in the relationship between CVAI and CAS. Notably, the FPG pathway contributed 23.2% of the total mediating effect (β = 0.0076, P < 0.001). The mediating contribution of the SBP pathway was 36.0% (β = 0.0117, P < 0.001). After accounting for these two mediators (FPG and SBP), the direct effect of CVAI on CAS remained significant (β = 0.0133, P = 0.025), accounting for 40.8% of the total effect. To further investigate other potential pathways, supplementary single-mediator analyses were conducted under the same covariate adjustments. The results indicated that while CVAI was significantly associated with elevated levels of inflammatory markers (hsCRP, SII), triglyceride–glucose index (TyG) and TG(β = 23.995, 22.840, 0.396, and 0.654; all P < 0.001), the mediation effects of hsCRP, SII, TyG, and TG were not statistically significant (all P > 0.05). This suggests that within the context of our model, these factors did not demonstrate significant independent mediating effects (Supplementary Table S1).

It is important to note that, given the cross-sectional design of this study, the observed mediation effects reflect statistical associations between variables rather than established causal mechanisms.

Subgroup analyses

To further assess the association between CVAI and CAS, we conducted stratified analyses by sex, age, smoking status, alcohol consumption, abdominal obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome (Fig. 3). The results showed that, with the exception of the hypertension and diabetes subgroups, the association between CVAI and CAS remained significantly positive across all other subgroups (all OR > 1, P < 0.001). More importantly, multiplicative interaction tests indicated no significant interaction between CVAI and any of these subgroup variables in relation to CAS ( P-interaction>0.05).

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of the observed CVAI-CAS association, we conducted three sensitivity analyses. First, after multiple imputation for missing data, the positive association between CVAI and CAS remained significant in the fully adjusted model (OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.25–1.47; P < 0.001). The dose-response relationship was consistent, with similar patterns across CVAI tertiles and in restricted cubic spline analysis (Supplementary Table S2 and Fig. S1). Second, exclusion of participants taking glucose-lowering or lipid-lowering medications (n = 791) yielded comparable results (Model 3 OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.23–1.49; P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S3 and Fig. S2). Third, exclusion of participants with extreme metabolic values (values exceeding mean ± 3 SD for FPG, TG, or LDL-C; n = 226) did not materially alter the findings (Model 3 OR 1.40, 95% CI 1.28–1.52; P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S4 and Fig. S3). These sensitivity analyses collectively confirm that the association between CVAI and CAS is stable under alternative data handling approaches.

Discussion

This large-scale cross-sectional study systematically investigated the association between CVAI and CAS, along with its potential underlying mechanisms. The main findings consistently demonstrated a significant positive association between CVAI and CAS risk, with a notable linear dose-response relationship. Most importantly, this study quantitatively evaluated the parallel mediating roles of FPG and SBP in the CVAI–CAS association, providing novel epidemiological insight into potential pathways linking visceral adiposity to atherosclerosis. It was found that the FPG pathway, representing dysglycemia, independently contributed to 23.2% of the mediation effect, supporting a potentially important role of glycemic metabolism in the pathophysiology of atherosclerosis associated with visceral obesity.

After comprehensive adjustment for multiple traditional confounding factors, each standard deviation increase in CVAI was associated with a 37% higher odds of CAS (OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.26–1.49). Compared to participants in the lowest tertile, those in the highest CVAI tertile had 71% higher odds of CAS (OR 1.71, 95% CI 1.41–2.07), and there was a significant linear positive dose-response relationship between CVAI and CAS. These findings are consistent with previous research, further supporting CVAI as an independent risk indicator for CAS. For instance, a large-scale cohort study reported a significant positive association between CVAI and carotid plaque risk14, each one standard deviation increase in CVAI was associated with a 53% increased risk of carotid plaque (HR 1.53, 95% CI 1.48–1.59). Furthermore, several studies have indicated an independent association of CVAI with CAS risk in specific populations, such as individuals with type 1 diabetes19, those with type 2 diabetes21, steelworkers22, and normal-weight individuals23. Unlike previous studies largely confined to association analyses, the present study further applied mediation analysis to explore potential biological pathways underlying the CVAI–CAS relationship.

The mediation analysis revealed that the glycemic metabolism pathway (FPG) and the blood pressure pathway (SBP) are two significant mediating pathways connecting visceral fat and atherosclerosis, jointly accounting for 59.2% of the total association within the constraints of a cross-sectional mediation framework and statistical assumptions. These findings highlight the substantial mediating roles of FPG and SBP in the CVAI–CAS association. This finding is consistent with previous large cohort mediation analyses showing that cardiometabolic mediators such as SBP and FPG explain a substantial portion of the association between adiposity and cardiovascular outcomes24. Our results suggest that similar pathways are operative when visceral adiposity is measured using CVAI, which may better capture metabolic dysfunction than traditional indices.

This finding provides significant epidemiological evidence supporting the theoretical hypothesis that visceral adipose tissue, as a metabolically active organ, may contribute to insulin resistance and glucose metabolism disorders through the release of free fatty acids and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby impairing vascular endothelial function25–27. Hyperglycemia itself is also detrimental to the endothelium. It promotes the accumulation of leukocytes within the endothelium, further disrupting its normal functions, such as nitric oxide production and the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS)28. The subsequent production of superoxide in endothelial cells further contributes to inflammation and endothelial damage29. The SBP pathway accounted for 36.0% of the mediating effect, which is consistent with the theory that visceral adiposity may influence blood pressure regulation mechanisms, such as the activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, sodium retention, and vascular endothelial dysfunction30,31. It is noteworthy that a significant direct effect of CVAI on CAS persisted even after concurrently adjusting for both SBP and FPG. This suggests the potential existence of other mechanisms not captured by the current model, such as oxidized LDL32, specific adipokine secretion33, abnormalities in lipoprotein particle quality34, or gut microbiota dysbiosis35. These findings indicate that the impact of visceral adiposity on atherosclerosis is likely multifactorial and warrants further investigation.

Supplementary mediating analysis showed that inflammatory markers (hsCRP, SII) and lipid metabolism indicators (TyG, TG) did not have significant independent mediating effects in the relationship between CVAI and CAS. This outcome diverges from some classical perspectives that emphasize the central role of insulin resistance and chronic inflammation36,37. Several possible explanations exist: First, the independent mediation effects of these factors might be inherently weak38 and failed to reach statistical significance given the current sample size. Second, they might exhibit collinearity with other adjusted variables (e.g., age, LDL-C), thereby attenuating their independent contribution39. Third, these indicators are more likely downstream effects or concomitant states of traditional cardiovascular risk factors (such as hyperglycemia and hypertension), their effect might be realized indirectly primarily by exacerbating glucolipid metabolism disorders (which subsequently elevate FPG and SBP). This interpretation aligns with the concept that inflammatory pathways rarely act in isolation but instead promote atherosclerosis by modulating traditional risk factors within a complex pathological network40,41.

Furthermore, extensive subgroup analyses demonstrated that the positive association between CVAI and CAS remained consistent across strata defined by sex, age, metabolic disease status, and lifestyle factors, with no significant interactions observed (all P-interaction > 0.05). This consistent finding, together with the robust results from sensitivity analyses, supports the robustness and generalizability of the CVAI–CAS association. From a clinical and population health perspective, individuals with elevated CVAI may warrant consideration of integrated risk management strategies, targeting not only weight reduction but also blood pressure and glucose optimization.

The major strengths of this study include its large sample size, stringent quality control procedures, application of advanced statistical methods, and the quantitative exploration of potential mediating pathways. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes inferences regarding causality. Although mediation analyses suggest potential pathways, the temporal sequence of these associations cannot be definitively established and requires confirmation in prospective studies. Second, although extensive adjustments were made for known confounders, the influence of residual confounding (e.g., from unmeasured factors like detailed dietary patterns and physical activity levels) cannot be entirely ruled out. Additionally, the study participants were primarily derived from a non-manual labor population undergoing health check-ups, who may differ from the general population in terms of occupational characteristics, educational background, and health awareness; thus, the generalizability of the conclusions should be approached with caution.

Conclusion

In summary, this large-scale cross-sectional study establishes a significant, robust, and independent positive association between CVAI and CAS. A key finding is the quantitative identification of parallel mediating effects of FPG and SBP in the CVAI–CAS association, accounting for 23.2% and 36.0% of the total effect, respectively. These results suggest that among individuals with elevated visceral adiposity, prioritizing rigorous monitoring and management of blood glucose and blood pressure may represent a core strategy for mitigating atherosclerotic risk. Future studies employing longitudinal designs are warranted to validate the temporal sequence and potential causal pathways of these associations and to further elucidate additional biological mechanisms linking visceral obesity to vascular health.

Methods

Study design and participants

This single-center cross-sectional study was conducted at the Shanghai Healthcare Medical Center and enrolled adults who underwent routine health examinations between April 2025 and June 2025. In total, 10,684 individuals were initially screened. After application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, 6767 eligible participants were included in the final analyses (see Fig. 4).

The flowchart of study participants.

The inclusion criteria were: ① age ≥ 18 years; ② availability of complete key demographic and clinical variables, including anthropometric measurements, blood pressure, and laboratory parameters.; and ③ completion of high-quality carotid ultrasound examination. The exclusion criteria were as follows: ① age < 18 years (n = 140); ② missing or incomplete medical history information or critical clinical data (n = 637); ③ History of major surgery (n = 352) or significant cardiopulmonary, hepatic, or renal diseases that could affect metabolism or vascular structure (n = 145). “Major surgery” was defined as procedures with potential systemic metabolic impact (e.g., bariatric surgery, major abdominal procedures, cardiovascular surgery); ④missing or incomplete carotid ultrasound data (n = 2643).

This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Shanghai Healthcare Medical Center (Approval No.: 2025-012). Informed consent was obtained from all study participants. In accordance with the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE) guidelines, prospective registration in a public trials registry is mandatory only for interventional clinical trials. Given that this study is a cross-sectional analysis of de-identified data from routine health examinations—an observational study design without any predefined intervention—it was not prospectively registered.

Data collection and definitions

Basic information including age, sex, smoking and alcohol history, recent medication use (including glucose-lowering or lipid-lowering agents), and disease history was obtained through a standardized questionnaire administered by trained investigators. All measurements were performed according to standardized procedures. Height and weight were measured using calibrated stadiometers and scales, recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm and 0.1 kg, respectively. Waist circumference was measured at the level of the umbilicus, and hip circumference was measured at the level of the greatest protrusion of the hips; both were recorded to the nearest 1 cm. Blood pressure was measured in a seated, rested position using a standard mercury sphygmomanometer on the right brachial artery. Two consecutive readings at least 2 min apart were taken, and the mean value was used for analysis; recorded to the nearest 1 mmHg. After fasting for at least 8 h, 10 ml of venous blood was collected from all participants in the morning. Laboratory assays for FPG, TG, TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, Scr, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) were conducted using an automated biochemical analyzer.

Smoking status was definedas current smoking of ≥ 1 cigarette per day for ≥ 6 months, including those who quit less than one month prior42. Current alcohol consumption was defined as any reported consumption in the past month43.

Definitions of clinical variables

Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, self-reported hypertension, or the use of antihypertensive medication44. Diabetes mellitus was defined as FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, glycated hemoglobin (A1C) ≥ 6.5%, a documented history of diabetes, or current use of antidiabetic agents45.

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) was diagnosed according to the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (2022 Edition)46, which requires the presence of three or more of the following five components: (1) Abdominal obesity (waist circumference ≥ 90 cm in men or ≥ 85 cm in women); (2) Elevated fasting glucose (FPG ≥ 6.1 mmol/L or previously diagnosed diabetes); (3) Elevated blood pressure (SBP ≥ 130 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 85 mmHg, or receiving antihypertensive treatment); (4) Elevated fasting triglycerides (TG ≥ 1.70 mmol/L); (5) Reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C < 1.04 mmol/L).

Calculation of variables

Derived anthropometric and metabolic indices were calculated as follows:

BMI = weight (kg)/height2 (m2).

WHR (waist-to-hip ratio) = WC (cm)/HC (cm).

WHtR (waist-to-height ratio) = WC (cm)/height (cm).

TyG (triglyceride-glucose index) = Ln [TG (mg/dL) × FPG (mg/dL)/2]

SII47 = platelet count × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count.

The CVAI was calculated using sex-specific formulas:

Male : CVAI = − 267.93 + 0.68 × age (years) + 0.03 × BMI (kg/m2) + 4.00 × WC (cm) + 22.00 × Log10TG (mmol/L) − 16.32 × HDL-C (mmol/L).

Females : CVAI = − 187.32 + 1.71 × age (years) + 4.23 × BMI (kg/m2) + 1.12 × WC (cm) + 39.76 × Log10TG (mmol/L) − 11.66 × HDL-C (mmol/L).

Carotid ultrasound and CAS definition

All carotid artery ultrasound examinations were performed by experienced sonographers. The examinations were conducted using a Siemens Acuson Sequoia 512 ultrasound system equipped with a 15 MHz linear array transducer. A systematic scan was performed on the bilateral common carotid arteries, carotid bifurcations, internal carotid arteries, external carotid arteries, and subclavian arteries.

The assessment of carotid intima-media thickness (cIMT) and plaques strictly adhered to the Mannheim Consensus48: cIMT thickening was defined as a cIMT measurement > 0.9 mm in any segment of either carotid artery49. Carotid plaque was defined as a focal structure protruding into the lumen with a thickness exceeding the cIMT of the adjacent normal vessel wall by ≥ 0.5 mm or ≥ 50%, or with a local cIMT value > 1.5 mm. In this study, CAS was defined as the presence of cIMT thickening and/or the presence of carotid plaque50.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1) and SPSS software (version 22.0). Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± SD and compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). While skewed variables are shown as median (interquartile range) [M (P25, P75)] and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical data are presented as numbers (percentages) [n (%)], and group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test (χ2 test).

To analyze the association between CVAI and CAS, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed, adjusting for confounding factors sequentially as follows: Model 1: Unadjusted; Model 2: Adjusted for age, sex, current smoking, and current alcohol consumption; Model 3: Additionally adjusted for FPG, LDL-C, and Scr based on Model 2. CVAI was incorporated into the models in two forms: (1) as a continuous variable (per one standard deviation increase); and (2) categorized into three groups based on tertiles (with Q1 as the reference group). The odds ratios (OR) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the association with CAS risk were calculated.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression with five knots placed at the 5th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 95th percentiles was employed to flexibly model the dose-response relationship between CVAI and CAS in Model 3, with the median CVAI value serving as the reference. To explore the potential mediating roles of FPG and SBP, a dual-path parallel mediation analysis based on structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted. This analysis calculated the total effect of CVAI on CAS, the direct effect, and the indirect effects mediated through FPG and SBP. The Bootstrap method (with 1000 resamples) was used to estimate the 95% CIs and assess the significance of the mediation proportions.

Multiplicative interaction models were used to evaluate the interaction effect on CAS risk between CVAI and variables including sex, age, smoking status, alcohol consumption, abdominal obesity, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and metabolic syndrome. Subgroup analyses were subsequently performed.

To assess robustness, three pre-specified sensitivity analyses were conducted: (1) multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) for missing data; (2) exclusion of participants taking glucose-lowering or lipid-lowering medications; and (3) exclusion of participants with extreme metabolic values (FPG, TG, or LDL-C > mean ± 3 SD). Multivariable logistic regression and restricted cubic spline regression with five knots were repeated using the same covariate adjustments as in Model 3.

All tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
