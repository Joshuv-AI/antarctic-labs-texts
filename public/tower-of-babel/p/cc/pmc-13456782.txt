Parietal hydatid cyst of the rectus abdominis muscle: case report and literature review
Authors: Ahmed vadel Jidou, Mohammed Hassani, Mohammed Mahmoudi, Abdelali Guellil, Rachid Jabi, Mohammed Bouziane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Hydatid disease is a zoonotic infection caused by the larval stage of Echinococcus granulosus. It remains endemic in Morocco and represents a significant public health concern. Although the liver and lungs are the most frequently affected organs, muscular involvement is rare. This report aims to describe an unusual case of a hydatid cyst in the rectus abdominis muscle and highlight the role of imaging in diagnosis and management.Case presentation:A 37-year-old woman presented with a progressively enlarging hypogastric swelling evolving over 5 months. Clinical examination revealed a soft, non-inflammatory mass in the lower abdominal wall. Hydatid serology was positive, while other laboratory findings were unremarkable. Imaging demonstrated a multilocular intramuscular cystic lesion separating the fibers of the left rectus abdominis muscle, consistent with a type III hydatid cyst. No hepatic or pulmonary involvement was identified. The patient underwent complete pericystectomy. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis by demonstrating hydatid membranes and protoscolices. Postoperatively, albendazole was administered for 4 weeks. The clinical course was favorable, with no residual lesion detected on follow-up imaging.Discussion:Primary muscular hydatid cysts are uncommon and may pose a diagnostic challenge because of their nonspecific clinical presentation. Imaging, particularly ultrasound and computed tomography, plays a crucial role in establishing the diagnosis, assessing lesion extent, and planning treatment. Surgical excision remains the treatment of choice, while albendazole may be used as an adjunctive therapy.Conclusion:Hydatid cyst of the rectus abdominis muscle is a rare entity that should be considered in the differential diagnosis of abdominal wall masses in endemic regions. Early diagnosis and complete surgical excision provide an excellent prognosis.

Introduction

A hydatid cyst, also known as hydatid disease, is an anthropozoonosis caused by human infestation with the larval form of the dog tapeworm Echinococcus granulosus[1]. It is endemic in Morocco and constitutes a real public health problem[2]. While hydatid disease can affect most organs, cysts are most commonly found in the liver and lungs. Localization to the rectus abdominis muscle is therefore unusual, even in endemic areas[3]. This unusual presentation can delay diagnosis due to the condition’s paucisymptomatic nature and the rarity of primary muscle involvement. Imaging plays a central role in the diagnostic approach by suggesting the cystic nature of the lesion and guiding the therapeutic strategy[6].

Management is primarily based on surgical treatment. When feasible, pericystectomy enables the entire cyst to be removed while minimizing the risk of parasite dissemination and recurrence. When combined with adjuvant antiparasitic treatment, it offers an excellent prognosis[7].

We emphasize the importance of considering a diagnosis of hydatid cyst for any abdominal wall mass, particularly in endemic areas.

Case presentation

A 37-year-old woman, a rural resident, was undergoing treatment for primary infertility and was known to have a uterus with multiple fibroids. She underwent a Pfannenstiel laparotomy myomectomy in 2018; however, the pathological results of this procedure are not documented. She presented with a hypogastric swelling that had been progressing for 5 months. Clinical examination revealed a mass in the hypogastric region, to the left of the midline and opposite the old scar. The mass was fixed in relation to both planes, soft in consistency, and without any signs of skin inflammation.

Biological analyses revealed positive hydatid serology. No other abnormalities were detected.

A thoracoabdominal pelvic CT scan revealed a polymyomatous uterus with a multilocular intramuscular cystic formation that was separating the fibers of the rectus abdominis muscle on the left side. Enhancement of this formation at the periphery after contrast injection suggested a type 3 GHARBI hydatid cyst measuring 72 × 56 × 33 mm, with no hepatic or pulmonary involvement (Fig. 1). The patient underwent surgery under general anesthesia through a lower midline infraumbilical incision. The surgical field was protected and irrigated with a hydrogen peroxide solution, taking care to avoid any spillage or dissemination of the cyst contents. A total pericystectomy was then performed successfully (Fig. 2). The pathological examination confirmed the presence of hydatid membranes and protoscolices. After the operation, the patient received albendazole for 4 weeks and recovered without incident, with no recurrence during follow-up and disappearance of lesions on the follow-up CT scan (Fig. 3). Discussion

Hydatid disease is an anthropozoonosis caused by the larval form of E. granulosus, a small tapeworm that parasitizes the digestive tract of dogs, its definitive host. Dogs become infected through the digestive tract after consuming parasitized viscera, particularly the liver and lungs of the intermediate host, sheep. Sheep are the main reservoir for the tapeworm and become infected by grazing on grass contaminated with dog feces containing the parasite’s eggs. Humans are only accidental intermediate hosts; they become infected either directly through contact with infected dogs or indirectly through the ingestion of contaminated food. Humans are an epidemiological dead end[1].

All locations of hydatid disease have been described[4]. Hepatic and pulmonary locations account for 90% of all hydatid cyst locations[9]. This distribution can be explained by the spread of the parasite through the bloodstream and by the blood flow in the portal circulation. Tapeworm eggs ingested by humans pass into the portal circulation, and the larvae are then transported and immobilized in the sinusoids, which act as a filter. A total of 10–20% of the larvae escape this hepatic barrier and enter the general circulation, where they lodge in another organ[9,10].

The rarity of muscle involvement in hydatid disease is thought to be due, on the one hand, to muscle contractions that prevent the larva from developing and, on the other hand, to the formation of lactic acid in the muscle, which prevents the hydatid from growing[10,11].

The clinical diagnosis of a muscular hydatid cyst is often difficult. Pain or signs of compression often prompt the patient to seek medical attention. This cyst can become infected and cause fever[10]. The patient may also present with a mass syndrome, which is rarely indicative of this condition, making our observation unique. Some cysts are revealed by neurological or vascular complications, or by a secondary infection that can lead to sepsis[9].

Biology essentially boils down to hydatid serology. It provides valuable assistance in diagnosis when positive. However, a negative result does not rule out the diagnosis of a hydatid cyst, hence the importance of comparing clinical findings, imaging, and biology. To improve sensitivity and specificity, most authors prefer to combine two serological techniques: one quantitative (indirect hemagglutination, immunofluorescence, and ELISA) and one qualitative (immunoelectrophoresis and electrosineresis)[11]. In addition to its role in confirming the diagnosis, hydatid serology allows the post-treatment evolution of the hydatid cyst to be monitored, a prognosis to be made, and secondary hydatid disease to be detected early. Thus, any elevation in the 6 months following the procedure is synonymous with recurrence or unnoticed hydatid locations. Qualitative and quantitative methods are difficult to interpret, but Western blot and immunoblotting are more sensitive and specific[12].

Imaging is a key element in diagnosis[6]. Ultrasound is a highly suggestive examination; it allows for the diagnosis of a psoas cystic mass and the identification of the hydatid origin of the lesion. The ultrasound findings are identical to those described by Gharbi et al[13], who classified hydatid cysts into five stages:

Stage 1: Homogeneously hypoechogenic, cystic, thin-walled lesion.

Stage 2: Septated cystic lesion.

Stage 3: Cystic lesion with daughter lesions.

Stage 4: Partially calcified lesion, pseudo-tumor lesion.

Stage 5: Calcified lesion (inactive cyst).

CT scans are effective for locating cysts. They also enable a complete assessment of the lesion by studying the relationship between the cyst and neighboring organs[14]. Our patient had a stage III cyst. Only stages I, IV, and V cysts can, in some cases, pose problems for differential diagnosis. Type I cysts can be confused with simple cysts, especially in women, and type IV and V cysts can resemble solid tumors[13]. MRI is rarely used and is only necessary when ultrasound and CT scans are insufficient to study the cyst’s relationship with surrounding structures[15].

Medical treatment with albendazole is the subject of much debate. According to some authors, it retains its place as an adjuvant treatment, administered in addition to percutaneous treatment, or is indicated for patients who are not candidates for surgery[16]. However, surgery remains the standard treatment[7,17]. Teams have proposed a laparoscopic approach to treat these cysts, particularly in the liver. Due to the exceptional nature of cysts developing at the expense of the rectus muscle, no data on laparoscopy for this indication are available. Excision consists of total pericystectomy, whenever possible, with protection of the surgical field[18].

Eradication of the parasite remains the best preventive treatment. The best prevention is to monitor the slaughter of animals for meat, systematically euthanize stray dogs, and treat domestic dogs[19].
