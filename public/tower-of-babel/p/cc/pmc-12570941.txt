Randomised controlled trial of indirect revascularisation combined with intensive medical therapy for symptomatic intracranial atherosclerotic stenosis/occlusion to prevent stroke recurrence: a study protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Haitao Liu, Hang Ji, Guicheng Kuang, Hailan Yang, Yue Li, Mamun Ahmed, Jianjian Zhang, Qiangping Wang, Huaqiu Zhang, Aijun Peng, Dong Zhang, Rong Wang, Yongbo Yang, Anqi Xiao, Haogeng Sun, Yi Liu

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIntracranial atherosclerotic disease (ICAD), prevalent in over 65% of Asian populations, is a major cause of global cerebrovascular morbidity and mortality through cerebral hypoperfusion. Despite guideline-directed medical therapy, recurrent ischaemic events remain common due to inadequate collateral circulation. Previous randomised controlled trials (ie, International Extracranial-Intracranial Bypass Study, carotid occlusion surgery study, carotid and middle cerebral artery occlusion surgery study) demonstrated high perioperative risks of extracranial-to-intracranial bypass surgery, while recent single-arm studies suggest that indirect cerebral revascularisation may improve cerebral perfusion with enhanced safety.Methods and analysisThis is a multicentre, randomised, open-label trial designed to enrol 378 participants (189 per group) with symptomatic ICAD (≥70% stenosis/occlusion, American Society of Interventional and Therapeutic Neuroradiology/Society of Interventional Radiology Collateral Score 0–2). Participants will be allocated using minimisation randomisation via an interactive web response system to receive multimodal indirect revascularisation plus intensive medical therapy or intensive medical therapy alone. The primary endpoints are the composite of stroke or death within 30 days and ipsilateral ischaemic stroke within 24 months. Secondary outcomes include transient ischaemic attack reduction, neurological function preservation (modified Rankin Scale), and cerebral perfusion improvements assessed by CT perfusion and MRI.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol has been approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (approval number: 2024 Review (2588)). Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants prior to enrolment. Introduction and rationale

Intracranial atherosclerotic disease (ICAD) represents the most prevalent aetiology of cerebral ischaemia, affecting over 65% of Asian populations.1,4 Characterised by high disability and mortality rates, ICAD imposes a substantial global disease burden and poses a critical public health challenge. Although standardised medical therapies (eg, antiplatelet agents, antihypertensives and lipid-lowering drugs) reduce stroke risk in some patients, subgroups with haemodynamic insufficiency remain vulnerable to recurrent events.5 6 Studies demonstrate annual stroke recurrence rates of 5%–8% in medically treated patients with intracranial artery (ICA) occlusion,7 8 while middle cerebral artery (MCA) occlusion carries an ipsilateral annual stroke risk of 9.1%–12.5%.9 The persistent recurrence rates over the past decade, coupled with a high proportion of non-fatal strokes, underscore limitations in current therapeutic strategies.10

Pathophysiologically, intracranial arterial stenosis or occlusion leads to cerebral hypoperfusion through two primary mechanisms. First, the stenotic or occluded vessel directly reduces distal perfusion pressure, triggering compensatory vasodilation of resistance arterioles. When this compensatory mechanism is exhausted, cerebral tissue increases oxygen extraction fraction (OEF) to maintain metabolic function, termed ‘misery perfusion’ or Stage II haemodynamic failure.11 12 Second, collateral circulation development varies significantly between patients, with approximately 30% of individuals exhibiting inadequate collateral compensation due to anatomical variations or comorbidities.13,15 Patients with impaired vasomotor reactivity and poor collaterals demonstrate substantially higher annual ipsilateral stroke rates of up to 32.7% despite optimal medical management,16 highlighting the critical need for alternative therapeutic approaches beyond pharmacological interventions.

The limitations of medical therapy alone for symptomatic intracranial stenosis/occlusion have been well-documented. The Warfarin-Aspirin symptomatic intracranial disease trial demonstrated a 12-month stroke recurrence rate of 18% in the territory of the stenotic artery despite antithrombotic therapy.17 Similarly, the Stenting and Aggressive Medical Management for Preventing Recurrent Stroke in Intracranial Stenosis trial reported a 1-year stroke recurrence rate of 12.2% in patients receiving intensive medical management.18 More concerning, this rate increased to 20% in the subgroup with poor collaterals,15 confirming that a significant proportion of patients remains at high risk despite optimal medical therapy.

Extracranial-intracranial (EC-IC) bypass surgery was developed to improve cerebral perfusion in patients with symptomatic intracranial stenosis/occlusion. The Japanese EC-IC Bypass Trial suggested potential benefits in carefully selected patients with haemodynamic compromise, reporting a reduction in ipsilateral stroke recurrence compared with medical therapy alone.19 However, the Carotid Occlusion Surgery Study (COSS) failed to demonstrate overall benefit, primarily due to a prohibitively high 30-day perioperative stroke rate of 15% that negated the long-term benefit of reduced recurrent stroke.19 Similarly, the recent Carotid and Middle Cerebral Artery Occlusion Surgery Study (CMOSS) reported a 30-day stroke or death rate of 6.2% in the surgical group compared with only 1.8% in the medical group, despite improved surgical techniques and stricter surgeon certification requirements.10 20 While these trials confirmed that direct bypass could improve cerebral perfusion and reduce long-term stroke risk, the high perioperative complication rates ultimately resulted in no net clinical benefit.

A paradigm shift emerged with the recent encephaloduroarteriosynangiosis revascularisation for symptomatic intracranial atherosclerotic stenosis (ERSIAS) phase-II trial, which evaluated indirect revascularisation via encephaloduroarteriosynangiosis (EDAS) in patients with symptomatic ICAD. This single-arm study demonstrated both improved cerebral perfusion and remarkably favourable safety profiles, with surgical complications occurring in only 3.8% of patients and no intracranial haemorrhages.21 During a median follow-up of 24.5 months, only 9.6% of patients experienced the primary endpoint event (stroke/death within 30 days or stroke in the territory of the qualifying artery beyond 30 days), compared with 21.2% in propensity-score-matched medically treated controls.21 These promising results suggest that indirect revascularisation may achieve the haemodynamic benefits of direct bypass while avoiding its high perioperative risks.

Despite these encouraging findings, whether patients with symptomatic intracranial stenosis/occlusion can derive overall clinical benefit from indirect revascularisation remains unproven in the absence of high-quality randomised controlled trial (RCT) evidence. Therefore, we designed this multicentre, prospective RCT to evaluate the safety and efficacy of multimodal indirect revascularisation surgery combined with intensive medical therapy for symptomatic intracranial atherosclerotic stenosis/occlusion. As the first global RCT evaluating indirect revascularisation for ICAD, this study aims to establish evidence-based surgical paradigms and advance secondary stroke prevention strategies. Details of the trial design and registration are available in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500099882)22

Methods

Aim

This study is designed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of multimodal cerebral indirect revascularisation surgery combined with intensive medical therapy in patients with symptomatic cerebral atherosclerotic stenosis/occlusion. Additionally, the secondary objective aims to explore a novel therapeutic strategy capable of improving cerebral haemodynamics while ensuring high safety and feasibility, with the ultimate goal of reducing stroke recurrence and mortality rates among participants. By promoting the adoption of this innovative surgical approach, we seek to alleviate the socioeconomic burden associated with recurrent cerebrovascular events and advance clinical practices for high-risk populations.

Hypothesis

We hypothesise that multimodal indirect revascularisation surgery combined with intensive medical therapy will significantly improve cerebral perfusion, reduce ischaemic events and lower stroke recurrence rates in patients with symptomatic cerebral atherosclerotic stenosis/occlusion, compared with conventional medical therapy alone. This combined approach is expected to demonstrate a favourable safety profile and long-term clinical benefits.

Randomisation

This study employed a minimisation randomisation method. On participant admission, clinicians immediately collected baseline data and submitted the information to the research centre via telephone or web-based platforms. Researchers responsible for randomisation used a centralised allocation system based on an interactive web response system or interactive voice response system to randomly assign participants in a 1:1 ratio. The group allocation was then promptly communicated to the treating clinicians.

Intervention

All non-surgical and non-intensive pharmacotherapy interventions (eg, blood pressure management, complication management) shall follow guideline recommendations. Surgical procedures must be performed by experienced neurosurgeons to ensure operative quality. Participating surgeons must have performed ≥50 cerebral revascularisation procedures (direct, indirect or combined) for moyamoya disease or ICAD patients within the preceding 12 months. Prior to performing any trial-related surgeries, all surgeons are required to undergo standardised surgical training at the lead centre and pass a formal competency assessment. The training programme includes protocol-specific operative observation, simulation-based technical practice and evaluation by senior faculty. Ongoing surgical quality will be monitored through periodic review of operative records and intraoperative photographs by the central surgical committee.

The primary aim of this trial is to determine whether the overall strategy of indirect cerebral revascularisation is effective and safe in patients with symptomatic ICAD, rather than to evaluate the superiority of a single surgical technique. Therefore, the protocol intentionally allows individualised surgical selection from established indirect revascularisation methods—including but not limited to: EDAS, Encephalomyosynangiosis (EMS), Combined Encephaloduroarteriomyosynangiosis, Encephalogaleosynangiosis and Encephalopericraniosynangiosis (EPS)—based on each patient’s anatomical and haemodynamic characteristics.

This pragmatic design reflects real-world clinical practice and ensures the broad applicability of the study findings. In addition, we plan to conduct secondary and exploratory analyses within the surgical arm to compare clinical and imaging outcomes among different techniques. These analyses aim to identify the most promising procedure(s) for future focused trials, thereby refining surgical decision-making for this patient population.

Serious adverse events

Serious adverse event (SAE) management framework

A serious adverse event (SAE) is defined as any medical occurrence during the trial that meets one or more of the following criteria: death, life-threatening conditions, hospitalisation or prolonged hospitalisation, persistent disability/incapacity, congenital anomalies, or medically significant events requiring urgent intervention to prevent severe outcomes (eg, anaphylaxis, vascular dissection). Exclusions include protocol-specified procedures (eg, angiography), emergency visits with discharge within 24 hours and admissions to non-acute care facilities (eg, rehabilitation centres).

SAE reporting protocol

All SAEs were documented in the case report form with detailed timelines (onset, duration), therapeutic interventions, causality assessment and clinical outcomes. Life-threatening or fatal SAEs were reported to the data safety monitoring board and ethics committee within 14 days of investigator awareness.

SAE management and follow-up

Immediate clinical interventions (eg, anticoagulation reversal for haemorrhagic complications) were mandated, with continuous monitoring until resolution, stabilisation or confirmation of irreversible endpoints (eg, permanent disability).

Data monitoring

The clinical study will establish corresponding data safety monitoring plans based on risk stratification. All adverse events will be comprehensively documented, appropriately managed and tracked until resolution or stabilisation, with SAEs and unexpected incidents promptly reported to the Ethics Review Committee, regulatory authorities, sponsors and drug regulatory agencies in compliance with regulations. The principal investigator will conduct cumulative reviews of all adverse events at regular intervals and convene investigator meetings as needed to assess the study’s risk-benefit profile. For double-blind trials, emergency unblinding may be performed when necessary to safeguard the safety and rights of study participants.

Sample size calculation

The sample size calculation for this trial was derived from outcome event rates reported in the ERSIAS Phase-II study,8 where the indirect revascularisation group demonstrated a 9.6% event rate compared with 21.2% in the medical therapy group. Using a two-sided significance level of 0.05 and 80% statistical power with 1:1 group allocation, the initial estimated sample size was calculated as 302 participants through standard comparative event rate formulas (eg, log-rank test for time-to-event endpoints). To account for potential attrition, a 20% dropout buffer was incorporated, resulting in a final enrolment target of 378 participants (189 per arm). This approach aligns with consolidated standards of reporting trials recommendations for RCTs requiring robust power analysis while addressing real-world attrition challenges in longitudinal cerebrovascular studies.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarised as mean±SD, with between-group comparisons performed using independent t-tests. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages, analysed via Pearson’s χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for small sample sizes. Time-to-event outcomes, including cumulative risk estimates, were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, and differences between groups were assessed with the log-rank test. Prognostic factors influencing primary endpoints were investigated through Cox proportional hazards regression models, with both univariate and multivariate analyses employed to quantify hazard ratios and their 95% CIs.

To address potential bias from missing data, intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) analyses were implemented. The ITT approach included all randomised participants regardless of protocol deviations, while PP analysis excluded those with major violations or incomplete follow-up. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the consistency of results across these frameworks. Missing data due to attrition (anticipated rate ≤10%) were managed through multiple imputation under the missing-at-random assumption, complemented by complete-case analysis for validation.

All statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance level set at α=0.05. Analyses were performed using SPSS software (V.27.0; IBM), incorporating rigorous data validation procedures such as double-entry verification and range consistency checks to ensure accuracy. Survival curves and regression outputs were graphically visualised to enhance the interpretability of time-dependent outcomes and risk stratification.

Discussion

Symptomatic intracranial atherosclerotic stenosis/occlusion represents a significant therapeutic challenge, with substantial morbidity and mortality despite current medical management. The quest for effective revascularisation strategies has evolved through several landmark RCTs, each contributing valuable insights while highlighting persistent limitations in our approach to this complex disease.

The COSS was among the first major trials to evaluate EC-IC bypass for symptomatic carotid occlusion with haemodynamic compromise. Despite theoretical benefits, COSS failed to demonstrate the superiority of surgical intervention over medical therapy, primarily due to a prohibitively high 30-day perioperative stroke rate of 15% that negated the long-term benefit of reduced recurrent stroke.19 Several methodological considerations warrant attention: COSS primarily enrolled patients with unilateral ICA occlusion, used positron emission tomography-derived OEF for haemodynamic assessment and included surgeons with varying experience levels, potentially contributing to the high complication rates.19

Building on lessons from COSS, the CMOSS implemented several methodological refinements. CMOSS employed CTP for haemodynamic assessment, included patients with MCA occlusion and implemented stricter surgeon certification requirements.20 Despite these optimisations, CMOSS reported a 30-day stroke or death rate of 6.2% in the surgical group compared with only 1.8% in the medical group.10 While this represented a substantial improvement from the 15% perioperative complication rate in COSS, the difference remained clinically significant and ultimately resulted in no overall benefit of EC-IC bypass in symptomatic intracranial arterial occlusion.10

These trials collectively confirm a fundamental challenge in direct revascularisation approaches: while EC-IC bypass can effectively improve cerebral perfusion and potentially reduce long-term stroke risk, the procedure carries inherent perioperative risks that may offset its benefits. This paradox has prompted exploration of alternative revascularisation strategies that might preserve the haemodynamic benefits while minimising procedural complications.23

The recent ERSIAS phase-II trial represents a potential paradigm shift in our approach to cerebral revascularisation. This single-arm study evaluated indirect revascularisation via EDAS in patients with symptomatic ICAD and demonstrated remarkably favourable safety profiles, with surgical complications occurring in only 3.8% of patients and no intracranial haemorrhages.21 During a median follow-up of 24.5 months, only 9.6% of patients experienced the primary endpoint event (stroke/death within 30 days or stroke in the territory of the qualifying artery beyond 30 days), compared with 21.2% in propensity-score-matched medically treated controls.21

The ERSIAS results suggest that indirect revascularisation may achieve the haemodynamic benefits of direct bypass while avoiding its high perioperative risks. This approach leverages the brain’s natural angiogenic capacity to develop collateral circulation gradually, potentially offering a safer alternative to direct anastomotic techniques.24 However, whether these promising findings translate to definitive clinical benefit requires validation in an RCT setting.

Our study addresses this critical evidence gap as the first multicentre prospective RCT evaluating indirect revascularisation for symptomatic ICAD. By combining rigorous patient selection based on haemodynamic criteria, standardised surgical techniques and comprehensive outcome assessment, we aim to establish whether multimodal indirect revascularisation surgery combined with intensive medical therapy can safely and effectively reduce stroke recurrence in this high-risk population.
