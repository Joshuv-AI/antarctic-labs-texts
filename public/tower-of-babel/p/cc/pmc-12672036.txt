Social Determinants of Health and Delirium in the PICU: Secondary Analysis of a 2014–2015 Observational Dataset
Authors: Kate Madden, Caroline Andy, Evan T. Sholle, Linda M. Gerber, Chani Traube
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objectives:To explore the association between geospatial determinants of health and prevalence of delirium in the PICU.Design:Nonprespecified secondary analysis of an observational study dataset.Setting:Urban academic tertiary care PICU.Patients:All children admitted over a 12-month period in 2014–2015.Interventions:None.Measurements and Main Results:Of 1547 admissions, there were individual 1264 patients, with race and ethnicity subgroup recorded for 961 (76%), insurance status for 1240 (98%), and language for 1227 (97%). Child Opportunity Index (COI) was determined by a patient’s 2010 Census Tract and was available for 1246 (98%). Data were grouped from 1 to 5 (very low to very high). PICU delirium (PD) was present in 193 patients during PICU admission, with PD rates highest in Asian/Pacific Islander, Black non-Hispanic, and Hispanic (respectively, 26, 29, and 39 patients) as compared with White non-Hispanic children (n = 45; p < 0.001). PD was higher for patients with public insurance (113/573 [20%]) compared with those with private insurance (67/583 [12%]) and those with “other” (8/84 [10%]) forms of support (p < 0.001). We failed to identify a difference in delirium rates by language preference. A higher prevalence of delirium was present among patients in the lowest COI groups (1–3) as compared with highest (4–5; 135/748 [18%] vs. 55/494 [11%]; p = 0.003). In multivariable analysis, COI was associated with greater adjusted odds of delirium (after adjusting for other demographic and clinical predictors of delirium, including age, developmental disability, severity of illness at admission, need for invasive mechanical ventilation, depth of sedation, and medication exposures), with adjusted odds ratio of 1.55 (95% CI, 1.05–2.3; p = 0.028).Conclusions:In a 2014–2015 PICU dataset, we have identified an association between lower COI and greater adjusted odds of delirium. This finding calls for further study to investigate potential mediators of this relationship.

Delirium is acute brain dysfunction found to be prevalent in PICU patients, due to both underlying disease processes and iatrogenic exposures (1–3). PICU delirium (PD) is of particular concern because it has been associated with worse outcomes, including longer PICU length of stay, increased mortality, post-discharge decrements in quality of life, and all-cause PICU readmissions (1, 4, 5). Multiple patient factors associated with PD have been delineated in observational studies, including younger age, increased illness severity, need for invasive mechanical ventilation, and atypical neurologic development. In addition, potentially modifiable iatrogenic factors (including depth of sedation, benzodiazepine exposure, and restraint use) have been implicated in the temporal development of delirium in critically ill children (1–6).

An increasing body of literature describes the impact of social determinants of health (SDoH) on PICU utilization and outcomes (7, 8). The mechanisms of these relationships have not yet been well delineated. However, structural racism, prior healthcare interactions, insurance status, language preference, and individual biases can impact a child’s hospitalization in multiple ways (7–15). As such, the Child Opportunity Index (COI) 2.0 is a norm-referenced measure that incorporates 29 social, environmental, educational, and health resources that can impact children, at the national census level (15–17), and this holistic value reflects social determinants impacting a child’s access to healthcare (18, 19). The COI scale—grouped by quintiles reflecting the span from very low to very high opportunity level—is known to be associated with illness severity in the PICU (12–17), and it is accepted as an accurate and nuanced measure of a child’s SDoH. In our research, we wondered whether SDoH, as assessed by COI quintile, may be associated with the risk of delirium during PICU admission. Therefore, we have used a previously published and curated prospective clinical research dataset of PICU admissions to a single center during 2014–2015 (20) to secondarily examine whether lower COI was associated with greater odds of delirium in this population.

METHODS

The original observational study published in 2017 was “A Prospective Longitudinal Assessment of Pediatric Delirium, Associated Risk Factors and Short Term Outcomes in Pediatric ICU Patients” (No. 1405015134) and was approved for a waiver of informed consent by the Weill Cornell Medical College Institutional Review Board (IRB) on July 11, 2014. This current work is a nonprespecified, secondary analysis of the original dataset. We did not require additional IRB approvals or informed consent for access to patient geocoded address data. All research work for the current analyses was carried out in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation (institutional or regional) and the Helsinki Declaration of 1975. In the original work, all children were admitted to the PICU between September 1, 2014, and August 31, 2015.

The cohort used in this report has been fully described in the 2017 publication (20). In brief, the operational definition of PD was a Cornell Assessment of Pediatric Delirium (CAPD) score of nine or higher, which was performed bid during the PICU stay. For children with developmental disabilities (as defined by a Pediatric Cerebral Performance Category of 4 at prehospital baseline [21]), in addition to the CAPD score, the physician had to determine that there was an alteration from the child’s baseline static encephalopathy before classifying as PD. Daily mental status was defined as “comatose” when the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) score (22) was –4 or –5, delirious, or “normal” when neither delirium nor coma was present. In 2014–2015, we used the term PD “incidence” to define the rate of PD occurring for at least 1 day in any child during the PICU stay. We now use the term “prevalence” in this secondary analysis. PD duration was the number of days spent with delirium. Classification of delirium as “hyperactive,” “hypoactive,” or “mixed” was performed according to the RASS scores. Patients with RASS of 0 to –3 were labeled “hypoactive,” RASS 0 to +4 were classified as “hyperactive” and children with RASS scores including both positive and negative values were classified as “mixed” delirium. Illness severity was quantified using the Pediatric Index of Mortality-3 score at admission (23). All data concerning SDoH presented in this report are novel and have not been published elsewhere.

In this analysis, only the first admission per patient was included. Race and ethnicity subgroup, language preference, insurance coverage, and home address were obtained from the electronic medical record (EMR). Race and ethnicity subgroups were defined as White non-Hispanic (WNH), Black non-Hispanic (BNH), Hispanic, Asian/Pacific Islander (API), and other. The “other” subgroup included patients with self-identified race listed as “other combinations not described” or “mixed,” and ethnicity listed as either “non-Hispanic” or “missing.” (Patients listed as “Other” race and “Hispanic” ethnicity were defined as “Hispanic”). Patient addresses were geocoded using the well-validated open-source Decentralized Geomarker Assessment for Multi-Site Studies software (24). The resultant 11-digit Federal Information Processing System (FIPS) codes for the corresponding census tracts were extracted. We then linked these census tract FIPS code to the COI 2.0. We assigned a COI quintile to each patient based on their census tract. Consistent with an example from the COI literature (14), COI was condensed from five groups to two, combining very low, low, and moderate groups (1–3) vs. high and very high groups (4–5), for the purpose of analyses.

Summary statistics of continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]). Pearson chi-square test was used to test the univariate relationships between delirium development and COI, race and ethnicity subgroup, language preference, and insurance type. As we expected considerable collinearity between these variables, which would become relevant in model building, chi-square and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to assess the association of these factors with COI. A raincloud plot was constructed to visualize the overlap between COI and race and ethnicity subgroup, language, and insurance. We then constructed a multivariable logistic regression model, containing the binary COI measure in addition to the demographic and clinical parameters identified as significantly and independently associated with delirium in our originally published 2017 model (20). This model included age, developmental disability, severity of illness at admission, need for invasive mechanical ventilation, depth of sedation, and medication exposures. Automated variable selection procedures, including stepwise variable selection and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) modeling, were applied to this full model to determine whether COI would remain as a meaningful explanatory variable.

We conducted a complete case analysis for this study. For univariate analyses, any observation with nonmissing data for both the variable of interest and the outcome was included in the corresponding hypothesis test. For the multivariable regression, only observations with complete data across all included variables were retained. This approach was deemed appropriate given most potential explanatory variables had less than 5% missing data. The only exception was race, which had a higher proportion of missingness (23%). However, the abovementioned strategic imputation methods were previously applied to mitigate this issue, and any residual missingness was assumed to be missing completely at random. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) are presented with 95% CIs.

RESULTS

In this secondary analysis of the 2017 report (20), we were able to include 1264 unique patient encounters (the original cohort had 1547 admissions with some duplicate patients). There were 193 of 1264 patients (15.3%) that developed PD for a total of 903 days of delirium in the PICU. Median duration of delirium was 2 days (IQR, 1–4 d). The frequency of PD phenotype was hypoactive in 406 of 903 (45%), hyperactive in 79 of 903 (9%), and mixed in 418 of 903 (46%) days of PD.

Race and ethnicity subgroup was missing for 303 of 1264 patients (24%), insurance status for 24 of 1264 patients (1.9%), and language preference for 37 of 1264 patients (2.9%; Table 1). The most common self-reported race and ethnicity subgroup was WNH (341/961 [35%]); the least common was API (119/961 [12%]). Insurance status was evenly split between private (583/1240 [47%]) and public (573/1240 [46%]). Patients’ language preference was English for 1046 of 1227 (85%), Spanish 87 of 1227 (7.1%), Mandarin for 23 of 1227 (1.9%), and another language in 64 of 1227 (5.3%). For 1246 of 1264 patients (98%), we were able to map home addresses to a census tract corresponding to one of the five COI groups. Using the nationally based COI quintiles, there were 28% of patients in the very low and the very high groups, with 12–18% in each of the middle three groups (Table 1).

Building on the multivariable model published in 2017 using the 2014–2015 dataset (20), showing associations between PD and younger age, higher severity of illness score, developmental delay, use of mechanical ventilation, being in coma, and benzodiazepine or anticholinergic medication use, we added the dichotomous COI category (groups 1–3 and groups 4/5). Being categorized in the three lowest COI groups, as opposed to the highest two COI groups, was independently associated with greater odds of developing PD (aOR, 1.55 [95% CI, 1.05–2.3]; p = 0.028; Fig. 2). Stepwise variable selection and LASSO modeling were applied to this full model; lower COI was retained as an independently meaningful explanatory variable for PD in both analyses.

Forest plot of multivariable model results. COI = Child Opportunity Index.

To investigate whether the effect of COI may be modified by race and ethnicity subgrouping, we considered an additional multivariable model including an interaction term between the two. The results indicated that there was no significant interaction effect. Because of the relationship between lower COI and deep sedation in the univariate analysis, we also explored whether this relationship was modified by including age, developmental delay, and language, and lower COI remained associated with greater odds of deep sedation (aOR, 1.78; p = 0.017).

DISCUSSION

In this secondary analysis of a 2014–2015, single-center PICU cohort, we have found an association between PD and a child’s neighborhood socioeconomic opportunity, as quantified by the COI. Lower COI grouping by census tract was independently associated with a 1.5-fold greater odds of delirium even after controlling for the demographic and clinical factors that are known to be related to PD in PICU.

We present these results in the context of an increasing body of literature describing the impact of SDoH on PICU presentation and outcomes. The COI serves as a validated summary measure of SDoH, capturing neighborhood-level factors such as education, environment, and socioeconomic resources that are known to influence child health (15–19). We had hypothesized that COI would be related to PD based on previously described associations. For example, children with higher severity of illness on PICU admission are more likely to develop delirium, and children with lower COI often present to the PICU when they are more severely ill (11, 12, 17).

We believe that future research should focus on the relationship between COI and PD and explore any modifiable or mitigating factors that may provide protection. It is noteworthy that we found that children with lower COI had a higher prevalence of administration of deep sedation—a factor that could have potentially mediated the association between COI and increased risk of delirium. Alternatively, perhaps optimizing and supporting family-centered care in the PICU is the potentially ameliorating factor (25). For example, we are learning about the considerable stresses that families experience in the PICU, especially when balancing the need to be with their critically ill child, yet also needing to maintain their employment (26–28). Prior research shows that decreased socioeconomic resources are a risk factor for less parental presence and engagement in the PICU (9–11). In this context, a secondary analysis of a single-center PICU cohort (2014–2017) found that greater family presence was associated with lower odds of PD (29). However, investigation of SDoH was not a focus in this analysis, which means that further exploration of possible mechanisms underlying the relationship between COI and PD are warranted. First, whether it is conceivable that contrary to recommendations there is inadvertent overuse of sedative medications because the calming influence of the family at the bedside is lacking—we need more studies (1–3, 30–32). Second, could it be that family presence and ease of bedside involvement in optimizing opportunities to mobilize and orient the child, as well as reinforcing sleep (all of which may have protective effects against delirium) are lacking—again, more investigation is warranted (1, 2, 9, 11, 13–17, 28, 29). Third, are there structural barriers such as socioeconomic factors or systemic racism that need to be addressed (7, 8, 14, 17) with a “shift in mindset to thinking about both microlevel individual and macrolevel neighborhood social risks as relevant to child health and critical illness in particular” (33).

The current study has four main limitations. First, the work examines the relationship between SDoH and odds of PD in the PICU, albeit in a dataset that is now 10 years old. Therefore, future prospective PD studies should consider SDoH in evaluating a patient’s risk of delirium. Second, as a post hoc analysis of a 2014–2015 prospective cohort (20), our report is limited by the historical nature of the data. However, as census results are delayed by years, our cohort was closely timed to the most recently available Census data at the time of analysis. Third, there was missing data: self-reported race and ethnicity subgrouping was not possible for 24% of patients. Even so, by using COI in our multivariable model, we were able to include 98% of the cohort and thus minimize the risk of ascertainment bias. Finally, we note that this single-center cohort did not represent the expected quintile distribution of the five COI groups, but instead was weighted toward the lowest (COI = 1) and highest (COI = 5) opportunity neighborhoods. Therefore, generalizability of the findings may be limited.

In conclusion, delirium is an undesired outcome in the PICU, and prevention requires awareness of risk factors and commitment to environmental and management strategies. In our nonprespecified secondary analysis of a 2014–2015 PICU cohort (20), we have shown that a lower COI is associated with greater odds of developing PD, a relationship that may be modifiable. Further research into potential mechanisms of this relationship is indicated.
