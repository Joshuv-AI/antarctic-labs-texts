In Vitro and In Vivo Comparison of a Novel Antimicrobial Wound Hydrogel With Commercial Wound Treatments: Antimicrobial, Antibiofilm and Biocompatibility Assessment
Authors: Melika Moradi, Ali Pormohammad, Charline Luong, Raymond J Turner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) and biofilm formation significantly hinder chronic wound management, necessitating safer and more effective therapeutic options. This study evaluates the antimicrobial, antibiofilm, cytocompatibility and anti‐inflammatory properties of a novel antimicrobial hydrogel Formulation (#1) compared with commercially available wound hydrogel and cream Formulations (#2–5). Antimicrobial activity was assessed using minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) assays against Staphylococcus aureus , Pseudomonas aeruginosa , Candida albicans , and mixed‐species cultures. Biofilm‐related efficacy was evaluated via crystal violet (CV) staining and minimum biofilm inhibitory concentration (MBIC) assays. Cytotoxicity was examined using ISO‐compliant MTT and confluency assays on L929 fibroblasts. In vivo anti‐inflammatory effects were assessed using intra‐cutaneous injection in New Zealand White rabbits with histological evaluation per ISO 10993‐23. Formulation 1 showed the lowest MIC and MBC values across all pathogens, including polymicrobial cultures, indicating strong broad‐spectrum efficacy. In biofilm assays, it reduced biofilm biomass by 50%–60% within 10 min and prevented new formation at lower MBIC values than Formulation 2, especially in mixed‐species models. Cytotoxicity testing confirmed Formulation 1 maintained ≥ 81% cell viability across all concentrations, outperforming other products and meeting ISO and USP safety thresholds. In vivo, both Formulation 1 and 2 induced minimal inflammation, with Formulation 1 showing slightly milder tissue responses. Formulation 1 demonstrated strong antimicrobial efficacy, reliable biofilm control, and favourable cytocompatibility compared with the comparator formulations tested in this study. These findings support further evaluation of this formulation for chronic wounds complicated by biofilms and antimicrobial resistance.Keywords: antimicrobials, biofilm disruption, biofilm prevention, mixed species, wound infection

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as a major issue in modern medicine, particularly in the management of chronic wounds. Over the past few decades, microorganisms have progressively evolved resistance mechanisms in part due to the misuse and overuse of antimicrobial treatments [1]. The rise of AMR is now a grave concern for the World Health Organization (WHO), as it is a leading cause of illness and mortality in the 21st century [2].

Chronic wounds present a significant clinical challenge, leading to prolonged patient suffering and increased healthcare costs. These wounds are often complicated by infections involving biofilm‐formation of the infecting bacteria [3]. Biofilms, composed of microbial communities encased in a protective extracellular matrix, have the distinctive phenotype of being highly tolerant to antimicrobials, which makes them difficult to eradicate [4]. This context, microorganisms exist either as planktonic cells or as sessile biofilm‐associated lifestyles, with the latter exhibiting substantially greater resistance to antimicrobial treatments than planktonic counterparts assessed by conventional MIC and MBC assays. This enhanced tolerance, together with the presence of antibiotic resistance determinants, contributes to treatment failure, persistent infection, and delayed wound healing [5, 6]. Biofilms are a major factor in the chronicity of wounds, as they protect bacteria like Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa , two common perpetrators in wound infections [7]. These bacteria in the biofilm can also evade the host immune system [8] and through resisting antimicrobial therapies, leads to prolonged inflammation, delayed healing, and increased risk of complications such as tissue necrosis and sepsis [9]. In addition to bacteria, fungal organisms such as Candida species are also frequently identified in chronic wounds and contribute to biofilm formation, further complicating infection management. Candida can form mixed‐species biofilms with bacteria, increasing the overall resistance to antimicrobial and antifungal therapies. These biofilms enhance immune evasion and sustain chronic inflammation, both of which interfere with wound healing and can prolong the course of infection [10, 11, 12].

Wound healing is a complex, multi‐stage process that includes haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodelling [13]. Biofilms in chronic wounds disrupt this process by prolonging the inflammatory phase, preventing progression to tissue regeneration [14]. As a result, wounds remain in a chronic inflammatory state, delaying healing and fostering the persistence of biofilm‐associated bacteria. This can contribute to the development and spread of AMR, further complicating treatment options [15].

Given the failures of traditional antibiotic treatments, there is increasing interest in exploring synergistic approaches that combine multiple antimicrobial agents or integrate antimicrobial treatments with other therapeutic agents for improved wound infection control and healing strategies [16]. For instance, combining different antimicrobials into antimicrobial hydrogels has shown promise in overcoming biofilm issues by disrupting the biofilm matrix and enhancing the penetration of antimicrobial agents [17].

In response to these challenges, this study compares several wound care formulations on the marked as well as a recently developed novel antimicrobial wound hydrogel formulation to evaluate their activity against biofilm‐associated infections, along with their cytocompatibility and in vivo tissue response profiles. The findings provide a comparative assessment of the antimicrobial, antibiofilm, cytocompatibility, and tissue response characteristics of these products.

Materials and Methods

Bacterial Strains, Culture Media and Test Materials

Bacterial and fungi strains were stored at −70°C in Microbank vials as described by the manufacturer (Pro‐Lab Diagnostics, Richmond Hill, ON, Canada). S. aureus (ATCC 25923), P. aeruginosa (PA01) and Candida albicans (ATCC 22019) were used in this study. Two different media were used as the growth and susceptibility testing media in this study: Lysogeny Broth (LB) (VWR chemicals; Lot# 190756384) and infection‐related simulated wound fluid (SWF) (50% peptone water [0.85% NaCl, 0.5 g peptone per 500.0 mL]:50% FCS [GIBCO, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Lot# 2212202RP]) [18].

The following wound care formulations were evaluated in this study: Formulation #1, a hydrogel containing zinc and 2‐Isopropyl‐5‐methylphenol; Formulation #2 (Prontosan Wound Gel; B. Braun Medical, Melsungen, Germany), containing betaine and polyhexanide; Formulation #3 (NanoSALV Catalytic Advanced Wound Care Treatment Matrix Hydrogel/NanoTess Gel; Calgary, Alberta, Canada), containing a micron copper‐based catalyst; Formulation #4 (Silver Biotics Gel; American Biotech Labs, Alpine, Utah, USA); and Formulation #5 (Silver Biotics Cream; American Biotech Labs, Alpine, Utah, USA) (this gel and cream's active ingredient is SilverSol nanosilver of 3–5 nm).

Preparation of Mixed‐Species Culture

S. aureus (ATCC 25923), P. aeruginosa (PA01) and C. albicans (ATCC 22019) were each cultured separately at 37°C overnight in LB. On the day of experimentation, each strain was sub‐cultured into fresh LB and grown to mid‐logarithmic phase. Cultures were centrifuged at 3000 g for 15 min at 4°C. The resulting pellets were re‐suspended in SWF and adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard, corresponding to approximately 1 × 108 CFU/mL. Equal volumes of each standardised suspension were mixed in a 1:1:1 ratio to create the final inoculum for polymicrobial cultures. The presence of S. aureus , P. aeruginosa , and C. albicans within the polymicrobial cultures was confirmed by plating samples on selective media: Mannitol Salt Agar for S. aureus , Cetrimide Agar for P. aeruginosa , and CHROMagar candida for C. albicans , followed by colony morphology assessment [19, 20, 21].

Antimicrobial Assays

Controls of untreated (media or SWF alone) and saline controls were included in all planktonic MIC, MBC antimicrobial analysis showing no effect against test pathogens. For the biofilm disruption and inhibition assays, a saline addition served as 100% biomass reference defined as untreated. We also tested the hydrogel that was part of our formulation as the carrier agent, which showed no antimicrobial effect at all and thus not included in the figures.

Minimum Inhibitory and Bactericidal Concentration (MIC and MBC) Assays

Bacterial and fungal isolates were sub‐cultured twice from −70°C glycerol stocks onto appropriate agar plates and incubated overnight at 37°C to ensure purity and to isolate single colonies. A 0.5 McFarland‐standardised mixed‐species inoculum was prepared and diluted 7.5‐fold in SWF to achieve a final concentration of approximately 1 × 108 CFU/mL. Then, 75 μL of the diluted inoculum was added to each well of a microtitre plate, followed by the addition of serial two‐fold dilutions of the test compounds, starting from a concentration of 2 mg/mL. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 h in a microplate shaker at 150 rpm [22]. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was defined as the lowest concentration of the test compound at which no visible growth was observed across all three species in the mixed inoculum. To determine the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC), cultures from each well were transferred onto LB agar plates using a replica pinning inoculator. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 18–24 h, after which bacterial growth was visually inspected. The MBC endpoint was defined as the lowest concentration at which no viable colonies from any of the three species were observed [23, 24].

Biofilm Disruption Assay

S. aureus , P. aeruginosa (PA01), C. albicans , and a mixed‐species culture ( S. aureus , P. aeruginosa and C. albicans ) were used in this study. Bacterial strains were retrieved from −70°C storage and plated onto appropriate solid media. Colonies were transferred into LB and incubated in a shaking incubator at 37°C for 2 h to reach early exponential phase. Following incubation, bacterial suspensions were adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard based on turbidity. Further dilutions were performed in SWF to achieve standardised concentrations suitable for biofilm formation assays.

Biofilm Cultivation

The organisms were cultured in sterile glass tubes and adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard; the bacterial suspension was then dispersed into a reservoir and further diluted 7.5‐fold with SWF to achieve the desired colony‐forming units (CFU), corresponding to approximately 1 × 108 CFU/mL. The culturing of the biofilm was done by placing 200 μL of diluted bacterial suspension into each well of the Calgary Biofilm Device [25]. Then the assay was incubated at 35°C on a microplate shaker (150 rpm) for 48–72 h with media changes every 24 h.

Challenging Biofilm With Antimicrobial Wound Formulations

Following incubation, the biofilm formation was visually assessed to confirm consistent bacterial growth. To remove planktonic bacteria, the plate was washed three times with 200 μL of sterile double‐distilled water (ddH2O). The biofilms were then exposed to the antimicrobial formulations and control groups (treated with sterile saline [0.1%]), SWF broth only (untreated), and left for 10 min, 30 min or 1 h.

Biofilm Quantification

After removing excess liquid from treatment, 200 μL of a 0.1% crystal violet (CV) solution was added to all the wells in order to stain the biofilm formed during culturing on the sides and bottoms of the microtitre plate, the peg lid was set aside for viable cell evaluations in the biofilm. The wells with CV were left for 30 min before removal of excess solution; then excess dye was rinsed off by dipping each plate in two successive water baths with sterile ddH2O [26, 27]. After drying, 200 μL of 70% ethanol was added to the wells for 15 min to elute the CV. Using a multichannel pipette, the extracted dye solutions in each well were then transferred to a new plate and measured in a microplate reader set at OD540 [27]. The untreated wells were referenced as 100% bacterial growth without treatment, used for comparison of % biofilm eradicated after treatment. This assay evaluates biofilm biomaterial, that is, both the cells and the biofilm matrix polymers, and is helpful in evaluating biofilm disruption [18, 28].

Biofilm Inhibition Assay (Minimum Biofilm Inhibitory Concentration [MBIC] Determination)

Test articles were serially diluted, and 75 μL of each concentration was added into designated wells of a sterile 96‐well microtitre plate. An equal volume (75 μL) of bacterial suspension in SWF (1.0 × 106 CFU/mL) was added to each well. Plates were incubated for 48 h at 37°C in a shaking microplate incubator (150 rpm). The planktonic cells and the spent medium were discarded, and the adhered biomass was rinsed two times with distilled water. Optical density (OD) at 650 nm (OD650) was measured. The MBIC was defined as the lowest concentration of the test article producing an OD650 ≤ 10% of the positive (untreated) control, corresponding to at least a 1‐log reduction in biomass after 6 h. Each assay was conducted in biological triplicate, and results were averaged [18].

Cell Cytotoxicity

Cell Viability and Cytotoxicity Assay

The following protocol was followed in accordance with ISO 10993‐12 [29] in sterile conditions. To evaluate biocompatibility, cytotoxicity testing was conducted using mouse fibroblast cells, NCTC clone 929 (L cell, L‐929, derivative of Strain L). L929 cells have been widely used in numerous in vitro cell culture model toxicity assessments [30]. To assess the conversion of yellow methyl tetrazolium salt (MTT) into blue formazan crystals by active cell mitochondrial dehydrogenases, the MTT (3‐(4,5‐Dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐Diphenyltetrazolium Bromide) assay was performed, following ISO standard 10 993–5:2009(E); Biological evaluation of medical devices for MTT cytotoxicity test. This transformation serves as the basis for determining cell viability and metabolic activity, employing a colorimetric technique to quantify live cells and their metabolic functions [31]. As previously conducted on multi–drug‐resistant human leukaemic cells assessing cytotoxicity, the MTT assay demonstrated significant results. Its versatility did not affect the outcomes in different cell lines, ensuring internal consistency and reliability for drug cytotoxicity assays. This consistency is why the MTT assay is widely used in determining drug sensitivity [32].

Thawing From Stock

L929 frozen cell pellets were retrieved from nitrogen tanks and thawed at room temperature. All sample content was then added to a falcon tube containing media (DMEM, 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin). The sample was centrifuged (1500 rpm, 10 min) and supernatant was removed then re‐suspended into new media and then added to cell culture flasks. After thawing from stock, cells were passaged two to three times before proceeding to begin the use for MTT Assay. Cells were cultured in a T‐75 flask consisting of Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS), and 1% Penicillin–Streptomycin (10 000 U/mL) at 37°C in a humidified CO2 (5%) chamber and > 90% humidity.

Cell Passaging

To initiate cell detachment, L929 cells were gently washed with 1 mL of sterile phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) pre‐warmed to 37°C. Gibco's 0.05% Trypsin–EDTA (Gibco 25 300 054) was obtained from −20°C storage and thawed at room temperature. Subsequently, cells were detached using 2–3 mL of trypsin–EDTA for no more than 5 min and then neutralised using DMEM. An automated pipette was employed to spray the surface area, aiding in the dispersion of cells into a suspension for subsequent cell density assessment and the cell suspension was transferred to a new falcon tube for further plating and or seeding.

Seeding 96‐Well Plate

Once cell culture had reached ~80% confluency, they were removed from culture flasks using Gibco's 2.5% Trypsin (10×), and the cell suspension was centrifuged (200 g, 3 min). The cells were then re‐suspended in culture medium, and the cell suspension was adjusted at a density of 1 × 105 cells/ml. A volume of 100 μL culture medium only (blank) was added into the peripheral wells of the 96‐well tissue culture plates. In the remaining wells, 100 μL of a cell suspension of 1 × 105 cells/mL was plated (1 × 104 cells/well). Cells were then incubated for 24 h before beginning the cell cytotoxicity assay.

After 24 h from seeding L929, the media was removed by aspirating and 100 μL of the appropriate treatment solution was added to each well. Concentrations of wound treatment gels are indicated as follows: A (5%), B (2.5%), C (1.25%) and D (0.625%); any required dilutions were done prior with DMEM. The plate was then returned to the incubator for the designated incubation period. Following the treatment period, each well was observed using a phase contrast microscope to document any morphological alterations attributable to the cytotoxic effects of the test sample extracts. Each condition, including various concentrations and treatments, was replicated in triplicate within each 96‐well plate, alongside control variables, including untreated cell controls and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) as a positive cytotoxic control. The plates were examined under a phase contrast microscope to ensure uniformity across the plate before being incubated for 24 h (5% CO2, 37°C, > 90% humidity).

Examining Cell Morphology

Before and after the treatment period, each plate was examined under a phase‐contrast microscope to identify any changes in cell morphology and growth characteristics between the control and treated cells. The observed changes were quantified as a percentage from 0% to 100%, with 100% representing the control condition.

Evaluation of Cell Respiration

A 1 mg/mL MTT (3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide) stock solution in sterile PBS was prepared on the day of the experiment and kept at room temperature, protected from light using an amber glass jar covered in aluminium foil. Following examination of cell morphology, the culture medium was aspirated from the plates and 50 μL of fresh MTT solution was then added to each test well. The plate was then incubated further for 2 h in the incubator. Following incubation, MTT solution was then removed and 100 μL of isopropanol was added into each well to dissolve remaining formazan crystals. The plate was swayed gently and transferred to a microplate reader (570 nm filter; reference wavelength 650 nm).

Animal Model

Ethics Statement

This study was designed to evaluate the potential irritation and biological reactivity of an antimicrobial wound gel following intra‐cutaneous injection in New Zealand White rabbits. This animal model was selected per ISO 10993‐23:2021 (Biological Evaluation of Medical Devices—Part 23: Tests for Irritation) [33], which recommends in vivo testing in rabbits for dermal and sub‐dermal irritation assessments when validated non‐animal alternatives are unavailable. New Zealand White rabbits are commonly used due to their predictable and translatable responses to human clinical conditions. All animal procedures were performed in compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, 8th Edition (National Research Council, US) [34], and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Labcorp Early Development Laboratories Inc., located in Madison, Wisconsin, USA. The protocol number for this study is p24‐0244‐00a, and the Test Site Reference Number is 8540‐245. The study was sponsored by Geneva Laboratories Inc. (Elkhorn, WI, USA) and conducted for MHCombiotic Inc. (Calgary, Alberta, Canada). Histological evaluation and animal testing were performed at Labcorp Early Development Laboratories Inc., a GLP‐compliant facility operating under the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) regulations as outlined in 21 CFR Part 58.

Experimental Design

Formulation 1 (test article) and Formulation 2 (Control article) were dosed as received from the Sponsor. No additional processing or dilution of the products occurred before their administration to the animals. Three New Zealand White rabbits were used in this study. Prior to the start of the experiment, each animal underwent a pre‐treatment screening to ensure they were free from irritation, trauma, or pre‐existing conditions that could affect the outcome of the test. Body weights were recorded before treatment administration, and the animals' dorsal sides were clipped of fur 4–18 h before the injections. For each rabbit, five intra‐cutaneous injection sites were selected on either side of the dorsal region. Formulation 1 was injected at five sites on one side, while Formulation 2 was injected at five sites on the other side, with 0.2 mL of the respective products injected into each site. The injection sites were marked with indelible ink for easy identification during the post‐dose observation periods.

Post‐Dose Observations

The injection sites were observed immediately after administration and at intervals of 24 ± 2 h, 48 ± 2 h, and 72 ± 2 h. Clinical observations, including erythema (skin redness) and oedema (swelling), were scored using a standard Classification System for Scoring Skin Reactions. Body weights were recorded again at the end of the observation period to monitor any changes. At the end of the study, the rabbits were humanely euthanised using an injectable barbiturate. The injection sites, along with surrounding tissue, were excised and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for histological evaluation.

Histopathological Evaluation

The fixed tissues were outsourced and processed at Labcorp (Madison, Wisconsin). Haematoxylin and eosin‐stained sections were prepared, and microscopic examination was performed to assess biological reactions, including signs of inflammation (e.g., infiltration of inflammatory cells), encapsulation, haemorrhaging, necrosis and discoloration. Macroscopic evaluations were also performed before the histological assessment. The following categories of biological reaction were assessed by microscopic observation, and the responses were graded according to ISO 10993‐23:2021 guidelines. Histological evaluations included polymorphonuclear cells, lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages, giant cells, necrosis, neovascularisation, fibrosis, and fatty infiltration. Each parameter was scored semi‐quantitatively on a scale from 0 to 4 based on established criteria (Table S4), and the relative site involvement was determined using a standardised grading scale.

After the 72 ± 2 h grading, all erythema grades plus oedema grades from 24 ± 2 h, 48 ± 2 h, and 72 ± 2 h were totalled separately for each product for each individual animal. To calculate the score on each individual animal, divide each of the totals by 15 (3 scoring time points x 5 test or vehicle control injection sites). To determine the overall mean score for each test article and each corresponding vehicle control, the scores were added for the three animals and divided by three. The final test article score was obtained by subtracting the score of the vehicle control from the test article score. The requirements of the test will be met if the difference between the test article mean score and the vehicle control mean score is 1.0 or less. If at any observation period the average reaction to the test article is questionably greater than the average reaction to the vehicle control, the test will be repeated using three additional rabbits. Each injection site was scored for inflammatory and tissue responses, and the individual scores were summed to determine the bioreactivity rating. The rating was calculated by averaging the scores across all animals and comparing the test article's performance with the control article. The Bioreactivity Rating system used was as follows:

0.0–2.9: No Reaction.

0.0–2.9: No Reaction.

3.0–8.9: Slight Reaction.

3.0–8.9: Slight Reaction.

9.0–15.0: Moderate Reaction.

9.0–15.0: Moderate Reaction.

15: Severe Reaction.

15: Severe Reaction.

Statistical Analysis

For in vivo experiments, erythema and oedema scores from each observation period were combined and averaged for each injection site. The overall mean score for the test article was calculated by subtracting the vehicle control score from the test article score. A Bioreactivity Rating of ≤ 1.0 was considered acceptable based on ISO 10993‐23 standards. If the results indicated questionable reactivity, the study protocol required repeating the experiment with additional rabbits. For other experiments, statistical analyses and data visualisation were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA) and Microsoft Excel. Two‐way ANOVA with ordinary and Tukey's multiple comparison tests was used to analyse the data. p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Antimicrobial Activity: Planktonic Microorganisms

To assess the antimicrobial efficacy of selected wound care products against planktonic microorganisms, MICs and minimum biocidal concentrations (MBCs) were determined for S. aureus , P. aeruginosa , C. albicans and a mixed‐species culture combining all three species. Mixed‐species systems often exhibit higher antimicrobial tolerances [35].

MIC values varied significantly among the tested products and organisms (Figure 1a). For S. aureus , only Formulation 1, the novel antimicrobial hydrogel, exhibited significantly low MICs compared to Formulation 5 (p < 0.05). Against P. aeruginosa , Formulation 1 showed lower MIC values than both Formulation 3 and Formulation 5 (p < 0.05), while Formulation 2 also demonstrated significantly lower MICs than Formulation 3 (p < 0.05). For C. albicans , Formulation 1 achieved significantly lower MICs in comparison to Formulation 3 (p < 0.01), Formulation 4 (p < 0.05), and Formulation 5 (p < 0.01). In the mixed‐species model, both Formulations 1 and 2 displayed significantly lower MICs compared to Formulations 3 and 5 (p < 0.001).

Data points are represented as means (n = replicates = 5). Error is shown in ± standard deviation (SD). MBC results mirrored the MIC trends, showing product‐ and organism‐specific differences (Figure 1b). Against S. aureus , Formulation 1 had significantly lower MBC values than Formulation 3 (p < 0.01) and Formulation 5 (p < 0.05). In P. aeruginosa , Formulations 1 and 2 both exhibited significantly lower MBC values than Formulation 5 (p < 0.05 and < 0.01, respectively), and Formulation 1 also showed significantly lower MBC values compared to Formulation 3 (p < 0.05). For C. albicans , Formulation 1 again showed significantly lower MBC values compared to both Formulation 3 and Formulation 5 (p < 0.05). In the mixed‐species condition, Formulation 1 and Formulation 2 exhibited significantly lower MBCs relative to Formulation 5 (p < 0.01 and < 0.05, respectively), suggesting better performance in a polymicrobial environment. Formulations 3, 4 and 5 consistently exhibited higher MIC and MBC values compared to the other antimicrobials, and no statistically significant differences were observed among them across treatment groups.

These results demonstrate that Formulation 1 and Formulation 2 generally exhibited superior antimicrobial activity, as evidenced by significantly lower MIC and MBC values compared to the other products. Formulation 1 consistently displayed the lowest mean MIC and MBC values across all tested organisms, including in the mixed‐species model, suggesting a broad and potent antimicrobial effect. Although no statistically significant difference was observed between Formulation 1 and Formulation 2, the mean concentrations required for inhibition and killing were consistently lower for Formulation 1, indicating greater efficacy at reduced doses. This trend was particularly pronounced in the mixed‐species condition, where Formulation 1 achieved MIC and MBC values below 0.25 mg/mL, whereas the other products, including Formulation 2, required higher concentrations. These findings suggest that while Formulation 1 and 2 perform similarly in terms of statistical significance, Formulation 1 may offer a practical advantage due to its lower effective concentrations used, which could enhance therapeutic efficiency and minimise any potential toxicity.

Antimicrobial Effects on Microbes in a Biofilm

Biofilm Disruption

The antibiofilm efficacy of the top two antimicrobial products, Formulation 1 and Formulation 2, was further evaluated using the CV staining approach to evaluate remaining biofilm after treatment [28]. Residual attached biofilm biomass was quantified by measuring OD at 540 nm after gel treatment. Four biofilm models were examined: a mixed‐species model, S. aureus , C. albicans and P. aeruginosa . Treatments were applied for 10 min, 30 min and 1 h, and outcomes were compared to untreated controls (Figure 2).

Residual biofilm biomass was assessed following treatment with Formulation #1 (novel antimicrobial hydrogel [black]) and Formulation #2 (Prontosan Gel [pink]) for 10 min, 30 min, and 1 h, compared to untreated controls (cyan). Biofilm models included: (a) mixed‐species ( S. aureus , P. aeruginosa , C. albicans ), (b) S. aureus , (c) C. albicans and (d) P. aeruginosa . Biofilms were stained with crystal violet, and biomass was quantified by optical density at 540 nm. Data are presented as per cent biofilm biomass relative to untreated controls (set to 100%). Bars represent mean ± standard deviation (SD) of biological replicates (n = 8). Statistical comparisons were conducted using two‐way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test. Significance is indicated as: p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), p < 0.0001 (****).

Across all biofilm models tested, mixed‐species, S. aureus , C. albicans and P. aeruginosa , both Formulation 1 and Formulation 2 demonstrated measurable antibiofilm activity, though with differing kinetics and magnitudes of effect. Formulation 1 consistently showed a rapid onset of action, reducing biofilm biomass by approximately 50%–60% within 10 min. This rapid action was significantly more effective than Formulation 2 at the same time point, with Formulation 1 outperforming Formulation 2 in early reduction across all organisms (p values ranging from 0.0002 to 0.0008). In comparison, Formulation 2 exhibited a more gradual effect, with modest reductions observed at the 10‐min time point (~17%–25%) and in some cases not reaching statistical significance (e.g., P. aeruginosa , p = 0.1120). However, its efficacy increased over time, with biofilm biomass reductions reaching ~38%–45% by 1 h across the tested organisms. At no point did Formulation 2 match the early efficacy achieved by Formulation 1, even after extended treatment times.

Importantly, Formulation 1's biofilm‐disrupting effect plateaued early, with no significant improvement observed beyond the initial 10 min, indicating that its efficacy is rapid and reaches a maximal effect quickly. This suggests that the gel effectively removes or disrupts biofilm early and primarily prevents regrowth rather than continuing to degrade biomass over time. In contrast, Formulation 2 demonstrated a time‐dependent increase in efficacy, indicating that longer exposure durations enhance its performance. While neither product allowed for biofilm regrowth over the 1‐h period, as indicated by comparison to untreated controls (which maintained or increased biomass), the observed differences in treatment dynamics provide insight into their potential clinical applications. Formulation 1 may be well‐suited for rapid interventions, whereas Formulation 2 may offer continued antibiofilm activity with prolonged contact time.

Biofilm Inhibition

Quantitative assessment of biofilm inhibition (MBIC) revealed comparable efficacy between Formulation 1 and Formulation 2 across single‐species biofilms of S. aureus and P. aeruginosa . For S. aureus , both products demonstrated a range of MBIC values between trials for their ability to prevent biofilm formation, with Formulation 1 ranging from 0.25 to 0.31 mg/mL and Formulation 2 from 0.25 to 0.5 mg/mL. In the case of P. aeruginosa , Formulation 1 exhibited MBICs between 0.0625 and 0.125 mg/mL, while Formulation 2 ranged from 0.125 to 0.31 mg/mL. For C. albicans , MBIC values with Formulation 1 ranged from 0.14 to 0.17 mg/mL, while Formulation 2 showed a broader and higher range of 0.17–0.4 mg/mL, indicating a relatively higher concentration needed for effective inhibition with Formulation 2. No statistically significant differences were observed between the treatments in these single‐species models. However, in the mixed‐species biofilm model of the three pathogens, a statistically significant difference (p < 0.01) was noted, with Formulation 2 requiring higher concentrations (0.125–0.625 mg/mL) to inhibit biofilm formation compared to Formulation 1 (0.0625–0.125 mg/mL). Both products showed effective biofilm prevention, though variation in MBIC values was more pronounced with Formulation 2, particularly in the S. aureus and C. albicans models, suggesting variability in its performance. Consistency across experimental replicates was observed with Formulation 1, supporting reliability in preventing biofilm formation (Figure 3).

Cytotoxicity Effects

Historically, cell cytotoxicity has been evaluated in vitro using various cell lines [36]. Here we use the L929 fibroblast cell line based on ISO recommendations due to its reliability and reproducibility in cytotoxicity assays [31]. Additionally, mice have been historically used to evaluate wound infection models, making the L929 cell line relevant due to its derivation from mouse connective tissue [31, 37]. The cytotoxicity of five wound care products was evaluated using the L929 murine fibroblast cell line, selected per ISO 10993‐5:2009 guidelines for its standardised role in biocompatibility testing (ISO 10993‐5:2009) [38]. Pre‐grown cells were exposed to serial dilutions (5%–0.625%) of test compounds for 24 h. Two complementary metrics were analysed: [1] metabolic activity via the metabolic MTT assay, which measures mitochondrial reductase‐dependent reduction of MTT to formazan (absorbance at 570 nm), and [2] cell confluency, assessed via phase‐contrast microscopy to quantify physical adherence and proliferation [39, 40]. Results for all treatment concentrations, including metabolic activity (OD 570 nm) and confluency (0%–100%), are summarised in Figures 4 and 5.

Cells were incubated for 24 h prior to initiating the cytotoxicity assay. MTT Assay: Metabolic Activity

The MTT assay demonstrated distinct metabolic responses across products. Formulation 1 exhibited minimal cytotoxicity, with metabolic activity values corresponding to 81%–100% (dark/light green) across all tested dilutions (5%–0.625%), exceeding the ISO 10993‐5:2009 acceptance threshold of ≥ 70% metabolic activity (Figure 4). Formulation 2 followed closely, showing 61%–85% metabolic activity (yellow/light green) at most concentrations, meeting ISO criteria at dilutions ≤ 2.5%. Formulation 3 displayed moderate activity (51%–75%, yellow), complying with ISO standards only at lower concentrations (≤ 1.25%). In contrast, Formulation 4 and 5 and the positive control SDS showed significant metabolic suppression (0%–40%, orange/red) at higher concentrations (≥ 1.25%), failing ISO compliance (Figure 4).

Cell Confluency and Microscopy Findings

Visual inspection under phase‐contrast microscopy (Figure 4) revealed concordance between metabolic activity and physical cell coverage. Formulation 1 maintained > 80% confluency at all dilutions, indicating robust cell adhesion and proliferation. Formulation 2 and Formulation 3 showed 50%–75% confluency, with minor detachment at higher concentrations. Conversely, Formulation 5 exhibited severe cell detachment (< 30% confluency) at ≥ 2.5% concentrations, corroborating their cytotoxic profiles. The acceptance criteria for 50% cell viability comply with USP Guidelines.

According to ISO 10993‐5:2009 standards, only Formulation 1 maintained ≥ 70% cell metabolic activity across all tested concentrations, indicating high biocompatibility and minimal cytotoxicity. Formulation 2 met the ISO threshold at concentrations ≤ 2.5%, while Formulation 3 complied only at ≤ 1.25%; in contrast, Formulation 5 failed to meet the standard at concentrations ≥ 1.25%. Similarly, per USP <87> guidelines [41], all products except Formulation 5 exhibited acceptable cytocompatibility at lower dilutions, maintaining ≥ 50% metabolic activity. These findings indicate that Formulation 1 was the only formulation in this study to meet both ISO and USP criteria across all concentrations tested. While Formulations 2 and 3 showed dose‐dependent cytotoxicity, they may still be considered for use at optimised, lower concentrations. Conversely, Formulation 5 consistently failed to meet safety thresholds, underscoring their potential cytotoxicity and the need for caution in clinical applications (Figure 5). The control treatment with the detergent SDS was used as recommended as it is expected to cause cell detachment, membrane disruption and cell damage and cell death.

In Vivo Local Tissue Reactivity and Biocompatibility

Mammalian animal models remain a critical tool for evaluating wound healing dynamics and inflammatory responses, as they closely mimic the physiological processes observed in humans. Among these, the New Zealand White rabbit is a well‐established model for intra‐cutaneous safety and irritation assessments, particularly in accordance with regulatory guidelines where validated non‐animal alternatives are lacking [42]. In the present study, we assessed and compared local tissue reactivity following intra‐cutaneous exposure to the two best‐performing formulations from the in vitro assays, Formulations 1 and 2. This comparison aimed to assess relative local tissue responses in a clinically relevant in vivo model. The use of these two products, selected on the basis of their demonstrated antimicrobial efficacy, allowed further evaluation of potential secondary effects related to local inflammatory responses during the wound‐healing process.

Animal Weight and Clinical Observations

All animals maintained stable body weights throughout the study, with a minimal weight loss of less than 2% observed in two of the rabbits (#B4491 and #B4507). No overt signs of systemic toxicity were noted in any of the animals during the observation period (Table S1).

Macroscopic Observations

Macroscopic evaluation of the injection sites indicated mild to moderate discoloration at all Formulation 1 and 2 treatment sites. Mild discoloration was also observed at three Formulation 2 sites. No significant signs of inflammation, encapsulation, haemorrhage, or necrosis were observed at any site during the 72‐h observation period. These findings suggest minimal and comparable adverse tissue reactions for both products (Tables S2 & S3).

Microscopic Observations

Histological analysis of the Formulation 1 injection sites revealed minimal to mild intra‐dermal oedema with scattered infiltration of mononuclear cells and granulocytes. Some dermal mononuclear cells appeared swollen, containing foamy material. Formulation 2 sites showed similar features, including mild oedema and moderate infiltration by inflammatory cells. Multi‐focal small areas of connective tissue disruption were apparent within regions of inflammation. Some mononuclear cells were swollen with foamy material, without signs of necrosis or significant disruption of connective tissue (Figure 6).

Microscopic observations (40× and 400× magnification) of histological sections from Formulation #1 (novel antimicrobial hydrogel) and Formulation #2 (Prontosan) injection sites.

Bioreactivity Rating

The Bioreactivity Rating, calculated based on histological and macroscopic observations, was 0.0 for both Formulations 1 and 2. According to ISO 10993‐23 guidelines, a rating of ≤ 1.0 indicates acceptable biocompatibility. Thus, both products demonstrated no significant biological reactivity and are considered safe options for clinical application, with minimal risk of inflammatory responses or tissue irritation.

Discussion

This study evaluated the antimicrobial, antibiofilm, cytocompatibility and local tissue reactivity profiles of a novel wound formulation (Formulation 1) compared with commercially available wound products. The findings contribute to the broader understanding of how various wound‐care products perform against single wound pathogens as well as in mixed species scenarios, although several limitations must be acknowledged.

Clinical guidelines consider various local antimicrobials, such as silver formulations, PHMB, octenidine dihydrochloride and iodine, equally effective for topical application [43]. However, direct comparisons among these agents, especially in biofilm or multi‐species infections or clinical models, remain sparse and inconsistent. As such, evidence‐based guidance for daily clinical use is still evolving [44, 45, 46, 47, 48].

Formulation 1 exhibited broad‐spectrum antimicrobial activity, with lower MIC and MBC values across the tested organisms, particularly in the mixed‐species condition, when compared to silver‐ and PHMB‐based products. Across all organisms tested, Formulations 1 and 2 showed lower MIC and MBC values than Formulation 3 and silver‐based products. Formulation 1 achieved significantly lower values in the mixed‐species model compared to Formulations 3 and 5, although differences between Formulations 2 and 2 were not statistically significant. These findings suggest that Formulation 1 may have potential for both bacteriostatic and bactericidal applications in managing complex infections and suggest greater potency at reduced doses. Thus, bactericidal agents are especially important as they can reduce the risk of bacterial resistance development and recovery post‐treatment compared to bacteriostatic agents [49].

Chronic wounds provide an ideal environment for microbial colonisation and biofilm formation, which significantly delays healing processes [15]. Biofilms protect resident microbes from both antimicrobial treatments and host immune responses, making their disruption and inhibition essential for effective wound management [15, 50]. Therefore, rapid biofilm disruption and prevention are key characteristics of an effective wound care product [51]. Polymicrobial biofilms, often composed of bacteria such as P. aeruginosa , S. aureus and fungi like C. albicans , exhibit complex interspecies interactions that further enhance their AMR [51]. In vivo studies have demonstrated that co‐infections by these organisms lead to denser biofilms, heightened immune activation, and systemic dissemination, particularly in deep or chronic wounds [19, 20, 35]. These observations reinforce the necessity for interventions that can act effectively across different microbial compositions and stages of biofilm evolution.

In this study, both Formulations 1 and 2 demonstrated effective biofilm inhibition and disruption across single‐ and mixed‐species models. Formulation 1 significantly inhibited biofilm formation at lower concentrations than Formulation 2 in mixed‐species conditions, suggesting a broader inhibitory spectrum. During biofilm disruption assays, Formulation 1 achieved notable disruption of mature biofilms within 10 min of exposure. In contrast, Formulation 2 took longer to disrupt biofilms and did not reach the same level of disruption as Formulation 1, which is consistent with previous findings that its polyhexamethylene biguanide (PHMB) component requires at least 1 h to significantly disrupt biofilm biomass [52]. Overall, both products contribute meaningfully to biofilm management, with Formulation 1 showing a more rapid and consistent response in the experimental models evaluated.

Prontosan, a wound cleanser containing PHMB, has had its anti‐biofilm activity only sporadically evaluated in the literature [35, 43, 53, 54, 55]. The relatively slower and concentration‐dependent inhibition and disruption observed with Prontosan in this study align with previous findings regarding PHMB. Although PHMB is widely used as an antiseptic, studies have indicated it may be less effective at fully preventing biofilm initiation compared to other agents such as octenidine or chlorhexidine [56]. Günther et al. [56] reported that PHMB exhibited lower inhibitory effects on biofilm metabolism among multi–drug‐resistant isolates, and another study highlighted Prontosan's limited ability to prevent biofilm formation under in vitro conditions [57]. Furthermore, previous studies on PHMB dressings showed ~2 log10 bacterial reduction followed by a bacteriostatic effect, though SEM imaging revealed only minimal biofilm surface disruption [58]. These findings underscore the challenges associated with achieving comprehensive biofilm control using PHMB‐based antiseptics.

Similarly, Silver‐containing dressings, although previously shown to be effective in planktonic or simple biofilm models, often perform inconsistently in complex biofilm environments or clinical studies of critically colonised wounds [59, 60, 61]. Halstead et al. observed considerable variability among silver dressings, with several failing to inhibit P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii biofilms even after prolonged exposure [62]. Silver ions are prone to sequestration by wound exudate proteins, reducing their bioavailability and antimicrobial efficacy [63]. This limitation is particularly evident in protein‐rich environments such as SWF [64], aligning with our findings where silver‐based products demonstrated lower inhibitory and bactericidal efficacy compared to Formulations 1 and 2. These observations are consistent with previous studies highlighting the diminished performance of silver treatments in chronic wound settings [60, 61, 65, 66, 67]. Furthermore, the inability of silver to sustain antimicrobial activity over time raises concerns about its long‐term effectiveness, as microbial regrowth has been frequently documented following treatment [68].

The observed performance of the novel antimicrobial hydrogel (Formulation 1) may be related to its multi‐component formulation strategy, combining multiple active agents to simultaneously target microbial membranes and biofilm matrix components. Unlike agents that rely primarily on oxidative stress mechanisms, key active components of Formulation 1 appear to disrupt bacterial membranes directly without inducing substantial reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation [69]. This rapid membrane‐disruptive action enables early and broad‐spectrum anti‐microbial and anti‐biofilm ability, an advantage especially important in mixed‐species biofilms where bacterial–fungal co‐operation can accelerate biofilm development. Combination antimicrobial approaches that are synergistic, like that used in Formulation 1, have been shown to lower MICs and biofilm reduction thresholds thus minimising host cytotoxicity [70, 71]. Persistent biofilm‐related infections often resist conventional antimicrobials, and the combined antibacterial and antibiofilm activity observed for this new formulation may therefore warrant further investigation [44, 46].

Cytotoxicity assays confirmed that Formulation 1 maintained ≥ 81% L929 fibroblast viability, even at high concentrations. In contrast, silver products showed significant cytotoxicity, and Formulation 2 exhibited moderate cytotoxicity. Silver's cytotoxicity stems from ROS generation and DNA damage [72], while PHMB affects mitochondria at higher concentrations [73]. The relatively low cytotoxic profile of Formulation 1 suggests favourable in vitro biocompatibility under the conditions tested and indicates that it can maintain cell viability without marked cytotoxic effects [69, 70]. This is critical, as many antimicrobials, particularly silver‐based ones, can adversely affect proliferating cells and delay wound closure [74]. A comparative study of povidone‐iodine, chlorhexidine acetate and PHMB reported strong cytotoxic effects in fibroblast and keratinocyte cells, with povidone‐iodine being the most cytotoxic, even at low concentrations [75]. These findings highlight the need to assess cytotoxic thresholds in wound care, especially for long‐term treatments.

Formulation 1 contains natural antimicrobial components comprising a combination of plant‐based small molecules/metabolite, 2‐Isopropyl‐5‐methylphenol and an inorganic salt of zinc. Together, these constituents are designed to confer antimicrobial and anti‐biofilm activity and are proposed to act potentially synergistically through multiple mechanisms, including disruption of ATP synthesis, inhibition of DNA replication, induction of oxidative stress and membrane damage, while demonstrating selective toxicity toward microbial cells [70]. Our previous study suggests that the component of Formulation 1 primarily disrupts bacterial membranes by exploiting structural differences between microbial and host cells, thereby minimising host toxicity. In the Caenorhabditis elegans model, these components were not only non‐toxic but also enhanced biological functions such as increased motility and growth [69].

In vivo assessments of local irritation and tissue responses in New Zealand White rabbits further validated safety and biocompatibility of Formulation 1. Both Formulations 1 and 2 caused only mild tissue reactions, with no necrosis, ulceration, or systemic toxicity observed. Body weight remained stable, and histopathology confirmed minimal inflammation. Formulation 1 showed slightly milder tissue responses than Formulation 2, suggesting better compatibility in managing wound inflammation and preserving tissue integrity. This aligns with regulatory assessments noting PHMB's irritation potential under controlled exposure [76], although higher concentrations may induce inflammation via NF‐κB pathway activation [77]. Previous histological evaluations of combinatorial treatments like Formulation 1 also support reduced tissue inflammation compared to single‐agent therapies [70]. The favorable cytotoxic profile of Formulation 1 in this study aligns with previous investigations into its natural components, which showed high safety margins both in vitro and in vivo.

Conclusion

The novel antimicrobial formulation evaluated here shows promise as a candidate for further evaluation as an alternative to conventional wound‐care products currently available. Its antimicrobial and antibiofilm activity, together with low cytotoxicity and a favourable inflammatory and local tissue response profile, differentiated it from the silver‐based and PHMB‐containing formulations in the assays performed. These findings support its potential relevance toward management of chronic wounds complicated by biofilm‐associated infections. However, in vivo efficacy studies in infected wound models, as well as evaluations of performance in the presence of wound exudate, are required to establish clinical relevance. A patent has been submitted for Formulation 1, and a partner company has trademarked the name ComBatic for this antimicrobial hydrogel.
