Diagnostic and therapeutic value of double-balloon enteroscopy in pediatric patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome
Authors: Wei-Chen Shen, Zhu-Bin Pan, Shi-Qin Qi, Jun-Jun Ju, Yu-Liang Zhou, Tao Zhang, Zhen-Qiang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeutz-Jeghers syndrome (PJS) is a rare autosomal dominant disorder characterized by mucocutaneous pigmentation and gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyposis, with a particularly high predilection for the small intestine. These polyps carry significant risks of intussusception, bleeding, and malignant transformation, making effective surveillance and intervention essential in affected children. This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic and therapeutic value of double-balloon enteroscopy (DBE) in children with PJS.MethodsClinical data were retrospectively collected from 22 children diagnosed with PJS who underwent DBE at Anhui Provincial Children’s Hospital between March 2024 and October 2025. The analyzed parameters included sex, age, duration of enteroscopy, examination approach, depth of insertion, number of polyps resected intraoperatively, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative complications, and length of hospital stay. Statistical analyses were performed for all variables.ResultsAmong the 22 pediatric patients, 16 were male and 6 were female, with a mean age of 11.35±3.96 years. The median duration of enteroscopy was 157.0 (90.75–253.5) minutes. An oral-only approach was performed in 7 patients, an anal-only approach in 5 patients, and combined oral and anal approaches in 10 patients. The mean insertion depth via the oral approach was 202.5±30.66 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz, whereas the mean insertion depth via the anal approach was 173.33±26.37 cm proximal to the ileocecal junction. A total of 76 polyps were resected using the oral approach and 135 polyps using the anal approach. One patient presented with a broad-based polyp located in the descending portion of the duodenum, which necessitated conversion to open laparotomy for polypectomy. Overall, 211 polyps were resected across all pediatric patients. The median intraoperative blood loss was 2.0 (1.0–2.0) mL per patient, and the mean postoperative hospital stay was 7.50±2.67 days. No postoperative gastrointestinal bleeding, intestinal perforation, or severe infectious complications were observed.ConclusionsDBE is a safe and feasible technique for the resection of multiple intestinal polyps in children with PJS. Compared with traditional open laparotomy, DBE offers advantages including scar-free healing, reduced surgical trauma, and improved postoperative recovery. These findings indicate that DBE has substantial clinical value and supports its broader application in pediatric clinical practice.Keywords: Children, double-balloon enteroscopy (DBE), Peutz-Jeghers syndrome (PJS), small intestinal polyps, treatment

Key findings

• Double‑balloon enteroscopy (DBE) was performed in 22 children with Peutz‑Jeghers syndrome (PJS), with a total of 211 polyps resected.

• No severe postoperative complications (bleeding, perforation, or infection) occurred. Median blood loss was 2.0 mL, and mean hospital stay was 7.5 days.

• Total enteroscopy was achieved in 30% of combined‑approach cases, with a 75% concordance with capsule endoscopy for polyp burden.

What is known and what is new?

• DBE is established for adult PJS, but pediatric evidence remains limited.

• This study provides a detailed perioperative profile in children and proposes a practical surveillance protocol (starting at 8–10 years, interval 1–3 years, with resection of polyps ≥15 mm).

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• DBE is safe and effective for pediatric PJS, supporting its broader use in children’s hospitals.

• Our institutional strategy may serve as a reference for standardizing DBE‑based management in younger patients.

Introduction

Peutz-Jeghers syndrome (PJS) is a rare autosomal dominant genetic disorder marked by mucocutaneous pigmentation and multiple hamartomatous polyps throughout the gastrointestinal tract (1). Its incidence in China is estimated at 1/8,300 to 1/200,000, and PJS is recognized as a rare childhood disease (2).

The primary clinical risks for children with PJS are recurrent intussusception and intestinal obstruction induced by small intestinal polyps. Severe complications include intestinal necrosis and abdominal infection. In addition, PJS polyps carry a lifelong malignant transformation risk, which greatly compromises children’s physical well-being and long-term prognosis. Given the low disease incidence, most current clinical investigations of pediatric PJS are small-sample retrospective reports. Systematic standardized comparative studies on diagnosis and treatment are lacking, and there lacks a unified pediatric-tailored management standard domestically, resulting in insufficient clinical experience. Meanwhile, the small intestine is anatomically concealed with a complex winding tract. Conventional examinations cannot fully visualize the whole small bowel, making it impossible to precisely assess polyp distribution and lesion severity, and failing to meet the demands of early diagnosis and targeted treatment.

Dofuku et al. reported satisfactory outcomes using double-balloon enteroscopy (DBE) for small intestinal disorders in children under 8 years old (3). This technique achieves full visualization of the entire small intestine, permits direct detection of tiny lesions, accurate lesion localization, and simultaneous minimally invasive polypectomy. Combining diagnostic and therapeutic merits, DBE is suitable for precise diagnosis and intervention in pediatric PJS.

However, adequate clinical data to define DBE indications, optimal intervention timing, procedural efficacy and long-term prognosis among children with PJS are still lacking. Therefore, this study enrolled 22 pediatric PJS patients treated with DBE in our hospital within one year. We summarize the real-world efficacy of DBE for this disease, further explore its clinical application value, and offer clinical evidence to support standardized minimally invasive diagnosis and treatment for pediatric PJS, as well as reduce procedure-related complications. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-1-0039/rc).

Methods

General information

A retrospective analysis was performed using clinical data from 22 pediatric patients diagnosed with PJS who were admitted to Anhui Provincial Children’s Hospital between March 2024 and October 2025. The collected variables included sex, age, duration of enteroscopy, examination approach, depth of endoscope insertion, number of polyps resected intraoperatively, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative complications, and length of hospital stay. All patients fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for PJS (4): (I) two or more histologically confirmed PJS polyps; (II) any number of PJS polyps in patients with a family history of PJS involving an immediate family member; (III) characteristic mucocutaneous pigmentation in patients with a family history of PJS involving an immediate family member; or (IV) any number of PJS polyps in patients presenting with characteristic mucocutaneous pigmentation. A diagnosis of PJS was established when any one of these criteria was met. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Anhui Provincial Children’s Hospital (Approval No. EYLL-2026-061). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

All patients had no contraindications to endoscopic intervention, including severe cardiac dysfunction, pulmonary dysfunction, or coagulation disorders, and all successfully underwent DBE. Among the included pediatric patients, 16 (72.7%) had a positive familial history. Abdominal pain was reported in 14 patients (63.6%), hematochezia was reported in 11 patients (50.0%), and concurrent abdominal pain and hematochezia were observed in 8 patients (36.4%). Varying degrees of growth and developmental delay were noted in 8 patients (36.4%), which were considered potentially associated with long-term dyspepsia and malabsorption. Among the 22 pediatric patients, 5 cases (22.7%) had a previous history of intussusception and all achieved remission via conservative management; 2 patients (9.1%) received partial small bowel resection more than 2 years ago due to intestinal ischemia secondary to recurrent PJS-related intussusception. A total of 14 patients (63.6%) completed STK11 germline genetic testing after obtaining written informed consent from guardians. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic STK11 variants were identified in 11 patients (78.6%), including 5 missense mutations, 3 frameshift mutations, 2 nonsense mutations and 1 splice-site mutation. No detectable STK11 variants were found via Sanger sequencing in the other 3 tested patients. The remaining 8 patients were diagnosed purely based on clinical diagnostic criteria: characteristic mucocutaneous pigmentation plus histologically confirmed hamartomatous polyps of PJS, with or without a positive family history. A total of 5 asymptomatic children (22.7%) were all identified through family screening (with at least one first-degree relative diagnosed with PJS). The monitoring protocol for PJS patients at Anhui Provincial Children’s Hospital. Patients diagnosed with PJS start annual capsule endoscopy (CE) screening from the age of 8 years. If polyps larger than 15 mm are found or if clinical symptoms occur, they will be referred for DBE intervention. Asymptomatic patients were identified through family screening. In this study, all 5 asymptomatic patients underwent DBE due to the detection of ≥15 mm polyps by CE during monitoring.

Treatment methods

Equipment and instruments

The equipment used included an enteroscope processor (Fujifilm EP-7000), a high-frequency electrosurgical unit (ERBE VIO 200D), an enteroscope (Fujifilm EN-580XT/T push-type), an overtube (Fujifilm ST-SB1), a CO2 insufflation pump (Fujifilm FI-10P), a water injection pump (Fujifilm APC-100), a balloon controller (Fujifilm PB-30), an injection needle (COOK NM-230L), a snare device (COOK DBE-snare-20 mm), hot biopsy forceps (COOK DBE-J-1), and titanium clips (Olympus HX-201LR).

Preoperative preparation

All pediatric patients underwent routine preoperative evaluations, including complete blood count, liver and renal function tests, coagulation profile, chest radiography, and electrocardiography.

Two to three days before the procedure, patients were placed on a low-residue or residue-free diet, such as congee, soft noodles, clear soup, steamed egg, and fruit juice, while foods rich in fiber, including vegetables, fruits, legumes, and coarse grains, were avoided. Fasting was initiated on the day before the procedure. Bowel preparation was performed using oral polyethylene glycol electrolyte powder (Staidson Biopharmaceuticals Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), consisting of component A and component B. Each portion of components A and B was dissolved together in 125 mL of warm water. For children weighing 10 to 20 kg, 9 portions of component A and 9 portions of component B were dissolved in 1,125 mL of warm water; for those weighing 20 to 30 kg, 12 portions of each component were dissolved in 1,500 mL of warm water; and for those weighing greater than 30 kg, 15 portions of each component were dissolved in 1,875 mL of warm water. The prepared solution was divided into three equal doses and administered orally at 8:00 AM and 4:00 PM on the day before the examination, and at 4 hours before the examination. For children with swallowing difficulties, nasogastric administration was used. Four hours before the examination, after completion of the final dose of polyethylene glycol electrolyte solution, simethicone (Sichuan Jewelland Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Zigong, China) was administered orally to reduce intestinal foam and gas bubbles at a dose of 40 mg dissolved in 10 mL of warm water.

DBE examination

DBE was performed via a transoral, transanal, or combined approach based on patients’ prior endoscopic results and follow-up information. The procedure was conducted by a standardized team including one primary operator, one assisting physician, and two specialized nurses for overtube manipulation and vital sign monitoring. All patients received combined intravenous-inhalation general anesthesia with nasotracheal intubation.

After advancing the endoscope into the jejunum (transoral route) or terminal ileum (transanal route), the double-balloon system was activated. Endoscopic progression was achieved through repeated inflation-deflation-retraction cycles. The total insertion depth was estimated at approximately 20 cm per cycle, in accordance with established DBE procedural protocols.

Resection method

The resection strategy was determined according to polyp location, morphology, and size. Polyps with a diameter of 3 mm or less were removed using biopsy forceps. Polyps measuring 4 to 9 mm in diameter were treated with cold snare polypectomy. For polyps measuring 10 to 19 mm, hot snare polypectomy or endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) was performed, with EMR preferred for sessile or broad-based lesions. Polyps with a diameter greater than 20 mm were treated with EMR. For large, sessile, or broad-based polyps that were not amenable to endoscopic management, the endoscope was withdrawn and surgical intervention was performed. In this study, the polyp resection strategy was implemented in accordance with the 2019 European Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) guidelines for PJS (5). For asymptomatic pediatric patients, only polyps with a diameter ≥15 mm were routinely resected. Smaller polyps were selectively removed under individualized conditions, including lesions with rapid interval growth (a diameter increase of ≥5 mm within 6–12 months during follow-up) and polyps with morphological changes suggestive of dysplasia. Polyps ≤3 mm were removed by forceps biopsy solely for histological diagnosis rather than therapeutic resection.

Standardized DBE surveillance and treatment strategy

Our institutional (Anhui Provincial Children’s Hospital) standardized DBE surveillance and treatment strategy for pediatric PJS is summarized as follows: (I) for all patients scheduled for combined oral-anal DBE, endoscopists attempt to achieve total enteroscopy whenever feasible, without mandatory completion in a single session; (II) a procedural time limit of 180 minutes is applied for each DBE session to reduce cumulative risks related to general anesthesia and repeated intestinal manipulation; the procedure will be terminated once the time threshold is reached; (III) for patients with incomplete small bowel visualization, further evaluation of residual small bowel segments is arranged according to age and intestinal patency: CE is preferred for children ≥4 years without suspected intestinal stenosis; computed tomography enterography (CTE) is performed in patients with suspected stenosis or poor tolerance of CE; magnetic resonance enterography (MRE) serves as an alternative modality.

Statistical analysis

Clinical data, including sex, age, enteroscopy duration, depth of endoscope insertion, number of resected polyps, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative complications, and length of hospital stay, were recorded for all pediatric patients with PJS. All patients underwent regular outpatient follow‑up after the procedure, with follow‑up intervals ranging from 2 months to 1 year and 9 months. Follow‑up assessments focused on postoperative growth and development and on changes in clinical symptoms, including abdominal pain and hematochezia. Continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non‑normally distributed data are reported as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. No regression models or subgroup comparisons were performed, as this study was primarily descriptive and aimed to report the perioperative outcomes of DBE in pediatric PJS patients. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

General data

Among the 22 children, 16 were male and 6 were female. The average age was 11.35±3.96 years (ranging from 4 to 17 years), the average height was 140.8±19.2 cm (ranging from 103 to 172 cm), and the average weight was 35.8±12.9 kg (ranging from 16.5 to 62.0 kg). The youngest patient, who was 4 years old and weighed 16.5 kg, successfully completed the DBE examination and polyp removal. Preoperative hemoglobin levels ranged from 79 to 153 g/L, with a mean value of 121.95±21.42 g/L. None of the pediatric patients required preoperative blood transfusion (Table 1).

Intraoperative outcomes of all pediatric patients

Positive findings were identified during enteroscopy in all 22 pediatric patients, and a total of 211 polyps were resected. In one patient, some polyps were located in the descending portion of the duodenum and exhibited broad bases, necessitating conversion to open laparotomy for polyp resection (Figures 1,2). The minimum polyp diameter was 0.3 cm, and the maximum diameter was 3.6 cm, with a mean diameter of 1.29±0.77 cm. The maximum intraoperative blood loss was 30 mL, while the median blood loss was 2.0 (1.0–2.0) mL. The DBE examination lasted from 35 to 312 minutes, with a median duration of 157.0 (90.75–253.5) minutes. One patient underwent open laparotomy with an operative time of 155 minutes, and three patients underwent enteroscopic rendezvous procedures in the mid-small intestine (Table 2). A total of 3 cases achieved total enteroscopy, accounting for 30% of children who underwent oral and anal combined examination. A total of 12 patients (54.5%) received preoperative CE. We defined consistent polyp burden as a polyp count difference ≤20% between CE and DBE, and the overall concordance rate reached 75% (9/12). The remaining 3 patients had polyp burden underestimated by CE. Among the 17 patients who underwent transanal DBE, successful intubation through the ileocecal valve into the terminal ileum was achieved in 16 cases (94.1%). One patient (5.9%) failed to pass the ileocecal valve due to an acutely angled valve anatomy, and subsequent CE was performed to complete small-bowel evaluation. The overall transanal intubation failure rate in our cohort was 5.9%.

Postoperative outcomes of all pediatric patients

Postoperative hospital stay ranged from 3 to 7 days in 21 pediatric patients. One patient who underwent open laparotomy had a hospital stay of 17 days, resulting in a mean postoperative hospital stay of 7.50±2.67 days for the entire cohort. None of the 17 pediatric patients developed clinical pancreatitis after undergoing oral DBE. Two cases (11.8%) presented with asymptomatic hyperamylasemia, which resolved spontaneously within 24 hours. All pediatric patients resumed normal oral intake after the procedure. No severe postoperative complications, including gastrointestinal bleeding, intestinal perforation, or severe infection, were observed. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of PJS in all cases (Figure 3).

Follow-up outcomes

During postoperative follow-up, most pediatric patients demonstrated marked improvement in abdominal pain and hematochezia. Fourteen patients exhibited improved growth and development, reflected by increases in body weight and an accelerated rate of height gain. Regular endoscopic surveillance identified polyp recurrence in 6 patients, corresponding to a recurrence rate of 27.2%. All recurrent polyps had diameters less than 1.4 cm and were successfully resected during repeat enteroscopy, with no subsequent complications reported.

Discussion

PJS is a hereditary disorder characterized by distinctive mucocutaneous pigmentation, most commonly involving the lips, buccal mucosa, and the distal regions of the fingers and toes, which frequently persists throughout life. Gastrointestinal polyps are widely distributed along the gastrointestinal tract, with the small intestine representing the most commonly affected site. These polyps are associated with a high recurrence rate even after surgical resection (6). Delayed diagnosis is associated with a substantially increased risk of complications, including intestinal obstruction, ulceration, and malignant transformation, thereby posing a significant threat to long-term health in pediatric patients. Given the current limitations in the clinical diagnosis and management of PJS, clinical data from 22 pediatric patients with PJS who underwent DBE at a single center were retrospectively analyzed in this study. Perioperative indicators were systematically evaluated, and the findings indicated that DBE demonstrated favorable safety and therapeutic effectiveness in pediatric PJS when compared with conventional open laparotomy.

The prevalence of PJS has been reported to be similar between males and females. In 2022, Matsubara et al. conducted a nationwide survey across 1,077 institutions in Japan and identified 701 patients with PJS, with males accounting for 53.5% and females for 46.5%, indicating no significant sex-related difference (7). In contrast, a higher proportion of males was observed in the present cohort (16 males vs. 6 females), which may be attributable to the larger male population base in China and the relatively small sample size. PJS is frequently associated with complications such as ulceration, intussusception, and intestinal obstruction, often presenting clinically with abdominal pain and hematochezia (8). In the present cohort, abdominal pain was observed in 14 patients (63.6%), and hematochezia was observed in 11 patients (50.0%), findings that are consistent with those reported by Wu et al. (9).

Since its initial clinical application in 2001, DBE has provided an effective approach for the diagnosis and treatment of pediatric patients with PJS. The size and distribution of polyps in pediatric patients exhibit marked heterogeneity. Findings reported by Wu et al. indicate that most polyps range from 0.2 to 2.0 cm in diameter and are predominantly located in the small intestine and colon (9). Similarly, Liu et al. reported that approximately 90% of polyps in pediatric patients are located in the jejunum and ileum, with variable sizes (10). These observations indicate that the small intestine, particularly the jejunum and ileum, represents a high-risk region for PJS-related polyps. Owing to their deep anatomical location, such lesions are often difficult to detect using conventional endoscopic techniques, underscoring the clinical value of DBE.

In the present study, 22 pediatric patients with PJS successfully underwent polypectomy using DBE, with a total of 211 polyps removed endoscopically. One additional patient required conversion to open laparotomy for resection of four polyps. The smallest polyp measured 0.3 cm in diameter, whereas the largest measured 3.6 cm, with a mean diameter of 1.29±0.77 cm. Polyps located in the stomach exhibited a smaller mean diameter than those in the small intestine and colon. Substantial interindividual variability was observed in polyp burden, with some patients requiring resection of more than 30 polyps. This variability may be closely related to differences in genetic background, age at disease onset, and disease duration. In accordance with the current ESGE guidelines, routine resection of asymptomatic small intestinal polyps smaller than 15 mm is not recommended in patients with PJS (5). In the present cohort, polyps less than 10 mm were resected only under specific clinical scenarios. First, tiny polyps adjacent to larger lesions within the same intestinal segment were removed simultaneously during en bloc resection to simplify regional lesion clearance without substantially increasing procedural risk. Second, polyps exhibiting rapid interval growth during surveillance were resected for histological evaluation to rule out dysplastic changes. Third, lesions ≤3 mm were merely removed by forceps biopsy for diagnostic sampling rather than therapeutic polypectomy. Consistent with standardized international recommendations, we will strictly adhere to the 15 mm threshold for therapeutic polypectomy in our future clinical practice.

With the increasing availability of DBE in recent years, opportunities for early detection and timely intervention of PJS-related polyps have improved, which may influence reported polyp numbers in different cohorts. Previous studies have demonstrated that early polypectomy targeting jejunal and ileal lesions can reduce the risk of intussusception in patients with PJS (11). Total enteroscopy examination in the small intestine was achieved in 3 pediatric patients (13.6%) in this study. Previous reports have indicated that the rate of complete small intestinal examination using DBE is approximately 25%, which is higher than that observed in the present cohort (12). The complex anatomy of the small intestine and the presence of pathological changes may limit procedural reach and feasibility in certain patients. Factors such as polyp location and intestinal adhesions may hinder complete small intestinal evaluation. Nevertheless, DBE remains a highly valuable clinical modality for the management of pediatric PJS.

In recent years, ischemic polypectomy (such as polypectomy assisted by pre-installed nylon ropes with snare, nylon rope ligation-assisted polypectomy) has shown potential advantages in preventing postoperative bleeding and perforation after the removal of larger polyps. This study mainly adopted traditional surgical methods (cold snare, hot snare, EMR), with extremely low rates of postoperative bleeding and perforation, verifying the safety of traditional surgical methods. However, for huge polyps with thick pedicles (≥20 mm), ischemic polypectomy techniques may provide additional safety guarantees, which is worthy of further exploration and integration in future clinical practice.

Bleeding at the polypectomy site represents the most common intraoperative complication, and prompt hemostatic management is essential for minimizing the risk of severe hemorrhage (13). Previous studies have reported complications associated with DBE, including gastrointestinal bleeding, perforation, and acute pancreatitis (14). Wang et al. reported postoperative massive gastrointestinal bleeding and perforation rates of 2.5% and 1.3%, respectively, in pediatric patients with PJS undergoing DBE (15). Yokoyama et al. reported an overall complication rate of 1.9% following DBE in children, and Funayama et al. reported lower postoperative complication rates with DBE compared with conventional surgical approaches (16,17).

In the present study, intraoperative blood loss ranged from 0 to 30 mL, with a median blood loss of 2.0 (1.0–2.0) mL. No cases of massive gastrointestinal bleeding or perforation were observed, which is consistent with previously reported findings. Conversion to open laparotomy was required in only one patient due to the presence of a broad-based polyp in the descending portion of the duodenum, highlighting the procedural limitations of DBE in select anatomical contexts.

The median procedural duration of DBE in our cohort was 157.0 (90.75–253.5) minutes, and the mean postoperative hospital stay was 7.50±2.67 days, which was relatively longer than that reported in several previous pediatric DBE studies. This discrepancy can be reasonably explained by the following factors. First, the total hospitalization duration in the current study included 2–3 days of preoperative admission for standardized bowel preparation and dietary adjustment. Second, considering the relatively high polyp burden (an average of approximately 10 polyps resected per patient), Anhui Provincial Children’s Hospital routinely maintained 48–72 hours of postoperative in-hospital observation to monitor for potential delayed bleeding. Third, one patient who required conversion to open laparotomy had a prolonged hospital stay of 17 days, which increased the overall average length of stay; the mean hospital stay decreased to 6.81±1.72 days after excluding this surgical case. Fourth, a proportion of patients resided in remote areas, and appropriately extended postoperative observation was arranged to ensure patient safety before discharge. A previous report described DBE procedures in 14 pediatric patients with PJS, with examination durations ranging from 95 to 195 minutes and a median duration of 118 minutes, which was shorter than that observed in the present study (3). Differences in operator experience and procedural complexity may account for this variation. Despite a slightly longer median examination duration, all procedures were completed safely, and no increase in severe complications was observed, supporting the safety and effectiveness of DBE in pediatric PJS.

All pediatric patients completed regular outpatient follow-up, during which clinical symptoms such as abdominal pain and hematochezia demonstrated marked improvement. Polyp recurrence was identified in 6 patients, corresponding to a recurrence rate of 27.2%.

Based on the results of this study, we summarized the application strategies of DBE in pediatric PJS as follows: (I) indications: symptomatic children, asymptomatic but with ≥15 mm polyps detected during monitoring, and those with known polyp burden requiring follow-up; (II) minimum safe weight ≥15 kg (approximately corresponding to 3–4 years old); (III) start DBE monitoring at approximately 8–10 years old; (IV) recommend DBE intervals of 1–3 years, and annual examination for those with high polyp burden; (V) combined strategy with CE: annual CE screening for asymptomatic individuals, and DBE intervention when ≥15 mm polyps are detected by CE.

Conclusions

The findings of this study indicate that long-term surveillance remains essential in pediatric PJS, even after successful polypectomy. Further multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are required to expand the clinical evidence base for DBE in pediatric PJS and to support the development of standardized diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. The present findings support the safety and efficacy of DBE and indicate its suitability for broader implementation in pediatric centers with appropriate expertise and resources.
