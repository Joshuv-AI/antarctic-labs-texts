Self‐Care Experiences and Support Needs of Community‐Dwelling Older Adults With Multimorbidity: A Qualitative Study Informed by the Caring Life‐Course Theory
Authors: Michael T. Lawless, Caitlin Wyman, Maria Alejandra Pinero de Plaza, Heather Block, Stacey George, Rachel Ambagtsheer, Malcolm Battersby, Alison Kitson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo explore how community‐dwelling older adults with multimorbidity experience, enact and navigate daily self‐care using the Caring Life‐Course Theory to identify opportunities for strengthening self‐care and self‐management support.DesignQualitative descriptive study.MethodsSemi‐structured interviews were conducted with community‐dwelling older adults aged ≥ 50 years living with two or more chronic conditions across three Australian states and territories. Data were analysed inductively and deductively using qualitative content analysis. Inductive coding was followed by theory‐informed analysis to interpret self‐care capability, capacity, care networks and system supports.ResultsEighteen participants (mean age = 70.9 years) described self‐care as an adaptive, experience‐based process influenced by lived experience, health transitions, informal care networks and system responsiveness. Participants generally demonstrated agency and resourcefulness in managing complex and changing care needs, often learning through trial and error. Psychosocial and relational needs were frequently under‐recognised in healthcare encounters, requiring individuals and informal carers to compensate for fragmented, inconsistent support. Self‐care capability and capacity were shaped by experiential learning, health and self‐care literacy and access to informal and online resources, particularly where formal education was limited or unavailable.ConclusionSelf‐care for older adults with multimorbidity is shaped by dynamic interactions between personal capability, relational support and system‐level factors across the life‐course. The Caring Life‐Course Theory provides a comprehensive approach for understanding these interdependencies and identifying opportunities for intervention.Implications for the Profession and/or Patient CareFindings highlight the need to strengthen coordinated, person‐centred and relationally grounded approaches to self‐care and self‐management in primary and community care, including improved access to evidence‐informed resources and anticipatory support.Reporting MethodThis study is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies.Patient or Public ContributionParticipants contributed through semi‐structured interviews and provided feedback on study findings.Impact What problem did the study address? Community‐dwelling older adults living with multimorbidity are expected to engage in self‐care, yet little is known about how they experience, enact and sustain daily self‐care, particularly when psychosocial and relational needs are inconsistently recognised within health and social care systems.What were the main findings? Self‐care was characterised as an adaptive, experiential process shaped by life experience, informal support networks and system responsiveness. Participants frequently compensated for under‐recognised psychosocial and relational needs through informal and online supports and resources.Where and on whom will the research have an impact? Findings can inform clinicians, service providers and policymakers in primary, community and aged care settings by identifying opportunities to strengthen coordinated, person‐centred and relational self‐care and self‐management support for older people with multimorbidity.

Introduction

Chronic conditions are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally, with their impact particularly pronounced among older adults, who now represent the fastest‐growing demographic group (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare [AIHW] 2024a). Multimorbidity, defined as the co‐existence of two or more chronic health conditions, is highly prevalent among older adults and increases with age. Recent systematic reviews indicate that multimorbidity affects more than half of adults aged over 60 years globally, with substantial regional variation (Chowdhury et al. 2023). Multimorbidity is associated with reduced health‐related quality of life (Gebreyohannes et al. 2023), functional decline (Aubert et al. 2022), frailty (Vetrano et al. 2019), polypharmacy (Nicholson et al. 2024) and premature mortality (Skou et al. 2022). It also contributes to unplanned hospitalisations, prolonged length of stay and higher healthcare costs (Soley‐Bori et al. 2020). Among community‐dwelling older adults, these challenges are often exacerbated by fragmented and poorly coordinated care across providers and settings, as well as by the cumulative burden of managing multiple conditions and treatments (Wu et al. 2023). Socioeconomic disadvantage and adverse life‐course exposures further affect the onset, progression and complexity of multimorbidity, reinforcing its significance as a growing challenge across primary care, community care and aged care systems (Jungo et al. 2022).

Self‐care and self‐management are critical to living well with chronic conditions and multimorbidity yet represent related but distinct concepts (Matarese et al. 2018). The World Health Organization (WHO 2022) defines self‐care as ‘the ability of individuals, families, and communities to promote health, prevent disease, maintain health and cope with illness and disability with or without the support of a health worker’. The Middle‐Range Theory of Self‐Care of Chronic Illness (Riegel et al. 2025) describes three interconnected processes: maintaining health‐promoting routines (self‐care maintenance); monitoring for signs and symptoms (self‐care monitoring); and managing symptoms when they occur (self‐care management). Self‐management is defined as ‘the intrinsically controlled ability of an active, responsible, informed and autonomous individual to live with the medical, role and emotional consequences of his chronic condition(s) in partnership with [their] social network and the healthcare provider(s)’ (Van de Velde et al. 2019, 10). In practice, overlap exists between the two concepts, with shared domains across models and measurement approaches. In this study, self‐care is used as the broader concept encompassing the everyday behavioural, cognitive and emotional responses individuals enact to meet care needs, while self‐management reflects their condition‐specific application within healthcare and formal support contexts (Kitson and Lawless 2025).

Established self‐care and self‐management support models, including the Chronic Care Model (Wagner et al. 2001), the Chronic Disease Self‐Management Program (Lorig et al. 2001) and the Flinders Chronic Condition Management Program (Battersby et al. 2015), incorporate patient education, goal setting, collaborative care planning and mobilisation of social supports. Across chronic conditions, such interventions typically produce modest, short‐term improvements in outcomes such as health‐related quality of life, self‐efficacy and self‐management behaviours (Lee et al. 2022). Among community‐dwelling older adults with multimorbidity, emerging evidence suggests similar benefits, including improved physical functioning and mental health (Lawless et al. 2025). However, long‐term improvements remain inconsistent and interventions are often less effective for socioeconomically disadvantaged groups who face barriers such as health literacy, social isolation, financial constraints, geographical distance, discrimination and lower trust or engagement with healthcare providers (Okpako et al. 2023). These persistent gaps highlight the need for deeper qualitative insight into how older adults understand, enact and sustain self‐care in everyday life and what forms of support they perceive as meaningful and accessible.

Qualitative studies and systematic reviews have documented that older adults with multimorbidity navigate interconnected physical, psychological, social and practical challenges affecting their capability and capacity to perform self‐care (e.g., Garnett et al. 2018; Tian et al. 2024). Physically, reduced functional ability, fluctuating symptoms and fragmented service pathways often impede the management of multiple interacting conditions and treatments (Mellum et al. 2019; Ploeg et al. 2019). Psychologically, older adults often report emotional distress and symptom‐related anxiety so draw on cognitive and emotional coping strategies to maintain control and identity (Meranius and Engstrom 2015). Socially, support from family, peers and healthcare providers is pivotal, though unevenly accessible and not routinely integrated into care planning (Mellum et al. 2019). Practically, treatment burden, financial pressures and complex medication regimens present ongoing challenges, often requiring trade‐offs between competing health and social priorities (Ploeg et al. 2019).

Although these studies provide relevant insights, many focus on specific aspects, such as symptom burden, decision‐making, treatment workload or recruit narrowly defined clinical or demographic groups. There remains limited understanding of how older adults make sense of, prioritise and perform self‐care across the multiple interacting demands of multimorbidity. In Australia, qualitative research in this area remains limited. Hardman et al. (2021), for example, found that among low‐income rural adults aged 47–72 years, multimorbidity increased treatment burden, disrupted capacity and required ongoing negotiation of condition interactions with minimal clinical guidance. Despite increasing policy attention, including the National Strategic Framework for Chronic Conditions (Australian Government Department of Health, Disability and Ageing 2026), relatively little is known about how community‐dwelling older Australians experience and navigate self‐care across primary, community and aged care sectors. Addressing this gap is timely, given Australia's evolving health and aged‐care reform agendas which, comparable to international efforts to strengthen primary health care and care integration, aim to reduce fragmentation and provide more person‐centred, coordinated support for people with complex care needs.

Against this backdrop, the Caring Life‐Course Theory (CLCT) provides a comprehensive theoretical approach for examining the self‐care and support needs (Kitson et al. 2022). Contemporary health and aged‐care policies assume that community‐dwelling older adults will experience better outcomes when they are supported to engage in self‐care and are embedded within networks that enable it. The CLCT makes it possible to interrogate these assumptions by conceptualising how care needs arise and are met (or unmet) through self‐care and care from others, and how these processes shift across transitions and trajectories across time. Building on earlier conceptual work, particularly the Fundamentals of Care Framework (Feo et al. 2018), the CLCT comprises 14 interrelated constructs describing how care‐related experiences, social relationships and broader system factors interact dynamically to influence the provision and receipt of care.

Within the CLCT self‐care is conceptualised as a set of dynamic, adaptive behavioural processes enacted to meet care needs. These responses are activated by personal resources and supported, often unevenly, by informal and formal care networks, embedded within relational, structural and temporal contexts (Lawless et al. 2021). The CLCT distinguishes between self‐care capability, referring to the knowledge, skills, experience, reflection, decision‐making and motivation required for self‐care and self‐care capacity, referring to the total amount of self‐directed care that can be enacted given available personal, relational and structural resources. The theory posits that the degree of alignment between an individual's care needs and the care that is available and enacted, whether self‐directed or supported through relationships and systems, shapes their health status, care experience and quality of life. By situating self‐care within interdependent micro (individual), meso (relational) and macro (structural) contexts and across the life‐course, the CLCT provides a framework for understanding not only how older adults with multimorbidity adapt to changing care needs and circumstances, but also where practical opportunities exist for strengthening support within health and social care systems (Lawless et al. 2026).

Aim and Objectives

The aim of this study was to explore the experiences of Australian community‐dwelling older adults with multimorbidity, focusing on their self‐care experiences, behaviours and supports they draw on in daily life, using the CLCT. The specific research objectives were to: (1) explore the physical, psychosocial and relational care needs and priorities of older adults with multimorbidity and whether and how they are met; (2) examine the strategies, resources and support networks (formal and informal) that shape their self‐care capability and capacity; and (3) identify priorities and opportunities for strengthening self‐care support across primary, community and home‐based aged care settings informed by participants' experiences.

Methods

Design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive design using semi‐structured interviews. A qualitative descriptive approach was considered appropriate for an in‐depth exploration of participants' experiences, allowing findings to remain close to participants' own language while providing rich, contextualised descriptions of the phenomena under investigation (Sandelowski 2010). This approach is characterised by methodological flexibility, enabling the application of both inductive and deductive analytical strategies aligned with the study aims while remaining grounded in empirical data. The study is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ) checklist (Tong et al. 2007; File S1).

Study Context

This study was conducted in Australia, where support for community‐dwelling older adults with chronic conditions involves a mix of primary care, community services and home‐based aged care. Primary care, delivered largely by general practitioners (GPs), nurses and allied health professionals, provides preventive care, chronic disease management and supports shared decision‐making. Recent reforms to the Medicare Benefits Schedule (MBS), effective from 2025, replaced prior GP Management Plans and Team Care Arrangements with a streamlined GP Chronic Condition Management Plan (Australian Government Department of Health, Disability and Ageing 2025). National aged‐care reforms introduced the new Support at Home programme in 2025, replacing existing Home Care Packages to improve access to coordinated, in‐home care services for older Australians. These reforms include a renewed restorative care pathway and a redesigned assessment process, including the use of an Integrated Assessment Tool aimed at delivering more consistent and timely evaluation and support. These reforms highlight the evolving structure of care provision in Australia and provide the context for exploring how older adults with multimorbidity navigate self‐care and support across primary, community and aged‐care sectors.

Setting and Participants

Participants were recruited from community settings across three Australian states and territories. Eligible participants were community‐dwelling adults aged over 50 years with at least two diagnosed chronic conditions requiring long‐term management, as diagnosed by a health professional. Although in Australia an ‘older adult’ is typically defined as someone aged over 65 years (AIHW 2024b), a lower threshold of 50 years was chosen to capture individuals experiencing multimorbidity earlier in the life‐course and to identify opportunities for proactive intervention. This approach also reflects variation across Australian community‐dwelling populations, particularly for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, for whom the burden of chronic conditions and earlier onset of age‐related health challenges is well documented. Community‐dwelling was defined as living in one's own home or private residence within the community, rather than an institutional setting such as a long‐term care facility or nursing home. A broad range of chronic conditions, including cardiovascular, respiratory, endocrine, musculoskeletal, neurological, renal, mental health, gastrointestinal, cancer, autoimmune and chronic pain syndromes, were eligible due to the exploratory nature of the study and focus on experiences of living with multiple chronic conditions. Excluded were individuals aged under 50 years, those with fewer than two chronic conditions or in inpatient, residential aged care or palliative care settings (File S2).

A combination of convenience and purposive sampling was used to include participants who met specific eligibility criteria while enabling recruitment efficiency, primarily through accessible community‐based networks. Participants were recruited through various community groups and clubs located across Australia, including senior citizens' associations, lifelong learning groups (e.g., the University of the Third Age [U3A]), social and activity clubs, Probus groups and organisations catering to specific populations, such as culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) communities, men's sheds and pensioner associations. Flyers were distributed in public libraries, community halls, churches and other public venues to expand reach. In total, 129 groups were contacted, of which seven responded and facilitated dissemination of study information to their members. Membership size and levels of engagement varied across groups, influencing the reach of study information. Additional recruitment strategies included social media advertisements posted on Facebook and Twitter and follow‐up contact with eligible participants from previous studies conducted by members of the research team involving community‐dwelling older adults with chronic conditions, who had consented to being approached for future research. All potential participants were screened by one author (C.W.) via telephone to confirm eligibility according to the criteria described above.

Data Collection

Data were collected through semi‐structured interviews conducted by the second author (C.W.; a female accredited practising dietician and research assistant with previous experience in qualitative research and no pre‐existing relationships with participants). Interviews were conducted either in‐person, by telephone or through videoconference via Microsoft Teams (Microsoft Corporation, Remond, WA, USA), depending on participants' preferences, between September and December 2024. Participants provided written or electronic informed consent via email prior to participation and received a $50 AUD voucher in recognition of their time. All participants were supplied with a Participant Information and Consent Form and were given the opportunity to ask questions before providing consent. Demographic information was collected using either a paper‐based questionnaire provided at the time of the interview or an electronic survey administered through Jotform (Jotform Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA). Participants were informed that they could decline to answer any demographic question. Data were collected via a secure, password‐protected account; no identifying information was stored on the survey platform to maintain participant confidentiality. Repeat interviews were not conducted.

An interview guide (Box 1) was developed based on the study objectives, relevant literature and sensitising concepts drawn from the CLCT. The guide was intentionally broad and flexible, allowing participants to describe their experiences while ensuring coverage of topics relevant to self‐care capability, capacity, transitions, trajectories and networks. All interviews were audio‐ and/or video‐recorded via Microsoft Teams with participants' consent and transcribed verbatim for analysis. Field notes were taken immediately after each interview to document contextual details and initial researcher impressions. Sample size was guided by the concept of information power rather than ‘saturation’, with analytic sufficiency determined by the study aims, the use of an established theoretical framework and the recurrence of concepts and patterns across the dataset (Malterud et al. 2016).

Data Analysis

Interview transcripts were checked for accuracy by two authors (M.T.L. and C.W.), anonymised and managed in NVivo (version 14; QSR International) and Microsoft Excel. A hybrid, three‐phase qualitative content analysis approach was employed, combining inductive and deductive strategies involving data familiarisation, systematically generating codes and defining and naming themes (Elo and Kyngäs 2008). During the familiarisation phase, three experienced qualitative researchers (M.T.L., C.W., H.B.) independently reviewed the interview recordings and re‐read transcripts to gain an in‐depth understanding of the data. Each noted emerging patterns, reflections and observations before meeting to discuss initial impressions. In the coding phase, the first author (M.T.L.) conducted line‐by‐line inductive coding to identify sections of text capturing units of meaning relevant to the research aims. Coding focused both on explicit (semantic) and underlying (latent) meanings, resulting in an initial data‐driven framework comprising 86 codes. This framework was then refined through deductive, theory‐informed coding using relevant CLCT constructs to organise and confirm patterns grounded in participants' narratives. The CLCT was applied after inductive coding to facilitate conceptual coherence and comparison with previous studies, while still allowing the generation of data‐driven codes. Applying the CLCT secondarily enabled theory validation and testing in terms of exploring the applicability of the CLCT in understanding self‐care experiences of community‐dwelling older adults with multimorbidity.

In the theme development phase, three authors (M.T.L., C.W., M.A.P.d.P.) independently grouped related codes into potential themes and sub‐themes, followed by collaborative discussions to compare interpretations and refine categorisations. The wider research team reviewed the preliminary themes iteratively to ensure they were coherent, conceptually distinct and supported by the data. Themes were checked against coded extracts and the entire dataset to confirm their scope and meaning. Final themes were defined and named to reflect their central concept and ensure descriptive clarity. The final step involved the production of this manuscript, in which themes were organised into a coherent narrative. Representative verbatim quotations were selected to illustrate key findings and provide transparency. Transcripts were labelled with alphanumeric identifiers (e.g., P01 denotes the first participant) to preserve participant anonymity.

Rigour and Trustworthiness

To ensure the trustworthiness of the findings, established criteria for credibility, confirmability, dependability and transferability were applied (Lincoln and Guba 1986). Credibility was established through extended engagement with the data, independent coding by multiple researchers and regular team discussions to compare and refine interpretations. The analytic process was validated by senior researchers with expertise in health and social sciences scholarship who were not directly involved in data collection, providing an additional layer of reflexive oversight. Confirmability and dependability were supported by maintaining a detailed audit trail documenting methodological and analytical decisions, revisions to the coding framework and theme development. Direct participant quotations are presented in the results, labelled with unique participant identifiers to demonstrate transparency and grounding in the data. Participants were also given the opportunity to review their transcript following data collection, clarify comments and provide feedback on a summary of the findings. Transferability was addressed by providing detailed descriptions of participants' sociodemographic characteristics, the research context and the analytical procedures to enable readers to assess the applicability of the findings to other settings.

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with national ethical guidelines and approved by the Flinders University Human Research Ethics Committee (Project No. HEG7436). Principles of anonymity, informed consent and the right to withdraw were upheld throughout all stages of the research. Participants provided written or electronic consent before participation and were informed that their involvement was voluntary. Those who consented to re‐contact were offered the opportunity to review a copy of their interview transcript and clarify or amend comments prior to data analysis and reporting.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 18 participants took part in the study (mean age = 70.9 years, SD = 9.4 years). Most participants were female (n = 13, 72%) and were recruited primarily through community‐based organisations catering to older adults, including volunteer‐led educational organisations and social groups for retired or semi‐retired individuals, across three Australian states and territories: Australian Capital Territory (n = 9, 50%); South Australia (n = 8, 44%); and Northern Territory (n = 1, 6%). Participants self‐reported between two and nine chronic conditions (mean = 4.0, SD = 2.1); these diagnoses were not verified by a healthcare provider. The most frequently reported chronic condition groups included: endocrine or metabolic conditions (n = 10); musculoskeletal conditions (n = 7); cancers (n = 6); cardiovascular conditions (n = 6); mental health conditions (n = 4); and respiratory conditions (n = 4). Chronic condition categories were based on the AIHW (2021) national classification of chronic conditions to align with current policy and epidemiological frameworks used by the Australian Government to guide chronic disease prevention and management. A summary of participant characteristics is presented in Table 1. In total, 13 h and 43 min of interview recordings were collected (range: 23–61 min; mean length: 42 min), resulting in 347 pages of verbatim transcripts for analysis.

Qualitative Results

Three themes were developed from the inductive and deductive content analysis: (1) self‐care is learned through experience and shaped by life events and personal history; (2) psychosocial and relational needs are often unmet in healthcare encounters and sustained through informal networks; and (3) supporting and sustaining self‐care necessitates integrated and responsive systems. Together, these themes describe an interdependent process in which self‐care capability and capacity evolve through lived experience and are both enabled and constrained by individual, relational and structural factors. Self‐Care Is Learned Through Experience and Shaped by Life Events and Personal History

Participants described how self‐care processes developed over time through lived experience and informal learning rather than through education provided in healthcare settings. These dynamic processes were influenced by personal health and care histories, transitions such as diagnosis or hospitalisation and an ongoing process of trial and error. Such processes reflected adaptive responses to adversity that might be understood as manifestations of self‐care capability or resilience emerging through experience. Self‐care was characterised as adaptive and individualised, shaped by each participant's care history and circumstances rather than through formal or structured instruction. This theme comprised three sub‐themes: ‘self‐awareness and experimentation as foundations of self‐care’; ‘health events and transitions as care‐shaping milestones’; and ‘personal histories and caregiving roles inform present‐day self‐care processes’.

Self‐Awareness and Experimentation as Foundations of Self‐Care

Participants described learning how to manage their chronic conditions and developing self‐care strategies through experiential, trial‐and‐error processes, often in the absence of structured guidance or formal support from healthcare providers. Developing personally relevant and effective self‐care strategies was framed as an iterative and ongoing process, guided by symptom recognition, monitoring and reflective adaptation over time. Initiating behavioural adjustments often involved noticing and responding to physical cues. As one participant explained, ‘if I'm out in the garden…as I feel my face going red, I stop. Because otherwise it still takes a fair while to come back to normal…I know that I can feel that I've obviously done too much’ (P13). Others described learning to anticipate sudden or temporary increases in symptom severity and modifying their routines accordingly (e.g., ‘I do know this sort of precursors to that…so that will probably make me cancel a couple of meetings and stay home for a couple of days and rest’, P1). Experimentation with lifestyle adjustments was common, particularly in relation to diet, activity levels and medication routines. One participant said, ‘I have a fairly strict Mediterranean diet, and I don't eat meat, and I don't eat anything with gluten in it or dairy’ (P15); another reflected on managing their energy and work commitments: ‘I am busy. And sometimes if I'm not feeling one hundred percent, I do think, oh, maybe I should stop doing [volunteer work]’ (P01). These accounts positioned self‐care as a situated and improvisational process shaped by individuals' ability to notice, interpret and respond effectively to care needs over time.

Health Events and Transitions as Care‐Shaping Milestones

Major health‐related and non‐health‐related transitions, including hospitalisations, surgeries, diagnoses and retirement, were identified as pivotal moments that reshaped participants' self‐care orientation and routines. The aftermath of these events prompted not only new self‐care behaviours but also shifts in attitudes and expectations. One participant reflected, ‘I know that I'm not going to get any better. I have a horrible feeling my lungs might get worse, which will be the thing that will let me down’ (P01). These events were described as turning points often associated with reassessments of daily activities and the need to adapt routines to changing capacities (e.g., ‘I used to swim a lot as a young person… now I do aqua aerobics… less impactful on your joints’; P05). Participants characterised their experiences of living with multimorbidity not merely as a sum of single, isolated conditions but as layered, cumulative and dynamic: ‘My health condition is, well, it's all over the place… whatever is worse will come up in my face… the one that was before become a backburner [sic], and then you come up with a new one’ (P06). For some participants, such transitions also marked emotional and existential adjustments as they came to terms with new limitations and the need to reorient their sense of control: ‘You're going to wake up every single day and feel like you're going to die? You won't. But your job now is to go home and learn how to live with that.’ (P11). Across accounts, these transitions functioned as key inflection points and biographical anchors that prompted participants to re‐evaluate priorities, redefine expectations and integrate self‐care more deliberately into everyday life.

Personal Histories and Caregiving Roles Inform Present‐Day Self‐Care

Across participants' accounts, self‐care knowledge and behaviours were described as learned through both lived experience from managing their own long‐term health conditions and providing care to others, rather than acquired through formal instruction and support. Participants traced their current approaches to earlier experiences of caring for family members and navigating care and support provision within formal and informal networks. One participant explained, ‘the other thing that's impacted on both our lives is I've got a son with disability as well…I've got to look after my health because I've got to support [my family]’ (P14). For some, family histories of chronic illness served as both emotional reference points and motivators for engaging in proactive behaviours: ‘my mother's side of the family has historically had hypertension and high cholesterol…so I take my health fairly seriously’ (P17). Another said, ‘my dad and all three of his brothers had [prostate cancer] …they tell me that that's a bit of a, you know, indicator sort of thing…so I got [my doctor] to send me to a urologist’ (P18). Professional and volunteer roles also reinforced participants' self‐care knowledge and commitment to learning about and utilising evidence‐informed approaches (e.g., ‘I had qualifications in biomedical science…I understand how the heart works’; P04). These narratives positioned self‐care capability as an accumulation of lived experience and knowledge—less of a discrete set of behaviours than an extension of lifelong care responsibilities sustained by lessons learned through personal, familial and professional roles.

Psychosocial and Relational Needs Are Often Unmet in Healthcare Encounters and Sustained Through Informal Networks

Participants described how the emotional consequences of living with multiple chronic conditions were frequently minimised or overlooked in formal healthcare interactions. While physical management and behavioural strategies were adequately supported, psychosocial and relational needs were often left unaddressed, being mostly navigated privately or within informal networks rather than through clinical encounters. This theme comprised three sub‐themes: ‘hidden emotional distress and limited psychosocial recognition in healthcare settings’; ‘informal relationships as primary sources of emotional support’; and ‘fragility and inconsistency of relational care’.

Hidden Emotional Distress and Limited Psychosocial Recognition in Healthcare Settings

Emotional distress often accompanied participants' experiences but was rarely disclosed or acknowledged within healthcare interactions. One participant living with fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue and Behçet's disease said, ‘I did fall into a very deep depression…when the doctor said you need to go home and live with [my health conditions], but nobody gave me any tools…I actually hit the alcohol and combined the Panadeine…and I almost completely destroyed my liver’ (P11). The same participant reflected on the emotional toll of chronic pain: ‘I've been angry, I've been mortified, I've been gas lit, I've been grateful…the overarching in chronic pain is the hopelessness’ (P11). Others spoke of enduring fatigue or distress quietly, maintaining stoicism in the absence of emotional support: ‘Sometimes too you get the end of the day, and I just go yeah tomorrow's another day…but it's just sort of muddling on’; (P02). Another explained, ‘you keep it to yourself because you don't want to bother anyone’ (P10). This hidden emotional burden was compounded by the lack of psychosocial or biographical inquiry within clinical encounters (e.g., ‘I had to wait hours just for a doctor to come in and say, oh, you're all right now. You can go home. You know, three hours sitting around’; P10). Such experiences reinforced the perception that psychosocial and relational needs were peripheral to physical care. As one participant noted, ‘just to have a human being recognise [my experience] …that relief can make you push harder, and that's very lacking in a medical kind of environment’ (P11). These narratives portray emotional distress as both persistent and unseen, managed through personal resilience or avoidance rather than addressed through empathetic care in healthcare settings.

Informal Relationships as Primary Sources of Emotional Support

Where formal services did not meet participants' psychosocial and relational needs, they relied on informal support networks comprising family, friends and peers with similar diagnoses and experiences. These networks provided mutual understanding, reassurance and companionship, meeting needs that healthcare systems were unable to address. One participant described, ‘when I really needed someone they were there and that helped me a lot…chronic health conditions I think…you need to be sort of seen or I need to feel heard’ (P02). Family and close friends were central to participants' wellbeing: ‘We've got other family friends around the place that we can call on if, you know, if need be’ (P18). Peer and community‐based groups, including online communities, were also identified as key sources of emotional and practical support (e.g., ‘So I just cannot impress…strongly enough the benefit of having a support group. And thanks to Facebook, you can do it’; P07). Others highlighted the sense of belonging these spaces created: ‘I belong to a lot of meetup groups… whatever your interests, there'll be a group that caters for you’ (P09). Peer communities, both in‐person and online, were regarded as spaces in which emotions could be expressed safely, enabling understanding unavailable elsewhere. Faith‐based and recovery communities similarly provided empathy and shared understanding: ‘I'm in other groups. I'm an [alcoholics anonymous] member…and [a community‐based mental health program with a peer‐support model] …to try and help myself’ (P11). In these accounts, emotional care within these relationships was self‐organised and reciprocal. These examples illustrated how informal networks compensated for the absence of structured support and fostered a shared responsibility and continuity of care outside formal systems.

Fragility and Inconsistency of Relational Care

Despite the value participants placed on informal supports, the availability and stability of these networks varied. Some participants described reliable support networks that reinforced their confidence and independence; others experienced distance, loss or limited access to help when it was needed. One participant shared, ‘My son and daughter‐in‐law, unfortunately, live in [regional town] …I don't see them as much as I'd like to because they're so far away’ (P01). Several participants lived alone or felt socially isolated: ‘I don't have sort of immediate family or anything so yeah I am sort of pretty much on my own in terms of family…I really only have one or two really good friends’ (P02); ‘I have no close friends that live very near me. My neighbours are all good, but they've all got young children’ (P15). Geographical and contextual factors, including rurality, further constrained opportunities for connection and support: ‘Well, it's very hard in the country because they don't have a lot of help there, honest’ (P10). Others spoke of losing partners or long‐term friends, and the sense of loneliness that ensued: ‘Since my wife passed, it's just quiet. You don't realise how much that company matters until it's gone’ (P07). Across these accounts, relational care was portrayed as emotionally vital yet fragile, dependent on proximity, personal initiative and the willingness of others to reciprocate. These experiences highlighted the need for formal systems to recognise, strengthen and supplement these informal social resources rather than assume their structure or stability.

Supporting and Sustaining Self‐Care Necessitates Integrated and Responsive Systems

Participants' ability to manage multiple chronic conditions depended not only on individual and condition‐related factors but also on how effectively health and social care systems recognised and enabled their efforts and support provided through informal networks. They emphasised the importance of accessible information, continuity and quality of relationships with healthcare providers and anticipatory planning that accommodated changing care needs over time. This theme comprised three sub‐themes: ‘health literacy and system navigation shape self‐care capability and capacity’; ‘planning ahead requires proactive efforts and system support’; and ‘unmet needs for coordinated and anticipatory care’.

Health Literacy and System Navigation Shape Self‐Care Capability and Capacity

Participants described learning about their conditions and how to manage them through a combination of self‐directed education and guidance from healthcare professionals. One participant explained, ‘Once my treating physicians find out what I do, they share more with me’ (P01). Others described a process of gradual learning shaped by interactions with healthcare professionals and reflection on personal practices: ‘I injured my hip two years ago… I found it very difficult to manage, to look after myself at home… but after seeing specialists and physios, I got a bit of ideas how to look after myself at home’ (P16). Participants complemented professional advice with independent information‐seeking: ‘I read a lot, like I'm on Twitter… I follow a lot of haematologists…I'm actually quite knowledgeable now’ (P07). Another remarked, ‘Just Google it. Don't worry about it. You can't remember the word. Just Google it’ (P10). However, accessing reliable information was not always straightforward: ‘I'm very grateful for the Internet… I do know you don't just take it as gospel’ (P02). Symptom recognition and medication management were central to this learning process (e.g., ‘I take three of those tablets a day. And I've been doing that for 10 years or more’; P18). Regular contact with professionals provided reassurance but varied in quality and depth (e.g., ‘I rely on his judgement on that [BP monitoring]…GP is first port of call’; P03; ‘They don't ask you how you're coping, it's all about the blood tests and medication’; P09). Across accounts, health literacy emerged as both a personal resource and a product of the system. The capacity to sustain self‐care hinged on clear information, supportive relationships and accessible guidance form healthcare professionals.

Planning Ahead Requires Proactive Efforts and System Support

Participants recognised the importance of planning ahead for their future health and living needs yet described this as a largely self‐driven process requiring persistence, adaptability and careful financial management. Most learned to anticipate and plan for changes in health status and care needs through personal experience and insight rather than system prompts or structured guidance. Other participants focused on adjusting the home environment and daily habits that made self‐care sustainable: ‘the aged care nurse and the occupational therapist come in and [my wife] got diagnosed as being eligible for like anti‐slip coatings on the floor in the shower…grab rails in the shower and the toilet sort of thing’ (P18). These accounts underscored the importance of home‐based aged care services in supporting ageing in place. As one participant explained, ‘I've only got a level two Home Care Package…everybody's saying that to go up a level you wait for about eight months…I'm not getting the help that I really need’ (P01). Financial planning was central to these decisions: ‘We'd cut back on other things like restaurants rather than give up health insurance’ (P03). Another participant reflected, ‘My Aged Care give you $1,000… we had to pay about probably 300 bucks on top…’ (P18). However, not everyone could afford to plan proactively, and financial constraints limited their ability to make desired changes or access services: ‘I'm a pensioner…I'd be struggling to afford [seeing a specialist]’ (P02). Participants emphasised the need for improved systemic support. Planning ahead required both individual initiative and accessible structures, including affordable services, anticipatory guidance and systems that recognised changing needs over time.

Inadequately Coordinated and Anticipatory Care

Participants frequently described how fragmented and reactive systems made it difficult to manage their care holistically. They expressed frustrations about the lack of coordination between services and the burden of navigating multiple appointments: ‘I'd love to have one place where I can see them all on the same day’ (P01). Others described feeling overlooked within systems seemingly focused more on tasks than people: ‘I don't feel like I'm being seen as an individual… just a set of conditions’ (P02). Long waiting times and limited follow‐up were recurring concerns: ‘That's annoying… I'm not getting the help that I really need’ (P01). Several reflected that these issues had persisted for years: ‘Twenty years down the track…I would say exactly the same thing…the system was not responsive enough’ (P09). Financial pressures further compounded these challenges: ‘Even if you can get in to see a psychiatrist…the fees are unbelievable’ (P02); ‘I hate having to pay the ophthalmologist. It's always hundreds of dollars and that hurts’ (P07). For many, these costs made specialist or preventive care difficult to sustain, particularly for those on fixed or limited incomes. Participants also recognised the broader systemic pressures contributing to these shortfalls (e.g., ‘There are just so many demands on the healthcare system… shortfalls everywhere’; P02). Participants called for a stronger focus on prevention and forward planning rather than crisis response (e.g., ‘Thinking about… not just mopping up after emergencies’; P12). Across accounts, participants conveyed a sense that the current system reacts to problems rather anticipate them. They envisioned more integrated, accessible and proactive care models and systems capable of connecting services, reducing duplication and supporting long‐term self‐management sustainably.

Discussion

This qualitative study explored how community‐dwelling older Australians with multimorbidity understand, enact and navigate self‐care across physical, psychosocial and relational domains. Participants described self‐care as largely learned and sustained through experience, personal resilience and informal support networks rather than through structured or ongoing guidance from healthcare providers, even among participants living in relatively socioeconomically advantaged contexts. Psychosocial and relational needs were frequently overlooked or insufficiently addressed in healthcare encounters, requiring participants to navigate fragmented and, at times, unresponsive systems. Despite these challenges, many participants spoke of utilising adaptable self‐care strategies, drawing on personal and social resources to maintain agency where formal supports were limited or absent. These findings highlight persistent gaps between policy aspirations and the lived experiences of ageing with multiple chronic conditions, in the context of population ageing and ongoing primary and aged care reforms in Australia and internationally. Although these findings align with broader literature on chronic illness self‐care, participants in this study additionally described challenges that reflect the complexity of multimorbidity, including the cumulative burden of managing multiple conditions and treatments while navigating fragmented care systems.

Interpretation of Findings and Comparison With Literature

Participants' accounts highlighted adaptive responses to adversity and cumulative health challenges, which can be understood as expressions of adaptive self‐care capability or resilience embedded within everyday health practices. These adaptive responses, evident in participants' trial‐and‐error learning, problem solving and adjustment to health transitions, align with the intrapersonal resources described in the Middle‐Range Theory of Self‐Care of Chronic Illness (Riegel et al. 2025) and in CLCT‐informed research on self‐care and self‐management (Lawless et al. 2021). Consistent with chronic illness literature, resilience in this study appeared both as an enabler and an outcome of effective self‐care, reflecting a bi‐directional relationship in which adaptive self‐care behaviours seemed to reinforce self‐efficacy, coping and emotional wellbeing, while ongoing challenges without adequate support could erode these resources and capacities (Jin et al. 2023). Resilience was not described as a fixed personal trait but as emerging and evolving through lived experience, shaped by prior adversity, accumulated knowledge and ongoing adjustment to changing care needs. This interpretation aligns with literature conceptualising resilience as a dynamic cognitive and behavioural process involving appraisal, emotional regulation and problem‐solving across the life‐course (van Rijn et al. 2023).

Participants recounted developing what can be understood as self‐care literacy largely in the absence of structured or ongoing education from healthcare professionals. Many participants described actively seeking information through online and peer‐based resources, including using search engines (e.g., Google) and social media communities (e.g., Facebook groups). These accounts reflect the influence of digital information environments in shaping how people access and interpret health information. Although online resources and peer communities can expand opportunities for information seeking and self‐care support, issues of accessibility, usability, digital literacy and trust may influence how older adults engage with those platforms with potential implications for equity in access to reliable health information. Online resources supported not only knowledge acquisition but also the development of practical self‐care skills. Consistent with prior research, self‐care literacy appeared to develop as participants recognised patterns, reflected on symptoms, social interactions and various information sources and refined strategies over time, resulting in tacit, context‐specific understanding alongside formal learning where available (Magi et al. 2026). Participants were generally aware of the variable reliability of online information, describing exercising judgement in evaluating sources and triangulating information with professional advice or similar peer experiences. This aligns with evidence that lower health literacy constrains self‐care and self‐management effectiveness across domains including self‐efficacy, treatment adherence, symptom interpretation, communication with providers (van der Gaag et al. 2022). Effective self‐care requires not only comprehension but also the capability and capacity to translate knowledge into behaviour, reflecting the distinction between ‘knowledge as cognition’ and ‘knowledge as action’ (Rademakers and Heijmans 2018). Within the CLCT, health literacy can be understood both as a personal resource shaping (self‐)care capability at the micro (individual) level and a relational and structural construct influenced by interactions with care networks and systems at meso (relational) and macro (structural) levels (Lawless et al. 2026).

Implications for Practice and Policy

Clinicians should adopt structured, holistic and regularly reviewed approaches to assessing older adults' self‐care capability and capacity, using appropriate disease‐specific and non‐disease‐specific instruments (De Maria et al. 2025). Proactive and anticipatory support across primary care and community care, including accessible, tailored and evidence‐informed resources for older adults, carers and clinicians, can strengthen self‐care engagement while helping to reduce treatment burden and system strain (Corry et al. 2021). Tailored tools and supports, including online resources, peer support approaches and decision aids, can further enhance capability by making self‐care guidance and support accessible and actionable for both consumers and providers. Additionally, structured assessment of psychological and relational needs should be embedded within care pathways, aligning with international calls to more consistently integrate physical, psychosocial and relational dimensions as core components of person‐centred care assessment and planning. In Australia, this could be supported through more comprehensive use of existing mechanisms, including MBS‐funded 75+ health assessment and multidisciplinary geriatric assessments (Price et al. 2017). Achieving this will require strengthening workforce capability and capacity, organisational support and accessible resources to build competencies in holistic assessment, collaborative goal setting and interprofessional coordination.

Integrated care models offer opportunities to strengthen informational and relational continuity, mobilise support networks and improve coordination for community‐dwelling older adults with complex care needs. Structured approaches to coordination within primary care and community settings, such as the Chronic Care Model and Shared Care Models, recognise the interdependence between individual, relational and system‐level factors shaping care needs and care provision (Khatri et al. 2023). Emerging innovations such as nurse‐led chronic disease management roles, patient navigation services and multidisciplinary, team‐based approaches have demonstrated promise in addressing persistent coordination gaps (Chan et al. 2023; McParland et al. 2022). Australian programmes, such as Queensland's Nurse Navigator initiative, implemented across aged care and other settings, further demonstrate how navigation and digitally enabled models can strengthen continuity and access for people with complex or chronic conditions (Hannan‐Jones et al. 2025).

At a system level, concurrent reforms, including Strengthening Medicare, updated GP Chronic Condition Management Plans and the transition from the Home Care Package to Support at Home, aim to support older Australians to remain independent by enhancing GP access, personalising care, improving quality and simplifying services. These reforms mark a shift towards a more integrated, needs‐based model designed to support ageing in place and respond to recommendations from the Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety. Internationally, integrated care models increasingly incorporate virtual care, mHealth, artificial intelligence and remote monitoring technologies to extend support beyond clinical settings, though such innovations may risk widening disparities for individuals with limited digital literacy or access to technology. Together, these developments provide a structural foundation for ageing in place and managing chronic conditions in community settings, translating person‐centred, relational and capacity‐oriented principles into coordinated, continuous and contextually responsive models of care. Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths, including its in‐depth, theoretically informed analysis using the CLCT while also allowing the identification of inductive, data‐driven insights. This approach enabled exploration of the physical, psychosocial and relational dimensions of self‐care among community‐dwelling older adults across diverse condition profiles and support contexts. The qualitative design prioritised analytic depth over breadth, generating detailed accounts that might not be captured in larger quantitative studies. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The sample, although adequate for exploratory qualitative inquiry, was relatively small and drawn from three Australian states and territories. Participants were predominantly women, highly educated and living in socio‐economically advantaged areas determined based on postcodes, suggesting potential self‐selection and limiting cultural, gender and socio‐economic diversity. While participants aged 50–59 years represented a small proportion of the sample, the lower age threshold allowed inclusion of individuals experiencing earlier trajectories of multimorbidity within community settings. These factors may limit the transferability of the findings. Future research should use targeted recruitment, community partnerships and culturally tailored engagement strategies to increase participation from men, culturally and linguistically diverse groups and older adults experiencing various forms of socioeconomic disadvantage, including financial insecurity, geographical isolation, digital exclusion and limited access to services.

Conclusion

This study examined how community‐dwelling older adults with multimorbidity experience, enact and navigate daily self‐care, using the CLCT. The findings demonstrate that self‐care involves adaptive, ongoing processes shaped by self‐care capability and capacity, informal care networks and the responsiveness of health and social care systems over time. Participants generally demonstrated agency and resourcefulness in managing complex and changing care needs; however, psychosocial and relational aspects of care were often under‐recognised within healthcare encounters, requiring individuals and their informal supporters to compensate for fragmented or inconsistent support. By integrating individual, relational and structural influences across the life‐course, the CLCT provides an approach for understanding how self‐care capability and capacity are built, sustained or constrained in the context of ageing with multimorbidity. The findings highlight the importance of experiential learning, accessible information and informal and online resources in supporting self‐care where formal education and guidance are limited or uneven. Strengthening the availability, accessibility and integration of evidence‐informed resources and services represents an opportunity to better support older adults with multimorbidity and informal carers. These insights reinforce the need for more coordinated, person‐centred and relationally oriented approaches to self‐care and self‐management support across primary, community and aged care systems.
