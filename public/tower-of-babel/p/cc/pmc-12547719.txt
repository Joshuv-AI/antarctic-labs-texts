Blunt traumatic aortic injury following a combat blast trauma
Authors: Pierre Majdalani, George Greenberg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlunt traumatic aortic injury (BTAI) is a rare and often fatal. We present the case of a 19-year-old male soldier with BTAI and left subclavian artery (LSA) dissection after a blast injury from a rocket-propelled grenade. Despite the absence of other significant primary blast injuries, imaging revealed a grade III pseudoaneurysm at the aortic isthmus and LSA dissection. Emergent thoracic endovascular aortic repair with LSA stenting and thrombectomy was performed successfully. This report is the first of an isolated BTAI after a combat blast injury. It underscores the need for heightened clinical suspicion and highlights the efficacy of endovascular management.Keywords: BTAI, Blast injury, TEVAR, Subclavian artery dissection, Vascular trauma, Case report

Blunt traumatic aortic injury (BTAI) is a critical and potentially fatal consequence of high-energy blunt trauma, most often caused by rapid acceleration/deceleration events such as motor vehicle collisions. Other known mechanisms include falls, crush injuries, and aviation-related accidents, with an incidence of 1.5% to 20% in blunt thoracic trauma cases.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 BTAI typically involves the aortic isthmus and is often accompanied by additional injuries, including head trauma, hemothorax, lung contusion, and cardiac lesions.6 Subclavian artery injuries secondary to blunt trauma are exceptionally rare, comprising only 1% to 3% of acute arterial injuries, and are often fatal owing to their mechanism.7

Blast injuries are categorized into four mechanisms: primary (barotrauma), secondary (shrapnel), tertiary (displacement), and quaternary (other factors such as burns or toxic exposure).8 The primary mechanism is the blast wave moving through the body, thus causing barotrauma, especially to air-filled organs, such as the lungs, auditory organs, eyes, brain, and gastrointestinal tract. The secondary mechanism is the debris that penetrates or interacts with the body surface. The tertiary mechanism is displacement through the air and impacts on other objects or a structure collapse on the person. The quaternary mechanism category encompasses all injuries not included in the other categories, such as exposure to fire, fumes, radiation, biological agents, smoke, dust, and toxins.8

We present a unique case of BTAI and left subclavian artery (LSA) dissection after a blast injury in the absence of other primary blast injuries, except for a minor lung contusion, a mechanism not previously reported in the literature.

The patient provided written consent for the publication of their case details and images.

Case report

A 19-year-old healthy male soldier was airlifted to the emergency department after an rocket-propelled grenade assault. According to preliminary information, the incident occurred within a closed space. Several other soldiers, all wearing standard military protective armor, were reportedly killed or injured. The exact distance between the patient and the explosion was unknown. On arrival, he was hemodynamically stable with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15. Vital signs included blood pressure 145/67 mm Hg (left arm), heart rate 99 bpm, and oxygen saturation 100% on a nasal oxygen mask.

Physical examination revealed superficial first-degree burns on the left anterior thorax and third-degree burns on the right posterior thigh. Chest and pelvic radiographs were unremarkable. Extended Focused Assessment with Sonography in Trauma revealed no intraperitoneal, pericardial, or pleural fluid.

A total body computed tomography (CT) scan identified a proximal dissection of the LSA with absent distal flow (Fig 1, A), a grade III pseudoaneurysm at the aortic isthmus (Fig 1, B and C), and a minor right lung contusion (Fig 1, D).

Computed tomography angiography (CTA) angiography showing a grade III pseudoaneurysm at the aortic isthmus, proximal LSA dissection, and right lung contusion. (A) Pseudoaneurysm at the aortic isthmus (yellow arrow), LSA thrombosed false lumen (red asterisk). (B and C) Axial and three-dimensional planes of the aortic pseudoaneurysm (blue arrow). (D) Right middle lobe lung contusion (black arrow). LSA, left subclavian artery.

An emergent thoracic endovascular aortic repair was performed within 90 minutes of arrival. A 32 mm × 112 mm Valiant Captivia thoracic stent graft (Medtronic) was deployed in the descending thoracic aorta, with the proximal covered graft part just distal to the LSA origin. To preserve LSA perfusion, the artery was recanalized, and an 8 mm × 37 mm BeGraft stent (Bentley) was subsequently deployed into the LSA, protruding 5 mm into the aortic lumen via the proximal FreeFlo configuration of the Valiant graft and terminating 11 mm proximal to the left vertebral artery origin (Fig 2, A and B).

An angiogram obtained after thoracic endovascular aortic repair showing stent placement and restored LSA flow. (A) Postdeployment stent grafts. (B) Restored LSA and LVA patency after stent graft deployment. (C) Retrograde transradial angiography showing brachial artery occlusion. (D) Patent brachial artery with restored distal limb perfusion. LSA, left subclavian artery; LVA, left vertebral artery.

Shortly after deployment, a sharp drop in oxygen saturation was detected via pulse oximetry on the left hand, accompanied by the disappearance of both radial and ulnar pulses. Angiography performed through the left radial arterial line revealed distal brachial artery embolization and occlusion (Fig 2, C). A left transbrachial thromboembolectomy was performed, successfully retrieving embolic material from the brachial bifurcation using a Fogarty catheter. Postoperative angiography confirmed restored LSA patency and distal limb perfusion (Fig 2, D).

The patient had an uneventful recovery, with follow-up CT angiography (CTA) 1 week later confirming optimal stent positioning and vessel patency (Fig 3). No signs of brain or spinal cord ischemia were observed.

Post-treatment computed tomography angiography (CTA). Sagittal and three-dimensional planes showing stent graft patency with optimal configuration and no sign of aortic pseudoaneurysm or left subclavian artery (LSA) dissection.

Discussion

BTAI is the second leading cause of death from blunt trauma, surpassed only by intracranial hemorrhage.6 To our knowledge, this case is the first to be documented of BTAI resulting from a combat blast injury caused by an rocket-propelled grenade, highlighting a rare and atypical mechanism of trauma. Traditionally, BTAI is associated with sudden deceleration forces observed in motor vehicle collisions or falls. Motor vehicle collisions, especially frontal impacts, account for approximately 81% of BTAI cases.9 Blast injuries, however, typically cause internal damage through pressure wave propagation rather than blunt force impact. Although the exact pathophysiological mechanism remains unclear, we hypothesize that a combination of primary and tertiary blast effects contributed to the development of a grade III pseudoaneurysm at the aortic isthmus. The blastwave may have transferred kinetic energy sufficient to cause vascular injury without direct penetration or displacement. Thoracic compression from blastwave propagation created a sudden force transmitted through the chest wall, which might have generated enough strain at the aortic isthmus to induce tearing. The hemodynamic surge upon detonation, resulting from the intense pressure differential in the vasculature during an explosion, could have triggered endothelial damage or arterial dissection. Interestingly, no injuries were observed in typically blast-sensitive organs, such as the ears, eyes, brain, or gastrointestinal tract.

According to the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma guidelines, endovascular repair is preferred over open surgical repair in anatomically suitable patients.10 At our institution, thoracic endovascular aortic repair has become the standard of care for BTAI, and increasing procedural experience has helped to mitigate complications and optimize outcomes.

In the presented case, the proximity of the injury to the LSA necessitated careful planning to achieve adequate proximal sealing while preserving access to the occluded LSA to facilitate its recanalization and stenting, thereby restoring perfusion to the upper extremity and posterior cerebral circulation.

Device selection was based on availability, anatomical suitability, institutional familiarity, and validated performance in the trauma setting. The Valiant Captivia offers excellent conformability, trackability, and a proximal FreeFlo configuration that allows partial preservation of branch vessel perfusion in zone 2 deployments. The BeGraft peripheral stent offers high radial force, good flexibility and reliable wall apposition, essential for treating dissections or occlusive lesions in high-flow vessels like the LSA.

Thromboembolic complications after endovascular repair in the setting of traumatic vascular injury, although uncommon, can be clinically significant and potentially devastating. In our opinion, thrombus within the dissection plane should always be considered, and covered stents should be used routinely in similar cases. When feasible, the use of an embolic protection device in the vertebral artery may help to mitigate this risk; however, the benefit-risk ratio is not certain and depends on device availability, operator familiarity, and the overall risk of embolization.

Our follow-up protocol includes dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin 100 mg and clopidogrel 75 mg/d for 3 months, followed by lifelong single antiplatelet therapy if stent patency is confirmed. Imaging and clinical surveillance consist of CTA at 1 week postoperatively, regular clinical follow-up with bilateral upper limb blood pressure measurements, annual duplex ultrasound examination of the left upper limb, and CTA if clinical or imaging abnormalities are detected.

The atypical presentation of BTAI in a blast casualty calls for further exploration into blast-related vascular injuries. Future studies could evaluate whether similar cases have gone unrecognized and assess the frequency of occult vascular trauma in blast survivors. Additionally, understanding the precise biomechanical forces involved can refine injury prevention strategies and the design of military protective gear.

This rare presentation highlights the evolving complexity of modern battlefield trauma care and emphasizes the importance of considering major vascular injuries even in the absence of classic signs, particularly in blast-related trauma. The combination of atypical injury patterns and emerging endovascular techniques suggests that continued research and protocol adjustments may be needed for optimal management.

Conclusions

This case presents a rare instance of BTAI and LSA dissection following a blast mechanism—an etiology not previously documented in the literature. Timely diagnosis and early endovascular intervention achieved a successful outcome. This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for major vascular injuries in blast-related scenarios and supports the expanding role of endovascular management in combat vascular trauma.
