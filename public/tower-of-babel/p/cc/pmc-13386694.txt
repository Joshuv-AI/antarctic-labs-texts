Development and application of a Workplace-Based Assessment (WPBA) instrument based on competency for medical internship
Authors: Sylvia Costa Lima Farhat, Gustavo da Silva Azevedo, Giulianna Simonetti, Matheus Belloni Torsani, Iolanda de Fatima Lopes Calvo Tibério
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCompetency-Based Medical Education (CBME) is crucial to prepare future physicians for independent clinical practice. Professionalism is a core competency, yet its definition and assessment remain a challenge in medical education. This study describes the development of an instrument based on competencies for Workplace-Based Assessment (WPBA) and its application to medical interns at the University of São Paulo, one of the leading medical schools in Brazil.MethodsThe instrument was developed based on established frameworks (ACGME Milestones, CanMEDS, the RIME model and Bloom’s taxonomy) and validated using a Delphi consensus strategy with 64 experts. The instrument comprises six domains (Communication/Collaboration/Interpersonal Skills, Professionalism, Patient Care, Applied Knowledge, Health System, and Self-Development). We applied this instrument to assess 344 fifth and sixth-year medical students during clinical rotations from August 2024 to June 2025. We assessed internal consistency with Cronbach's alpha and conducted descriptive analyses to investigate overall academic performance correlates of the competency-based assessment scores.ResultsThe assessment instrument demonstrated high internal consistency (α = 0.97). Significant correlations (P < 0.001) were found between most competencies, with a strong correlation between the professionalism and patient care domains, confirmed by Bland–Altman analysis. There were 53 interns (15%) who received at least one rating of ‘unacceptable’ or ‘below expectations’ in professionalism. Students rated ‘below expectations’ in reliability in task completion demonstrated significantly worse performance in the patient care domain compared to those rated ‘satisfactory’ or ‘excellent’.ConclusionsThis study presents a comprehensive, competency-based Workplace-Based Assessment (WPBA) instrument with initial sources of validity evidence, which helps identify the strengths and weaknesses of medical interns, particularly in the complex domain of professionalism.

Background

Competency-Based Medical Education (CBME) is crucial for preparing future physicians for independent clinical practice [1–3]. A successful CBME program requires robust Workplace-Based Assessments (WPBA) to ensure that medical interns acquire the necessary knowledge, skills, and attitudes. Effective assessment must move beyond traditional testing, incorporating structured observations of clinical encounters to evaluate a wide range of competencies [1, 4].

Despite the global shift towards CBME, implementing structured assessments remains a challenge in many settings. Until 2023, at our institution, the evaluation of medical interns during their clinical rotations relied almost entirely on a preceptor’s unstructured "global impression." This subjective approach lacked operational definitions for key competencies, leading to variability among evaluators, difficulty in tracking student progression, and challenges in providing specific, actionable feedback. Therefore, there was a pressing need to develop and implement a comprehensive, multi-domain assessment instrument tailored to our institutional context.

To address this gap, we sought to create a structured WPBA instrument based on competency encompassing essential domains: Patient Care, Communication, Applied Knowledge, Health Systems, Self-Development, and Professionalism. Within this multi-domain framework, the assessment of professionalism presents a unique and persistent challenge [5]. While cognitive and technical skills are often more straightforward to measure, professionalism is frequently hindered by subjective interpretations [6, 7]. Literature shows that while educators observe unprofessional behavior in up to 20% of students, only 3% to 5% officially report it [8–10], often due to a lack of structured assessment criteria and fear of harming the student's reputation, and uncertainty about the tutoring process [7, 8, 11].

In our context, the challenge was not the absence of a theoretical definition of professionalism, which encompasses: clinical competence, honor and integrity, respect for others, and ethical and legal understanding, founded on excellence, humanism, accountability [6, 7, 12–14]; but the lack of an operational definition that could be easily used by preceptors during clinical shifts. By integrating professionalism into a broader competency-based instrument alongside other clinical skills, we aimed to translate overarching ethical concepts into observable, measurable behaviors (such as punctuality, feedback reception, and reliability) [11, 15]. It is important that the assessment framework for professionalism is aligned with social norms and the local context [7, 12].

Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to describe the development, implementation, and initial results of a comprehensive, Workplace-Based Assessments instrument based on competency for the medical internship. As a secondary objective, given the historical difficulties in evaluating professional behavior, we aimed to analyze how this new structured instrument captured professionalism scores and their correlation with other competency domains and cognitive test performances.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study utilized a multiphase design: initially, an instrument development and content validation phase (using a Delphi consensus method), followed by a cross-sectional observational phase where the instrument was implemented and analyzed across internship rotations during the 2024–2025 period.

This study was conducted at the University of São Paulo School of Medicine (FMUSP). In the Brazilian medical education system, the undergraduate medical degree lasts six years. The "internship" refers to the mandatory, full-time, supervised clinical rotations during the final two years (5th and 6th years) prior to graduation. This period corresponds to what is internationally known as clinical clerkships (5th year) and sub-internships or advanced clinical rotations (6th year). During this time, all students must mandatorily rotate through five core areas: pediatrics, internal medicine, obstetrics and gynecology, surgery, and preventive medicine, without the option to select specific specialties. Within this setting, students are continuously observed, and formal assessments using the WPBA instrument are completed by attending physicians (preceptors) at least at the end of each clinical rotation. The instrument serves both summative (grading) and formative purposes. Ideally, preceptors use WPBA to guide face-to-face feedback sessions, after which students receive a detailed PDF report of their performance.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee for the Analysis of Research Projects of Faculdade de Medicina da Universidade de Sao Paulo (Study Protocol Number 7.782.966). Due to the retrospective nature of the assessment record review, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Participants

The study assessed 344 fifth and sixth-year medical interns. The evaluation period was from August 4, 2024, to June 10, 2025.

Development of the assessment instrument

A Workplace-Based Assessment instrument based on competency (WPBA) was initially drafted by a core focal group of five qualified medical educators from FMUSP. All five members were active clinical supervisors and preceptors during the clerkship, representing diverse core specialties such as pediatrics, internal medicine, obstetrics and gynecology, surgery, and preventive medicine. Within the curriculum, they held leadership roles, including clerkship directors, coordinators and curriculum committee members, bringing specialized expertise in competency-based medical education, curriculum design, and clinical assessment.

While this local expertise was vital for contextualizing the instrument, the initial draft was subsequently subjected to an extensive content validation process (the Delphi method) involving a wider, multidisciplinary pool of experts to mitigate bias.

Because a single existing model did not fully address our institutional needs for assessing observable behaviors across all clinical rotations, we adopted a hybrid approach grounded in Competency-Based Medical Education (CBME). The instrument's design is a composite of several elements and principles from established frameworks: ACGME Milestones- The overarching architecture of our six domains closely resembles the core competencies found in the ACGME Internal Medicine Milestones. Furthermore, the developmental, evolutionary nature of the Milestones inspired our descriptive rubrics across the performance spectrum; CanMEDS: While the domains parallel the ACGME framework, the specific items within them incorporate competencies valued in CanMEDS. For instance, collaborative and advocacy roles were integrated into our Communication and Health System domains; RIME Model: The progressive character of the RIME (Reporter-Interpreter-Manager-Educator) model further inspired the evolutionary expectations within the instrument, particularly structuring the progression of clinical skills in the Patient Care and Applied Knowledge domains; Bloom's Taxonomy: Finally, Bloom's Taxonomy informed the selection of cognitive and affective verbs used in the instrument's items to differentiate varying levels of student proficiency.

Each item within a domain is evaluated using a qualitative rubric with four performance levels, plus a 'not assessed' option. To integrate this WPBA instrument with the university's mandatory 0–10 numerical grading system, these qualitative ratings are converted into points: 'unacceptable' scores 0; 'below expectations' scores 1; 'satisfactory' scores 2; and 'excellent' scores 3. Items marked as ‘not assessed’ are excluded from the calculation and do not penalize the student. The sum of the points obtained is divided by the maximum possible score for the evaluated items, and this ratio is normalized to a scale of 0 to 10. Mathematically, a consistently 'satisfactory' performance on all items results in a raw reference score of 6.7. Consequently, this value is established as the official minimum passing grade for the WPBA.

The draft instrument underwent content validation through a two-round Delphi consensus process. In the first round, a purposive sample of 34 core internship coordinators and senior preceptors received the instrument via email. They were asked to rank the proposed items in terms of relevance and suggest modifications or additional observable behaviors.

To ensure broader representation of the faculty who would ultimately use the tool on the wards, the panel was expanded in the second round. We invited additional active clinical preceptors across all five core specialties via an institutional mailing list, resulting in a panel of 64 participants. This larger group reviewed the aggregated, anonymized feedback from the first round. The consensus criterion for retaining an item in the final instrument was set at an 80% agreement rate, meaning that at least 80% of the respondents had to rate a specific competency item as 'essential' for internship evaluation. The final refined version was then officially approved by the undergraduate program committee.

The WPBA instrument was piloted to evaluate its reliability and construct validity. A team of seven internship coordinators and preceptors from the departments of Pediatrics, Internal Medicine, and Obstetrics & Gynecology applied the instrument to fifth- and sixth-year medical students during their internship rotations. Inter-rater reliability was measured yielding a Cohen’s kappa of 0.71.

To support this process and ensure standardized application of WPBA instrument based on competency, faculty development was conducted prior to institutional implementation through dedicated workshops focused on effective feedback delivery and the practical use of the new WPBA instrument.

Statistical analysis

Internal consistency reliability was determined using Cronbach's alpha coefficient.

Data were presented as frequency (percentage) for categorical variables and median (minimum to maximum values) for continuous variables.

For our analysis, raw score of 6.7 was rounded to a cut-off score of 7.0. Therefore, achieving a 7.0 or higher corresponds to an overall 'satisfactory' or 'excellent' performance, while any grade below this threshold reflects behaviors that fall below expectations.

We evaluated the correlation between 'professionalism', the 'other five domains', and mean of two annual Multiple-Choice Tests (MCTs) using Pearson correlation analysis. We compared the mean score differences for the 'professionalism' and 'other five domains' scores and MCT scores using Student’s t-tests and analyzed the distribution of differences using Bland–Altman plots.

Comparisons between students with low assessments (below-expected) and those with high assessments (above-expected) were made using the Mann–Whitney U test or Student's T-test for continuous variables, and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

We used SPSS software version 22.0 for the analysis. The significance level was set at 0.05.

Results

From August 2024 to June 2025, 1,284 WPB assessments were carried out. Of those, 736 assessments were completed for fifth-year students (across 13 rotations) and 548 for sixth-year students (across 9 rotations). Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.97 across both years, demonstrating high internal consistency of our instrument.

Discussion

This study contributes to medical education by addressing the persistent challenge of defining and assessing professionalism in future physicians [5–7, 9, 10]. Its findings reinforce the critical importance of structured, competency-based assessments in preparing medical interns for independent clinical practice and ultimately enhancing the quality of healthcare delivery. Nevertheless, professionalism, which encompasses attitudes, character traits, virtues, and values [13, 16, 17], is notoriously difficult to assess.

Our study details the robust development and validation of the competency-based assessment instrument, which is a cornerstone of its strength. The instrument was built upon established frameworks such as CanMEDS, ACGME Milestones, the RIME model and Blooms’ taxonomy and was rigorously validated through a Delphi consensus strategy with 64 experts. Crucially, it demonstrated high internal consistency reliability, evidenced by a Cronbach's alpha of 0.97, and good inter-rater reliability (Cohen's kappa of 0.71). This level of psychometric rigor is essential and directly addresses a recognized gap in medical education literature: the lack of validated assessment tools for professional behaviors [5, 6, 9]. It is important to note that the primary aim of this study was the local development and implementation of a WPBA instrument, rather than comparative psychometric analysis. Therefore, while the instrument demonstrated strong internal consistency and inter-rater reliability, we do not claim these properties are superior to existing international rating scales based on CanMEDS or the ACGME Milestones. Instead, these metrics provide initial evidence that a hybrid instrument, tailored to specific institutional needs, can operate reliably in replacing unstructured subjective assessment.

The successful implementation of this instrument at the University of São Paulo moves beyond a “general overall impression of the student at each stage”, thereby representing a significant step toward standardizing WPBA and generating valuable longitudinal data. Such standardization is particularly important given the ambiguity in literature and the uncertainty among faculty regarding what constitutes professionalism. By providing a structured and reliable framework, our instrument contributes to establishing an institutional definition of professionalism that is aligned with social norms and specific community contexts, a need highlighted in multiple studies [6, 7, 12–14]. It also supports structured feedback and programmatic assessment [4]. Moreover, the professionalism items included in our instrument align with the comprehensive set of professional attributes identified by health professions educators [5, 11, 15].

The fifth and highest level of Miller’s Pyramid, “Is”, refers to thinking, feeling, and acting in accordance with professional excellence [18]. Integrating this concept into assessment practice substantially increases the complexity of evaluation systems. In this regard, our competency-based assessment instrument aligns with the need for a strategic educational approach that develops knowledge, attitudes, judgment, and skills.

Challenges in assessing professionalism have been widely reported. Igarashi et al. [7] and Mak-van der Vossen et al. [19] noted that educators commonly observe unprofessional behaviors in a considerable proportion of medical students (up to 20%), although only a small fraction (3–5%) of these concerns are formally documented. Underreporting is often attributed to conceptual ambiguity, concerns about subjectivity, fear of harming students’ reputations, and insufficient faculty training [7, 8, 11, 20]. In our study, 15% of students performed below expectations in competencies related to professionalism, a proportion consistent with previous reports. While this rate aligns with the literature, capturing this realistic proportion locally represents a significant improvement in formal documentation. Previously, preceptors may have recognized unprofessional behaviors but rarely documented them due to the lack of an operational definition and the subjective nature of global ratings. By shifting to a structured WPBA, the instrument increased reporting. For the clerkship coordinator, this structured documentation enabled the rapid identification of underperforming students and provided a concrete, behavior-based framework for targeted feedback.

Several authors have proposed approaches to assess and remediate professionalism [11, 14, 15]. McFarland et al. [15] described the use of a weekly questionnaire to teach professionalism to undergraduate students, emphasizing the importance of clear expectations, consistent rule enforcement, and explicit behavioral descriptors. The authors underscored this strategy as a fundamental behavioral framework for medical education, supporting both objective measurement of behavioral change and structured intervention. Barnhoorn et al. [11] proposed a multilevel professionalism framework for remediation that encourages students to reflect on the environment, behavior, competencies, beliefs and values, identity, and mission when unprofessional behavior occurs. Khan et al. [14] developed an instrument based on the six domains of the American Board of Internal Medicine (Respect, Responsibility, Excellence, Altruism, Integrity, and Duty), enabling faculty to assess specific professional behaviors and provide targeted guidance for improvement.

In our study, the use of a competency-based assessment instrument across all clinical rotations allowed us to identify students with persistent deficiencies in professionalism. This facilitated structured feedback and helped students recognize specific competencies requiring enhancement. Consequently, remediation plans were developed to support longitudinal improvement, particularly in professionalism-related domains. Our findings align with studies asserting that assessment plays a critical role in identifying professionalism deficits and promoting professional behavior through reflective, structured action plans [11, 14, 15]. Brennan et al. [21], in a systematic review, identified only six of 23 studies addressing remediation for professionalism lapses in medical students, noting that none evaluated students’ progress following intervention, underscoring an important area for future research.

A major strength of our instrument was its ability to assess student performance across multiple competency domains and demonstrate a correlation between professionalism and patient care. Our results showed a strong correlation between professionalism scores and patient care scores. Notably, students rated below expectations in ‘reliability for task completion’, a professionalism subdomain, also performed poorly across all items in the patient care domain. Interestingly, the strong correlations observed persist among multiple independent raters in different contexts reflecting the interconnected nature of clinical competence. The next step will be to longitudinally monitor the progression of fifth-year students following remediation, using the WPBA instrument during their sixth year.

The structure of this new WPBA instrument actively addresses common barriers to reporting unprofessional behavior, such as preceptors' fear of harming a student's reputation and uncertainty regarding remediation. The underlying theory of use relies on translating abstract character traits into observable, actionable behaviors via descriptive rubrics. By doing so, the tool depersonalizes negative feedback; preceptors are not labeling a student as 'unprofessional,' but rather documenting specific behavioral deficits, such as poor punctuality or failure to integrate clinical feedback. Furthermore, this behavioral breakdown serves as a cognitive aid for faculty who lack formal pedagogical training. The explicit items on the rubric provide a clear, objective roadmap for the tutoring process, allowing interventions to be highly specific and focused on observable improvement rather than subjective judgments.

Previous studies have also shown that unprofessional behavior negatively affects nursing care, team interaction, team performance, and patient safety [22–24]. Consistent with these findings, we observed that students with the lowest overall WPBA scores in at least three rotations also demonstrated weaker performance in theoretical knowledge compared with their peers. This underscores the importance of early identification and timely implementation of remediation plans to support improvements in knowledge acquisition. Ultimately, recognizing unprofessional behaviors early, along with implementing timely, supportive interventions, has the potential to contribute meaningfully to the development of better professionals for society.

Our study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, this research was conducted at a single academic institution, which necessitates further validation in other contexts. As our WPBA instrument was designed as a hybrid composite (integrating elements of the ACGME, CanMEDS, and RIME frameworks) to specifically meet our local internship assessment policies, its direct application to other contexts may require local adaptations. Second, while the items for domains such as Professionalism and Patient Care were carefully crafted to capture distinct behaviors, the strong positive correlations observed between them should be interpreted with caution. Although the use of behavioral rubrics has successfully increased the documentation of unprofessional behaviors, assessments in clinical settings remain highly susceptible to common method biases, conceptual overlap in clinical practice, and residual rater biases (such as leniency or halo effect). This underscores the ongoing need for faculty development. Third, while our very high Cronbach's alpha indicates strong internal reliability, this may also suggest some redundancy items that could be refined in future studies. Fourth, the use of rounding scores in our analyses may mask subtle performance differences among borderline students. Finally, while we have gathered initial sources of validity evidence by comparing WPBA scores with theoretical performance on the MCT, further longitudinal studies are needed to assess whether this structured assessment translates into better clinical outcomes in students' future medical practice.

Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive, competency-based Workplace-Based Assessment (WPBA) instrument with initial sources of validity evidence, which helps identify the strengths and weaknesses of medical interns, particularly in the complex domain of professionalism. By offering a more standardized and structured approach, this instrument addresses critical challenges in medical education, providing valuable data for targeted educational interventions and supporting the broader goals of Competency-Based Medical Education.
