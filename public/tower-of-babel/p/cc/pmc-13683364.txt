Impact of methicillin resistance on mortality in Staphylococcus aureus endocarditis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Durga Shankar Meena, Deepak Kumar, Gopal Krishana Bohra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMethicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) has emerged as a leading cause of endocarditis with poor outcomes. The magnitude and consistency of its mortality risk compared with methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) remain uncertain.MethodsData were extracted from PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Cochrane Library from database inception to 30 November 2025, without language restrictions. A random-effects model (Restricted Maximum-Likelihood) was used to calculate pooled risk ratios (RRs). Heterogeneity was explored by stratified and meta-regression analyses. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plot symmetry and Egger’s regression.ResultsTwenty-four studies, comprising 3291 patients (1,011 with MRSA and 2280 with MSSA), met the inclusion criteria. Pooled short-term mortality was significantly higher in MRSA endocarditis (30.9% vs 25.6%; RR 1.48, 95% CI 1.21-1.80; I² = 34%). Across stratified analyses by study design, setting, region, and study era, mortality remained consistently higher in MRSA endocarditis. No significant differences were found for heart failure (RR 1.08, 95% CI 0.90-1.30), embolic events (RR 0.82, 95% CI 0.51-1.33), stroke (RR 0.88, 95% CI 0.78-1.00), or surgery performed (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.56-1.68) in MRSA and MSSA groups. No publication bias was revealed by Funnel-plot inspection and Egger’s test (p = 0.082). Meta-regression showed no effect modification by age, hospital-acquired infection, prosthetic valve, or right-sided endocarditis.ConclusionsMRSA endocarditis carries a significantly higher short-term mortality than MSSA, consistent across study designs and study-level characteristics. The major complications were comparable between the two groups. Subject terms: Diseases, Bacterial infection

Introduction

The landscape of infective endocarditis (IE) has evolved considerably over recent decades. Staphylococcus aureus has now emerged as the leading cause of both community-acquired and healthcare-associated infections1–4. The management of S. aureus endocarditis is challenging owing to its aggressive course, propensity to cause frequent metastatic and disseminated foci, and rapid development of complications such as embolic events and heart failure. Despite significant advances in diagnostic modalities, antimicrobial therapy (newer anti-MRSA drugs), and supportive care, mortality associated with S. aureus endocarditis remains unacceptably high, which is reported from 28% to 46% in most series5–8. Poor prognosis in S. aureus endocarditis is attributed to several factors such as delayed diagnosis, increasing incidence of device-related infections, comorbid conditions and lack of early access to surgical management9–11.

Apart from the aforementioned factors, the emergence of methicillin resistance has drawn particular concern. MRSA infections are associated with persistent bacteremia and a higher prevalence of hospital-acquired and device-related infections. Limited therapeutic options, along with the suboptimal bactericidal activity of glycopeptides, may further worsen clinical outcomes. Numerous studies have compared outcomes of MRSA and methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) bacteremia12–15. Prior studies have focused on bloodstream infections broadly, without stratifying for endocarditis, which has limited the understanding of resistance-associated risk in this unique clinical entity. This systematic review and meta-analysis was therefore conducted to compare mortality and major complications between MRSA and MSSA endocarditis. This study aimed to assess how methicillin resistance independently influences mortality and clinical outcomes in S. aureus endocarditis, and whether this association remains consistent across study designs, geographical regions, and time periods.

Methods

We conducted the literature search in four major electronic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Scopus, for eligible studies from their inception to 30 November 2025. The following combination of keywords and MeSH terms were used: “infective endocarditis”, “staphylococcus aureus”, “methicillin-resistance”, “treatment outcome”, and “mortality”. No language restriction was applied during the database search. The detailed search methodology is attached in the supplementary file. This meta-analysis was designed and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines16 and was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251139622).

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was all-cause short-term mortality defined as in-hospital mortality or early follow-up mortality ( ≤ 60 days) as reported in the individual studies. Among Secondary outcomes, embolic events, heart failure, CNS complications (ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke) and surgery performed were included. All these outcomes were documented at the time of hospital admission or during the in-hospital course, as defined in the relevant studies. Secondary outcomes were prioritised based on their established clinical relevance in IE, as defined by commonly reported complications and management outcomes in prior literature. Another reason to include these secondary outcomes was their consistent availability across included studies.

Study selection and eligibility criteria

All eligible studies (prospective and retrospective cohorts) that compared the outcomes between MRSA and MSSA endocarditis were included in the analysis. The inclusion criteria were as follows: a). The study had to report all-cause short-term mortality, with or without other major complications (heart failure, embolic events, or stroke). b) Cases fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for IE according to Modified Duke’s or Duke-ISCVID (International Society for Cardiovascular Infectious Diseases)17. Methicillin resistance in S. aureus is defined as an oxacillin minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of greater than or equal to 4 micrograms/mL18. Conference papers, review articles, editorials and case reports were excluded. Studies were excluded if they did not report short-term mortality data (in-hospital or early follow-up up to 60 days) or did not provide comparative outcomes between MRSA and MSSA IE. Studies with mixed bacteremia cohorts without separate IE data, were also excluded.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened study titles, abstracts, and full texts to determine final eligibility. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus with the assistance of a third reviewer. The following data were extracted from each study: study design and setting, geographic region, S. aureus resistance pattern, and clinical outcomes. Outcomes of interest included mortality, embolic events, heart failure, stroke, and surgery performed. Data extraction was cross-checked for accuracy before analysis.

Risk of bias assessment

The ROBINS-E (Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies - of Exposures) tool was used for the quality assessment of all eligible studies to identify possible bias19. The following seven domains were evaluated: confounding, participant selection, classification and deviation from intervention, missing data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results. Two independent reviewers performed the risk of bias assessment; any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. Each study was assessed across predefined domains and categorized as having ‘low,’ ‘moderate,’ or ‘critical risk’ of bias. Most included studies were judged to have a moderate to critical risk of bias. The primary source of bias was confounding. Selection bias and incomplete reporting of key variables also contributed to moderate risk in some studies. In contrast, classification of exposure and outcome measurement were generally at low risk, as mortality is an objective outcome. A detailed domain-wise assessment for individual studies is visualized in the ROBINS-E traffic plot (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

Risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used as the effect measure for all outcomes to compare MRSA with MSSA endocarditis. A random-effects model with the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method was used to generate pooled estimates. Cochran’s Q and the I² statistic were used to quantify the between-study heterogeneity and interpreted as low ( < 25%), moderate (25–50%), or high ( > 50%). We performed a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis to examine the influence of individual studies on pooled estimates. We also used the DerSimonian-Laird (DL) method in sensitivity analyses to assess the strength of the pooled estimate. To evaluate the consistency of estimates across different studies, stratified analysis was performed based on various subgroups [study design (prospective vs. retrospective), study setting (single-centre vs. multicenter), region (Asian vs. European/USA), and publication era (before vs. ≥2010)]. This pragmatic cutoff was chosen to reflect the shift in diagnostic modalities and the availability of newer therapeutic options for S. aureus endocarditis. Stratification by geographic region was performed based on the hypothesis that differences in healthcare infrastructure, antimicrobial practices, and surgical management strategies may influence the outcomes in S. aureus endocarditis. Publication bias for the primary outcome was assessed visually using a funnel plot and statistically with Egger’s regression test. Univariate and multivariable random-effects meta-regression analyses were performed to examine the potential study-level modifiers of mortality. Mean age, hospital-acquired infection, prosthetic valve involvement, and right-sided endocarditis were the covariates included in the meta-regression, based on their potential impact on the outcome and the availability of data. Meta -analyses were conducted using RevMan (version 5.4; Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, UK).

Results

Study characteristics

A total of 3215 studies were identified through the search strategy using electronic database searches. We also performed a manual search of the reference lists of all included articles to identify additional eligible studies. A total of 214 full-text studies were assessed for the final eligibility after removing duplicates and screening of study titles and abstracts. In this meta-analysis, a total of 24 articles6,20–42 were included after meeting the inclusion criteria. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). Overall, this meta-analysis evaluated a total of 3291 patients diagnosed with S. aureus endocarditis; of these, 2280 (69.3%) had MSSA endocarditis and 1011 (30.7%) had MRSA endocarditis. A total of 24 observational cohort studies published between 1985 and 2025 were included, comprising both retrospective and prospective designs from North America, Europe, and Asia. Most studies were retrospective cohort (n = 16), while the remaining were prospective design (n = 8). Among these, 10 studies were multicentric. The proportion of MRSA endocarditis varies from 12.9% to 91.3%. The mean age across cohorts ranged from 35 to 70 years, with most cohorts having a mean age above 50 years (78.9%). Among studies that reported these parameters, the proportion of hospital-acquired IE ranged from 15.6% to 49.9%, and prosthetic-valve IE from 2% to 47%. Overall mortality in S. aureus endocarditis was 27.2%. A detailed summary of study-level characteristics is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

PRISMA flow diagram showing the study selection process for the meta-analysis.

Primary outcome

Pooled analysis of 24 studies reporting short-term mortality showed 25.6% (583/2280) mortality in the MSSA and 30.9% (312/1011) in the MRSA groups. MRSA infection was associated with significantly higher mortality compared with MSSA (RR = 1.48, 95% CI 1.21–1.80, p = 0.0005) (Fig. 2). Between-study heterogeneity was low to moderate (I² = 34%), with consistent direction of effect across most cohorts.

Forest plot showing the pooled comparison of all-cause mortality between MRSA and MSSA endocarditis across included studies.

Secondary outcomes

Nine studies reported the embolic complications. The pooled estimate showed no significant difference in embolic event rates (RR = 0.82, 95% CI 0.51–1.33; p = 0.38; Fig. 3A). Heterogeneity was high (I² = 84%), reflecting variation in study design and definitions of embolic events. Seven studies reported the incidence of heart failure in MRSA and MSSA endocarditis. Pooled random-effects analysis showed no significant difference in the risk of heart failure between the two groups (RR = 1.08, 95% CI 0.90–1.30; p = 0.34), with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%; Fig. 3B). The incidence of ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke was reported in ten studies; this meta-analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in stroke incidence between MRSA and MSSA endocarditis (RR = 0.88, 95% CI 0.78–1.00; p = 0.05; I² = 0%; Fig. 4A). Lastly, no difference was found in surgery rates between MRSA and MSSA endocarditis (RR = 0.97, 95% CI 0.56–1.68; p = 0.90; Fig. 4B).

Stratified analysis

Stratified analyses were performed to explore the clinical relevance of study-level factors that could contribute to heterogeneity in mortality. We analysed study design and setting (prospective vs. retrospective, single-centre vs. multicentre), geographical region (Asian vs. European/USA), and publication year (before 2010 vs. 2010 or later) subgroups. These parameters are known to reflect variations in diagnostic facilities, antimicrobial access, and surgical practices of S. aureus endocarditis.

Across all subgroups, mortality remained consistently higher in MRSA endocarditis compared to MSSA (p for interaction >0.05 for all). Retrospective and single-centre studies tended to show a greater effect size and moderate heterogeneity, while multicentre and prospective cohorts demonstrated more conservative but directionally similar results. Stratification by study era showed a persistent excess risk of death in MRSA even in contemporary cohorts, indicating that temporal improvements in management have not eliminated the survival gap. Interestingly, regional differences were more prominent: Asian studies showed a markedly higher relative mortality associated with MRSA than those conducted in Europe or North America. Overall, study-level characteristics did not substantially modify the association between MRSA resistance and mortality, suggesting that the poorer outcomes in MRSA endocarditis are consistent across diverse settings. Detailed Stratified analysis for mortality is summarised in Table 1 and the supplementary Figs. 2-5. In addition to the primary outcome, stratified analyses were also conducted for secondary outcomes. The detailed stratified forest plots for these outcomes are provided in the supplementary material (supplementary Figs. 6-21).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially removing each study from the meta-analysis (leave-one-out method). The pooled RR varied between 1.41 and 1.51 for mortality in MRSA vs MSSA infection. No single study significantly altered the overall pooled estimate or its statistical significance. These findings suggest that no single study has a significant impact on mortality, which is consistent across all studies. In addition, the results of sensitivity analysis were comparable under both fixed and random-effects models (DerSimonian-Laird and REML estimators). This underscores the stability and reliability of the pooled estimates.

Publication bias

Visual inspection of the funnel plot for mortality revealed an approximately symmetrical distribution of studies around the pooled log risk ratio (Supplementary Fig. 22.). The pseudo-95% confidence limits formed a well-defined funnel shape, and no systematic asymmetry, suggesting selective publication, was evident. Egger’s regression intercept was 0.98 (p = 0.082), indicating the absence of significant small-study effects. The pooled random-effects estimate (RR = 1.48), therefore, appears robust to potential publication bias. Overall, the findings suggest that selective reporting or small-study bias is unlikely to have influenced the observed association between MRSA infection and higher mortality in S. aureus endocarditis.

Meta-regression

Univariate meta-regression was performed to assess factors contributing to heterogeneity in mortality across studies. Mean age, hospital-acquired infection, prosthetic-valve endocarditis, and right-sided involvement were the covariate selected based on clinical relevance and data availability. Mean age was not significantly associated with the log risk ratio (β = −0.009 per year; p = 0.71; Fig. 5A). The proportion of hospital-acquired IE also showed no significant association (β = −0.079 per 10% increase; p = 0.78; Fig. 5B). Similarly, prosthetic valve IE (β = −0.023 per 10% increase; p = 0.97; Fig. 6A) and right-sided IE (β = +0.035 per 10% increase; p = 0.64; Fig. 6B) showed no significant relationship with pooled estimates. Overall, none of the evaluated study-level covariates explained the mortality difference between MRSA and MSSA in univariate analyses. Notably, key study-level variables (age, hospital-acquired infection, prosthetic valve involvement, and right-sided endocarditis) were not consistently reported across all studies, which may limit the meta-regression analyses.

A multivariable random-effects meta-regression, including mean age, hospital-acquired infection, prosthetic-valve endocarditis, and right-sided involvement (k = 9), did not reveal any independent study-level predictors of mortality differences between MRSA and MSSA endocarditis. None of the covariates reached statistical significance (all p > 0.10). The multivariate model explained only a small proportion of residual heterogeneity (R² ≈20%). This suggests that the excess mortality in MRSA endocarditis is unlikely to be explained by these study level covariates.

Discussion

This meta-analysis analyzed 24 observational cohorts involving over 3200 patients and provides a detailed comparison of clinical outcomes in MRSA and MSSA endocarditis. Despite advances in diagnostics, antimicrobial therapy, and surgical management, the mortality associated with S. aureus endocarditis remains unacceptably high. This pooled analysis demonstrated that MRSA endocarditis is associated with significantly higher short-term mortality compared with MSSA. However, rates of major complications such as heart failure, embolic events, and stroke were comparable between the two groups. These findings underscore that methicillin resistance is associated with worse prognosis, reflecting the interplay of microbiologic virulence, therapeutic limitations, and host factors.

High mortality in MRSA endocarditis may reflect underlying enhanced pathogen virulence and biofilm-forming capacity, persistent bacteremia, and poorer treatment response43,44. Furthermore, the delayed bactericidal activity of glycopeptides such as vancomycin compared with β-lactams used for MSSA may result in slower bacterial clearance and suboptimal penetration into vegetations45,46. Interestingly, we did not find a significant difference in the rates of heart failure, embolic events, and stroke between MRSA and MSSA endocarditis. This suggests that although methicillin resistance impacts survival, it may not substantially modify the immediate complication profile once endocardial infection has occurred. Vegetation size, intracardiac destruction, and embolic potential are largely determined by host and anatomical factors rather than antibiotic susceptibility. Nevertheless, the persistently higher mortality in MRSA (despite similar complication rates) implies that systemic burden of infection and therapeutic response may play a larger role in determining the outcomes rather than mechanical complications alone.

In stratified analysis, the pooled effect appeared more pronounced in Asian cohorts compared with Western studies. This regional gradient may reflect the difference in healthcare infrastructure, empirical therapy, and surgical intervention thresholds. Regional variation in outcomes may also reflect the differences in management strategies, including therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) targets and dosing practices for anti-MRSA agents such as vancomycin. However, detailed data on antimicrobial dosing, serum concentration monitoring, and pharmacokinetic targets were not consistently reported in the included studies, which preclude the analysis for aforementioned factors. These factors may represent an important source of heterogeneity and warrant further investigation in future studies. Retrospective and single-centre studies also tended to show more potent effects, which may be attributed to unmeasured confounding or referral bias. Importantly, even in more contemporary (post-2010) studies, mortality remained significantly higher in MRSA, suggesting that improvements in antimicrobial and surgical strategies have not eliminated this gap. Together, these observations highlight methicillin resistance as a persistent, global determinant of poor outcomes in S. aureus endocarditis.

MRSA infections are also more likely to be nosocomial, affecting older, comorbid, or device-bearing populations compared to the MSSA population, further amplifying mortality risk11,47. However, we did not find a significant association of these MRSA-related covariates (mean age, hospital-acquired infection, prosthetic-valve involvement) with mortality differences between the two groups in univariate and multivariate regression., This suggests that study-level differences do not fully account for the observed increase in mortality risk. The modest R² ( ≈ 20%) in the multivariable model suggests residual unexplained heterogeneity, which may potentially be attributed to treatment-level factors such as antibiotic timing, dosing optimisation, or surgical decision-making, which were rarely reported in source studies.

The type of antimicrobial therapy used for MRSA endocarditis may also influence the clinical outcomes. In most of the included studies, vancomycin was the primary treatment for MRSA IE, particularly in earlier cohorts. However, reduced vancomycin susceptibility, including MIC creep, has been associated with reduced clinical efficacy. Furthermore, newer agents such as daptomycin and ceftaroline, which showed more rapid bactericidal activity, were under-represented in the included studies. It limits the assessment of their impact on outcomes. Data on MIC distributions and resistance patterns were inconsistently reported, precluding formal analysis. Importantly, the findings of this study should not be interpreted as evidence against the effectiveness of vancomycin. It remains a cornerstone of therapy for MRSA IE. Rather, the observed differences in outcomes likely reflect an interplay of pathogen-related factors, host characteristics, and treatment-related variables. These factors highlight the importance of antimicrobial selection and susceptibility profiles as potential modifiers of outcomes in MRSA IE. Future studies should incorporate antibiotic specific data, which can explore its impact on outcome in MRSA IE.

The strength of this meta-analysis is its broad inclusion of studies spanning diverse geographic regions and study designs. Heterogeneity was carefully examined through stratified analysis. Notably, the results remained stable in leave-one-out analyses and consistent across both fixed and random-effects models. Unlike previous studies, this meta-analysis focuses specifically on IE rather than mixed bacteremia cohorts, delineating disease-specific prognostic implications of methicillin resistance and providing more clinically relevant insights for the prognosis of S. aureus endocarditis.

Despite its strengths, this meta-analysis has several limitations that should be considered while interpreting the findings. The possibility of confounding and selection bias can’t be excluded due to the observational nature of all studies. Most studies reported only crude mortality data without adjusted effect estimates. Thus, our pooled analyses primarily reflect unadjusted risk ratios, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. Patients with MRSA IE are more likely to have healthcare-associated infections, comorbidities, and prosthetic devices, which may independently influence mortality. Although we performed study-level meta-regression, this approach cannot fully adjust for individual-level confounders. Therefore, the observed association should be interpreted cautiously and not as a direct causal relationship. Long-term mortality, relapse, and antibiotic-specific outcomes could not be assessed due to insufficient data. The definition of short-term mortality varied across studies, including in-hospital, 30-day, or up to 60-day mortality. This heterogeneity may introduce variability in outcome assessment. Although these endpoints broadly reflect early mortality in IE, they may not capture long-term outcomes or complications. The number of studies per subgroup was limited for some analyses, which may have reduced statistical power to detect modifiers of effect. Additionally, most MRSA cohorts were treated with vancomycin, while newer agents such as daptomycin or ceftaroline were under-represented. This could potentially limit applicability to current practice. Finally, small-study bias cannot be entirely excluded, although statistical tests did not indicate significant asymmetry.

Conclusions

MRSA endocarditis is associated with significantly higher short-term mortality compared with MSSA, despite comparable rates of complications such as heart failure, embolic events, and stroke. This persistently high mortality highlights the intrinsic virulence and therapeutic limitations associated with methicillin resistance. Future research should focus on early detection, improving therapeutic regimens, and exploring adjunctive interventions to mitigate the high mortality associated with MRSA endocarditis.
