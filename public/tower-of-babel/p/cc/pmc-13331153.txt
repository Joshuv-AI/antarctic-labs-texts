Readiness for collaboration: an implementation science perspective on integrating Nigerian religious leaders into mental health systems – findings from the CLERIC study
Authors: Abiodun O Adewuya, Olabisi E Oladipo, Azizat Lebimoyo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe mental health treatment gap in low- and middle-income countries, particularly Nigeria is profound. Religious leaders serve as de facto community gatekeepers for mental distress. This study, forming the quantitative and mixed-methods component of the CLErgy and Religious leader Involvement in Community mental health project, moves beyond acknowledging this role by systematically assessing the collaboration readiness of Nigerian religious leaders, identifying its predictors and classifying leaders into distinct implementation profiles to inform task-sharing initiatives.MethodsA cross-sectional, explanatory mixed-methods study was conducted in Lagos State, Nigeria. Of 255 religious leaders approached, 207 Christian and Muslim leaders were enrolled (response rate 81.2%). A supplementary sample of 25 mental health professionals was recruited (response rate 71.4%). Surveys and focus group discussions were administered in English and Yoruba; the focus group sessions lasted 75–90 min. Collaboration readiness was assessed using a validated 10-item scale alongside the Mental Health Literacy Scale, the Community Attitude Towards Mental Illness Scale-16 and a structured barrier assessment. Analyses included multiple linear regression, structural equation modelling (SEM) and latent class analysis (LCA). Two focus groups (n=15) and open-ended healthcare professional survey responses were analysed thematically.ResultsOverall readiness was moderate (M=25.8/40), with 71.5% demonstrating moderate-to-high readiness. Notably, 48.8% had never made a medical referral, revealing a persistent know-do gap. Regression (R²=0.64) identified mental health literacy (β=0.41), education (β=0.26) and prior training (β=0.23) as strongest predictors. SEM confirmed education improves readiness primarily via literacy (indirect effect=0.13). LCA identified four implementation profiles: implementation ready (18.4%), conditionally ready (31.9%), reluctantly engaged (29.5%) and implementation resistant (20.2%). Four qualitative themes emerged: structural constraints, ideological conflict, trust barriers and conditional openness from healthcare providers. Knowledge gaps and trust deficits were bidirectional.ConclusionsSubstantial collaboration potential exists among Nigerian religious leaders and appears to be shaped by a combination of intervention-sensitive factors, such as mental health literacy and training exposure, and less directly modifiable structural characteristics, such as educational background and service access. A stratified, bidirectional implementation strategy tailored to distinct readiness profiles is essential to bridge the mental health treatment gap.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study provides the first systematic, mixed-methods assessment of collaboration readiness among Nigerian religious leaders within a programmatic research context. Mental health literacy (β=0.41), education (β=0.26) and prior training (β=0.23) are the strongest predictors of readiness, and structural equation modelling confirms these operate through a sequential knowledge-attitude-behaviour pathway. Latent class analysis identifies four distinct implementation profiles and qualitative findings reveal that collaboration barriers, including knowledge gaps and trust deficits, are genuinely bidirectional. The persistent referral gap (48.8% never referred) reflects structural system failure rather than theological opposition.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

A uniform engagement strategy is insufficient. Implementation programmes should adopt a stratified approach: enrolling implementation ready leaders as early adopters; delivering structured literacy interventions and facility familiarisation for the conditionally ready majority; prioritising trust-building and bidirectional training for the reluctantly engaged and focusing on harm reduction for the implementation resistant group. Service mapping, formal referral protocols and a national training curriculum co-developed with diverse faith communities represent the most urgent policy priorities, underpinned by Nigeria’s Mental Health Act 2021.

Introduction

The global mental health treatment gap remains one of the most persistent and consequential failures of health systems worldwide, a challenge that is particularly pronounced in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where severe shortages of specialist workforces leave an already substantial burden of unmet need largely unaddressed.1 Across sub-Saharan Africa, this gap reflects not merely high demand but the severely constrained capacity of health systems to respond adequately to widespread and often chronic mental health needs.2 Nigeria illustrates this disparity acutely: with an estimated 0.09 psychiatrists per 100 000 people, biomedical mental healthcare remains structurally inaccessible to the overwhelming majority of the population.3 This workforce deficit reflects decades of compounding structural failures, including Nigeria’s chronic allocation of less than 1% of its national health budget to mental health, geographically concentrated training infrastructure and significant professional out-migration to higher-income settings, a pattern that constitutes a structural haemorrhage of human capital from already under-resourced systems.2 4

Against this backdrop, religious institutions have emerged as primary and often sole sources of psychosocial support for many Nigerians. As established and trusted community gatekeepers, religious leaders are frequently the first point of contact for individuals experiencing mental distress, representing a central component of pathways to care across sub-Saharan Africa.5 Foundational national survey data confirmed that a substantial proportion of Nigerians sought help primarily from religious sources.6 These leaders offer spiritual support, counselling and crisis intervention that congregants explicitly understand as responses to mental health problems, situating them not as peripheral figures but as primary actors within the nation’s de facto mental health landscape.7 They wield considerable moral authority and operate within complex psychosocial ecosystems where spiritual, social and health concerns intersect.

The global health community has increasingly endorsed task-sharing frameworks (redistributing specific mental health functions from specialist to non-specialist community providers) as a pragmatic response to this structural gap.8 9 Within this movement, religious leaders represent a potentially valuable but underused resource for bridging treatment gaps. However, despite growing interest in faith-health partnerships, a critical implementation gap persists: many religious leaders express willingness to collaborate yet rarely refer congregants to formal services. This ‘collaboration paradox’, whereby leaders are willing but structurally unable or conditional collaborators constrained by epistemic disrespect and system failures, has been identified as the defining challenge for faith-based integration in Nigerian mental health care.10 11 Prior research has largely focused on documenting explanatory models of mental illness and patterns of spiritual healing, without systematically examining whether, how and which leaders are prepared to engage in integrated care models.12 13 Early investigations identified heterogeneous practice patterns, from spiritual-exclusive to collaborative-medical orientations, but offered limited guidance for implementation.14

To address these gaps, an implementation science perspective is required. The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) provides a structured multilevel examination of readiness across inner setting (attitudes and beliefs within religious organisations), outer setting (service availability and policy context) and individual characteristics (knowledge, education and motivation) domains.15 16 The collaborative care model specifies the operational requirements for effective partnerships, including role clarity, shared care planning and stepped-care approaches.17 The theory of planned behaviour (TPB) grounds the analysis of individual collaboration intentions in three constructs: attitudes toward collaboration, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control.18 19 Together, these frameworks recognise that readiness is not a singular construct but a product of interacting individual, organisational and contextual influences, and that understanding its heterogeneity across subgroups is essential for designing targeted, scalable interventions.20

The present study addresses this gap as part of the CLERIC (CLErgy and Religious leader Involvement in Community mental health) research programme, a multiphase investigation examining the determinants, dynamics and implementation pathways for faith-health collaboration in Nigerian mental healthcare. This paper focuses on five primary aims: (1) to quantify collaboration readiness using a validated scale; (2) to identify structural, educational and attitudinal predictors of readiness; (3) to examine, through SEM, how mental health knowledge mediates the relationship between background characteristics and readiness; (4) to classify leaders into distinct implementation profiles through latent class analysis (LCA) and (5) to explore systemic barriers from the dual perspectives of religious leaders and healthcare professionals. By integrating quantitative assessment with qualitative exploration, this investigation provides an empirical foundation for designing feasible, culturally congruent and stratified faith-health partnerships to help bridge Nigeria’s mental health treatment gap.

Methods

Study design and setting

This investigation employed a cross-sectional, explanatory mixed-methods design integrating quantitative surveys with qualitative focus group discussions and open-ended questionnaires. It forms the quantitative and mixed-methods component of the CLERIC research programme, with companion qualitative work exploring explanatory models, structural constraints and bidirectional relational dynamics reported separately.11 Lagos State was selected for its diverse religious landscape, varied healthcare infrastructure and demographic breadth encompassing urban, periurban and rural communities, representative of sub-Saharan African contexts.

Participants and sampling

Religious leaders

Of 255 religious leaders identified and approached through stratified purposive sampling, 27 were excluded on eligibility grounds and 21 declined participation, yielding a final analytical sample of 207 and a response rate of 81.2% (figure 1). The sample comprised Christian leaders (64.3%; n=133) and Muslim leaders (35.7%; n=74), was predominantly male (75.4%; n=156), with a mean age of 46.8 years (SD=11.2, range: 28–74). Approximately one third practised in rural or periurban settings (32.9%; n=68). In terms of education, 28.5% had secondary schooling or below, 45.9% held tertiary qualifications, and 25.6% had postgraduate education. Nearly a quarter (23.2%) had prior mental health training. Sampling was stratified across denomination, geographic location, gender and education to ensure variation in predictor variables necessary for robust modelling. Eligibility required formal ordination, current active congregational leadership, pastoral care provision in the preceding 12 months and written informed consent.

Participant recruitment flow diagram. FGDs, focus group discussions.

Healthcare professionals

A supplementary sample of 25 mental health professionals including psychiatrists (n=9), clinical psychologists (n=8), and psychiatric nurses (n=8), was recruited through professional networks, listservs and snowball sampling, of whom 14 were male and 11 female. Of 35 approached, 25 were enrolled (response rate 71.4%). Mean professional experience was 10.3 years (SD=6.8, range: 2–28).

Sample size considerations

No formal a priori power calculation was conducted for this exploratory study. The achieved N=207 exceeds established thresholds for multiple linear regression with six predictors (minimum 60–12),21 structural equation modelling (SEM) of moderate complexity (N≥150–200)21 and four-class latent class solutions (N≥200).22 The entropy statistic of 0.79 provides additional support for adequate sample size for reliable class recovery.

Patient and public involvement

Prior to data collection, a separate advisory group comprising five religious leaders and three mental health professionals (none of whom subsequently participated in the main study) reviewed draft survey instruments through two structured feedback sessions over a 4-week period, informing revisions to item wording, vignette appropriateness, and face validity. This group was not involved in study design, analysis or manuscript preparation.

Measures and instrumentation

Instruments for religious leaders

Collaboration Readiness Scale. The primary outcome was assessed using a novel 10-item scale developed for this study (online supplemental file SF1). The eight-item subscale used a five-point Likert format (1=strongly disagree to 5=strongly agree), evaluating willingness to train, comfort with referral, trust in professionals and perceived respect from clinicians. Total scores ranged from 8 to 40, with participants categorised as low (8–18), moderate (19–29) or high (30–40) readiness. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=0.82; item-total r=0.43–0.71).

Predictor Variables Mental health literacy was assessed using the Mental Health Literacy Scale (MHLS),23 adapted for the Nigerian Muslim-Christian context through a three-stage process: forward-back translation into Yoruba; expert review by three bilingual community informants (one Islamic scholar, one Christian pastor, one community mental health worker); and cognitive debriefing with eight community members. Two items were reworded for local comprehensibility, and the adapted scale demonstrated acceptable reliability (α=0.79). Attitudes were measured via the Community Attitude Towards Mental Illness Scale (CAMI-16) Benevolence subscale.24 Sociodemographic variables included education, age, gender, religious tradition, prior mental health training and practice location.

Behavioural Outcomes. Referral frequency was self-reported ordinally over the preceding 12 months and dichotomised for logistic regression (never vs at least once). Treatment preferences were assessed using two clinical vignettes depicting depression and psychosis25 (online supplemental file SF2).

Barrier Assessment Participants selected their single most significant barrier from an eight-item checklist, subsequently collapsed into four categories: structural, ideological, social and trust-related (online supplemental file SF2).

Healthcare provider survey

Mental health professionals completed a structured instrument comprising Likert-scale collaboration willingness items and open-ended written questions on perceived benefits, concerns and prior collaboration experiences (online supplemental file SF3). These written responses constituted one of the two qualitative data sources.

Qualitative component

Two focus group discussions (FG1: n=8; FG2: n=7) were conducted with 15 religious leaders drawn from the original religious leader survey sample and purposively stratified by latent class membership to ensure representation of all readiness orientations. The group size was selected to support interactive discussion while remaining small enough to manage sensitive topics, religious diversity and potential status differences. Participants ranged in age from 32 to 68 years (mean: 47.3, SD=10.8); 11 were male and 4 female; 9 were Christian and 6 Muslim; 10 were urban and 5 rural or periurban. Of 22 approached, seven declined (six citing time constraints; one without reason). Sessions were held at participants’ places of worship in English and Yoruba, facilitated by a female bilingual psychiatrist with 4 years of community qualitative research experience (AL), with a second researcher serving as note-taker (OEO). Neither facilitator had a prior relationship with participants. Sessions lasted 75–90 min, were audio-recorded with consent and transcribed verbatim. Field notes supplemented audio data. No non-participants were present at any session. The complete discussion guide is provided in online supplemental file SF4.

Research team and reflexivity

The research team’s biomedical institutional identity may have introduced social desirability pressure. Facilitators explicitly framed discussions as exploratory and non-evaluative, actively encouraging reservations and barriers as openly as positive orientations. Potential power differentials related to age, gender, religious authority and denominational hierarchy were considered during group composition and facilitation. The facilitator invited quieter participants to speak, avoided ranking clerical authority and treated divergent theological positions as legitimate data rather than as errors to be corrected. Independent double-coding provided a further safeguard against interpretive bias shaped by professional investment in demonstrating collaboration potential.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics, independent t-tests and χ² tests were computed for all variables. Multiple linear regression identified predictors of the continuous readiness score with standardised beta (β) coefficients. Binary logistic regression estimated odds of referral behaviour, reported as ORs with 95% CIs. SEM was estimated using R (V.4.2) with the lavaan package,26 with model fit assessed via Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). Indirect effects were estimated using bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals (5000 resamples). LCA was conducted using the poLCA package in R, evaluating two-to-six class solutions; the optimal solution was selected on Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), entropy and theoretical interpretability. Qualitative transcripts and open-ended provider responses were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase reflexive thematic analysis.27 Two researchers (AL and OEO) coded independently; all discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with AOA arbitrating unresolved disagreements. Analysis was conducted manually using a structured coding matrix. Themes were triangulated with quantitative findings to provide an integrated understanding of the implementation context.

Results

Levels and distribution of collaboration readiness

The mean collaboration readiness score for the 207 religious leaders was 25.8 (SD=7.1) on a scale of 8–40, indicating moderate overall willingness to partner with the formal health system. Significant heterogeneity was observed: 28.5% demonstrated low readiness (8–18), 48.3% moderate (19–29) and 23.2% high (30–40), with 71.5% in the moderate-to-high range overall. Item-level analysis revealed that willingness to attend mental health training was highest (78.3%), while trust in professionals (43.5%) and perceived respect from clinicians (39.1%) were the lowest-endorsed items, an early signal of the trust and relational dynamics that the qualitative data would subsequently illuminate. Readiness was significantly stratified by religious tradition (Christians M=27.3, SD=6.4; Muslims M=23.6, SD=7.2; t(205)=3.89, p<0.001), education level (p<0.001) and geographic location (p<0.05). Predictors of readiness and referral behaviour

Multiple regression explained 64% of variance in readiness scores (R²=0.64, p<0.001; table 2). Mental health literacy emerged as the strongest independent predictor (β=0.41, p<0.001), followed by education level (β=0.26, p<0.001) and prior mental health training (β=0.23, p<0.001). Positive attitudes (CAMI-Benevolence β=0.19, p<0.01), Christian denomination (β=0.16, p<0.01) and younger age (β=−0.14, p<0.05) were also significant. These findings require a differentiated interpretation of modifiability: mental health literacy and prior training are directly intervention-sensitive, whereas education level is not readily modifiable in the short term but may help identify leaders who need more intensive training, supervision or simplified referral tools.

Overall, 48.8% of respondents had never made a medical referral for a mental health concern in the preceding 12 months: the quantitative expression of the collaboration paradox. Binary logistic regression found that each one-point readiness increase raised referral odds by 12% (OR=1.12, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.17, p<0.001). University education compared with primary (OR=3.45, 95% CI 1.52 to 7.83, p<0.01) and membership in the collaborative-medical practice cluster compared with spiritual-exclusive (OR=4.73, 95% CI 2.18 to 10.27, p<0.001) also significantly predicted referral (table 2).

SEM: Testing the pathway to collaboration

The SEM demonstrated excellent fit (χ²(24)=31.2, p=0.14; CFI=0.96; TLI=0.94; RMSEA=0.04 90% CI 0.00 to 0.07; figure 2). Education predicted increased mental health literacy (β=0.38), which directly predicted higher readiness (β=0.35) and more benevolent attitudes (β=0.44). Attitudes independently predicted readiness (β=0.23), and readiness strongly predicted referral behaviour (β=0.67). Education’s effect on readiness was significantly mediated through MHLS (indirect effect=0.13, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.20), supporting a sequential knowledge-attitude-behaviour pathway while also indicating that pathway activation depends on service access, trust, and role clarity. A secondary pathway via attitudes was also significant (indirect effect=0.10, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.17).

Structural barriers were dominant (65.2%), making this class the most strategically important for scaled intervention.

Class 3 - Reluctantly Engaged (n=61, 29.5%): Low-to-moderate readiness (M=20.3, SD=2.9), with low professional trust (M=2.3/5.0) and theological ambivalence as defining features, despite some interest in training (M=3.1/5.0). Only 42.6% had ever referred. Ideological barriers were more prominent (29.5%) than in higher classes, suggesting knowledge-based intervention alone will be insufficient without prior trust-building.

Qualitative themes: barriers from the field

Focus group participants (FG1: n=8; FG2: n=7) had a mean age of 47.3 years (SD=10.8, range: 32–68); 11 were male and 4 female; 9 were Christian and 6 Muslim; 10 were urban and 5 rural or periurban. Participants were drawn from the original survey cohort and from across all four latent classes to ensure diversity of readiness perspectives. Thematic analysis of focus group transcripts and open-ended healthcare provider survey responses identified four primary themes, presented below in order of frequency. Together, these themes illuminate the mechanisms sustaining the collaboration paradox identified in the quantitative data.

Theme 1 - Structural Constraints (cited by 13/15 focus group participants): The most pervasively cited barrier was neither theological nor attitudinal but material. Across denominational lines and readiness classes, participants described a formal mental health system whose physical distance, financial cost and functional unreliability rendered referral practically impossible regardless of individual willingness, a pattern that maps directly onto Class 2’s ‘willing but unable’ profile. A rural imam stated:

We don’t have clinics to send them to. And even when we do, families complain it’s too far or too expensive. I have referred people. They come back and tell me the bus fare alone was more than they had. So what did I achieve by referring them? (Male imam, rural, FG1)

A periurban pastor extended this to the temporal dimension of inaccessibility:

Even when I refer them, they come back because the hospital told them to come back in three months. What am I supposed to tell the family in those three months? I need somewhere that can actually receive them, not just put them on a waiting list. (Female pastor, peri-urban, FG2)

The subtheme of medication unaffordability was particularly prominent, with leaders describing treatment discontinuation as a recurrent outcome that damaged not only the referral pathway but their own pastoral standing:

I sent a man for treatment. He got better on the medication. Then the money ran out and he stopped. He got worse again. The family blamed me for sending him. Now I think twice before I refer anyone. (Male pastor, urban, FG2)

Theme 2 - Ideological Conflict (cited by 9/15 participants): Most leaders articulated pluralistic rather than exclusively spiritual frameworks. Biomedical referral was conceptually permissible, but contingent on spiritual precedence, a hierarchical causation model in which divine agency occupies a foundational explanatory position while biomedical mechanisms address proximate pathways:

Prayer should always come first. Medicine is plan B, but only if the spirit agrees. The spiritual is the root. If you treat only the branch, the problem will return. (Male pastor, FG1)

A female pastor from the Conditionally Ready class described explicit triage criteria governing the decision to refer:

I refer when I see that the spiritual intervention is not working after a reasonable time. If there is violence, if the person cannot function, if they are hearing things… those are signs for me that this is a hospital matter. I still pray for them. But I add the doctor. (Female pastor, urban, FG2)

Importantly, denominational heterogeneity in ideological positioning was evident, challenging any homogeneous characterisation of the religious leader population:

In my church we were taught that medicine and prayer go together. My bishop has said this publicly. But I know pastors in other churches who would say I am wrong. You cannot design one programme for all of us. (Male Anglican leader, FG1)

Theme 3 - Trust Barriers (cited by 10/15 participants): A third and analytically distinct barrier concerned the relational quality of the interface between religious leaders and the formal health system. Accounts converged on a perception of epistemic disrespect (that mental health professionals did not honour the role, knowledge or authority of religious leaders), frequently grounded in specific reported clinical encounters:

They (psychiatrists) don’t understand our beliefs. They just want to take over everything. One of my members went to a hospital and the doctor said that praying instead of taking his tablets was what made him sick. Whether that is true or not, that is not how you speak to a Muslim man. After that, four people refused my referral. (Male imam, FG1)

A female pastor described the institutionalised deterrence effect that radiates outward from such encounters:

When my members come back and report that the doctor laughed at them for fasting or praying, what do I do with that? I cannot send more people into that. Collaboration sounds good in a conference room. In my community, it means sending people to be judged. (Female pastor, Reluctantly Engaged class, FG2)

The conditions for trust were articulated most clearly by a pastor who described a desired but currently absent form of mutual legitimation:

What I want is a doctor who comes to me and says: ‘Pastor, I know you see these people first. Let us work together.’ Not a doctor who sees me as the problem to be solved before the real treatment can begin. If they come to us as partners, we will respond as partners. (Male pastor, urban, FG2)

Theme 4 - Conditional Openness Healthcare Providers (cited by 18/25 providers): Mental health professionals expressed broadly collaborative orientations qualified by patient safety concerns and scope boundary requirements. This conditionality was not simply cautious; it contained genuine recognition of religious leaders' community assets:

We’re open to working with clerics, but not if they insist on chaining or fasting people as treatment. That’s where we draw the line. Within those boundaries, I think they can do something we cannot do…they can reach people before the illness becomes severe. (Psychiatrist, survey response)

A clinical psychologist articulated the differential impact of faith-based involvement on treatment engagement:

I have patients who stopped taking medication because their pastor told them it showed lack of faith. And I have others who came initially because their imam referred them…without judgment, without stigma. The difference in treatment engagement is remarkable. (Clinical psychologist, survey response)

The most consequential subtheme was providers’ acknowledgement of their own preparedness gaps, a finding that reframes the collaboration challenge as genuinely bidirectional:

I was never taught how to talk with pastors or imams, or how to include someone’s faith without undermining treatment. That is a skill gap on our side that nobody talks about. (Clinical psychologist, survey response)

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

Taken together, the quantitative and qualitative findings reveal that collaboration readiness is substantial but heterogeneous, shaped by interacting literacy, structural, ideological and relational factors. Structural constraints were dominant across all four LCA classes, including the Implementation Ready class (78.9%), confirming this as a cross-cutting challenge not amenable to attitudinal intervention alone. Ideological and trust barriers were concentrated in Classes 3 and 4, consistent with their lower MHLS and CAMI-Benevolence scores. The bidirectional knowledge gap identified in provider responses directly extends the quantitative finding that trust in professionals (M=2.7/5.0) and perceived professional respect (M=2.5/5.0) were the two lowest-scoring readiness items overall. The four latent profiles correspond to distinct constellations of these factors: highly literate, trained leaders who already enact complementary models (class 1); leaders who are willing but unable because of system-level barriers (class 2); leaders constrained by stigma and relational ambivalence (class 3) and leaders whose resistance is rooted in spiritual-exclusive models and mistrust (class 4). This integrated mapping provides the empirical foundation for the stratified implementation approach proposed in the Discussion. Discussion

Overview

This mixed-methods investigation provides a systematic, implementation-focused assessment of Nigerian religious leaders’ readiness to collaborate with formal mental health services. The findings reveal a landscape of considerable but unevenly distributed potential. While 71.5% demonstrated moderate-to-high readiness, this willingness did not consistently translate into referral behaviour, with 48.8% having never referred. This study advances the field by moving beyond descriptive accounts of clergy beliefs toward a more nuanced understanding of readiness as a multidimensional construct with direct implications for implementation, confirming that the gap between willingness and action reflects systemic and relational failure rather than theological opposition.

The know-do gap: systemic rather than theological

The disconnect between stated willingness and observed referral is consistent with what implementation science describes as the ‘know-do gap’, whereby positive attitudes fail to generate corresponding practice in the absence of enabling structural conditions.1 The qualitative data illuminate its specific mechanisms in Lagos: leaders who expressed genuine willingness consistently described encountering a health system that was geographically inaccessible, financially prohibitive and functionally unreliable. This finding reinforces longstanding global mental health concerns that expanding community demand for care must be matched by adequate service provision.8 Without accessible referral pathways, even highly motivated actors remain unable to translate willingness into action, a structural reality captured precisely in the class 2 profile.

Theoretical contributions

The SEM provides empirical support for the knowledge-attitude-behaviour pathway implied by the TPB.18 Education improves collaboration behaviour primarily through increased mental health literacy, which in turn fosters attitudinal benevolence and directly enhances readiness. This sequential mediated pathway is consistent with broader evidence on mental health literacy as a primary driver of help-seeking behaviour,28 but the results should not be read as implying that all predictors are equally modifiable. Literacy and training exposure can be targeted directly through intervention, while educational background is better understood as a stratification variable for tailoring training intensity, format and follow-up support.

However, the persistence of the referral gap despite high readiness among a substantial minority suggests that the TPB’s perceived behavioural control construct requires expansion in LMIC structural constraint contexts. Where the absence of accessible services physically prevents referral, individual agency is superseded by systemic failure.18 The CFIR better captures these outer setting determinants,15 29 and the integration of both frameworks appears necessary for implementation planning in comparable settings. More broadly, the prominence of ideological and epistemic tensions in the qualitative data suggests that CFIR’s standard organisational focus may inadequately capture the theological and identity-based constraints that operate within religious institutions, an important limitation for implementation researchers applying this framework to faith-based settings.

The qualitative findings additionally challenge the assumption of theological opposition as the primary collaboration barrier. Most leaders employed pluralistic hierarchical or dual-track explanatory models that accommodate biomedical care under specific conditions, rather than categorically rejecting collaboration.30 This mirrors findings from comparable African contexts,31 32 and implies that effective programmes must engage with existing triage logics rather than attempt to replace them.10

Bidirectionality of the collaboration challenge

A critical and underappreciated finding is that collaboration barriers are genuinely bidirectional. Healthcare providers described systematic lack of religious literacy as a personal preparedness gap, including discomfort initiating faith-related discussions and uncertainty about incorporating spiritual dimensions into clinical care, that is as consequential as religious leaders’ mental health knowledge deficits. This implies that programmes focused exclusively on building religious leaders’ competencies will produce asymmetric and ultimately unsustainable partnerships.10 33 Individual clinical encounters in which congregants’ faith was pathologised rippled outward to affect entire congregations’ engagement with formal services, a trust economy that is as much at risk as the referral pathway itself.

A stratified implementation strategy

The LCA-derived typology provides an empirically grounded basis for the stratified approach proposed here. Rather than applying a uniform strategy, implementation science increasingly recognises that different actors require different forms and intensities of support tailored to their specific readiness orientation.20 34 This approach is consistent with Rogers’ adopter categories,35 with differentiated service delivery models from HIV programming in sub-Saharan African LMIC contexts providing direct empirical precedent for the effectiveness of tiered strategies.16 36 Figure 4 translates these empirical results into a stratified implementation model linking readiness determinants, LCA-derived profiles, differentiated strategies, bidirectional provider engagement and cross-cutting policy prerequisites.

Three immediate policy priorities follow. First, comprehensive service mapping and formal referral protocols linking religious leaders to primary healthcare facilities are foundational prerequisites for any collaboration programme.9 Second, a standardised national mental health training curriculum co-produced with diverse faith communities, underpinned by Nigeria’s Mental Health Act 2021, is both evidence-supported and legislatively timely.12 41 Third, formal regulatory frameworks specifying roles, ethical boundaries and mutual accountability mechanisms are needed to protect patient safety and provide religious leaders with the institutional recognition they require as a condition of sustained engagement.10 40

Limitations

The cross-sectional design precludes definitive causal inference. Self-report measures introduce social desirability risk, mitigated but not eliminated by the reflexivity procedures implemented. Findings are geographically confined to Lagos State; generalisability to other parts of Nigeria and rural contexts with different religious compositions and healthcare infrastructure profiles remains uncertain.42 The healthcare professional sample (N=25) is insufficient for robust inferential analysis of provider-side predictors. Service user perspectives, essential for fully co-designed implementation, are absent and represent a priority for subsequent work. Objective referral verification against facility records was not feasible; future studies should seek to link self-reported data with clinical attendance records.

Conclusions

This investigation confirms substantial, modifiable collaboration readiness among Nigerian religious leaders, while illuminating the structural and relational mechanisms preventing this readiness from consistently translating into referral behaviour. The collaboration gap is primarily a systemic and relational failure rather than a theological one. A phased, stratified and bidirectionally conceived implementation strategy (simultaneously addressing religious leaders’ knowledge, clinicians’ religious literacy, structural service availability and mutual relational trust) offers the most promising pathway toward sustainable faith-health partnerships capable of contributing meaningfully to bridging Nigeria’s profound mental health treatment gap.43
