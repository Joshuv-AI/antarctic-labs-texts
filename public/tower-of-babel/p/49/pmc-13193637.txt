Pseudo-Tumoral Lesions in Subacute Sclerosing Panencephalitis: A Case Series
Authors: Lekshmi S Hema, Farsana Mustafa, Ayush Agarwal, Archita Makharia, Ajay Garg, Achal K Srivastava, Divyani Garg
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that primarily affects children and adolescents. The pathogenesis is attributed to the persistent infection of the brain by mutated measles virus strains, in which genetic alterations in viral proteins allow immune evasion and direct cell-to-cell spread.[1] The diagnosis relies on Dyken’s criteria, with histopathological or molecular confirmation reserved for atypical or inconclusive cases.[2]

The radiological spectrum of SSPE typically includes asymmetric periventricular or subcortical white matter hyperintensities, predominantly involving the parieto-occipital regions, which progress to confluent lesions with atrophy as the disease advances.[3] Here, we report two cases of SSPE showing atypical pseudotumoral magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) lesions. This is an uncommon radiological variant that can closely mimic demyelinating, neoplastic, or infectious pathologies, thereby posing significant diagnostic challenges. Our observations expand the known neuroimaging spectrum of SSPE.

A 15-year-old male presented with a 4-month history of recurrent falls. Approximately 1 month after symptom onset, he developed a progressive decline in scholastic performance and calculation ability, which worsened over the subsequent 3 months. This was followed by a reduction in speech output over the preceding 2 months. During the same period, he became completely bedbound and dependent for all activities of daily living. He had not been immunized against measles and had no known history of measles infection.

On examination, he was mute, precluding a detailed cognitive assessment. Generalized myoclonus with a slow relaxation phase was observed. MRI of the brain revealed a mass-like T2/ fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) hyperintensity involving the right parieto-occipital region, splenium of the corpus callosum, right parahippocampal and hippocampal gyri, right cingulate, and left temporal lobe, with focal diffusion restriction in the splenium. No contrast enhancement was observed [Figure 1]. Electroencephalography (EEG) revealed generalized slowing with long-interval generalized periodic complexes.

A 22-year-old male presented with a 6-month history of progressive visual impairment, seizures, cognitive decline, and myoclonic jerks involving the right upper and lower limbs. He was unvaccinated but had no history of measles infection. He had deficits in temporal, occipital, and parietal lobe functions. Fundus examination showed acute neuroretinitis. He exhibited myoclonic jerks involving the right upper and lower limbs, right diaphragmatic myoclonus, and tremors. MRI of the brain showed multifocal T2/FLAIR hyperintensities involving the bilateral middle cerebellar peduncles (left > right), right temporo-parieto-occipital region, splenium, right thalamus, and bilateral frontal and parietal lobes, with associated mass effect and midline shift. Multiple foci of diffusion restriction were seen, with no contrast enhancement [Figure 2]. Follow-up MRI at 6 months showed an increase in lesion size. MR spectroscopy was suggestive of low N-acetylaspartate and creatinine and increased choline, lipid, and lactate peaks. EEG showed long-interval periodic discharges.

We undertook an extensive infective workup in both patients, including serology for hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV); Lyme serology; serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) Venereal Diseases Research Laboratory (VDRL); CSF pan-viral polymerase chain reaction; mycobacterial evaluation with acid-fast bacilli smear, Ziehl–Neelsen staining, GeneXpert, and cultures; CSF Gram stain; bacterial and fungal cultures; latex agglutination; and India ink staining, which was noncontributory. CSF analysis showed elevated measles-specific IgG antibody levels, reported as a sample dilution of 625 by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, confirming the diagnosis of SSPE.

Both patients were initiated on weekly intrathecal interferon-alpha and oral isoprinosine. On short-term follow-up at 3 months, patient 1 showed no further neurological deterioration. Despite therapy, patient 2’s disease progressed, with worsening lesion size and mass effect, and he succumbed after 9 months. Neuroimaging abnormalities have been reported in approximately 75% of patients in Jabbour Stage 2 and up to 95% in Stage 3 of SSPE.[4] While the classical imaging pattern consists of asymmetric hyperintensities in the parieto-occipital white matter, several atypical patterns include (1) small focal lesions measuring less than 5 mm; (2) large confluent or tumefactive lesions; (3) symmetrical involvement of the thalami; (4) hemorrhagic encephalitic appearance; and (5) diffuse, bilateral, nonenhancing white-matter hyperintensities resembling a pseudo-leukodystrophy-like pattern.[56] The latter pattern resembled acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM). Panda et al.[5] described a 13-year-old male with SSPE presenting with ADEM-like features and pseudo-leukodystrophic MRI changes, characterized by widespread white matter involvement. Similarly, Manek et al.[7] reported fulminant SSPE with an initially normal MRI that evolved into extensive asymmetric parieto-occipital and thalamic signal abnormalities, with cranial nerve enhancement and venous sinus thrombosis.

Our cases further expand the neuroimaging spectrum of SSPE by demonstrating pseudotumoral (tumefactive) lesions, an uncommon presentation that poses significant diagnostic challenges. Such lesions on MRI can mimic a broad range of inflammatory, demyelinating, neoplastic, and infectious etiologies. Important differential diagnoses include tumefactive demyelination (such as ADEM or tumefactive multiple sclerosis), high-grade gliomas, primary central nervous system lymphoma, and infectious etiologies including tuberculoma and progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy.[8] Notably, a recent cross-sectional study by Garg et al.[9] identified mass-like or pseudotumoral lesions in 2 of 43 patients (4.7%), indicating that such atypical imaging patterns, though uncommon, are part of the broader radiological spectrum of SSPE. Our observation aligns with previous reports that tumefactive or fulminant variants of SSPE may exhibit active demyelination and cerebral edema.[56] MRI is an integral part of the workup in SSPE, but atypical pseudotumoral lesions can make diagnosis challenging.

Recognizing uncommon imaging patterns is crucial to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary interventions in patients with SSPE.
