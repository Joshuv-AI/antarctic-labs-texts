The contributions of genetic diversity to the performance of sun-cured tobacco
Authors: Dursun Kurt, İbrahim Saygili, Ahmet Kinay, Erdem Karakoc
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetic diversity is a fundamental component in improving yield potential, quality, and adaptability in economically important crops such as tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.). Turkish sun-cured tobaccos, renowned worldwide for their distinctive aromatic profiles, are cultivated across diverse agroecological regions of Türkiye using genetically heterogeneous local genotypes. This study aimed to characterize the genetic diversity among Turkish sun-cured tobacco landraces using SSR markers and to elucidate its association on agronomic and chemical traits across major production regions. For this purpose, a total of 331 genotypes were collected from three major tobacco-growing regions. To assess genetic diversity at the DNA level, molecular characterization of the genotypes was conducted using nine SSR markers. All SSR markers were polymorphic and generated between 3 and 6 alleles per locus. Among the 331 genotypes analyzed, 259 were identified as genetically unique, including 146 from İzmir, 71 from Samsun, and 42 from Tokat. A dendrogram constructed from the SSR data revealed that most genotypes clustered according to their respective tobacco production regions. Distinct regional variations were observed among the studied materials; specifically, Izmir genotypes excelled in reducing sugar content, Samsun genotypes in dry leaf yield and nicotine levels, and Basma genotypes in both dry leaf yield and reducing sugar accumulation. Based on dry leaf yield and regional adaptation traits, 19% of the İzmir genotypes, 24% of the Samsun genotypes, and 18% of the Tokat genotypes outperformed the commonly cultivated commercial varieties in their respective regions. The pronounced regional differences in sun-cured tobacco quality largely stem from unique landraces, each genetically adapted and named after their region of origin. These findings show the fundamental role of genetic diversity in sustaining and enhancing sun-cured tobacco production and affirm its contribution to the continued cultivation of distinctive Turkish oriental tobaccos.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08505-x.Keywords: Characterization, Microsatellite, Nicotiana tabacum, Oriental tobacco, Variation

Introduction

Tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.) is a non-food industrial crop of substantial economic importance, with a global market value estimated at approximately 980 billion USD. Sun-cured (oriental) tobaccos account for around 4% of global tobacco production, and more than half of this production is concentrated in Türkiye [4]. In Türkiye, the cultivation of oriental tobacco spans diverse ecological regions, primarily utilizing landraces and ecotypes, alongside a limited number of commercial cultivars [39]. Turkish sun-cured tobacco varieties are largely considered as landraces, traditionally maintained by independent growers and families through decadal cycle of seed collection. This localized preservation strategy, which is particularly distinctive to oriental and cigar-type tobaccos, has facilitated significant regional adaptation and the long-term maintenance of high genetic diversity within the germplasm. The unique interaction between the region-specific climatic conditions [27] and the genetically diverse plant materials contributes to the distinctive quality of Turkish-type sun-cured tobacco, which is globally recognized for its desirable agronomic traits and unique chemical composition [29].

Despite its economic significance and unique characteristics, the long-term sustainability of tobacco production, particularly for traditional varieties, faces challenges. Modern agricultural practices and breeding efforts, while enhancing productivity, have inadvertently led to a narrowing of the genetic base in many cultivated plants, including tobacco [35]. This genetic erosion can compromise the resilience of crops to emerging environmental stresses, pests, and diseases, and limit future breeding potential. Therefore, understanding and preserving the genetic diversity within existing tobacco germplasm is crucial for developing new cultivars that are adaptable, high-yielding, and maintain desirable quality attributes in the face of changing climatic conditions and evolving market demands.

Genetic diversity underpins biodiversity conservation, sustains evolutionary processes, and constitutes a critical resource for enhancing the efficiency of plant breeding programs. The assessment of diversity typically involves molecular, morphological, agronomic, and biochemical approaches. In tobacco, molecular markers such as RAPD, SSRs, AFLPs, iPBS, and SNPs have been widely employed to characterize genetic variation at the DNA level [1, 31, 32, 37, 44]. These molecular techniques are considered highly reliable, reproducible, and practical for detecting genetic variation [29, 30, 38]. However, for practical application in breeding and cultivation strategies, integrating molecular data with comprehensive agronomic characterization provides a more holistic and meaningful understanding of genetic resources strategies [26, 36, 42].

Effective assessment of genetic diversity in large populations, especially those collected from natural habitats or cultivation areas, is often constrained by resource limitations and labor-intensive methodologies. To manage the number of lines analyzed while still capturing adequate genetic variation, pre-elimination of genetically similar lines using DNA markers can be advantageous for field-based trials and applied research [29, 37]. Furthermore, robust statistical tools are essential for comprehensively evaluating a large number of genotypes and understanding the genetic structure and variation within a population. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) facilitates the visualization of relationships between genotypes and traits, while dendrograms offer a practical representation of genetic distances or similarities among genotypes [29, 39]. These tools are highly effective for clustering genotypes based on their geographic origins or breeding histories.

Modern breeding efforts, while focused on improving performance, have significantly narrowed the genetic base of cultivated plants [37]. The development of new, trait-specific varieties through allelic studies, hybridization, and selection is only feasible through the effective characterization and utilization of existing genetic variation [29].Türkiye, which harbors nearly the entire plant biodiversity found across the European continent, also exhibits a comparable level of genetic diversity in tobacco germplasm [25]. Despite the historical and economic importance of Turkish sun-cured tobacco, a comprehensive characterization that bridges the gap between SSR-based genetic diversity and specific agronomic-chemical performance remains limited. Understanding this relationship is crucial for modern breeding programs and the preservation of regional landraces. Understanding this relationship is essential for the effective management of these genetic resources and for future breeding efforts. To address this need, the present study aimed to characterize the genetic diversity of Turkish sun cured tobacco using SSR markers and to evaluate how this diversity relates to key agronomic and chemical traits across major production regions.

Material and method

Plant material

In Türkiye, sun-cured (oriental) tobacco production areas are classified into three major types, Izmir, Samsun, and Tokat (Basma), based on the distinct characteristics of their landraces (Fig. 1). To account for the genetic heterogeneity inherent in farmer-held landraces, a total of 331 tobacco accessions were collected from these three primary production zones: 201 from Izmir, 86 from Samsun, and 44 from Tokat. Rather than sampling a single individual per landrace, multiple plants displaying distinct morphological and phenological characteristics were selected to capture the internal variability of each population.

Sun-cured tobacco growing areas in Türkiye

The flowers of these selected plants were bagged to prevent cross-pollination, and selfed seeds were harvested upon maturity to ensure genetic purity for further analysis. The plant material also included representative registered cultivars for comparison: Birlik-124, Birlik-128, and Özbaş for İzmir; Canik, Sıtmasuyu, and Nail for Samsun; and Xanthi-2A, Xanthi-81, and Prilep for the Basma (Tokat) region.

Simple sequence repeat (SSR) marker analysis

For the genetic characterization of these 331 accessions, nine SSR markers were selected based on their documented high allelic richness and robust discriminatory power [29, 30, 32, 39]. These specific loci were chosen following an initial screening of 21 SSR markers, as they exhibited superior Polymorphic Information Content (PIC) and provided sufficient resolution to distinguish between closely related landraces. Previous studies have validated that this marker set effectively resolves intra-specific utilization groups and is highly efficient in identifying genetic redundancies [36, 39]. Detailed information on the SSR markers [6, 7, 41] used in this study is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Genomic DNA was extracted from tobacco plants at the 4–5 leaf stage using the Turkuaz DNA Isolation Kit [23]. The quality of extracted DNA was assessed via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometric analysis. Any insufficient or degraded DNA samples were re-isolated, and all final DNA concentrations were adjusted to 50 ng/µl. Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) was conducted in a 30 µl reaction volume containing 250 nM of each primer, 0.2 mM of each dNTP, 1.5 mM MgCl₂, 1 µl of genomic DNA, and 1 unit of Taq DNA polymerase [19]. The thermal cycling conditions included an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at either 55 °C or 60 °C depending on the primer (Table S1) for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final elongation step at 72 °C for 5 min.

Amplified products were separated on a 4% MetaPhor agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide and visualized using a UV gel documentation system. Alleles were scored using Biocapt software version 11.02. Alleles with a frequency below 5% were classified as rare [14]. Field experiments

The landraces were subjected to field trials in their respective regions of origin, alongside standard cultivars, to assess their agronomic performance. These trials were conducted in 2020 using an augmented design across three major sun-cured tobacco production regions. Due to the extensive number of genotypes and limited seed availability, the field experiment was established using an Augmented Block Design. This design allows for the evaluation of a large number of unreplicated entries by using replicated check varieties across blocks to estimate experimental error and environmental variance. The phenotypic data for each landrace were adjusted for block effects using the means of the replicated checks, following the standard linear model for augmented designs. In each region, the genotypes were divided into six blocks. The standard cultivars were repeated three times and placed randomly within the blocks. İzmir-type genotypes were evaluated in Oğlananası village of Menderes district, İzmir (38°13′59" N, 27°13′39" E, 125 m); Basma-type genotypes in Tanoba village, Erbaa district, Tokat (40°38′40" N, 36°24′04" E, 583 m); and Samsun-type genotypes in Aktekke village, Bafra district, Samsun (41°32′07" N, 35°53′16" E, 86 m).

During the growing season, the average temperatures in all trial sites were approximately 1 °C higher than the long-term averages (Table S2). The mean temperature recorded was highest in İzmir/Menderes (23.0 °C), followed by Samsun/Bafra (18.4 °C), and Tokat/Erbaa (18.3 °C). Total precipitation during the growing season was 281.2 mm in Samsun/Bafra, 231.1 mm in Tokat/Erbaa, and 190.4 mm in İzmir/Menderes. Compared to long-term averages, rainfall in 2020 decreased by 53% in Samsun/Bafra and 24% in Tokat/Erbaa, while it increased by 17% in İzmir/Menderes. The soils of the Samsun and Tokat trial sites are characterized by a sandy-loam texture, low phosphorus and organic matter content, calcareous, and slightly alkaline (Table 1). In Tokat, the extractable potassium level was adequate, whereas zinc content was low, and the soil was slightly saline. In contrast, the Samsun site soils were low in potassium but had sufficient zinc levels, with a high degree of salinity. The İzmir location featured a clay-loam texture with sufficient levels of phosphorus, potassium, and zinc. The soil also had moderate organic matter and lime content, was neutral, and highly saline.

The soil characteristics of the sites

Landraces originating from the same region and found to be genetically indistinguishable through DNA analysis were excluded from field trials. A total of 259 genetically distinct genotypes, 146 from İzmir, 71 from Samsun, and 42 from Tokat, as well as standard reference cultivars, were propagated using the float system in peat-based media. For nutrient supplementation, a solution containing 100 mg/L of pure nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), along with a micronutrient mixture including 0.4% Fe, 0.4% Mn, and 0.4% Zn, was applied to float trays.

During seedling development, pest and disease control measures were implemented, and the seedlings were trimmed four times to promote uniform growth. Once seedlings reached appropriate physiological maturity, they were transplanted to the field. Each experimental plot consisted of 42 plants spaced at 45 cm between rows and 12 cm within rows. Prior to transplanting, base fertilization was applied at a rate of 6 kg/da N, 4 kg/da P₂O₅, and 6 kg/da K₂O. Throughout the trial, routine agronomic practices, including weed control and integrated pest management, were consistently carried out.

Plots that reached optimal harvest maturity were hand-harvested in three successive rounds. All collected leaves were cured using the traditional sun-cured method. Morphological measurements, including plant height (PLH), number of leaves (NoL), leaf width (LeW), and leaf length (LeL), were conducted on the second main stalk position (middle priming) of each plant. Dry leaf yield (YLD) was calculated based on sun-cured leaves at a standardized moisture content of 15%. Leaf quality was assessed using the Quality Grade Index (QGI) according to the American grading system [38]. Chemical analyses were performed on dry leaf samples to determine nicotine content (NIC) following the “CORESTA Recommended Method No. 85” [3] and reducing sugar content (RES) based on the “CORESTA Recommended Method No. 38” [2]. Both analyses were conducted using continuous flow analysis at the chemical testing laboratory of Öz-Ege Tobacco Industry and Trade Inc.

Statistical analysis

Genetic distances among landraces were calculated using Nei’s genetic distance coefficient in PopGen version 1.31 software [45]. A dendrogram was then constructed based on these distances using the UPGMA (Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean) clustering algorithm in MEGA software (version 4).

To evaluate the significance of the observed variation, agronomic and chemical data were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) adapted for the Augmented Design framework using JMP v.13.0. software. Experimental error was estimated through the replicated control varieties within the augmented blocks. For the validation of differences among landraces and to support our characterization, Least Significant Difference (LSD) tests were employed as a post-hoc measure, with results provided in the supplementary material (Table S4). To visualize the relationships among landraces and traits, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Biplot representations were performed using XLSTAT software (v.2016, Addinsoft, NY).

Results

Genetic diversity

A total of 40 alleles were detected across nine SSR markers used to assess DNA-level genetic diversity among 331 genotypes and nine commercial cultivars, all belonging to Nicotiana tabacum L. (Table S1). The number of alleles per marker was as follows: three alleles for PT30274 and PT30137; four for PT61056, TM10013, and TM10821; five for PT20242, PT10654, and PT30034; and six for TM10181. Rare alleles (frequency < 5%) were observed in TM10181 (3 alleles), TM10013 and PT20242 (2 alleles each), and PT30034, PT61056, and TM10821 (1 allele each). No rare alleles were detected in the remaining markers. The polymorphism information content (PIC) values of the markers ranged from 0.350 (TM10821) to 0.708 (PT30034). The other PIC values were as follows: PT10654: 0.513, PT20242: 0.542, PT30137: 0.561, PT61056: 0.574, PT30274: 0.587, TM10181: 0.654, and TM10013: 0.697. The number of heterozygous genotypes per marker was as follows: one for PT61056, two for PT20242 and TM10821, three for PT30274 and PT30137, and seven for TM10013 and PT30034. No heterozygotes were detected in the remaining markers.

DNA analysis revealed that 259 out of the 331 genotypes examined were genetically unique. These included 146 İzmir-type, 71 Samsun-type, and 42 Basma-type genotypes, each possessing at least one distinct allele at a given locus. A dendrogram constructed from SSR marker data delineated six distinct genetic clusters (Fig. 2). The cluster 1 (genotypes B31 to A118, indicated by blue lines) predominantly consisted of Basma-type tobaccos, designated by the initial "B" in their identifiers. These genotypes exhibited clear genetic divergence from those of other regions. The registered Basma-type cultivar Xanthi-2A was also clustered within this group. The cluster 2 (genotypes B8 to A1, indicated by orange lines) primarily included İzmir-type tobaccos (designated by the prefix "A"). These genotypes formed a distinct and coherent cluster, suggesting regional genetic uniformity. The cluster 3 (genotypes A19 to B5, indicated by purple lines) comprised both Samsun (designated by "C") and Basma-type tobaccos. Within this cluster, Samsun and Basma genotypes formed separate sub-clusters, highlighting their underlying genetic differentiation despite geographic proximity. The cluster 4 (genotypes C18–A10, indicated by gray lines) included a small number of İzmir-type tobaccos. The cluster 5 (genotypes B39–A28, red lines) represented a mixed cluster of Basma and Samsun types. This group also included registered cultivars such as Xanthi-81 and Prilep (Basma type), as well as Canik and Nail (Samsun type). The cluster 6 (genotypes A127–A59) was predominantly composed of İzmir-type tobaccos. İzmir cultivars Birlik-124, Birlik-128, and Özbaş were clustered together within this group. Additionally, several Samsun-type genotypes and the Samsun-type cultivar Sıtmasuyu formed a clearly distinct subcluster within this group. Among all genotypes examined, the Basma-type tobaccos exhibited the greatest genetic divergence. Nevertheless, genetically similar genotypes were identified across different regions. The following genotype pairings demonstrated such cross-regional genetic similarity: C8–B36, Canik–C14, C42–A88, C40–Nail, Prilep–C29, C25–A108, C38–A89, A105–Birlik-124, C10–A93, A138–B13, A107–C23, Özbaş–A141, A85–Birlik-128, A96–C67, A134–C64–C71–Sıtmasuyu, A122–C39. Based on these findings, eight identical genotypes were found between İzmir and Samsun types, two between İzmir and Basma types, and one between Samsun and Basma types.

Agronomic characterization

The agronomic traits of landraces were significantly different (p < 0.05) (Table S3). Plant height (PLH) ranged from 53.4 to 103.2 cm in İzmir genotypes, 76.8 to 140.0 cm in Basma genotypes, and 75.2 to 218.5 cm in Samsun genotypes (Table S4). In comparison, the standard cultivars displayed PLH values between 65.6–88.0 cm for İzmir types, 63.6–96.4 cm for Basma types, and 76.8–172.1 cm for Samsun types. These results indicate that Samsun tobacco genotypes tend to be taller, Basma types are intermediate, and İzmir types are generally shorter. Furthermore, most genotypes demonstrated greater plant height than the standard varieties within their respective regions.

Number of leaves (NoL) varied from 26.4 to 51.0 in İzmir genotypes, 26.5 to 44.5 in Basma genotypes, and 21.0 to 60.0 in Samsun genotypes (Tables S5–S8). Among standard cultivars, NoL ranged from 29.0–42.8 in İzmir types, 28.2–52.4 in Basma types, and 26.3–40.3 in Samsun types. Although the mean NoL values were similar across genotype groups, maximum values indicated that İzmir and Samsun genotypes exhibited higher leaf counts than their respective standard cultivars, while Basma genotypes presented lower values.

Leaf width (LeW) ranged from 6.2 to 13.7 cm in İzmir genotypes, 7.9 to 16.7 cm in Basma genotypes, and 9.4 to 17.2 cm in Samsun genotypes (Tables S5–S8). Among standard cultivars, LeW values ranged from 6.5 to 10.5 cm for İzmir types, 7.3 to 12.9 cm for Basma types, and 9.4 to 14.9 cm for Samsun types. LeW values were similarly higher in Basma and Samsun genotypes, while İzmir genotypes exhibited comparatively lower LeW. This pattern was also evident in the standard cultivars, where maximum LeW values tended to be lower overall.

Leaf length (LeL) varied between 11.9 and 24.9 cm in İzmir genotypes, 14.9 to 27.2 cm in Basma genotypes, and 18.1 to 28.3 cm in Samsun genotypes. For standard cultivars, LeL ranged from 13.2 to 18.6 cm in İzmir types, 15.5 to 22.9 cm in Basma types, and 21.2 to 32.2 cm in Samsun types. LeL averages were similar between Basma and Samsun genotypes, but lower in İzmir genotypes. Among standard cultivars, leaf length in İzmir (15.5 cm) and Basma (18.8 cm) types was comparable, whereas Samsun types displayed substantially greater leaf length (26.21 cm), significantly exceeding those of both genotypes and cultivars from other regions.

Dry leaf yield (YLD) ranged from 95.0 to 250.3 kg/da in İzmir genotypes, 127.9 to 287.0 kg/da in Basma genotypes, and 99.0 to 271.0 kg/da in Samsun genotypes (Tables S5–S8). Among standard cultivars, YLD values were 121.1–172.5 kg/da for İzmir types, 143.2–265.9 kg/da for Basma types, and 172.1–231.2 kg/da for Samsun types. While average yield levels between genotypes and cultivars were comparable, the maximum yield values recorded in genotypes surpassed those of standard varieties.

The Quality Grade Index (QGI) ranged from 36.3% to 77.7% in İzmir genotypes, 53.3% to 73.7% in Basma genotypes, and 53.5% to 78.8% in Samsun genotypes. In contrast, QGI values for standard cultivars ranged from 50.6% to 77.2% for İzmir types, 68.7% to 80.0% for Basma types, and 61.0% to 78.4% for Samsun types. Mean QGI values among genotypes were relatively consistent across regions, while cultivars generally displayed higher QGI scores. However, the maximum quality values were comparable between genotypes and cultivars.

Reducing sugar content (RES) ranged from 5.6% to 30.3% in İzmir genotypes, 5.4% to 22.0% in Basma genotypes, and 2.3% to 9.5% in Samsun genotypes (Tables S5–S8). For standard cultivars, RES values ranged from 10.4% to 24.3% in İzmir types, 8.8% to 22.6% in Basma types, and 4.3% to 24.3% in Samsun types. Overall, both İzmir and Basma genotypes and their corresponding cultivars exhibited higher RES levels, whereas Samsun genotypes had significantly lower RES values. In terms of maximum values, İzmir genotypes surpassed the standard cultivars, while Basma and Samsun genotypes showed similar upper RES limits to their respective standards.

Nicotine content (NIC) ranged from 0.27% to 2.76% in İzmir genotypes, 0.32% to 1.75% in Basma genotypes, and 0.83% to 2.79% in Samsun genotypes. Among standard cultivars, NIC values varied between 0.56% and 1.90% for İzmir types, 0.33% and 1.19% for Basma types, and 0.27% and 2.79% for Samsun types (Tables S5–S8). In terms of mean, maximum, and minimum values, İzmir and Samsun genotypes were largely comparable to their corresponding cultivars. However, Basma genotypes demonstrated higher NIC levels than their standard counterparts.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed using data from all genotypes and standard cultivars to assess multivariate relationships across the evaluated traits (Fig. 3). PCA was performed to visualize the multivariate relationships among the 331 genotypes. The first two principal components accounted for a total of 61.04% of the observed variation, with PC1 explaining 39.96% and PC2 explaining 21.08% of the total variance. The results showed that genotypes originating from the same region predominantly clustered together based on similar morphological and chemical profiles. The major contributors to total variation were LeL, LeW, and RES. In contrast, the lowest variation was associated with NoL and QGI. Region-specific trait contributions included: İzmir genotypes exhibiting distinct patterns in RES, Basma types in both YLD and RES, and Samsun genotypes in NIC and PLH. With the exception of Basma types, standard cultivars largely clustered with their corresponding regional genotypes.

To evaluate environmental effects, the same genotypes grown in different locations were compared. For instance, genetically identical pairs B36–C8 and Prilep–C29 exhibited higher NoL, YLD, and RES in Tokat, but higher LeW and LeL in Samsun. These patterns support that both are Basma-type tobaccos, consistent with the classification of Prilep as a Basma cultivar.

Similarly, the genetically identical pair B13 (Basma) and A138 (İzmir) showed higher YLD and RES in Tokat and higher NIC in İzmir, indicating closer affiliation with the Basma type. Further comparative assessments of genetically identical pairs, C42–A88, C25–A108, C38–A89, C10–A93, A107–C23, A96–C67, and A122–C39, allowed regional inference based on phenotypic expression. Specifically, C23, C25, and C42 are likely İzmir types due to elevated RES in that location, while C67, showing high YLD and low NIC in both regions, also aligns with İzmir types. Conversely, A89, A93, and A122 are likely Samsun types, based on elevated YLD and NIC observed in the Samsun location.

Region-specific evaluations of YLD and associated quality traits revealed that among 146 İzmir genotypes, 76 surpassed the regional standard cultivars in YLD, 39 in RES, 47 in QGI, and 70 in NIC (Table S5). One key observation is that 27 genotypes exhibited superior performance in both YLD and RES, traits characteristic of the İzmir production region. Among these, genotypes A36 (250.3 kg/da), A8 (238.3 kg/da), A132 (236.7 kg/da), and A57 (229.1 kg/da) demonstrated exceptional YLD values. Genotypes A124 (2.76%) and A118 (2.75%) showed the highest NIC concentrations, whereas A38 recorded the lowest NIC (0.27%). Genotype A9 was outstanding in RES, with a maximum value of 30.32%.

Of the 42 Basma-type genotypes evaluated, 24 exceeded the standard cultivars in YLD, 20 in RES, and 3 in NIC (Table S6). However, none surpassed the standard cultivars in QGI. Ten genotypes showed superior values in both YLD and the RES trait characteristic of the Tokat region. Significant performers included B14 with the highest yield (287.5 kg/da) and B12 with the highest NoL (44.5). Genotypes B19 (73.7%), B26 (73.3%), and B6 (72.3%) displayed the highest QGI scores among the Basma genotypes. For NIC, B26, B43, and B12 each reached 1.7%, while B40 had the lowest value (0.3%). In terms of RES, B15 (22.0%) and B37 (21.8%) were the top-ranking genotypes.

Among Samsun genotypes, 25 lines showed higher YLD, 4 higher RES, 19 higher QGI, and 35 higher NIC compared to the control cultivars (Table S7). Furthermore, 13 genotypes exceeded the general mean for both YLD and NIC, with NIC being a key distinguishing trait of the Samsun region. Top-performing genotypes in YLD included C70 (271.1 kg/da) and C5 (270.5 kg/da), while C41 stood out for NoL with 60 leaves per plant. The highest QGI values were recorded in C7 (78.8%), C10 (78.7%), C22 (78.6%), C54 (78.5%), C33 (78.2%), C51 (77.9%), and C9 (77.7%). Genotypes with the highest NIC levels were C12 (2.79%), C63 (2.77%), C28 (2.72%), C56 (2.72%), and C61 (2.72%). Conversely, low NIC values were observed in C26 (0.83%), C35 (0.88%), and C16 (0.97%). Genotype C48 showed the highest RES performance, with a value of 9.48%.

Discussion

All nine SSR markers used in this study produced polymorphic bands, consistent with previous reports highlighting their high polymorphism potential [30, 32, 41]. Therefore, the observation that all markers were polymorphic was anticipated. Likewise, other studies on tobacco populations have reported polymorphism rates ranging between 80 and 100%, indicating substantial allelic diversity at the DNA level [10, 11, 18, 32].

The genetic diversity parameters observed in this study place the Turkish sun-cured germplasm in a competitive position relative to global oriental tobacco panels. The mean number of alleles per locus 5.7—was significantly higher than the 2.53 reported by Darvishzadeh et al. (2013) for original oriental genotypes, yet remained below the 8.14 reported by Zhang et al. [46] in a multi-national study encompassing11 different countries. Furthermore, our allelic richness exceeded the 4.05 determined by Saygili et al. [39] for diverse Turkish set, while remaining comparable to the 6.3 reported by Kalivas et al. [18] for mixed Greek-oriental panel. These fluctuations across studies reinforce the notion that allelic richness is highly sensitive to population size and the specific SSR sets employed Regarding marker informativeness, the mean PIC value of 0.576 indicates a substantial level of genetic polymorphism within our population. This value is significantly higher than the levels reported for Macedonian (0.39 [11],) and other Turkish (0.463, [39]) tobaccos, while showing a striking similarity to Greek oriental varieties (0.577; [18]). Such moderate-to-high PIC values confirm that the selected markers were sufficiently robust to capture the unique genetic architecture of Turkish landraces and distinguish them effectively within the broader oriental tobacco germplasm. These results suggest that PIC values are influenced by factors such as the type of tobacco, the genetic background of the materials, and the number of genotypes included in the analyses.

Rare alleles represent one of the most effective methods for identifying unique genotypes within a population. Moon et al. [32] reported a rare allele frequency of 56.5%, while Fricano et al. [14] found it to be 14%. In this study, the rare allele frequency was determined to be 30%. The level of genetic diversity observed in this research is lower than that reported by Moon et al. [32], but higher than the levels reported by Ganesh et al. [16] and Kalivas et al. [18]. It is important to note that Ganesh et al. [16] and Kalivas et al. [18] analyzed fewer genotypes than this study, while Moon et al. [32] investigated a much broader collection of genotypes from diverse categories. The genetic diversity identified in this study, conducted within a restricted geographical area and using seed-propagated populations maintained by farmers, can be considered notably high. The identification of a substantial rare allele frequency (30%) within the studied population has profound implications for tobacco breeding. These rare alleles often represent site-specific adaptations or unique metabolic traits that are typically lost in high-yielding commercial cultivars. From a breeding perspective, genotypes harboring these alleles function as primary genetic reservoirs for introducing novel variation into stagnant gene pool [13]. Incorporating such alleles is not merely about increasing polymorphism,it is a strategic necessity for widening the narrow genetic base of modern tobacco. By unlocking these sources of functional variation, breeders can enhance the adaptive plasticity of new varieties against emerging biotic and abiotic stressors, while simultaneously preserving the authentic organoleptic qualities characteristic of oriental tobacco.

A dendrogram revealed that the majority of tobacco genotypes clustered according to their respective production regions. Tobacco types originating from the same geographical area were consistently grouped within the same cluster, indicating the capability of SSR markers to reliably distinguish regional genetic structures. SSR markers effectively discriminated between distinct tobacco types, as evidenced by the clustering of registered cultivars with genotypes of their corresponding tobacco type. Our findings strongly confirm the genetic divergence among Samsun, İzmir, and Basma-type tobaccos. These results are consistent with previous research [18, 32, 36, 39] demonstrating that SSR markers effectively differentiate tobacco types, even within the same species but across different usage classes. This consistent performance shows the robustness and reproducibility of SSR-based genetic assessments. Furthermore, the capacity of SSR markers to exclude genetically similar individuals from a divergent population, an advantage well-documented in earlier studies [15, 37, 38], proved particularly beneficial in this study. This selective ability enabled a more precise evaluation of how genuine genetic variation contributes to yield and quality attributes, thereby directly supporting the primary objective of the research.

The clustering analyses revealed a distinct separation of İzmir genotypes from the rest of the germplasm, suggesting a more isolated genetic background. In contrast, Basma and Samsun genotypes exhibited significant overlap within a cluster where regional affiliation remained unresolved. This lack of clear genetic boundaries is likely a consequence of frequent inter-regional seed exchange among producers in neighboring regions, particularly between Samsun and Tokat provinces. Among the types evaluated, Basma exhibited the highest levels of genetic diversity, consistent with previous molecular characterizations of Turkish oriental tobacco [39]. This elevated diversity can be attributed to the long-term cultivation of indigenous landraces in Tokat, alongside the historical introgression of germplasm from other major Basma-producing countries, especially Greece [9]. We hypothesize that the widespread adoption of these foreign cultivars, followed by natural hybridization with localized populations, has enriched the allelic pool. Consequently, the current genetic structure of Basma tobacco reflects a dynamic synergy between historical local adaptation and contemporary transboundary germplasm movement.

Field trials demonstrated that İzmir tobaccos excelled in RES, while Samsun tobaccos were important for their YLD and NIC. Basma tobaccos, conversely, performed exceptionally well in both YLD and RES. We attribute the elevated YLD in Samsun and Basma genotypes to the higher rainfall and comparatively lower average temperatures in their respective regions compared to İzmir (Table S2). These specific climatic conditions appear to promote yield accumulation in Samsun and Tokat environments. The LeW and LeL, key yield components strongly associated with YLD [24, 34], were also more prominent in these two regions. The NIC levels are directly linked to nitrogen assimilation [28]. The ecological conditions in Samsun likely enhance nitrogen uptake, leading to higher NIC accumulation in local tobacco genotypes. Although the Tokat location, where Basma-type tobacco is grown, displayed high values for YLD, LeW, and LeL, it registered the lowest NIC levels among all sites. This suggests that despite substantial biomass accumulation, the capacity for nitrogen assimilation and subsequent nicotine synthesis in Tokat-grown genotypes may be comparatively limited. Oriental tobaccos are generally characterized by relatively high levels of reducing sugars and low nicotine content [12, 21, 24]. These chemical attributes are essential for providing the characteristic sweetness and aroma to tobacco blends, where high-sugar oriental types are often preferred to balance the harsher notes of other tobacco components. However, the ideal range for reducing sugars can vary across specific types, such as Izmir, Samsun, and Basma, and is significantly influenced by environmental conditions [24, 27]. While superior -sugar levels are frequently sought for specific blending purposes, varieties with moderate sugar content also maintain industrial value depending on the desired smoke profile.

Total precipitation during the study exceeded long-term averages (Table S2), potentially leading to nitrogen leaching from the soil. Consequently, higher LeW and LeL values in Basma genotypes, relative to the standard cultivars, likely reduced the alkaloid concentration per unit leaf area, thereby enhancing leaf quality. At the İzmir site, elevated temperatures compared to Samsun and Tokat induced physiological stress in plants, resulting in lower yields. Additionally, high temperatures increase dry matter accumulation in leaves, which substantially influences RES [43]. Accelerated drying under such heat further boosts RES levels. Conversely, cooler temperatures in Samsun prolong the drying period, thereby increasing leaf respiration and reducing RES [12]. These climatic factors have favored the preference for region-adapted genotypes and contributed to the enduring legacy of unique tobacco varieties associated with specific regional identities. The variation observed in reducing sugars, nicotine, and yield across different regions is closely linked to specific environmental stressors and soil nutrient availability. Temperature and precipitation act as primary drivers,for instance, moderate moisture stress and higher solar radiation, characteristic of traditional oriental tobacco regions, are known to enhance the synthesis of secondary metabolites and reducing sugars while limiting leaf expansion, thereby concentrating nicotine levels [17, 40]. Furthermore, even when identical genotypes are cultivated across diverse locations, differences in soil nitrogen availability and thermal accumulation significantly alter the carbon-to-nitrogen balance within the leaf, leading to the distinct chemical profiles observed in our study [21, 27]. These environmental factors dictate the final quality of sun-cured tobacco by modulating the plant's metabolic pathways during the curing process.

In PCA clustering, standard cultivars of İzmir and Samsun types grouped together with their corresponding local genotypes, while Basma genotypes did not co-cluster with Basma standards. This divergence likely stems from the fact that Basma standards are of Greek origin [20], which may explain their agronomic differences from local Basma genotypes. In contrast, İzmir and Samsun standards were developed from local germplasm and thus shared similar phenotypic traits [33]. However, attributing all phenotypic differences solely to environmental variation was not justifiable. To further investigate regional connectivity, genotypes exhibiting identical allelic profiles across different cultivation regions were analyzed. These findings provide evidence of informal seed networks and the historical movement of plant materials among growers. While such cross-regional seed movement and uncontrolled propagation could theoretically lead to genetic homogenization, our results suggest a more complex dynamic. The maintenance of pronounced regional quality differences, despite genetic identity, indicates the significant role of Genotype by Environment interactions and site-specific selection pressures applied by local families over generations.

Analysis of individual genotypes revealed that 18.5% of İzmir, 18.3% of Samsun, and 23.8% of Basma genotypes outperformed their commercial counterparts in YLD and regional characteristic traits. This outcome is consistent with previous research [20, 29] that also identified superior genotypes and suggested their registration as new cultivars and utilization as parents in breeding programs. Selecting high-performing individuals from a background of genetic diversity offers an optimal pathway for standardized and reproducible cultivation. This study distinctly reveals how the inherent genetic variability in oriental tobacco contributes to quality. In predominantly uniform industrial production systems, this seemingly undesired diversity actually confers significant quality and yield advantages. Although not directly addressed in this study, it is well established that tobacco grown in marginal ecological zones exhibits enhanced stress tolerance due to genetic diversity [22]. Such variability provides essential strategic support by safeguarding producer income and ensuring the continuous supply of leaf tobacco to the market.

Conclusion

The global diversification of tobacco products is expanding, creating an increasing demand for raw materials with different agronomic and chemical properties. Oriental tobacco is a fundamental component of virtually all cigarette blends, and its genetic and phenotypic diversity are pivotal in ensuring product consistency. Therefore, conserving and strategically improving diverse oriental tobacco germplasm is critical for sustaining innovation and resilience within the global tobacco industry. The oriental tobacco landraces used in the present study exhibit exceptionally high levels of genetic diversity, resulting in unique agronomic and chemical characteristics. These distinct characteristics are readily observed across the production regions of Turkish sun‑cured tobaccos. Beyond climate, eco‑geographical factors such as soil conditions and agronomic practices play a direct role in the development of genotype‑specific traits. Traditional in-situ conservation and seed‑saving practices remain the primary drivers of regional identity in Turkish tobacco production. Farmers typically select seeds from plants that demonstrate superior adaptation to their specific micro-climates, a process that reinforces regional ecotypes even when the initial germplasm is shared. These findings confirm that genetic diversity is a cornerstone for supporting sun‑cured. From a breeding perspective, these identical genotypes found in diverse locations offer a unique opportunity to study phenotypic plasticity. Our findings confirm that this preserved genetic diversity, sustained through decadal grower-led selection, serves as a vital genetic reservoir for the long‑term sustainability of Türkiye’s sun‑cured tobacco varieties.

Landraces, farmer‑maintained genetic materials, and ecotypes have long served as critical resources supporting plant breeding efforts. The findings clearly demonstrate that comprehensive genetic characterization of Turkish sun‑cured tobacco landraces has been achieved, providing a foundational prerequisite for their integration into modern breeding programs. While high genetic diversity is often considered unsuitable for standardized agricultural systems, especially where uniformity in dried tobacco leaf is desired, this diversity has shown to positively influence both yield and quality in the final product. Importantly, this study reinforces the breeding value of landraces, which frequently harbor a wide range of agronomic traits and demonstrate resilience to biotic and abiotic stresses. In the context of oriental tobacco, these landraces offer irreplaceable contributions to both productivity and product excellence.
