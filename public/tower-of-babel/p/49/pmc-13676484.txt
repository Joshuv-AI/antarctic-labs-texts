Performance evaluation of antimicrobial susceptibility testing methods for ceftazidime-avibactam and imipenem-relebactam against Klebsiella pneumoniae and Pseudomonas aeruginosa
Authors: Xiaobo Xu, Erjie Lu, Lixin Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CR-KP) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CR-PA) present critical therapeutic challenges. Ceftazidime-avibactam (CAZ-AVI) and imipenem-relebactam (IMI-REL) are key treatment options, but optimal susceptibility testing methods and local resistance mechanisms need clarification.MethodsA total of 108 CR‑KP and 102 CR‑PA non‑duplicate clinical isolates (June 2025–February 2026) were tested. Susceptibility to CAZ‑AVI and IMI‑REL was determined by broth microdilution (BMD, reference method), gradient diffusion, and Kirby‑Bauer (K‑B) disk diffusion. Essential agreement (EA), categorical agreement (CA), and error rates were calculated. Carbapenemase types were detected by immunochromatography (CARBA5).ResultsBy BMD, CAZ-AVI susceptibility rates were 88.9% for CR-KP and 96.1% for CR-PA; IMI-REL susceptibility rates were 84.3% and 75.5%, respectively. For CAZ-AVI, gradient diffusion showed excellent CA with BMD (99.1% for CR-KP and 94.1% for CR-PA) and EA of 91.7% and 97.1%, respectively. In contrast, gradient diffusion for IMI-REL was suboptimal (CA 94.4% and EA 86.1% for CR-KP, and CA 84.3% and EA 87.3% for CR-PA). K-B disk diffusion performed poorly for IMI-REL (CA 75.0% for CR-KP and 77.5% for CR-PA; minor errors rates were 24.1% and 20.6%, respectively).ConclusionCAZ-AVI and IMI-REL retain potent in vitro activity against contemporary CR-KP and CR-PA isolates. Gradient diffusion is a reliable alternative to BMD for ceftazidime-avibactam, whereas its accuracy is limited for imipenem-relebactam. K-B disk diffusion for IMI-REL is associated with unacceptably high error rates; reference method confirmation is recommended for isolates yielding non-susceptible results by this method.

Introduction

According to the 2024 list of bacterial priority pathogens released by the World Health Organization, carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) are classified as “critical priority pathogens,” while carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CR-PA) is categorized as a “high priority pathogen,” highlighting their severe threat to global public health [1]. Among these, carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CR-KP), one of the most significant CRE pathogens, together with CR-PA, poses a major challenge to nosocomial infection control. These pathogens are widely prevalent in medical environments such as intensive care units and frequently cause severe infections, including bloodstream infections, pneumonia, intra-abdominal infections, and urinary tract infections, which are closely associated with prolonged hospital stays and significantly increased mortality rates [2, 3]. The resistance of CR-KP is primarily mediated by carbapenemase production, enzymes that can hydrolyze nearly all β-lactam antibiotics, including carbapenems [4]. The resistance mechanisms of CR-PA are more complex, involving not only enzyme-mediated processes but also non-enzymatic mechanisms such as the deletion or downregulation of the outer membrane porin OprD, and the overexpression of efflux pump systems (e.g., MexAB-OprM) [5]. The extensive resistance exhibited by both CR-KP and CR-PA has rendered traditional antimicrobial treatment regimens ineffective, posing a significant clinical challenge characterized by extremely limited treatment options [6].

Against this backdrop, novel β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combination agents, represented by ceftazidime-avibactam (CAZ-AVI) and imipenem-relebactam (IMI-REL), have emerged as critical therapeutic options for addressing this challenge [7]. Authoritative clinical guidelines, including those from the Infectious Diseases Society of America, recommend these agents for treating various severe infections caused by CRE, including hospital-acquired/ventilator-associated bacterial pneumonia, complicated intra-abdominal infections, and complicated urinary tract infections [8]. Although both are combination agents, the inhibitory spectra of avibactam and relebactam differ. Avibactam inhibits Ambler classes A, C, and certain class D enzymes (e.g., OXA-48), whereas relebactam exhibits a narrower spectrum, primarily targeting class A and C enzymes. Neither inhibitor demonstrates activity against Ambler class B metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs) [9].

Clinical laboratories use various antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) methods with different principles, advantages, and limitations. Broth microdilution (BMD) is the gold standard, determining MIC accurately and reproducibly [10], but it is cumbersome, time-consuming, and labor-intensive for routine use. The Kirby-Bauer (K-B) method qualitatively assesses susceptibility by measuring inhibition zone diameters, offering low cost and flexibility. The gradient diffusion method allows direct MIC reading on agar plates, combining dilution method quantification with diffusion method simplicity. However, susceptibility testing cards for new antimicrobial agents often lag behind in development. Although alternative methods are more convenient, their accuracy—especially for novel agents and complex resistance mechanisms—may differ significantly from BMD, leading to major or very major errors [11]. Therefore, before these convenient methods can be applied to complex cases, their performance must be validated and supported by more comprehensive laboratory data.

Given the significant regional variations in the epidemiology and resistance mechanisms of carbapenem-resistant bacteria, establishing reliable local in vitro AST methods is crucial for guiding clinical medication and combating drug-resistant strains. As CAZ-AVI and IMI-REL have become key agents for treating CR-KP and CR-PA, it is essential to develop accurate and reliable in vitro AST methods for these drugs in the region. This study, using BMD as the benchmark, aims to systematically evaluate the performance of two commonly used AST methods in detecting CAZ-AVI and IMI-REL susceptibility, providing data support for method selection in local clinical laboratories.

Materials and methods

Bacterial isolates and identification

This study included a total of 210 clinical non-duplicate isolates, comprising 108 CR-KP and 102 CR-PA strains isolated between June 2025 and February 2026. All strains were subjected to final identification using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (bioMérieux, Lyon, France). The strains were stored in 20% glycerol broth at -80 °C ultra-low temperature freezers.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The MICs of routine antimicrobial agents were determined using the VITEK® 2 system (bioMérieux, Lyon, France) with AST-GN13 cards for CR-KP isolates and AST-N335 cards for CR-PA isolates. For CAZ-AVI and IMI-REL, three methods were employed: BMD, gradient diffusion, and K-B. BMD panels (CAZ-AVI: 0.25/4–128/4 µg/mL, batch YK04154; IMI-REL: 0.25/4–32/4 µg/mL, batch YI11128) were obtained from Kangtai Biotechnology (Wenzhou, Zhejiang, China). gradient diffusion (CAZ-AVI, 0.25/4–32/4 µg/mL, batch YI0821S1, Kangtai; IMI-REL MTS™, 0.002/4–32/4 µg/mL, batch 063025079, Liofilchem s.r.l., Italy) and disks (CAZ-AVI 30/20 µg, batch YL0207Z1; IMI-REL 10/25 µg, batch 102825051, Liofilchem) were also used. Quality control strains Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 and P. aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 were tested in parallel, and all results fell within CLSI M100 acceptable ranges.

For BMD, bacterial suspensions were prepared by direct colony suspension to a 0.5 McFarland standard, inoculated into panels, and incubated at 35 ± 1 °C for 16–20 h. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration completely inhibiting growth. gradient diffusion and K-B tests were performed on Mueller-Hinton (Antu Biologicals, Zhengzhou, China) agar plates streaked with the same suspension; after applying strips or disks, plates were incubated under identical conditions. The MIC was read at the intersection of the elliptical inhibition zone with the strip, and inhibition zone diameters were measured for K-B. All tests were performed in triplicate independently, and the final MIC was taken as the value from at least two concordant readings; if discrepancies exceeded one two-fold dilution, the isolate was retested.

CARBA5 immunochromatographic assay for carbapenemase detection

For the immunochromatographic strip test, a single colony was selected from a culture grown for 16–20 h and placed in a centrifuge tube containing extraction buffer; the suspension was thoroughly resuspended to prepare a high-concentration bacterial suspension. Then, 100 µL of the suspension was added vertically into the sample well of the test strip, and the strip was incubated horizontally at room temperature for 15 min before visual interpretation. The criteria for interpreting results were as follows: a positive result was indicated by the simultaneous appearance of the control line (C) and any test line (T); a negative result was indicated by the appearance of the C line alone; and the absence of the C line rendered the test invalid. This method could detect KPC, NDM, OXA-48, IMP and VIM. A negative result obtained using this method merely indicated that the target enzyme types had not been detected; it did not rule out the presence of other mechanisms of resistance [12].

Data analysis and methodological evaluation

All MICs and inhibition zone diameters were interpreted as susceptible (S), intermediate (I), or resistant (R) according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M100 guidelines (34th ed.) [13]. Using BMD as the reference method, the performance of gradient diffusion and K-B was evaluated by calculating categorical agreement (CA, the percentage of isolates with identical S/I/R interpretations between the test method and the reference), essential agreement (EA, applicable only to gradient diffusion, defined as MIC values within ± 1 two-fold dilution), and error rates. Errors were classified as very major errors (VME, false susceptible: reference = R, test = S), major errors (ME, false resistant: reference = S, test = R), and minor errors (mE, one method yielded I while the other yielded S or R).

Results

Reference drug susceptibility results

BMD results showed that both CR-KP and CR-PA isolates were highly resistant to most tested antimicrobial agents, including cephalosporins, carbapenems, and fluoroquinolones. The susceptibility rates of CR-KP and CR-PA to CAZ-AVI were 88.9% and 96.1%, respectively; those to IMI-REL were 84.3% and 75.5%, respectively. Compared with ceftazidime and imipenem alone, the combination formulations CAZ-AVI and IMI-REL demonstrated markedly lower MIC50 and MIC90 values against CR-KP and CR-PA, reflecting the restorative activity of avibactam and relebactam (Tables 1 and 2).

Methodological evaluation for CR-PA strains

Table 4; Fig. 2 present the performance metrics for CR-PA isolates. For CAZ-AVI, both gradient diffusion and K-B yielded high CA values (94.1% and 93.1%, respectively). The K-B method had a major error rate of 6.9%, which may lead to false resistance reports for CAZ-AVI against CR-PA. For IMI-REL, gradient diffusion gave CA 84.3%, EA 87.3%, and mE 14.7%, which did not meet acceptable performance criteria, whereas K-B again performed poorly (CA 77.5%, mE 20.6%). These findings underscore that K-B for IMI-REL is insufficiently reliable for routine testing.

Drug susceptibility and carbapenemase distribution

Of 108 CR-KP isolates, 96 (88.9%) were susceptible to CAZ‑AVI, of which 91 (94.8%) were also susceptible to IMI‑REL, primarily producing KPC. All 12 CAZ‑AVI‑resistant isolates carried MBLs (mainly NDM) and were non‑susceptible to IMI‑REL. Notably, five CAZ‑AVI‑susceptible isolates (including OXA‑48‑like producers) showed intermediate or resistant IMI‑REL profiles, suggesting better activity of CAZ‑AVI against certain OXA‑producing strains (Table 5).

Discussion

In the era of escalating antimicrobial resistance, novel β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations like CAZ-AVI and IMI-REL are last-resort agents for infections caused by CR-KP and CR-PA [14]. This study confirms their potent in vitro activity against clinical isolates from our region, with CAZ-AVI demonstrating susceptibility rates of 88.9% against CR-KP and 96.1% against CR-PA, whereas IMI-REL showed susceptibility rates of 84.3% and 75.5%, respectively. These data align well with global surveillance programs like SMART and other recent multicenter studies [15, 16]. Our findings clearly indicate that the production of KPC carbapenemases is the predominant resistance mechanism among CR-KP in our region. For CR-KP, the dominance of class A enzymes is consistent with epidemiological patterns reported from many parts of the world [17]. In contrast, the resistance epidemiology of P. aeruginosa shows marked geographic variation. A European surveillance study from 2015 to 2017 found that 72% of P. aeruginosa isolates that were non-susceptible to imipenem-relebactam produced MBLs [18]. A report from Zhejiang, China, identified clonal dissemination of KPC-producing P. aeruginosa (KPC-prevalence of 90.6%), with co-circulation of GES-type enzymes within the same high-risk clone [19].In the present study, the majority (72.5%, 74/102) of CAZ‑AVI‑susceptible CR‑PA isolates did not carry any of the five carbapenemase types detected by the CARBA5 assay, indicating that other resistance mechanisms predominate in our region.

For CR-KP isolates, susceptibility profiles for CAZ-AVI and IMI-REL were largely concordant with the carbapenemase phenotype. All 12 CAZ-AVI-resistant isolates were non-susceptible to IMI-REL and were found to produce MBLs. This suggests that for infections where CAZ-AVI fails due to MBL production, IMI-REL is unlikely to be an effective monotherapy salvage option. In such cases, alternative regimens like aztreonam-avibactam [20] or cefiderocol [21] should be considered. A key advantage of IMI-REL is its retained activity against certain KPC variants (e.g., D179Y) that confer CAZ-AVI resistance [22], and its potential to reduce the frequency of emergent resistance during therapy [23]. Mechanisms of IMI-REL resistance encompass not only carbapenemase production but also membrane pore protein mutations (such as OmpK36 deletion or structural alteration), upregulation of efflux pumps, and increased copy number of KPC enzyme genes [24]. Among the four OXA-48-producing CR-KP isolates, only one was susceptible to IMI-REL, while the others showed intermediate or resistant phenotypes, suggesting variable activity of relebactam against different OXA-48-like variants. Although some isolates may be susceptible [25], clinical use of IMI-REL against OXA-48 producers is generally not recommended.

The susceptibility patterns of CR‑PA reflect distinct regional characteristics. Among 102 CR‑PA isolates, 98 (96.1%) were susceptible to CAZ‑AVI, while 77 (75.5%) were susceptible to IMI‑REL. Notably, 19 of 21 (90.5%) IMI‑REL‑resistant isolates remained susceptible to CAZ‑AVI. Using the CARBA5 assay, which detects KPC, NDM, OXA‑48, IMP and VIM, we identified four CAZ‑AVI‑resistant CR‑PA isolates. Among these, two were KPC‑only producers that showed susceptibility (MIC ≤ 2 µg/mL) or intermediate (MIC 4 µg/mL) to IMI‑REL; however, KPC enzymes are typically inhibited by avibactam, so the observed CAZ‑AVI resistance in these two isolates cannot be explained by KPC production alone, indicating the presence of additional, uncharacterized resistance mechanisms (which may include non-enzymatic pathways). The remaining two CAZ‑AVI‑resistant isolates co‑produced KPC and NDM and were resistant to IMI‑REL. Notably, among the 98 CAZ‑AVI‑susceptible isolates, 74 were not detected with any of the five carbapenemase types (designated as “Other” in Table 6), indicating that the majority of CR‑PA isolates in our region harbor resistance mechanisms, including carbapenemases not detected by the CARBA5 assay or other non-enzymatic mechanisms. Further investigations using molecular methods (e.g., whole‑genome sequencing or targeted PCR) are warranted to characterize the underlying resistance determinants in these isolates.

Accurate AST is a prerequisite for precision medicine. This study shows that gradient diffusion is a reliable alternative to BMD for MIC determination of CAZ‑AVI, with CA rates exceeding 94% (range, 94.1%–99.1%) and EA values ranging from 91.7% to 97.1%. For IMI‑REL, gradient diffusion demonstrated suboptimal performance, with CA rates ranging from 84.3% to 94.4% and EA values from 86.1% to 87.3%, indicating that it may not be consistently reliable. For CR‑KP, K‑B testing of CAZ‑AVI was also reliable (CA 98.1%), but its performance for IMI‑REL was unacceptable (CA 75.0%, mE 24.1%). Similarly for CR‑PA, K‑B testing of IMI‑REL was poor (CA 77.5%, mE 20.6%). For IMI‑REL, gradient diffusion showed minor errors of 5.6% (CR‑KP) and 14.7% (CR‑PA), occurring mostly when MICs fell exactly at the intermediate value (2 µg/mL or 4 µg/mL). In contrast, breakpoint‑focused analysis of K‑B testing revealed severe clustering: for CR‑KP, 24.1% minor errors (26/108), with 21/26 (80.8%) within 20–22 mm; for CR‑PA, 20.6% (21/102), with 16/21 (76.2%) at 21–23 mm, and 13/16 (81.3%) had susceptible BMD MICs (≤ 2 µg/mL).

The poor performance of the K‑B method arises from the clustering of inhibition zone diameters around the CLSI breakpoint, where small technical variations can readily shift the interpretation from susceptible to intermediate or resistant. Our findings are corroborated by other studies; Hakvoort et al. reported a CA of only 73.1% for IMI‑REL disk diffusion against Gram‑negative bacilli [26]. A study focused on CR‑PA by Biguenet et al. found that disk diffusion miscategorized 25.8% (16/62) of resistant isolates as susceptible and recommended establishing an “Area of Technical Uncertainty” around the breakpoint to mitigate this risk [27]. This poor performance may be related to the diffusion kinetics of relebactam in agar, highlighting the need for caution.From a clinical perspective, a high mE means that laboratories are likely to report truly susceptible isolates as intermediate or resistant. This may lead clinicians to avoid an effective, guideline‑recommended drug (such as IMI-REL) in favor of broader‑spectrum, more toxic, or more expensive alternatives, or to delay appropriate therapy.

Beyond these technical factors, a more fundamental issue complicating IMI‑REL susceptibility interpretation is the lack of harmonized breakpoints between CLSI and EUCAST [28]. This study used CLSI breakpoints (34th ed.), but notable discrepancies exist. For Enterobacterales, CLSI sets the susceptible MIC at ≤ 1 µg/mL (intermediate 2 µg/mL), whereas EUCAST uses ≤ 2 µg/mL (resistant ≥ 4 µg/mL). For P. aeruginosa, CLSI defines susceptible at ≤ 2 µg/mL (intermediate 4 µg/mL), while EUCAST uses ≤ 2 µg/mL as susceptible and > 2 µg/mL as resistant. Consequently, four CR‑KP isolates with MIC 2 µg/mL are intermediate by CLSI but susceptible by EUCAST, and four CR‑PA isolates with MIC 4 µg/mL are intermediate by CLSI but resistant by EUCAST [29]. Discrepancies are even greater for disk diffusion: for P. aeruginosa, the CLSI intermediate range (20–21 mm) is mostly interpreted as resistant by EUCAST, since EUCAST sets the susceptible cutoff at ≥ 22 mm without an intermediate category. Thus, although our conclusions are based strictly on CLSI breakpoints, clinicians should be aware that applying EUCAST criteria would reclassify some non‑susceptible isolates as susceptible. Such discrepancies can lead to discordant treatment recommendations and underscore the need for regional harmonization of breakpoint standards.

This study has limitations. As a single-center study, its findings may not be generalizable. We did not perform whole-genome sequencing (WGS), precluding molecular confirmation of specific resistance mechanisms. Finally, the use of different commercial brands for AST methods reflects local availability and may not represent all available products.

Conclusion

CAZ-AVI and IMI-REL demonstrate potent in vitro activity against contemporary CR-KP and CR-PA isolates in our region. Given the suboptimal performance of K-B method for IMI-REL, laboratories must interpret these results with caution and prioritize MIC confirmation for non-susceptible isolates to ensure accurate clinical management.
