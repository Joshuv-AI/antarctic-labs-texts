The Interplay of Dy Doping and Sulfur Vacancies in MoS2 for an Efficient Hydrogen Evolution Reaction
Authors: Nutnaree Petcharat, Nadcha Kaewmuntree, Navapat Krobkrong, Wisit Hirunpinyopas, Panwad Chavalekvirat, Pawin Iamprasertkun, Thanasee Thanasarnsurapong, Adisak Boonchun, Krongthong Kamonsuangkasem, Weekit Sirisaksoontorn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A comprehensive understanding of rare-earth-doped MoS2 is essential for the rational design of catalysts with increasing active sites to improve hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) performance. In this work, we synthesized Dy-doped MoS2 (MoS2/Dy) via a citrate-assisted hydrothermal method with varying Dy loadings. Citrate plays a vital role in stabilizing Dy ions in a solution mixture and preventing their precipitation. The successful formation of 2H-phase MoS2 and its morphology were confirmed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and electron microscopy analyses. The Dy-induced alteration in the electronic structure of MoS2 and the local chemical environment of Dy were also elucidated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS). Upon variation of Dy doping, a decrease in the Mo:S ratio was observed with the presence of sulfur vacancies, which further enriches the exposure of the active sites. Among all synthesized catalysts, MoS2 with the optimum Dy loading (7 wt %) exhibits the superior electrocatalytic performance in 0.5 M H2SO4, delivering a low overpotential of 202 mV at a current density of 10 mA cm–2 and a favorable Tafel slope of 53.9 mV dec–1. The MoS2/Dy catalyst also demonstrates excellent durability, maintaining stable HER performance over 36 h without significant degradation. Moreover, density functional theory (DFT) calculations suggest that hydrogen adsorption near the sulfur vacancy and Dy atom provides the optimum value of −0.30 eV for HER. These results highlight the synergistic relation of Dy doping and sulfur vacancies in enhancing the electrocatalytic activity of MoS2, positioning it as a promising candidate for sustainable hydrogen production in acid media.

Introduction

In recent decades, the global energy supply has remained dependent upon conventional fossil fuels, consisting of coal, petroleum, and natural gas, which collectively generate massive CO2 emission. , The continued exploitation of these fuels causes a severe threat to climate change, air pollution, and environmental degradation. , To address this global challenge, there is an urgent need to transition to renewable and green energy sources. Hydrogen is emerging as a key energy source for modern industries aiming to reduce their carbon footprint. As a clean fuel, hydrogen offers a pathway toward decarbonization and more sustainable energy system due to its zero carbon emission of combustion products. − Among the various methods of hydrogen production, water electrolysis is recognized as one of the most efficient and eco-friendly approaches in acid media through the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER, 2H+ + 2e– → H2). − However, this large-scale production is still limited by the availability of low-cost, highly efficient, and durable electrocatalysts. , Besides the benchmark Pt-based catalysts, significant efforts have been devoted to developing nonprecious metal catalysts, such as metal alloys, transition-metal sulfides/phosphides/nitrides/hydroxides, and various composite materials. − Among them, MoS2 has garnered much attention as a promising alternative to Pt-based catalysts due to its unique electrochemical activity, particularly on the unsaturated sulfur atoms at the edge sites. Two distinct crystallographic phases of MoS2, namely, 2H- and 1T-phases, have been extensively explored for their potential in HER applications. , As previously reported, the conversion from semiconducting 2H-phase to metallic 1T-phase MoS2 can markedly enhance HER performance, but the 1T-phase is metastable and tends to revert to the more thermodynamically stable 2H-phase during prolonged reaction. Thus, many attempts have been directed toward improving the performance of 2H-MoS2 through a variety of strategies, such as element doping and anchoring, defect and strain engineering, heterostructure design, and morphology control. − These approaches aim to increase the exposure of active sites on both the basal planes and edges, improve electronic conductivity, and optimize the hydrogen adsorption activity. Among them, heteroatom-doped MoS2 via the hydrothermal/solvothermal method offers key advantages, including a facile synthesis procedure, high-yield production, and chemical stability. −

Fortunately, rare-earth-doped MoS2 has emerged as a potential material for numerous applications, such as photonic devices, luminescent materials, magnetic materials, supercapacitors, photocatalysts, and electrocatalysts. − This is because rare-earth doping can significantly modify the electronic, optical, and catalytic properties of MoS2. More specifically, introducing rare-earth elements can create defect sites or alter the local structure, which further promotes catalytic activity and storage performance. For example, Rathinam et al. synthesized 4 wt % Nd- and Gd-doped MoS2 using hydrothermal, followed by annealing. Their enhanced specific capacitance was ascribed to the doping effect, which increased the number of active reaction sites and improved the electronic conductivity. Huang et al. predicted the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) activity on rare-earth-doped MoS2 using density functional theory (DFT) calculations and polarization curve simulations. The enhanced activity was proposed by the defect-state pairing mechanism and biperiodic chemical trend. Hu et al. prepared Er-doped MoS2 nanosheets coated on porous nitrogen-doped carbon using hydrothermal followed by calcination for HER application. The synergy between the porous framework and Er doping was found to improve the electrocatalytic activity by exposing more active sites. Thus, a comprehensive understanding of rare-earth-doped MoS2 is essential for the rational design aimed at increasing the exposure of active sites, particularly on the basal plane, to achieve more efficient and durable HER performance.

Herein, we report the detailed study of the synthesis of Dy-doped MoS2 (denoted MoS2/Dy) via a citrate-assisted hydrothermal method for enhanced electrocatalytic performance toward HER in acid media. The crystal structure, composition (wt % Dy and Mo:S molar ratio), and microscopic morphology of MoS2/Dy samples were systematically characterized in relation to their electrochemical performance. The effect of Dy doping on the electronic structure alteration of MoS2 was also demonstrated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS). Upon the optimum loading of Dy, MoS2/Dy exhibits excellent electrocatalytic activity, which originates from the increased number of active sites on the MoS2 basal plane. The DFT calculations further reveal the thermodynamic synergy of Dy doping and sulfur vacancies to optimize the H adsorption energy. These findings underscore the effectiveness of the rare-earth element in modifying the electronic structure of MoS2 for enhanced HER activity in acid media.

Experimental Section

Preparation of Dy-Doped MoS2

Dy-doped MoS2 catalysts were synthesized by using a citrate-assisted hydrothermal method. In a typical procedure, 2 mL of 0.25 mmol of sodium citrate tribasic dihydrate (C6H5O7Na3·2H2O, Sigma-Aldrich) was added dropwise into 2 mL of 0.074 mmol of dysprosium acetate hydrate ((C2H3O2)3Dy·xH2O, Sigma-Aldrich) in DI water. Then, a separate solution of 2 mmol of l-cysteine (C3H7NO2S, Sigma-Aldrich) and 1 mmol of sodium molybdate dihydrate (Na2MoO4·2H2O, Carlo Erba) in 6 mL of DI water was gradually mixed with the former solution under vigorous stirring for 30 min. After mixing, the homogeneous solution was then transferred to a 20 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and heated to 200 °C for 24 h. Upon cooling to room temperature, the solid product was washed several times with deionized water and ethanol, followed by vacuum drying at 70 °C for 6 h. The as-obtained catalyst, containing calcd 7 wt % Dy-doped MoS2, was labeled as MoS2/Dy-7. A series of MoS2/Dy catalysts with different Dy mass loadings (1–15 wt %) and pristine MoS2 were prepared similarly and denoted MoS2/Dy-1, MoS2/Dy-3, MoS2/Dy-5, MoS2/Dy-7, MoS2/Dy-10, MoS2/Dy-15, and MoS2, respectively.

Characterization

The crystal structure of all samples was determined by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) utilizing a Bruker D8 ADVANCE X-ray diffractometer operated at 40 kV and 40 mA with Cu Kα radiation (1.5406 Å). The XRD patterns were collected over 2θ from 5° to 70° with a step size of 0.02° s–1. The morphology and elemental distribution of the samples were examined by an FEI Quanta 450 scanning electron microscope (SEM) operated at 25.00 kV, mode SE, equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), and by a JEOL JEM3100F high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM) operated at 300 kV. The chemical identities and phase information on the samples were analyzed using Raman spectroscopy operated on an XploRA PLUS Raman microscope (HORIBA) with a 532 nm excitation source. The oxidation states of Mo, S, and Dy were determined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) using the AXIS SUPRA XPS system with Al Kα (1486.6 eV) as an X-ray source. The binding energy was calibrated by the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. The X-ray absorption near-edge spectra (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) were measured at the BL1.1W: MXT, Synchrotron Light Research Institute (SLRI), Thailand. The Dy L3-edge XAS spectra were collected in the fluorescence mode. The extent of catalyst detachment into electrolyte was quantified using inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) on a Perkin Elmer Avio 200 spectrometer.

Electrochemical Measurement

The electrochemical measurement was performed on a glassy carbon electrode (GCE, Ø 3 mm, surface area of 0.071 cm2). 3 mg of the MoS2/Dy sample was dispersed using a bath sonicator in 985 μL of 1:1 v/v water-dimethylformamide (DMF) with 15 μL of 5 wt % Nafion. After sonication for 1 h, 4 μL of homogeneous ink was cast onto the GCE and then dried under vacuum at 50 °C for 1 h. The sample loading was calculated to be about 170 μg cm–2 on a 3 mm GCE. For the electrochemical test, a standard three-electrode setup was equipped with a MoS2/Dy-coated GCE as the working electrode, platinum as the counter electrode, and Ag/AgCl (3 M KCl) as the reference electrode. An aqueous solution of N2-saturated 0.5 M H2SO4 was used as an electrolyte. The recorded potentials were converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale by using the following equation E RHE = E Ag/AgCl + 0.207 + (0.059 × pH). To ensure system stability, 20 cyclic voltammetry (CV) scans were performed before linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was recorded at a scan rate of 50 mV s–1. All reported potentials were manually postcorrected for 90% iR-compensation. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted across a frequency range from 10–2 to 105 Hz at non-Faradaic potentials to determine the uncompensated solution resistance and at Faradaic potentials to assess the charge transfer resistance. In addition, the double-layer capacitance (C dl) was calculated from CV data at different scan rates of 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 mV s–1 to estimate the electrochemically active surface area. The electrochemical stability of the MoS2/Dy sample was investigated using chronoamperometry at an applied potential of −0.218 V (vs RHE) for 36 h. The MoS2/Dy catalyst was drop-cast onto a carbon felt electrode with a geometric area of 0.5 × 0.5 cm2 and a loading mass of ∼0.3 mg.

DFT Calculations

First-principles calculations were carried out by the density functional theory (DFT) as implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP). The exchange–correlation interactions were described by the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA). − The projected augmented wave (PAW) method was utilized by employing a plane-wave basis set. The kinetic energy cutoff was set to 520 eV. The energy convergence criteria were set to 10–6 eV, and the residual force on each ion was set to 10–2 eV·Å–1. The Γ-centered Monkhorst–Pack scheme was used for sampling the first Brillouin zone with a spacing of 0.03 2π·Å–1. A vacuum space of 20 Å was added perpendicular to the layer to avoid self-interactions between periodic images. Finally, the adsorption energy (ΔE ads) was calculated as ΔEads=EH/surf−EMoS2−1/2EH2 where E H/surf, E MoS2 , and E H2 are the total energy of the MoS2 surface with one H adsorbed, the clean MoS2 surface, and the H2 molecule in the gas phase, respectively. The hydrogen adsorption free energy for HER (ΔG H ads) was calculated as ΔGHads=ΔEads+ΔEZPE−TΔS where ΔE ZPE is the change in zero-point energy, T is the temperature, and ΔS is the entropy change before and after H adsorption, respectively.

Results and Discussion

Figure presents the XRD patterns of MoS2/Dy with varying Dy loadings (1–7 wt %) in comparison with pristine MoS2. The distinct diffraction peaks observed at 2θ of 13.9°, 32.6°, and 57.3° correspond to (002), (100), and (110) planes of the hexagonal 2H-MoS2phase, respectively, which agrees with the standard diffraction pattern (PDF 00-037-1492). , By increasing the amount of Dy, the (002) peak of MoS2/Dy shows a broadening and reduction in intensity as supported by the quantitative data in Table S1, suggesting a gradual decrease in crystallinity and structural distortion of the layered 2H-MoS2 phase upon Dy incorporation. It is worth noting that no other impurity phases were detected in the XRD patterns of MoS2/Dy within the optimum range of Dy loading. Owing to the chemistry of rare-earth elements, it is known that these elements tend to form highly insoluble precipitates under alkaline/carbonate conditions. − In the absence of sodium citrate as a stabilizing ligand, it was found that Dy3+ ions were readily precipitated upon mixing with l-cysteine and sodium molybdate (Figure S1). This demonstrates that citrate plays a vital role in forming a stable chelating complex with Dy3+ ions in solution, thereby preventing the precipitation during the synthesis. As a result, pure-phase MoS2/Dy was successfully synthesized using the citrate-assisted hydrothermal method. However, when the mass loading of Dy exceeded 10 wt % with a molar ratio of citrate to Dy of about 2.3 for MoS2/Dy-10 and 1.4 for MoS2/Dy-15, the XRD patterns (Figure S2) show the additional sharp peaks corresponding to the presence of impurity, which might be the formation of a mixed Dy-rich oxide phase, e.g., NaDy­(MoO4)2, due to the oversaturation of Dy3+ ions beyond the coordination capacity of citrate. This suggests that the segregation and crystallization of the Dy-rich oxide phase easily occur when the molar ratio of citrate to Dy is below 3. Besides MoS2/Dy, this citrate-assisted hydrothermal method can be further applied to synthesize other rare-earth element-doped MoS2, including MoS2/Sm and MoS2/Gd, and their XRD patterns were investigated, as shown in Figure S3.

Figure b,c exhibits the SEM images of MoS2/Dy-7 at different magnifications, revealing its morphological characteristics. At low magnification, MoS2/Dy-7 exhibits a granular structure with a nonuniform but well-defined surface texture. Meanwhile, at higher magnification, a thin-layer structure becomes apparent, which is associated with MoS2 nanosheets. This hierarchical morphology is beneficial for enhancing the surface area and improving catalytic performance in electrochemical applications. The EDS spectrum of MoS2/Dy-7 in Figure d exhibits the presence of molybdenum (Mo), sulfur (S), and dysprosium (Dy), with their uniform distribution confirmed by the elemental mapping in Figure e–g, respectively. This suggests the successful incorporation and homogeneous dispersion of Dy within the MoS2 structure. Moreover, the EDS analysis of the Mo:S atomic ratio in relation to the amount of Dy found in all samples is presented in Figure h. The results demonstrate that the amount of Dy (wt %) found in MoS2 was consistent with the initial Dy loading. The Mo:S ratio of pristine MoS2 is 1:2.02, which closely agrees with its empirical formula. Meanwhile, with increasing doping level of Dy, the Mo:S ratio significantly decreases, suggesting the formation of sulfur vacancies within the MoS2 structure. Based on the decreasing Mo:S ratio, the concentrations of sulfur vacancies were determined (see Supporting Information) to be approximately 1.5% for MoS2/Dy-1, 5% for MoS2/Dy-3, 7% for MoS2/Dy-5, and 10.5% for MoS2/Dy-7. This points out the fact that substitution of Mo4+ by Dy3+ acquires sulfur vacancies to maintain overall charge neutrality in the MoS2 lattice. In addition, the HRTEM images of MoS2/Dy-7 in Figure i,j display the layered structure with a well-defined d-spacing of 0.62 nm corresponding to the (002) lattice plane of 2H-MoS2. Further high-resolution observation (Figure a) clearly resolves the atomic arrangement within the basal plane of MoS2/Dy-7, where vacancy defects are discernible as brighter contrast at the sulfur lattice sites. The uniform distribution of Dy within the MoS2 lattice is also supported by STEM-EDS elemental mapping. The results (Figure b–e) show that Dy signals are spatially codistributed with Mo and S across the examined regions without detectable Dy aggregation or phase segregation. Moreover, consistent Dy distributions were also observed in an additional representative area (Figure S4), confirming the uniformity of the Dy incorporation across the sample.

The chemical nature and valence states of MoS2/Dy were studied by XPS, and the results are shown in Figure . The Mo 3d XPS spectrum of pristine MoS2 in Figure a exhibits intense doublet peaks at 229.08 eV (Mo4+ 3d5/2) and 232.21 eV (Mo4+ 3d3/2), assigned to the presence of Mo4+ as the main valence state. Due to partial oxidation on the edge surface of MoS2, a small fraction of Mo6+ was also detected as seen by the doublet peaks at 233.35 eV (Mo6+ 3d5/2) and 236.03 eV (Mo6+ 3d3/2). The singlet S 2s peak emerges at 226.40 eV, suggesting the presence of a S2– species. In Figure b, the S 2p spectrum of MoS2 shows two obvious peaks at 161.97 and 163.27 eV, which belong to S2– 2p3/2 and S2– 2p1/2, respectively. Upon Dy substitution, both Mo 3d and S 2p peaks of MoS2/Dy-7 negatively shift toward lower binding energies; ΔE ∼ 0.31 eV for Mo4+ and ΔE ∼ 0.25 eV for S2–, which is indicative of electronic alteration with increased electron density at Mo and S atoms. This XPS shift can be primarily attributed to the electronegativity differences, where Dy3+ is less electronegative compared to Mo4+ and S2–. In Figure c, the Dy 4d XPS spectrum of MoS2/Dy-7 exhibits the doublet peaks at 154.44 eV (Dy 4d5/2) and 157.61 eV (Dy 4d3/2), demonstrating the incorporation of Dy3+ within the MoS2 structure. However, at the oversaturation of Dy loading, e.g., MoS2/Dy-15, no significant peak shifts of Mo 3d and S 2p XPS spectra (Figure S5) were observed compared to those peaks in pristine MoS2. Meanwhile, Dy 4d peaks of MoS2/Dy-15 slightly shift toward higher binding energy due to the different local environment in comparison with Dy3+ existing in MoS2/Dy-7.

Figure a presents the Raman spectra of MoS2/Dy with different Dy loadings (0–7 wt %) in comparison with pristine MoS2. Two Raman peaks are observed at 378 and 403 cm–1, which are assigned to in-plane E2g 1 mode and out-of-plane A1g mode of layered 2H-MoS2, respectively. These two vibration modes show gradual shifts in their positions (red shift from 378 to 376 cm–1 for E2g 1 and blue shift from 403 to 405 cm–1 for A1g) with increasing Dy loadings, especially MoS2/Dy-7. This indicates the variation in interlayer coupling on the local lattice environment of MoS2 due to the effects of Dy doping and the presence of sulfur vacancy. With a high loading of Dy, e.g., MoS2/Dy-15, the Raman spectrum in Figure S6 exhibits two typical characteristic peaks of 2H-MoS2 without any noticeable peak shift, suggesting the formation of a segregated Dy-containing phase mixed with MoS2, rather than substitutional doping in the lattice. Moreover, the X-ray absorption near-edge (XANES) and extended fine structure (EXAFS) at the Dy L3-edge were analyzed to elucidate the chemical environment of Dy in MoS2. In Figure b, the Dy L3-edge XANES spectra show that the absorption edge of MoS2/Dy is slightly shifted to higher energy compared to standard Dy2O3, indicating that Dy3+ possesses a less effective charge when incorporated in MoS2. This shift may be ascribed to the lower electronegativity of Dy relative to Mo and S. The white-line intensity of MoS2/Dy is obviously higher than that of Dy2O3 due to differences in the electronic structure of Dy. In Figures c and S7, the Fourier transform (FT) analysis of EXAFS spectra reveals that MoS2/Dy exhibits the first-shell peak at around 1.97 Å, corresponding to the Dy–S coordination, which notably occurs at a longer scattering distance compared to 1.72 Å for Dy2O3. These results demonstrate that Dy3+ is covalently doped into the MoS2 structure via substitution of Mo4+ sites.

The electrocatalytic performance of MoS2/Dy with different Dy loadings toward the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) was examined in a N2-saturated 0.5 M H2SO4 solution (pH ∼ 0.3). The LSV curves exhibit the HER performance of MoS2/Dy, and the overpotentials at a current density of 10 mA cm–2 are reported with iR-compensation in Figure a to reflect the inherent catalytic activity of the samples. The LSV curves without iR-compensation in Figure S8 were also plotted for comparison. The overall results in Figure c demonstrate that the overpotentials of MoS2/Dy with different Dy loadings were found to be 247 mV for MoS2/Dy-3, 233 mV for MoS2/Dy-5, 202 mV for MoS2/Dy-7, and 332 mV for pristine MoS2. These results suggest that the electrocatalytic activity of MoS2/Dy toward HER gets improved with increasing Dy incorporation, reaching an optimum performance at a Dy loading of 7 wt %. This enhancement can be ascribed to the increased number of active sites on the MoS2 basal plane, facilitated by the formation of sulfur vacancies in addition to the inherent edge sites. By varying Dy loadings, the concentration of sulfur vacancies is about 10.5% for MoS2/Dy-7, which agrees with the value previously reported by Zheng et al., who found that a sulfur vacancy concentration of around 11% maximizes the active size density. However, further increasing the Dy content leads to a decrease in HER performance, as seen by the higher overpotentials of 225 mV for MoS2/Dy-10 and 242 mV for MoS2/Dy-15. This performance degradation is ascribed to the formation of a segregated Dy-rich oxide phase, e.g., NaDy­(MoO4)2, which blocks the MoS2 active sites and hinders electron transfer kinetics. For comparison, MoS2/Sm-7 and MoS2/Gd-7 exhibit overpotentials of 252 and 262 mV, respectively, indicating relatively lower HER performance than MoS2/Dy-7, as shown in Figure S9.

(a) LSV curves, (b) Tafel plots, (c) overpotentials measured at 10 mA cm–2 and Tafel plots for comparison, (d) CV plots of MoS2/Dy-7 at different scan rates, (e) double-layer capacitance (C dl) by plotting current against the scan rate, and (f) Nyquist plots.

The inherent reaction kinetics of MoS2/Dy toward HER was analyzed from the Tafel plots, which was obtained from LSV curves, as shown in Figure b. The Tafel slopes of MoS2/Dy with different Dy loadings were found to be 113.7 mV dec–1 for MoS2/Dy-3, 101.3 mV dec–1 for MoS2/Dy-5, and 53.9 mV dec–1 for MoS2/Dy-7, in comparison with 227.7 mV dec–1 for pristine MoS2. This marked decrease in Tafel slopes suggests that incorporation of Dy leads to the enhanced reaction kinetics of MoS2 toward HER, and the reaction involves a Volmer–Heyrovsky mechanism. Moreover, CV was performed in 0.5 M H2SO4 at various scan rates from 2.5 mV to 50 mV s–1 within the non-Faradaic region. In Figure d, the MoS2/Dy-7 electrode exhibits a characteristic square-shaped curve at low scan rates, indicating capacitive behavior. At higher scan rates, the CV curves deviate from this square shape, displaying a combination of capacitive and redox features. This deviation suggests a transition in the charge storage mechanism, where fast redox reactions contribute greatly to the overall capacitance due to a pseudocapacitive behavior. The non-Faradaic process mainly involves proton adsorption onto the MoS2/Dy-7 surface. This mechanism aligns with the Volmer step in HER, where hydrogen atoms are adsorbed on the catalyst surface before forming molecular hydrogen. The pseudocapacitive behavior of MoS2/Dy-7 plays a crucial role in enhancing the HER activity by facilitating proton adsorption and charge transfer kinetics. This phenomenon can be confirmed by evaluating the electrochemical surface area, which is reflected by C dl. As shown in Figure e, MoS2/Dy-7 exhibits a higher C dl (7.57 mF cm–2) compared to that of pristine MoS2 (0.73 mF cm–2), demonstrating an increased number of electrochemically active sites attributable to the presence of sulfur vacancies as induced by Dy doping. In addition, Nyquist plots of all electrocatalysts in Figure f show a distinct semicircle, which refers to resistance at the interface between the electrode and electrolyte. The charge transfer resistance (R ct) was determined to be 555 Ω for MoS2/Dy-3, 440 Ω for MoS2/Dy-5, and 130 Ω for MoS2/Dy-7, in comparison with 1800 Ω for pristine MoS2. It reveals that the R ct decreases significantly with the increase of Dy loading from 0 to 7 wt %, indicating a quick charge transfer and enhanced electrocatalytic activity of MoS2 toward HER. However, excessive Dy loading (10–15 wt %) leads to negative results on the HER performance of MoS2 with an increase in both Tafel slope and Rct. Based on all above analyses, MoS2/Dy with the optimum Dy loading of 7 wt % demonstrates the most efficient electrocatalytic activity with the lowest overpotential, Tafel slope, and charge transfer resistance, which collectively facilitate hydrogen production in acid electrolytes. This enhancement in electrochemical performance can be supported by the increased availability of active sites, especially on the basal planes, due to the presence of sulfur vacancies and Dy3+ incorporation in the MoS2 structure.

To further elucidate the correlation between intrinsic HER activity and sulfur vacancy concentration, the turnover frequency per sulfur vacancy (TOFS‑vacancy) at 0 V (vs RHE) was calculated from the exchange current density (j 0) and the sulfur vacancy site density (N S‑vacancy) following a previously reported method (see the Supporting Information for detailed calculations). As shown in Figure , TOFS‑vacancy initially increases from MoS2/Dy-1 (1.5%S-vacancy) to MoS2/Dy-7 (10.5%S-vacancy), reaching the maximum of 0.465 s–1 at MoS2/Dy-7, and then decreases at MoS2/Dy-10. With an optimal amount of Dy loading and sulfur vacancy concentration, MoS2/Dy exhibits a TOFS‑vacancy of 0.209–0.465 s–1, exceeding those of the MoS2 edge sites (0.013–0.02 s–1) and [Mo3S4]4+ cluster of amorphous MoS2 (0.07 s–1) previously reported. − These results further confirm that the sulfur vacancies in the basal plane of 2H-MoS2 when incorporated with Dy can serve as additional, active, and tunable catalytic sites for HER.

The HER electrocatalytic stability of MoS2/Dy-7 was evaluated based on continuous CV at a scan rate of 50 mV s–1 and a chronoamperometric test. As shown in Figure a, the LSV curves obtained before and after 2000 cycles exhibit only slight changes in overpotentials, indicating a sustained hydrogen production performance. For chronoamperometric measurement at a current density of about 10 mA cm–2, Figure b shows that the current density exhibits only minimal variation over 36 h, suggesting a steady current under the applied potential for HER. To further evaluate the structural stability of the catalyst, MoS2/Dy-7 after reacting for 36 h was further characterized using TEM. The HRTEM images of the used MoS2/Dy-7 (Figure S10) reveal that the layered structure, well-defined lattice spacing, and uniform elemental distribution were preserved after prolonged HER. Moreover, the extent of catalyst detachment into the electrolyte was quantified by ICP-OES analysis, illustrating a Mo dissolution amount of 0.017 mg (0.72 ppm in 25 mL of electrolyte) after the stability test. These results demonstrate that Dy doping along with the formation of sulfur vacancies can synergistically enhance the electrocatalytic activity and stability of MoS2 toward HER under acid conditions.

Compared with previously reported transition-metal dopants, Dy possesses a larger ionic radius with a strong affinity for anion-deficient environments, which facilitates the stabilization of sulfur vacancy and induces strong electronic perturbation of the MoS2 lattice. This Dy substitution effectively activates basal-plane sulfur atoms through synergistic electronic and defect effects, resulting in an enhanced HER performance. As summarized in Table , the HER activity based on overpotentials and Tafel slopes achieved by Dy-doped MoS2 is competitive with or superior to that of transition-metal-doped MoS2.

To gain in-depth insight into the mechanism during HER, in situ Raman spectroscopy was employed to monitor the dynamic change in the lattice structure of the MoS2/Dy catalyst. During the HER process, the in situ Raman spectra of MoS2/Dy-7 (Figure a) exhibit two characteristic bands of E2g 1 and A1g, and their peak positions are dependent on applied potentials. Upon changing the potential toward negative values (from the OCV to −0.4 V), a pronounced blue shift is observed, particularly for the A1g mode. This behavior can be attributed to the adsorption of hydrogen intermediates at sulfur vacancies, which reduces the structural disorder and strengthens the MoS2 lattice. Similar to the previous report, no direct evidence of the Mo–H vibration mode is detected in the expected range of 1714–1942 cm–1, probably due to the rapid chemisorption and subsequent recombination during the HER process. When the potential is further decreased to −0.6 V, vigorous H2 bubble evolution with a rapid release rate occurs, accompanied by slight Raman peak shifts and a decrease in peak intensity. Moreover, it is worth noting that no S–H vibration band at 2560 cm–1 is observed (Figure S11) in contrast to the previous reports. , This indicates that hydrogen intermediates are predominantly involved in adsorption at sulfur vacancy sites on the MoS2 basal surface during the HER.

DFT calculations were further performed to unveil the synergy between vacancy (V s) and Dy doping in tuning the HER activity of MoS2 in an acidic solution. In general, the HER reaction follows a two-step pathway, and the hydrogen adsorption free energy (ΔG H ads) serves as a descriptor for HER activity. For the optimum catalyst performance, ΔG H ads should be close to zero, ensuring that the active site binds hydrogen neither too strongly nor weakly. As shown in Figure b, hydrogen adsorption on the basal plane of pristine 2H-MoS2 is thermodynamically unfavorable with a ΔG H ads of 2.10 eV. Upon substitution of a Mo atom with Dy, the hydrogen adsorption on a neighboring sulfur atom has a marked decrease in ΔG H ads to 0.39 eV, suggesting the activation of the in-plane sulfur site adjacent to the Dy atom. When the sulfur vacancy is introduced along with Dy substitution, the ΔG H ads of the hydrogen adsorption near the vacancy becomes negative (−0.30 eV), providing more favorable energetics for the HER. Additional calculations were performed on other representative active sites: (i) a Dy-related substitutional site and (ii) an S atom adjacent to a sulfur vacancy. The corresponding ΔG H ads were calculated to be +0.51 eV and +0.64 eV, respectively, as shown in Figure S12. Although these values are not optimal, they are significantly lower than those of pristine 2H-MoS2 (2.10 eV), indicating that Dy incorporation and defect formation substantially activate inert basal-plane sulfur sites. These results highlight that Dy doping does not create a single dominant active site, but instead generates multiple catalytic active sites with different hydrogen-binding strengths. Based on in situ Raman and DFT calculation results, the enhanced HER performance arises from a concentration-dependent balance between Dy-induced electronic modulation, defect-mediated formation of basal-plane sulfur atoms, and their cooperative contribution to hydrogen adsorption and evolution.

Conclusion

In summary, Dy-doped MoS2 was successfully synthesized via citrate-assisted hydrothermal treatment with the variation of Dy loadings. Citrate can serve as a complexing agent to stabilize Dy ions and prevent their premature precipitation in the reaction mixture. The incorporation of Dy3+ ions into the MoS2 lattice induced a significant change in its electronic structure and facilitated the formation of sulfur vacancies. Among all catalysts, MoS2 with the optimum Dy loading (7 wt %) exhibits the superior performance for hydrogen evolution, achieving 10 mA cm–2 at an overpotential of 202 mV in acid media. The Tafel slope of 53 mV dec–1 also indicates that hydrogen production is proposed to follow the Volmer–Heyrovsky mechanism. Both experimental and DFT calculation results suggest that the enhanced electrochemical performance of MoS2/Dy arises from the increased number of active sites, particularly on the basal planes, due to Dy3+ incorporation and the presence of sulfur vacancies within the MoS2 structure. Moreover, the modification strategy developed in this study can be extended to other rare-earth dopants, such as Sm and Gd, with characteristics similar to those of Dy. This work provides valuable insights into the role of rare-earth doping in modulating the structure and electronic properties of MoS2 and its impact on the electrocatalytic performance toward HER.
