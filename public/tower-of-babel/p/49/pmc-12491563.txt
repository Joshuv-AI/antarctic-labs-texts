CeOx-Integrated dual site enhanced urea electrosynthesis from nitrate and carbon dioxide
Authors: Xu Wu, Yang Chen, Bing Tang, Qiong Yan, Deyu Wu, Heng Zhou, Hao Wang, Heng Zhang, Daoping He, Hui Li, Jianrong Zeng, Lanlu Lu, Song Yang, Tianyi Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Electrocatalytic urea synthesis via the co-reduction of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{NO}}}}_{3}^{-}$$\end{document}NO3− and CO2 as a promising option to the conventional Bosch-Meiser remains challenged by regulating desired intermediates to simultaneously achieve a high yield and Faradaic efficiency. Here, we integrate the substrate material (SiO2) and functionally atomic sites (Cu and Sn) utilizing CeOx nanoclusters as ‘adhesive’, in which the CeOx and SiO2 form the composite carrier (CS) construct Cu and Sn diatomic electrocatalyst (CuSn/CS−1). Spectroscopic techniques and density functional theory calculations reveal that overall charge redistribution in the CeOx−CuSn modules forms bifunctional active sites with unique electronic properties and abundant oxygen vacancies. The Cu sites mediate the conversion of CO2 to *CO through a single carbon-coordinated structure with *CO2−, while Sn sites regulate the reduction of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{NO}}}}_{3}^{-}$$\end{document}NO3− to stabilize the formation of *NH2, broadening the C−N coupling route. Oxygen vacancies provide additional electron storage sites and promote the electron flow during the electrocatalytic process. CuSn/CS−1 achieves a urea yield of 55.81 mmol g−1cat. h−1 with a Faradaic efficiency of 79.27% in H-cell at −0.7 V versus the reversible hydrogen electrode. This work overcomes the traditional trade-off between urea yield and Faradaic efficiency, providing a feasible and sustainable strategy.

Introduction

Urea serves as a vital nitrogen fertilizer and chemical feedstock, performing a major function in supporting the rapid expansion of global agricultural production in response to the growing demands of a burgeoning population1,2. However, industrial urea production depends on the Bosch-Meiser method, in which NH3 reacts with CO2 under aggressive circumstances of 150–200 °C and 15–25 MPa (Fig. 1a). The synthesis of NH3, a key substrate, is achieved through the Haber-Bosch route, which alone accounts for 1–2% of energy consumption on Earth3. The energy-intensive nature of this synthesis, coupled with its substantial carbon footprint, has exacerbated the ongoing global environmental and energy crises4,5. These environmental and energy burdens underscore the necessity for greener urea synthesis strategies. Recent research suggests that employing renewable electricity to convert wastewater pollutants, specifically CO2 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{NO}}}}_{3}^{-}$$\end{document}NO3−, into urea represents a sustainable and mutually beneficial strategy6–8. The approach offers a promising route to more sustainable and eco-friendly urea production by efficiently capturing CO2, which can utilize the abundant small molecule resources of the Earth9–11.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of urea synthesis.a–c Schematic illustration of urea production via (a) traditional industrial process, (b) electrocatalysis method, and (c) the proposed strategy in this work.

Production of value-added compounds through the co-reduction system of CO2 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{NO}}}}_{3}^{-}$$\end{document}NO3− represents a forward-looking and sustainable approach. Although several well-designed studies have been reported in this field of research, the CO2 molecule remains chemically inert due to the large energy difference between the highest-occupancy and lowest-empty orbitals12–14, so decoupling of the C=O bond demands considerable energy input of up to 800 kJ mol−1, which tends to produce significant conversion barriers such as slow kinetics and poor reactivity9,11. Carbon/oxygen coordination is the primary bonding mode between CO2 and active sites in the CO2 reduction reaction (CRR). Designing active sites with unique electronic and structural properties is a proven strategy to reduce activation energy and establish specific protonation path evolution by forming distinct adsorption models with substrate molecules15. Similarly, modulating the adsorption configuration of carbon-containing species by local charge modification is a promising tactic to enhance the overall performance of urea synthesis16. This strategy addresses the critical challenge of selectively converting inert CO2 to *CO, while simultaneously avoiding the formation of undesirable carbon-containing byproducts and enhancing the potential for C−N bond formation.

The controlled formation of C−N coupling precursors remains challenging due to competing hydrogenation pathways that generate complex intermediate species and undesirable byproducts. Typically, urea formation necessitates the combination of two nitrogen precursors with a carbon precursor, but a substantial number of nitrogen-containing byproducts severely impede the formation of these nitrogen precursors17. The low concentration of nitrogen precursors in the solution further exacerbates the challenge, making it difficult for the hard-won carbon precursors to couple effectively in subsequent steps8,18. These challenges collectively contribute to the narrow synthetic window for urea synthesis from CO2 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{NO}}}}_{3}^{-}$$\end{document}NO3− (Fig. 1b). Consequently, precise engineering of active sites and modulation of reactant adsorption behaviors have emerged as critical scientific hurdles that must be addressed to advance this field. Recent breakthroughs in bimetallic catalysts have established intermediate stabilization (NH2/CO or *CO/*NO2) as an effective urea Faradaic efficiency (FE) enhancement strategy, including Pdδ−−Cuδ+ (66.4%, FEurea) and Cu−W (70%, FEurea) systems12,19. However, the trade-off between FE and yield, coupled with energy efficiency concerns, reveals fundamental limitations in current research. Therefore, it is imperative to actively explore integrated strategies to address the above requirements and analyze the complex electron transfer mechanism and stability regulation of intermediates.

In this work, we ingeniously utilize specific defects and isolated CeOx nanoislands as an ‘adhesive’ to integrate diverse components, achieving effective dispersion of the islands on SiO2 and spatial isolation of Cu−Sn atoms between islands. The strategy successfully constructs highly active Cu and Sn atomic sites for capturing and activating both carbon and nitrogen species (Fig. 1c), achieving an impressive urea yield of 55.81 mmol g−1cat. h−1 at − 0.7 V versus the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE, all potentials are versus RHE). The CeOx-integrated diatomics induce an overall charge redistribution in the module, resulting in the formation of lots of bifunctional active centers and oxygen vacancies (Ov). These features suppress competing CO2 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{NO}}}}_{3}^{-}$$\end{document}NO3− reduction pathways while broadening the C−N coupling channel. In situ electrochemical characterization techniques confirm *CO and *NH2 as pivotal intermediates, whose formation rates directly correlate with urea production. Remarkably, 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) oxidation replaces the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) at the anode in the gas diffusion electrode (GDE), achieving a yield of 2,5-furandicarboxylic acid (FDCA) of 84.56%, while the cathode provides a urea yield of 154.50 mmol g−1cat. h−1, highlighting the potential in industrial-scale applications and advancing sustainable electrochemical urea synthesis.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of catalysts

Fumed SiO2 (300 m2 g−1) was selected as the primary substrate material, onto which small CeOx nanoislands were grafted via the strong electrostatic adsorption20. Following NH4OH treatment, the Ce precursor in an aqueous solution was converted into [Ce(OH)x]y+ species, which subsequently formed an electrostatic adsorption layer on the SiO2 surface after deprotonation of OH groups20,21. CeOx/SiO2 (CS) prepared by high-temperature calcination at 600 °C yielded high-density CeOx nanoislands that dispersed on SiO2 (Fig. 2a). CeOx nanoislands have average dimensions of 1.85 nm × 2.00 nm and exhibit a uniform elemental distribution proven by Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis, confirming the successful preparation of CS composite support (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 1). Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) indicated Ce content of 11.32 wt.% in CS (Supplementary Table 1). Subsequently, the isolated CeOx served as the main anchors for the deposition of metal atoms, synthesizing catalysts loaded Cu (Cu/CS) and CuSn (CuSn/CS−1) (Fig. 2e, i). Nanoislands still maintained their small dimension with average sizes at 1.77 nm × 1.90 nm and 1.99 nm × 2.02 nm after loading metal atoms, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). The CS and CuSn/CS−1 exhibited a crystalline CeOx phase and an amorphous SiO2 phase (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). According to AC-HAADF-STEM imaging, CeOx clusters showed uniform bright clusters, while the introduction of metals caused a noticeable variation in brightness across the clusters (Fig. 2c, f, j), indicating the integration of Cu and Sn atoms. This observation was further supported by Z-contrast difference images, which enabled the approximate localization of loaded metal atoms using corresponding 3D-fitted models. The intensity profiles along line a and line b also revealed the dispersion of atoms across the CeOx surface (Fig. 2g, l). Noteworthily, EDS analysis of Cu/CS and CuSn/CS−1 revealed that while Cu and Sn atoms were uniformly dispersed across the CS support (Fig. 2h, m), they were predominantly concentrated on the CeOx nanoislands (Fig. 2k). This resulted in spatial dispersion of small size CeOx on SiO2 while confining most Cu and Sn atoms to the nanoislands, thereby preventing metal atom agglomeration during high-temperature calcination and enabling the successful preparation of atomically dispersed active sites. The distribution of dual sites in a limited spatial region provides favorable conditions for the interactional effect of electronic structures between atoms and the transfer of key intermediates. By using comparative experiments, the AC-HAADF-STEM images of Cu and Sn species supported on a single SiO2 carrier (CuSn/SiO2) showed that the metal sites sintered into fine metallic nanoparticles after high-temperature preparation (Supplementary Fig. 6a), leading to a significant reduction in atomic dispersion. The X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) spectra of CS, CuSn/CS−1, and other samples showed the dominant structure of cubic CeOx with a fluorite structure (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 6b, c). The corresponding diffraction peaks of CS at the [111] plane slightly shifted to a lower angle compared to CeO2 (CeO2: PDF#34-0394), which broadens the lattice spacing in CeOx due to the lattice expansion20. This observation was further confirmed by HRTEM, where the lattice spacing of CeOx was measured to be 0.33 nm at the [111] plane, which was wider than the 0.31 nm spacing observed for CeO2 (Fig. 2d). In Raman spectra, CeO2 has a clear robust band at 465 cm−1 attributed to the F2g vibration mode of the fluorite structure. The peak in the sample containing CS is red-shifted and widened in the range of 465–442 cm−1, indicating significant lattice distortion in CeOx (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 7). Furthermore, a weak band appears at approximately 600 cm−1 in the CS-containing sample, and its intensity increases with metal loading, signifying an increase in the concentration of Ov. Elemental charge states were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Supplementary Fig. 8). The Ce 3d spectrum can be deconvoluted into ten peaks (Fig. 3c), and the Ce3+ content was calculated based on the integrated area of the two pairs of peaks in the green-filled region. The surface concentration of Ce3+ in CeOx gradually increased from CeO2, CS, to CuSn/CS−1, leading to a progressive rise in the [Ce3+]/[Ce4+] ratio, which reflected the content of Ov (Supplementary Fig. 9)22,23. This trend is likely attributed to the partial substitution of Ce4+ by Cu or Sn metal ions doped into the CeOx lattice, which generates additional Ov on the surface to maintain charge neutrality. This hypothesis was further supported by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements (Fig. 3d), wherein introduction of Cu or Sn metals correlated with an increase in Ov, consistent with the findings from Raman and XPS analyses24. The XPS spectrum of Cu/CS featured prominent peaks at 932.9 and 952.8 eV assigned to Cu+, alongside peaks at 934.9 and 954.9 eV attributed to Cu2+ (Fig. 3e)25. High-energy-resolution Auger electron spectroscopy was employed to further distinguish between Cu0 and Cu+ states (Supplementary Fig. 10), revealing a peak corresponding to Cu+ at ~ 570.0 eV, with no signal for Cu0 at ~ 567.9 eV in the Cu LMM spectrum. For Sn/CS, Sn4+ and Sn2+ were identified by peaks at 487.3 eV and 486.3 eV, respectively (Fig. 3f)26. Notably, the influence of the introduction of Cu/Sn atoms on the electronic structures of both sides was evidenced by opposing binding energy shifts, with positive shifts in the Cu 2p orbitals and negative shifts in Sn 3 d orbitals in CuSn/CS−1. Simultaneously, the valence state of Si observed in the Si 2p spectra continuously remains unchanged with the increase of [Ce3+]/[Ce4+] ratio (Supplementary Fig. 11a), which indicates that there is an overall charge redistribution in the Cu and Sn diatomic modules of the CeOx combination, effectively regulating the electrons at Cu and Sn. The XANES was employed to determine the Cu K-edge of CuSn/CS−1 along with a series of standard references, which revealed that the white line peak of Cu within CuSn/CS−1 was positioned between Cu2O and CuO standards, demonstrating an average valence state of Cu atoms between Cu+ and Cu2+ (Fig. 3g)27. The chemical environment of Cu was deeply investigated using FT-EXAFS (Fig. 3h). The data show a prominent first-shell peak attributed to the Cu−O bond at ~ 1.52 Å, while no peak associated with the Cu−Cu pathway at ~ 2.22 Å is observed. The Sn K-edge XANES spectra exhibited a white line peak intermediate between Sn foil and SnO2 (Fig. 3i), suggesting that the average valence state for Sn in the sample is between Sn0 and Sn4+28. A characteristic Sn−O bond was identified at 1.56 Å in the FT-EXAFS spectrum of CuSn/CS−1, without detectable Sn−Sn bond around 2.76 Å (Fig. 3j). This analysis confirmed the atomic dispersion of Cu and Sn atoms and their stabilization through oxygen coordination in the structure. Quantitative fitting of the EXAFS data for both Cu and Sn atoms revealed coordination numbers of 4 for Cu and 6 for Sn (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13 and Supplementary Table 2). The coordination information is further confirmed by the horizontal axis representing the photoelectron wavevector (k) in the wavelet transform spectrum, which shows that the Cu−O and Sn−O bonds coordinated by light elements (k: 4–6) exhibit the strongest oscillations in the lower k regions compared to the transition metal-coordinated Cu−Cu and Sn−Sn bonds (k: 7–9). Although both Cu foil and Sn foil also display a single dominant peak, their observed k values show distinct differences compared to those of the Cu−O and Sn−O bonds in CuSn/CS−1 (Fig. 3k, l). Comparative XANES spectra for Cu/CS and Sn/CS further validate that the incorporation of Cu and Sn individually exerts distinct influences on the electronic structure (Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15). In particular, the right shift for the adsorption edge position of Cu K-edge indicates the elevated oxidation state of Cu, and the left shift for the adsorption edge position of Sn K-edge indicates the decrease in the oxidation state of Sn in CuSn/CS−1, which contributes to the electron-deficient and electron-rich properties of the Cu and Sn sites, respectively, providing important evidence for the charge redistribution in the aforementioned proposed CeOx−CuSn module. However, Cu−Sn bonds were not observed in either FT-EXAFS and wavelet transform spectra, further confirming the mutual influence of the electronic structures between Cu and Sn occurred, which is highly consistent with XPS results.

Electrocatalytic performance of urea synthesis

Electrocatalytic performance of CuSn/CS−1 for urea synthesis was rigorously evaluated in an H-cell under ambient conditions, utilizing various electrolyte solutions with saturated CO2 with 0.1 M KNO3 (Supplementary Fig. 16). CuSn/CS−1 and other samples were subjected to linear scanning voltammetry (LSV) experiments in Ar and CO2 atmospheres (Fig. 4a) and other electrolytic conditions (Supplementary Fig. 17) to get a deeper understanding of the electrochemical behavior. The data demonstrated that the introduction of CO2 led to a more pronounced current response, suggesting that CRR caused additional current consumption. CuSn/CS−1 exhibited the most pronounced current response when both CO2 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{NO}}}}_{3}^{-}$$\end{document}NO3− were introduced simultaneously, undergoing a sharp voltage shift at approximately − 0.7 V. This sudden change showed that CuSn/CS−1 actively promoted the reduction of both CO2 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{NO}}}}_{3}^{-}$$\end{document}NO3−, possibly sparking a C−N coupling that was beneficial for urea formation. Urea electrosynthesis was rigorously evaluated through chronoamperometry (CA) tests (Supplementary Fig. 18), conducted within an active potential window (− 0.5 V to − 1.0 V). To avoid potential interference from NO2− on the quantification of urea in diacetylmonoxime-thiosemicarbazide (DAMO-TSC), its concentration was measured using the urease decomposition method (Supplementary Figs. 19−24). High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) analysis was performed on the post-electrolysis products utilizing 14N- and 15N-labeled KNO3 as nitrogen sources, with reference to standard urea samples for accurate identification (Fig. 4b). Signals corresponding to CO(14NH2)2 and CO(15NH2)2 were detected at mass-to-charge ratios of 61 and 63, respectively, confirming that urea is synthesized via the co-reduction of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{NO}}}}_{3}^{-}$$\end{document}NO3− and CO229. These findings were further validated by 1H NMR (Supplementary Fig. 25). First, the sample with a Cu:Sn mass ratio of 2.50:0.42 was experimentally screened as the optimum catalyst, which had a urea yield of 55.81 mmol g−1cat. h−1 and an FEurea of 79.27% at − 0.7 V, and this result was further verified by 1H NMR spectra (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Figs. 28−30). Urea yield of CS, Sn/CS, and Cu/CS was experimentally evaluated for 3.20 mmol g−1cat. h−1, 11.81 mmol g−1cat. h−1, and 35.69 mmol g−1cat. h−1, all of which were substantially lower than that of CuSn/CS−1 (Supplementary Figs. 31−34). Comparatively, the urea generation rate of the small amount of CS is almost negligible, and the combination of previous experimental results at low metal loading further verifies that CuSn plays a decisive role. NH3 is mainly a nitrogenous byproduct in the urea synthesis on Cu/CS, which indicates that it has a higher selectivity to deeply reduce \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathrm{NO}}}_{2}^{-}$$\end{document}NO2− to NH3, but it caused unnecessary current energy consumption (Fig. 4d), resulting in a FEurea of only 18.02% at the urea yield of 35.69 mmol g−1cat. h−1. The prominent activation and reduction of CO2 by Cu as the active site have been well-documented in the field of CRR30,31. While the FECO of Cu/CS and Sn/CS catalysts is comparable, the quantity of CO produced on Cu/CS substantially exceeds that of Sn/CS, with a maximum ratio reaching 12.8 times (Supplementary Fig. 35). An enhancement in the FECO of CuSn/CS−1 was observed, indicating an improved conversion process from CO2 to CO, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathrm{NO}}}_{2}^{-}$$\end{document}NO2− being the primarily nitrogen-containing byproduct (Fig. 4f). Hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) activity intensifies across those catalysts at elevated potentials, compromising urea selectivity. CuSn/CS−1 maintains favorable performance at moderate potentials (− 0.5 V to − 0.8 V), where FEurea surpasses the cumulative FE of all byproducts. The NH3 production rate rises with the applied potential, and this augmentation is especially noticeable at potentials beyond − 0.7 V, according to quantitative examination of the nitrogen-containing byproducts produced over CuSn/CS−1 (Supplementary Fig. 36). In contrast, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathrm{NO}}}_{2}^{-}$$\end{document}NO2− exhibits a high production rate across a wide range of potentials, with a slight decrease observed at − 0.7 V, where both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathrm{FE}}}_{{{\mathrm{NO}}}_{2}^{-}}$$\end{document}FENO2− and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathrm{FE}}}_{{{\mathrm{NH}}}_{3}}$$\end{document}FENH3 reach their suitable values. Under equivalent potentials, CuSn/CS−1 exhibited 3.15-fold and 3.70-fold enhancements in nitrogen selectivity toward urea formation compared to Sn/CS and Cu/CS, respectively (Fig. 4g). Moreover, carbon selectivity of over 90.0% is maintained within a broad potential window (Fig. 4h). Performance of urea production is compared with published electrocatalysts, highlighting its intrinsic high activity (Fig. 4i). These catalysts can be divided into two groups: those with high urea yield rates but low FEurea (pink region), and those with high FEurea but low urea yield rates (green region). Achieving high performance in both urea yield and FEurea is notably rare, with CuSn/CS−1 outperforming the vast majority of existing catalysts by a significant margin (Supplementary Table 8)8,12,14,19,32–50.

Systematic evaluations were conducted in 0.1 M KNO3 solutions across a pH range of 1–14 to further optimize the electrocatalytic urea synthesis performance. The catalyst exhibited robust activity under mildly acidic to neutral conditions, with excessively acidic (pH < 4) or alkaline (pH > 9) environments significantly suppressing performance. The catalyst achieved peak performance with a remarkable urea yield rate of 60.99 mmol g−1cat. h−1 at pH 7 and a favorable FEurea of 81.20% at pH 6 (Supplementary Fig. 37). Under standard testing conditions (CO2-saturated 0.1 M KNO3, pH 4.7), both yield and efficiency are included within their favorable activity ranges. To address mass transfer limitations, a GDE system was employed, significantly enhancing CO2 availability and electron utilization. At applied potentials of − 0.5, − 0.6, − 0.7, − 0.8, − 0.9, and − 1.0 V, the average urea yield rate is 40.12, 89.45, 137.50, 148.10, 127.50, and 100.14 mmol g−1cat. h−1, with corresponding FEurea of 46.08%, 69.53%, 61.42%, 50.94%, 35.80%, and 19.63% (Supplementary Figs. 38a−c). The GDE system produced 2.46 times more urea than the H-cell system at − 0.7 V, demonstrating its potential for industrial-scale applications despite a moderate decline in FEurea. The experiment scaled up the catalyst loading to 0.2 mg cm−2 and conducted a 10 h long-term electrolysis in GDE (Supplementary Figs. 38d, e). The electrolyte was lyophilized to remove water, and urea was extracted from the solids using benzene, followed by solvent evaporation to obtain 38.5 mg of purified urea. Furthermore, replacing the conventional oxygen evolution reaction (OER) with 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) oxidation achieved dual benefits (Supplementary Fig. 39): (1) Thermodynamically favorable HMF oxidation reduced overall energy consumption and increased the overall FEurea. (2) The anode achieved 84.56% 2,5-Furandicarboxylic acid (FDCA) yield and 93.52% HMF conversion, while the cathode delivered a urea yield rate of 154.50 mmol g−1cat. h−1—a significant improvement over OER-coupled systems.

Electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) was measured using double-layer capacitance to evaluate the intrinsic activities of the electrocatalyst (Cdl, Supplementary Fig. 40). The ECSA values increased with higher metal atom loading (CS < Su/CS < Cu/CS < CuSn/CS−1), with CuSn/CS−1 exhibiting the highest ECSA value of 3.325. The results demonstrate that the CuSn/CS−1 electrode possesses a higher charge storage capacity and more dense active sites, enhancing the performance of mediating charge transfer at the electrode-electrolyte interface. CuSn/CS−1 still exhibits the maximum intrinsic activity even after normalization by ECSA, suggesting that the enhanced performance of the synthesized urea originates from the nature of active sites rather than the changes in ECSA (Supplementary Fig. 41). Nyquist plots from electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) reveal (Fig. 4j and Supplementary Fig. 42a) that pure CeO2 exhibits poor conductivity due to its semiconductor nature. However, the formation of CS after compounding with SiO2 significantly reduces the internal resistance of the sample, providing superior charge transfer capability. Conversion of Ce4+ to Ce3+ provides free electrons and Ov according to the Kröger-Vink notation (defect reaction: 2CeCe + Oo→Vo”+2Ce’Ce + 1/2 O2), enhancing the conductivity of the material (Supplementary Fig. 42b)51–53. The relationship between mass transfer and electrochemical properties was further investigated using DRT analysis (Fig. 4k). Three distinct features—substrate diffusion, charge transfer, and mass transfer processes—were identified in the low-frequency (LF), intermediate-frequency (IF), and high-frequency (HF) regions, respectively54. The intensity of these three peaks diminished with increasing metal loading with the introduction of Cu and Sn, suggesting reduced polarization resistances and enhanced transport properties for the electrode. To verify the relationship between Ov and conductivity, CeO2/SiO2 (with lower Ov content) is employed as an alternative supporting to replace the Ov-rich CeOx (Supplementary Fig. 42c). The EIS measurements clearly demonstrated that these systems exhibited significantly higher charge transfer resistance compared to CuSn/CS−1 (Supplementary Fig. 42d), conclusively proving that samples with higher Ov content possess superior conductivity. Collectively, it can be seen that the unique interactions between the components effectively compensate for the conductive defects caused by SiO2 in the structure. The experiment also performed electrochemical tests on CuSn/CS−1 with conductive carbon black and a SiO2-free sample (CuSn/CeOx). The results showed that although the bulk conductivity of the electrodes was improved, the urea yield of CuSn/CS−1 (carbon black) was not enhanced, and also led to a slight decrease in FEurea (Supplementary Fig. 43a, b). Meanwhile, the urea yield of CuSn/CeOx not only decreased, but also the FEurea was obviously reduced (Supplementary Fig. 43c). This suggests that the higher conductive components form a lower resistance pathway, which does not necessarily enhance the relevant catalytic performance. The introduction of additional conductive constituents may complicate the reaction system, thereby reducing catalyst selectivity, which demonstrates that the presence of highly active catalytic species is ultimately more crucial than bulk electrical conductivity for determining overall performance. By comparison, core-shell SiO2@CeOx (SC) supporting is also fabricated to elucidate structure-performance relationships (Supplementary Figs. 44 and 45a), revealing significant geometric effects on catalytic behavior. The core-shell CuSn/SC exhibited improved charge transfer resistance compared to the original CuSn/CS−1 (Supplementary Fig. 43a), but its urea synthesis performance significantly declined (Supplementary Fig. 43d). The CeOx clusters exhibit non-uniform sizes, irregular shapes, and relatively larger dimensions in the core-shell CuSn/SC (Supplementary Fig. 45b, c). The narrowing and shifting of XRD diffraction peaks further confirms that this structural configuration is unfavorable for the formation of small-sized CeOx (Supplementary Fig. 45d). The comparison of the activity for dual atomic sites supported by different carriers demonstrates that the structural, property, and synthesis method differences among carriers significantly influence the catalytic performance of active sites, highlighting the critical role of carrier design in the construction of atomic-level catalysts.

CuSn/CS−1 maintained its urea yield and FEurea over 9 cycles, confirming its excellent reusability (Fig. 4l). After prolonged electrolysis, the electrochemical impedance spectra were reanalyzed using the DRT technique (Supplementary Fig. 46), revealing only a slight increase in resistance. CuSn/CS−1 demonstrated favorable catalytic performance compared to other variants while maintaining structural integrity, as evidenced by nearly identical pre- and post-catalytic morphology and crystallographic features (Supplementary Figs. 47 and 48), which proved structural stability under operating conditions, confirming the robustness of the catalyst design. Examination of Cu and Sn K-edge in CuSn/CS−1 verified that Cu and Sn atoms preserved their atom-disperse configuration, with no notable alterations in the valence configurations of the metal atoms after electrolysis (Supplementary Fig. 49). The oxidation states of Ce and Ov concentrations in CeOx remain nearly unchanged before and after the reaction, as confirmed by XPS and EPR analyses (Supplementary Figs. 50a, b). To further probe structural integrity under operating conditions, the experiment is conducted in situ Raman spectroscopy at reductive potentials. The characteristic Raman bands at 465 cm−1 (F2g vibrational mode of CeO2) and 600 cm−1 (Ov-related mode) exhibit negligible shifts in position or intensity across applied potentials (Supplementary Fig. 50c). No new Raman bands associated with metallic species emerge during electrolysis, confirming the absence of structural reconstruction or derivative species formation. This stability strongly supports that the metal sites are intrinsic to the original catalyst rather than dynamically generated intermediates. Retention of electronic and lattice properties in CeOx throughout the reaction underscores its role as a robust support for stabilizing atomically dispersed Cu/Sn sites. To demonstrate reproducibility over extended periods, the experiment repeated the 20 h electrolysis test twice using the same electrode. The long-term stability data reveal that the catalyst retains its activity throughout uninterrupted electrolysis, maintaining a urea yield rate of 56.40 mmol g−1cat. h−1 during the initial 20 h continuous operation (Supplementary Fig. 51). After electrolyte replacement, the rate slightly decreased to 52.03 mmol g−1cat. h−1, likely due to partial catalyst detachment or environmental factors. Notably, FEurea remained above 70% even after two consecutive extended-duration tests. While the duration of continuous electrolysis tests remains relatively limited, the results collectively reflect the robustness of the electrocatalyst under prolonged operation.

The C−N coupling process faces inherent challenges because of competing electrochemical pathways, particularly HER, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathrm{NO}}}_{3}^{-}$$\end{document}NO3− reduction reaction (NRR), and CRR, which often result in low FEurea for most catalysts14,55. The C−N coupling mechanism of CuSn/CS−1 was investigated by DFT calculations to gain further detailed insights into the favorable urea synthesis performance of CuSn/CS−1. Theoretical models were screened by calculating the formation energies of atomically loaded CeOx, SiO2, and CS. The formation energies of the overall dispersions of Cu, Sn, and CuSn on CS are − 0.894 eV, − 0.759 eV, and − 1.865 eV, respectively, which are consistently lower than only dispersion on SiO2 and CeOx (Supplementary Fig. 61), suggesting a potentially more favorable thermodynamic stability on the CS support within the context of the theoretical model (Supplementary Data 1). Differential charge density plots reveal that the loading of Cu and Sn atoms on the CS support significantly enhances electron transfer, which is quantified by Bader charge analysis and shows charge transfer values of ∆QCS = 0.512 e, ∆QSn/CS = 0.597 e, ∆QCu/CS = 0.691 e, and ∆QCuSn/CS−1 = 0.871 e, respectively (Fig. 6a). Partial density of states (PDOS) demonstrate enhanced orbital hybridization and narrower band gaps for successively introduced Cu and Sn metal atoms, which might indicate that interatomic electron transfer could effectively tune electronic structure (Supplementary Fig. 62)26. The d-band center of CuSn/CS−1 is closest to the Fermi energy level, implying anti-bonding states of more adsorbing intermediates are drawn up to the Fermi energy level, potentially leading to a stronger affinity for the intermediates (Fig. 6b)62,63. This suggests that Sn/CS is more likely to produce HCOO− and CO as products during CRR. In the CO2 → COOH step, the energy barriers for Sn/CS and Cu/CS are approximately 2.55 and 2.23 times higher than those for CuSn/CS−1, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 64b). The reduction channel of *CO2 → *CO is broadened by Cu sites in CuSn/CS−1, and the binding energy barrier of the carbon coordination decreased from − 0.50 eV to − 0.72 eV. The nitrogen-containing intermediates involved include *NH2, *NH, *NO2, *NO, and NHO, and their coupling with *CO is an exothermic process in each case (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 75). Although *CO + *NH has the lowest free energy (0.41 eV), the formation pathway of *NH involves two highly endothermic steps: *NO2H → *N(OH)2 (0.82 eV) and *NO → *NOH (1.80 eV), which energetically disfavor the formation of *NH. Consequently, the thermodynamically favorable choice of coupling *NH2 with *CO emerges as the optimal approach. In addition, *CO on CuSn/CS−1 is less prone to further protonation and more conducive to direct coupling with *NH2 (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 76). CuSn/CS−1 exhibits a low coupling energy barrier in urea synthesis, while the RDS (*NH2 → *CONH2) for Cu/CS is 0.90 eV (Fig. 6g), consistent with the earlier infrared results. Sn/CS encounters a high energy barrier (1.10 eV) at both *NO2 → *NO2H and *NH2 → *CONH2 (Supplementary Fig. 77 and Supplementary Table 7). Observing the barriers for the twice C−N couplings, CuSn/CS−1 is significantly lower than both Sn/CS and Cu/CS (Supplementary Fig. 78). The energy barrier of coupling reaction between *CO adsorbed on Cu and *NH2 released from Sn is 0.51 eV, while the coupling of *NH2 adsorbed on Sn with *CO released from Cu requires 0.53 eV as shown in Fig. 6e, g, which further elucidates the detailed C−N coupling step: adsorbed *CO at the Cu site binds to form *CONH2 by receiving NH2 released from the Sn site, and *CONH2 is released after second coupling at the Cu site to form NH2CONH2. Differential charge density analysis reveals that the adsorption of *CO on Cu and *NH2 on Sn could result in more pronounced charge transfer compared to Cu/CS (Supplementary Figs. 79 and 80). During the coupling process, electron exchange occurs between the intermediates to facilitate C−N bond formation.

In summary, this work innovatively leverages the dispersive and domain-limiting effects of SiO2 materials and CeOx clusters to successfully develop a CuSn dual-site catalyst for urea synthesis from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathrm{NO}}}_{3}^{-}$$\end{document}NO3− and CO2 via electrocatalysis. The CuSn/CS−1 achieves a maximum average urea yield rate of 154.50 mmol g−1cat. h−1 in the GDE system, which is 2.77-fold higher than that in the conventional H-cell. Furthermore, under doubled catalyst loading (0.2 mg cm−2), 38.5 mg of high-purity urea was successfully extracted after 10 h of continuous electrolysis, demonstrating favorable long-term stability and scalability. The charge compensation process inherent in the CeOx−CuSn module stabilizes the binding of Cu sites with CO2·−, forming a carbon-coordinated configuration that plays a pivotal role in generating abundant *CO intermediates. Concurrently, the Sn site focuses on maintaining an appropriate level of NRR, effectively suppressing the formation of byproducts. By effectively compressing the CRR and NRR pathways, the possibilities for C−N coupling are expanded. DFT reveals that the energy barrier between *CO and *NH2 as the key coupling step on CuSn/CS−1 is only 0.53 eV, which is substantially lower than that of the single-metal catalysts at 0.90 eV (Cu/CS) and 1.10 eV (Sn/CS). This research confirms the essentiality of precise modulation by carefully designed dual active sites of target intermediates from diverse substrates and lays the groundwork for the fabrication of electrocatalysts for future reduction systems involving a wider range of substrates.

Methods

Chemicals

All chemicals and gases were purchased commercially unless otherwise stated. Fumed SiO2 (300 m2 g−1), SnCl4·5H2O (99%), Ce(NO3)3·6H2O (99.95%), CuCl2·2H2O (99.99%), NH3·H2O (25 ~ 28%), Na2CO3 (99.99%), phosphoric acid (H3PO4, > 85% in H2O), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 95%–98%), FeCl3 (≥ 99.9%), diacetylmonoxime (> 98%), thiosemicarbazide (99%), KH2PO4 (99%), EDTA−Na2 (99%), urease (~ 1 µ/mg), monopotassium monosodium tartrate tetrahydrate (KNaC4H12O10, 99%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 96%), sodium hypochloritee (NaClO, active chlorine > 10%), salicylic acid (C7H6O3, 99.5%), sodium nitroferricyanide dihydrate (C5FeN6Na2O·2H2O, 99%), K15NO3 (98 atom%), Urea−15N (99 atom%), NH4Cl (> 99.5%), Urea (99%), KNO3 (99%), KNO2 (97%), KHCO3 (99.5%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, ≥ 99.9%), formylic acid (99%), carboxamide (99%), NH2OH (10% solution in H2O), tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), high−pure CO2 (> 99.999%), CO (> 99.999%) and Ar (> 99.999%). All aqueous solutions were prepared using deionized water with a resistivity of 18.25 MΩ‧cm−1.

Preparation of CeO2 nanoparticles and SiO2 nanospheres

1.736 g of Ce(NO3)3·6H2O was dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water, and slowly poured into 70 mL 6 M NaOH solution while stirring at 600 rpm, and reacted for 0.5 h, and then the reaction solution was placed in a muffle furnace at 100 °C for 24 h. After the reaction, it was washed with deionized water and anhydrous ethanol three times, respectively, and then dried in an oven at 60 °C. The dried solid sample was calcined at 500 °C for 4 h to obtain CeO2 powder. 9.15 mL TEOS was added dropwise to a mixed solution of 50 mL ethanol, 30 mL deionized water, and 10 mL NH3·H2O under continuous stirring. The reaction proceeded at room temperature for 12 h. The resulting solid was washed three times with ethanol and dried in an oven at 60 °C to obtain spherical SiO264.

Preparation of CS, core-shell SC, and CeO2/SiO2

0.72 g of fumed SiO2/SiO2 nanospheres was sonicated in 200 mL deionized water for 0.5 h, and 0.347 g Ce(NO3)3 ∙ 6H2O was added with stirring. After complete dissolution, 1.6 mL NH3 ∙ H2O (2 M) was introduced quickly and was continued for 3 min. The produced substance was separated by filtering and rinsed with deionized water until neutral. Acquired solid was dried naturally under ambient conditions, and then calcined at 500 °C (12 h) to acquire CS/core-shell SC. 0.7 g of Ce(NO3)3·6H2O was dissolved in 4 mL of deionized water. Under continuous stirring, 2 g of SiO2 was added and thoroughly mixed. The mixture was dried naturally and then placed in a 60 °C oven for 12 h, resulting powder that was calcined in a 600 °C muffle furnace to form CeO2/SiO2.

Preparation of Sn/CS or Cu/CS

0.129 g CS and 10 mL deionized water were sonicated for 0.5 h, and 1 mL Sn or 2 mL Cu precursor solution (5 mg mL−1 SnCl4 or 5 mg mL−1 CuCl2) was added dropwise at 600 rpm with stirring for a while. Then a suitable amount of 50 mg mL−1 Na2CO3 was added and reacted (5 h). Subsequently, the produced substance was separated by filtering and rinsed in deionized water until neutral. The acquired solid was dried naturally under ambient conditions. The solid sample was calcined at 600 °C (12 h) and then activated at 300 °C with hydrogen (1 h).

Preparation of CuSn/CS−X (X = 1−6)

CuSn/CS−1 was synthesized by simultaneous addition of 2 mL Cu and 1 mL Sn precursor solution, reacting under the synthesis conditions of Sn/CS or Cu/CS. The synthesis pathways for catalysts with other ratios are obtained by varying the injection amounts of the Cu and Sn precursor solutions.

Preparation of CuSn/CeOx, CuSn/SiO2, and core-shell CuSn/SC

The loading process of Cu and Sn in CuSn/CeOx, CuSn/SiO2, and core-shell CuSn/SC follows the same preparation procedure as CuSn/CS−1, with CS being replaced by CeO2, SiO2, and core-shell SC, respectively, and the calcination step at 600 °C was omitted.

Electrochemical measurements

Electrochemical tests were conducted in an H-cell equipped with a three-electrode system. Platinum foil (1 cm × 1 cm) and Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) were used as the counter electrode and reference electrode, respectively. The working electrode was prepared by sonically mixing the sample (2 mg) with 315 μL deionized water, 635 μL isopropanol, and 50 μL Nafion solution (5 wt%) for 40 min. Subsequently, 50 μL of the homogeneous ink was drop-casted onto a 1.0 cm × 1.0 cm carbon paper (HESEN, HCP020N) and naturally dried (catalyst loading: 0.1 mg cm−2). The cathode chamber and anode chamber were separated by a Nafion 117 membrane (DuPont, thickness: 183 µm, radius: 1.5 cm). The Nafion membrane was first heated in a 5% H2O2 solution at 80 °C for 1 h before use, then heated in water at 80 °C for another h, and finally treated with 0.05 M H2SO4 for 1 h before being soaked in water for 3 h. Isotope−labeling experiment in 1H NMR and HRMS measurement

An electrolyte solution of CO2-saturated K15NO3 (99%) was used for isotope labeling experiments, and an electrocatalytic reaction was carried out at − 0.7 V optimum potential. 5 mL of the reacted electrolyte was taken and rotary evaporated at 60 °C to remove water. The solid was sonicated and dispersed in 700 µL of d6-DMSO for 1H NMR measurements on a 500 MHz instrument. HRMS measurements were taken directly from 1 mL of the electrolyte and filtered through a 0.45 µ aqueous filter membrane into a sample vial with an injection volume of 10 µL and detected on an HRMS (LTQ Orbitrap XL).

In situ Raman measurements

To ensure consistency and controllability of the reaction environment, the electrolyte was purged with CO2 gas before initiating electrolysis. A higher-sensitivity immersive probe was employed to capture surface intermediates, which improves signal-to-noise ratio and enables recognition of subtle spectral features. Spectral data were accumulated through four independent measurements, each lasting 30 s.

In situ ATR-SEIRAS measurements

Spectroelectrochemical investigations were performed in a two-compartment cell designed for ATR-SEIRAS. Working electrode, comprising the Au film with the sample layer, was positioned in a direct interface with reaction solution. Using a three-electrode system and electrolyte as in electrochemical measurements. The equipment was equipped with a high-performance Fourier Transform Infrared spectrophotometer (Bruker INVENIO S). Online DEMS measurements

The online DEMS experiments were conducted in an electrochemical cell equipped with a standard three-electrode system, utilizing a QAS 100 device (Shanghai Linglu Instruments Co., Ltd.). Ionization of all species was performed with electron energies ranging from 4 to 150 eV. The hydrophobic PTFE membrane was used (pore porosity: ≥ 50%, pore size: ≤ 20 nm), and the carrier flow rate was 2 mL min−1. DEMS measurements by LSV were performed from − 0.5 V to − 1.4 V (scan rate: 5 mV s−1) after achieving baseline stability in CO2-saturated 0.1 M KNO3, and signals were recorded throughout the LSV process. Upon completion of the electrochemical test, the signals were allowed to return to baseline, and the experiment was terminated after five consecutive test cycles under identical conditions.

Computational details

All the DFT have employed the first−principles65,66 to perform calculations within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) using the Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof (PBE)67 formulation. Ionic cores have chosen the projected augmented wave (PAW) potentials68,69 to describe and take valence electrons into account using a plane wave basis set with a kinetic energy cutoff of 500 eV. Partial occupancies of the Kohn-Sham orbitals were allowed using the Gaussian smearing method and a width of 0.05 eV. The electronic energy was considered self−consistent when the energy change was smaller than 10−5 eV. A geometry optimization was considered convergent when the energy change was smaller than 0.05 eV Å−1. The Brillouin zone integration is performed using 2 × 2 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack k-point sampling for a structure. The equilibrium lattice constants of SiO2 and CeO2 were optimized, and then the SiO2 surface model was constructed. During structural optimizations, a 2 × 2 × 1 k-point grid in the Brillouin zone was used for k-point sampling for CS, Sn/CS, Cu/CS, and CuSn/CS−1 structures. Finally, the adsorption energies (Eads) were calculated as Eads = Ead/sub−Ead−Esub, where Ead/sub, Ead, and Esub are the total energies of the optimized adsorbate/substrate system, the adsorbate in the structure, and the clean substrate, respectively. The free energy was calculated using the equation: G = Eads + ZPE−TS, where G, Eads, ZPE, and TS are the free energy, total energy from DFT calculations, zero point energy, and entropic contributions, respectively, where T is set to 300 K. The optimized structure and charge density difference were visualized using VESTA70.

XAFS analysis

The acquired EXAFS data were processed according to standard procedures using the ATHENA module in the IFFFFIT software package. Post-edge background subtraction and normalization with respect to the edge-jump step were performed. The Sn and Cu K−edge k3−weighted χ(k) data in k−space were Fourier-transformed to R−space using Hanning windows in the k−space range 3−12 Å−1. Quantitative curve fitting of the Fourier-transformed k3χ(k) in R−space was conducted using the ARTEMIS module of IFFFFIT to determine the structural parameters of Sn and Cu atoms in the samples. The effective backscattering amplitudes F(k) and phase shifts Φ(k) for all fitting paths were calculated using the ab initio code FEFF 8.0.

Materials characterization

The crystal information was collected by XRD on a SmartLab 9 kw X−ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (PANalytical X’Pert Pro MPD, λ = 1.5406 Å). X−ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed on a Nexsa spectrometer, and the data were analyzed by the XPSPEAK software. Raman spectra were recorded under an excitation of 532 nm laser light. HRTEM images were acquired on a Japanese JEM−2100 F with an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. HAADF−STEM images were captured on a JEMARM200F instrument at 200 kV with a spherical aberration corrector. Cu K−edge and Sn K−edge XAFS spectra were measured in transmission mode at the Beijing Synchrotron Radiation Facility (BSRF) 1W1B beamline. EPR spectra were collected by Bruker A300. ICP−MS was performed by dissolving the samples with a mixture of hydrochloric acid and nitric acid (3:1, V/V) and performed in a PerkinElmer NexION 2000 ICP−MS. TPD was conducted on the Micromeritics AutoChem 2950 HP chemisorption analyzer.
