CSDE1 Drives Glycolysis and the Progression of Prostate Cancer Through RAC1‐Dependent RAS/MAPK Activation
Authors: Zhen Yin, Zefeng Wang, Wei Gong, Zitao Wang, Zongze Li, Lizhe Xu, Jinzhuo Ning, Lei Gao, Tiejun Pan, Weimin Yu, Yu Zhou, Jinbo Sun, Fan Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTProstate cancer (PCa) remains a leading cause of cancer‐related morbidity and mortality among men, necessitating the elucidation of molecular drivers behind its progression. In this study, we demonstrated that CSDE1 was upregulated in PCa tissues, and its expression was associated with poorer progression‐free survival in patients with high Gleason scores. Functional analyses revealed that CSDE1 knockdown reduced PCa cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while suppressing metabolic activity, as evidenced by reduced extracellular acidification rates (ECAR), oxygen consumption rates (OCR), and lactate production. At the mechanistic level, CSDE1 was found to be associated with RAC1 expression and RAC1‐related signaling markers, accompanied by changes in MAPK pathway phosphorylation. Genetic modulation of RAC1 partially reversed the cellular and metabolic effects associated with altered CSDE1 expression, supporting a functional role for RAC1 in CSDE1‐associated phenotypes. In addition, CSDE1 expression was accompanied by increased levels of the glycolytic regulators GLUT1 and LDHA, and pharmacological inhibition of MAPK signaling partially attenuated CSDE1‐associated glycolytic changes. In vivo models validated the oncogenic role of the CSDE1‐RAC1 axis in tumor growth. Together, these findings support CSDE1 as a key driver that enhances RAC1‐mediated MAPK signaling and metabolic profiles in PCa, suggesting that CSDE1 may serve as a promising prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target.Keywords: biomarker, CSDE1, glycolysis, prostate cancer, RAC1, RAS/MAPK

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the leading causes of cancer‐related morbidity and mortality among men worldwide, and current treatment options remain limited [1, 2, 3]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular pathogenesis of PCa is crucial for guiding the development of novel treatment strategies. Notably, the mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling cascade—a key pathway regulating cell proliferation, migration, and survival—is frequently dysregulated and hyperactivated in a variety of human cancers [4, 5, 6].

CSDE1 is an RNA‐binding protein that is involved in several cellular functions, such as transcriptional regulation, RNA stabilization, and translational regulation [7]. Emerging evidence indicates that it has oncogenic functions in various malignancies, including melanoma [8, 9] and breast [10], lung [11], pancreatic [12], and colorectal tumors [13]. However, the expression pattern and functional role of CSDE1 in prostate cancer remain incompletely characterized.

RAC1, a small GTPase [14], regulates cytoskeletal changes, cell migration, and metabolic processes and has been implicated in MAPK activation [15]. Notably, recent studies have revealed an interaction between CSDE1 and RAC1 [10, 16]; however, their potential association has not been systematically examined in prostate cancer.

Aerobic glycolysis, also known as the Warburg effect, is a hallmark of cancer metabolism [17]. This phenomenon reflects the preference of tumor cells for glycolysis over oxidative phosphorylation, even in the presence of oxygen, thus bypassing the more efficient ATP production pathway. This metabolic shift enables rapid ATP generation while providing essential intermediates for nucleotide, amino acid, and lipid synthesis required for accelerated cellular proliferation and growth. In hypoxic TME regions, glycolysis is particularly important for the growth and propagation of tumors [18].

Here, we investigated the expression and functional relevance of CSDE1 in PCa and examined its associations with RAC1‐related signaling, MAPK pathway activity, and glycolysis‐related phenotypes. These findings suggest that CSDE1 may represent a candidate biomarker and potential therapeutic target in PCa.

Materials and Methods

Human Tissue Specimens

The current analysis utilized a collection of 12 paired PCa and normal tissue samples acquired from radical prostatectomy procedures at Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University. Histopathological evaluation of all the samples was performed independently by two qualified pathologists. The experimental design received ethical clearance from the institutional committee (WDRM2021KS010) and was executed in full compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Dataset and Bioinformatics Analysis

RNA sequencing data together with matched clinical annotations for prostate carcinoma and adjacent nonmalignant tissues were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Genotype‐Tissue Expression (GTEx) project (accessed June 1, 2024). All expression profiles were processed via the UCSC Xena Toil recompute pipeline and transformed to log2(TPM + 1) values.

Differential expression analysis between prostate tumor tissues and normal tissues was performed via the GEPIA2 and Xiantao Academic online analysis platforms on the basis of the TCGA and GTEx datasets. Genes with a |log2‐fold change| > 1 and adjusted p value < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Protein–protein interaction networks, gene correlation analyses, and immune‐related associations were analyzed via STRING, TIMER 2.0, and GEPIA2. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted to explore the biological functions of genes coexpressed with CSDE1.

For survival analysis, the TCGA cohort was used. CSDE1 expression was modeled as a continuous variable in a Cox proportional hazards regression model to estimate the hazard ratio (HR) with a 95% confidence interval (95% CI) for progression‐free survival (PFS). Kaplan–Meier curves were used for visualization of survival differences.

Cell Culture, Pharmacological Treatments, and Quality Control

Human prostate cancer cell lines were cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

For pharmacological experiments, cycloheximide (CHX), actinomycin D (ActD), and U0126 were applied as indicated. For protein stability analysis, cells were treated with CHX (50 μg/mL) to inhibit de novo protein synthesis and harvested at 0, 2, 4, and 6 h for immunoblot analysis. For the mRNA stability assays, transcription was blocked with ActD (5 μg/mL), and total RNA was collected at 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h for quantitative real‐time PCR analysis. To inhibit the MEK/ERK signaling pathway, cells were pretreated with U0126 (10 μM) for 1 h before subsequent experiments, and pathway inhibition was verified by decreased ERK1/2 phosphorylation. Unless otherwise specified, all the inhibitors were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and the cells treated with an equivalent volume of DMSO served as vehicle controls.

All prostate cancer cell lines used in this study were obtained from authenticated commercial sources. The identity of the cell lines was confirmed via short tandem repeat (STR) profiling, with DU145 cells authenticated on December 30, 2024, and PC3 cells authenticated on November 29, 2024. The cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination via PCR‐based assays, and no mycoplasma contamination was detected during the study.

Genetic Manipulation by Lentiviral Transduction and Transient Transfection

Lentiviral vectors expressing CSDE1 short hairpin RNA (sh‐CSDE1), a corresponding negative control (sh‐NC), or full‐length CSDE1 for overexpression (CSDE1‐OE) were used in this study. Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK‐293T cells via cotransfection with psPAX2 and pMD2.G packaging plasmids via Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer's instructions. Prostate cancer cells were infected with lentiviral supernatants and selected with puromycin (5 μg/mL) to establish stable cell lines. The knockdown or overexpression efficiency of CSDE1 was confirmed prior to subsequent experiments.

For transient regulation of RAC1 expression, cells were transfected with small interfering RNA targeting RAC1 (si‐RAC1) or a corresponding negative control siRNA via Lipofectamine 3000. RAC1 overexpression was achieved by transfection with an RAC1 overexpression plasmid (OE‐RAC1). Where indicated, RAC1 modulation was performed alone or in combination with CSDE1 knockdown or overexpression.

Western Blotting and Coimmunoprecipitation Analysis

Total cellular proteins were extracted via RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined via a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS–PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4°C with the indicated primary antibodies, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein signals were visualized via enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents.

For coimmunoprecipitation assays, cells were lysed under nondenaturing conditions, and equal amounts of protein lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti‐CSDE1 antibody or control IgG. Immune complexes were captured via protein A/G agarose beads, washed extensively, and eluted by boiling in SDS loading buffer. The precipitated proteins were analyzed by Western blotting to assess protein–protein interactions.

RNA Extraction and qRT‐PCR

Total RNA was extracted from prostate cancer cells via TRIzol reagent (CW0580S, CWBIO) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The RNA concentration and purity were assessed via a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. One microgram of total RNA was reverse‐transcribed into complementary DNA. Quantitative real‐time PCR was performed via SYBR Green Master Mix (RK21203, ABclonal) on a Roche LightCycler 480 system. Relative gene expression levels were calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method, with ACTB used as the internal control.

The sequences of primers used were as follows:

CSDE1 forward, 5′‐CATGAAGGAGGCATTTGGCTT‐3′;

CSDE1 reverse, 5′‐TGCAACTTCTTTACCATTTCTGTCC‐3′;

RAC1 forward, 5′‐CGGTGAATCTGGGCTTATGGGA‐3′;

RAC1 reverse, 5′‐GGAGGTTATATCCTTACCGTACG‐3′;

SLC2A1 (GLUT1) forward, 5′‐TTGCAGGCTTCTCCAACTGGAC‐3′;

SLC2A1 (GLUT1) reverse, 5′‐CAGAACCAGGAGCACAGTGAAG‐3′;

LDHA forward, 5′‐GGATCTCCAACATGGCAGCCTT‐3′;

LDHA reverse, 5′‐AGACGGCTTTCTCCCTCTTGCT‐3′;

ACTB forward, 5′‐CATGTACGTTGCTATCCAGGC‐3′;

ACTB reverse, 5′‐CTCCTTAATGTCACGCACGAT‐3′.

Immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence for Protein Localization

Paraffin‐embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) via heat‐induced epitope retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide. The sections were then blocked and incubated overnight at 4°C with the indicated primary antibodies.

For immunohistochemistry (IHC), the sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies and visualized via diaminobenzidine (DAB). Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Images were captured via a light microscope, and the staining intensity was quantified via ImageJ software.

For IF, the sections were incubated with fluorophore‐conjugated secondary antibodies in the dark, followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired via an inverted fluorescence microscope. Protein localization and fluorescence intensity were analyzed via ImageJ software. Detailed information on the primary antibodies is provided in Table S1.

Assays for Cell Proliferation

Cell proliferation was evaluated via Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) and 5‐ethynyl‐2′‐deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assays. For the CCK‐8 assay, prostate cancer cells were seeded into 96‐well plates at a density of 2000 cells per well. Cell viability was assessed daily for three consecutive days by the addition of CCK‐8 reagent, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm via a microplate reader. Wells containing medium alone served as blanks.

For the EdU incorporation assay, the cells were incubated with EdU and processed via a Click‐iT EdU detection kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. EdU‐positive cells were visualized under a fluorescence microscope, and the proportion of EdU‐positive cells was quantified to assess cell proliferation.

Assays for Cell Migration and Invasion

Cell migration and invasion assays were performed using Transwell chambers with 8‐μm pore membranes. For the migration assays, prostate cancer cells (2 × 104 cells per well) were seeded into the upper chambers in serum‐free medium. For invasion assays, the inserts were precoated with Matrigel, and cells (2 × 105 cells per well) were seeded under the same conditions. The lower chambers were filled with complete medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum as a chemoattractant. After incubation for 24 h (migration) or 48 h (invasion), non‐migrated or noninvaded cells on the upper surface of the membrane were removed. The cells that had migrated or invaded the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and counted under a light microscope. The cells were quantified from at least five randomly selected fields per membrane.

Wound Healing and Colony Formation Assays

Cell migration was further evaluated via wound‐healing assays. PC3 and DU145 cells were grown to confluence in six‐well plates, and uniform wounds were created via sterile pipette tips. The cells were then cultured in serum‐free medium to minimize the effect on cell proliferation. Images of the same wound areas were captured at 0 and 48 h via an inverted microscope, and wound closure was assessed to evaluate migratory ability.

For the colony formation assays, prostate cancer cells were seeded into six‐well plates at a density of 1 × 103 cells per well and cultured for 14 days. Colonies were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. Colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted to assess clonogenic capacity.

Measurement of Lactate Production

Glycolytic activity was assessed by measuring lactate levels in PC3 and DU145 cells via a commercial kit (Beyotime, S0208S). After treatment, the culture supernatants were collected following the protocol and subjected to analysis. The lactate concentration was determined by measuring the optical density at 450 nm. To account for differences in cell density, lactate concentrations were normalized to the corresponding cell numbers and expressed as relative lactate production.

Measurement of the Extracellular Acidification Rate (ECAR) and Oxygen Consumption Rate (OCR)

The extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) was measured via an Agilent Seahorse XF extracellular flux analyzer with the Seahorse Glycolysis Stress Test Kit. PC3 and DU145 cells were seeded into Seahorse XF cell culture microplates at an optimized density and allowed to adhere overnight. On the day of the assay, the culture medium was replaced with Seahorse XF Base Medium (pH 7.4) supplemented with 2 mM glutamine, and the cells were equilibrated in a 37°C incubator without CO2 for 45–60 min.

ECAR measurements were recorded at baseline and after sequential injections as follows: glucose (final concentration of 10 mM) to measure basal glycolysis, oligomycin (final concentration of 1 μM) to assess maximal glycolytic capacity, and 2‐deoxy‐D‐glucose (2‐DG; final concentration of 50 mM) to confirm the inhibition of glycolysis. The ECAR values were normalized to the number of cells and analyzed via Wave software. Each condition was run with technical replicates and repeated in at least three independent experiments.

The oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was measured on the same Seahorse XF analyzer using the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test. OCR was recorded at baseline and after sequential injections of oligomycin (final 1.5 μM), FCCP (final 0.5 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (final 0.5 μM). OCR values were normalized to cell number and analyzed using Wave software. Each condition included technical replicates and was repeated in at least three independent experiments.

Tumor Formation Assay

Male BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old) were used for the subcutaneous tumor formation assay. PC3 cells were suspended in PBS, and 5 × 106 cells in a total volume of 100 μL were injected subcutaneously into the inguinal region of each mouse. The mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups (n = 5 per group) and housed under specific pathogen‐free conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle. Tumor size was measured every 5 days via a digital caliper, and tumor volume was calculated via the following formula: volume = (length × width2)/2. At the experimental endpoint, the mice were euthanized, and the tumor tissues were excised for further analysis. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Review Committee of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University (WDRM20230505A) and were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. The mice were euthanized by gradual CO2 inhalation at a displacement rate of 20%–30% of the chamber volume per minute, followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death, in compliance with the recommendations of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020).

RNA Immunoprecipitation (RIP)

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays were performed to examine the interaction between CSDE1 and RAC1 mRNA. Prostate cancer cells were harvested and lysed in RIP lysis buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail and an RNase inhibitor. Equal amounts of cell lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with rotation with magnetic beads conjugated to an anti‐CSDE1 antibody or normal rabbit IgG as a negative control.

After extensive washing, the immunoprecipitated RNA was purified via the RNeasy Animal RNA Isolation Kit with a Spin Column (Beyotime, R0024) according to the manufacturer's instructions. A portion of the lysate was reserved as input RNA. Purified RNA was reverse‐transcribed into cDNA, and RAC1 mRNA enrichment in CSDE1 immunoprecipitates was analyzed via quantitative real‐time PCR. The RIP‐qPCR results were normalized to those of the IgG controls or the input RNA, as specified. All the RIP experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism 10. The data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent biological experiments, unless otherwise indicated. Data distribution was assessed for normality prior to statistical testing. Comparisons between two groups were performed via two‐tailed Student's t‐tests, with paired t‐tests applied for matched tumor and adjacent normal tissue samples. Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted via one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's post hoc test when appropriate. Tumor growth curves were analyzed via two‐way ANOVA.

Kaplan–Meier survival curves were compared via Cox proportional hazards model. Correlation analyses were performed via Pearson's correlation for normally distributed data or Spearman's rank correlation for nonnormally distributed data. For in vivo experiments, each mouse was considered an independent experimental unit. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

High Expression of CSDE1 in Prostate Cancer Tissues

Bioinformatics analysis of the TCGA and GTEx datasets revealed broadly increased CSDE1 mRNA across cancers, with a particularly marked elevation in PCa (Figure 1A). CSDE1 expression was significantly higher in PCa tissues than in normal prostate tissues (Figure 1B). Consistently, Western blotting of 12 paired clinical samples (n = 12) revealed higher CSDE1 protein levels in tumors than in matched nontumor tissues (Figure 1C,D). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) further confirmed higher expression of CSDE1 in PCa tissues than in adjacent benign tissues (Figure 1E). In the subgroup of patients with PCa (Gleason score 9–10), Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed a correlation between high expression of CSDE1 and significantly shortened progression‐free survival (PFS) (Figure 1F) (HR = 2.10, 95% CI 1.12–3.96; log‐rank p = 0.021) (Figure 1F). In the TCGA‐PRAD cohort, CSDE1 expression was positively correlated with estimated tumor infiltration by activated aDCs, T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, eosinophils, and B cells into the tumors (all p < 0.05; Figure S1A–F).

Taken together, these data show that CSDE1 is upregulated in PCa tissues and is associated with shorter progression‐free survival in the Gleason score 9–10 subgroup.

The efficiency of CSDE1 knockdown was confirmed by quantitative real‐time PCR and Western blot analysis in PC3 and DU145 cells (Figure 2A–C). CSDE1 knockdown significantly reduced cell proliferation, as demonstrated by CCK‐8 growth curves, decreased EdU incorporation (Figure 2D,E,K,L), and markedly impaired clonogenic capacity in colony formation assays (Figure 2F). In addition, CSDE1 knockdown suppressed cell migration and invasion, as evidenced by reduced numbers of migrating and invading cells in Transwell assays and delayed wound closure in wound healing assays (Figure 2G–J).

CSDE1 Expression Is Associated With RAC1‐Related Signaling in Prostate Cancer Cells

To explore signaling pathways associated with CSDE1, protein–protein interaction analysis via STRING and KEGG pathway enrichment identified multiple CSDE1‐related pathways, including the MAPK signaling pathway (Figure 4A,B). In the TCGA prostate cancer dataset, CSDE1 mRNA expression was positively correlated with RAC1 mRNA expression (Figure 4C). At the protein level, CSDE1 knockdown in PC3 and DU145 cells was accompanied by reduced RAC1 protein expression, decreased phosphorylation of the RAC1 downstream effector PAK, and reduced phosphorylation of RAF, MEK, and ERK, whereas total PAK, RAF, MEK, and ERK levels remained largely unchanged (Figure 4D–F). Conversely, CSDE1 overexpression was associated with increased RAC1 protein levels and enhanced phosphorylation of PAK, RAF, MEK, and ERK in both cell lines (Figure S2A–C). Coimmunoprecipitation assays revealed an association between CSDE1 and RAC1, and immunofluorescence analysis revealed overlapping cytoplasmic distributions of CSDE1 and RAC1 in PC3 and DU145 cells (Figure 4G,H). To assess whether CSDE1 expression is associated with changes in RAC1 protein turnover, cycloheximide chase assays were performed. In both PC3 and DU145 cells, RAC1 protein levels decreased over time following cycloheximide treatment, with CSDE1 overexpression associated with a decrease in RAC1 protein turnover compared with that in vector controls (Figure 4I,J). At the transcript level, actinomycin D chase assays revealed comparable RAC1 mRNA decay kinetics between vector control and CSDE1‐overexpressing cells, with no significant difference observed in RAC1 mRNA stability (Figure S3A,B). In parallel, RNA immunoprecipitation assays demonstrated significant enrichment of RAC1 mRNA in CSDE1 immunoprecipitates compared with IgG controls in both PC3 and DU145 cells (Figure S3C,D).

Together, these findings support an association between CSDE1 expression and RAC1 protein turnover with corresponding changes in downstream signaling without a significant effect on RAC1 mRNA stability in prostate cancer cells.

CSDE1 Expression Is Associated With Glycolytic Features in Prostate Cancer Cells

To investigate whether CSDE1 affects metabolic reprogramming in PCa, we analyzed data from the TCGA‐PRAD cohort. The analysis revealed that CSDE1 expression levels were positively correlated with overall glycolysis scores and were particularly associated with increased mRNA expression of key glycolytic regulators, LDHA and GLUT1 (Figure 5A–C). CSDE1 knockdown markedly reduced LDHA and GLUT1 expression at both the mRNA (Figure 5D,E) and protein levels (Figure 5K,L). It also led to decreased lactate production (Figure 5F). Conversely, CSDE1 overexpression led to elevated expression of both LDHA and GLUT1 (Figure S2D,E,G,H) and increased lactate production (Figure S2F). Consistent with these findings, extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) measurements revealed reduced glycolytic activity in PC3 and DU145 cells following CSDE1 knockdown compared to control cells (Figure 5G,H). It also altered OCR profiles, indicating corresponding changes in cellular bioenergetic status (Figure 5I,J). To examine the contribution of MAPK signaling to this metabolic phenotype, we treated CSDE1‐overexpressing cells with the MEK inhibitor U0126. U0126 effectively suppressed ERK1/2 phosphorylation and partially attenuated CSDE1 overexpression–associated increases in GLUT1/LDHA expression and lactate production (Figure S4A–G). U0126 also partially reduced CSDE1‐associated ECAR elevation and produced corresponding effects on OCR phenotypes (Figure S3H–K).

Collectively, these results support that CSDE1 enhances glycolysis‐related activity in prostate cancer cells, and that this phenotype is at least partly dependent on MAPK pathway activity.

RAC1 Is Required for CSDE1‐Associated Cellular and Metabolic Phenotypes in Prostate Cancer Cells

To further examine the involvement of RAC1 in CSDE1‐associated cellular and metabolic features, rescue experiments were performed in PC3 and DU145 cells. CSDE1 knockdown was associated with reduced RAC1 expression, decreased activation of RAS/MAPK signaling components, and reduced expression of the glycolytic regulators GLUT1 and LDHA; these changes were partially attenuated by RAC1 overexpression (Figure 6A). Densitometric quantification of p‐RAF, p‐MEK, and p‐ERK further supported this trend: RAC1 overexpression partly restored the reduced MAPK phosphorylation caused by CSDE1 knockdown in both PC3 and DU145 cells (Figure 6E,F). Functionally, RAC1 overexpression partially alleviated the reductions in cell proliferation and colony formation observed following CSDE1 knockdown (Figure 6B–D) and partially restored migratory and invasive capacities (Figure 6G,H). In parallel, the decrease in lactate production associated with CSDE1 knockdown was also attenuated by RAC1 overexpression in both cell lines (Figure 6I,J). Conversely, RAC1 silencing in CSDE1‐overexpressing cells attenuated CSDE1‐associated increases in cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and lactate production (Figure S5A–H).

In Vivo Validation of CSDE1–RAC1 Axis in Prostate Tumor Growth

To validate the roles of CSDE1 and RAC1 in tumorigenesis in vivo, a xenograft model was established by subcutaneously injecting PC3 cells into immunodeficient mice. Tumor growth was significantly reduced in CSDE1‐silenced group, as indicated by a decreased tumor volume and reduced tumor weight. Importantly, RAC1 overexpression partially reversed this inhibitory effect in CSDE1‐knockdown cells (Figure 7A–C). Tumor weight is presented as mean ± SD, and individual mouse values are shown (Figure 7B). One‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test showed that tumor weight was lower in the sh‐CSDE1 group than in the sh‐NC group (mean difference 151.0 mg, 95% CI 115.7–186.4; adjusted p < 0.0001). In CSDE1‐knockdown background, RAC1 overexpression increased tumor weight compared with sh‐CSDE1 tumors (mean difference 95.29 mg, 95% CI 59.96–130.6; adjusted p < 0.0001). However, tumor weight remained lower than that of sh‐NC controls (mean difference 55.75 mg, 95% CI 20.41–91.09; adjusted p = 0.0032). These results indicate a partial rescue in vivo (Figure 7B). Immunohistochemistry revealed reduced levels of RAC1, Ki‐67, p‐PAK, p‐MEK, p‐ERK, GLUT1, and LDHA in tumors from the CSDE1‐knockdown group, with these markers partially restored upon RAC1 overexpression (Figure 7D). Western blot analysis of tumor tissue lysates confirmed these observations, further supporting the regulatory interplay between CSDE1 and RAC1 in vivo, which is consistent with the in vitro results (Figure 7E–G).

On the basis of our in vitro and in vivo findings, we propose a model in which CSDE1 may enhance RAC1‐associated MAPK signaling and glycolytic activity, thereby contributing to prostate tumor growth (Figure 7F).

Discussion

Although most prostate cancers have favorable long‐term outcomes, patients with Gleason score 9–10 represent a high‐risk subgroup with markedly poorer clinical outcomes and a greater likelihood of recurrence and metastasis, underscoring the need to better define the molecular features associated with aggressive PCa [19]. Previous studies have demonstrated that CSDE1, an RNA‐binding protein (RBP), exerts significant oncogenic effects on various types of malignancies. For example, in melanoma, CSDE1 promotes tumor cell invasion and metastasis by regulating posttranscriptional gene expression. In pancreatic, colorectal, and breast cancers, CSDE1 has been shown to be closely associated with the maintenance of cancer cell stemness, chemoresistance, and enhanced metastatic potential [7, 8, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. However, the biological role and mechanistic relevance of CSDE1 in prostate cancer have not been systematically characterized.

In this study, we systematically evaluated CSDE1 expression and functional relevance in prostate cancer and examined its relationships with RAC1‐related signaling, MAPK pathway activity, and glycolysis‐related metabolic phenotypes. We found that CSDE1 is upregulated in prostate cancer tissues and is associated with shorter progression‐free survival in the high‐Gleason subgroup. Functionally, altering CSDE1 levels was accompanied by changes in cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, together with shifts in glycolysis‐related readouts (ECAR and lactate production) and accompanying changes in mitochondrial respiration (OCR), consistent with altered GLUT1 and LDHA expression. Collectively, these results extend prior observations to a prostate cancer context and support a link between CSDE1 and tumor growth–related phenotypes and glycolysis‐associated metabolic features, with potential translational relevance in high‐grade disease.

RAC1 plays a central regulatory role in processes such as cytoskeleton remodeling, cell migration, and malignant progression of tumors [25, 26]. Its expression is increased in many cancer types [27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. The RAS/MAPK pathway, as a core oncogenic network, is widely involved in the regulation of the proliferation, survival, and metastasis of various advanced malignant tumors, including PCa [6, 32, 33]. Prior studies in melanoma and other tumors have suggested a link between CSDE1 and RAC1‐related signaling [16]. However, in prostate cancer, systematic evidence is still limited regarding the expression features of CSDE1 and its functional relationship with RAC1/MAPK signaling. It also remains unclear whether these signaling changes are connected to metabolic phenotypes such as enhanced glycolysis.

To address these gaps, in our study, the modulation of CSDE1 expression in PCa cells was accompanied by changes in proliferative and invasive behaviors and altered phosphorylation of MAPK pathway components. Together with RAC1 rescue experiments and the attenuation of related phenotypes by MEK inhibition, these results support a functional role for RAC1 in CSDE1‐associated cellular phenotypes. Collectively, these findings support a functional association between CSDE1 expression, RAC1‐related signaling, and MAPK pathway activity in PCa models.

Tumor cells with high proliferative activity typically undergo metabolic reprogramming to meet increased biosynthetic demands [34, 35]. Early‐stage or well‐differentiated tumors often rely more on oxidative phosphorylation. In contrast, in high‐grade disease, during progression, or under treatment pressure, increased glycolysis and altered lactate metabolism are more often linked to aggressive behavior and poor outcomes [36]. In this context, glucose uptake mediated by GLUT1 (SLC2A1) and lactate production driven by LDHA have been repeatedly reported to correlate with metabolic adaptation and invasive phenotypes in prostate cancer [37, 38, 39], Glycolytic enzymes are regulated by several signaling pathways, including the PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MAPK, and HIF‐1α pathways, which collectively facilitate metabolic adaptation to environmental changes [40, 41, 42, 43]. However, it remains unclear whether RNA‐binding proteins (RBPs), as post‐transcriptional regulators, can serve as a “signal–metabolism” linker in prostate cancer and shape glycolytic phenotypes by influencing the expression of key metabolic regulators such as GLUT1 and LDHA.

In this study, the modulation of CSDE1 expression in PCa cells was associated with changes in glycolysis‐related phenotypes. High CSDE1 expression was associated with increased glycolytic activity, as reflected by elevated ECAR, OCR, and lactate production, together with increased expression of the glycolytic regulators GLUT1 and LDHA. Moreover, MEK inhibition attenuated CSDE1‐associated increases in GLUT1/LDHA and glycolysis‐related readouts. Collectively, these findings suggest that CSDE1 expression is associated with changes in RAC1‐related MAPK signaling and with glycolysis‐related phenotypes in PCa cells.

The TME is a key determinant of cancer progression and therapeutic resistance [44]. In this study, TIMER analysis of the TCGA‐PRAD cohort showed that CSDE1 expression was positively correlated with estimated infiltration scores of multiple immune cell subsets; however, these results are based on computational deconvolution and should be considered exploratory. Future work will validate these associations in independent cohorts and experimental models.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although we observed an association between CSDE1 and RAC1, the precise molecular basis of this association—including whether it involves direct protein–protein interaction or RNA‐mediated mechanisms—remains to be clarified. Second, while our analyses suggested a correlation between CSDE1 expression and immune cell infiltration, we did not perform functional experiments to directly examine the role of CSDE1 in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment. Third, the clinical validation was based on a small paired tissue cohort (n = 12), and we did not perform multivariable survival analyses to assess whether CSDE1 provides independent prognostic information beyond established clinicopathologic factors. In addition, our experimental models primarily reflect hormone‐sensitive PCa cell lines and xenografts; validation in CRPC settings and patient‐derived models will be important in future studies.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that CSDE1 expression is closely associated with aggressive cellular behaviors and glycolysis‐related phenotypes in prostate cancer. Our findings support a functional association between CSDE1 expression, RAC1‐related MAPK signaling activity, and metabolic features, suggesting that CSDE1 may participate in prostate cancer progression through the coordinated regulation of signaling and metabolic pathways. These findings expand the current understanding of the biological role of CSDE1 in prostate cancer and provide a rationale for further investigation of CSDE1 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic intervention target.
