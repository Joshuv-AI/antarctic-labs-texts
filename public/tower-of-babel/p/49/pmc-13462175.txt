Thrombocytosis and the generation of platelet‐derived microparticles in the pathophysiology of sickle cell disease
Authors: Giao N Lê, Anna Dillon, Owen P Smith, Corrina McMahon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySickle cell disease (SCD) is a complex thrombo‐inflammatory disorder in which haemolysis, platelet activation, thrombocytosis, inflammation and endothelial activation operate as an intricate network creating a perpetual cycle of cellular injury and vascular dysfunction that drives vaso‐occlusive crises, tissue ischaemia and progressive end‐organ damage. Thrombocytosis is common in SCD, especially in response to acute chest syndrome, vaso‐occlusive crises and chronic haemolysis. Increased platelet activity contributes to the proinflammatory and prothrombotic environment. Activated platelets release platelet‐derived microparticles (PMPs) containing phospholipids and inflammatory mediators, which enhance thrombin generation, entrap leucocytes and promote cellular adhesions leading to endothelial dysfunction and vaso‐occlusion within the microvasculature. This review provides insights into the mechanisms underlying thrombocytosis and PMP formation in SCD and discusses therapeutic strategies targeting platelet activation to mitigate inflammation and vascular complications.Keywords: platelet microparticles, sickle cell disease, thrombocytosis

INTRODUCTION

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is the most severe inherited red blood cell disorder worldwide. The total prevalence of homozygous SCD is estimated at 112 per 100 000 live births, with Africa exhibiting the highest prevalence of 1125 per 100 000 live births. 1 The Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 reported a 13.7% increase globally, with up to 515 000 neonates born with SCD between 2000 and 2021. An estimated 7.7 million people have SCD, mostly concentrated in sub‐Saharan Africa. 2 The mortality rate is estimated at 7.3 per 100 child‐years, with the highest rates in children under 5 years of age. 1 , 2

The spectrum of SCD encompasses a heterogeneous group of genotypes, including homozygous haemoglobin S (HbSS) and various compound heterozygous states resulting from the co‐inheritance of HbS with other haemoglobin variants or beta‐thalassaemia variants (β+ or β0), with homozygous HbSS and compound heterozygous HbS/β0 being the more severe forms. HbS results from a single nucleotide substitution in the HBB gene (c.20A>T, p.Glu7Val). 3 In the deoxygenated state, HbS molecules form branching polymers that induce the sickling effect, causing decreased deformability and increased rigidity and fragility of the red cells. These conformational changes lead to vaso‐occlusion and intravascular haemolysis, initiating a complex cascade of inflammatory alarm signals that activate immune and endothelial cells, ultimately resulting in endothelial damage and tissue injury. 4

Thrombocytosis is common in SCD and has been observed during the steady state; during vaso‐occlusive crisis; and post‐crisis as a response to chronic inflammation, tissue hypoxia and endothelial injury. Platelets play a significant role in the inflammatory cascade of ischaemia‐reperfusion injury and in the prothrombotic state of SCD. At the site of endothelial damage, platelets are activated via interaction between platelet glycoproteins, endothelial P‐selectin and von Willebrand factor. Circulating activated platelets have been shown to stimulate endothelial activation via direct contact, adhesion molecules and NF‐kB‐dependent signalling. 5 Furthermore, an increased level of endothelial and platelet microparticles (PMPs) is observed in patients with sickle cell crisis. 6 , 7 Platelet‐rich thrombi are found in the pulmonary vasculature of SCD patients with acute chest syndrome. 8 In the inflammatory state, microparticles derived from circulating platelets express activation markers 9 and have upregulated procoagulant properties (phosphatidylserine [PS], platelet glycoproteins CD61, CD62P and Factor X) on their surface membranes. 10

In this review, we analyse the mechanisms of thrombocytosis in SCD and the pathophysiological interplay between thrombocytosis, platelet activation and PMP generation. Finally, we discuss the therapeutic approaches targeting platelets and PMPs to reduce inflammation, thrombotic risk and vaso‐occlusion in SCD.

MECHANISMS UNDERLYING THROMBOCYTOSIS IN SCD

Thrombocytosis, defined as a platelet count above 450 × 109/L, is a well‐documented feature of SCD. Historically, thrombocytosis in SCD was considered an incidental and reactive finding. Platelets are inherently activated in SCD due to exposure to ADP, thrombin and inflammatory mediators released from haemolysed red cells. A higher absolute number of activated platelets in SCD increases the potential for adhesion to the endothelium and leucocytes, contributing to the multicellular aggregates that obstruct blood flow, thereby perpetuating the cycle of tissue injury.

The aetiology of thrombocytosis in SCD is multifactorial, stemming from the loss of splenic function, chronic inflammation, reperfusion injury following ischaemia and compensatory upregulation of the thrombopoietic drive (Figure 1).

Studies examining the association between thrombocytosis and sickle crisis or steady state remain inconsistent. A recent cross‐sectional study of children with sickle cell anaemia in steady state has reported thrombocytosis in approximately 30% of patients. 11 In adults, elevated platelets and megathrombocytes can be observed during the asymptomatic period. 12 Furthermore, some studies have reported an increase in platelet production and aggregation during and after sickle vaso‐occlusive crises. 13 , 14 Similarly, Yee et al. have described a case report of extreme thrombocytosis with acquired von Willebrand disease in a patient with SCD during a vaso‐occlusive pain crisis and priapism. 15

Thrombocytosis in SCD has clinical implications as there is increasing evidence linking it to acute and long‐term morbidity. A cohort study of 432 patients has demonstrated correlations between elevated platelet and white cell counts and low haemoglobin with the increased frequency of emergency room admission. 16 More notably, the authors have reported that each additional 50 × 109/L increase in platelets above baseline was associated with a 22% higher risk of emergency room admission. 16 Platelets are implicated in the pathogenesis of acute chest syndrome through the release of vasoactive and pro‐inflammatory substances that exacerbate lung injury and thrombosis. 8 Activated platelets contribute to a pro‐thrombotic state, increasing the risk of overt and silent cerebral infarcts. 17 Elevated platelet counts were observed in female SCD patients with chronic leg ulcers, 18 a condition associated with vascular dysfunction and thrombosis. 19

Platelet biogenesis has been shown in the lungs, 20 especially during systemic inflammation. Intrapulmonary megakaryocyte thrombopoiesis contributes to platelet production following acute lung injury. 21 It has been shown that cytokines, such as interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β), can influence megakaryocyte maturation and thrombopoiesis via an inflammatory signalling pathway through Toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4) expressed on megakaryocytes. 21 In an autopsy study of SCD cases, disseminated pulmonary platelet microthrombi associated with deposition of endothelial von Willebrand factor (vWF) aggregates and lesions of pulmonary vasculature were found in cases with acute chest syndrome, linking evidence of platelet activation and endothelial dysfunction with clinical correlation. 8

Hyposplenism and autosplenectomy

The spleen plays a significant role in platelet homeostasis and regulation. As a platelet reservoir, the spleen filters aged and abnormal platelets from the circulation. Impairment of splenic function results in the accumulation of circulating platelets. Reactive thrombocytosis is a well‐recognised feature post‐splenectomy due to the loss of regulatory splenic sequestration of platelets, leading to an increase in platelets released into the circulation. 22 The slow flow rate in the splenic microcirculation induces in vivo deoxygenation, causing red cell sickling. Repeated splenic infarctions lead to progressive fibrosis and atrophy (autosplenectomy), usually by the age of 5. 23 Loss of splenic function has been reported in infants with sickle cell anaemia before 12 months of age. 24 Hyposplenism or autosplenectomy in SCD can lead to fluctuating thrombocytosis due to the loss of splenic pooling and platelet filtration.

Increase cytokine drive due to haemolysis and chronic inflammation

Although it is difficult to identify the trigger events that define the chronic inflammatory state in SCD, haemolysis plays a starting role. The release of cell‐free haemoglobin from high‐rate haemolysis overwhelms the protective endogenous mechanism of haemoglobin and haem clearance by haptoglobin and haemopexin. Both free haemoglobin and haem scavenge nitric oxide (NO), reducing its bioavailability. The depletion of NO leads to increased reactive oxygen species (ROS), vasoconstriction and endothelial dysfunction. 25 , 26 Plasma levels of NO precursor (l‐arginine) are decreased during vaso‐occlusive crisis and acute chest syndrome. 27 NO‐dependent flow‐mediated dilation of blood vessels is impaired in patients with SCD. 28 Inflammatory mediators (interleukins, tumour necrosis factor, chemokines, platelet‐activating factor) recruit macrophages and leucocytes, activate platelets and increase the expression of vascular cell adhesion molecules (VCAM‐1) and selectins (P‐selectin and E‐selectin). 29

Circulating human platelets contain a diverse transcriptome and are capable of protein synthesis. Under signal‐dependent control, activated platelets can synthesise de novo proteins, such as procoagulant tissue factor (TF) and pro‐inflammatory IL‐1β. 30 Previous studies have shown that platelets from SCD patients have higher levels of cytokine mRNA and secrete increased levels of interleukins IL‐1β, IL‐6 and soluble CD40 ligand (sCD40L) 31 as well as express the abundant levels of membrane‐bound CD40L and soluble CD62P (P‐selectin). 32 The interaction between CD40L and its receptor, expressed on antigen‐presenting cells, B lymphocytes and endothelial cells, activates and primes the vascular endothelium. 33 , 34 This milieu of chronic inflammation fuels the release of IL‐6 by both immune cells and vascular endothelial cells. IL‐6, a key factor in reactive thrombocytosis, stimulates hepatic thrombopoietin (TPO) production, leading to the upregulation of proplatelet production by megakaryocytes. 35 Increased level of IL‐6 has been shown to be associated with a higher frequency of vaso‐occlusive crises, acute chest syndrome, leg ulcers, osteonecrosis, stroke and priapism in patients with SCD. 36

Ischaemia‐reperfusion injury enhances platelet activation and thrombopoietic drive

In SCD, repeated cycles of sickling and unsickling during deoxygenation and reoxygenation generate excessive ROS, which enhances the activation of endothelial cells and leucocytes, thereby increasing the expression of adhesion molecules. 37 Restoration of blood flow after ischaemia leads to reperfusion injury, furthering the pro‐inflammatory response and tissue damage. At a cellular level, anaerobic metabolism and lactic acid production occur during prolonged tissue hypoxia, resulting in depletion of ATP and acidosis, leading to the production of hypoxanthine. 29 The re‐establishment of oxygen delivery paradoxically produces superoxide anions as hypoxanthine is converted to xanthine and uric acid.

As more ROS are produced during ischaemia‐reperfusion injury, the level of NO is further depleted, and the threshold for platelet activation and aggregation is lowered. 38

Injured cells release damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as high‐mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), which can bind to platelet TLR4 to induce platelet activation, granule secretion and aggregation. 39 Previous studies on cardiac tissue injury, burn injury and sepsis have demonstrated positive correlations between increased TPO levels and leucocyte‐platelet aggregation, as well as between TPO and platelet P‐selectin expression. 40 , 41 TPO blockade experiments have shown reduced organ damage and infiltration of inflammatory cells. 41 Therefore, in the presence of tissue injury, TPO acts as an acute‐phase protein, upregulated by inflammatory cytokines. The elevated TPO level, as the primary cytokine regulator of megakaryocyte proliferation, drives platelet production by binding to its receptor MPL (myeloproliferative leukaemia protein), activating the TPO‐MPL signalling axis 42 as part of the systemic inflammatory response, with reactive thrombocytosis and increased platelet turnover.

THE INTERPLAY BETWEEN PLATELET ACTIVATION, THROMBOCYTOSIS AND PLATELET‐DERIVED MICROPARTICLES (PMPs)

Reactive thrombocytosis amplifies the haemolysis–inflammation–tissue ischaemia cycle by boosting the pool of platelets available for activation, thereby fuelling a procoagulant microenvironment. High platelet turnover fosters the presence of immature, hyperactive platelets with increased surface expression of activation markers.

It has been shown that activated platelets release PMPs, which further augment inflammation by increasing PS exposure and boosting the surface area for thrombin generation. 43 Higher levels of PMPs are found in sickle cell patients with severe disease, defined as at least three episodes of vaso‐occlusive crises over a 2‐year period, compared to those with non‐severe disease. 44 However, the role of PMPs in the pathogenesis of SCD has not yet been fully elucidated, as there are many challenges in PMP isolation and recovery. Based on the current literature, we propose a potential mechanistic framework whereby PMPs contribute to the inflammatory response and the procoagulant effects in SCD. Thus, Virchow's triad of hypercoagulability (from platelet‐induced procoagulant activities), endothelial damage (from haemolysis, ROS, inflammation, platelet activation and abnormal cell–cell adhesions) and vascular stasis (from vaso‐occlusion) establish a perfect milieu for arterial and venous thrombosis, furthering the tissue damage observed in SCD.

PMPs formation, detection and characterisation

Extracellular vesicles are phospholipid membrane‐derived vesicles containing heterogeneous cargos such as genetic materials, proteins, lipids and metabolites that play crucial roles in physiological and pathological processes. 45 These vesicles are further classified into subgroups: nanosized exosomes (<100 nm), microvesicles or microparticles (100–1000 nm) and apoptotic bodies (>1000 nm). 46 PMPs are budded from the plasma membrane of platelets or megakaryocytes. 47 PMP formation occurs during cytoskeletal rearrangements and membrane budding when platelets or megakaryocytes are activated or undergo apoptosis. 48 Approximately 104 PMPs/μL are present in healthy individuals, with the majority of CD41+ PMPs derived from megakaryocytes, suggesting that their generation in vivo contributes to normal haemostasis. 49 However, significantly higher concentrations of PMPs generated from activated platelets are detected in inflammatory disorders such as SCD, heparin‐induced thrombocytopenia, cancer, anti‐phospholipid syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus. 50 Similar to their activated platelet parents, these PMPs carry a variety of biomolecules, including miRNA, transcription factors, proteins, P‐selectin, cytokines, chemokines, inflammatory mediators and various receptors and ligands, capable of de novo protein synthesis and inducing endothelial dysfunction in SCD 48 , 51 , 52 (Figure 2).

PMPs' specific markers are CD41, CD61, CD42b or CD62P (P‐selectin). Under basal conditions, PMPs are generally thought to derive from megakaryocytes that express CD41+, CD42b+ and full‐length filamin A and are negative for activation markers. Under inflammatory conditions, PMPs are derived from circulating activated platelets that externalise PS, P‐selectin, lysosome‐associated membrane protein‐1 and factor X. 9 , 10 The externalised PS also provides additional phospholipid surfaces for the procoagulant complexes, such as prothrombinase and X'ase. 48 Although the functional role of PMP miRNAs in SCD remains unexplored, Laffont et al. have shown that PMPs from activated platelets contain miRNA (miR‐223) that is internalised by endothelial cells in vitro. 53 Specifically, miR‐223 regulates the endogenous endothelial genes FBXW7 and EFNA1 at the mRNA and protein levels. 53 Studies in other diseases have also observed the transfer of PMP‐derived miRNA into hepatocytes following partial hepatectomy in mice, 54 into tumour cells resulting in growth inhibition 55 and into macrophages to modify their transcriptome and reprogramme their function. 56

There are methodological challenges in PMP detection that must be addressed to ensure accurate quantification and reproducible clinical interpretation. Pre‐analytical variables, such as collection tube selection, anti‐coagulant effects, transport, storage, temperature and the time between collection and processing, are critical determinants of measurement validity yet remain an undervalued source of variability.

The landmark large‐scale EVBB study has demonstrated the effects of different processing intervals and collection tubes, including serum, routinely used anti‐coagulants and specialised preservatives, on extracellular vesicle analysis. 57 Several studies have shown that serum contains higher levels of activated platelet extracellular vesicles than plasma and EDTA‐containing tubes, due to platelet activation during clot formation. 57 , 58 , 59 Palviainen et al. have observed that citrated plasma yields higher concentrations of platelet‐derived extra‐cellular vesicles than EDTA plasma, suggesting that citrate is less effective at inhibiting platelet activation, thereby resulting in the generation of microparticles in the sample during and after blood collection. 58 In contrast, other studies have concluded that citrated collection tube was the most suitable for microparticle analysis. 60 , 61 , 62

Discrepancies are also evident in the time to sample processing. The EVBB study recommends up to 1 hour after sample collection in acid citrate dextrose (ACD), or EDTA tubes, whereas Baek et al. suggest either immediate processing or waiting 24 hours to avoid the suspected removal of extra‐cellular vesicles during centrifugation. 57 , 61 Sample shaking during transport has been shown to increase PMPs' release, likely due to platelet activation. 61 However, vertical immobilisation of sample tubes during transport seems to limit microparticle generation. 62 Although freeze–thaw cycles are known to affect PMP levels ex vivo, 63 the impact of deep‐freeze storage at −80°C has not been observed. 62 Residual platelets prior to freezing have the potential to generate more microparticles. 64 Therefore, strict centrifugation protocols should be employed to generate platelet‐poor plasma and PMPs. 57 , 62 , 65

For structural, size and population studies, flow cytometry is most frequently used. The major limitation of conventional flow cytometry is the difficulty in detecting small populations of PMPs that do not express or have low density of specific protein markers, and in quantifying PMPs <400 nm, as the laser excitation wavelength in the flow cytometer is 488 nm for annexin‐V, which is often used as a general marker for PS on microparticles. 65 Individual instrument calibration to select the appropriate trigger channel for data acquisition, the use of polymer beads as reference particles and the selection of a buffer to set an acceptable threshold level are experimental techniques to employ. 66 , 67 More recently, nano flow cytometry has been demonstrated to provide a more sensitive detection of extra‐cellular vesicles. 68

Microscopy methods such as transmission electron microscopy and atomic force microscopy enable visualisation of PMPs and determination of their structure, but are labour‐intensive and require extensive sample preparation time. 69 Combining with immunoassay, atomic force microscopy has been shown to be better than conventional flow cytometry at detecting CD41‐positive PMPs less than 475 nm. 70 Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), which depends on Brownian motion of extra‐cellular vesicles in liquid phase for quantification, has also demonstrated superior sensitivity compared to conventional flow cytometry in detecting vesicles less than 300 nm. 71 The major limitation of NTA is its inability to distinguish extra‐cellular vesicles of interest from similar‐sized particles in solution, such as protein aggregates or lipoproteins. 72 The addition of fluorescence mode to NTA for detecting fluorescently labelled extra‐cellular vesicles can refine the technique and enhance accuracy. 68

To characterise the biological function of PMPs, procoagulant‐based assays, enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay based on PS exposure or CD42a (GPIX) and CD42b (GP1b) expression, 73 proteomic, lipidomic and microRNA analyses 53 , 74 , 75 , 76 have all been employed. Notably, these studies have different approaches in the pre‐analytical phase, particularly in collection tubes and centrifugation protocols. Therefore, it is difficult to determine the most robust method for PMP quantification and characterisation.

Activated procoagulant platelets externalise PS, exhibit membrane blebbing and release microparticles. 77 Considering the mentioned modalities and their limitations, the variability in quantifying PS externalised on PMPs can be rationalised. Arraud et al., using cryo‐transmission electron microscopy and annexin‐V conjugated gold nanoparticles labelling targeting PS, have reported that approximately 50% of PMPs do not express PS on their surfaces. 50 , 78 Notably, the study samples were collected from five healthy donors, with the post‐centrifugation platelet‐free plasma containing very rare non‐activated platelets, indicating that the methods used did not cause platelet activation. 78 Therefore, the PMPs detected were likely from these healthy donors, who were unlikely to have an underlying inflammatory condition that could induce platelet activation and a procoagulant state. Since most externalised PS are observed on activated platelets and their PMPs, non‐activated platelets and their respective PMPs may not have abundant exposed PS. Another possibility for the lower PS detection could be harsh sample preparation procedures used in electron microscopy, which may alter the lipid bilayer structure and affect the exposed PS.

Thus, the results of PMP quantification and characterisation can vary depending on the patient cohort, pre‐analytical variables and techniques used. The International Society for Extracellular Vesicles has acknowledged that the lack of standardised methods represents a major obstacle. 79 Orthogonal methods for quality control and internal comparison would likely be the preferred approach.

Many studies have investigated the role of microparticles originating from various cellular sources, including erythrocytes, leucocytes and endothelial cells, in SCD. 80 , 81 , 82 , 83 A comprehensive discussion of these microparticles falls outside the scope of this review. In brief, fragile erythrocytes, due to their weakened membranes, bud off microparticles with surface‐exposed PS. Haemoglobin from erythrocyte‐derived microparticles can scavenge NO, 80 thereby adding to the already depleted NO burden during an acute sickle cell crisis. Furthermore, these microparticles have been shown to transfer free haem to cultured endothelial cells in vitro, inducing oxidative stress and apoptosis. 84 Erythrocyte‐derived microparticles can induce intercellular adhesion molecule (ICAM‐1) overexpression in endothelial cells, thereby triggering a procoagulant phenotype and promoting neutrophil adherence. 81 In patients with homozygous SCD, Dembélé et al. have observed higher concentrations of erythrocyte‐derived microparticles, although a clear association with the hyper‐haemolytic sickle phenotype was not evident. 85 In the inflammatory state of sickle cell crisis, activated monocytes and endothelial cells also shed microparticles bearing TF, which can activate the coagulation cascade, promote further endothelial cell adhesion and amplify the thrombo‐inflammatory response. 82

Platelet inflammasome in SCD

Inflammasomes, large protein complexes that mediate the inflammatory response, have three major components: an upstream sensor molecule, an adaptor protein and a downstream effector protein. 86 The nucleotide‐binding oligomerization domain (NOD)‐like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome has emerged as a critical inflammatory mediator in SCD. The complex comprises NLRP3 (an immune sensor that responds to damage‐associated molecular patterns, or DAMPs), pro‐caspase‐1 (which, once activated, can cleave inflammatory cytokines) and apoptosis‐associated speck‐like protein containing a CARD (ASC) (an adaptor protein that links NLRP3 and pro‐caspase‐1). 87 , 88 NLRP3 inflammasome activation begins with the priming phase, in which DAMPs engage TLRs, leading to transcription and expression of NLRP3, caspase‐1 and pro‐IL‐1β. The activation phase then follows, characterised by recruitment of ASC and pro‐caspase‐1. 86

Platelets from SCD patients exhibit upregulated NLRP3 inflammasome activation, characterised by increased formation of the NLRP3:ASC complex and elevated caspase‐1 activity. This upregulation is observed both at steady state and is more pronounced during vaso‐occlusive crises. The activation is driven by both sickled erythrocytes and inflammatory stimuli in plasma, including HMGB1, which signals through TLR4 and is correlated with caspase‐1 activation in platelets. 89 Bruton kinase (BTK) plays a role in regulating NLRP3 inflammasome formation and caspase‐1 activation 89 (Figure 2).

In SCD mice, the activated platelet NLRP3 inflammasome promotes the release of extra‐cellular vesicles (50–200 nm) containing potent IL‐1β and caspase‐1, which bind to neutrophils, leading to platelet–neutrophil aggregation in the lung vasculature. Inhibition of caspase‐1 or IL‐1β not only abolishes extra‐cellular vesicle production but also mitigates NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby preventing downstream platelet–neutrophil aggregation and restoring microvascular blood flow. 90

PMPs contributing to the procoagulant effect and vascular dysfunction in SCD

Endothelial dysfunction, driven by chronic inflammation, platelet activation and increased cell–cell adhesions, leads to vasculopathy in SCD. The role of microparticles in SCD vascular dysfunction is extrapolated from studies in cardiovascular diseases with similar features of inflammation, thrombosis, endothelial damage and ischaemia‐reperfusion injury. 91 PMPs have been shown to modulate the adhesion of monocytes to endothelial cells in vitro 92 and exert procoagulant effects by providing surfaces for the assembly of coagulation factors and releasing TF in many thrombotic disorders. 93

An in vitro comparison of procoagulant activities has found that the PMPs' surface is approximately 50–100‐fold more procoagulant than that of activated platelets. 10 During a sickle cell crisis, microparticles containing TF are released from activated platelets, monocytes and endothelial cells, shortening plasma clotting time, thus demonstrating procoagulant activity. 7 This prothrombotic state is perpetuated by haemostatic abnormalities such as a decrease in the natural anti‐coagulants (protein C and S), an increase in thrombin generation, fibrinogen, TF, d‐dimer, platelet αIIb β3 integrin activation and thrombocytosis. 26 Activated platelets and their PMPs express CD62P (P‐selectin) and CD40L, facilitating binding to endothelium and leucocytes, further potentiating adhesions, neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation and endothelial dysfunction 94 (Figure 3).

PMPs containing IL‐1β and caspase‐1 have been linked to causing pulmonary vasculature occlusion in acute chest syndrome in SCD mice models and in human blood in microfluidic systems. 90 Acute pulmonary hypertension, assessed indirectly by tricuspid regurgitation velocity ≥2.5 m/s, has been reported to be a major risk factor for mortality. 95 Pulmonary vascular dysfunction is found to be present in SCD patients with acute respiratory distress because of acute chest syndrome. 96 A small prospective cohort study has demonstrated that patients with acute chest syndrome and pulmonary hypertension have higher levels of erythrocytes and PMPs compared to non‐SCD patients with severe community‐acquired pneumonia and healthy controls. 97

PLATELETS AND PMPs AS THERAPEUTIC TARGETS

Hydroxyurea

Hydroxyurea (HU; hydroxycarbamide), a ribonucleotide reductase inhibitor, has been the cornerstone of SCD treatment for over three decades. Beyond the clinical benefit of increasing fetal haemoglobin production, therefore partially inhibiting HbS polymerisation, it has been shown to reduce platelet counts and leucocytes, decrease endothelial adhesion molecules and increase NO and cyclic nucleotide levels, 98 thereby exerting its anti‐inflammatory and anti‐thrombotic effects. Nébor et al. have demonstrated that treatment with HU is associated with a reduction in total plasma microparticles, mostly from erythrocyte‐derived microparticles and PMPs, in paediatric SCD. 99 In contrast, a negative correlation between fetal haemoglobin and plasma microparticles (including PMPs) has been observed in untreated children. 99

Garnier et al. 81 have shown that microparticles from erythrocytes and platelets with high PS exposure preferentially bind to endothelial cells and induce ICAM‐1 expression, thereby increasing neutrophil adhesion. This induction of ICAM‐1 is decreased by HU therapy and increased during sickle crisis; therefore, suggesting that HU may reduce the externalisation of PS on microparticles, mitigating their abilities to induce a proinflammatory phenotype in endothelial cells. 81 , 100 These findings highlight that HU may play a dual role in targeting microparticles: reducing their number and modulating their functional capacity to trigger an inflammatory cascade.

Anti‐platelet therapy

The evidence for anti‐platelet therapy in SCD remains limited. Despite aspirin's widespread use in cardiovascular disease, its utility in SCD lacks robust clinical trial support. Theoretically, anti‐platelet agents hold promise as a potential therapeutic approach by targeting platelet activation and aggregation, thereby reducing inflammation, improving microvascular flow and mitigating vaso‐occlusion. However, studies regarding the use of aspirin were historic, underpowered and yielded negative results. 101 , 102 , 103 A large multinational randomised controlled trial of prasugrel, a thienopyridine that binds to the ADP P2Y12 receptor on platelets, has not demonstrated a reduction in vaso‐occlusive crisis in children and adolescents with SCD. 104 The HESTIA1 dose‐finding study has established the pharmacokinetic and safety data for ticagrelor, another P2Y12 receptor inhibitor, for paediatric and adolescent SCD patients, showing dose‐dependent platelet inhibition with good tolerability. 105 However, the follow‐up study, HESTIA3, has found no evidence of the efficacy of ticagrelor compared with placebo in reducing vaso‐occlusive crises, leading to early trial termination. 106 The age distribution was similar in both the placebo and ticagrelor groups, with approximately 60% of patients aged 12 years or older and 40% aged 12 years or younger. Platelet inhibition ranged from 6% to 73% 2 h post‐dosing in HESTIA1, while the median inhibition was 55.7% in HESTIA3. Although bleeding events were similar (at 9%) in the ticagrelor and placebo groups, one patient had a fatal intracranial haemorrhage in the ticagrelor group. 106 The failure to meet the primary end‐point may be attributable to suboptimal platelet inhibition. Moreover, the complexity of in vivo platelet activation, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction and microvascular occlusion in SCD may not be mitigated by a one‐prong approach. It remains challenging to determine whether higher levels of platelet inhibition would yield beneficial outcomes while balancing the bleeding risk.

P‐selectin blockade

Crizanlizumab, a humanised IgG2 kappa monoclonal antibody targeting P‐selectin on activated platelets and endothelial cells, blocks the interaction between P‐selectin and P‐selectin glycoprotein ligand 1 (PSGL‐1) on leucocytes, thereby inhibiting platelet–leucocyte aggregates. In preclinical studies, P‐selectin blockade also reduces TNF‐α‐induced neutrophil–platelet aggregate formation, improves microvascular perfusion and decreases pulmonary congestion in SCD mice. 107 The landmark Phase 2 SUSTAIN trial has demonstrated a 45.3% reduction in median annual vaso‐occlusive crisis rates compared to placebo. 108 However, the Phase 3 STAND trial has not shown crizanlizumab to be statistically superior to placebo on primary and secondary end‐points. The non‐superiority outcome is attributed to patient selection, geographic differences in healthcare settings and a low number of patients reporting crises during the pandemic. 109 , 110

Other novel agents targeting the interaction between activated platelets, endothelium and leucocytes are under investigation in preclinical studies. IHP‐102, a novel glycan‐based drug with dual anti‐P‐selectin and anti‐complement activities, has shown greater than 75% reduction in pulmonary vaso‐occlusion in SCD mice. 111 PSGL‐1 glycomimetic (P‐G6), another potent P‐selectin inhibitor, has demonstrated a decrease in platelet–leucocyte aggregation in vitro and in vivo, thus representing a potential candidate for anti‐vaso‐occlusion without the increased bleeding risk. 112

BTK inhibitor

Activated platelets are known to express the inflammasome complex comprising the pattern recognition receptor NLRP3, the ASC and BTK, which regulates the activation of caspase‐1 and the cleavage of IL‐1β. 89 , 113 , 114 The NLRP3 inflammasome mediates platelet activation, aggregation and IL‐1β‐dependent inflammation, all of which lead to endothelial dysfunction. 89 , 115 An increase in platelet P‐selectin expression, ATP secretion, platelet spreading, aggregation, thrombus formation, NLRP3:ASC formation and caspase‐1 activity has been observed in SCD mice but not in control mice. 113 Inhibition of caspase‐1 and IL‐1β pathways prevents PMP generation and platelet–neutrophil aggregation. 90 Treatment with ibrutinib, a BTK inhibitor, significantly suppresses all these processes, whereas the NLRP3 activator, nigericin, reverses these inhibitory effects, indicating that ibrutinib interferes with platelet activation via the NLRP3 inflammasome complex. 89 , 113

CONCLUSION

In SCD, haemolysis initiates the excessive release of cell‐free haemoglobin, haem and erythrocytic danger‐associated molecular patterns, creating overwhelming oxidative stress, activating the innate immune system through TLR4 signalling and triggering endothelial activation. 116 Due to the ongoing inflammatory state in SCD, platelets are chronically activated, evidenced by increased surface expression of P‐selectin, release of soluble inflammatory mediator (sCD40L) and inflammatory cytokines. 117 HMGB1 regulates inflammation and activates the NLRP3 inflammasome by stimulating ADP secretion and increasing P2Y12 receptors on platelets' surfaces. 118 Thrombocytosis, commonly observed in SCD patients at steady state and during crises, further amplifies the prothrombotic environment by increasing the number of activated platelets available for pathological interactions.

Considering the limitations of pre‐analytical variables and various techniques in quantification and characterisation of extracellular vesicles, in vivo and ex vivo studies have shown that PMPs released from activated platelets are associated with externalisation of PS on their surface and may act as parcels of biomolecules, including miRNA, transcription factors, TF and cytokines such as IL‐1β and caspase‐1. The release of these contents promotes platelet–leucocyte–erythrocyte aggregation, neutrophil extracellular trap formation and activation of the coagulation cascade, contributing to vaso‐occlusion and endothelial dysfunction. Further studies are needed to assess the extent of platelet activation and PMP formation during thrombocytosis, both in steady state and during crises, in SCD patients.

Clinical trials of therapeutics targeting platelet activation have not yet demonstrated substantial benefits. Currently, HU remains the only drug effective at reducing the number and pro‐inflammatory effects of platelets and microparticles. Preclinical studies of therapeutics targeting platelet–leucocyte aggregation and platelet inflammasome show promising results and may offer future advantages.
