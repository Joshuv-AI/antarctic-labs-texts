Population Pharmacokinetics of Valemetostat and Exposure–Response Analyses of Efficacy and Safety in Patients with Relapsed/Refractory Peripheral T–Cell Lymphoma
Authors: Hiroyuki Inoue, Xiaoning Wang, Ramon Garcia, Brian Reilly, Masaya Tachibana, YoungJun Yoo, Yvonne Lau, Yang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractValemetostat is a dual inhibitor of EZH2/1 approved in Japan for the treatment of relapsed/refractory (R/R) adult T–cell lymphoma/leukemia (ATLL) and R/R peripheral T–cell lymphoma (PTCL). It is administered orally once daily (QD) at 200 mg. Here, we present comprehensive population pharmacokinetic (PPK) and exposure–response (ER) analyses of valemetostat. The PPK model included data from six clinical trials in patients with non–Hodgkin lymphoma, including ATLL/PTCL, or healthy participants. ER efficacy analyses were based on data from one phase 2 clinical trial in patients with PTCL; ER safety analyses used data from three trials in patients with ATLL or PTCL. Valemetostat unbound average concentration up to event was used as the exposure metric. ER analyses included overall response rates for efficacy and six safety endpoints (any Grade ≥3 treatment–emergent adverse event [TEAE]; Grade ≥3 thrombocytopenia, anemia, and neutropenia; and dose reduction and interruption due to TEAEs). Valemetostat pharmacokinetics were well described by a three–compartment model, with a sequential linked zero–/first–order absorption, a saturable binding component in the central compartment, and linear elimination of unbound valemetostat from the central compartment. Alpha‐1‐acid glycoprotein was the only identified covariate significantly affecting total valemetostat exposure but had no impact on unbound exposure. The ER relationship on efficacy was not significant, and positive relationships were identified for multiple safety endpoints. Safety simulations across different doses suggested an acceptable safety profile for 200 mg QD. Overall, these analyses support the favorable benefit–risk profile of valemetostat at 200 mg QD in patients with R/R PTCL.Keywords: exposure–response analysis, peripheral T–cell lymphoma, population pharmacokinetic modeling, valemetostat

Introduction

Enhancer of zeste homolog (EZH)2 and EZH1 are histone methyltransferases involved in various solid and hematologic malignancies. 1 , 2 , 3 Gain–of–function mutations or overexpression of these enzymes are associated with poor outcomes. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Valemetostat tosylate (valemetostat) is a novel and potent dual inhibitor of EZH2 and EZH1 that is currently approved in Japan for the treatment of relapsed/refractory (R/R) adult T–cell lymphoma/leukemia (ATLL) 6 and R/R peripheral T–cell lymphoma (PTCL) 7 at an oral dose of 200 mg once daily (QD).

These approvals are based on the positive results from multiple trials. Initially, the phase 2 DS3201‐A‐J201 (J201) trial enrolled adults with R/R ATLL, leading to response rates of 48%, while the most common Grade ≥3 treatment–emergent adverse events (TEAEs) included thrombocytopenia, anemia, and lymphopenia. 8 The later phase 2 DS3201‐A‐U202 (VALENTINE–PTCL01) trial was a multicenter, open–label, single–arm study of valemetostat monotherapy in adult patients with R/R PTCL and R/R ATLL. In this study, treatment consisted of valemetostat 200 mg QD administered in 28‐day cycles. The response rate in the PTCL population was 44%, while the most commonly reported Grade ≥3 TEAEs were also hematologic in nature (thrombocytopenia, anemia, and neutropenia) in both patient populations. 9 Valemetostat is currently under investigation in patients with various solid tumors. 10 , 11

We have previously reported a population pharmacokinetic (PPK) model, which provided a good description of the observed concentrations of total and unbound valemetostat in both healthy individuals and patients with R/R ATLL. No particular subpopulations were identified as needing a different dose from the recommended valemetostat 200 mg QD. 12 A pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic model for platelet dynamics has shown that taking valemetostat 200 mg QD, with dosing reduction in response to TEAEs, can lead to the best outcomes in terms of minimizing Grade 4 thrombocytopenia. 12 Further development and application of exposure–response (ER) models for multiple efficacy and safety endpoints confirmed the benefit–risk profile of the 200 mg QD dose in adults with R/R ATLL. 13

In this study, we updated the PPK and ER safety analyses to include the R/R PTCL population from the VALENTINE–PTCL01 study and conducted ER efficacy analysis on patients with R/R PTCL. The results of these analyses were used to assess the benefit–risk profile of valemetostat 200 mg QD in adult patients with R/R PTCL.

Methods

Ethics Statement

All trials were conducted in compliance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and Good Clinical Practice guidelines outlined by the International Council for Harmonization, and in accordance with applicable local regulatory requirements. The study protocols were approved by the corresponding Institutional Review Boards, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to any trial procedure.

Study Population

The PPK model included data from a total of six valemetostat studies: the DS3201‐A‐J101 (“J101”; NCT02732275) study in patients with R/R non–Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), including B–cell lymphomas, ATLL, and PTCL 14 ; the J201 (NCT04102150) study in patients with R/R ATLL 8 ; the VALENTINE–PTCL01 (NCT04703192) study in R/R PTCL and R/R ATLL 9 , 15 ; the DS3201‐A‐J107 (“J107”; jRCT2080225242) 16 ; drug–drug interaction (DDI) study in healthy Japanese participants; the DS3201‐A‐J109 (“J109”; jRCT2071200043) 17 assessing the effect of food on valemetostat in healthy participants; and the DS3201‐A‐U106 (“U106”; NCT04276662) study in non–Japanese participants with hepatic impairment 18 (Table S1). The ER model for efficacy included only data from patients with R/R PTCL enrolled in the VALENTINE–PTCL01 study, while the safety model employed data from patients with R/R PTCL and R/R ATLL enrolled in the J101, J201, and VALENTINE–PTCL01 studies (Table S1).

PPK Model Development

Base model development started by implementing a previously developed PPK model of valemetostat based on J101, J201, J107, J109, and U106, with data from the VALENTINE–PTCL01 study. 12 Valemetostat plasma concentrations were determined using a validated liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) method with a lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) of 1 ng/mL for total concentration; LLOQ for unbound concentration is not applicable. 18 This model is a three–compartment pharmacokinetic (PK) model with a sequential linked zero–/first–order absorption, a saturable binding sub–model in the central compartment, and linear elimination of the unbound drug from the central compartment (Figure 1), with both total and unbound valemetostat concentrations modeled simultaneously by a single PPK model. The saturable binding component included parameters for binding affinity (KD) and for total binding capacity (RMAX). RMAX was modeled as a function of alpha‐1‐acid glycoprotein (AAG), which explained a significant portion of the variability in total valemetostat PK.

AAG, alpha‐1‐acid glycoprotein; CL, unbound valemetostat clearance; D1, duration of zero–order input; F1, bioavailability of zero–order input; KA, first–order absorption rate constant; KD, binding affinity; Q2, Q3, intercompartmental clearances; RMAX, total binding capacity; V1, V2, V3, volumes of distribution.

A full covariate model was developed including all predefined covariates (Table S2): baseline AAG, age, weight, creatinine clearance, population type (healthy participant, PTCL, ATLL, and other NHL), sex, race/country, hepatic function based on the National Cancer Institute–Organ Dysfunction Working Group (NCI–ODWG), and concomitant medications. Concomitant medications, such as P–glycoprotein (P–gp) and/or cytochrome P450 3A (CYP3A) inhibitors, were treated as time–varying covariates. For model–based inference, forest plots demonstrated the effect of covariates on total and unbound valemetostat area under the concentration–time curve for a dosing interval at steady state (AUCss), maximal concentration at steady state (Cmaxss), and minimal concentration at steady state (Cminss) from the PPK model.

The model was evaluated using a prediction–corrected visual predictive check (pcVPC) and basic goodness–of–fit plots including plots comparing predicted and observed data, and normalized prediction distribution errors (NPDE). Patient–specific post hoc estimates were derived from the final PPK model and used to simulate total and unbound valemetostat exposure metrics. Subsequent ER analyses were based on these exposure metrics.

ER Model Development

In the ER analyses, logistic regression models were fitted to the objective response rate (ORR) by blinded independent central review (BICR) in patients with PTCL, and to six safety endpoints, including Grade ≥3 anemia, Grade ≥3 neutropenia, Grade ≥3 thrombocytopenia, any Grade ≥3 TEAEs, dose reduction due to TEAEs, and dose interruption due to TEAEs, using the pooled dataset of patients with PTCL and patients with ATLL.

After exploratory data analysis that examined the average dose per individual in the studies, valemetostat unbound average concentration up to event (Cavgtte) was considered an appropriate measure of exposure for the ER analyses since it accounted for dose reductions or interruptions during the treatment. Additional analyses using valemetostat unbound average concentration of Cycle 1 (CavgC1) were also conducted as sensitivity analyses.

All ER model endpoints were dichotomous and were modeled using logistic regression in a Bayesian framework using weakly informative priors. 19 , 20 , 21 The structural model was selected from the null model (without exposure effect) and following four functional forms of ER relationships: linear, log–linear, maximum effect (Emax), and sigmoidal Emax. The model selection criteria included the expected log predictive density (ELPD) and the parameter identifiability assessment.

The effects of covariates were evaluated by a full covariate modeling approach. For the efficacy endpoints, the baseline covariates considered were (Table S3) age, body weight, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) concentration, AAG concentration, tumor size, race/country, sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) score, number of prior regimens, PTCL subtypes, hepatic function based on NCI–ODWG, and prior transplants. Furthermore, the safety endpoints considered the following baseline covariates (Table S3): age, body weight, LDH, AAG, race/country, sex, hepatic function based on NCI–ODWG, ECOG PS score, number of prior regimens, prior transplants, and disease type. Endpoint–specific baseline covariates were also included for specific safety analyses (Table S3), including hemoglobin concentration for Grade ≥3 anemia, neutrophil count for Grade ≥3 neutropenia, and platelet count for Grade ≥3 thrombocytopenia. For all models, posterior samples were obtained from four Markov chains, starting from dispersed initial values. Following a warm–up sequence with 1000 samples, iterations were continued to attain 2500 samples. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) convergence was diagnosed included the effective sample size (ESS) and Gelman–Rubin diagnostic, 23 while trace plots and overlay density plots were used to assess the stationarity in the MCMC algorithm. Simulation

To identify subgroups with potential differences in efficacy and safety for valemetostat 200 mg QD, or predict safety outcomes at other dose levels, 1000 patients with PTCL were resampled, and individual unbound average concentrations at steady state (Cavgss), corresponding to Cavgtte in the absence of dose modifications and calculated as the ratio of AUCss to dosing interval and at Cycle 1 (CavgC1), were simulated using the PPK model while considering the interindividual variability (IIV). These simulated Cavgss exposures were then used to predict the probabilities of ORR and safety events based on posterior samples from the Cavgtte–based ER model parameters. Parallel simulations were also conducted using the CavgC1–based ER models.

Software Used

The PPK analysis was conducted via nonlinear mixed–effects modeling using the NONMEM software version 7.5 (ICON PLC, Ireland). The stochastic approximation expectation maximization (SAEM) with interaction followed by Monte Carlo importance sampling (IMP) was employed for estimation of the PPK model. ER models were implemented in Stan 24 using the default no–U–turn sampler (NUTS) with the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo method, through the cmdstanr interface (cmdstanr v.0.5.3, CmdStan v.2.31.0). Data manipulation, visualization, and simulations were conducted using R version 4.1.0 or above. 25

Results

Participants and Sample Disposition

The PPK analysis dataset included 4635 total valemetostat observations from 342 participants, and 3085 unbound valemetostat observations, from 339 participants. Within the dataset, 131 (2.8%) total valemetostat concentrations and 43 (1.4%) unbound valemetostat concentrations were below the limit of quantification (BLQ).

Demographics and clinical characteristics of patients and healthy participants are provided in Table 1, with the majority of participants included in the PPK analysis dataset being male (64.3%), Asian Japanese (41.8%), and most commonly with PTCL (55.6%). Furthermore, patients with ATLL, PTCL, or other forms of NHL tended to have higher AAG levels than non–patients (Figure S1).

The ER efficacy dataset included 119 patients with PTCL enrolled in VALENTINE–PTCL01, and the demographics and clinical characteristics are provided in Table 1. The majority of patients were White (62.2%), male (71.4%), and with less than half (42.9%) having an ECOG PS score of 0. Angioimmunoblastic T–cell lymphoma (AITL) and PTCL not otherwise specified (NOS) were the most common PTCL subtypes (35.3% and 34.5%, respectively). In terms of efficacy response, 43.7% of patients achieved either a complete or partial response based on BICR assessment.

The ER safety dataset included a total of 251 patients, of whom 71 were from J101, 25 from J201, 133 from the VALENTINE–PTCL01 PTCL cohort, and 22 from the VALENTINE–PTCL01 ATLL cohort. Demographics and clinical characteristics are provided in Table 1, with the majority of included participants as male (64.1%). Patient race was predominantly reported as White (45.4%), with the remaining categorized as Asian Japanese (30.3%), Asian non–Japanese (8.4%), or Other (15.9%). The majority of patients had PTCL (75.7%), while the rest had ATLL (24.3%).

Final PPK Model Including Covariate Effects

The base model development began by implementing the previously developed model describing the population PK. With additional data included in the current analyses compared with the previous model development, the random effect structures were modified from the previous model to incorporate IIV on apparent clearance (CL/F), apparent central volume (V1/F), absorption rate constant (KA), zero–order release into an absorption compartment (D1), RMAX, and bioavailability (F1), with two block matrices: one for CL/F and V1/F, and another for KA and D1. Visual inspection of the model diagnostics suggested that this base model provided a reasonable description of the data.

The final PPK model expanded the base model to include all the pre–specified covariate effects. Effect of body weight was included with fixed allometric exponents of 0.75 and 1 for all clearance and volume terms. AAG concentration may influence total valemetostat exposure, as valemetostat primarily binds to plasma proteins, particularly AAG. 12 Additionally, valemetostat is a substrate of CYP3A and P–gp, and its concentration may be affected by concomitant use of CYP3A and P–gp inhibitors. 26 To account for these factors, concomitant medications (none vs concomitant use of a P–gp inhibitor, CYP3A inhibitor, or combined P–gp/CYP3A inhibitors; multiple categories of concomitant medications were grouped together due to small sample size) were incorporated into F1, and AAG was included in RMAX, CL/F, and F1 by estimating a common effect on both CL/F and F1. Other covariate effects were estimated on clearance. The final model parameters are shown in Table 2. All model parameters, including covariate effect parameters, were reasonably estimated. The final model adequately captured the observed total and unbound valemetostat data as shown in the pcVPC plots (Figure S2). In addition, no apparent bias was identified from the goodness‐of‐fit plots (Figure S3 and S4). The impact of covariates on total and unbound exposure is shown in Figure 2. The analysis suggested that binding of valemetostat to AAG in the plasma influenced total valemetostat exposure, but had little effect on unbound valemetostat exposure. Disease type (PTCL vs ATLL vs other NHL vs non–patients) did not show a clinical meaningful effect on total and unbound valemetostat exposure, as the point estimates and uncertainty were almost fully contained within the reference range (Figure 2). This general lack of disease–type effect was supported by the unbound valemetostat exposure simulations using post hoc PK parameters, but total valemetostat exposure metrics showed a slightly higher trend in patients than in non–patients (Figure S5). Furthermore, participants with moderate hepatic impairment had slightly increased unbound valemetostat exposure compared with participants with normal hepatic function, with unbound AUCss increasing by 29% (95% confidence interval [CI] 1%‐66%). Additionally, patients that used P–gp inhibitors or strong or moderate CYP3A inhibitors with/without a P–gp inhibitor during the study had increased total and unbound valemetostat AUCss.

ER Model for Efficacy

A logistic regression model was fitted for ORR by BICR. A base model using unbound Cavgtte as an exposure metric with a linear ER relationship resulted in favorable ELPD values, with the estimated effect of Cavgtte on the odds of ORR being 0.756 (95% credible interval [CrI] 0.492‐1.11) for every 12.2 ng/mL increase of Cavgtte. The full model included effects from all the pre–specified covariates, and the parameter estimates are presented in Table S4. The estimated odds ratio for the effect of exposure was 1.10 (95% CrI 0.330‐4.05), while the ER relationship on ORR appears to be flat (Figure 3); the effect remained not statistically significant as the 95% CrI crossed the null value of 1 (Table S4 and Figure S6). Furthermore, the ORR decreased with increasing baseline LDH, with an estimated main effect of 0.570 (95% CrI 0.305‐0.960). Simulated ORRs were compared across subpopulations of interest for a dose of 200 mg (Figure 4); ORR was predicted to be 53.6% (95% CrI 40.2‐66.4) in the subpopulation with low LDH, and 29.7% (95% CrI 18.6‐42.7) in the subpopulation with high LDH.

ER Analysis of Safety

Logistic regression models were fitted for each of the six safety endpoints. Positive associations were observed between unbound valemetostat exposure (Cavgtte) and the probabilities of any Grade ≥3 TEAE, Grade ≥3 thrombocytopenia, Grade ≥3 anemia, and dose interruption due to TEAEs. The positive ER relationships were best described by a linear function of Cavgtte. No clear ER trend was observed for Grade ≥3 neutropenia or dose reduction due to TEAEs, and the null model (without exposure effect) was selected for these endpoints. The results from the full covariate models are shown in Tables S5‐S10. For endpoints where the null model was selected, the second appropriate fitting model was used to develop the full model to retain the exposure metric in the model, such as a model using Cavgtte with an Emax model for Grade ≥3 neutropenia and with a linear ER relationship for dose reduction due to TEAEs. In general, covariates tended to include the null value, except for baseline hemoglobin concentration affecting Grade ≥3 anemia, baseline platelet count affecting Grade ≥3 thrombocytopenia, and NCI–ODWG hepatic function, and baseline LDH impacting Grade ≥3 TEAEs. Visual inspection of the univariate PPCs suggested that the observed overall ER trends were adequately captured by the models. (Figure S7B‐G).

The probabilities of developing each TEAE were simulated at varying dose levels of valemetostat (Figure 5). For endpoints demonstrating positive ER relationships (Grade ≥3 anemia, Grade ≥3 thrombocytopenia, any Grade ≥3 TEAE, and dose interruptions due to TEAEs), the simulated probabilities were comparable between 200 and 100 mg, with median difference of ≤6% and overlapping distributions (Table S11). The probabilities of TEAE rates were also simulated across different subpopulations. Overall, no subpopulation exhibited an obviously different TEAE profile or ER safety relationship (Figures S8 and S9).

Discussion

Despite being a PTCL subtype, ATLL is typically characterized by different histopathology, etiology, and clinical presentation, compared with the other PTCL subtypes. ATLL is particularly aggressive and carries one of the poorest prognoses. 27 It is directly linked to HTLV‐1 infection and its incidence is higher in regions where HTLV‐1 is endemic. 28 For this reason, PTCLs are normally treated as a distinct group of diseases from ATLL.

The present study further characterized valemetostat PK by a PPK model and expanded ER analyses of valemetostat efficacy and safety in patients with R/R PTCL.

In this analysis, we used total and unbound valemetostat concentrations from six clinical studies including patients with ATLL, PTCL, and healthy participants, which were fitted simultaneously using nonlinear mixed–effects modeling. Valemetostat PKs were well described by a three–compartment model with a sequential linked zero–/first–order absorption, and a saturable binding component between AAG and valemetostat. The saturable binding sub–model, without target–mediated elimination, was included on the central compartment of unbound valemetostat to simultaneously model both total and unbound valemetostat concentrations and parameterized in terms of KD and RMAX, with AAG modeled as a sole binding partner in the central compartment only. 12 The analysis dataset exhibited substantial PK variability. To stabilize model estimation and ensure convergence, the model was expanded in terms of IIV and covariates and re–estimated with the SAEM method. The SAEM allowed for inclusion of complex block omega matrices for parameters likely to be correlated such as CL/F and V1/F or KA and D1, by overcoming the limitations associated with estimating a block matrix that are often encountered with the less flexible First–Order Conditional Estimation with Interaction (FOCE–INTER) method. 29 A large eta shrinkage was observed on KA in the final model. Compared with the previous model, the shrinkage increased from 19.6% to 69%, which was likely due to the addition of data from the VALENTINE–PTCL01 study, which contained sparse data with limited sample collections (the median number of samples per patients is 9) in the absorption phase of valemetostat. Such high shrinkage is not indicative of a model structure problem, rather it reflects the lack of sufficient information of the individual KA estimates. Since the exposure metric of average concentration (Cavg) used for ER analyses is less impacted by the absorption rate, the impact of high shrinkage of IIV on KA on the ER analysis is expected to be minimal.

Among the covariate effects included in the model, baseline AAG had the greatest impact on total valemetostat exposure, but it had a negligible impact on unbound exposure. For orally administered drugs primarily eliminated by the liver, plasma protein binding is not expected to influence the AUC of the unbound drug. 30 Since valemetostat is administered orally and primarily eliminated via the hepatic route, 31 its average unbound concentration is unlikely to be influenced by AAG levels. Notably, the higher total valemetostat exposures seen in patients compared with non–patients did not translate to higher unbound valemetostat exposures, which is the more clinically relevant measure. Simulated total valemetostat exposures showed generally similar trends between patients with PTCL and patients with ATLL, but patients overall (ATLL, PTCL, or other forms of NHL) tended to have higher total valemetostat exposures than non–patients. This aligns with the observation that patients had higher AAG levels compared with healthy participants and participants with no cancer but with mild/moderate hepatic impairment (Figure S1). The increased level of AAG in patients with cancer is eventually reflected in an increased protein binding and, consequently, reduced systemic clearance for total valemetostat, compared with individuals with no cancer. Despite these differences, unbound exposure metrics remained comparable between patients and non–patients, reinforcing that the observed variations in total exposure are related to the differences in protein binding rather than unbound drug levels. Therefore, no dose adjustments based on AAG levels are required for clinical practice in both patients with PTCL and patients with ATLL.

In a prior DDI study, AUC extrapolated to infinity (AUCinf) of total valemetostat increased approximately 4‐ and 1.6‐fold with concomitant use of itraconazole (a P–gp and strong CYP3A inhibitor) and fluconazole (a moderate CYP3A inhibitor), respectively. 26 In this study, simulations using post hoc PK parameters suggested that participants (n = 3) who switched on and off P–gp inhibitors had higher exposure while taking P–gp inhibitors, with unbound AUCss increased by 30%. Similarly, for the five participants who went on and off moderate CYP3A inhibitors (with or without P–gp inhibitors) during the study, unbound AUCss increased by 23% while taking CYP3A inhibitors. The predicted trend of increasing valemetostat exposure with concomitant use of CYP3A or P–gp inhibitors was in line with previous DDI studies, but the estimated magnitude of the effect differed. It should be noted that the assessment of DDI effects within this PPK model was limited due to a small sample size and lack of details on dosing information, the assumption of a time–varying effect with immediate inhibition effect upon onset of the inhibitors and loss of effect upon discontinuation, and many other confounding factors. Additionally, previous DDI studies assessed moderate–to–strong CYP3A inhibitors, while the PPK dataset did not account for the exact inhibition potency and did not contain a record of concomitant medication duration to the same degree of accuracy.

Aside from the effect of AAG on total valemetostat, there were no covariates with point estimates and 90% CIs fully outside the range of 0.8‐1.25. Therefore, no covariates were considered to have a clinically meaningful effect on exposure that would require dose adjustments.

Unbound AUCss was previously used as the exposure metric in the ER analyses for patients with ATLL. 13 However, dose reductions and interruptions often occurred during the valemetostat studies in patients, and AUCss would not be able to account for these dosing changes during the treatment, potentially introducing bias in identifying ER analyses. To account for dose reductions and treatment interruptions, Cavgtte was selected as the primary exposure metric for the ER analyses in this study. Wiens et al pointed out a potential confounding effect with using Cavgtte and recommended the use of PK metrics that are independent of the timing of the event to avoid bias. 32 Therefore, additional sensitivity analyses using CavgC1 were performed. The ER relationship for ORR remained flat and statistically nonsignificant. Simulated ORR at 200 mg dose were also comparable (Table S11). For safety endpoints, CavgC1–based models resulted in slightly higher estimated exposure effects for some safety endpoints and led to statistical significance for a few outcomes that were not significant with Cavgtte including Grade ≥3 thrombocytopenia and dose interruptions due to TEAEs. However, overall trends remained consistent, and simulated TEAE rates between 100 and 200 mg doses differed by less than 6% (Table S11). Therefore, conclusions from using CavgC1–based models were consistent with those from Cavgtte.

Covariates were assessed using a full model approach within a Bayesian framework that employed spike and slab priors. 33 This method does not exclude covariates on an algorithmic or statistical basis; instead, it utilizes regularization by intentionally applying shrinkage to covariate effects with negligible magnitudes. Although more than 10 covariates were included in the model, this method helps to prevent overfitting and ensures stable estimation. Varying ER relationships can occur when analyses populations are reflective of heterogeneous patient subpopulations. For example, baseline LDH demonstrated a statistically significant effect on the interaction with exposure in the thrombocytopenia model, indicating a steeper ER relationship in patients with high LDH. Hence, inclusion of the interaction effects has allowed for the ER relationship to vary across different covariate subpopulations.

All the developed ER models were able to adequately describe the observed data, indicating a flat ER relationship for ORR and positive relationships for multiple safety endpoints. The simulated probabilities of each TEAE rate for doses ranging from 100 to 300 mg demonstrated positive dose–response relationships in Grade ≥3 anemia, Grade ≥3 thrombocytopenia, any Grade ≥3 TEAE, and dose interruptions due to TEAEs as expected. However, the differences between 100 and 200 mg were less than 6% in median simulated rates and overlapping distributions. Thus, ER analyses overall suggested that the exposure range of valemetostat 200 mg QD was associated with favorable efficacy and safety in the target population of patients with R/R PTCL.

A key limitation of this study is that only a single dose level of 200 mg was assigned to all patients with PTCL in the VALENTINE–PTCL01 study. 9 This resulted in a relatively narrow exposure range in the ER efficacy analysis and likely led to the estimated nonsignificant ER relationship for ORR. Generally, ER analysis based on a single–dose study tends to have a higher confounding risk than that based on a study with multiple dose levels. 34 , 35 Indeed, univariate regression suggested a negative association of valemetostat exposure with ORR, although this did not reach statistical significance. Additionally, after accounting for covariates, the ER relationship in the ORR appears to be flat. Discrepancies in the estimates of exposure to ORR relationships between univariate and multivariate regressions were likely attributed to data limitations and the confounding effects from covariates such as AAG, moderate hepatic impairment, and PTCL subtype. Mitigation strategies are often employed to partially address ER confounding and to enhance confidence in ER results, with multiple regression being a simple and commonly used approach. In this study, an analysis with a full model approach was deemed appropriate to comprehensively assess both exposure and covariate effects, thereby minimizing potential bias to some extent. 34 Given that the potential impact of confounding factors and limitations in effect identification are likely primarily due to data constraints, additional analyses will be warranted as more data become available in the future.

Conclusions

The PPK model well described the PK profiles of total and unbound valemetostat in patients with R/R PTCL. Covariate analysis suggested that no intrinsic or extrinsic factors, including AAG, were clinically meaningful for valemetostat unbound exposure, which is presumed to be a more clinically relevant exposure metric for efficacy and safety. Although the ER relationship for ORR was flat, the observed ORR of 44% suggests that the selected dose of 200 mg oral QD provides a meaningful therapeutic benefit to patients with PTCL compared with existing treatments. 9 Positive ER relationships were observed for multiple safety endpoints (Grade ≥3 anemia, Grade ≥3 thrombocytopenia, any Grade ≥3 TEAE, and dose interruptions due to TEAEs), while flat ER relationships were noted for Grade ≥3 neutropenia and dose reduction due to TEAEs. Safety simulations across different doses suggested that a lower dose (100 mg QD), half of the investigated 200 mg dose, may not significantly reduce probabilities of TEAEs. These findings support the use of a 200 mg QD dose as the treatment regimen in patients with R/R PTCL.
