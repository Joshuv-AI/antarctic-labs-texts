Efficacy of Preoperative Autologous Blood Donation for Surgical Treatment of Thoracic Spinal Stenosis: A Propensity‐Matched Cohort Study
Authors: Junbo Qi, Yuanyu Hu, Xiaoyan Niu, Yanlei Dong, Xin Zhang, Nanfang Xu, Zhongqiang Chen, Weishi Li, Yun Tian, Chuiguo Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThoracic spinal stenosis (TSS) surgeries necessitate a substantial amount of allogeneic blood resources. However, the efficacy of preoperative autologous blood donation (PABD) in TSS surgery has not been clearly evaluated. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the efficacy of PABD for TSS surgery.MethodsThis study is a retrospective study. Totally 397 patients who underwent TSS surgeries at our institution from January 2019 to June 2023 were included. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to make the PABD and Non‐PABD groups comparable at baseline. Regarding outcome measures, the incidence and amount of allogeneic blood transfusion, changes in postoperative hemoglobin and hematocrit levels, occurrence of postoperative complications, medical costs, drainage time, length of hospital stay, and postoperative neurological function were analyzed. The outcomes were compared between the matched PABD (n = 79) and Non‐PABD (n = 79) groups. Univariate analysis methods were used for statistical analysis, including independent samples t‐test, Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, and chi‐square test.ResultsThe incidence of allogeneic blood transfusion (8.9% vs. 25.3%, p = 0.006) and volume of intraoperative red blood cell (RBC) transfusion (10.12 ± 54.52 vs. 122.78 ± 275.00 mL, p < 0.001) in the PABD group were significantly lower than those in the Non‐PABD group. The PABD group had significantly higher average postoperative hemoglobin and hematocrit levels than the Non‐PABD group at 1, 3, and 5 days after surgery (p < 0.05). Similarly, the PABD group exhibited a smaller reduction in hemoglobin and hematocrit levels compared with the Non‐PABD group on 1, 3, and 5 days postoperatively. There were no significant intergroup differences in terms of transfusion‐related complications, medical expenses, neurological function, length of hospital stay, or drainage time. Notably, PABD was an independent protective factor of allogeneic transfusion in the multivariate regression analysis (OR = 0.334, 95%CI = 0.051–0.966).ConclusionsPABD can effectively reduce the incidence of allogeneic blood transfusion and amount of allogeneic blood in TSS surgeries with safety. It also significantly improved the postoperative hemoglobin and hematocrit levels. Under the premise of clear indications, PABD is worth promoting for the surgical treatment of TSS.

Introduction

Preoperative autologous blood donation (PABD) has been used in general spinal surgeries.1, 2, 3, 4 This is particularly true in surgeries with large amounts of blood loss, such as spinal deformity surgeries and spinal tumors.1, 3 Some studies confirmed that PABD can effectively reduce the number of allogeneic transfusions during the perioperative period, correct postoperative anemia, and reduce the risk associated with allogeneic blood transfusion.1, 4, 5 However, in certain specific circumstances, the application of PABD may result in limited improvement in postoperative anemia, preoperative iatrogenic anemia, and additional economic burden.6, 7

Thoracic spinal stenosis (TSS) is a rare syndrome but can lead to severe neurological deficit.8, 9, 10 To relieve the symptoms, surgery is the main effective treatment. 11 Previous studies have found that compared with cervical and lumbar degenerative surgeries, TSS surgery involves a longer segment and relatively more complex operation due to its pathological features. 9 Additionally, the thoracic spinal cord is prone to ischemic injury, leading to worse prognosis and requiring a higher demand for intraoperative circulatory stability.12, 13 In our institution, the incidence of allogeneic transfusion for complicated TSS surgery (posterior decompressed levels ≥5 or circumferential decompression) was 31.8% in the past decade. However, currently, there is a serious shortage of allogeneic blood resources, 14 which may delay surgery. Additionally, allogeneic transfusion‐related complications may have serious perioperative consequences.9, 15, 16, 17, 18 Therefore, PABD can be applied in the population undergoing TSS surgeries. However, there is currently a lack of evidence regarding the benefits of PABD in TSS surgeries.

The study aimed to (i) describe the use of PABD in TSS surgery at our center; (ii) evaluate the effectiveness and safety of PABD in TSS surgery; (iii) advocate for PABD as a blood management strategy for perioperative care in TSS and other spinal surgeries.

Methods

Ethical Consideration

This study received approval from the Medical Science Research Ethics Committee of our hospital (No. M2022177). Informed consent was obtained from all patients who were assigned to receive PABD. Due to the retrospective design of the study, informed consent was waived for patients who did not receive PABD.

Patient Population

We collected and analyzed electronic medical records of 423 patients who were diagnosed with TSS and underwent procedure between January 2019 and June 2023. This study is a retrospective study.The eligibility criteria for this study included age > 18 years with neurological symptoms and presenting imaging evidence of TSS, such as ossification of ligamentum flavum (OLF), ossification of posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL), or thoracic disc herniation (TDH). Patients with underlying causes such as tumors, infections, traumas, or deformities, as well as those who underwent minimal invasive surgeries or anterior approach procedures, were excluded. Ultimately, a total of 397 patients fulfilling the inclusion criteria were included in the study. All these surgeries were performed by experienced surgeons who are defined as having performed more than 300 spinal surgeries annually. Figure 1 illustrates the inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as the selection process followed in our study.

The flowchart of the study.

PABD Program and Interventions

All patients underwent a preoperative evaluation. Among them, those who received long segment surgery (posterior decompressed levels ≥5) or complex operations (circumferential decompression) were empirically considered with a high risk of allogeneic blood transfusion. The patients who fulfilled the following criteria: (1) age < 70 years; (2) preoperative hemoglobin ≥120 g/L; (3) no severe cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases; and (4) willing to participate in the PABD program, signed the informed consent and were included in the study. At 1–2 weeks before surgery, patients receiving the PABD program donated 400‐mL autologous blood. All donated blood was automatically transfused during surgery. The criteria for intraoperative allogeneic blood transfusion were (1) hemoglobin <90 g/L; (2) hemoglobin between 90 and 100 g/L with hypotension or tachycardia; (3) an intraoperative decrease in hemoglobin exceeding 40 g/L compared with preoperative levels. The criteria for postoperative allogeneic blood transfusion were (1) hemoglobin <70 g/L; (2) hemoglobin between 70 and 90 g/L with hypotension or tachycardia.

Data Collection

We retrieved patient demographics, preoperative laboratory test results, radiographic findings, and operative details from electronic medical records. We also extracted records of transfusion, postoperative laboratory values, medical costs, complications, postoperative modified Japanese Orthopedic Association (mJOA) scores (Supplementary file 1), 19 length of hospital stay, and drainage time to evaluate the efficacy of PABD.

Sample Size Calculation

We set the incidence of allogenic transfusion as the primary outcome. In the past decade, the incidence of allogeneic transfusion for the Non‐PABD patients who underwent posterior decompressed levels more than 5 or received circumferential decompression is about 30% at our institution. And the incidence of allogeneic transfusion for the PABD group is estimated to be about 10%. Based on α at 0.05 and power at 80% with a 1:1 allocation rate, more than 62 patients for each group can ensure the reliability of the primary outcome.

Statistical Analysis

As mentioned above, PABD is not a random intervention but rather a management strategy for patients who may undergo long segments or complex operations. To mitigate the possible confounding factors and ensure baseline comparability between the PABD and Non‐PABD groups, we performed propensity score matching (PSM)with a 1:1 ratio. We included variables in a logistic regression model (Figure 1). Notably, we quantified the scale of surgery and participated in PSM as covariates using the TSS invasiveness index (TII), which were strongly associated with intraoperative blood loss and operative duration. 9 Subsequently, we utilized SPSS's built‐in functions to generate propensity scores and conduct 1:1 matching using greedy nearest neighbor matching. We verified covariate balance using standardized mean differences (SMD), ensuring they were less than 0.1. Normality tests were performed on all continuous variables. To compare baseline characteristics and outcomes between the PABD and Non‐PABD groups, we employed the independent samples t‐test to compare normally distributed continuous variables and Wilcoxon rank‐sum test were used to compare non‐normally distributed continuous variables, and the chi‐square test was used to compare categorical variables. Univariate analyses and multiple logistical regression were used to screen for independent risk factors and evaluate whether PABD was the protective factor of allogeneic transfusion. IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (Armonk, NY, USA) was used for the PSM and data analysis. The statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

General Data

A total of 397 patients with comprehensive clinical data were included in the study (53.47 ± 11.96 years, 50.3% Female). In total, 112 patients took part in the PABD program, of whom 285 were assigned to the Non‐PABD group. Table 1 displays a comparative analysis of the demographic characteristics between the two cohorts. Approximately 49% of patients in the Non‐PABD group were pathologically diagnosed with OLF, and only 37.5% were diagnosed with OPLL and OPLL + OLF, indicating that simple posterior decompression could relieve symptoms. In the PABD group, OPLL and OPLL + OLF (55.9%) were the predominant diagnoses, indicating a more complex surgical operation. Operative time, estimated blood loss, and cell saver re‐transfusion in the PABD group were significantly higher than those in the Non‐PABD group (p < 0.001). Additionally, in more complex surgical operations, higher TII scores (28.82 ± 9.03 vs. 24.48 ± 9.25, p < 0.001) indicated that PABD was more commonly used in complex TSS surgeries. Without controlling for confounding factors, the comparison of outcomes between the two groups might have resulted in serious bias.

Allogeneic Transfusion

Intergroup comparisons of the outcomes are presented in Table 2. Before PSM adjustment, the PABD group had higher medical expenses and longer hospital stays but less red blood cell (RBC) transfusion volume than the Non‐PABD group, although there was no difference in allogeneic transfusion rates (11.6% vs. 12.3%, p = 0.853). After PSM adjustment, the PABD group showed a lower allogeneic blood transfusion rate than the Non‐PABD group (8.9% vs. 25.3%, p = 0.006). Moreover, the average transfusion volume of RBC (10.12 ± 54.52 vs. 122.78 ± 275.00 mL, p < 0.001) in the PABD group was lower than that in the Non‐PABD group, so does the total RBC transfusion volume (1600 vs. 9700 mL, Figure 3).

Postoperative Hemoglobin and Hematocrit Levels

Table 3 shows that PABD group had significantly higher average postoperative hemoglobin levels than the Non‐PABD group at 1, 3, and 5 days after surgery (Day 1: 130.08 ± 17.21 vs. 122.90 ± 17.32 g/L, Day 3: 122.41 ± 18.07 vs. 107.81 ± 17.83 g/L, Day 5: 129.00 ± 23.33 vs. 113.60 ± 19.35 g/L, p < 0.05). Additionally, hematocrit levels at 1, 3 and 5 days after surgery were also significantly higher in the PABD group compared with the Non‐PABD group (Day 1: 0.431 ± 0.036 vs. 0.352 ± 0.046, Day 3: 0.369 ± 0.050 vs. 0.315 ± 0.047, Day 5: 0.394 ± 0.066 vs. 0.331 ± 0.054, p < 0.05). Similarly, the PABD group exhibited a smaller reduction in hemoglobin and hematocrit levels compared with the Non‐PABD group on 1, 3, and 5 days postoperatively (Table 3).

Complications

As shown in Table 2, the incidence of overall transfusion‐related complications was relatively low in the two groups, with no notable disparities between the groups. In addition, PABD‐related complications were rare. Among the 112 patients receiving the PABD program, there was one case of vasovagal response (0.8%), two cases of poor vascular condition resulting in less blood collection (1.6%). One case (0.8%) experienced blood waste due to the surgical team's oversight in retrieving prestored autologous blood, as the patient had minimal intraoperative blood loss and stable circulation.

Medical Costs

Additionally, there was no significant difference in total medical (15310.73 ± 4962.42 vs. 15639.10 ± 5849.38$, p = 0.704) and operation expenses (9213.45 ± 3206.51 vs. 9836.74 ± 3765.64$, p = 0.265) between the matched groups. Moreover, the mean transfusion‐related expenses in the PABD group were found to be lower compared with the Non‐PABD group (117.77 ± 205.38 vs. 61.83 ± 129.42$, p = 0.043) (Table 2).

Significant Factors of Allogeneic Transfusion

The binary logistic regression results in Table 4 show that PABD and operative time were both significant factors of the occurrence of allogeneic transfusion, and PABD was an independent preventive factor (OR = 0.334, 95%CI = 0.051–0.966).

Discussion

In this study, first, we found that PABD can effectively reduce the incidence of allogeneic transfusion (25.3%→8.9%) and the volume of transfusion. Secondly, PABD can effectively protect against postoperative hemoglobin and hematocrit loss. Third, transfusion‐related and PABD‐related complications were rare in both groups.

The Significance of Research

In previous studies, the PABD program has performed well in various degenerative spine diseases (especially those involving long segments) and surgeries for spinal deformities such as adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS).1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 However, due to the pathological characteristics of TSS, we cannot directly apply the experience of PABD in other types of surgeries. The prognosis of TSS may be relatively worse compared with other degenerative spine diseases, with 5.5% patients experiencing no relief or even worsening of neurological functions after TSS surgery.20, 21 Due to the susceptibility of the thoracic spinal cord to ischemic injury, which can have a severe negative impact on postoperative neurological functions,12, 13 the correction of perioperative anemia and the stabilization of intraoperative circulation are extremely important in the perioperative management of TSS surgery. We need to verify the effectiveness of PABD in TSS surgery, with the ultimate goal of improving patient outcomes.

Indications for PABD

Posterior decompressed levels ≥5 or circumferential decompression are indications for the application of PABD in TSS surgery at our institution. The main pathological diagnosis of the two groups after matching was OPLL and OLF + OPLL (51.2%). A complex resection of the anterior wall of the spinal canal and interbody fusion based on the resection of the posterior wall which is called “circumferential decompression” has to be performed for OPLL.9, 22, 23, 24, 25 The characteristics of the two groups after PSM also showed longer average posterior decompression levels (5.75 vs. 5.05) and higher circumferential decompression rates (54.4% vs. 39.7%) than those before matching. In other words, the efficacy of PABD confirmed in our study was based on long‐segment or more complex TSS surgeries. Among patients who received complicated TSS surgery (operated levels ≥5 or circumferential decompression) over the past decade without PABD at our institution, the incidence of allogeneic transfusion was 31.8%, which suggested that our current indication can better identify the high‐risk population of allogeneic transfusion. Although the application of ultrasonic bone scalpel,26, 27 the use of tranexamic acid, and the accumulation of surgical experience have led to a decrease in intraoperative blood loss, resulting in a lower allogeneic transfusion rate of complicated surgery in the past four years compared with the previous decade (31.8%→25.3%), it still reflects a trend of relatively high allogeneic transfusion rate. In a study by Kennedy et al., 2 a similar conclusion was reached that the use of autologous blood was significantly more efficient in patients who underwent more complex instrumented fusion. This suggests that the effective use of PABD is based on the premise of relatively complex surgery.

Less Use of Allogeneic Blood

Some evidence suggests that the application of PABD can decrease the allogeneic transfusion rate by up to 52% during spine surgery. 4 However, some studies have reported an increased rate of allogeneic transfusion in adult spinal deformity surgery. 3 Our results partially were in accordance with the study by Mikhail et al. 1 They believed that the latter likely reflected the use of PABD in patients with inadequate compensatory erythropoiesis before surgery or the occurrence of iatrogenic anemia. In our study, the reductions in the incidence and total volume of allogeneic transfusions for TSS surgeries were significant. Interestingly, we also found that PABD provides considerable help in improving anemia caused by postoperative hidden blood loss (Table 3), ensuring more stable perioperative circulation. Similar trends in hemoglobin and hematocrit changes have been reported in other literature,28, 29 which may be interpreted as multiple factors such as intraoperative blood loss, postoperative hidden blood loss, and hemodilution in early stage.30, 31, 32, 33 PABD effectively helps patients withstand early postoperative anemia (within 5 days), which may decrease the risk of complications and adverse symptoms caused by anemia32, 33, 34 and facilitate a safe transition to erythrocyte regeneration.

Compared with other studies, the PABD process in our institution has unique advantages, which may better explain the reduction in allogeneic blood transfusions. First, we avoided the application of PABD in an anemic population to prevent a second hit caused by blood donation. Second, PABD in TSS surgery is somewhat unique. Our data of the past decade show that only 19% of the patients lost more than 1000 mL of blood during the operation, while the average blood loss in adult spinal deformity surgery is 1923 to 3024 mL,3, 35, 36, 37 which suggests that patient with spine deformity still need a large amount of intraoperative allogeneic blood transfusion as supplement after PABD. The significant differences in blood loss characteristics between the two procedures might explain the effectiveness of PABD in TSS surgery and its poor performance in spinal deformity surgery.2, 3 Meanwhile, the amount of blood transfusion was no more than 400 mL in 61% of the transfused TSS patients before we carried out the PABD program, which suggests that our current 400mL‐PABD program can effectively avoid the occurrence of this part of allogeneic transfusion. The transfusion of donated blood, cell saver, perioperative fluid transfusion, and vasoactive agents can effectively maintain the stability of circulatory system during operation, and no additional allogenic transfusion is needed. This may also suggest that the PABD in TSS surgery will be more efficient than that in other spinal surgery.

Low Incidence of Complications

Another benefit of PABD is that it avoids allogeneic transfusion‐related complications. However, it is currently limited to theory. Allogeneic transfusion may induce infectious complications, hemolytic reactions, and circulatory overload, which can lead to delayed rehabilitation, reoperation, and even death.8, 38 In a large‐sample study by Kato et al., 15 allogeneic blood transfusions during spinal surgery increased the incidence of infections and in‐hospital deaths. However, perhaps limited by the sample size and low probability of complications, both prior research on PABD and our study did not indicate a significant disparity in the occurrence of complications associated with allogeneic transfusion between the PABD and Non‐PABD groups. Similar to the study by Kelly et al., 3 we found no differences in postoperative neurological function between the two groups at 3 months (Table 2). The lack of significant differences in preoperative conditions and neurological function between the matched groups can explain this finding (Table 1).

In some views,1, 3 PABD may increase the total blood transfusion rate (including both autologous and allogeneic transfusion). However, our research has demonstrated its safety, as a higher total blood transfusion rate is not correlated with a higher incidence of transfusion‐related complications. According to the study conducted by Jiang et al., 39 3.19% of patients who underwent PABD experienced a vasovagal response during the blood donation process, which was more common in underage and anemia populations. 40 In our study, only one patient (0.8%) experienced a vasovagal response, which is lower that than reported in previous literature. However, there should be a contingency plan for such adverse reactions.

The Controversial Nature of PABD

Over the past decade, PABD has become a controversial blood management strategy.1, 6 The first controversy concerns the wastage of autologous blood. A high rate of autologous blood discarding has been reported in previous literature, ranging from 18% to 51%. 3 Most research institutions have errors in the management and use of PABD, which may cause autologous blood discarding. 4 In addition, the optimal collection time of autologous blood is ambiguous, and management costs are high. 6 However, we hold the view that the aforementioned issues can be solved by optimizing the PABD process. All donated blood in our study was stored for a relatively short period (<2 weeks) and transfused during the operation to avoid blood being discarded. According to previous studies, a minor amount of erythrocyte regeneration occurs within 5–10 days post‐donation. 41 This indicates that a 2‐week gap between donation and surgery would improve erythrocyte regeneration and facilitate better recovery. Of all patients with PABD, only one case (0.8%) in the early stage resulted in blood discarding due to management errors. Additionally, PABD did not impose an additional financial burden on the patients in our study. Owing to this well‐regulated process, the efficacy of PABD is exact.

Strengths and Limitations

Through a cohort study utilizing PSM, our research is the first to explore the application of PABD in TSS surgery. It reveals that this method can reduce the use of allogeneic blood during the perioperative period, can ensure circulatory stability, and does not introduce additional risks or costs.

There are some limitations in our study. First, its retrospective nature led to inevitable data unavailability and biased controls. Second, we selected the proper timing for PABD to avoid preoperative anemia. Owing to the lack of laboratory examinations on the day before surgery, it is difficult to fully understand the occurrence of preoperative anemia and complete trend of hemoglobin and hematocrit values between the preoperative and postoperative periods. Third, our indication of PABD is based on the previous clinical experience, and more precise indications require further statistically significant prediction models to be established and evaluated. In the future, we plan to generalize the PABD protocol and conduct a prospective, larger multi‐institutional cohort study.

Conclusions

PABD can effectively reduce the incidence of allogeneic blood transfusions and amount of allogeneic blood required for TSS surgeries with safety. It also significantly improved the postoperative hemoglobin and hematocrit levels. Under the premise of clear indications and a complete management process, PABD is worth promoting for the surgical treatment of TSS and other spinal surgeries.
