Moral distress of primary health care workers during the global pandemic
Authors: Meltem Doğan, Aslıhan Akpınar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the prevalence and intensity of moral distress among family health workers in Istanbul during the COVID-19 pandemic, as well as its association with the perceived ethical climate within family health centers. This descriptive, cross-sectional study was conducted in Istanbul between November 2021 and June 2022. Using Turkish versions of the “Measure of Moral Distress for Healthcare Professionals” and “Hospital Ethical Climate Survey-Shortened” scales, data were collected from three districts with the highest and three with the lowest mortality rates due to infectious diseases (n = 135 each). Mortality rates were based on deaths from infectious diseases, largely reflecting COVID-19–related mortality. The sample included 270 healthcare workers (77.4% women, mean age 39.92 ± 9.19 years; 44.9% family physicians, 26.6% midwives, 25.5% nurses). Participants in high-mortality districts reported significantly higher moral distress (p < 0.05). Family physicians exhibited greater moral distress and a poorer perception of the ethical climate compared with other staff (p < 0.05). Additionally, those experiencing the highest moral distress reported the lowest ethical climate scores. The results underscore the need for interventions to prevent moral distress, particularly among family physicians, during extraordinary situations. Ethical guidelines for primary care in such contexts are anticipated to enhance healthcare worker well-being and improve patient care quality.Keywords: Moral distress, Ethical climate, COVID-19 pandemic, Family health centers, Family health workersSubject terms: Medical ethics, Health care, Health occupations

Introduction

Primary healthcare (PHC) serves as the cornerstone of health systems, founded on the principles of social justice, equity, and solidarity, with the goal of ensuring the highest attainable standard of health for all1. In Türkiye, the backbone of PHC is the Family Health Center (FHC) model. The evolution of this system is deeply rooted in the “Socialization of Health Services” law of 1961, which initiated a network of health centers to guarantee equitable access2,3. This framework was subsequently transformed into the current FHC system under the Health Transformation Program, officially regulated since 2013. Within FHCs, family physicians and family health staff (nurses, midwives, and other health personnel) work collaboratively to deliver continuous, comprehensive care4.

The provision of healthcare in PHC settings is inherently complex, involving diverse patient profiles and ethical challenges. These challenges were magnified during the COVID-19 pandemic, which placed unprecedented strain on FHCs5,6. Family physicians and their staff faced a surge in responsibilities, encompassing contact tracing, home monitoring, and mass vaccination, while simultaneously grappling with shortages of protective equipment, fear of infection, and economic pressures7–10. This extraordinary situation created a fertile ground for ethical dilemmas concerning resource allocation, triage, and professional duty, similar to challenges reported in primary care settings globally11.

Such conditions are known to precipitate moral distress, a concept in healthcare ethics first defined by Jameton as the psychological distress that arises when one knows the morally right action to take but is constrained from pursuing it12. Moral distress stems from a conflict between personal ethical values and professional or institutional constraints, leading to significant psychological pressure that can adversely affect job satisfaction, mental well-being, and professional performance12,13.

However, research on moral distress has predominantly focused on hospital settings, particularly among nurses. While studies concerning moral distress during the pandemic exist, research specifically examining the experiences of family physicians and primary care teams—especially within the context of a public health crisis like the COVID-19 pandemic—remains limited. Yet understanding the moral distress experienced by these frontline workers is crucial, as their psychological well-being and moral integrity directly affect the resilience and responsiveness of the primary care system. Moreover, identifying the specific drivers and manifestations of their moral distress can inform the development of tailored institutional support mechanisms and evidence-based health policies, ensuring a more ethically sustainable primary care infrastructure during future crises14–17.

Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to determine the prevalence and intensity of moral distress among family health workers in Istanbul during the COVID-19 pandemic. A secondary aim was to examine their perception of the ethical climate within FHCs and to explore the correlation between ethical climate and moral distress levels.

Methods

Study design and setting

This descriptive, cross-sectional study was conducted in FHCs located in six districts with the highest and lowest COVID-19 death rates in Istanbul, the most populous city in Türkiye, between November 2021 and June 2022. Ethical approval was obtained from the Non-Interventional Ethics Committee of Kocaeli University on 21/10/2021, with project number GOKAEK-2021/18.15 (2021/301). Informed consent for participation in the study was obtained from all participants.

Participants and sample size determination

The participant selection process is summarized in Fig. 1. The study population consisted of physicians, nurses, midwives, and other healthcare professionals working in FHCs. The districts were selected to capture potential differences in the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Flowchart of participant selection and data collection.

Because official COVID-19 mortality statistics at the district level were not available, mortality rates from infectious diseases were used instead. During the pandemic period, deaths attributed to infectious diseases were known to largely reflect COVID-19–related mortality and therefore served as a proxy indicator for pandemic-related impact.

The minimum sample size was determined with reference to the scale with the highest number of items (the MMD-HP, with 27 items), using a recommended subject-to-item ratio of 10:1, resulting in a target sample of 270 participants. For the factor analysis conducted to validate the scales, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure was 0.911 (excellent) and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity was significant (χ2 = 3339.522; p < 0.001)18.

The measure of moral distress for healthcare professionals-Türkiye (MMD-HP-TR)

The MMD-HP was developed by Epstein in 2019 and adapted into Turkish by Doğan and Akpınar in 202418. The Turkish-adapted MMD-HP-TR scale was used for this study. The Cronbach’s alpha value for the MMD-HP-TR scale was 0.931. The 27-item scale includes a composite score ranging from a minimum of 0 to a maximum of 432. Higher scores indicated greater moral distress. Similar to the original scale, four dimensions were used in the study: i) System; ii) Clinical; iii) Team/Staff; and iv) Team/Patient19.

Hospital ethics climate survey-shortened-Türkiye (HECS-S-TR)

The HECS was developed by Olson20 and shortened by Hamric et al.21, (HECS-S) and adapted into Turkish by Doğan and Akpınar in 2024 (HECS-S-TR). The Turkish adapted HECS-S scale was used for this study and included 14 items. The Cronbach’s alpha value for the HECS-S-TR scale was 0.93518. The participants’ sociodemographic characteristics were defined using a questionnaire developed by the researchers.

The validity and reliability analyses of both Turkish-adapted scales (MMD-HP-TR and HECS-S-TR) were conducted within the same research project and sample. The present article reports the ethical findings obtained from that dataset.

The researcher (MD) conducted the study face-to-face between November 2021 and June 2022 using a stratified random sampling method. The strata were defined as two groups of districts—those with the highest and those with the lowest supposed COVID-19 mortality rates—based on deaths from infectious diseases, which largely reflected COVID-19–related mortality during that period. These strata were selected to capture the differential impact of the pandemic on healthcare workers. The target sample size was 270 healthcare professionals, with 135 participants to be recruited from each stratum. All family health centres (FHCs) within the selected districts were initially included in the sampling frame. Data collection in each stratum was concluded once the target of 135 participants was reached; therefore, not all FHCs were visited after the required sample size had been achieved.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R programs. Compliance with normal distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and non-normally distributed variables as median (25th–75th percentiles–interquartile range). Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percentage). Differences between groups were analysed using independent sample t-tests, Mann–Whitney U, and Kruskal–Wallis tests, as appropriate. Dunn’s test was conducted for multiple comparisons. Relationships between numerical variables were evaluated using Pearson or Spearman correlation analysis, as appropriate, and relationships between categorical variables were assessed using the Chi-square test. Statistical significance was accepted as p < 0.05.

In summary, there was a weak inverse relationship between the MMD-HP-TR scale and the HECS-S-TR scale (r = − 0.279).

The MMD-HP-TR scale had a four-factor structure similar to the original scale. According to the moral distress experience, the factor structures were system, clinical, team/staff, and team/patient dimensions, respectively19,22 (Table 2). Regarding sources of moral distress, the highest source was in the system sub-dimension, and the most intense moral distress-inducing situation was identified as “Having to care for more patients than I can safely handle.” According to the average results of the HECS-S-TR, the highest-scoring item was “My colleagues listen to my concerns about patient care.”

Regarding the relationship between personal data and scales, having children was found to increase moral distress (p = 0.01), while the HECS-S-TR showed that not having children increased the perception of the ethical climate (p = 0.010).

Discussion

This study examined moral distress and ethical climate among primary healthcare workers in Istanbul during the COVID-19 pandemic. Our findings indicate that pandemic intensity, professional role, and systemic factors significantly influenced these perceptions.

Overall levels of moral distress and international comparison

The overall level of moral distress in our study (110.77 ± 74.84) presents a mixed picture when compared to findings reported from different countries both before and during the pandemic. For instance, our finding is similar to the mean score (108.9 ± 70.7) reported by Epstein et al.19 among various healthcare workers in the USA, yet lower than the higher scores reported among primary care workers in Spain (127.3 ± 66.7)22. The lower median values reported in Japan23 and the relatively low scores in Sweden24 suggest that cultural, institutional, and pandemic management differences play a role in shaping moral distress levels. Importantly, as our data were not normally distributed, our results are reported as median [Value] (IQR [Value-IQR]). Therefore, direct comparisons with mean values in the literature are avoided, and interpretations are made in the context of general trends.

The impact of the pandemic: high vs. low mortality districts

The direct impact of pandemic intensity on moral distress is supported by the significantly higher moral distress scores reported by workers in high-mortality districts. This finding aligns with the high scores reported in primary care in Spain during the pandemic25. A significant contributing factor to this difference was the substantially higher daily patient numbers in high-mortality districts. Therefore, the workload during the pandemic appears to have increased moral distress, consistent with earlier reported findings. Studies by Lamiani (Italy), Miljeteig (Sweden), and Kızıltepe (Türkiye) have shown the impact of the pandemic on moral distress26,27,28. Issues such as resource scarcity, triage, changes in patient selection criteria, inappropriate working conditions, poor pandemic management, unequal resource distribution, healthcare worker shortages, infection risk, and work stress were identified12,26–30. This reinforces the view that workload is a primary trigger for moral distress and that the pandemic exacerbated this systemic challenge.

Professional differences: the experience of family physicians

Although the literature generally reports higher moral distress among nurses19,25,31, our study found that family physicians experienced higher levels of moral distress compared to other healthcare workers. This unexpected finding can be explained by the unique position of family physicians within the Turkish primary care system. They are individuals ultimately responsible for patient treatment pathways, making decisions under increasing administrative burdens and limited resources. Reports from Turkey highlight systemic stressors such as challenges in providing preventive services, inadequate physical infrastructure, excessive patient load, and ambiguous job descriptions32,33. The pandemic further exposed these pre-existing systemic vulnerabilities and increased the moral burden on physicians. This finding is supported by other studies where physicians reported higher moral distress than nurses23,34.

Systemic contributors and correlation with ethical climate

Participants experienced the highest moral distress from the “system dimension,” as measured by items like “having to care for more patients than I can safely manage.” This points to a fundamental challenge within the Turkish family medicine system. A low but significant negative correlation was observed between the Moral Distress in Healthcare Professionals Scale (MDHPS) and its subdimensions and the Hospital Ethical Climate Scale (HECS-K) (r = − 0.01 to − 0.29), indicating that as healthcare professionals’ perception of the ethical climate decreases, their level of moral distress increases. This finding aligns with previous studies showing that a weak ethical climate is associated with higher moral distress levels19,35,36. In primary care, a weak ethical climate may manifest as poor communication from leadership, a lack of structured forums for discussing ethical dilemmas, and a feeling among staff that they are not safe to voice concerns. Strengthening the ethical climate requires vital institutional support systems (e.g., mental health services, ethical consultancy, and supportive leadership that fosters open communication about ethics). Such support can help healthcare workers share and manage the moral challenges they face, thereby mitigating moral distress. We posit that strengthening these areas—through responsive leadership, structured ethical debriefings, and clear channels for voicing concerns—could serve as a key buffer against moral distress.

Limitations of the study

Requests for official COVID-19 mortality data from the Ministry of Health and local authorities were not granted; therefore, mortality data from the Istanbul Metropolitan Municipality were used. Deaths from infectious diseases served as a proxy, as most were attributable to COVID-19 during the study period. The purposive inclusion of districts with the highest and lowest infectious disease mortality rates may have introduced selection bias and limited representativeness. Conducting the study solely in Istanbul, with its distinctive demographic and healthcare characteristics, further limits generalisability. In the absence of pre-pandemic baseline data on moral distress and ethical climate, longitudinal comparison was not possible, and the results should be interpreted as a cross-sectional reflection of the pandemic period.

Additionally, the inclusion of only districts with extreme mortality rates may have introduced selection bias by excluding regions with moderate pandemic impact. While this design limits generalisability, it enabled the study to capture participants from settings that reflected the widest variation in moral distress and ethical climate, ensuring sufficient variance for meaningful comparison and scale validation. Future research including a broader range of districts would enhance representativeness and strengthen psychometric generalisability.

Conclusions

Healthcare professionals working in districts with the highest mortality rates reported significantly higher levels of moral distress. Supporting the postulated inverse relationship between moral distress and ethical climate, the ethical climate was perceived more negatively in these high-mortality areas, while it was viewed more positively in regions with lower mortality rates. These findings strongly suggest that the intensity of the pandemic had a direct impact on these psychological and ethical dimensions. Among the professional groups studied, family health physicians experienced greater moral distress compared to their colleagues. The primary source of this distress was rooted in the “system dimension,” with the item “having to care for more patients than I can safely manage” receiving the highest mean score. This highlights that the pre-existing issue of high patient load in Türkiye was critically exacerbated by the pandemic. Consequently, addressing these systemic flaws is paramount to mitigating moral distress in future healthcare crises. Proactive initiatives, such as implementing transparent protocols for workload redistribution, establishing robust institutional support systems like mental health services and ethical debriefings, and providing ongoing ethical training for crisis situations, are not merely beneficial but essential to safeguard the well-being of the healthcare workforce and ensure the resilience of the healthcare system.
