Multi-view learning framework for predicting unknown types of cancer markers via directed graph neural networks fitting regulatory networks
Authors: Xin-Fei Wang, Lan Huang, Yan Wang, Ren-Chu Guan, Zhu-Hong You, Nan Sheng, Xu-Ping Xie, Wen-Ju Hou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe discovery of diagnostic and therapeutic biomarkers for complex diseases, especially cancer, has always been a central and long-term challenge in molecular association prediction research, offering promising avenues for advancing the understanding of complex diseases. To this end, researchers have developed various network-based prediction techniques targeting specific molecular associations. However, limitations imposed by reductionism and network representation learning have led existing studies to narrowly focus on high prediction efficiency within single association type, thereby glossing over the discovery of unknown types of associations. Additionally, effectively utilizing network structure to fit the interaction properties of regulatory networks and combining specific case biomarker validations remains an unresolved issue in cancer biomarker prediction methods. To overcome these limitations, we propose a multi-view learning framework, CeRVE, based on directed graph neural networks (DGNN) for predicting unknown type cancer biomarkers. CeRVE effectively extracts and integrates subgraph information through multi-view feature learning. Subsequently, CeRVE utilizes DGNN to simulate the entire regulatory network, propagating node attribute features and extracting various interaction relationships between molecules. Furthermore, CeRVE constructed a comparative analysis matrix of three cancers and adjacent normal tissues through The Cancer Genome Atlas and identified multiple types of potential cancer biomarkers through differential expression analysis of mRNA, microRNA, and long noncoding RNA. Computational testing of multiple types of biomarkers for 72 cancers demonstrates that CeRVE exhibits superior performance in cancer biomarker prediction, providing a powerful tool and insightful approach for AI-assisted disease biomarker discovery.

Introduction

Compelling evidence implicated that a variety of complex human diseases, especially cancer, are associated with aberrant transcriptional and post-transcriptional expression of genes [1]. Transcriptome analysis of human cells shows that there are a large number of noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) that have not been translated into proteins in the human genome transcripts, and these RNAs are widely involved in the gene regulation of eukaryotes [2] and have important regulatory functions in a variety of complex diseases [3]. Therefore, identifying potential pathogenic ncRNAs has brought new perspectives and hopes for the diagnosis and treatment of diseases.

In recent years, researchers have developed numerous targeted computational methods to investigate different types of molecular associations within cells [4–6]. Based on the research objects, these associations can be categorized as follows: for microRNA (miRNA)-disease association prediction, Sheng et al. proposed the GCLMTP method based on graph contrastive learning, which effectively predicts potential miRNA-disease associations [7]. Wang et al. introduced the SAEMDA model based on stacked autoencoders to predict unknown miRNA-disease associations [8]. Additionally, Huang et al. provided a comprehensive overview of methods, data, and challenges related to disease-associated miRNA prediction [9–11]. In the domain of long noncoding RNA (lncRNA)-disease association prediction, Xuan et al. proposed the AGLDA model based on adaptive hypergraphs and gated convolution to predict unknown lncRNA-disease associations [12]. Sheng et al. combined convolutional and variational autoencoders to construct VADLP, which effectively predicts disease-related lncRNAs [13]. Furthermore, Sheng et al. conducted a thorough review of lncRNA-disease association prediction, addressing data resources and computational methods [14]. In the area of circRNA–miRNA interactions (CMI) prediction, Wang et al. proposed the KS-CMI model by integrating social theory and signed graph representations to predict potential competitive circRNAs for miRNAs in real-world scenarios [15]. Wei et al. introduced the BioKG-CMI model, which combines biological knowledge graphs to predict unknown CMI [16]. Additionally, Li et al. developed the DEEPCMI model based on rich text embeddings to predict CMI [17]. In addition, existing research has been further expanded in areas such as lncRNA-miRNA association prediction [18–21], CircRNA-disease association prediction [22–25]. Influenced by reductionism, these methods modularize the complete regulatory system to conduct localized studies, modeling and predicting specific singular associations. This approach effectively allows for a detailed analysis of molecular associations to gain deeper insights and is a key principle of bioinformatics.

However, modeling and feature extraction tasks based on single associations exhibit significant limitations. Primarily, using homogenous types of training and prediction data may result in the loss of features in downstream tasks. Given that biological networks are often constructed based on regulatory mechanisms, specific molecules may have relatively isolated regulatory relationships. Consequently, these molecules and their regulatory relationships may only be utilized for training or prediction, implying that network representation learning methods might fail to capture the features of these molecules or that the learned features may not apply to downstream tasks. To mitigate performance degradation due to feature loss, existing methods often significantly expand the training data size for feature extraction. However, increasing the labeled training data can propagate false negatives and label leakage, primarily because the features used for prediction might be learned based on assumptions about known labels. Moreover, due to the limitations of reductionism and network representation learning, modeling data of single association types restrict the predictive scope of existing techniques to homologous biomarkers present in the training data, thereby overlooking the discovery of unknown association types.

Given the limitations of single association modeling, efforts have been made to incorporate multiple types of prior molecular associations to enrich the associative information of heterogeneous or homogeneous networks [26–28]. Leveraging network embedding learning, specific large-scale biological network relation-capturing mechanisms have been devised to acquire embeddings representing the contextual neighborhood structure of nodes in the network, thereby inferring unknown links of target types [29]. On the other hand, common paradigms involve assigning initial representations to each node and creating graph-level representations by aggregating node attribute features within the neighborhood scope [30, 31]. Indeed, modeling biological molecular networks based on multi-type associations has demonstrated impressive performance in link prediction, propelling biomarker prediction research into a new and broader era. However, it is undeniable that existing studies still have certain limitations. The downstream target tasks of these studies still focus on predicting single specific associations, with little attention given to the study of unknown types of associations. This implies that the incorporation of multi-type associations is aimed at improving the sparsity of single-association network modeling, thus these prior molecular associations are only irregularly added to enrich the molecular network. This may impede their ability to fully understand the local and global interaction characteristics of molecules in the complete regulatory network, lacking targeted analysis and understanding.

On the other hand, computational methods for predicting biomarkers based on prior knowledge are often considered idealized candidate approaches, with practical utility in real-world scenarios proving challenging to substantiate. Existing studies typically validate the utility of models by comparing high-probability predictions with known databases or published research. This reverse-validation approach, based on existing research, faces criticism because the features learned by the model may be based on assumptions about known labels, presenting risks of label leakage. Additionally, straightforward comparisons with existing studies in databases largely depend on the randomness of dataset construction, making it difficult to achieve convincing results.

It is evident that the life activities of a cell, as a complete entity, are undoubtedly accomplished by comprehensive regulatory mechanisms. Therefore, modeling and feature extraction of the entire cell regulatory network have irreplaceable advantages. To address the aforementioned issues, we propose a novel multi-view learning framework, CeRVE, based on directed graphs neural networks of directed regulatory networks. CeRVE models the competitive endogenous RNA regulatory networks (CENA) of 72 cancer types at both graph and node levels to conduct predictions and validations of unknown biomarkers. CeRVE utilizes fused similarity views for each single association type in multi-view learning to gain local association insights of nodes. It then simulates molecular interaction mechanisms in CENA through directed signed graph neural networks, aggregating and propagating multi-view learned features as initial node features to obtain global graph-level node features for the inference of unknown biomarkers. Finally, CeRVE identifies various types of potential cancer biomarkers by differential expression analysis of predicted mRNA, miRNA, and lncRNA in three types of cancer compared to normal tissue. Materials and methods

Materials

Graph-level regulatory network modeling requires strict consistency in the types and numbers of chain regulatory mechanisms involved in a specific disease. In this study, we rigorously filtered data from multiple established databases to construct a comprehensive regulatory network dataset with high reference value.

The circRNA-miRNA-cancer regulatory network data are derived from the circR2Cancer database [32], which manually curated 1439 experimentally supported associations among 1135 circRNAs and 82 cancer types. Additionally, due to the competitive endogenous RNA nature of circRNAs, which competitively bind to miRNAs, the data also include miRNA-cancer associations (MCA). Following stringent filtering and de-duplication, we ultimately retained 72 cancer types within the circRNA-miRNA-cancer regulatory network, consisting of 648 circRNA-cancer associations (CCA), 753 CMI, and 731 MCA.

The lncRNA-miRNA-cancer regulatory network data are sourced from the lncRNAdisease3.0 database [33]. We downloaded the latest version of experimentally supported lncRNA-cancer association (LCA) data, from which we filtered those involving miRNA-mediated regulatory mechanisms, ultimately yielding 308 lncRNA-miRNA interactions (LMI), 732 MCA, and 1066 LCA.

The miRNA-target gene associations (MGA) are from the miRTarBase database [34], and we downloaded 1048 576 pairs of miRNA-target associations in the latest version of the miRTarBase database. We use miRNA as a reference for outlier removal and only select data with two or more experimental supports as the final reference. After data processing, we finally got 5330 pairs of miRNA-target associations.

The differential expression analysis data of three cancer and normal tissues used for predictive biomarker validation were derived from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [35].

We integrated the circRNA-miRNA-cancer network data, MGA, and lncRNA-miRNA-cancer network data to obtain a regulatory network encompassing 72 cancer types, 515 circRNAs, 568 miRNAs, 573 lncRNAs, and 2526 target genes. We designate the ceRNA regulatory network data utilized in our study as CENA 1.0, intended for reference and extension in subsequent research endeavors. Methods

The architecture of CeRVE is illustrated in Fig. 1. CeRVE modularly decomposes different associations in the regulatory network through a multi-view learning approach to acquire modular association insights of nodes. Subsequently, it constructs the graph-level representation of nodes by propagating and aggregating these insights through a directed graph neural networks (DGNN) that fits the regulatory mechanism, enabling support for downstream high-level tasks. It is worth noting that the target association type does not participate in feature engineering; thus, CeRVE has a unique advantage in predicting unknown types of associations.

Multi-view learning approach

The multi-view learning approach modularly decomposes and isolates all single associations in the regulatory network. Subsequently, Gaussian kernel similarity views and sigmoid similarity views are constructed separately for each association and organically integrated. Next, the fused similarity views are treated as single-channel grayscale feature representation graphs for feature extraction. It’s worth noting that molecules often exhibit a step-like distribution in similarity views. To address this, we construct a snake convolutional kernel by elongating traditional convolutional kernels from both x and y directions, making them more adaptable to the feature extraction task of similarity views. Finally, we integrate the homologous molecular similarity view features into a matrix input sparse autoencoder to obtain descriptors of local network insights for molecules.

In the prediction task of cancer marker miRNA, we segment the CENA network into five views: CMI, CCA, LMI, LCA, and MGA. For each independent view, we use an adjacency matrix to store molecular association information and calculate Gaussian kernel similarity and sigmoid similarity for the molecules. Directed signed graph neural network for fitting regulatory mechanisms

The signed directed relationships between nodes can reflect various relationships and semantics between different types of molecules. By analyzing different types of associations in the regulatory system, we construct different types of neighborhoods to simulate the regulatory association mechanism of molecules in CENA. This will contribute to enhancing the ability of graph-level modeling to understand the global interaction characteristics of molecules.

In the competitive endogenous RNA mechanism, given the objective law that the influence of biomarkers leads to the occurrence of diseases, all RNA molecules that cause diseases are established as a single positive association from RNA to disease.

Given the fact that competing endogenous RNAs compete for binding to the same response element, all interactions between RNA molecules were defined as bidirectional positive associations, and the same applies to negative samples.

Additionally, drawing from the application of balance theory in biological networks [15], the associations between nodes are represented using recursive chain associations. Simply put, in regulatory relationships of length L, regulatory chains with an even number of negative associations are ultimately represented as positive links, while those with an odd number of negative associations are ultimately represented as negative associations (We assume that the association between molecules has a conductive nature, that is, molecules that have an association relationship with the target molecule may have similar properties, and vice versa). We can recursively define regulatory chains based on this principle.

Based on the aforementioned principles, all types of regulatory chains can ultimately be simplified into four distinct sets of neighborhoods.

Results

Cancer marker prediction

To evaluate the effectiveness of CeRVE in predicting cancer biomarkers, we performed prediction tasks on 72 cancer-related miRNAs, circRNAs, and lncRNAs.

Thanks to the construction of CENA, two different training methods were provided for cancer biomarker prediction. Method one (ME1) involved pre-training the classifier with all known associations outside the target association, which was then used for predicting unknown types of biomarkers. This means that through ME1, CeRVE could predict unknown types of biomarkers. Method two (ME2) involved feature extraction of all known associations outside the target association using CeRVE’s feature engineering framework. However, ME2 used 5-fold cross-validation with the target association, meaning that some associations of the same type were used for classifier training. The results shown in Fig. 2 demonstrate that the effectiveness of feature extraction is influenced by the different aggregation methods used. In the MCA prediction task, the propagation method with two layers of attention aggregation achieved the best predictive performance, with AUC and AUPR values of 78.69 and 75.63%, respectively. In the CCA prediction task, the two-layer aggregation method outperformed the single-layer aggregation, with the performance of attention_mean aggregation slightly better than other methods. In the LCA prediction task, the attention_attention aggregation method yielded the best predictive results, significantly outperforming other methods. In general, using different aggregation methods for feature extraction provides a variety of options for adapting to different task environments. Attention aggregation is suitable for tasks that require dynamic adjustment of neighbor node weights and can take into account the importance of neighbor nodes when aggregating features. Mean aggregation is suitable for tasks that require smooth features or high stability, and its calculation is simple and robust. Therefore, the flexible combination of multiple aggregation methods can ensure that CeRVE can effectively extract features for various independent data sets, thereby enhancing generalization and robustness.

Validity evaluation of the multi-view learning approach

CeRVE utilizes a multi-view learning approach to gain insights into the local interactions of molecules within regulatory networks, incorporating these insights as initial features for propagation in graph neural networks. To validate the effectiveness of this method, we conducted ablation experiments. Specifically, we used molecular similarity topologies and randomly generated molecular feature representations (non) as initial features. Comparison with state-of-the-art models

Currently, existing research mainly focuses on the modeling and prediction of single associations, with limited studies referencing modeling and prediction methods based on regulatory networks. Despite this, we still employ an approach of extending models for comparison to emphasize the superior performance of CeRVE. Specifically, we expand on the main ideas and methods of state-of-the-art (SOTA) models within the CeRVE framework to construct different prediction models and compare them in the context of predicting cancer-related circRNA biomarkers.

We selected representative models for extension, with the WSCD [37] using network embedding-based structural deep network embedding (SDNE) [38] to capture the structural embedding representations of nodes in the network; the JSNDCMI [36] employing a multi-feature extraction framework to extract features from molecular topological and functional similarities in the network; the SGCNCMI [39] combining graph convolutional neural networks [40] to aggregate node attribute features; and the KS-CMI [15] utilizing signed graph convolutional neural networks for graph-level modeling in a tripartite heterogeneous network to predict unknown associations. We extended the main modeling methods used by these SOTA models into the CeRVE framework, primarily achieved by replacing the directed symbolic graph neural network module. For methods that cannot propagate molecular attribute features, we solely utilized a single type of feature for ME2 training and prediction tasks. The comparison results of different models are shown in Fig. 4.

The results in Fig. 4 demonstrate that CeRVE achieved the best predictive performance across all prediction models. In all prediction models, Ce-GCN and Ce-SGCN utilizing graph convolutional neural networks and signed graph convolutional neural networks, respectively, obtained the second and third-best predictive performance, showcasing the advantages of graph-level modeling in network feature extraction. Case study on cancer marker prediction

In existing studies, the utility of models is typically validated by checking the high-probability candidates predicted by the model against public research or databases. This reverse inference-based case study overlooks the overlap between the data labels used in model training and the existing research conclusions. Thus, the method resembles more of a test of feature extraction effectiveness rather than a proof of the utility of unknown biomarkers.

In this study, we aim to combine gene expression analysis of disease tissues with existing research to validate the ability of the model to predict potential cancer biomarkers. Our approach involves conducting gene expression analysis of CeRVE’s prediction results in actual cases to determine if CeRVE can provide candidate biomarkers with significant expression differences, and then comparing and checking these biomarkers against existing studies to ultimately validate the practicality of CeRVE.

We conducted model predictions for biomarkers related to bladder cancer, breast cancer, and cervical cancer, focusing on genes, miRNAs, and lncRNAs. From these predictions, we selected the most promising candidates for differential expression analysis. Notably, the CeRVE training dataset did not include target-type training data to avoid potential label leakage. Specifically, we obtained gene expression RNA-seq and miRNA expression quantification data for bladder cancer, breast cancer, and cervical cancer from TCGA. We performed differential expression analysis of the candidate genes in adjacent normal tissues versus cancer tissues to determine whether these candidates exhibit statistically significant differences between sample types. We used t-tests to calculate the statistical significance P-values between normal and cancer tissues, considering P < 0.05 as indicative of a significant statistical difference. Additionally, a case study on bladder cancer was used to generate heatmaps of candidate gene expression across different samples to observe expression differences. Finally, we cross-checked our analysis results with existing research and databases to identify biomarkers for different cancer types. The boxplots for gene differential expression analysis in bladder cancer and the gene expression heatmaps are shown in Fig. 5. Discussion

The rapid and cost-effective prediction of potential disease biomarkers through computational methods has gradually become a necessary means to provide pre-selection for wet experiments. However, limited by reductionism and network representation methods, existing studies tend to focus more on modeling and predicting tasks associated with single associations, thereby overlooking the discovery of unknown types of associations. Furthermore, current research typically validates the practical performance of models by overlapping prediction results with existing studies, a reverse inference pattern that is highly susceptible to label leakage during the feature propagation process and lacks convincing validation of real cases.

To address the existing challenges, we propose an efficient cancer biomarker prediction framework called CeRVE. The main idea of CeRVE is to gain modular insights into the regulatory network and network-wide propagation insights through multi-view learning methods and DGNN. Specifically, the multi-view learning method constructs two similarity views based on each single association and learns the representation of molecular interactions, representing the local interaction mechanism of molecules. Subsequently, the learned representations serve as the initial features to propagate in the DGNN fitting the complete regulatory network, to obtain a comprehensive understanding of the entire network. By extracting local and global features of the regulatory network, CeRVE is able to balance in-depth understanding of single associations and network-wide propagation of the regulatory network. Thus, it obtains representative feature descriptors of molecules to predict unknown types of cancer biomarkers. CeRVE demonstrates competitive performance in three cancer biomarker prediction tasks, representing a successful attempt at biomarker prediction from the perspective of modeling and analyzing networks from the regulatory network.

To validate the practicality of the prediction methods, we initially downloaded tissue and adjacent tissue samples for three types of cancers from the TCGA database and performed differential expression analysis for the three types of biomarkers. Additionally, we constructed gene expression heatmaps for bladder cancer (BLAD) and validated the practical utility of the predictions through case studies. Subsequently, we further validated the accuracy of the results by cross-referencing them with existing research and literature. CeRVE yielded promising results in the expression analysis of 60 biomarkers, with 38 biomarkers showing significant differential expression between cancer and adjacent tissues. Moreover, although some predicted results did not exhibit significant differences in differential expression analysis, they were still supported by existing literature. This suggests that CeRVE’s predictions are practically reliable and have the advantage of mitigating the impact of individual variability.

While CeRVE has shown promising results in predicting cancer biomarkers, there are still areas for improvement. On one hand, although CeRVE effectively predicts unknown cancer biomarkers, its predictive performance is limited due to the absence of same-type data for prediction in the training dataset, resulting in a current performance of ~80%. We believe that the incorporation of multi-source information, particularly molecular attribute features such as RNA nucleotide sequence representation and disease semantic representation, may effectively enhance the model’s predictive performance. Furthermore, for large-scale regulatory networks, collecting attribute features for all molecules is challenging; thus, employing rational incomplete feature embedding methods or adopting a unified feature generation approach may be potential solutions.

On the other hand, in this study, we used randomly generated negative samples as training data, which may lead to the propagation of false negative samples, where some undiscovered positive samples are mistakenly treated as negative samples for training, thereby impacting the model’s predictive performance. We look forward to the efforts of related researchers and our continued refinement of data to address this issue. Additionally, we aim to incorporate more comprehensive data into existing association networks to construct a more complete regulatory network, further facilitating the discovery of unknown disease biomarkers. In future work, we will innovate targeted solutions within these limitations to seek breakthroughs.

In conclusion, we have reason to believe that our proposed framework can provide a reliable tool and valuable insights for cancer biomarker prediction research, thereby accelerating the progress of artificial intelligence-assisted biomarker discovery.
