The impact of propolis-silver nanoparticles coated sutures versus conventional sutures on early intraoral wound healing in healthy and diabetic rats: a preliminary histological study
Authors: Hawra A. Aljanobi, Muneer Alshuyukh, Hassan Ghonaim, Mohammed Alhulami, Zahra Aldawood, Ahmed Alsubaie, Hassan Alramadan, Ali Alkhalaf, Intisar Siddiqui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Optimal wound healing is crucial in oral surgery, particularly in compromised conditions. Diabetes mellitus is associated with impaired wound healing due to reduced vascularization, immune dysfunction, and altered collagen deposition. Propolis, a bioactive resin produced by honeybees, has demonstrated antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and pro-healing properties. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of propolis-silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) coated sutures on early wound healing in the oral mucosa of healthy and diabetic rats through histological assessments. Propolis-silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) were synthesized and examined using FTIR. The propolis-AgNPs then used to coat polyglycolic acid (PGA) sutures. Before using the sutures in the experimental surgery, sutures Tensile Strength and SEM Analysis were performed. 12 Sprague–Dawley female rats (6 healthy, 6 diabetic) were used to create bilateral buccal mucosa punch incisions, with one side sutured using the propolis-AgNPs-coated PGA and the other with non-coated PGA sutures as a control. Wound healing was assessed after seven days through histological analysis using hematoxylin & eosin and Masson’s trichrome staining. Data were analyzed using Chi-square, Fisher’s exact test, and Welch’s t test (p ≤ 0.05 considered significant). In healthy rats, propolis-coated sutures significantly improved collagen fiber organization and mature collagen deposition (p = 0.030). Although diabetic rats did not show statistically significant differences (p > 0.05), a trend toward enhanced collagen fiber alignment and mature collagen formation was observed. A slight reduction in blood vessel formation was noted in both groups. Propolis-AgNPs-coated sutures demonstrated enhanced collagen organization in healthy rats and showed potential for improving wound healing in diabetic models.

Introduction

Over the past three decades, the prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM) has risen significantly within the global population (“World Health Organization. Diabetes,” n.d.). Diabetes is a chronic condition marked by elevated blood glucose levels, which can result from either the destruction of insulin-producing cells (DM-1) or a reduced cellular sensitivity to insulin (DM-2) (“World Health Organization. Diabetes,” n.d.). In terms of post-surgical healing, individuals with diabetes may experience disruptions in the normal healing process due to decreased blood vascularization, weakened immune responses, impaired release of growth factors, and, most notably, changes in salivary components (Abiko and Selimovic 2010).

Many studies have been conducted to evaluate the wound healing in the oral cavity of DM patients. One study compared the extraction socket healing between non-diabetic, prediabetic, and type-2 diabetic patients and concluded that the diabetic group had a wider socket size with more complications, including swelling and infection. In another study, the number of blood vessels in diabetic rats was evaluated after inducing buccal incisions and found that the blood vessels were much lower and more permeable than the control group (Brizeno et al. 2016). The connective tissue is shown to be affected negatively by DM as well, reducing both the number of fibroblasts and the expression of pro-collagen (Liu et al. 2004).

According to the complicated wound healing in such patients, science is regularly required to develop or find new promising substances. Apitherapy is the practice of utilizing bee products to prevent or treat different illnesses (Weis et al. 2022). Propolis is considered one of the most recognized products in this field, it is a naturally produced resin derived from honeybees which originally collected from various plants and mixed with bees’ secretions (Vagish Kumar 2014). Several components are present in propolis based on the geographical zone from where it has been taken (Vagish Kumar 2014). However, its biological activity is mainly related to flavonoids and hydroxycinnamic acid (Khurshid et al. 2017).

It has been reported that propolis possesses a remarkable anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antiviral, and antibacterial effect and capacity of immunomodulatory (Altan et al. 2013; Mujica et al. 2019). Various studies have examined the wound healing effect of propolis in diabetic rats; unsurprisingly, there was a direct correlation between the rate of healing and the presence of propolis (Ahmed et al. 2011; McLennan et al. 2008; Patel et al. 2020). The positive influence of propolis extends to affect bone healing and remodeling (Ekeuku and Chin 2021). In vivo experiments found higher new bone synthesis and mineral bone density in propolis-loaded dental implant compared to the regular implant (Somsanith et al. 2018). This could be due to the role of propolis in reducing inflammation cytokines and promoting osteogenic differentiation proteins, including BMP-2 and 7 (Somsanith et al. 2018). Interestingly, propolis may also help reduce the incidence of dry socket and postoperative pain after impacted wisdoms extraction in healthy individuals (González-Serrano et al. 2021). However, similar to any other pharmaceutical drug, intraoral application of propolis may cause burning and allergic reactions in some people (Fernandez et al. 2004; Wimardhani and Soegyanto 2014).

Silver has been used for centuries due to its potent antimicrobial properties (Rai et al. 2009). Silver nanoparticles, in particular, have gained popularity because of their ability to effectively inhibit bacterial growth. These nanoparticles have been applied widely in medical and dental fields, through different applications such as wound dressing, antimicrobial coating, and various dental applications due to their high surface area and reactivity. (Bruna et al. 2021; Mallineni et al. 2023). Several studies have been conducted by combining different bioactive materials with silver nanoparticles incorporated into surgical sutures to improve their antimicrobial and healing properties (BAYGAR 2020; Baygar et al. 2024; Selvaraju et al. 2022).

Accordingly, using surgical sutures coated with propolis silver nanoparticles formulations in a diabetic animal wound model is a crucial step in evaluating their efficacy before potential human application, particularly because diabetes impairs the healing process. Although propolis combined with silver nanoparticles has been investigated in various laboratory and animal studies, its potential in promoting oral mucosal healing under diabetic conditions has not yet been assessed.

The integration of propolis particles in surgical sutures matches the single-use application of the pharmaceutical industry. Such material may improve the drawbacks of healing in uncontrolled diabetes, offering good healing boosters, inflammation reduction, and postoperative analgesia. Thus, this experimental study examines the effects of propolis-silver nanoparticles coated sutures on the early healing of oral mucosa in healthy and diabetic rats using histological assessments.

This study hypothesized that propolis-silver nanoparticles coated sutures would enhance early oral mucosal wound healing compared with conventional sutures in both healthy and diabetic rats.

Materials and methods

This is an experimental study with a study protocol approved by Imam Abdulrahman bin Faisal University Institutional Review Board (IRB-2022-02-319). All experiments with animals were performed according to the recommendations of the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health.

Propolis ethanolic extract

Propolis extraction process was followed as Elkhateeb et al. proposed (Elkhateeb et al. 2022). Where 60 g of Southeast Asian Propolis powder (Bee Propolis, Imtenan shop; Egypt) dissolved in 690 mL of 70% ethanol (Ethanol 96%, Honeywell; US). The solution was sonicated for three hours at room temperature using a probe sonicator, followed by filtration using cotton wool. Then, the mixture undergoes rotary evaporation at a temperature of 40 –50 °C, pressure of 650 mmHg, and speed of 80 rpm. Finally, lyophilization was performed using a Freeze Dryer (FreeZone, Labconco, US) for two days and stored in the refrigerator. The formed product was dark brown and viscous at this stage.

Preparation of propolis-silver nanoformulation

Silver nanoparticles were prepared using ethanolic propolis extract as the reducing and stabilizing agent. Briefly, 5 mg of silver nitrate was dissolved in 50 mL of Milli-Q water to obtain a 1000 ppm solution. Separately, 5 g of ethanolic propolis extract was dissolved in 500 mL of distilled water, and the pH was adjusted using NaOH. Subsequently, 500 mL of 50 mM AgNO3 solution was added to the propolis solution and the mixture was stirred continuously at room temperature for 4 h at 120 rpm using a magnetic stirrer.

The formation of silver nanoparticles was indicated by a color change from light brown to dark brown. The resulting suspension was then incubated overnight in a shaker under dark conditions. On the following day, the mixture was exposed to sunlight to enhance photocatalytic nanoparticles formation. The synthesized suspension was then centrifuged at 7000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C using a (Sigma 3-30KS, Germany) centrifuge. The pellet was collected, frozen, and lyophilized to obtain purified propolis-silver nanoparticles for further characterization and biological evaluation (Selvaraju et al. 2022).

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometry analysis

FTIR test is an easy and non-destructive method to identify the main chemical functional groups, and the new bonds created after propolis-AgNPs nanoformulation. The solution was tested on a range of 4000–400 cm − 1, and a resolution of 4 cm − 1 using an FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) (Fig. 1).

Suture coating of propolis-silver nanoparticles (AgNPs)

Braided 6 − 0 Polyglycolic acid sutures (Truglyde, Healthium; India) were coated via slurry dipping technique using sodium alginate as an immobilizing agent (Selvaraju et al. 2022). 10 mL of sodium alginate (2% w/v) and 10 mL of sodium chloride (0.1% w/v) were mixed and stirred continuously in a magnetic stirrer at room temperature for eight hours. Then, for the creation of an alginate colloid, propolis-AgNPs (100 mg/ml) were added and stirred for 30 min.

In the prepared propolis-AgNPs solution, the surgical sutures were completely immersed for 10 min, then air dried for 5 min. This procedure was repeated ten times for each suture, followed by immersion in 4% calcium chloride, contributing to alginate cross-linking. Eventually, the sutures were washed with double distilled water and kept in a cool-dry environment.

Tensile strength

The tensile strength assessment was conducted on 12 sutures measuring 12 cm in length, with six Propolis coated Polyglycolic acid (PGA) and six non-coated PGA sutures. The Universal Testing machine (Instron 5965, Instron crop, US) was employed to measure the tensile strength (Straight-Pull Test). Each suture sample was carefully positioned and stretched between two arms with zero force. The tensile strength assessment was conducted at a 20–25 cm/min cross-head speed. Each suture specimen was stretched until failure, and the maximum load required to cause failure was recorded in Newtons (N) (Khiste et al. 2013).

Characterization of the propolis-AgNPs coated sutures

SEM analysis

The propolis-AgNPs coated suture was mounted on a metal stub and sputtered with a thin layer of gold under vacuum using a sputter coater (Cressington 108 auto, Ted pella INC; US) and scanned under electron microscope (JEOL JSM-6390 LA, Japan) (Fig. 2).

Releasing test

100 mg of standard alcoholic extract of propolis powder was weighed accurately and dissolved in 10 ml of methanol in a volumetric flask then sonicated for 20 min to get a stock clear solution, then diluted up to 100 ml to get 1000 µg/ml concentration of the standard stock solution. From this stock solution, 10 ml was diluted to 100 ml in a volumetric flask to get 100 µg/ml of drug concentration. Then, using this stock solution for further serial dilutions, six different concentrations were prepared 0.003–0.06 µg/ml. The average absorbance values of these concentrations were measured at 274 nm using a UV spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan) (Fig. 3a). The standard graph was plotted by taking absorbance values on Y-axis and concentration values on X-axis, R2 = 0.9989.

Three sutures were cut into 2 cm in length and placed in separate beakers filled with 10 mL of Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and covered with paraffin film. The beakers were placed in a thermomixer at 37∘C, and 25 rpm. After 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, 2 mL was withdrawn from each beaker and replaced by new PBS. At the mentioned time points, the amount of released propolis was measured by UV spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan) at wavelength 274 nm (Fig. 3b), and the corresponding concentration was calculated using the equation from the standard calibration curve (De La Harpe et al. 2022).

Animals preparation and diabetes indication

A total of 12 Sprague–Dawley female rats, (6–8)-week-old, weighted (200–230 g) were divided into two groups (healthy rats N = 6, diabetic rats N = 6). Each group was subdivided based on the suture type used to close the wounds (coated and non-coated) (Fig. 4). Each rat was caged alone, at 24 °C ± 1 °C and 55% ± 5% humidity, with a 12-hr light-dark cycle. The rats had free access to the standard laboratory food and water. Rats were allowed to acclimate to this environment for one week before induction of diabetes.

Fig. 4Diagram of the study design. Created in BioRender. Alkhalaf, A. (2026) https://BioRender.com/y934094

Diagram of the study design. Created in BioRender. Alkhalaf, A. (2026) https://BioRender.com/y934094

Diabetes was induced per the protocol proposed by Furman et al.(2021), where the rats were restricted from food for 6–8 h before injecting them with a single dose of intraperitoneal Streptozotocin (65 mg/kg). After the injection, the animals were fed ad libitum and regular water. The rats’ fasting blood glucose level was assessed via glucometer (OnCall Sharp, OnCall; US) by punching the tail. A fasting blood glucose above > 250 mg/dl after 48 h was considered as a border line to include the rats in this study. Surgical intervention was performed after the confirmation of diabetes induction.

Buccal mucosa injury and treatment protocol

Prior to the surgical procedure, animals were anesthetized with intraperitoneal ketamine (80–100 mg/kg) and xylazine (5–10 mg/kg). Each surgical procedure required approximately 20 min per rat. During surgery, each animal was placed on a pre-heated surgical table, and the oral cavity was opened using a small retractor or surgical sutures placed between the incisors. Bilateral punch incisions (4 mm in diameter and up to 2 mm in depth) were created on the buccal mucosa using a punch-biopsy technique, as previously described in similar experimental wound-healing studies (Rahmadhini and Wahyuni, 2023). The right-sided wound was approximated using propolis coated PGA sutures in a simple-interrupted-stitch fashion, while the contralateral wound was closed using PGA regular (non-coated) suture (Fig. 5).

Post-surgical animals care and sample collection

Immediately after the surgery, all rats were injected with an SC nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory analgesic Flunixin meglumine (2.5 mg/kg) for three days. The diabetic group received an additional antibiotic SC Enrofloxacin (mg/kg) for three days. The animals were allowed to feed standard laboratory food, with an option of soft food aside. The rats were allowed to heal and euthanized after 7 days by placing them in a CO2 chamber. Following euthanasia, the tissues were collected using 8 mm punch biopsy method and were placed in 10% formalin.

Histopathological assessment

The 24 samples underwent hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s Trichrome staining according to standard protocols. Histological sections were independently examined and evaluated by two oral and maxillofacial pathologists using calibrated methods (Fig. 4).

A modified semi-quantitative protocol based on Sultana et al.(2009) was employed for histological evaluation. Six histological parameters were assessed using a three-point scoring system (1, 2, and 3) (Table 1) (Fig. 6) (Fig. 7).

Data analysis

All statistical data were stored using SPSS version-29 (IBM product, Chicago-USA). The wound healing data in terms of categorical assessment were presented into frequencies and percentages; and the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was applied to compare these findings between non-coated and coated materials in healthy and diabetic cases. Tensile strength between coated versus non-coated group was analyzed using Welch’s t test. Calibration between two assessors was performed by using Wilcoxon sign-rank test. Cohen’s kappa statistics were calculated for the evaluation of inter-examiner consistency and validity. P-value ≤ 0.05 was considered a statistically significant result.

The coated material was significantly effective in wound healing of the healthy cases with regard to the pattern of collagen (p = 0.030) and the amount of mature collagen (p = 0.030). A non-significant difference between non-coated versus coated materials was observed in healthy cases with regards to the amount of blood (p = 0.135), amount of inflammation (p = 0.50), amount of early collagen (p = 0.999), and type of inflammation (p = 0.999). However, a trend was observed towards reduced blood vessel formation in the coated group compared to the non-coated group in healthy cases (p = 0.135), as well as in diabetic cases (p = 0.135). Most of the cases in the diabetic group had a moderate state of wound inflammation associated with coated and non-coated material (0.455). However, in the healthy group, the profound state of inflammation was substantially higher than moderate or scanty inflammation associated with coated versus non-coated materials (p = 0.500). In diabetic cases, although none of the assessed parameters was significant between non-coated and coated materials (p > 0.05), better collagen fiber orientation and deposition of more mature collagen was observed in the coated group when compared to the non-coated group as presented in (Table 2).

Discussion

The therapeutic potential of topical propolis in wound healing is well-supported by literature, highlighting its ability to induce substantial wound contraction, ulcer healing, and reduce pain through its antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and pro-angiogenic activities, particularly in diabetic patients (Afkhamizadeh et al. 2018; Javadzadeh Bolouri et al. 2015; Rocha et al. 2024).In this context, we investigated the use of propolis-coated sutures to enhance wound healing in both healthy and diabetic rat models. Our findings generally demonstrate a positive impact of propolis-coated sutures on both clinical and histological aspects of wound healing. The null hypothesis related to no effect was rejected, and the alternate hypothetical statement that propolis-coated material has a positive effect on wound healing in healthy and diabetic subjects was accepted.

In the healthy rat model, we observed a statistically significant improvement in both the pattern and amount of mature collagen in the wounds closed with the propolis-coated suture compared to the non-coated suture. Specifically, the coated-suture group exhibited a more organized collagen fiber orientation and a greater deposition of mature collagen. While the improvement in the diabetic rat model did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the inherent challenges of diabetic wound healing, we still observed a trend towards better collagen fiber orientation and increased mature collagen deposition in the coated group compared to the non-coated group. This suggests that the propolis coating may promote wound healing by enhancing the formation of a well-organized and mature collagen matrix, a critical step in effective tissue regeneration. A well-structured collagen matrix provides structural support and facilitates the migration of fibroblasts, crucial for wound closure and strength (Mathew-Steiner et al. 2021; San Antonio et al. 2020). The observed improvements in collagen organization and maturation in the coated groups, even in the diabetic model, suggest a potential benefit of propolis in promoting this essential aspect of wound repair. This finding is in alignment with other studies done to observe the effect of propolis on wound healing (Akpınar and Özdemir, 2023; El-Sakhawy et al. 2023).

Interestingly, while not statistically significant, a trend towards reduced vascularity was observed in both healthy and diabetic groups treated with the coated suture, which reflects an advanced stage of healing. A study done by Puspasari et al., demonstrated that topical propolis application increased fibroblast growth factor-2 (FGF-2) expression and fibroblast proliferation, both of which are critical regulators of angiogenesis. This may indicate that propolis could potentially modulate angiogenesis, a process also crucial for wound healing (Kareva et al. 2016; Marchand et al. 2019; Puspasari et al. 2018). Further research is needed to fully elucidate the mechanisms by which propolis exerts these effects on collagen deposition and maturation, as well as its influence on angiogenesis, to determine the clinical implications of these findings.

Conclusions

Overall, this study provides preliminary evidence that the propolis-coated material may have positive effects on wound healing in healthy and diabetic rats by promoting collagen deposition and potentially modulating angiogenesis. Further research with larger sample sizes and human clinical trials is necessary to fully evaluate the clinical potential of this material in promoting wound healing, particularly in diabetic patients.
