Intelligent performance prediction of nanoparticle-enhanced automotive radiator cooling using CFD and machine learning
Authors: Jitendra B Satpute, Dilip Kumar Jang Bahadur Saini, Pooja Sharma, Bipin Kumar Rai, Pravin M Thorat, Gautam Kumar, Kumar Dilip
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractActive thermal control of automotive engines is vital for improving performance, robustness and conventional coolants like water and air frequently bring insufficient heat dissipation. To counterpart this ill effect, the propose study examines and investigate the performance enrichment of an automotive radiator using Al₂O₃–ethylene glycol (EG) nanofluids concluded by collective numerical, experimental, and data-driven approach. Initially, three-dimensional radiator model was developed using AUTOCAD software and computational study was performed in ANSYS-FLUENT, using a pressure-based model and a realizable k–ε turbulence model. The Grid-independence test was directed to ensure numerical accuracy, and CFD predictions were experimentally validated through experimental radiator test rig. The CFD result for pure EG predicted an outlet temperature of 70.11 °C that was closely agreed with the experimental value of 69.11 °C confirming the consistency of the numerical model. The blend of Al₂O₃ nanoparticles significantly enhanced heat transfer characteristics, whereas growing nanoparticle blend leads to progressive reductions in outlet temperature. At 2% concentration, the outlet temperature reduced to 65.51 °C, while the maximum cooling performance was achieved at 5% concentration, yielding an outlet temperature of 60.87 °C. Although the pressure drop amplified moderately with nanoparticle loading—from 36.79 Pa at 1% to 38.72 Pa at 5%, the rise remained relatively small, representing a constructive trade-off between thermal enhancement and hydraulic disadvantage. The highest PEC value of 1.83 was obtained at 5% Al₂O₃ concentration, demonstrating that the enhancement in heat transfer outweighs the associated pressure drop penalty. In addition, a machine learning based predictive framework has been proposed in the present work. This outline has been developed to evaluate the radiator outlet temperature and pressure drop using nanoparticle concentration and functioning settings that helped in rapid performance prediction and parametric optimization. Overall, the combined CFD, experimental, and data-driven findings shows that Al₂O₃–EG nanofluids are a capable coolant choice for high-performance radiator cooling applications.Keywords: Al₂O₃, Blending, Ethylene glycol, Engine cooling system, Pressure dropSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing, Nanoscience and technology, Physics

Introduction

The engine cooling arrangement is crucial for overall engine efficiency, and the radiator is a key part that regulates thermal performance1. The study2 shows significant research on using nanofluids to advance radiator heat removal. In the previous research, researchers have explored various nanoparticles, such as Al₂O₃, CuO, TiO₂, SiO₂, and ZnO, blended with multiple liquid coolant and analyze cooling extent. They studied regarding particle concentration, inlet-outlet temperature variation, and thermal behavior, and reported notable improvements in heat transfer, with some studies shown heat transfer enhancements up to 30%. Research on hybrid nanofluids has pushed this field further. For example, the MWCNT + Al₂O₃ hybrid nanofluids made with 0.05 vol% MWCNT and 0.05 vol% Al₂O₃ in distilled water was tested from 50 to 70 °C3. The outcomes showed that higher temperatures greatly improved thermal conductivity (K) and specific heat capacity (Cp), whereas viscosity and density reduced. Similarly, the investigation4 looked at turbulent convective heat transfer performance of γ-Al₂O₃+water coolant in an engine cooling channel modeled as a cast iron rectangular shaped duct. The γ-Al₂O₃ nanoparticles, ranging from 21 to 37 nm, achieved a maximum heat transfer coefficient (HTC) improvement of 78.67% at a 1% blending compared to pure water. An investigation5 suggested water+ nanofluids + PCM for heat transfer enhancement applications. When PCM was encompassed, the enhancement rose to 39%. At a higher TiO₂ concentration of 0.16%, thermal conductivity improved by 65%, with PCM providing extra benefits. Furthermore6, evaluated the thermal degradation characteristics of Al₂O₃/TiO₂ hybrid nano-lubricants using TGA/DTG/DSC analysis and studied engine thermal performance with an AVL dynamometer. The hybrid nano-lubricants showed better thermal stability, delaying oxidation onset temperatures by 54.9 °C. The effect of nanoparticle blending on cooling system performance has also been widely investigated7. Increasing particle concentration consistently improved heat transfer, with an enhancement from 0% to 1% volume fraction leading to about a 15% improvement. However, this improvement came with an increased pressure drop as a counter-effect; a 1% volume fraction was resulted in an additional pressure loss of around 0.5 bar, indicating a need for more pumping power. A CFD analysis using SolidWorks 2016 and Flow Simulation software8 confirmed the better thermal performance of nanofluids in an aluminum radiator. With an inlet temperature of 90 °C and ambient conditions of 25 °C, nanofluids outperformed water. Increases in thermal conductivity were seen with TiO₂, CuO, and Al₂O₃ nanofluids at 25%, 40%, and 35%, respectively. The temperature drop along the radiator tube was 10 °C for water and 15 °C, 18 °C, and 17 °C for TiO₂, CuO, and Al₂O₃ nanofluids, respectively.

Recently, the effect of Al2O3–SiO2–titanium dioxide (TiO2) nanoparticles on engine cooling was investigated36. The optimum blending fraction was established by incorporating artificial intelligence-based model. The ideal operative blend of Al2O3, SiO2 and TiO2 was found from machine learning (ML) algorithms, and resulted in an augmentation of about 33% in active environments, with 0.24% Al2O3, 0.26% SiO2 and 0.25% TiO2 nanoparticle blending. The comprehensive thermal regulation of the radiator with composite fin was successfully analysed by Ramesh Kumar et al.37. They inspected velocity pathway, pressure variances, temperature profile in the radiator and result showed that nanoparticles and fins caused improved heat transfer and provided effective 12% improvement in cooling. The hybrid blend of graphene nanoplatelets (GnP), cellulose nanocrystals (CNC) nanoparticles was studied for heat transfer application38. The result recommended GNP/CNC hybrid nanofluid improved the efficiency of heat transfer of radiator as convective heat transfer coefficient improved by 51.91% while overall heat transfer coefficient by 46.72%. it was also important to see that pressure drop increased by 34.06% compared to water.

The Aluminum oxide (Al2O3)-based nanofluid at varying volume concentrations was studied39 with water and ethylene glycol. The coolant was investigated at different inlet temperatures ranging 40–60 °C and flow rates 2 L/min- 6 L/min and radiator fan running at 1500 rpm. At the 0.08% volume fraction, nanofluid delivered significant augmentation in the heat transfer charactristics. An engine cooling system was investigated40 using Ethylene Glycol (EG)+copper nano-particles based nanofluid. the numerical analysis and experimental study for validation was performed to examine the effect on engine cooling and pressure drop. It was seen that the highest cooling of 19.3 °C was observed at a 5% concentration of Cu nanoparticles while the least cooling at 10 °C degree was observed with Ethylene glycol coolant.

Currently, machine learning (ML) approaches have gained attention in thermal and energy systems for their ability to predict system performance with reduced computational cost. ML-based surrogate models have been successfully applied to heat transfer enhancement, nanofluid property prediction, and engine cooling performance assessment. These methods complement CFD simulations by enabling rapid parametric studies and optimization while preserving acceptable accuracy26–30.

The nanoparticle volume fraction plays a crucial role in enhancing heat transfer in automotive radiator cooling systems; however, increasing concentration also leads to a rise in pressure drop due to higher flow resistance. Though abundant studies have established the usefulness of nanofluids in enlightening radiator thermal performance, numerous key research gaps remain untouched. Most presented experimental investigations emphasis mainly on water-based nanofluids, while comparatively limited studies reported ethylene glycol–based nanofluids, despite ethylene glycol being the primary coolant used in modern automobiles industries. Additionally, many studies emphasize thermal enhancement alone and incompetently compute the trade-off between improved heat transfer and corresponding pumping power. Organized hydro-thermal performance assessment and optimization of nanoparticle concentration are rarely reported in the literature.

In this context, Al₂O₃–EG based nanofluids arise as a capable advanced coolant for automotive sector. The current study focuses these gaps through a combined numerical and experimental investigation using Al₂O₃–EG nanofluids at varying concentrations. The novelty of this study lies in the integrated assessment of thermal and hydraulic performance using hydro-thermal efficiency and performance evaluation criteria, enabling identification of an optimal nanoparticle concentration that maximizes cooling performance while minimizing pressure-drop penalties. The present study proposes numerical and real time outdoor examination on the hydro-thermal performance of an radiator using Al₂O₃–EG nanofluids (Tables 1 and 2).

Experimental works

The experimental set up is indigenously fabricated to vaalidate cooling performance of EG-Al2O3 nanofluids. The experimental test rig shown in Fig. 4b comprises of an automotive radiator assembly mounted on a rigid supporting frame, along with a coolant, circulation pump, pipelines, and cooling fan. The complete setup is proposed as a closed-loop system to ensure controlled operating conditions. The radiator core is rectangular in shape and fabricated from copper tubes with fins. An axial cooling fan is mounted directly behind the radiator to provide forced convection heat transfer. The fan is centrally aligned with the radiator core to confirm unchanging airflow circulation.

(a) Schematic view of experimental Test bench. (b) Pictorial view of Test bench.

The coolant reservoir is typically cylindrical shaped tank with a capacity of 50 L. A centrifugal pump of 50 W power rating is submerged inside the tank to circulate the coolant through the radiator. Flexible piping of 12 mm inner diameter are used to connect the reservoir, pump, flow meter, and radiator, ensuring leak-free operation and ease of maintenance. The entire assembly is supported on a rectangular mild steel frame of dimensions 600 mm × 400 mm × 700 mm (height). The experimental setup consists of a nanofluids storage tank that stores the working fluid, with a centrigugal pump placed inside to pump the fluid is recirculated. From the storage tank, the coolant fluid passes to the radiator inlet. The inlet and outlet temperature of coolant is measured with digital thermocouple. The radiator aids as the primary heat exchanger, where the circulating coolant releases heat to the surrounding air. An axial cooling fan mounted behind the radiator as shown in Fig. 4 that delivers forced airflow to enhance convective cooling. After heat dissipation, the fluid exits the radiator and returns to the reservoir to complete the cycle. The Digital thermocouples sensors are installed at the radiator inlet and outlet to record temperature. A pressure gauge is connected diagonally to the radiator to determine the pressure drop (Table 5).

Machine learning–based performance prediction framework

While CFD simulations provide accurate insight into the thermo-hydraulic behavior of nanofluid-based radiators, repeated parametric simulations are computationally expensive. To complement the CFD analysis, a machine learning (ML)–based surrogate modeling approach is proposed to rapidly predict radiator outlet temperature and pressure drop under varying operating conditions.

Dataset preparation

The dataset for ML model development was generated using validated CFD simulation results and corresponding experimental measurements. Input features included nanoparticle concentration (%), inlet coolant temperature, and mass flow rate, while output targets were radiator outlet temperature and pressure drop. In order to improve the prediction accuracy and stability of the model, normalization was applied on the dataset.

Model selection and training

Supervised regression models—namely Linear Regression (LR), Support Vector Regression (SVR), and Random Forest Regression (RFR)—were used to predict performance outcomes28,31. These methods were chosen because they are reliable, well suited for engineering-related datasets, and relatively easy to interpret. For the training and testing of the model dataset has been split into 80% for training and 20% for testing. In order to improve the prediction accuracy, model hyperparameters tuning has been performed. A grid-search based optimization approach has been applied to perform the hyperparameter tuning.

Model evaluation

The performance of the machine learning models was evaluated by applying standard statistical measures. These measures include the coefficient of determination (R²), mean absolute error (MAE), and root mean square error (RMSE). It has been observed that among all the models tested, the Random Forest Regression model delivered the most accurate predictions. It performed better for both outlet temperature and pressure drop. This shows ML model’s strong capability to capture the nonlinear relationship between nanoparticle concentration and the thermal–hydraulic behavior of the system.

Integration with CFD results

The trained machine learning model like random forest serves as a fast and efficient surrogate for computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations. This helps in quick predictions of radiator performance under new nanoparticle concentrations and operating conditions27,29,32. By integrating CFD with machine learning models, the proposed framework is able to significantly reduce computational effort. At the same it also maintains the acceptable accuracy as well. Due to this, it makes it suitable for preliminary radiator design optimization and informed coolant selection.

Uncertainty quantification and model validation

Further, uncertainty quantification and model validation has been performed to ensure the numerical reliability and statistical robustness of the proposed CFD–ML framework. A formal uncertainty analysis has been conducted in this paper. Discretization uncertainty in the CFD simulations has been systematically evaluated. Further, statistical uncertainty in machine learning predictions were also evaluated systematically.

Mesh independence and grid convergence index (GCI)

In order to evaluate the discretization error, a mesh independence study has also been performed. For mesh independence study three progressively refined computational grids have been applied. These include coarse, medium, and fine meshes consisted of approximately 150,000, 300,000, and 600,000 control volumes, respectively. The predicted outlet temperature for these meshes is given in Table 6. The variation in outlet temperature between the medium and fine meshes was less than 0.1 °C. This indicates minimal sensitivity to further grid refinement. Further, the Grid Convergence Index (GCI) has been calculated using Roache’s method to quantify discretization uncertainty. The refinement ratio between successive meshes was maintained at r = 2, and the observed order of accuracy was approximately second order.

The pressure-drop behavior of the nanofluids shows a clear increasing trend with rising aluminum nanoparticle concentration in the ethylene glycol base fluid. Base coolant ethylene glycol showed the minimum pressure drop of 35.294 Pa due to its relatively low viscosity and smooth flow characteristics within the radiator tubes. When nanoparticles are blended with base fluid, the pressure drop start to rise progressively to 38.72 Pa at 5% concentration. This increase arises due to the accumulation of concentrated nanoparticles, that raised its viscosity, and increase resistance to flow of nanofluid19–21. With rise in the concentration level, the fluid flow becomes more viscus that needs more pumping power to keep coolant fluid circulation. It was seen that The Al₂O₃ nanofluids improved heat transfer, but the analogous rise in pressure drop indicates that the higher nanoparticle concentrations place supplementary burden on the pumping system. In the present work, as shown in Fig. 11, the disparity in the pressure drop seen was noticeably low.

The relative CFD and experimental results validate the similar qualitative tendencies but trivial systematic quantitative difference is detected. As shown in Fig. 12, CFD results forecasted slightly higher cooling capability than experiments one. The observed deviation is less than 5%, and consistent with nanofluid coolant studies reported in the literature. It is also important to note that The CFD studies were accomplished under steady-state conditions with idealized boundary constraints, uniform inlet velocity, and perfectly distributed airflow across the radiator core. In contrast, the experimental setup comprise of unsteady effects, including airflow non-uniformity, ambient temperature variations, and heat losses to surroundings through radiation and convection, and flow misdistribution within the radiator. It is also important to mentioned that nanoparticle characteristics such as accumulation, sedimentation, fouling further effect effective heat transfer.

Conclusion

The numerical investigation using ANSYS FLUENT-CFD and its experimental validation showed that the combination of Al₂O₃ + EG significantly augmented the thermal performance of the engine radiator. The pure ethylene glycol delivered effective cooling, but the addition of nanoparticles enhanced heat absorption and heat-recovery efficiency due to augmented thermal conductivity and improved convective heat coefficient. As the nanoparticle concentration increased from 1% to 5%, the degree of cooling gradually increased from 9.89 °C for pure EG to 19.13 °C at 5% concentration( CFD case), that confirmed the higher cooling competency of Al₂O₃–EG nanofluids. At the higher concentrations, the temperature plot showed uniform cooling that show better heat dissipation at radiator. It was also observed that the pressure drop rises gradually with increseibng nanoparticles in experimental and CFD study, but thar rise was comparatively small. In CFD analysis, pressure drop rises from 35.29 Pa for EG to 38.72 Pa at 5% (8.85% rise) while in experimental work it was raised from 33.21 Pa for EG to 39.98 Pa at 5% (16.93% rise). It signifies that the enhanced thermal performance resulted in a minor surge in pumping power. The variation in the CFD and experimental result was mainly due to assumption of steady state condition which was difficult to maintain in reality. Inclusively, the study authenticated Al₂O₃–EG nanofluids as one of the promising coolants for automotive applications, offering considerable enhancements in heat-recovery, and overall temperature control equated to the conventional coolants. In addition to the conventional CFD approach, a machine learning–based predictive framework was proposed to estimate radiator outlet temperature and pressure drop efficiently. The ML model shows strong similarities or agreement with CFD and experimental results. This helps to rapid prediction of radiator performance under varying operating conditions. The hybrid CFD–ML methodology can offer a practical and computationally efficient tool for radiator design optimization and coolant selection27,33. Overall, the study establishes Al₂O₃–EG nanofluids as a promising coolant for automotive applications. Further, it also signifies the prospective of data-driven approaches to improve conventional thermal analysis.
