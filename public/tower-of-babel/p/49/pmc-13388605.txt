Multi-omic discovery of SCG5 as a shared driver and prognostic biomarker in head-neck and non-small cell lung cancers
Authors: Fenghui Zhao, Haining Wang, Tao Qin, Yize Wang, Huiyuan Yang, Honghai Wang, Mi Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC) face a high risk of developing non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). However, the shared molecular drivers linking these two malignancies remain poorly defined.MethodsWe integrated TCGA and GEO datasets to identify shared differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between HNSC and NSCLC, subsequently evaluating their prognostic value, immune infiltration patterns (ssGSEA), and enriched pathways. Clinical validation of SCG5 protein expression was comprehensively performed using tissue microarrays (TMA) via immunohistochemistry. in vitro assays, including siRNA-mediated knockdown, CCK-8, colony formation, and Western blotting, were conducted in both HNSC (SCC-25) and NSCLC (A549) cell lines to ascertain its oncogenic functions and underlying molecular mechanisms.ResultsSCG5 was identified as a consistently upregulated, independent predictor of poor survival in both cancers. IHC confirmed SCG5 protein overexpression, correlating with lymph node metastasis and advanced clinical stages. GSEA revealed that SCG5-associated genes were significantly enriched in focal adhesion and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in both HNSC and NSCLC. SCG5 expression correlated positively with tumor-infiltrating macrophages (P < 0.001) and with PDCD1LG2, HAVCR2 and SIGLEC15 (P < 0.05). SCG5 knockdown significantly suppressed cell proliferation and colony formation in both SCC-25 and A549 cells, concurrently attenuating PI3K and AKT phosphorylation.ConclusionSCG5 is a novel shared oncogenic driver and prognostic biomarker for HNSC and NSCLC. Downregulation of SCG5 suppresses the growth of HNSC and NSCLC cells, potentially through modulating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, thereby presenting a potential therapeutic target for both malignancies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05356-8.Keywords: Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, SCG5, Non-small cell lung cancer, Second primary malignancy, Biomarker

Introduction

Cancer of the head and neck has high global morbidity and mortality rates, with approximately 870,000 new cases and 440,000 deaths annually, of which head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC) is the most prevalent type [1]. HNSC patients are at substantial risk of developing a second primary malignancy (SPM) [2] which is associated with significantly decreased survival. The average incidence of SPM in HNSC is 13.2%, comprising 5.3% synchronous and 9.4% metachronous SPMs. The most common sites for SPMs are the head and neck, lungs and esophagus [3]. Among these, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is a common SPM in HNSC, and its development reduces the overall survival of HNSC patients. According to an earlier report [4], SPMs of NSCLC are more likely to occur in HNSC patients with aggressive clinicopathological features, such as advanced disease stage, hypopharyngeal tumor location, older age, and male sex. A study of Asian patients has also identified risk factors for lung SPMs in HNSC patients [5]. However, the molecular determinants underlying this cross-organ susceptibility remain poorly understood.

Advances in high-throughput technologies have enabled large-scale expression profiling of individual cancer types. These features can assist clinicians in early diagnosis, risk assessment, treatment efficiency evaluation, and disease survival prediction. Nevertheless, studies on shared risk factors between HNSC and NSCLC remain scarce. Existing research has largely focused on each cancer type independently, lacking systematic exploration of their common molecular basis.

To bridge this crucial knowledge gap, the present study employed a comprehensive cross-cancer multi-omic strategy combined with in vitro functional validation. By integrating independent transcriptomic datasets of HNSC and NSCLC, we sought to identify common differentially expressed genes driving both malignancies. We successfully uncovered Secretogranin V (SCG5) as a consistently upregulated, shared oncogenic driver and a robust independent prognostic biomarker for both HNSC and NSCLC. Furthermore, we comprehensively validated SCG5 expression at the protein level using clinical tissue microarrays (TMAs) and evaluated its impact on tumor immune microenvironment infiltration. Finally, through in vitro assays in both HNSC and NSCLC cell lines, we elucidated the biological function of SCG5 and its potential mechanism involving the hyperactivation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. This study provides potential mechanistic insights into the shared pathogenesis of HNSC and NSCLC, highlighting SCG5 as a promising dual-therapeutic target.

Materials and Methods

Data Acquisition

The technical roadmap of this study is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Microarray datasets were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) under accession numbers GSE85195, GSE180077 and GSE18842, comprising 39 HNSC tumor and 19 normal tissues, as well as 46 NSCLC patients and 45 controls. Furthermore, bulk RNA-seq data and clinical information including gender, TNM stage and survival status etc. for 548 HNSC patients and 44 controls, as well as 1149 NSCLC patients and 108 controls were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas Program [6] (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov).

Identification of Common DEGs

R software (version 4.2.1) was used for all analyses. To mitigate cross-platform batch effects, we applied a “separate analysis, then intersection” strategy: edgeR [7] for RNA-seq datasets and limma [8] for the microarray dataset. DEGs were defined by adjusted P < 0.05 and |logFC| > 2 (upregulation: logFC > 2; downregulation: logFC < -2) in each dataset. Common DEGs were identified by taking the intersection across all three datasets. Visualization was performed using VennDiagram[1.7.3] and ggplot2[3.4.4].

GO and KEGG Analysis

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the common differential genes were performed using the “clusterProfiler” package [9] to identify signaling pathways and biological processes that may be enriched in the patients of HNSC developing NSCLC. Adjusted P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) was used to predict protein functional relationships and interactions. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was visualized with the Cytoscape software.

Prognostic Evaluation and Survival Analysis

According to TCGA database, the expression and survival of 14 up-regulated genes in the HNSC and the NSCLC cohorts were analyzed. The combined analysis of the TCGA-LUAD and TCGA-LUSC data represents the NSCLC cohort (TCGA-LUADLUSC). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed using the “Survival” package to screen genes associated with overall survival (P < 0.05), and the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated. Kaplan-Meier estimate was used to construct survival curves and calculate overall survival (OS), progression-free interval (PFI), and disease-specific survival (DSS). Genes with prognostic significance in both cancer types were considered as key genes. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to investigate diagnostic performance. External validation was performed using the GEPIA (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) and Kaplan-Meier Plotter (http://kmplot.com/) to confirm the association between SCG5 expression and patient prognosis.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis

SCG5 was identified as the key player through the above steps. To analyze the potential cancer-promoting pathways shared by these two cancers, we further used “clusterProfiler” for Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) [10]. GSEA was performed using c2.cp.all.v2022.1.Hs.symbols.gmt [All Canonical Pathways](3050) in MSigDB Collections. Firstly, the HNSC and the NSCLC cohorts were divided into SCG5-high and SCG5-low expression groups, respectively, according to the median expression level of SCG5. Then “DESeq2 [11]” and “edgeR” were used to analyze the differentially expressed genes related to SCG5 for functional enrichment. Adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |logFC| > 2 were identified as related to SCG5. The thresholds were set as false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25 and adjusted P-value < 0.05.

Immune Infiltration and Immune Checkpoint Analyses

We explored the relationship between the expression of key genes and the degree of tumor infiltration, as well as the expression of immune checkpoint genes, in order to deeply explore the potential molecular mechanisms underlying tumor promotion. The status of 24 immune cell types’ tumor infiltration [12] was assessed using the ssGSEA [13] algorithm in GSVA [1.46.0] R package and visualized with ggplot2 package. Spearman’s correlation analysis was applied to examine the relationship of molecular expression with immune cell infiltration and with nine extensively studied immune checkpoint genes, previously implicated in immune checkpoint function assessment [14–16]. Wilcoxon rank sum test was utilized to compare the difference between different SCG5 expression groups. The above analyses were conducted independently on the lung cancer dataset and the head and neck squamous cell carcinoma dataset, respectively.

Tissue Microarray and Immunohistochemistry

Tissue microarrays of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HN810001) and lung cancer (R121Lu01) were purchased from zhongke guanghua biotechnology (http://bioaitech.com). Immunohistochemical staining was performed on tissue microarray (TMA) sections. Briefly, tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through a graded alcohol series. Antigen retrieval was carried out using a high-pressure heating method with EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). Subsequently, the sections were sequentially treated as follows: endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with a peroxidase block, and non-specific binding sites were blocked with a serum solution. The sections were then incubated with the primary antibody (Proteintech, 10761-1-AP, diluted at 1:50) at 37℃ for 1 h, followed by a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody at 37℃ for 20 min. Signal visualization was achieved using a 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate, and the nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. SCG5 expression levels were subsequently compared between tumor samples and matched normal controls.

Immunohistochemical Scoring

Immunohistochemical staining was evaluated using a combined scoring system based on the percentage of positive cells and staining intensity [17]. All sections were independently assessed by two pathologists blinded to clinical data. Staining intensity was graded as follows: 0 (negative), 1 (pale yellow), 2 (deep yellow), and 3 (brownish yellow). The percentage of positive cells was scored as: 0 (< 5%), 1 (5–25%), 2 (26–50%), 3 (51–75%), and 4 (76–100%). The final score was calculated as the product of the intensity score and the proportion score, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 12. Based on the median score of the cohort (4), samples were classified into low-expression group (final score < 4) and high-expression group (final score ≥ 4). The association between SCG5 expression level (high vs. low) and clinicopathological parameters was analysed using the Chi-square test.

Cell Culture

The human tongue squamous cell carcinoma cell line SCC-25 and the human non-small cell lung cancer cell line A549 were obtained from Wuhan Procell Life Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). SCC-25 cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Procell, Wuhan, China), whereas A549 cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Procell, Wuhan, China). Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Procell, Wuhan, China). All cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2.

Colony Formation Assay

For the colony formation assay, siRNA-transfected cells were harvested and seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 600 cells per well. The cells were cultured in medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, which was refreshed every 4 days. After 2 weeks of cultivation, the resulting colonies were fixed with methanol and stained with 1% crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich). Visible colonies were photographed and counted using an optical microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Cell Counting kit-8 (CCK-8) Assay

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (TransGen Biotech, China) to determine whether SCG5 affects cancer cell proliferation. siRNA-transfected cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3–5 × 103 cells per well (n = 3) and incubated for 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. At each time point, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to wells containing 100 µL of medium and incubated for 4 h at 37°C. Absorbance was then measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Transient Transfection

Transient transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. siRNA oligonucleotides targeting SCG5 were as follows: siRNA#1, 5’-AAACAGUAGGCCAGAUAGCAUUUGCUAUCUGGCCUACUGUUUUGUU-3’; siRNA#2, 5’-UGGCAAUGCCCAAUUGCUCCAUUGAGCAAUUGGGCAUUGCCAGGUU-3’; and siRNA#3, 5’-ACGAUGUUGGGGAUGUUGCCAUUGCAACAUCCCCAACAUCGUGGUU-3’. Cells were transfected with one of the SCG5-targeting siRNAs or with a non-targeting negative control (NC) siRNA (GenePharma, China). Forty-eight hours after transfection, total RNA and protein were collected, and SCG5 knockdown efficiency and downstream protein expression were assessed by RT-qPCR and Western blotting, respectively.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (TransGen Biotech, China). One microgram of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA with the All-In-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (MedChemExpress, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed on an iCycler Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Richmond, CA, USA) using SYBR Premix (TransGen Biotech, China). β-actin (ACTB) served as the internal reference, and relative mRNA expression levels were determined by the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences were: SCG5 forward, AAAACACCCCTGACACTGCA; SCG5 reverse, CTTGTTCCACTTGCCCAAGC; β-actin forward, TGGCACCCAGCACAATGAA; β-actin reverse, CTAAGTCATAGTCCGCCTAGAAGCA.

Western Blot Analysis

Protein lysates were prepared as described previously [18]. Protein concentrations were determined using the Easy II Protein Quantitative Kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE on 8% or 10% gels and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Boston, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 2 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4℃ with primary antibodies against SCG5, PI3K, p-PI3K, AKT, p-AKT, and β-tubulin (MedchemExpress LLC, Shanghai, China). The membranes were subsequently incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies (1:16,000 in TBST) for 1 h at 37℃. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 5.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Data from at least three independent experiments are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test, and comparisons among three or more groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification and Functional Enrichment of DEGs between HNSC and NSCLC

We acquired and analyzed expression profiles to identify DEGs between tumor and matched normal tissues in both HNSC and NSCLC. Based on our screening criteria as described in the method section, we identified DEGs from three datasets: In GSE85195 (16 Control vs. 34 OPSCC), there were 635 down- and 359 up-regulated DEGs (Fig. 1A); in GSE180077 (3 Normal vs. 5 HPSCC), 1223 down- and 1055 up-regulated DEGs (Fig. 1B); and in GSE18842 (45 Control vs. 46 NSCLC), 373 down- and 270 up-regulated DEGs (Fig. 1C). Venn and Upset plot analyses identified 68 common differentially expressed genes shared by tumors of HNSC and NSCLC, and their corresponding normal tissues. Among these, 14 genes were consistently up-regulated, while 45 were down-regulated (Fig. 1D–G). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed significant enrichment in several processes, including the IL-17 signaling pathway, collagen metabolic process, extracellular matrix organization, reactive oxygen species metabolic process, enzyme activity, and transmembrane transporter activity (Fig. 1E). Finally, the PPI network analysis reveals a core interaction network and highlights the top 10 genes ranked by their degree of connectivity (Fig. 1H–J).

Identification of SCG5 as a Shared Prognostic Biomarker in HNSC and NSCLC

We further analyzed the expression and prognostic value of the 14 co-upregulated genes using TCGA data. These genes were significantly highly expressed in both HNSC and NSCLC tumors compared to the matched normal tissues (P < 0.001; Fig. 2A).Through univariate Cox regression analysis, SPP1, DSG2, SCG5 and PFN2 were identified as potential prognostic factors for HNSC and were subsequently included in multivariate Cox regression analysis. The results showed that (Fig. 2F) high expression of SCG5 (HR = 1.449, P < 0.01) and PFN2 (HR = 1.496, P < 0.01) were independent risk factors for poorer overall survival in HNSC. ROC curve analysis validated the diagnostic accuracy for SCG5 (AUC = 0.911; Fig. 2G) and PFN2 (AUC = 0.784; Fig. 2E) in HNSC. Similarly, MMP10, SPP1, DSG2, SCG5, and MAGEA4 were identified by univariate regression analysis as prognostic variables in NSCLC. Multivariate analysis revealed that high SCG5 (HR = 1.265, P < 0.05) and DSG2 (HR = 1.255, P < 0.05) expression were independent predictors of reduced overall survival (Fig. 2D). These genes also showed high diagnostic value in ROC curves: SCG5 (AUC = 0.850; Fig. 2B) and DSG2 (AUC = 0.900; Fig. 2C). In summary, SCG5 was widely expressed in both cancers and was established as an independent prognostic risk factor in each, supporting it may represent a potential shared molecular feature of HNSC and NSCLC.

High SCG5 Expression Associates with Survival Outcomes in HNSC and NSCLC

Kaplan-Meier analysis of TCGA data identified SCG5 as a stratification factor. In the NSCLC cohort, increased SCG5 expression was linked with significantly worse survival in patients with DSS (HR = 1.49, P < 0.01), PFI (HR = 1.69, P < 0.001) and OS (HR = 1.24, P < 0.05) (Fig. 3A–C). Similar to this, HNSC patients with high SCG5 expression had worse outcomes for DSS (HR = 1.73, P < 0.01), PFI (HR = 1.38, P < 0.05) and OS (HR = 1.52, P < 0.01) (Fig. 3D–F). In addition, we performed external validation using the GEPIA and Kaplan-Meier Plotter open-source databases, which similarly confirmed that high SCG5 expression is associated with poor prognosis in HNSC and NSCLC patients (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Enrichment Analysis of Signaling Pathways Related to SCG5

GSEA analysis revealed 164 and 87 pathways significantly associated with SCG5 in HNSC and NSCLC, respectively, with 48 pathways common to both malignancies.Many key clusters, including “PI3K/AKT signaling pathway”, “Focal adhesion”, and a related mTOR-integrated pathway—were significantly enriched in both NSCLC and HNSC (Fig. 3G–L), and are known to regulate fundamental malignant behaviors in these cancers.

The Correlation between SCG5 Expression and Tumor Immune Microenvironment

Given the crucial role of the tumor immune microenvironment (TME) and immune checkpoint inhibitors in cancer therapy, we investigated the relationship between SCG5 expression and immune invasion. The infiltration of B cells, T cells and CD8+ T cells was lower in the SCG5 high expression group (P < 0.001), while the infiltration of macrophages and NK cells was more in the SCG5 high expression group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4A). Further analysis showed that SCG5 expression showed positive associations with Macrophages (R = 0.334, P < 0.001), NK cells (R = 0.328, P < 0.001) and negatively correlated with B cells (R = -0.280, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4B). In NSCLC, SCG5 expression level showed positive associations with macrophages (R = 0.294, P < 0.001), eosinophils (R = 0.246, P < 0.001), Th1 cells (R = 0.235, P < 0.001) and T follicular helper cells (R = 0.219, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4C).

We next analyzed the correlations between 14 key genes and immune checkpoint genes. Upregulation of PDCD1LG2, HAVCR2 and SIGLEC15 was observed in HNSC and NSCLC patients exhibiting high key gene expression, where their expression levels showed a strong positive correlation with SCG5 (Fig. 4D, E). Moreover, CD276 expression was positively correlated with the expression of most of the key upregulated genes.

Validation of SCG5 Expression in Head and Neck Squamous Cell Carcinoma Tissues

To validate the bioinformatics analysis, we performed immunohistochemical staining on a tissue microarray comprising 30 HNSC and 11 matched adjacent normal tissues. Figure 5 illustrates the representative immunohistochemical staining patterns of SCG5 in tumor and matched normal tissues from the HNSC tissue microarray. SCG5 immunoreactivity was predominantly localized within the cytoplasm. As quantified and confirmed by the Chi-square test (Table 1), SCG5 expression was up-regulated in tumor tissues compared to normal controls (Fig. 5C), a finding consistent with its mRNA expression profile from our bioinformatic analysis. Combined with the data of patients in tissue microarray, the expression level of SCG5 was significantly correlated with the clinicopathological characteristics of patients in HNSC, including T stage, lymph node metastasis and clinical stage (P < 0.05). Validation of SCG5 Expression in Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer Tissues

Immunohistochemical staining was performed to verify the results of bioinformatics analysis on a tissue microarray containing 33 cases of NSCLC tissues and adjacent normal tissues, including 19 cases of lung adenocarcinoma and 13 cases of lung squamous cell carcinoma. As shown in Fig. 6, SCG5 exhibited a predominantly cytoplasmic staining pattern in NSCLC tissues compared to normal controls. Chi-square analysis confirmed a significant upregulation of SCG5 protein in tumor tissues (P < 0.05; Fig. 6C; Table 3), consistent with the bioinformatic findings at the mRNA level. Furthermore, high SCG5 expression was associated with lymph node metastasis (pN) and clinical stage (P < 0.05; Table 4) in the NSCLC cohort. The expression of SCG5 in lung squamous cell carcinoma and lung adenocarcinoma tissues was not different between groups (P > 0.05).

SCG5 Silencing Inhibits Cell Proliferation and Attenuates PI3K/AKT Signaling in both SCC-25 and A549 Cells

To investigate the role of SCG5 in HNSC, we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown in SCC-25 cells. Efficient reduction of SCG5 expression was confirmed by Western blot and qRT-PCR (Fig. 7A, B). CCK-8 assays demonstrated that SCG5 silencing significantly suppressed SCC-25 cell proliferation (Fig. 7D). Moreover, colony formation assays showed that SCG5 knockdown markedly decreased the colony-forming ability of SCC-25 cells (Fig. 7C). To further solidify our conclusion that SCG5 acts as a shared driver in these malignancies, we also performed siRNA-mediated SCG5 knockdown in the NSCLC cell line A549. Consistent with the findings in HNSC, CCK-8 and colony formation assays revealed that SCG5 knockdown significantly inhibited the proliferation and colony-forming capacity of NSCLC cells (Fig. 8). Together, these results indicate that SCG5 plays a potential role in promoting cell proliferation in both HNSC and NSCLC, and that its inhibition may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for these tumors.

Furthermore, to experimentally validate our GSEA predictions regarding the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, we evaluated the phosphorylation levels of key pathway components following SCG5 silencing. Western blot analysis revealed that the knockdown of SCG5 markedly reduced the expression of phosphorylated PI3K and phosphorylated AKT in both SCC-25 and A549 cells, while the total PI3K and AKT protein levels remained unaffected (Fig. 9). These data suggest that SCG5 exerts its oncogenic effects, at least in part, by actively modulating the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade.

Discussion

NSCLC is a relatively common second primary tumor in HNSC, and is associated with worse overall survival. Previous studies have reported risk factors for second primary tumors in patients with HNSC [2, 3, 19, 20]. Several genomic studies have investigated prognostic biomarkers for second primary tumorigenesis in HNSC [21]. Although numerous studies have examined HNSC and NSCLC independently, the common molecules and pathways shared between these two malignancies remain poorly understood. In the present study, utilizing a comprehensive multi-omic approach combined with experimental validation, we identified SCG5 as a potential shared oncogenic factor and a poor prognostic indicator for both HNSC and NSCLC.

SCG5, also known as SGNE1 or 7B2, encodes a neuroendocrine chaperone protein that traditionally facilitates the trafficking and activation of prohormone convertase PC2. Emerging epidemiological and molecular studies have gradually unveiled the multifaceted role of SCG5 in oncology. For instance, SCG5 alterations have been linked to polyposis syndrome [22], increasing the susceptibility to colorectal cancer. In the central nervous system, SCG5 is frequently silenced by DNA methylation, exerting significant regulatory effects on the growth and apoptosis of glioblastoma cells [23]. Furthermore, recent transcriptomic profiling has implicated SCG5 in modulating the viability, migration, and invasion of cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma [24]. In the context of head and neck malignancies, recent preliminary bioinformatics analyses have flagged SCG5 as a potential prognostic indicator in tongue cancer [25] and oral squamous cell carcinoma [26]. However, most existing studies are confined to bioinformatic correlation analyses within a single cancer type with corresponding experimental validation. By contrast, our study identifies SCG5 not merely as an isolated biomarker but as a shared oncogenic driver linking head and neck squamous cell carcinoma and its most common second primary malignancy, non–small cell lung cancer. From cross‑cancer, multi‑omics screening to clinical protein‑level validation using tissue microarrays, and further in vitro functional assays, we demonstrate that SCG5 promotes cell proliferation in both HNSC (SCC‑25) and NSCLC (A549) cell lines. These findings highlight the pivotal role of SCG5 as a potential therapeutic target involved in the initiation and progression of both malignancies.

Mechanistically, our functional enrichment analyses revealed that high SCG5 expression is significantly associated with key tumor-related cascades, prominently including the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, focal adhesion, and the interconnected focal adhesion: PI3K/AKT axis in both HNSC and NSCLC. Focal adhesion complexes serve as vital signaling hubs comprising pro-survival molecules, such as integrins and growth factor receptors, which intricately dictate tumor cell activity and survival, thereby representing actionable targets for cancer therapy [27]. Concurrently, the downstream PI3K/AKT pathway is frequently altered in a variety of malignancies, including HNSC and NSCLC, acting as a notoriously recognized master regulator of cancer proliferation, survival, and development. Notably, the aberrant activation of this signaling network is strongly correlated with resistance to radiation therapy and cytostatic agents, potentially by enhancing DNA repair mechanisms [28]. Consequently, it represents a highly promising target for novel anticancer therapies in both NSCLC [29] and HNSC [30]. To experimentally validate these in silico GSEA predictions, we evaluated the core components of this cascade in vitro. Consistent with our hypotheses, our experiments demonstrated that siRNA-mediated silencing of SCG5 significantly attenuated the phosphorylation levels of PI3K and AKT in tumor cells without affecting their total protein levels. These results imply that SCG5 exerts its oncogenic effects, at least in part, by actively modulating and constitutively hyperactivating the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade.

In HNSC, high SCG5 expression correlates with reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration, suggesting impaired antitumor immunity linked to poor prognosis and immune evasion [31, 32]. It also correlates with decreased B cell infiltration, which may compromise humoral immunity and antigen presentation given B cells’ dual functions in antibody production and antigen presentation [33]. Moreover, the tumor immune microenvironment plays a decisive role in cancer progression and the efficacy of immunotherapy. Our immune infiltration analysis revealed significant correlations between SCG5 expression and specific immune cell populations. Specifically, we observed that high SCG5 expression was positively associated with macrophage infiltration in both HNSC and NSCLC. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), particularly those polarized towards an M2-like phenotype, are characterized by anti-inflammatory, angiogenic, and immunosuppressive functions that promote tumor progression. Across solid tumors, the presence of TAMs is a well-established marker of poor prognosis. They are known to limit chemotherapy efficacy, diminish the effects of anti-angiogenic agents, and attenuate the inhibitory effects of radiotherapy [34]. In NSCLC, TAMs promote progression by facilitating lymphangiogenesis and angiogenesis [35], while in HNSC, they similarly exert profound immunosuppressive effects. Thus, novel therapeutic strategies targeting TAMs—either by inhibiting their suppressive functions or repolarizing them to a proinflammatory state—are gaining attention [36].

Beyond cellular infiltration, SCG5 expression was closely associated with multiple targetable immune checkpoints. Our analysis revealed that the expression levels of PDCD1LG2 (PD-L2), HAVCR2 (TIM-3), and SIGLEC15 (Siglec-15) showed a significant positive correlation with SCG5 in both cancer types. PD-L2 acts as a second ligand for PD-1, cooperating with PD-L1 to suppress T cell function [37]. TIM-3 marks exhausted T cells and mediates CD8+ T cell dysfunction [38, 39]. Siglec-15 is an emerging immune checkpoint that inhibits T cell responses across multiple cancer types and operates through a regulatory mechanism that does not overlap with the PD-1/PD-L1 axis [40, 41]. Additionally, SCG5 correlated with CD274 (PD-L1), CTLA-4, and importantly, CD276 (B7-H3). Recent large-cohort pan-cancer studies have established CD276 as a critical immunosuppressive checkpoint, often associated with a decreased proportion of CD8 + T cells and immune evasion [42, 43]. High SCG5 expression co-occurs with diverse immune checkpoints and macrophage infiltration, suggesting that SCG5 may help shape the tumour microenvironment. The SCG5-high microenvironment may not rely on a single immune escape pathway but instead employs a multidimensional, cooperative immunosuppressive network to counteract host anti-tumour immunity. This could explain why some PD-L1-low tumours show immunotherapy resistance — the SCG5-high subgroup may engage alternative checkpoints, including PD-L2, TIM-3, Siglec-15, CD276 and CTLA-4. However, direct experimental evidence confirming a functional regulatory role of SCG5 on these immune components is lacking. Further in vitro studies, such as examining immune checkpoint expression upon SCG5 knockdown and conducting tumour–macrophage co-culture assays, are needed to clarify the immunomodulatory mechanisms of SCG5 in the tumour microenvironment.

Despite providing multi-omic and experimental evidence, this study has several specific limitations. First, although we performed in vitro functional assays in both HNSC and NSCLC, the validation was limited to a single cell line for each cancer type (SCC-25 and A549, respectively). Relying on a single cell line may limit the generalizability of the findings due to cell-line-specific genetic backgrounds. Future studies should include a broader panel of diverse cell lines and patient-derived organoids (PDOs) to fully capture tumor heterogeneity. Second, our study currently lacks in vivo animal models. While the proliferative role of SCG5 was confirmed in vitro, xenograft mouse models are required to evaluate its impact on tumor microenvironment modulation, metastasis, and tumor formation in vivo. Third, SCG5 is intrinsically a secretory neuroendocrine protein, whereas our current functional assays focused exclusively on its intracellular mechanisms. Further investigations using methods such as ELISA are needed to evaluate extracellular SCG5 levels and explore its potential paracrine mechanisms. Finally, the clinical and prognostic analyses were based on retrospective public cohorts; therefore, large-scale, multicenter prospective epidemiological studies are required to confirm SCG5 as a biomarker indicating a shared risk factor for both malignancies.

In summary, SCG5 serves as a novel shared oncogenic driver and prognostic biomarker in both HNSC and NSCLC. Knocking down SCG5 inhibits the proliferation of HNSC and NSCLC cells, with the underlying mechanism likely involving the regulation of the PI3K/AKT pathway. Therefore, SCG5 provides a promising therapeutic target for these two malignancies.
