Sialendoscopic Extraction of 32 Salivary Stones From Stensen's Duct: A Case Report
Authors: Mohammad Farhadi, Saleh Mohebbi, Marjan Mirsalehi, Mohammadsadegh Zabihidan, Mehdi Abrishami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSialolithiasis is a common cause of salivary gland obstruction, but cases with multiple stones are rare and challenging. We present a 32‐year‐old woman with several months of recurrent right parotid swelling and pain, especially during meals. Ultrasound revealed multiple hyperechoic foci, including an 11 mm stone near the second upper molar, and sialendoscopy enabled the successful removal of 32 stones ranging from 2 to 11 mm. A multistage extraction under general anesthesia with probe dilatation, micro‐instruments, and baskets was completed without complications. At follow‐up, the patient was asymptomatic with normal salivary flow, no residual stones on ultrasound, and no adverse events. This case demonstrates the feasibility and safety of sialendoscopic management of an unusually high stone burden, highlighting the value of balloon‐assisted ductal access, careful imaging, and preventive care in preserving gland function.

Summary This case demonstrates that even an exceptionally high stone burden in the parotid duct can be safely managed with minimally invasive sialendoscopy.Multistage extraction with balloon‐assisted access preserves gland function, avoids surgical morbidity, and highlights the importance of tailored imaging, preventive care, and long‐term follow‐up in complex sialolithiasis.

Introduction

Sialolithiasis, a prevalent cause of salivary gland obstruction, poses significant clinical challenges due to its potential to disrupt salivary flow, leading to painful symptoms and recurrent infections that impair patients' quality of life. This condition arises from the formation of calcified deposits within the salivary glands or their ducts, with stone sizes ranging from 2 mm to over 1.7 cm [1, 2, 3, 4].

These calculi can obstruct the ductal lumen, resulting in sialadenitis, characterized by glandular swelling, pain, purulent discharge, and systemic symptoms such as fever and malaise [2, 3]. Compositional analyses of sialoliths indicate a combination of organic and inorganic materials, contributing to their variable size and clinical impact [5].

While conservative treatments, such as hydration, salivary stimulation, and antibiotics, are often employed, they may be insufficient for larger or multiple stones [6]. Traditional surgical approaches, such as open sialadenectomy, carry risks including facial nerve damage, scarring, and loss of salivary gland function [7]. Sialendoscopy has revolutionized the management of sialolithiasis by offering a minimally invasive method for direct visualization and stone extraction, achieving success rates of 80%–90% while preserving gland function [8, 9].

A study involving over 1000 patients demonstrated that sialendoscopy successfully removed stones in 85% of parotid cases with minimal complications [10]. However, the literature also highlights challenges in managing complex cases, such as those involving multiple or large stones, which may require advanced techniques or combined approaches [11]. For instance, Koch et al. emphasized that multiple stones within a single duct, particularly Stensen's duct, are rare and technically challenging due to the duct's narrow anatomy and proximity to critical structures [7].

This case report describes the sialendoscopic removal of 32 salivary stones from a single Stensen's duct, an exceptionally rare presentation of parotid sialolithiasis. The unprecedented number of calculi and the successful application of sialendoscopy in this complex scenario underscore the technique's potential in highly demanding cases. The clinical significance of this report lies in demonstrating that even an extreme stone burden can be managed safely and effectively with minimally invasive sialendoscopy, thereby avoiding the morbidity of open surgery, preserving glandular function, and offering a practical reference for clinicians facing similarly challenging cases. By contributing to the sparse literature on extensive multi‐stone sialolithiasis, this report provides valuable insights into technical considerations, patient outcomes, and the broader role of sialendoscopy in complex salivary gland disease.

Case History/Examination

A 32‐year‐old female presented with a several‐month history of intermittent right parotid gland swelling, particularly during meals, and recurrent pain in the right preauricular region, without systemic symptoms such as fever or malaise. Physical examination revealed a tender, swollen right parotid gland with no palpable masses, lymphadenopathy, purulent discharge from the Stensen's duct orifice, or facial nerve dysfunction. Salivary flow on the right side was reduced compared to the contralateral side. The patient's medical history was unremarkable, with no prior salivary gland issues, trauma, or systemic disorders associated with sialolithiasis, such as autoimmune or metabolic diseases.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Several months earlier, symptoms began with swelling and meal‐related pain. One month prior to the initial visit, an ultrasound with a 7 MHz probe showed dilatation of the right Stensen's duct up to 8 mm and an 11 mm stone near the second upper molar (Stensen's duct). A subsequent ultrasound confirmed multiple hyperechoic foci in the parenchymal ducts of the right parotid gland, supporting the presence of numerous stones (Figures 1 and 2). Ultrasound was chosen for its accessibility, noninvasive nature, and avoidance of additional radiation exposure, while also allowing dynamic assessment of salivary flow. Considering the patient's relatively young age and high stone burden, CT sialography was deemed unnecessary. The main diagnostic challenge was the unusually high stone burden, which made accurate preoperative counting via ultrasound difficult due to echo overlap, necessitating sialoendoscopy to differentiate from ductal stricture. The absence of systemic symptoms ruled out chronic sialadenitis or neoplasm.

The therapeutic intervention involved sialendoscopy using a 1.1 mm sialendoscope (Figure 3), during which 32 stones (ranging from 2 to 8 mm) in the right Stensen's duct were identified and removed (Figure 4). Initially, probe dilation was performed to gently widen the narrowed duct to 8 mm, facilitating access to the largest 11 mm stone. Stone extraction was performed in a multistage approach: the largest stone was removed first with forceps, followed by smaller stones gradually extracted using baskets and micro‐instruments. This multistage approach, conducted over 90 min under general anesthesia, enhanced precision and safety, with no intraoperative complications reported. Compositional analysis of the extracted stones revealed calcium phosphate and carbonate in combination with an organic matrix of glycoproteins and mucopolysaccharides, consistent with typical parotid calculi.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Validated outcome measures were used to evaluate treatment success, including the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for pain, Saxon's test for salivary flow, and the Chronic Obstructive Sialadenitis Symptoms Questionnaire (COSS‐Q) for patient‐reported outcomes. Preventive measures included adequate hydration (2 L of water daily) and sugar‐free chewing gum to stimulate salivary flow, initiated immediately post‐procedure and continued indefinitely. Follow‐up at 2 weeks revealed resolution of pain and swelling, with salivary flow restored to normal and comparable to the contralateral side. At 12 months, ultrasound confirmed the absence of residual stones, ductal dilatation, or parenchymal abnormalities. The patient fully adhered to the recommended preventive measures, and no adverse events such as ductal injury, bleeding, or infection were reported. No unanticipated events, including facial nerve dysfunction, were observed.

Discussion

The approach to this case demonstrated several strengths, including the successful implementation of sialendoscopy with balloon dilatation and multistage stone extraction, effectively managing a complex scenario involving 32 stones, including a large 11 mm stone, without intraoperative or postoperative complications. Preoperative ultrasound imaging enhanced diagnostic accuracy and enabled precise procedural planning, while the patient's adherence to preventive measures post‐intervention contributed to a favorable outcome.

Nevertheless, several limitations were evident. The procedure relied on a single operator's specialized skills, which may not be widely available. The follow‐up period, although extended to 12 months in this case, remains limited for definitive assessment of recurrence, especially given the unusually high stone burden. Additionally, the absence of comprehensive laboratory data to investigate metabolic or systemic predispositions represents a diagnostic gap. Potential risk factors for extensive stone formation in this patient could include subtle metabolic abnormalities, dietary factors, chronic dehydration, or minor salivary flow impairment, though none were identified clinically or in routine laboratory tests.

The relevant literature supports the efficacy of sialendoscopy for sialolithiasis. Koch et al. reported a success rate exceeding 85% in parotid cases and highlighted advancements in techniques such as balloon dilatation to address ductal strictures or larger stones, consistent with the approach used here [7]. Zenk et al. documented outcomes in over 1000 patients, confirming procedural safety, although cases with multiple stones remain rare and technically challenging [10]. Lommen et al. emphasized the need for tailored strategies in such complex scenarios [11]. Compared to typical cases in the literature, the 32 stones in this patient far exceed the usual range of 1 to 5 stones per case. For instance, Skalias et al. in a meta‐analysis of pediatric sialolithiasis reported successful outcomes in patients with fewer than 10 stones [8].

The 90‐min procedure duration in this case also exceeded the average 45–60 min reported in most studies [7], reflecting the high stone count and necessity of multistage extraction. In the context of complex sialolithiasis, this case emphasizes both the technical challenges of managing an exceptional stone burden and the importance of meticulous planning and specialized techniques.

The rationale for the conclusions is based on complete symptom resolution, supported by imaging findings at 12‐month follow‐up and the absence of adverse events, indicating that multistage extraction with balloon dilatation was effective and well tolerated. This case additionally contributes to the literature on complex sialolithiasis by illustrating the successful management of an unusually high number of stones, highlighting the potential need for individualized approaches, long‐term follow‐up, and consideration of underlying metabolic or systemic risk factors.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case illustrates the efficacy of advanced sialendoscopic techniques in managing complex sialolithiasis, underscores the critical role of preoperative imaging in treatment planning, and highlights patient education on preventive care as a key factor in success, providing a valuable model for addressing rare presentations with expertise and flexibility.
