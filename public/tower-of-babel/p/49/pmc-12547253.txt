Plant growth-promoting traits of Rhizophora mangle-associated bacteria and their effects on tomato (Solanum lycopersicum)
Authors: Lucumi-Banguero Ruby Stella, Bustos-Barona María Alejandra, López-Velázquez Ximena Andrea, Aguirre-Ramírez Elkin, Benítez-Campo Neyla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Bioinoculants enhanced S. lycopersicum yield, which was reflected in its growth.•All bacterial isolates exhibited nitrogen-fixing capacity, confirmed by nifH gene amplification.•First report of IAA production in N. resinovorum, A. insuavis and Curtobacterium sp.•Potassium solubilization was observed in 72 % of strains; 90.9 % solubilized phosphate.

Introduction

Mangroves are coastal forest ecosystems found in intertidal regions at tropical and subtropical latitudes, and are composed of tree species adapted to stress conditions such as high salinity, flooding, desiccation, and anoxic soils [1]. These ecosystems harbour vast microbial diversity, including bacteria, fungi, cyanobacteria, protists and algae [2,3], with significant but still underexplored biotechnological potential. These microorganisms play essential roles in nutrient recycling and transformation, contributing to soil quality improvement [[4], [5], [6]]. Recent studies have shown that bacteria isolated from mangrove sediments and rhizospheres exhibit high potential as plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB) [7,8]. These indigenous rhizosphere bacteria increase plant growth and germination through mechanisms such as phytohormone production (indole-3-acetic acid and gibberellic acid), solubilisation of essential nutrients (potassium, phosphorus, and zinc), iron acquisition via siderophore, and nitrogen fixation [4,[7], [8], [9], [10]].

Nitrogen is essential for plants, yet they cannot assimilate it directly. This process requires the action of diazotrophic bacteria, which fix atmospheric nitrogen by converting it into ammonia (NH₃), making it available to plants [11,12]. Nitrogen fixation is catalysed by the enzyme nitrogenase, encoded by genes such as nifD, nifK, and nifH. The latter encodes the iron–molybdenum (Mo–Fe) subunit, a widely used molecular marker for studying the diversity of nitrogen-fixing microorganisms [9,13]. The importance of nitrogen in agriculture has led to the widespread use of chemical fertilisers, which, while increasing productivity, also cause adverse effects such as soil acidification, water eutrophication, and heavy metal contamination of groundwater aquifers [14,15].

Among the crops that require substantial fertiliser inputs, tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) stands out as one of the most widely cultivated horticultural crops worldwide, owing to its commercial significance and role in global dietary consumption [16,17]. Tomatoes are a key component of the human diet due to their high water content, dietary fibre, proteins, and essential nutrients such as organic acids, lipids, amino acids, and carotenoids [18]. According to FAO (2022), global fresh tomato production in 2023 reached approximately 192,317,000 tonnes, with China as the largest producer, contributing 70,119,693 tonnes [19]. In Colombia, the cultivation of S. lycopersicum var. cerasiforme extends across 21 of the country’s 32 departments, achieving an average yield of 50 tonnes per hectare, with primary export destinations including Spain, France, Aruba, Curaçao, and Cuba [19].

The environmental and agronomic concerns associated with chemical fertiliser use have driven the search for alternative approaches on the basis of the properties of plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB). These bacteria contribute to soil sustainability and agricultural productivity by enhancing plant health, facilitating nutrient availability, modulating phytohormone production, and protecting plants against biotic and abiotic stress [10,20]. These beneficial effects have been documented in various crops, including cotton, tomato, maize, and rice, improving parameters such as vigour, flowering, fruit set, and nutritional quality [4,7,21].

Despite the growing interest in this area, there is limited information on bacteria isolated from mangroves with plant growth-promoting properties, particularly when inoculated into economically and nutritionally important crops such as cherry tomato (Solanum lycopersicum var. cerasiforme). Therefore, this study aimed to analyse the plant growth-promoting capacity of bacteria isolated from Rhizophora mangle (red mangrove), the predominant mangrove species in the Colombian Pacific. Additionally, we evaluated their effects on the growth of S. lycopersicum var. cerasiforme, testing these bacteria as inoculants, both individually and in combination, under greenhouse conditions.

Materials and methods

Sampling

Samples were collected from the rhizosphere of three Rhizophora mangle individuals at two sites located at the mouth of the Dagua River, in front of Buenaventura Bay (Station 1: 3°51′25.9″N, 77°04′16.9″W; Station 2: 3°51′5.161″N, 77°3′39.41"W). Rhizoplane samples were obtained by gently rubbing the aerial roots with a sterile cotton swab and transferring them into sterile conical tubes (15 mL) containing Synthetic Seawater (SSW) to ensure stable conditions during sample transport from an environment with moderate salinity (17.5–18.5 PSU), avoiding variations that could affect microbial viability, as recommended by Henson [22]. For the rhizosphere soil samples, a 10 cm deep hole was dug near the mangrove roots via a sterile hand auger, and the soil was placed in airtight plastic bags. All samples were stored in a portable cooler at 4 °C and transported to the Microbiological Research Laboratory (LIM) of the Department of Biology, University of Valle (Cali, Colombia), for further analysis.

Planting and isolation of nitrogen-fixing bacteria

For the isolation of diazotrophic bacteria, samples were pooled according to the collection zone (rhizoplane or rhizosphere soil). From each composite sample, serial dilutions (10⁻²–10⁻⁵) were prepared, and 0.1 mL of each dilution was inoculated onto Petri dishes containing Norris Glucose Nitrogen-Free Medium (NGNF; HiMedia, India) agar or Ashby agar [14], both selective nitrogen-free media. The plates were incubated at 28 °C for 5 days.

In parallel, 10 mL of each composite sample was transferred into Erlenmeyer flasks containing 90 mL of NGNF or Ashby broth, which were incubated at 28 °C and 150 rpm for 7 days. After incubation, serial dilutions up to 10⁻⁴ were performed, and 0.1 mL of each dilution was inoculated onto NGNF or Ashby agar, depending on the initial enrichment medium. Plates were incubated at 28 °C for 5 days.

Colony growth was evaluated macroscopically, and morphologically distinct colonies were purified by streak plating onto the same selective medium used for initial growth and subsequently onto nutrient agar (NA). The purity of each culture was confirmed by Gram staining.

Molecular identification of bacterial isolates

Genomic DNA from the isolated bacteria was extracted from pure cultures using the Monarch Genomic DNA Extraction and Purification Kit (New England Biolabs, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (New England Biolabs, 2023). The bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR with the universal primers 63F (CAGGCCTAACACATGCAAGTC) and 1387R (GGGCGGWGTGTACAAGGC), generating a fragment of approximately ∼1300 bp [23]. The PCR was performed in a total volume of 25 μL containing 1X reaction buffer (New England Biolabs, USA), 200 μM of each dNTP (New England Biolabs), 0.2 μM of each primer, 1.25 U Taq DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs), and ≤1 μg of template DNA. Thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 30 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 1 min, and 68 °C for 1 min 20 s, with a final extension at 68 °C for 10 min. PCR products were checked by electrophoresis on a 1 % agarose gel prepared in 1X TAE buffer, stained with GelRed (Biotium, USA), and visualised using a gel documentation system (Bio-Rad Universal Hood II). The amplified products were purified using the sodium acetate precipitation method.

Finally, PCR amplicons were sent to Macrogen (South Korea) for Sanger sequencing. Sequence similarity searches were conducted using the BLAST program against the NCBI database. To support taxonomic classification of bacterial strains, phylogenetic relationships were inferred from partial 16S rRNA gene sequences. Escherichia coli (LC848137.1) was included as an outgroup to root the phylogenetic tree. Sequence alignment was conducted with the MUSCLE algorithm [24], and Bayesian phylogenetic inference was performed in BEAST2 (v2.7.8) [25] under the Jukes–Cantor (JC) substitution model [26]. The robustness of the tree topology was assessed through a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analysis run for 10 million generations to ensure convergence.

Evaluation of plant growth promotion characteristics

Nitrogen fixation determination

To evaluate the nitrogen-fixing capacity of the bacterial isolates in nitrogen-free media, three successive inoculations were performed using the streak plate method on Ashby and NGNF agar plates. Each inoculation was incubated at 28 °C for 48 h. Bacterial growth was classified as abundant (growth present on all four streaks), moderate (growth on at least two streaks), or sparse (growth only on the first streak). Isolates that failed to grow after successive transfers were considered non-diazotrophic and discarded.

Amplification of the nifH gene

DNA from each bacterial isolate underwent via PCR amplification with the following primer pairs: Ueda19F/R6, IGK3/DVV, PolF/PolR, nifH2/R6, F2/R6, and nifH2/nifH1. The PCR conditions were followed as described by the authors in Table 1. Each reaction was performed in a final volume of 25 μL containing 1 × PCR buffer (New England Biolabs, USA), 200 μM of each dNTP (New England Biolabs), 0.2 μM of each primer (Macrogen, South Korea), 1.25 U of Taq DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs), and ≤1 μg of template DNA. Thermal cycling conditions included an initial denaturation at 94–95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30–35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30–60 s, annealing at 44–60 °C for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. PCR products were analysed by electrophoresis on a 2 % agarose gel prepared in 1 × TAE buffer, stained with GelRed (Biotium, USA), and visualised using a gel documentation system (Bio-Rad Universal Hood II). Siderophore production

Siderophore production was assessed using Chrome Azurol S (CAS) agar, following the protocol of Louden, Haarmann, and Lynne [32]. Inoculation was performed in duplicate. A colony from a fresh culture was collected with a sterile swab and streaked onto CAS medium. Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 was used as a positive control [33,34]. Plates were incubated at 28 °C for seven days. Siderophore production was interpreted by a color change from the characteristic blue of the medium to violet/purple (catecholate), yellow (carboxylate), or orange (hydroxamate) [4,32,35].

Determination of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA)

Bacterial isolates were inoculated, in triplicate, into Erlenmeyer flasks containing 10 mL of King B broth (Condalab) supplemented with 2.5 mM tryptophan and incubated for five days at 28 °C with shaking at 150 rpm. After incubation, cultures were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min, and 1 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 1 mL of Salkowski reagent [36]. The mixture was vortexed and incubated in the dark for 30 min, after which absorbance was measured at 530 nm in a QLS UV-5800 PC spectrophotometer. Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) concentration was determined from a standard curve in the range 0–200 µg/mL. All assays were performed in triplicate.

Evaluation of the phosphate solubilisation capacity

Bacterial isolates were inoculated in duplicate onto National Botanical Research Institute’s Phosphate (NBRIP) medium by spot inoculation using sterile sticks [[37], [38], [39]] and incubated at 28 °C for 24 h. The appearance of a yellow–orange halo around the colony was considered a positive result. The phosphate solubilisation index (SI) was calculated as the ratio of the total diameter (colony + halo) to the colony diameter.

Evaluation of potassium solubilisation capacity

The inoculation procedure described for phosphate solubilisation was applied using the Aleksandrov medium for potassium solubilisation [40]. Colonies that developed a yellow–orange halo were considered indicative of potassium solubilisation, and the solubilisation index (SI) was calculated as described for phosphate solubilisation.

Selection of bacteria for greenhouse trials with solanum lycopersicum

In order to determine the bacterial strains with the highest potential as plant growth promoters, a mixed data factorial analysis (FAMD) was performed [41], which allowed for the simultaneous analysis of both quantitative and qualitative variables related to nitrogen fixation, siderophore production, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) production, and phosphate and potassium solubilisation. The first dimensions of the FAMD were retained to explain at least 70–80 % of the total variability were retained, which makes it possible to capture the general structure of the data without including dimensions that provide redundant information or noise. Additionally, the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalue> 1) was applied, as these dimensions represent more variance than does a single standard original variable [42,43]. Hierarchical clustering analysis (HCPC) was performed on the factorial axes to group the isolates into clusters of similar properties. All analyses were conducted via R software version 4.2.2.

Additionally, an antagonistic assay was performed between the selected strains. A horizontal streak of one strain was made in the central part of a nutrient agar Petri dish, and a vertical streak of the other strain was drawn, forming a cross. The plates were incubated at 28 °C for 24 h. The intersection zone was then examined to detect whether bacterial growth inhibition was present [8].

Determination of the influence of selected bacterial strains on the growth of S. lycopersicum

Experimental setup

Two trials were conducted: the first took place from January to March 2023 and used Cellulosimicrobium sp. RFN73 and Klebsiella sp. RFN75. The second trial was carried out between May and August 2024 and employed R. terrigena RFN53 and K. variicola RFN55. Each trial followed a completely randomised design, consisting of four treatments: two with individual strains, a third with a combination of both strains, and a control without bacterial inoculum. Each treatment included 20 replicates, resulting in a total of 80 experimental units. The relative humidity and ambient temperature ranged from 43 % to 85 % and from 24.3 °C to 38.6 °C, respectively.

Inoculum preparation

Two to three colonies of each bacterial culture were inoculated into 250 ml of LB broth in 500 ml Erlenmeyer flasks, which were incubated at 28 °C for 14 h with shaking at 130 rpm. The cultures were subsequently centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min. The pellets of each strain were resuspended in 10 ml of Sterile Saline Solution (SSS) and washed twice with SSS, following the same centrifugation program. The pellets were allowed to drain for 5 min, after which the wet weight was recorded, and the concentrations were standardised with SSS. For the mixed inoculum preparation, equal volumes of both strains were combined in a sterile tube. Finally, the concentration was verified to be between 10⁸ and 10⁹ CFU/ml via the standard plate count method.

Seed preparation

The Solanum lycopersicum var. cerasiforme seeds were washed with 2 % sodium hypochlorite for 5 min, rinsed with sterile distilled water, and then with 70 % alcohol for 3 min. The seeds were subsequently placed in humid chambers and incubated for 5 days in a growth chamber (HiPoint) at 28 °C and 30 % relative humidity. Finally, the seeds for the treatments were inoculated with 2 ml of the corresponding bacterial inoculum, while the control seeds were immersed in SSS for 2 h.

Sowing to seedbeds

The germinated seeds were sown in seedbeds prepared with a 1:1 mixture of black soil and rice husk. Three Solanum lycopersicum var. cerasiforme seeds were placed in each cavity. The first irrigation was carried out with 1 ml of bacterial inoculum according to the treatment type, while the control was irrigated with 1 ml of sterile potable water. The seeds were incubated for 20 days under the conditions described for the seeds.

Transplantation

The seedlings were subsequently transplanted into 1 kg plastic bags containing the same type of substrate described for the seedbed. At the time of transplantation, 1 ml of bacterial inoculum was applied directly to the rhizosphere of each seedling. The experiment was conducted in the greenhouse of the Experimental Station of the Department of Biology at the University of Valle, Meléndez Campus, in Cali, Colombia.

Measurement of response variables

Weekly measurements were taken for stem diameter (cm), chlorophyll index ( %), and leaf nitrogen percentage ( %) on three plants per treatment were randomly selected. The chlorophyll index was measured via a SPAD-502 Plus chlorophyll meter, and the leaf nitrogen percentage was calculated via the equation proposed by Rodríguez et al. Every two weeks, measurements were taken from three plants per treatment, including the total plant length, diameter of the main root (cm), leaf area (cm²), fresh weight (g), and dry weight (g). Fresh weight was determined immediately after each plant was harvested, and dry weight was determined after the plants were dried in an oven at 70 °C for 72 h.

Statistical analysis

The analysis of the response variables was performed using the RStudio software, version 4.2.2. Two-way factorial analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to assess the effects of the treatments, time, and their interaction. For each variable, a conventional ANOVA was conducted, checking the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances by analysing the residuals of the model. Tukey’s multiple comparison test (α = 0.05) was subsequently performed to identify significant differences between treatments.

Results

Isolation and identification of bacteria

A total of 26 bacterial strains were isolated using nitrogen-free media from both the rhizosphere and rhizoplane of Rhizophora mangle. Molecular identification based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing revealed affiliation with 13 distinct genera. The most frequently isolated genus was Raoultella (7 strains), followed by Enterobacter (5 strains) and Klebsiella (4 strains). Single isolates were identified from the genera Achromobacter, Buttiauxella, Cellulosimicrobium, Curtobacterium, Delftia, Exiguobacterium, Kosakonia, Novosphingobium, Pantoea, and Pseudomonas (Table 2; Supplementary Table S1).

The phylogenetic reconstruction revealed well-defined clades that reflect the evolutionary relationships among the isolates, consistent with the BLAST-based taxonomic assignments. (Supplementary Figure S1). A strongly supported clade (posterior probability = 100 %) grouped the isolates R. terrigena RFN38, RFN53, RFN52, RFN58, RFN63, RFN65, and RFN54, confirming their close phylogenetic affinity. Similarly, K. variicola RFN55, RFN56, RFN61, and RFN75 formed a distinct and well-supported cluster (posterior probability = 100 %).

Isolates of Enterobacter RFN39, RFN41, RFN48, RFN49, and RFN50 were positioned in closely related branches, indicating intra-generic diversity within the group. Additional taxa such as K. cowanii RFN70, Pectobacterium cypripedii RFN60, Buttiauxella sp. RFN76, A. insuavis RFN71, Delftia sp. RFN74, N. resinovorum RFN36, Curtobacterium sp. RFN72, Cellulosimicrobium sp. RFN73, and E. acetylicum RFN64 were each located in distinct clades of the phylogenetic tree.

Siderophore production

Among the 26 isolates, 20 produced siderophores, with a predominance of the hydroxamate type in 17. This type of siderophore was detected in all isolates of R. terrigena (RFN38, RFN52, RFN53, RFN54, RFN58, RFN63, RFN65), three of Enterobacter sp. (RFN39, RFN48, RFN49), two of K. variicola (RFN55, RFN56), Achromobacter insuavis (RFN71), Kosakonia kowani (RFN70), and Pantoea cypripedii (RFN60). On the other hand, catecholate-type siderophores were identified in isolates of Klebsiella variicola (RFN61), Exiguobacterium acetylicum (RFN64), and Buttiauxella sp. (RFN76) (Table 2).

Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) production

All 26 bacterial isolates exhibited the ability to produce indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), with concentrations ranging from 1.31 to 366.97 μg/ml. A total of 23 % of the isolates presented high IAA production (151–225 μg/ml), while 15.4 % presented the highest levels (>225 μg/ml). The isolates RFN55, RFN56, and RFN39 of Klebsiella variicola presented the highest IAA production, with values of 366.97, 263.41, and 242.39 μg/ml, respectively (Fig. 2a).Fig. 2Results by range for the tests of (a) IAA production (µg/ml), (b) the phosphate solubilisation index and (c) the potassium solubilisation index in isolates from Rhizophora mangle.Fig 2

The phosphate solubilisation

The phosphate solubilisation index values among the isolates ranged from 3.83–10.50, excluding Exiguobacterium acetylicum RFN64, which did not exhibit this trait (0.00). As shown in Fig. 2b, 27 % of the isolates demonstrated high phosphate solubilisation efficiency (>7.68), with Klebsiella variicola RFN55 exhibiting the highest recorded value (10.50), followed by Enterobacter cloacae RFN41 (10.00) and Raoultella terrigena RFN53 (9.80). Additionally, 23 % of the isolates displayed intermediate solubilisation capacity (5.01–7.68), whereas another 27 % presented moderate activity (4.01–5.00).

Potassium solubilisation

Potassium solubilisation ranged from 1.34–4.92. The distribution revealed that 32 % of the isolates exhibited moderate solubilisation capacity (1.34–3.22), whereas 36 % demonstrated high efficiency (3.23–4.42), with Raoultella terrigena RFN38 reaching the highest value in this range (4.42). Only 4 % of the isolates exceeded the 4.42 threshold, with Klebsiella variicola RFN55 (4.86) and Buttiauxella sp. RFN76 (4.92) displaying the highest solubilisation values. Seven isolates did not exhibit this activity (Fig. 2c).

Selection of bacteria for solanum lycopersicum greenhouse trials

Factorial Analysis of Mixed Data (FAMD) identified three distinct clusters based on the physiological and biochemical characteristics of the isolates (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S1; Supplementary Table S2). Cluster 1 (blue) comprised strains with sparse growth in nitrogen-free media, predominantly producing siderophores and exhibiting a low proportion of potassium-solubilising strains. Cluster 2 (yellow) included isolates with abundant growth in nitrogen-free medium, where non-siderophore producers predominated, and moderate IAA production was observed. Meanwhile, Cluster 3 (grey) grouped isolates that generally showed moderate to abundant growth in nitrogen-free media and siderophore production. From this cluster, R. terrigena RFN53 and K. variicola RFN55 were selected as they exhibited abundant growth in both media together with high IAA production and phosphate and potassium solubilisation. Discussion

The taxonomic diversity revealed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing underscores the richness of diazotrophic bacteria associated with R. mangle. The genera Raoultella, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas, Enterobacter, and Exiguobacterium have been found in various environments, including mangroves [45]. Previous studies have detected these microorganisms in other environments, where they demonstrated plant growth-promoting (PGPR) characteristics, such as nitrogen fixation, siderophore production, and phosphate solubilization [7,17,46].

All bacterial isolates in this study exhibited IAA production and nitrogen fixation. IAA not only promotes plant growth but also affects the soil pH, increasing the availability of essential nutrients. Furthermore, this compound influences the development of other microorganisms, creating favourable or unfavourable conditions for the microbial community [8,47]. Additionally, IAA plays a crucial role in key processes such as cell differentiation and vascular tissue regeneration [7,11,48]. The characterization of the ability to produce IAA is well studied in plant growth-promoting bacteria, especially in genera such as Pseudomonas, Enterobacter, Klebsiella, and Agrobacterium, which are also shown to be efficient in the biosynthesis of this phytohormone [11,17,21].

In this study, production of IAA was confirmed in many several of these bacteria, such as Buttiauxella sp., E. cloacae, K. variicola, E. acetylicum, R. terrigena, Delftia sp., P. cypripedii, and K. kowani, corroborating previous studies científica [11,45,[47], [48], [49], [50], [51], [52]]. Conversely, species such as N. resinovorum, A. insuavis, and Curtobacterium sp. had not been previously reported as IAA producers, suggesting that this may be the first report of this property for these microorganisms, specifically also under tryptophan addition. The combined findings extend our knowledge of metabolic diversity for mangrove-associated microorganisms and their potential as plant growth-promoting bacteria.

Nitrogen is an important structural element in chlorophyll, proteins, and nucleic acids, all of which are crucial for plant growth and development [48] . This importance is even greater when considering the productivity of the mangrove ecosystem, where, in addition to nutrient cycling, nitrogen fixation promotes plant uptake of nitrogen [12,13]. In this study, we have demonstrated the growth of bacterial isolates in Ashby and NGNF media and employed identification of the nifH gene associated with nitrogen fixation [9,13,53], thereby confirming that the isolated microorganisms had the capacity to fix, or reduce this nutrient. These findings align with previous studies reporting the nitrogen-fixing capacity of the genera identified in this study, highlighting their key role in plant growth promotion [17,46,48,[54], [55], [56], [57]].

Moreover, the isolated bacteria were capable of solubilising phosphate and potassium which are two important macronutrients for plant growth and development. In mangrove ecosystems specifically, phosphorus can precipitate with various cations within the water, limiting its availability to plant uptake [13,46,53]. Likewise, potassium is abundant in the soil, but between 90 and 98 % of it is found in mineral forms that cannot be used by plants [40,58]. The bacteria’s ability to solubilise these nutrients is crucial for enhancing plant productivity across ecosystems by increasing the availability of both essential elements. This study, the genera Achromobacter, Buttiauxella, Cellulosimicrobium, Curtobacterium, Delftia, Enterobacter, Kosakonia, Klebsiella, Novosphingobium, Pseudomonas and Raoultella demonstrated this capacity, which are consistently identified in previous reports as being phosphate and potassium solubilising bacteria [45,54,55,[59], [60], [61]].

Another observed mechanism in the isolates was the production of siderophores, which can promote plant growth through increasing iron availability in environments where this nutrient is limited [8,62]. Additionally, siderophores are biocontrol mechanisms because they limit phytopathogenic bacteria and fungi from utilizing iron, necessary for their development [38,47,62]. In this study, the isolates produced hydroxamate- and catecholate-type siderophores (Table 2), which are among the most common in nature [4,32,35]. The production of different siderophore types by the isolates obtained in this study reflects the functional diversity of mangrove-associated bacterial microflora, suggesting significant potential for their application in plant growth promotion and biocontrol strategies.

The results from both experiments demonstrate that bacterial inoculation can positively influence the growth of Solanum lycopersicum, although the effects varied depending on the strain or combination of strains applied. The results of the first trial demonstrated that Cellulosimicrobium sp. RFN73 exhibited plant growth-promoting effects on S. lycopersicum var. cerasiforme, the treatment with RFN73 stood out for its relatively high growth parameter values, which aligns with previous studies reporting its ability to increase plant development through mechanisms such as biological nitrogen fixation [63,64]. Moreover, Klebsiella sp. RFN75 also exhibited positive effects, albeit to a lesser extent, consistent with previous studies reporting its ability to produce catecholate-type siderophores and synthesize phytohormones [65,66]. The combination of both strains had an intermediate effect, suggesting potential synergy between these microorganisms under the evaluated conditions [11,[67], [68], [69]].

In contrast, in the second trial, the combination of R. terrigena RFN53 and K. variicola RFN55 had significant effects (p < 0.05) on four of the seven evaluated variables, whereas the individual treatments did not significantly differ from the control. This pattern aligns with previous studies demonstrating how microbial consortia can increase plant growth through nutrient acquisition, bioactive compound production, and adaptation to adverse conditions [11,67,69].. The observed differences in growth parameters between bacterial mixtures suggest that these interactions are highly specific and dependent on plant physiology, interactions with the surrounding microbiota, and environmental conditions [45,70].

The lack of significant effects in the individual treatments of the second trial with R. terrigena RFN53 and K. variicola RFN55 contrasts with previous findings highlighting the efficiency of Raoultella and Klebsiella as plant growth-promoting bacteria owing to their ability to produce phytohormones, solubilise nutrients, and synthesise siderophores [11,17,71]. This discrepancy could be attributed to strain-specific differences, interactions with the native rhizosphere microbiota, and, most importantly, experimental conditions. Although both trials evaluated bacteria isolated from R. mangle, the differences in their effects underscore the importance of strain specificity when designing bioinoculation strategies. Future studies should further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying these interactions and assess the performance of these strains under different environmental conditions and in other tomato varieties to determine their potential as commercial biofertilisers.

Given the potential application of Klebsiella and Raoultella strains as biofertilizers, it is essential to consider biorisk assessment, as both genera include species associated with opportunistic infections in humans [72,73] However, several studies have shown that environmental strains, particularly those isolated from soils and rhizospheres, often display plant growth-promoting (PGP) traits—such as nitrogen fixation, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) production, phosphate solubilization, and siderophore synthesis—without carrying clinically relevant virulence genes [74,75]. For example, Klebsiella variicola subsp. variicola ZH07, isolated from peanut rhizosphere, exhibited strong PGP activity and the capacity to degrade soil autotoxins, while whole-genome sequencing confirmed the absence of pathogenicity-related genes or resistance determinants typically found in clinical strains [74]. These findings support the importance of distinguishing between pathogenic and non-pathogenic strains within the same genus when considering agricultural applications.

With regard to Raoultella, although it has been historically considered less virulent than Klebsiella, clinical reports have documented human infections caused by R. planticola and R. ornithinolytica, mostly in immunocompromised individuals [73,75]. Nevertheless, several environmental isolates of Raoultella have shown promising PGP characteristics, and recent genomic studies have reported low levels of virulence and resistance genes in strains from soils and plant-associated environments [[75], [76], [77]].

Therefore, while the results of this study highlight the beneficial effects of some Klebsiella and Raoultella strains on S. lycopersicum growth, we acknowledge the importance of comprehensive biorisk assessments prior to field application. These should include phenotypic assays (e.g., biofilm formation, antibiotic susceptibility) and whole-genome analysis to detect potential virulence or resistance determinants, in accordance with current safety guidelines for microbial-based agricultural products.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that mangroves in the Colombian Pacific harbor bacteria with high potential as plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB), particularly those associated with R. mangle. The identified bacterial genera exhibited notable capacities for the production of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and siderophores; nitrogen fixation; and the solubilisation of essential nutrients such as phosphate and potassium. The bioinoculant effects of Cellulosimicrobium sp. RFN73 and the combination of R. terrigena RFN53 + K. variicola RFN55 on S. lycopersicum seedlings were particularly noteworthy. Given the taxonomic affiliation of some isolates with opportunistic microorganisms, such as Klebsiella and Raoultella, biosafety assessments—both phenotypic and genomic—are essential prior to field application. These findings highlight the value of bacteria associated with this ecosystem as potential biofertilizers to enhance agricultural productivity, supporting the development of more sustainable crop management strategies.
