Structural and allosteric effects of the membrane-bound PI3Kα H1047R mutation and opportunities for drug discovery
Authors: Danai Maria Kotzampasi, Zoe Cournia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PI3Kα is a lipid kinase that regulates cell growth and survival via the PI3Kα/Akt pathway. As a peripheral membrane protein, PI3Kα is activated at the membrane, however, its active conformation remains unknown. To investigate how PI3Kα and its most prevalent oncogenic mutation, H1047R, interact with the membrane, we performed Molecular Dynamics simulations of active-like PI3Kα WT and H1047R mutant in complex with HRAS on a model membrane. Our simulations reveal that the H1047R mutation enhances substrate coordination, promotes catalytic interactions, and induces an open C-terminal conformation that stabilizes the protein on the membrane. Moreover, H1047R strengthens allosteric coupling between the C-terminus and membrane-binding loop 2 in both solution and membrane-bound states. WT and mutant proteins adopt distinct conformational ensembles on the membrane, enabling structure-based prediction of allosteric pockets at the protein-membrane interface. These findings offer a mechanistic basis for the H1047R enhanced membrane-binding ability and insights for new therapeutic strategies targeting the protein-membrane interface.

Introduction

Class I phosphoinositide 3-kinase alpha isoform (PI3Kα) is a lipid kinase central to the activation of the Akt/mTOR signaling cascade, a pathway essential for regulating cell proliferation, growth, and survival. Upon binding to the activated receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) and activated RAS at the plasma membrane, PI3Kα catalyzes the phosphorylation of phosphatidylinositol (4,5)-bisphosphate (PIP2) at the 3′-position, generating phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-trisphosphate (PIP3)1,2. PIP3 acts as a critical second messenger, triggering the recruitment and activation of multiple signaling proteins and kinases that drive cellular growth and biosynthesis3.

PI3Kα is a heterodimer composed of a catalytic p110α subunit and a regulatory p85α subunit, encoded by the PIK3CA and PIK3R1 genes, respectively (Fig. 1A). The catalytic p110α subunit contains several domains with distinct functional roles: an adapter-binding domain (ABD) mediating association with p85α, a Ras-binding domain (RBD) that interacts with RAS and stabilizes PI3Kα at the membrane4,5, a C2 domain contributing to membrane engagement, a helical domain interacting with p85α, and a kinase domain harboring binding sites for both ATP and PIP2 (Fig. 1A, B). Notably, PIK3CA is among the most commonly mutated oncogenes in human solid tumors, with activating mutations broadly distributed across its sequence2,6. These mutations predominantly cluster within exons 9 and 20, which encode the helical and kinase domains, respectively, and give rise to two hotspot mutants: E542K and E545K at the helical domain, which modulates the catalytic and regulatory subunit interactions7,8, and H1047R at the kinase domain, which modulates membrane interactions of the PI3Kα active state9,10 (Fig. 1C). Activating PIK3CA mutations drive oncogenic signaling by increasing PIP3 levels, promoting tumorigenesis and metastasis. These mutations occur in approximately 36% of breast cancers15, ~30% of endometrial cancers16, and 20–25% of colorectal cancers17, and therefore, PI3Kα has emerged as a promising therapeutic target. However, despite extensive efforts to develop PI3Kα-targeted therapies, many inhibitors exhibit limited isoform selectivity and produce dose-limiting toxicities18–20. Resistance mechanisms, often mediated by feedback regulation within the PI3K/Akt network20, further undermines treatment efficacy21. To date, two PI3Kα-targeted drugs have received FDA approval: Alpelisib, a p110α-selective inhibitor used in breast cancer therapy22, and Inavolisib (GDC-0077), an isoform-selective PI3Kα inhibitor, which promotes degradation of mutant p110α variants such as E545K and H1047R with an unclear mechanism. While both drugs show clinical promise, they are ATP-competitive inhibitors and still suffer from off-target effects due to the conserved nature of ATP-binding pockets across PI3K isoforms. This has sparked interest in developing allosteric inhibitors that do not bind to the active site and may offer improved specificity and safety profiles. Among the most promising allosteric inhibitors are STX-47823, currently in Phase 1/2 clinical trials for advanced breast and gynecologic malignancies, and RLY-260824, in early-phase trials exploring monotherapy and combination strategies for solid tumors. A deeper understanding of PI3Kα structural dynamics, particularly at the protein-membrane interface, is critical for guiding the rational design of such next-generation inhibitors. Targeting the protein-membrane interface presents a novel, promising therapeutic approach, as PI3Kα activation is directly linked to membrane recruitment, and modulating the membrane-binding regions may allosterically influence the active site. Therefore, insight into PI3Kα allosteric pathways and its mechanism of action will facilitate the design of mutant-specific inhibitors that can selectively regulate membrane binding and activation.

It was recently shown using Fluorescence Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET) experiments that the oncogenic H1047R mutation enhances the protein-lipid normalized FRET signal by ~75% in the phosphopeptide-activated PI3Kα states, which explains the increased membrane recruitment of the H1047R mutant. Also, hydrogen deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HDX-MS) studies were used to identify key membrane-interacting regions of PI3Kα WT, including residues 716-744 (membrane-binding loop 1), 848-859 (active site), 859-872 (membrane-binding loop 2), 930-956 (activation loop), and 1039-1068 (C-terminus)2,11,25,26. Moreover, the cryo-EM structure of an activated PI3Kα state indicated that upon activation, the ABD domain and the p85α regulatory subunit are mobile relative to the rest of the catalytic subunit, and they cannot be observed in the obtained 2D images of the p110α catalytic core27. Although extensive knowledge exists regarding the active site, the specific contribution of individual residues to ATP hydrolysis and substrate phosphorylation remains undefined. Biochemical studies have shown that K802 contributes to phosphate transfer28,29, while residues such as K942 and K949 within the activation loop are necessary for lipid substrate recognition30. Additionally, R916, part of the conserved DRH motif in the catalytic loop, is essential for kinase activity31. Utilizing site-directed mutagenesis, kinetic assays, and quantitative mass spectrometry, Maheshwari et al.32 mapped key residues implicated in substrate recognition and catalysis, including K776 in the P-loop, which is essential for ATP and lipid binding. Mutations such as H917A and H936A, located in the catalytic and activation loops, respectively, were found to abrogate PI3Kα activity32. Nevertheless, the exact molecular mechanism of PI3Kα membrane binding and the conformational changes induced by oncogenic mutations on the membrane remain unresolved. Integrating structural and computational studies using Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations, it may be possible to investigate the detailed underlying molecular interactions on complex protein-membrane systems at the atomic level. Although several MD simulations of PI3Kα have been published over the past decade2,5,7–10,24,33,34, the mechanisms underlying its interaction with the plasma membrane, especially in the context of oncogenic mutations, remain poorly characterized. To examine the active-like structure of PI3Kα at the membrane interface and the structural ensembles induced by the H1047R mutant, we constructed and simulated systems related to the active state of PI3Kα, ΔABD p110α, WT, and H1047R in complex with HRAS on a model membrane (Fig. 1D). Using unbiased MD simulations, we assessed the structural and dynamic features of PI3Kα membrane engagement, aiming to investigate the membrane-binding mechanisms of the WT and H1047R mutant proteins. The present findings advance the understanding of membrane-binding interactions and highlight the structural and dynamical changes triggered by the H1047R mutation and the lipid bilayer. This work provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the membrane binding of PI3Kα and reveals critical insights into conformational changes that may be exploited for the design of mutant-specific anti-cancer therapeutics targeting the protein-membrane interface.

Results

Convergence of the WT and H1047R systems on the model membrane

Three independent, unbiased MD simulations of 600 ns each were conducted for both the WT and H1047R mutant ΔABD p110α systems in complex with HRAS on the model membrane, starting from different configurations. To assess the convergence of our simulations, Root Mean Squared Deviation (RMSD) calculations and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) were performed. The RMSD curves indicated that a plateau was achieved after 200 ns for both the WT and H1047R systems (Fig. S1). Thus, for the trajectory analysis, the final 400 ns of each trajectory were used. Moreover, PCA was performed on the protein Cα atoms for each independent simulation for the WT and H1047R mutant to map the conformational space of each simulation. The overlap of the 2D projections of the trajectories of each system on the first two eigenvectors indicated that the three independent simulations of each system span the same conformational phase space (Fig. S2). Notably, the PCA plots revealed that the conformational space explored by the H1047R mutant system (Fig. S2B) was broader than that of the WT system (Fig. S2A), indicating greater conformational variability in the mutant. While all three H1047R replica simulations sampled overlapping regions along PC1, suggesting convergence in the dominant mode of motion, the spread along PC2 likely reflects subtle but reproducible differences in secondary modes of fluctuation, with PC2 accounting for 15.6% of the total variance. This variability may reflect altered dynamics in the H1047R mutant, potentially contributing to its oncogenic behavior.

Active site dynamics and substrate interactions in the WT and H1047R membrane-bound systems

Although extensive structural information is available on the active site of PI3Kα, the precise functional roles of several key residues involved in ATP hydrolysis and PIP2 phosphorylation are not fully characterized. To investigate how active site residues interact with substrates in the active site, we modeled and simulated the ΔABD p110α WT and H1047R mutant in complex with ATP and PIP2 based on prior structural data and literature describing key molecular interactions between ATP, PIP2, and conserved active-site residues28–31. For each independent MD trajectory, the distance between the PIP2 3′ inositol oxygen (O3) and the ATP γ-phosphate (PG) was monitored throughout the simulation, and distance distributions were plotted as histograms for both the WT and H1047R (Fig. 2A) systems. In the WT simulations, all three replica simulations exhibited two dominant distance peaks within the 7-10 Å range, with distributions consistently centered around 8 Å, in agreement with earlier findings by Chakrabarti et al.33. In contrast, the H1047R mutant displayed a more heterogeneous distance profile, with three distinct peaks: a peak between 4-6 Å in replica 1, a peak between 6 Å and 8 Å in replicas 1 and 3, and a peak between 8 Å and 10 Å in replicas 2 and 3. This variability in distance distribution suggests that the H1047R mutation introduces structural and dynamic changes in the active site, potentially triggering functional alterations. To further explore interactions within the active site, a comparative analysis of the hydrogen bond network between ATP, PIP2, and protein residues in each replica for both the WT and H1047R systems was conducted (Table S1). In the WT simulations, PIP2 forms hydrogen bonds with K942, a residue in the activation loop implicated in lipid substrate recognition, in 86.7% of frames in replica 1 (Fig. S3) and 61.4% in replica 3, while this interaction was absent in replica 2 (Table S1). Across all WT trajectories, ATP forms hydrogen bonds with P778 (68.0% ± 9.6%), M772 (62.0% ± 8.7%), and I932 (99.5% ± 0.5%). In the H1047R mutant system, hydrogen bonding between PIP2 and K942, which was present in the WT, showed greater variability: 82.0% in replica 3, 18.2% in replica 1, and was completely absent in replica 2, suggesting a more transient or flexible interaction. Notably, the catalytically important residue R916, part of the conserved DRH motif, was observed to engage strongly with PIP2 in replica 1 (97.0% occupancy), while this interaction was absent in the other two replicas. This residue was also found to interact with ATP in 84.7% ± 20.8% of frames across all H1047R simulations, suggesting a potential dual role in coordinating both substrates. This interaction between the PIP2 and the R916 is not present in the WT. In the H1047R replica 1 trajectory, PIP2 is recognized by K942, and ATP interacts with R916 (Fig. 2B). The distance between the PIP2 3′ inositol oxygen (O3) and the ATP γ-phosphate (PG) in this state is 6.57 Å. R916 brings PIP2 into closer proximity to the ATP molecule, with the PIP2 3′ inositol oxygen (O3) and the ATP γ-phosphate (PG) distance being 4.90 Å (Fig. 2C). The two substrates are then stabilized in close proximity, 3.70 Å, through interactions with R916 (Fig. 2D). Hydrogen bonding patterns of ATP in the H1047R mutant resembled those in the WT system in part, with interactions detected with P778 (41.2% ± 5.8%), M772 (58.8% ± 5.6%), and I932 (94.5% ± 3.0%). K802, a residue essential for phosphate transfer, was found to interact with ATP in 60.1% ± 32.4% of the frames. Low-frequency interactions between ATP and PIP2 with K776 in the P-loop were also noted in replica 1 (15.9% and 7.2%, respectively).

Collectively, these findings highlight the significance of catalytic residues K942 of the activation loop involved in lipid substrate recognition, R916 of the DRH motif with a potential role in coordinating both ATP and PIP2, and K802, a residue essential for phosphate transfer, in substrate positioning and catalytic function. These findings are consistent with previous biochemical and structural studies28–33. The altered interaction patterns observed in the H1047R mutant suggest a shift in substrate coordination dynamics. The H1047R mutation introduces greater heterogeneity in substrate binding modes compared to the WT, as evidenced by the variable PIP2-K942 interactions in the mutant system-82.0% in replica 3, 18.2% in replica 1, and absent in replica 2. In contrast, the WT system shows more consistent PIP2-K942 interactions, with 86.7% occupancy in replica 1 and 61.4% in replica 3, and is absent in replica 2 (Table S1). Additionally, R916, which does not engage with the substrates in the WT, interacts with PIP2 in 97.0% of frames in H1047R replica 1 and with ATP in 84.7% ± 20.8% of frames across all mutant simulations, further highlighting mutation-induced diversification in substrate coordination. Functionally, this altered coordination may enhance the catalytic efficiency of the mutant by stabilizing a pre-reactive state in which ATP and PIP2 are brought into closer proximity, potentially facilitating phosphoryl transfer.

The interface between HRAS and ΔABD p110α

To explore the RAS-binding dynamics, we analyzed the flexibility of the RBD in both WT and H1047R membrane-bound systems. RMSF analysis revealed comparable flexibility in the RBD across both systems, with mean Root Mean Squared Fluctuation (RMSF) values of 1.4 ± 0.9 Å in the WT and 1.5 ± 0.7 Å in H1047R. These results suggest that the H1047R mutation does not significantly affect the intrinsic dynamics of the RBD when associated with the membrane. In parallel, we computed residue-level contact occupancies between ΔABD p110α and RAS (Fig. S4). Both WT and H1047R systems showed conserved key interactions at the RAS-PI3Kα interface. Among the most frequent contacts were residues T208, K210, and K227 of p110α with S39, E37, and D33 of RAS, respectively; these interactions were previously identified as critical for RBD-RAS binding5,35. Mutations of these interface residues, particularly T208D, K210E, and K227E, have been shown to disrupt complex formation and downstream signaling. Our simulations align with these findings, showing that these residues contribute prominently to the RAS-RBD interaction via stable interactions. Notably, the WT exhibits higher occupancies of these interactions (Fig. S4) with respect to the H1047R mutant, pointing to the direction that H1047R recruitment to the membrane is independent of RAS binding36,37. More specifically, contact analysis of the persistent contacts ( ≥ 50% occupancy in at least one system) revealed that multiple high-occupancy contacts in the WT, including Q205-Y40, K206-S39, T208-E37, K227-I36, M282-M67, K886-R73, and E888-D69, were weakened or lost in H1047R. The average change in occupancy of the persistent contacts equals −15.29% ± 40.03% in H1047R relative to WT (Fig. S4C). These results indicate a redistribution of stabilizing contacts upon mutation.

Differential flexibility and membrane interactions of PI3Kα WT and H1047R mutant

To assess the dynamics of functionally significant regions, RMSF analysis was performed on the MD trajectories of both the WT and H1047R mutant PI3Kα systems. The RMSF analysis revealed differential flexibility in two key functional regions, the membrane-binding loop 2 and the C-terminal tail, between the WT and mutant systems in the presence of the model membrane (Fig. S5). Residues 942-955 within the activation loop exhibited reduced flexibility in both WT and H1047R systems in the membrane-bound state (Fig. S5A). For the WT system, the mean RMSF decreased from 2.8 ± 1.0 Å in solution to 1.5 ± 0.6 Å upon membrane association. Similarly, for the H1047R mutant, RMSF dropped from 2.8 ± 0.7 Å in solution to 1.6 ± 0.6 Å in the membrane-bound state. Although the error bars overlap, this decreased flexibility of the proteins upon membrane association is consistent with previously reported HDX-MS data25 (Fig. S5). A complementary residue-wise minimum distance analysis to the different types of membrane lipids revealed that the activation loop in both systems is in close proximity to PIP2 in residues 941-945, with the H1047R mutant showing tighter contact across residues 947-953 (Fig. S6A). This direct proximity supports that PIP2 binding stabilizes this loop. Regarding distances to POPE and POPS, both the WT and mutant showed similar profiles.

Interestingly, residues 916-920, which are part of the catalytic loop, exhibited stabilization in the WT system upon membrane association, whereas this effect was absent in the H1047R mutant (Fig. S5B). In WT, the mean RMSF decreased from 1.3 ± 0.1 Å in solution to 0.8 ± 0.1 Å in the membrane-bound state. In contrast, the H1047R mutant showed no significant change, with RMSF values of 1.2 ± 0.1 Å in solution and 1.1 ± 0.2 Å when membrane-associated. This differential behavior may be linked to the enhanced kinase activity associated with the H1047R mutation25. Notably, the distance analysis showed that residues 916-918 of the mutant are in closer proximity to the lipids PIP2 and POPE compared to the WT (Fig. S6B).

Moreover, the protein-membrane interface residues of the WT and H1047R systems were investigated. Residues of the membrane binding loop 1 (Fig. S5C) and C2 domain (Fig. S5D), interact with the model membrane and show reduced flexibility in the membrane-bound state compared to the system in solution. Residues 720‐735 within membrane binding loop 1 exhibited reduced flexibility upon membrane association in both WT and H1047R systems. In the WT, mean RMSF decreased from 2.3 ± 0.7 Å in solution to 1.3 ± 0.2 Å in the membrane-bound state. The H1047R mutant showed a similar pattern, with RMSF values dropping from 2.8 ± 0.9 Å in solution to 1.4 ± 0.2 Å upon membrane interaction.

For the residues 343‐355 of the C2 domain, in the WT, the mean RMSF decreased from 2.1 ± 0.5 Å in solution to 1.5 ± 0.2 Å in the membrane-bound state. A similar trend was observed in the H1047R mutant, where RMSF dropped from 2.6 ± 0.5 Å in solution to 1.6 ± 0.2 Å upon membrane interaction. Consistent with this, residues in the membrane binding loop 1 and C2 domain showed minimal distances to all lipid types (Fig. S6C, D), supporting stable, lipid-mediated anchoring of this loop in both systems. These results align with the available HDX-MS experimental data25 that suggest that the regions including the C2 domain, membrane binding loops 1 and 2, and the activation loop are indeed the membrane-interacting regions of PI3Kα WT and H1047R mutant (Fig. S5). Residues 863-868 of membrane binding loop 2 show increased flexibility in the H1047R mutant in solution, compared to the WT, while residues 869-873 were stabilized in the WT protein even in the absence of a membrane, and in the H1047R mutant in both membrane-bound and solution state (Fig. S5E). In the WT system, the region 869-873 in solution exhibited a mean RMSF of 3.5 ± 1.1 Å, which further decreased to 1.7 ± 0.5 Å upon membrane binding. In contrast, the H1047R mutant displayed moderate flexibility in solution (2.2 ± 0.6 Å), which remained similar in the membrane-bound state (2.7 ± 0.6 Å). Distance analysis (Fig. S6E) revealed that both WT and H1047R remained in close contact with POPE and POPS, but the mutant exhibited increased distances to PIP2 compared to WT. These observations suggest that the H1047R mutation alters the intrinsic dynamics of membrane-binding loop 2. Overall, the increased flexibility of residues 863-868 in solution may facilitate membrane recognition, while the sustained flexibility in the membrane-bound state could support dynamic interaction with the lipid bilayer, contributing to the enhanced membrane affinity of the mutant protein.

Finally, residues 1059-1068 of the C-terminal tail exhibited markedly reduced flexibility in the membrane-bound H1047R mutant, as compared to both the WT and the H1047R mutant in solution and the membrane-bound WT (Fig. S5F). In the membrane-bound H1047R system, the mean RMSF was 2.3 ± 0.4 Å, significantly lower than in the H1047R solution system (7.0 ± 2.6 Å). Distance analysis (Fig. S6F) indicated that the H1047R mutant maintains consistently shorter distances to PIP2, POPE, and POPS, whereas the WT displayed greater fluctuations and a more distant average profile, indicating tighter lipid association in the mutant at the C-terminus. These changes are consistent with the experimentally observed increase in membrane binding of the H1047R mutant by Surface Plasmon Resonance9 and FRET experiments11,25 and indicate that the C-terminal region may play a modulatory role in stabilizing membrane interactions.

Structural reorganization of the C-terminus enables membrane binding in PI3Kα H1047R mutant

To further explore the alterations in C-terminal flexibility observed in the ΔABD p110α H1047R mutant in the membrane-bound state, structural clustering was performed on the Cα atoms of the C-terminal residues (residues 1032-1068) from each independent, unbiased MD trajectory. This analysis was conducted for both WT and H1047R systems in the presence of the model membrane to identify representative conformations of the C-terminal region.

In the WT system, the C-terminal tail reverts to a closed conformation, wherein the kα12 helix is positioned in proximity to the kα11 helix (Fig. 3A). H1047R mutation enhances allosteric communication between the C-terminal tail and membrane-binding loop 2

To investigate the observed alterations in the dynamics of both the C-terminal tail and membrane-binding loop 2 in the presence of the H1047R mutation, dynamical network analysis was performed. Specifically, the shortest communication paths between the mutation site (residue 1047) and membrane-binding loop 2 (residue 870) were extracted for both WT and H1047R systems, under solution and membrane-bound conditions. This analysis was conducted on three independent replicas of each system, enabling a comparison of path length-defined as the sum of edge weights along the paths used to measure the overall coupling strength of the network, as well as specific residues involved and the number of residues (nodes) in each pathway. This comparative analysis facilitated an assessment of the influence of the H1047R mutation and the membrane on residue-residue communication within the network.

Initially, differences in the shortest paths between the ΔABD p110α WT and ΔABD p110α H1047R systems in solution were examined (Fig. S7). For the WT system in solution, shortest path lengths of 176, 275, and 400 were observed across replicas (Fig. S7A). This variability in path lengths indicated a flexible network with alternative pathways for allosteric communication between residues L870 and H1047. In the H1047R mutant, the shortest paths were consistently shorter, with path lengths of 128, 188, and 161 across replicas (Fig. S7B). The reduced variability in path lengths, compared to the WT, suggests a more established communication pathway in the mutant. Furthermore, while the WT system uses an average of 7 to 9 residues per path (Fig. S7A), the H1047R mutant consistently uses only 5-6 residues per path (Fig. S7B), fewer than the WT. Thus, it can be concluded that the H1047R mutation results in shorter, more consistent communication pathways in solution compared to the WT. These findings are consistent with our previous results, where dynamical network analysis38 was performed on the WT and H1047R mutant p110α trajectories of ref. 38 and indicated that H1047 connects to the PI3Kα membrane binding loop through a pathway of ten residues, while the H1047R mutation shortens this pathway to six residues. Overall, these findings indicate that the H1047R mutation enhances coupling between the two regions compared to the WT.

A comparison of the residues involved in these pathways revealed that, in the WT system, H1047 is connected to L870 of the membrane binding loop 2 via residues of the kα8 helix (Fig. S7A). In contrast, in the H1047R mutant systems, R1047 communicates with L870 through residues of the activation loop, with no involvement of residues from the kα8 helix (Fig. S7B). This result could be associated with the findings of our metadynamics simulations reported in ref. 6, where the H1047R mutation was observed to alter the interface between the kα8 and kα11 helices.

Differences in the shortest paths between the ΔABD p110α WT and ΔABD p110α H1047R systems on the model membrane were then examined (Fig. 4). For the WT in the membrane-bound state, path lengths of 389, 343, and 446 were observed for the different replicas (Fig. 4A). In contrast, significantly shorter paths were observed in the H1047R mutant in the membrane-bound state, with lengths of 212, 191, and 154 across replicas (Fig. 4B), indicating that the mutation strengthens coupling between the two regions compared to the WT. In the WT system, the H1047 residue, located in the C-terminal tail of PI3Kα, is connected to membrane binding loop 2 (residue 870) through pathways involving 14 residues in replica 1, 15 residues in replica 2, and 19 residues in replica 3 (Fig. 4A). These pathways include residues from the kα11 helix, kα8 helix, the activation loop, and the kinase domain. In contrast, in the H1047R mutant, the R1047 residue significantly shortens this pathway, connecting to the membrane binding loop 2 through a shorter path of 7 residues in replicas 1 and 2, and 10 residues in replica 3 (Fig. 4B), thereby allowing more direct communication between the residues. Comparing the WT in solution to the WT in the membrane-bound state, path lengths in the WT system are generally shorter in solution (176-400) (Fig. S7A) than in the membrane-bound state (343 to 446). This difference suggests that the membrane imposes structural constraints that lengthen the communication paths, possibly due to additional interactions with the membrane. For the H1047R mutant, path lengths remain consistently short in both solution (128-188) (Fig. S7B) and membrane-bound states (154-212) (Fig. 4B). This consistency suggests that the H1047R mutation stabilizes communication pathways that are less influenced by the presence of the model membrane. Additionally, in the membrane-bound state, the WT network requires significantly more residues (14-19) (Fig. 4A) compared to the solution state (7-9) (Fig. S7A), indicating that the presence of the membrane triggers a more complex communication network with additional intermediate nodes, including residues in the kα8 helix. In contrast, the H1047R mutant consistently includes fewer residues per path, with 5 to 10 residues in both solution and membrane-bound states, with no involvement of residues from the kα8 helix (Figs. S7B and 7B).

Overall, the H1047R mutation establishes a robust network between the C-terminal tail and membrane binding loop 2 that remains relatively unaffected by the presence of the model membrane and enhances allosteric communication between the two regions compared to the WT system. This finding aligns with previous observations that the H1047R mutant exhibits increased membrane-binding ability9,11,25.

Pocket site identification reveals allosteric targetable sites at the protein–membrane interface of the H1047R mutant

To explore potential allosteric sites and druggable pockets at the protein-membrane interface of the kinase, AllositePro39 was used to analyze cluster representative structures from each of the three MD replicas of WT and H1047R mutant. In the WT system, three pockets were detected at the protein-membrane interface. Pocket p1, with a volume of 873.2 Å³, comprised a cluster of residues surrounding the activation loop. Pocket p2 (538.2 Å³) was located along the C-terminal interface. Pocket p3, at 766.9 Å³, included residues of membrane-binding loop 2. The H1047R mutant, in contrast, exhibited an expanded and more diverse set of cavities with larger volumes and increased surface definition. Pocket p1, the largest with a volume of 1171.4 Å³, spanned a wide region including residues of the activation loop and the open C-terminal conformation observed in simulations. Pocket p2, at 444.5 Å³, overlapped spatially with this region but also extended toward catalytically important residues, including R916. Pockets p3 (577.7 Å³) and p4 (400.7 Å³), are localized to a region close to membrane-binding loop 2, a configuration unique to the H1047R state. A fifth pocket, p5 (164.9 Å³), consisted of a smaller set of residues bridging the catalytic loop and activation loop residues such as Y904, I913, D915, and F954.

In contrast to WT, the H1047R system consistently revealed deeper and more topologically enclosed cavities, especially around the C-terminal tail and membrane-binding loop 2, regions shown earlier in our study to exhibit enhanced allosteric coupling and membrane interaction in the presence of the oncogenic mutation. All predicted pockets are located in direct contact with the lipid bilayer, highlighting the potential to design lipid-interacting allosteric inhibitors, a novel therapeutic strategy that has remained largely unexplored, likely due to the complexity of the interface, limited structural data on protein-membrane interactions, and the absence of computational methodologies that target this interface.

While our study focused on the ΔABD p110α system to model the active-like, membrane-bound state, we conducted allosteric pocket prediction for the crystal structure containing p110α/p85α (full-length PI3Kα, PDB ID: 4OVU) to compare with the allosteric pockets at the protein-membrane interface that were predicted in the ΔABD p110α structure, and found that the pockets at the protein-membrane interface are not identified in the full-length structure. It should be noted that the presence of p85α may influence the accessibility and stability of protein–membrane interface pockets. However, as previously mentioned, in the phosphopeptide-activated state, p85α becomes highly flexible or unresolved, suggesting it may exert limited influence on the membrane-associated regions of p110α in the activated conformation27. Therefore, in the active-like, membrane-engaged model used here, the regulatory subunit is expected to have minimal impact on the specific pockets identified.

These findings underscore the potential of targeting the protein-membrane interface in peripheral membrane proteins such as PI3Kα—an area that has remained largely unexplored in structure-based drug discovery. The presence of allosteric and accessible pockets on the protein-membrane interface opens the possibility of developing allosteric inhibitors that selectively modulate membrane-stabilized oncogenic conformations of PI3Kα and disrupt the protein-membrane attachment.

Discussion

Results presented herein describe the dynamics of the membrane-bound state of ΔABD p110α WT and H1047R mutant and provide insights into the design of mutant-specific inhibitors. Unbiased MD simulations were performed for the ΔABD p110α WT and H1047R systems in complex with HRAS on a model membrane, and the performed MD simulations of both the ΔABD p110α WT and H1047R converged after 200 ns.

The investigation of the active site of the membrane-bound systems using the retrieved trajectories, showed that in the H1047R mutant system, PIP2-ATP distances were reduced compared to the WT, with distinct peaks in the distance distribution as low as 4-6 Å. In the retrieved trajectories of the H1047R mutant, it was observed that PIP2 interacts with K942 and ATP interacts with R916. The two substrates are then stabilized in close proximity, 3.70 Å, through interactions with R916, confirming that residues K942 and R916 play crucial roles in the interaction with the substrates. Moreover, in all the retrieved trajectories of both the WT and H1047R mutant, ATP showed hydrogen bonding with the functionally significant residue K802, for which it is reported that participates in the phosphate transfer reaction. The emergence of R916 as a dual-coordinating residue of both PIP2 and ATP in the mutant and the reduced reliance on interactions with K942, suggest that the mutation enables alternative, possibly more catalytically efficient, substrate-binding configurations.

Beyond the active site, the H1047R mutation affects broader structural dynamics. RMSF and clustering analyses show that the mutant exhibits enhanced membrane association, facilitated by increased rigidity in key membrane-interacting regions-especially membrane-binding loop 2 and the C-terminal tail. In both WT and H1047R, residues 942-955 in the activation loop are stabilized by interactions with membrane-embedded PIP2, aligning with experimental HDX-MS data25. However, residues 916-920 in the catalytic loop stabilize only in WT, potentially correlating with increased kinase activity of the H1047R mutant. Despite these structural changes, our simulations revealed that the RBD exhibited comparable flexibility in both WT and H1047R systems in the membrane-bound state. Consistent with prior computational models5 and experimental data35, key interface residues, including T208, K210, and K227, formed stable contacts with HRAS in both systems. However, the WT system exhibited higher occupancies of these interactions compared to the H1047R mutant, supporting that membrane recruitment of the H1047R variant occurs independently of RAS binding36,37. Additionally, membrane binding loop 1 and the C2 domain, showed reduced flexibility in the presence of the model membrane, supporting their role in membrane-binding. Residues 869-873 of membrane-binding loop 2 are stabilized in the WT without the membrane and in H1047R in the presence and absence of the membrane, suggesting that the H1047R mutation has an impact on the dynamics of membrane-binding loop 2 and may contribute to enhanced membrane-binding of the mutant. Moreover, in H1047R, residues 1059-1068 in the C-terminal tail are less flexible in the presence of the membrane compared to the WT system, which is consistent with the increased membrane-binding ability of H1047R mutant9,11,25.

To further investigate conformational changes of the C-terminal tail in the ΔABD p110α WT and H1047R mutant on a model membrane, clustering was performed on C-terminal Cα atoms. In the WT system, the C-terminus adopts a closed conformation, with the kα12 helix interacting with kα11 and the membrane-binding WIF motif forming stabilizing hydrogen bonds with Q1033 and N1044 on kα11. Conversely, in the H1047R mutant, the C-terminal tail adopts an open conformation, interacting directly with the negatively charged lipid bilayer. Here, hydrophobic residues in the WIF motif partially insert into the hydrophobic core of the membrane, and additional residues in the C-terminal tail, including the mutated R1047, interact with the lipid bilayer. These findings align with experimental data indicating enhanced membrane-binding ability for the H1047R mutant9,11,25.

These local structural effects translate into global allosteric changes. The observed differences in the dynamics of both the C-terminal tail and membrane binding loop 2 in the presence of the H1047R mutation raise the question of how these two regions allosterically communicate within the system and whether the H1047R mutation and/or the membrane impacts their communication. To address this question, dynamical network analysis was performed, and the shortest paths connecting the mutation site at residue 1047 with membrane binding loop 2 (residue 870) were extracted for both the WT and H1047R systems, in solution and on the model membrane. In solution, the WT system showed variable path lengths (176, 275, and 400) across replicas, indicating a flexible allosteric network involving 7-9 residues per path. In contrast, the H1047R mutant demonstrated consistently shorter paths (128, 188, and 161), requiring only 5-6 residues, suggesting a more established and direct communication pathway. In the membrane-bound state, WT path lengths further increased (343, 389, and 446), indicating structural constraints imposed by membrane interactions, with 14-19 residues involved, including nodes in the kα8 helix, activation loop, and kinase domain. In contrast, the H1047R mutant showed significantly shorter and more stable paths (154, 191, and 212), involving only 7-10 residues and bypassing the kα8 helix to use a direct path via the activation loop. This pathway was consistent across all replicas, indicating that the H1047R mutation enhances the coupling of residue-residue communication between the C-terminal tail and membrane binding loop 2. Interestingly, the allosteric network of H1047R remains largely unaffected by the presence of the model membrane, whereas the WT network shows weaker coupling upon membrane-binding. Overall, these findings demonstrate that the H1047R mutation triggers a more efficient communication network that remains stable and relatively unaffected by the presence of the membrane. This enhanced coupling likely contributes to the increased membrane-binding ability observed for the H1047R mutant, aligning with prior experimental data9,11,25.

To our knowledge, this is the first time that a model related to the active state of PI3Kα has been simulated on the model membrane. The present findings advance the understanding of membrane-binding interactions and highlight the structural and dynamical changes triggered by the H1047R mutation and the lipid bilayer. The mutation stabilizes membrane-binding regions and promotes a direct allosteric communication network between the membrane-binding loop 2 and the C-terminus, enhancing membrane-binding ability. Notably, pocket detection analysis using AllositePro on cluster representative structures revealed several potent allosteric sites at the protein-membrane interface in the H1047R mutant, highlighting the potential to design allosteric inhibitors targeting the protein-membrane interface.

While targeting the protein-membrane interface has traditionally been considered challenging for drug discovery due to its transient nature and limited structural characterization, recent studies show that both peripheral and integral membrane proteins can possess stable, druggable pockets at the membrane interface40–46. Small molecules that interact with the protein-membrane interfaces have been shown to either disrupt or stabilize membrane-associated states, thereby influencing protein activity in systems ranging from kinases to G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs). For peripheral membrane proteins, several examples, though still limited, report small molecules that bind membrane-binding domains and inhibit the protein–membrane interactions40–46. The first inhibitors for targeting the protein-membrane binding interactions were designed for the C2 domain of coagulation factor V and coagulation factor VIII proteins40,42,43. For integral membrane proteins, and GPCRs in particular, Hedderich et al. systematically analyzed over 550 experimental structures and identified a diverse set of allosteric binding pockets, many located at the protein-membrane interface. Several of these pockets were validated through mutagenesis, confirming their functional relevance and druggability47. Similarly, Kaneko et al. demonstrated that the positive allosteric modulator BMS-986122 binds to a membrane-embedded cleft in the μ-opioid receptor, stabilizing its fully active conformation and enhancing G-protein signaling48. Further supporting these findings, Payandeh and Volgraf emphasized that many drug targets are embedded in the phospholipid bilayer, and that binding often occurs at the protein-membrane interface, representing underexplored targets for allosteric drug design49. In the context of PI3Kα, and particularly the H1047R mutant, our identification of pockets at the protein-membrane suggests that these regions could serve as potent targets for allosteric modulator inhibitors. These results support the potential of targeting the protein-membrane interface for selective inhibition of oncogenic, membrane-bound PI3Kα conformations. From a functional standpoint, small molecules targeting the protein–membrane interface could, in principle, either disrupt or stabilize membrane association depending on their specific binding mode and effects on local dynamics. Our preliminary results show that small molecules binding at the PI3Kα-membrane interface enhance membrane-binding, stabilize a membrane-bound inactive conformation of the protein. However, one cannot exclude the possibility that a small molecule could act as a gain-of-function (GOF) modulator and stabilize an active protein conformation on the membrane. Overall, the findings of the present study highlight the potential of protein-membrane interface pockets to modulate PI3Kα function in distinct ways and support further exploration.

In the present study, our simulations focused on the ΔABD p110α subunit in complex with HRAS, omitting the ABD and p85α regulatory subunit. Although PI3Kα exists physiologically in the cytosol as a heterodimer of p110α and p85α, the exclusion of ABD and p85α was motivated by structural and functional considerations. Recent cryo-EM studies have shown that in the phosphopeptide-activated state, the ABD and p85α become highly flexible and are unresolved, suggesting that their interactions with p110α are weakened or displaced upon activation27. Because our aim was to study the membrane-engaged conformation and interactions of the active-like PI3Kα state and detect the direct effects of the H1047R mutation on membrane binding and allostery, we considered the ΔABD p110α construct of PI3Kα in our calculations. While p85α plays an important role in regulating the autoinhibited state and may influence broader allosteric interactions, its absence does not preclude meaningful insights into the active, membrane-bound conformation. Future work incorporating full-length PI3Kα, including the p85α subunit and phosphorylated RTK motifs, will be essential to capture the interplay between regulatory subunits and the membrane in greater detail and to assess how oncogenic mutations may differentially affect the full heterodimeric complex.

Together, these findings provide mechanistic insights into PI3Kα activation on the membrane and offer a promising avenue for developing selective allosteric inhibitors targeting oncogenic PI3Kα conformations in cancer therapy.

Methods

Model construction of the ΔABD p110α systems in complex with HRAS on the model membrane

Initially, the lipid bilayer was constructed in a rectangular box with a composition of 2.5 mol% PIP2 mono-protonated on the 4-phosphate (SAPI24), 2.5 mol% PIP2 mono-protonated on the 5-phosphate (SAPI25), 30.0 mol% POPS, and 65.0 mol% POPE. This bilayer was generated using the CHARMM-GUI Membrane Builder50–52 and had a surface area of 160 × 160 Å² with 440 lipids per leaflet (see the SI for the membrane setup). The membrane was then solvated with water molecules. The system was neutralized and supplemented with Na⁺ and Cl− ions to a salt concentration of 100 mM. The CHARMM36 all-atom force field was used for lipids, solvent, and ions, and TIP3P for water53. The system then underwent a 400 ns equilibration according to the CHARMM-GUI protocol51, beginning with energy minimization and progressing through a series of restrained MD simulations to stabilize the lipid environment (see the SI for the exact protocol).

Following the lipid bilayer setup, models of the active-like state of PI3Kα, ΔABD p110α WT, and the H1047R mutant were constructed. For the construction of the ΔABD p110α model WT, our previously published ΔABD p110α WT model6 was used. The H1047R mutant was built in silico by introducing a single substitution in the respective residue. Residues 1051-1068 of the C-terminus were completed using the open C-terminal conformations from our previously published ΔABD p110α WT and H1047R metadynamics simulations, respectively6.

Next, the membrane-binding regions of the kinase were determined from the literature11,25,54 and the model was placed on the model membrane according to HDX-MS data. In the generated model, the ATP and Mg2+ ions were placed in the ATP pocket using the 1E8X structure55, and the PIP2 molecule was built in the binding pocket using diC4-PIP2 as scaffold56. Then, the model of PI3Kα in complex with HRAS was built using the 1HE8 structure4. For the HRAS model, the HVR of HRAS was built based on a model of KRAS-4B from ref. 57 and palmitoylated C181 and C184. Finally, GTP was placed in the active site of HRAS and the side chain rotamers at the interface of HRAS and ΔABD p110α were modified to reflect the interactions between the PI3Kα and HRAS as reported in ref. 5. The resulting ΔABD p110α WT and H1047R complexes with HRAS on the lipid bilayer were then solvated with TIP3P water molecules and neutralized with Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. Finally, a NaCl salt concentration of 150 mM was added (see the SI for the system setup).

Setup of the unbiased MD simulations

The unbiased MD trajectories for the systems were generated with the ACEMD3 package58, using the CHARMM36m59 all-atom force field for protein atoms, the CHARMM36 all-atom force field for the rest of the system (lipids, solvent, ions), and the TIP3P potential for modeling water molecules53. All systems were relaxed by 10,000 steps of energy minimization using the steepest descent algorithm of ACEMD358. Then, the systems were equilibrated using ACEMD3, first in the NVT ensemble for 30 ns with the Langevin leap-frog integrator and the Langevin thermostat with a damping constant of 1 ps−1 at 310 K with restraints on the deprotonated PIP2 molecule in the active site (5 kcal mol−1 Å−2) and on the Cα atoms (1 kcal mol−1 Å−2). Within the first 20 ns, these force constants were reduced linearly, reaching 0 kcal mol−1 Å−2. A second equilibration followed in the NPT ensemble for 40 ns with the Langevin leap-frog integrator, a Monte Carlo isotropic barostat60 to keep the pressure constant at 1 bar, and the Langevin thermostat61 with a damping constant of 0.1 ps−1 to keep the temperature at 310 K without restraints. All bonds containing hydrogen atoms were constrained using the M-SHAKE algorithm62. The hydrogen mass was repartitioned to 4.0 au, and a timestep of 4 fs was used63. The cut-off distance for van der Waals and electrostatic interactions was set at 12 Å with a switching function applied beyond 10 Å. Long-range electrostatic interactions were handled using the particle-mesh Ewald summation method64,65. After equilibration, the production runs were performed under constant pressure, temperature, and number of particles, using the same algorithms and parameters as in the second equilibration phase described above, for three independent replicas of 600 ns each, starting from different conformations (see the SI).

Analyses of the MD simulations

A series of computational analyses was performed on the final 400 ns of each unbiased MD trajectory. Structural convergence was assessed using RMSD and PCA. To examine the flexibility of the functionally important regions, RMSF calculations were performed. Substrate interactions were characterized through hydrogen bond analysis and interatomic distance monitoring. Clustering of trajectory frames was performed using the GROMOS method to identify representative structural states, which were further analyzed for allosteric pocket detection using the AllositePro server39. Dynamical network analysis66 was employed to examine allosteric communication pathways via VMD67 (see the SI for more information).
