Genetically Guided Pharmacotherapy for Structural Neurovascular Lesions
Authors: Andrew T. Hale, Adam J. Kundishora, Kristopher T. Kahle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Structural neurovascular lesions (SNVLs)—arteriovenous malformations, cavernous malformations, and vein of Galen aneurysmal malformations, among others—have historically been treated using neurosurgical, radiotherapeutic, and endovascular approaches. However, lesion size, location, and presence of high-risk angioarchitectural features preclude many patients from receiving these treatments. Advances in human genetics and cerebrovascular biology have redefined SNVLs as dynamic, genetically driven lesions where sporadic cases are caused by somatic variants. Intriguingly, many SNVL-causing variants are also oncogenic and active drug targets. These data provide the rationale for a genetically driven taxonomy to guide targeted therapeutic selection. In this commentary, we synthesize efforts toward pharmacologic treatments of SNVLs and highlight how variant-specific or pathway-modulating therapies may be investigated, incorporating key considerations in molecular diagnosis, cerebrovascular biology, and molecular and phenotypic outcome measures, in forthcoming clinical trials. Together, these advances support the investigation of pharmacologic treatment strategies in carefully selected patients with SNVLs.

Introduction

Treatment of structural neurovascular lesions (SNVLs)—arteriovenous malformations (AVM), cavernous malformations (CM), vein of Galen arteriovenous malformations (VOGM), arteriovenous fistulas (AVF), and aneurysms, among others—has been based on anatomical location, angioarchitecture, hemodynamic state, and estimated risk of hemorrhage observational natural history studies. Neurosurgical resection/disconnection, endovascular embolization, and targeted radiation carry considerable risk of morbidity and mortality thus precluding many patients as candidates for these therapies. These observations challenge the long-standing view of SNVLs as static anatomic lesions and instead position them as dynamic, genetically encoded disorders of neurovascular development and maintenance.

Advances in human genetics and cerebrovascular biology—combined with the development of mutant-specific and pathway-modulating pharmacotherapies for oncologic indications—have provided the rationale for an integrated, systems-level approach toward pharmacologic treatment for SNVLs (Figure 1). SNVLs are now recognized as dynamic lesions caused by somatic variants in endothelial cells (ECs), with germline variants restricted to syndromic cases (i.e., capillary-malformation AVM syndrome, hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia, familial CMs). Specifically, 50%–80% of AVMs are driven by somatic variants in oncogenes KRAS and less commonly BRAF.1,2 Conversely, both sporadic and familial causes of CM converge on (1) PI3K/AKT/MTOR, a key regulator of cell growth and metabolism, and (2) RHO-A/ROCK signaling, a pathway controlling actin dynamics, cell contractility, and migration,3 offering multiple points for pharmacologic intervention. Somatic variants in genes regulating cell growth, proliferation, and differentiation largely define the genetic landscape of SNVLs. In this framework, vascular phenotype reflects not simply abnormal vessel architecture, but the downstream manifestations of cell-type–specific genetic perturbations within the neurovascular unit.

Implementing genetically guided pharmacologic treatment represents a radical shift in SNVL nosology and management. Advances in endovascular-enabled sampling strategies—coupled with bespoke molecular-genetic diagnostics—will determine patient/drug candidacy and biomarker-defined treatment responses (Figure 1). Consensus thresholds for variant pathogenicity, detection, and “successful” molecular and phenotypic outcomes are needed. Anchoring therapeutics to genetics, careful consideration of outcome measures (rupture rates against historical cohorts, angioarchitecture remodeling, reducing pathogenic variant allele fraction [VAF], etc.), and rethinking classic clinical trial design (Figure 2) will be required to demonstrate treatment efficacy.

Somatic Variants Underlie Sporadic, Structural Neurovascular Lesions

Somatic variant acquisition underlies sporadic SNVLs pathogenesis,4 explaining key clinical features including stereotyped anatomy, progressive course, and the absence of germline variants. However, how low-frequency EC-restricted variants shape disease trajectory and treatment response remains unclear. Emerging reports of successful pharmacologic therapy support feasibility, and deeper mechanistic insight will both clarify pathobiology and identify new targetable pathways governing SNLV growth, maintenance, and regression.

Targeted Therapies in Arteriovenous Malformations

Genetic profiling of sporadic neuroaxis AVMs has converged on KRAS, and less commonly BRAF, in up to 80% of cases.1,2 KRAS variants are enriched at the G12 amino acid position (G12D/C/V/A) and the oncogenic BRAF V600E has also been identified.1 Somatic activating variants in KRAS cause MEK hyperactivation,2 a progrowth pathway that regulates cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival. However, it is unclear if KRAS-wildtype AVMs display similar molecular mechanisms, necessitating genotype-stratified treatment paradigms. KRAS mutant–specific pharmacotherapies (i.e., sotorasib) and MEK signaling inhibitors (i.e., trametinib) have been tested in human patients and mice with AVMs.5-9 Pharmacologic treatment of AVMs in humans has largely focused on inhibiting MEK,10 and a Phase II clinical trial of genotype-agnostic adjuvant trametinib therapy in AVMs undergoing microsurgical resection is ongoing (NCT06098872). Targeted pharmacologic therapy against KRASG12C (i.e., sotorasib) ameliorates brain AVMs in mice and extracranial AVMs in humans,7 whereas vemurafenib obliterates AVM-like vascular features in zebrafish harboring BRAFV600E variants.11 Finally, somatic “second hit” variants in the presence of a germline pathogenic allele determine AVM pathogenesis in hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (ENG, ACRVL, SMAD2) and cavernous malformation AVM syndrome (RASA1, EPHB4), where antiangiogenic and MEK/mTOR pathway inhibitors may provide benefit.12

Clinical and preclinical data support the feasibility of pharmacologic treatment for AVMs, but further investigation is needed to clarify patient selection, study design, molecular diagnostics, and outcome measures.13 Specifically, clinical trial designs that include (1) molecular response (i.e., reduction in SNVL variant allele frequency), (2) angioarchitectural remodeling, (3) flow dynamic assessments, and (4) attenuation of growth—rather than complete volumetric obliteration are needed (Figure 2).

Pharmacologic Strategies to Treat Cavernous Malformations

Cavernous malformations (CMs) are low-pressure lesions with familial forms caused by an autosomal dominant inherited variant in CCM1-3 and a somatic “second hit.”3 Familial CMs display incomplete penetrance and marked phenotypic variability (asymptomatic to cavernomatosis with multiple lesions requiring treatment). Unfortunately, the SYCAMORE study (NCT05085561), a phase 2 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of redox-cycling nitrous oxide compound REC-994 for treatment of symptomatic CMs was terminated for lack of efficacy, but additional targets may be feasible. CMs converge on the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and RhoA-ROCK pathways,3 PI3K activation is associated with increased risk of rupture of CMs14 and mTOR inhibitors (i.e., sirolimus) have successfully treated CMs in mice.15 On the other hand, inhibition of RhoA-ROCK signaling has also been attempted with specific and pleiotropic (i.e., statin) agents to achieve beneficial endpoints in mice.16-18 Therefore, targeting PI3K, mTOR, and/or RhoA-ROCK may provide clinical benefit.

Clinical trial design for CM pharmacotherapy should stratify by sporadic vs familial groups and prioritize long-term outcomes, including hemorrhagic incidence, seizure frequency, biomarkers of EC stability, noninvasive imaging measures of EC barrier function, and inflammatory markers (Figure 2). Because CMs converge on PI3K/AKT/mTOR activation, genotype stratification may be less critical for clinical trial design, but in situ measures of pathway activation and response will inform molecular surveillance and determine dose-escalation protocols beyond neuroimaging evaluation.

Emerging Pharmacologic Targets in Aneurysms, Vein of Galen Aneurysmal Malformations, and Arteriovenous Fistulas

Although intracranial saccular aneurysms are the most common SNVL, the genetic basis of the most lesions is unknown. However, analysis of postmortem aneurysm tissue has identified somatic variants in supervillin, which modulates KLF4, PDGF, and RhoA-ROCK activity.19 Moreover, endoluminal biopsy of a fusiform aneurysm identified a potentially actionable variant in PDGFRB20 and immune-related single-cell phenotypic signatures.21 Genetic analysis of VOGMs, on the other hand, identified variants in genes converging on the EPHB4-RASA1 signaling axis22 that may be amenable to PI3K activation or MEK inhibition.23 Recently, next-generation sequencing identified a gain-of-function variant in PI3KCA in a patient with multiple dural AVFs successfully treated with PI3K inhibitor alpelisib (Philbrick et al., Neurol Genet, under review). These data reinforce that genetic discovery can enable precision pharmacotherapy.

Molecular Diagnosis of Structural Neurovascular Lesions

The critical translational gaps for implementing genetically guided pharmacotherapies for SNVL are (1) detecting the somatic driver variant, and (2) molecular monitoring of treatment response in situ. Endoluminal and liquid biopsy are minimally invasive approaches that can enable detection of pathogenic variants in SNVL cells from retrieved endovascular coils or cell-free DNA in blood, respectively. While VAF for SNVLs is typically low (<4%), an ultra-deep unique molecular identifiers sequencing panel (∼0.15% VAF detection limit) has been shown to change clinical care and treatment outcomes for extracranial vascular malformations.24 However, endoluminal biopsy raises reproducibility concerns due angioarchitectural remodeling, while liquid biopsy relies on sensitive and specific detection of variants from nanomolar range DNA from endovascular-enabled blood collection. Prospective validation of molecular diagnostics is essential to enable genotype-directed clinical trials in SNVLs.

Clinical Trial Design Considerations for Pharmacologic Therapy in SNVLs

We propose that clinical trials in SNLVs should shift from anatomy-based enrollment to genetically or pathway-defined cohorts (Figure 2). Study considerations include: (1) single-arm trials relying on natural history data controls, (2) adaptive trial design based on interim analyses (VAF, volumetric reduction, etc.), (3) N-of-1 trial design for rare lesions and genotypes, (4) “basket” trials, where a single pharmacologic agent can be tested for more than 1 SNVL subtype, (5) Bayesian trial design using historical data, (6) surrogate endpoint-driven trials (i.e., biomarkers of treatment response), and (7) artificial intelligence enabled segmentation analysis of SNVL angioarchitecture. However, genotype-stratified trials face practical barriers including cost, molecular diagnostic sensitivity and specificity, and regulatory complexity that may be mitigated in part by pathway-based enrollment. Collectively, these approaches provide a flexible framework to evaluate targeted therapies in SNVLs.

Conclusions

Despite clear and rational targets for intervention, many critical questions remain unanswered in the genetically guided pharmacotherapy for SNVLs. First, there is no consensus strategy to define SNVL genetic or molecular activity in situ. While liquid and endoluminal biopsy approaches are promising, large-scale prospective evaluation has not been performed. Second, there is no consensus on patient candidacy, dose-escalation protocols, or SNVL-specific outcome measures for targeted therapies. For example, comparison of SNVL rupture rates across historical cohorts, artificial intelligence-based segmentation analysis of high-risk angioarchitectural features, and post-treatment conversion to microsurgical or radiation-amenable lesions are critical endpoints to define for any future clinical trials. Third, the risk-benefit analysis for implementing systemic therapies, especially in pediatric patients, remains largely unexplored. Accelerated FDA approval for genetically-defined, molecularly-targetable disorders with high unmet need, such as SNVLs, will likely streamline precision medicine approaches.25 Establishing this framework will be essential to transition SNLVs from surgically managed lesions to molecularly tractable neurovascular diseases.
