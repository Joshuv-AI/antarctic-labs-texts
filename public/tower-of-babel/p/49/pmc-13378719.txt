A Survey of Knowledge, Attitudes, and Behaviours of Canadian Family Physicians in Response to the Canadian Guideline on Prescribing Opioids for Chronic Non-cancer Pain
Authors: Lillian Saberian, Nagina Parmar, Jenny Lau, Craig M Dale, Abhimanyu Sud, Lydia Hatcher, Angela Carol, Najam Mian, Nicole Bootsman, Peter Selby, Andrea D Furlan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChronic non-cancer pain (CNCP) is widespread in Canada, with Family Physicians (FPs) playing a crucial role in patient care and opioid stewardship amidst high prescribing rates. Two national surveys conducted in 2010 and 2018 revealed notable knowledge gaps and poor adherence to safe opioid prescribing practices among Canadian FPs.AimsTo provide an update on Canadian FPs’ knowledge, attitudes, and behavior regarding opioid prescribing for CNCP.MethodsThis study is the third national cross-sectional online survey of Canadian FPs. The results are presented narratively, and only descriptive statistics are offered.ResultsA total of 529 responses were collected from July 2023 to June 2024, primarily from female participants (52%) and Ontario (50%). Eighteen percent of respondents did not prescribe opioids for CNCP, and 17% prescribed only weak opioids. Wait time for a pain specialist and for a non-urgent referral to an addiction specialist were less than one month among 23% and 25% of respondents, respectively. Only 27% of respondents were able to correctly identify the minimum daily dose of morphine equivalents to start prescribing a transdermal fentanyl patch. Respondents’ adherence to recommended practices before starting opioids varied from 16% for “providing written information about opioid therapy” to 93% for “explaining potential harms of long-term opioid therapy.”ConclusionSeveral knowledge gaps and a lack of adherence to certain guideline-recommended practices were observed among respondents, along with reduced wait times for consultations with pain specialists.KEYWORDS: Opioid, chronic non-cancer pain, family physicians, Canadian guidelines, knowledge, attitudes, practices, chronic pain

Introduction

Chronic non-cancer pain (CNCP) encompasses various pain disorders that persist for at least three months and are not linked to malignant conditions.1 Approximately 8 million Canadians, or one in every five individuals, live with chronic pain.2,3 In Canada, the percentage of individuals receiving opioid prescriptions has declined from 12.8% in 2019 to 11.7% in 2023. Despite this decrease, approximately 1 in 9 Canadians obtained at least one prescription for opioids from a community pharmacy in 2023.4,5 Opioid use is associated with a range of issues, including the development of opioid use disorder, diversion and overdose fatalities.6,7 In response to these concerns in Canada, the Guideline for the Safe and Effective Use of Opioids for CNCP was initially released in 2010 and updated in 2017.1,8–10 and 2025.11

Over the years, national surveys have been conducted in Canada to evaluate whether implementing national opioid guidelines has led to the intended changes.12–14 These surveys have highlighted the gaps in knowledge, attitudes, and practices that FPs have faced, as well as the efforts made to address these challenges through successive guidelines.

The first national survey conducted by Dr. Allen in 2010 established a baseline assessment of FPs’ practices and knowledge regarding opioid prescriptions for CNCP before the release of the 2010 guideline.12 Their findings indicated that the respondents showed knowledge gaps in several key areas of opioid management, including safe transitions between opioids, the long-term efficacy of opioids for CNCP, the comparative effectiveness of strong opioids, and the benefits of controlled-release over immediate-release formulations.12

In 2018, Furlan et al. conducted a follow-up survey to assess the impact of the 2010 guideline on FPs’ practices and knowledge regarding opioid prescribing for CNCP.14 They identified ongoing gaps in FP’s knowledge translation, exchange, and education regarding the prescription of opioids for patients with chronic pain.14 For example, many surveyed FPs had a lack of understanding about switching patients to transdermal fentanyl. Additionally, there were gaps in knowledge about alternative routes of opioid administration and the comparisons of effectiveness and risk-benefit ratios between opioids and non-opioid medications.14 However, in the 2018 survey, the respondents demonstrated adequate knowledge regarding the clinical significance of pain reduction, the prioritization of pain relief in opioid effectiveness, and the efficacy of opioid replacement therapy for patients with opioid use disorder.14

Since 2018, several important developments may have influenced FPs’ knowledge, attitudes, and prescribing practices regarding opioid use for CNCP. Although no major national opioid prescribing guideline was released before our survey, the landscape of opioid prescribing has shifted significantly. Ongoing educational initiatives, including webinars, workshops, and updated training modules,15–22 have been launched across Canada to enhance prescribers’ knowledge and skills in CNCP management following the dissemination of the 2017 Canadian guideline.

Furthermore, the establishment of the Canadian Pain Task Force in 2019 has guided effective methods for managing chronic pain, emphasizing multimodal approaches and non-opioid alternatives.2

In addition, recent reorganizations within the healthcare system have affected chronic pain care trajectories, leading to increased focus on interdisciplinary pain management teams and the integration of alternative therapies into primary care settings.2

Canadian guidelines for opioid prescribing serve as essential tools reflecting the current understanding of pain management and the responsible use of opioids. However, they also represent a snapshot in time, shaped by evolving evidence and clinical experiences.

As Canada navigates the challenges related to opioid prescriptions, there is an urgent need to assess whether FPs are adequately equipped to prescribe opioids safely and effectively. Given that the 2025 guideline released only a few months after our survey, it is crucial to evaluate how these factors have shaped FPs’ practices and perspectives.

The primary aim of this study is to improve adherence among FPs in Canada to best practices and promote safer pain management strategies in patients with CNCP.

Objectives

The primary objective is to provide an update about knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported behaviors toward opioid prescribing practices for CNCP among FPs who manage patients with CNCP in Canada.

This update will help identify specific areas where prescribers may need additional training or resources, leading to targeted educational initiatives designed to address knowledge and practice gaps. Ultimately, this aims to improve adherence to best practices and promote safer pain management strategies. Other objectives are as follows:

To assess Canadian FPs’ adherence to the recommendations of the updated Canadian National Opioid Guideline published in 2017 in their self-reported practices, before prescribing opioids for CNCP and while monitoring CNCP patients on opioids

Material and methods

Study design

This study was an observational, cross-sectional national online survey of Canadian FPs. The survey contained the same questions used in the 2010 and 2018 surveys, with minor corrections and additional questions concerning switching, tapering, dose reduction and experience of opioid-related harms. This study aimed to include all FPs from the 10 provinces and 3 territories in Canada. In 2023, there were approximately 48,200 FPs across Canada.23 They practice across diverse clinical settings, including comprehensive community-based primary care, emergency department care, hospitalist medicine, and focused areas of practice such as chronic pain management, palliative care, addiction medicine, sports medicine, and obstetrics.

A structured, self-administered online survey was developed in SurveyMonkey™ and was accessible from July 2023 to June 2024. We sent invitation e-mails with a survey link to a group of Canadian stakeholders (Appendix 1) to share with FPs in their organizations. We did not have information about the number of FPs who received these e-mails from the stakeholders. Another set of invitation emails was sent to a database of 4,191 FPs, established by the senior author of this study (A.D.F.). The database included FPs who attended previous courses about opioids and those who downloaded the University Health Network (UHN) Opioid Manager Tool. Additionally, an external vendor was contracted to send faxes containing a link and a QR code to the survey to 44,294 FPs in Canada.

Participants

To be eligible for the survey, FPs had to be practicing in a province or territory in Canada. We excluded FPs who had a focused practice in chronic pain, addiction medicine, or palliative care, and FPs with current or past employment by or consultancy with any pharmaceutical industry, the UHN, or Health Canada.

Health Canada was the primary stakeholder in the survey and provided feedback on its content and execution. Moreover, the survey was conducted at UHN, a comprehensive health care and medical research organization in Canada, raising concerns about potential coercion by UHN doctors to participate in order to boost participation rates. To prevent biased results, UHN physicians were excluded from the survey.

Survey questions

The survey consisted of 30 closed-ended and open-ended questions or statements with multiple response options (Appendix 2). The survey was available in English and French, and it was estimated to take 10 minutes to complete.

The survey begins with a screening module with three questions to confirm eligibility as follows:

Currently practicing as an FP in Canada

Currently practicing as an FP in Canada

Current or past employment with any pharmaceutical industry, UHN, or Health Canada

Current or past employment with any pharmaceutical industry, UHN, or Health Canada

Having a focused practice; if yes, what kind of focused practice?

Having a focused practice; if yes, what kind of focused practice?

Survey participation either terminated or continued depending on the answers to the screening questions. If participants had current or past employment with any pharmaceutical industry, UHN, or Health Canada, they were not eligible to continue to complete the survey. If the participants had focused practice in “chronic pain,” “addiction medicine,” or “palliative care,” they were not eligible to continue with the survey. If ineligible, the respondent was immediately notified with a “thank you” message stating that their participation in the survey had ended. If eligible, the respondent was permitted to continue participating in the survey.

The survey ended with demographic questions asking about participants’ age, gender, ethnicity, duration of practicing as an FP, province of the practice, rural or urban setting of the practice, size of the practice community, the total number of patients seen per month, number of patients to be written prescriptions for weak opioids for CNCP per month, number of patients to be written prescriptions for strong opioids for CNCP per month, having advanced training in pain management, waiting time for patients to see a PAIN specialist for a NON-URGENT referral, waiting time for patients for a referral to a colleague for a second opinion regarding the possibility of increasing the dose to more than 90 mg morphine equivalents daily, and waiting time for patients to see an ADDICTION specialist for a NON URGENT referral.

Regarding the barriers to prescribing opioids for CNCP, respondents who indicated that they do not prescribe opioids were asked to rate various factors influencing their decision. Additionally, those who stated they only prescribe weak opioids for CNCP were also asked to evaluate several influencing factors. This section included eleven reasons for not prescribing opioids and ten questions addressing the reasons for prescribing only weak opioids (Appendix 3).

Regarding knowledge, respondents were asked to indicate their level of agreement with nine statements about opioid prescriptions for CNCP, presented in an agree/disagree format. Additionally, there was a question regarding the definition of CNCP based on the 2017 guideline, a stand-alone question asking about the minimum daily dose required to switch from codeine to a fentanyl patch, and a miscellaneous question about non-inhaled cannabis or cannabinoids for neuropathic pain.

Regarding attitude, the survey included one question asking respondents’ level of confidence in prescribing opioids for CNCP, indicating their ability to determine when it is appropriate to prescribe opioids for patients with CNCP and when it is not appropriate to do so.

The survey included questions about self-reported behaviors related to guideline-recommended practices, focusing on two main areas: practices before starting opioids for CNCP and practices while monitoring patients on opioids.

For the practices before prescribing opioids, the survey featured fourteen practices, with one new addition for the current survey, “Optimize non-opioid pain management.” Moreover, two options were included as distractors: “Refer to a colleague for assessment” and “Conduct formal psychological screening.” Notably, the recommendation to “prescribe a dose less than 50 mg morphine equivalents daily” was removed because it was considered a WEAK recommendation in the 2017 guideline.

In the section regarding practices while monitoring patients, thirteen practices were included. Additionally, there was an open-ended question concerning the daily morphine equivalent dose relevant to the reassessment or close monitoring of patients on opioids.

The question on enabling factors for optimizing opioid use for CNCP asked respondents to rate the importance of 15 factors.

Health Canada staff and four individuals representing four stakeholder organizations tested the survey questions used in 2018 for content validity and suggested changes. They provided feedback on the clarity of the questions, redundancy, flow, ease of understanding, and completeness of the survey. A team consisting of the first author (LS), one FP (AS), and research team members (LS, NP, JL, C.M.D., A.D.F.) reviewed their comments. In addition, the research ethics board (REB) requested modifications before the survey was deployed. Modifications to the survey were made based on the feedback received and approved by the research team.

Data management

Questions about physicians’ knowledge asked whether they “Agree,” “Disagree,” or “Have no opinion” about various statements. For these questions, respondents’ opinions were reported in three categories: Disagree, Agree, and No opinion.

FPs’ attitudes were measured by asking respondents to rate their level of confidence in prescribing opioids for CNCP on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Not very confident, 2 = Not confident, 3 = Neutral, 4 = Confident, 5 = Very confident). For each factor, the percentage of responses was reported in three categories: 1 and 2 (not confident), 3 (neutral), and 4 and 5 (confident).

Questions about physicians’ behaviors listed recommended practices and asked respondents how frequently they performed each practice. To quantify their responses, the results were categorized into six groups based on the percentage of patients to whom these practices were applied. These categories included: never, <25% of patients, <50% of patients, >50% of patients, >75% of patients, and always. For the interpretation of the results, the percentage of respondents performing these practices was categorized into three groups: never and <25% of patients, 25% to 75% of patients, and >75% and always.

Questions about opioid prescription barriers asked respondents to rate the importance of multiple factors on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = Not very important, 2 = Not important, 3 = Neutral, 4 = Important, 5 = Very important, and 6 = No opinion). For the results interpretation, the responses were divided into four groups: Categories 1 and 2 combined indicated “Not important,” Category 3 was “Neutral,” Categories 4 and 5 combined indicated “Important,” and Category 6 was “No opinion” with percentages reported for each category.

Questions relating to enabling factors for prescribing opioids asked respondents to rate the importance of multiple factors on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = Not very useful, 2 = Not useful, 3 = Neutral, 4 = Useful, 5 = Very useful, and 6 = No opinion). For the interpretation of the results, the responses were divided into four groups: Categories 1 and 2 combined indicated “Not useful”; Category 3 was “Neutral”; Categories 4 and 5 combined indicated “Useful”; and Category 6 was “No opinion,” with percentages reported for each category.

Variables

The study examined outcome variables related to FPs’ knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors regarding opioid use, including their understanding of CNCP, effectiveness of opioids, prescribing practices, reasons for abstaining from opioid prescriptions, management strategies prior to and during opioid treatment, and the perceived value of supplementary management tools.

Statistical analysis

All aggregated data from the previous surveys were obtained from the published papers to conduct a narrative comparison with this survey’s results.

For this survey, descriptive statistics were applied to summarize the data. Continuous variables were presented as means, medians, and standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables were summarized as percentages.

To determine whether the survey respondents accurately represent the broader population, a demographic analysis was conducted that compared key characteristics, such as age, gender, province/territory of practice, and rural versus urban setting, of the 2024 survey participants against the overall demographic profile of Canadian FPs as of 2023. This analysis employed Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests in Microsoft Excel, along with Proportional Tests in R.

The current survey results (participants’ characteristics) were compared with those from 2018 and 2010, using Chi-square and/or Fisher’s exact tests in Microsoft Excel and Prop-Test in R for categorical variables.12,14 However, the change in FPs’ knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors could not be assessed by comparing the 2024 survey with the 2010 and 2018 surveys due to significant differences in participants’ characteristics. Moreover, as these studies are cross-sectional, they do not accurately reflect trends over time. Consequently, the results for the 2024 sample were presented as a stand-alone cross-sectional survey.

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Version 29.0, RStudio Version 4.2.2, and Microsoft Excel Version 2408, with α = 0.05 as the statistical significance level.

Results

Of the 834 individuals who opened the survey, 529 eligible participants completed it, yielding a 63% completion rate (491 in English and 38 in French). These 529 participants represented approximately 1% of the total number of FPs in Canada (48,200 FPs in 2023).

Over half of the participants in the present survey were female (201, 52%) with a mean age of 50 (SD: 13). Forty-four percent had more than 20 years in practice, 66% saw less than 400 patients per month, 60% practiced in a community of more than 100,000 people, 17% were from rural areas, and 13% had advanced training in pain.

Compared to the previous surveys, there were significant and clinically meaningful differences in demographic characteristics among respondents in 2024: more females, from different geographic distributions, community sizes, years in practice and the number of patients seen per month.

Access to physicians with expertise in pain and/or addiction medicine

Among survey respondents, wait times for non-urgent referrals to pain specialists have improved, with 23% now waiting less than one month for an appointment, up from 3% in 2010 and 2% in 2018. However, 49% reported a lack of access to a pain specialist, up from 23% in 2018 (Table 1 and Figure 1).

Opioid prescribing practices

In 2024, 18% of the survey participants reported not prescribing opioids for CNCP management. In the 2010 and 2018 surveys, these responses were 5% and 11%, respectively (Table 1).

Barriers to prescribing opioids for CNCP

Table 2 highlights barriers to prescribing opioids for CNCP. Of 95 physicians who responded, “I do not prescribe opioids for CNCP,” 93 respondents gave reasons for their decisions. The most frequent reasons affecting their decisions were concerns about long-term adverse effects at 91% (95% CI: 85% to 97%) and a lack of evidence for the effectiveness of opioids in CNCP at 83% (95% CI: 75% to 91%).

In the current survey, 89 (17%) of FPs prescribed only weak opioids for CNCP compared with 43 (16%) in 2018 and 58 (8%) in 2010 (Table 1). Knowledge related to opioid prescribing for CNCP

In response to the question about the definition of CNCP based on the 2017 guideline, out of 529 participants, only 112 (21%) selected the correct answer (“Any painful condition that persists for three months or longer and is not associated with malignancy”). This question was not included in the 2010 survey, and 46 respondents (17%) answered it correctly in the 2018 survey.

Table 3 presents the responses to the nine knowledge questions about opioid prescriptions for CNCP. Of the 529 FPs who started the survey, only 304 (57%) answered the knowledge questions. Only one question was correctly selected by more than 80% of respondents (“Pain relief is a more important indicator of opioid effectiveness than functional ability,” correct answer is “Disagree”). The percentage of correct responses ranged from 29% to 91% (Mean: 52%, Median: 53%).

Stand-alone question about minimum daily dose to switch from codeine to fentanyl patch

In addition to the nine agree/disagree questions, a stand-alone question was asked: “What is the MINIMUM daily dose of opioid in morphine equivalents that your patient would be taking before you would prescribe FENTANYL patch?” Seventy-nine out of 297 respondents (27%) (95% CI: 22% to 32%) correctly identified the minimum dosage as 60 mg. In contrast, 134 respondents (45%) (95% CI: 39% to 51%) reported being “unsure” about this minimum dosage requirement (Figure 4).

Miscellaneous question about non–inhaled cannabis or cannabinoids for neuropathic pain

Regarding the question asking “Non-inhaled cannabis or cannabinoids are effective for neuropathic pain,” among 304 respondents, 136 (45%) (95% CI: 39% to 50%) of physicians selected the correct response of “Agree,” 92 (30%) had “No opinion,” and 76 (25%) disagreed with this statement. However, in 2018, 59 participants (35%) answered this question correctly.

Family physicians’ attitudes (confidence) in prescribing opioids for CNCP

Regarding respondents’ current attitudes (confidence) in prescribing opioids for CNCP, just 41% (95% CI: 34% to 47%) of the respondents reported being confident in prescribing opioids for patients with CNCP (Table 1).

Discussion

This study is the third national survey providing an update on the knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported behaviors of FPs in Canada regarding opioid prescribing for CNCP10,12,14

Opioid prescribing practices

Regarding the opioid prescribing practices, there was an increasing trend of respondents who did not prescribe opioids for CNCP from 2010 to 2024. This finding suggests that patients with CNCP who require opioid prescriptions may face challenges in finding a FP willing to prescribe them.14 A study conducted in Nova Scotia found that 28% of FPs willing to accept new patients would refuse those currently using opioids.24

The results of the subgroup comparison analysis between respondents with advanced training in pain management and those who do not prescribe opioids for CNCP showed that there was no significant difference in the likelihood of prescribing opioids between those with advanced training and those without. This suggests that advanced training does not have a notable impact on opioid prescribing practices in this context.

Barriers to prescribing opioids for CNCP

The top-ranked barriers resulting in not prescribing opioids for patients with CNCP across the three surveys were concerns about long-term adverse effects and lack of evidence for their effectiveness. The growing awareness of the risks associated with opioid use, such as addiction and overdose, may have led to a shift in pain management strategies.25 This shift has prompted respondents to consider safer alternatives, including non-opioid medications, physical therapy, behavioral therapies, and evidence-based interventional pain management techniques.26,27 A 2016 online survey of FPs in Quebec also indicated that worries about abuse, misuse, diversion, and dependence influenced their decision to avoid opioid prescriptions for CNCP.28

Access to physicians with expertise in pain and/or addiction medicine

Our survey found notable gaps in waiting times for non-urgent referrals to pain specialists, for second opinions on high-dose opioids, and non-urgent referrals to addiction specialists. The increased inaccessibility of specialty care negatively affects patient management of chronic pain. Although some wait times have improved, the overall decline in access can lead to prolonged suffering and inadequate pain relief. To address this issue, it is essential that family practitioners are equipped with the necessary allied health and clinical resources to manage pain effectively, such as access to physical therapists for rehabilitation, mental health professionals for cognitive behavioral therapy, and occupational therapists for functional support, rather than relying solely on the availability of more pain specialists.

Additionally, worsening wait times for second opinions on high-dose opioids can hamper timely interventions, further compromising patients’ quality of life. However, our survey received very few responses from some Canadian provinces and territories, where this problem could be even worse.29,30 A study of patients in six multidisciplinary pain clinics in urban areas of Canada from 2020 to 2022 found that about 53% reported waiting six months or less for their first appointment.31 Further research is necessary to compare access and waiting times for patients in both urban and rural settings across Canada to reach a more definitive conclusion.

Knowledge related to opioid prescribing for CNCP

Regarding the knowledge question, although many respondents demonstrate a solid understanding of the therapeutic goals related to managing CNCP with opioids and opioid replacement therapy for patients with opioid use disorder, a significant number still lack awareness of the short-term and long-term benefits of opioids and dose escalation. Moreover, a noteworthy knowledge gap exists regarding the process of switching patients to transdermal fentanyl. The existence of this knowledge gap in CNCP management with opioids may have arisen from challenges posed by the COVID-19 pandemic. These challenges include disruptions to educational programs, increased workloads for family practitioners, and a lack of time to expand their knowledge. However, it is important to recognize that there are also significant complexities surrounding the nature and use of morphine equivalents,32 particularly in relation to fentanyl,33 which should be carefully considered. Further data on these issues will provide a clearer understanding of the factors contributing to this knowledge gap.

Additionally, survey results indicate that although a small proportion of respondents align with the 2017 guideline on the efficacy of strong opioids, the effectiveness of controlled-release opioids, and the recognition of opioid addiction potential, others may still feel uncertain or influenced by alternative sources of information. This highlights the urgent need for enhanced education and the development of clear, evidence-based guidelines to support FPs in managing pain effectively while reducing the risks associated with opioid use. Furthermore, it is crucial to create a supportive environment within primary care that addresses the systemic barriers and socio-economic factors impacting the management of chronic pain, as these elements can significantly contribute to non-adherence to existing guidelines.

Family physicians’ attitudes (confidence) in prescribing opioids for CNCP

Regarding attitudes, 41% of respondents felt confident in prescribing opioids for CNCP in the 2024 survey, indicating significant uncertainty about prescribing these medications. While direct comparison of FPs’ confidence across the three surveys is not applicable, it is noteworthy that 58% of participants in both the 2010 and 2018 surveys expressed confidence in prescribing opioids for CNCP.12,14 Additionally, the 2015 survey by Roy et al. found that most participants felt confident prescribing opioids for CNCP, with 15.2% strongly agreeing and 70.9% somewhat agreeing.28

Future research using qualitative methods is recommended to explore what FPs understand by “being confident” in prescribing opioids for CNCP, as it may indicate a lack of skills to initiate a trial, to switch from one opioid to another, or even to taper and stop opioids safely.

Practices before starting opioids for patients with CNCP

Practices explicitly addressed as STRONG recommendations in the 2017 guideline

The 2017 guideline strongly recommended certain practices to follow before initiating opioids for CNCP management. As a result, we expect that the majority of respondents in the 2024 survey will report performing these practices if respondents adhere to the 2017 guideline. For instance, 93% of respondents reported optimizing non-opioid pharmacotherapy and non-pharmacological therapies before prescribing opioids for CNCP. The 2017 guideline strongly recommends this approach instead of starting with opioids.10 The results indicate that respondents are aware of the 2017 guideline and are adhering to it effectively.

However, another factor contributing to this result may be the growing concern surrounding the opioid crisis and its effects on patients and communities. As a result, respondents might be actively seeking alternative pain management strategies to ensure patient safety and reduce the potential for addiction. Finally, the emergence of new non-opioid therapies and recent studies demonstrating their efficacy may also encourage this shift toward non-opioid management practices.

Practices not explicitly addressed as STRONG recommendations in the 2017 guideline

In contrast, the 2010 guideline provided recommendations for practices before starting opioids for patients with CNCP. However, the 2017 guideline did not explicitly reinforce some of these earlier recommendations as STRONG recommendations. As a result, if respondents follow the 2017 guideline, we anticipate that fewer than 50% will implement these practices in the 2024 survey. This approach indicates that FPs’ practices are highly aligned with the 2017 guideline.

For instance, regarding the statement, “Performing urine drug screening before starting opioids,” the frequency of performing this practice was 22% in the 2024 survey. The 2017 guideline removed this formal recommendation. It seems that most respondents follow the updated guideline regarding this practice. However, the low frequency of performing this practice, as reported by respondents, may also be due to time and resource constraints when conducting routine urine drug screenings, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Other examples of high alignment with the 2017 guideline were noted, especially in areas such as assessing the risk of substance use disorder by a screening tool, having patients sign a treatment agreement, and giving the patient written information about opioid therapy.

Conversely, if respondents do not follow the 2017 guideline, we anticipate that the majority will implement these practices in the 2024 survey. This expectation highlights the low alignment of FPs with the 2017 guideline for these practices, rather than a lack of adherence.

For instance, another top-rated practice before starting opioids for CNCP was to explain the potential harms of long-term opioid therapy. The guideline released in 2010 recommended performing this practice before initiating opioids for CNCP (Recommendation # 5).9 This statement is not mentioned as a recommendation in the 2017 guideline, but it is under the Best Practice Statements (BPSs), which describe the uncertainty surrounding their effectiveness.10 Nevertheless, this result may reflect a positive behavior, showcasing that respondents to the present survey are prioritizing patient safety. This suggests that, despite the changes in the guideline, FPs are adopting more cautious strategies when it comes to prescribing opioids, demonstrating a commitment to the well-being of their patients.

Other examples of low alignment with the 2017 guideline were noted, especially in areas such as explaining the potential harms and benefits of long-term opioid use, assessing patients for psychiatric disorders, assessing their pain intensity and functional levels, and managing the use of benzodiazepines among patients.

Practices while monitoring patients with CNCP on opioids

Practices explicitly addressed as STRONG recommendations in the 2017 guideline

The 2017 guideline strongly recommended certain practices to follow while monitoring patients with CNCP on opioids. As a result, we expect that the majority of respondents in the current survey will report performing these practices if respondents adhere to the 2017 guideline.

Regarding the statement, “If patient is experiencing a serious challenge in tapering, try a referral to a formal multidisciplinary program,” the updated 2017 guideline considers this statement a STRONG recommendation (#10).10 The low frequency of this practice in the current survey suggests several barriers to referring patients to formal multidisciplinary programs for tapering. First, respondents may not be aware of these programs or may be uncertain about how to access them. Additionally, there may be a lack of resources in certain areas, particularly in rural or underserved regions. Finally, time constraints can hinder their ability to coordinate referrals effectively.

Similarly, regarding the statement, “If patient has insufficient pain relief, increase the dose up to maximum of 90 morphine equivalents per day,” the 2017 guideline strongly recommends restricting the prescribed dose to less than 90 mg morphine equivalents daily for CNCP patients who are beginning long-term opioid therapy (R # 6).10

The low frequency of respondents performing this practice suggests several contributing factors to their reluctance. Increased awareness of the risks associated with high-dose opioid therapy, such as addiction and overdose, may cause respondents to be more cautious and refrain from increasing the dosage. Additionally, there appears to be a growing preference for alternative pain management strategies that do not involve opioids. The reluctance to increase the dose of opioids up to the maximum of 90 morphine equivalents per day if patients are not meeting treatment goals may lead to patients being left on the medications inappropriately.

Practices not explicitly addressed as STRONG recommendations in the 2017 guideline

In contrast, the 2010 guideline provided recommendations for practices while monitoring patients with CNCP on opioids. However, the 2017 update did not explicitly reinforce some of these earlier recommendations as STRONG recommendations. As a result, if respondents follow the 2017 guideline, we anticipate that fewer than 50% will implement these practices in the current survey. This approach reflects that FPs are highly aligned with the 2017 guideline regarding these practices.

For instance, regarding the statement “Do routine urine drug screening,” the 2017 guideline removed this statement from the recommendations and added it under guidance statement #6.10 It appears that the majority of respondents now align with the 2017 guideline when implementing this practice.

Other examples of high alignment with the 2017 guideline were noted, especially in areas such as tapering off opioids and trying another modality or switching to a different opioid if the patient has insufficient pain relief, and asking the patient to bring remaining medication.

However, the infrequent application of these practices, as highlighted by the respondents, can be primarily due to time and resource limitations, particularly during the challenging circumstances posed by the COVID-19 pandemic. Additionally, some respondents might disagree with the guideline’s content or feel unprepared due to insufficient training or knowledge of recent updates.34

Conversely, if respondents do not follow the 2017 guideline, we anticipate that the majority will implement these practices in the 2024 survey. This expectation highlights the low alignment of FPs with the 2017 guideline for these practices, rather than a lack of adherence. However, this approach likely reflects the persistence of practices recommended in the 2010 guideline or a cautious, individualized approach to opioid management in clinical practice. This pattern may also indicate variability in how clinicians incorporate evolving guideline recommendations into their practice.

Overall, these findings emphasize an ongoing commitment to patient safety in opioid prescribing and monitoring, which aligns with the overall decrease in opioid prescriptions and signs of misuse or abuse in Canada. These practices include observing for aberrant drug-related behavior, assessing for adverse effects, advising caution while driving or operating machinery, and assessing the patient’s level of function and pain intensity.

The daily dose of morphine equivalent for reassessing or closely monitoring

The shift in guidelines regarding the daily dose of morphine equivalents for reassessing or closely monitoring patients has raised important considerations for patient safety and physician awareness. While the 2010 guideline defined a “watchful dose” at 200 mg,9 the 2017 update lowered this threshold to 90 mg, marking it as a “STRONG recommendation” for patients initiating long-term opioid therapy for CNCP.10 Only 23% of respondents accurately identified the new dose in the current survey, suggesting that the transition from 200 mg to 90 mg may have confused them. This finding highlights a critical need for enhanced education and resources to ensure that physicians are adequately informed about opioid prescribing practices, thereby improving adherence to guidelines designed to prioritize patient safety and effective care.

Actual contradictions to the guideline raise concerns about communication, clinicians’ perceptions of pain assessment, and varying clinical contexts. However, it is essential to acknowledge that many respondents might not report engaging in non-recommended practices because they trust their clinical judgment and view these practices as unnecessary. Patient expectations can also impact adherence to guidelines. Additionally, systemic issues like limited access to alternative therapies and a lack of support for chronic pain management pose challenges. Furthermore, the minimal education on chronic pain in Canadian medical schools underscores the need for better training and resources in this area.

A previous qualitative study with Ontario FPs identified barriers, such as the time and effort required to understand the guideline, which are considered labor-intensive.14,34 Additionally, much of the evidence on opioid prescribing is of low to moderate quality, with only four out of ten recommendations classified as “STRONG.” This may lead physicians to feel less compelled to adhere to them, as STRONG recommendations suggest that nearly all informed patients would choose the recommended action.1,14 Furthermore, there is a noticeable inadequacy of evidence supporting the guideline statements, and many relevant practices lack attached guidance, which ultimately limits the practical utility of the guideline. This can lead to guideline being seen as irrelevant or inapplicable in various clinical situations, contributing to adherence issues.

Enabling factors for optimizing the use of opioids for CNCP management

Regarding the enabling factors for optimizing opioid use in CNCP management, access to a patient’s opioid prescription history has been ranked as a top factor through the three surveys (2010, 2018, and 2024) due to its significant impact on patient safety and the quality of care. The rise in opioid misuse and overdose rates highlighted the need for better monitoring and informed prescribing practices. By having visibility into a patient’s prescription history, healthcare providers can more effectively identify patterns of misuse, avoid dangerous drug interactions, and ensure that patients receive appropriate and safe pain management.

In the current survey, the availability of non-pharmacological options is rated as important due to their holistic approach to healthcare, which considers physical, emotional, and social factors for comprehensive treatment. These options often have fewer side effects than medications, empower patients to manage their health actively, and can be more cost-effective in the long run, reducing burdens on healthcare systems.35

“Up-to-date guideline for using opioids to treat CNCP” was rated important because it helps address the opioid crisis by guiding physicians to prescribe carefully and reduce addiction risks. Moreover, they incorporate the latest research on the effectiveness and safety of opioids, promote consistent care across different settings, and suggest alternative pain management options.

Respondents to the survey highlighted the importance of better access to consultants with expertise in pain management and addiction. They take a careful approach to prescribing opioid medications, recognizing the complexities involved. They also pointed out the need for a thorough education on the risks and benefits of different opioid options. This knowledge is crucial for healthcare providers to make informed decisions, leading to safer and more effective pain management for patients.

Finally, the respondents’ emphasis on the importance of continuing medical education in the effective use of opioids for CNCP stems from the urgent need for healthcare professionals to remain informed about best practices and the evolving guidelines related to opioid prescriptions. They may have recognized the existing knowledge gap in opioid prescribing practices.

Overall, high-rated factors in optimizing opioid use, combined with respondents’ knowledge gaps and low adherence to guideline, highlight the need to enhance ongoing educational programs, improve access to comprehensive resources, and implement regular audits to ensure adherence to best practices in opioid prescribing for patients with CNCP.

Strenghts

This study showcases several strengths, including a comprehensive analysis of Canadian FPs’ knowledge, attitudes, and self-reported behaviors regarding opioid prescribing for CNCP, based on the third consecutive national survey. Aligning with the revised Canadian National Opioid Guideline published in 2017, it reinforces the discussion on best practices while identifying specific barriers and enablers that could improve management. The assessment of adherence to the 2017 guideline among survey respondents offers valuable insights into current practices and highlights potential gaps for enhancing compliance and patient care.

Furthermore, the study features a carefully structured survey with thoroughly tested questions, ensuring the reliability and validity of the findings and accurately reflecting the experiences of Canadian FPs. Finally, this research establishes a solid foundation for future longitudinal studies or larger-scale initiatives to explore the evolving landscape of opioid use in clinical practice.

Limitations

Despite our efforts to contact FPs and invite them to participate in the survey, we received only 529 completed responses. This number raises concerns about the response rate, as we cannot accurately determine it without knowing how many FPs were invited to participate.

Based on the demographic analysis of the respondents in the 2024 survey, the key demographic characteristics, including age, gender, province/territory of practice, and urban versus rural setting, are consistent with the established demographics of the overall FPs’ population in Canada as of 2023–24. However, this consistency does not guarantee that the respondents represent the entire FP population, as the final sample of 529 responses is Ontario-centric and accounts for only a small fraction of the approximately 48,200 FPs in Canada.

Additionally, recruiting FPs who have previously attended opioid-related courses or downloaded the UHN Opioid Manager Tool could lead to a biased sample, as these individuals may possess above-average knowledge about opioids.

Another limitation of this survey methodology is that guideline recommendations may change over time; some recommendations may be added or removed, while the survey questions remain static, thus failing to reflect alignment with the guidelines.

Finally, it is important to note that directly comparing the current survey results with those from 2010 and 2018 is not feasible. Although a chi-square test was performed, the differences in FPs’ characteristics across these surveys may indicate sampling bias rather than genuine changes in knowledge, attitudes, or behaviors related to CNCP management with opioids. This potential for differential sampling bias should be clearly acknowledged, as it limits the interpretation of the findings.

Future directions

It is essential to engage physicians in ongoing training, update educational programs, include the latest research and techniques, and utilize patient feedback to address knowledge gaps.2

Longitudinal studies can track changes in practices over time, providing valuable insights into adherence to clinical guidelines among FPs. By incorporating participant feedback, future surveys can be refined to enhance their relevance and accuracy. This iterative process may yield practical recommendations that enhance adherence to clinical guidelines, ultimately improving patient care.

In future surveys, ensuring a diverse and representative participant pool is crucial for generalizability. Additionally, targeted outreach, collaboration with diverse organizations, and providing incentives can encourage broader participation.

Conclusion

This survey relies on self-reported practices, and the number of responding physicians represented approximately 1% of all FPs in Canada. Several knowledge gaps and a lack of adherence to certain guideline-recommended practices were observed among respondents, along with reduced wait times for consultations with pain specialists. Additionally, the respondents’ confidence in prescribing opioids for CNCP was concerning.
