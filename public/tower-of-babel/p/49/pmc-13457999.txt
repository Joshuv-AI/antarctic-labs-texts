Association Between Time-Averaged Serum Uric Acid and Renal Outcomes in Patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus with Chronic Kidney Disease: A Multicenter Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Shaogui Zhang, Xuan Zhao, Jianteng Xie, Yifan Zhang, Yanhui Wang, Xiaojie Chen, Qiuling Li, Danfeng Liu, Runli Jia, Weiting He, Yaxi Zhu, Hanchen Hou, Sheng Li, Fanna Liu, Yiming Zhang, Rongshao Tan, Hongquan Peng, Jie Li, Wenjian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe aim of this work is to explore the association of time-averaged serum uric acid (TA-SUA) with renal outcomes of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) with chronic kidney disease (CKD).MethodsA cohort of T2DM with CKD from eight centers was followed from January 2013 to January 2023. A Cox proportional hazards regression model was designed to evaluate the association of TA-SUA with renal outcomes (defined as serum creatinine doubling, or initiation of dialysis, or kidney transplantation, or progression to end-stage kidney disease). Cox proportional hazards regression with cubic spline functions and smooth curve fitting were used to explore the nonlinear relationship. Detailed evaluations for TA-SUA were also performed using propensity score matching (PSM), subgroup analyses, and sensitivity analyses.ResultsA total of 1072 patients were enrolled with a mean follow-up duration of 60.4 ± 5.6 months. The baseline prevalence of hyperuricemia was 46.1% (50.4% in male vs. 37.9% in female). Patients with TA-SUA < 360 μmol/l demonstrated significantly better renal survival compared to those with TA-SUA ≥ 360 μmol/l (adjusted hazard ratio 0.63, 95% confidence interval, 0.41–0.98; P = 0.038). Restriction cubic spline analysis showed a rapid risk increase when TA-SUA exceeded 360 μmol/l, which was confirmed by PSM (P < 0.001). Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses showed that patients with eGFR between 30 and 59 ml/min/1.73 m2 were the most likely to benefit from maintaining TA-SUA < 360 μmol/l.ConclusionLong-term maintenance of relatively low TA-SUA levels is independently associated with slowed renal function decline and favorable renal outcomes among patients with type 2 DM with CKD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13300-026-01895-z.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Chronic kidney disease, Hyperuricemia, Time-averaged serum uric acid, Diabetic kidney disease

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) complicated by chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a high-risk population with heavy global disease burden, and traditional risk factors cannot fully explain the heterogeneity of renal function decline, while hyperuricemia is highly prevalent in this population but its relationship with renal outcomes remains controversial.Single-point serum uric acid (SUA) measurements are prone to fluctuation and reverse causation, whether time-averaged SUA (TA-SUA) can better reflect long-term uric acid exposure and its relationship with renal outcomes in patients with T2DM with CKD is unclear.What was learned from the study?This study explored the association between TA-SUA levels and renal outcomes among patients with T2DM with CKD.Our investigation indicated that long-term maintenance of relatively low TA-SUA levels is independently associated with slowed renal function decline and favorable renal outcomes among patients with T2DM with CKD.TA-SUA is a more reliable prognostic marker than baseline SUA, supporting long-term stable uric acid control to slow renal progression in T2DM with CKD.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a leading cause of mortality and disability globally [1]. In 2022, the prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) among Chinese adults reached 14.92%, and the total number of patients with T2DM is projected to reach 150 million by 2030, of whom 32.5% are expected to have chronic kidney disease (CKD). This group of patients with T2DM with CKD represents a clinically high-risk population, characterized by heterogeneous etiologies of renal injury—including diabetic kidney disease (DKD), non-diabetic kidney disease (NDKD) alone, or DKD superimposed on NDKD. All of these conditions can eventually progress to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), posing significant challenges to clinical management [2, 3].

Numerous risk factors contribute to the progression of DKD [4]. Traditional factors such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, hyperglycemia, overweight, and albuminuria are well-established to be strongly associated with adverse outcomes and complications. However, despite strict control of these factors, some patients still experience a rapid decline in renal function, suggesting that traditional risk factors alone cannot fully account for the heterogeneity in disease progression among patients with T2DM with CKD. Thus, identifying new, modifiable metabolic risk factors is crucial for optimizing the stratified management of this high-risk population.

Hyperuricemia, commonly defined as a serum uric acid (SUA) exceeding 420 μmol/l (6.8 mg/dl), is an indicator closely linked to metabolic disorders. It has been verified as an independent risk factor for CKD, cardiovascular disease, and stroke [5–7], and its prevalence is significantly elevated in T2DM populations [8–11], making it a potential candidate for explaining the heterogeneity in renal progression. However, the association between SUA and renal outcomes in patients with T2DM with CKD remains controversial. Previous studies suggest that hyperuricemia may independently contribute to the progression of renal injury [12–15]. Urate-lowering therapy has been shown to improve endothelial function, slow the decline of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and reduce the risk of renal failure [16–20]. Nevertheless, other randomized controlled trials have shown that urate-lowering therapy did not to significantly delay CKD progression in patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) regardless of CKD status [21, 22]. Several methodological factors may account for these inconsistent findings: First, prior studies predominantly relied on single SUA measurements, which are susceptible to fluctuations due to diet, lifestyle, medications, and changes in renal function, thereby failing to capture the long-term cumulative exposure. Second, renal function decline per se may reduce uric acid excretion, introducing reverse causality that confounds the association between single SUA assessments and renal outcomes [4, 14]. Third, previous studies primarily enrolled patients with DKD of single etiology or white patients with T1DM, with limited data from real-world T2DM with CKD populations with high etiological heterogeneity. Fourth, prior studies have predominantly examined the association of baseline SUA and DKD prognosis, whereas time-averaged SUA may better reflect long-term exposure status compared with single baseline measurements, potentially yielding more stable and reliable results [23, 24]. To our knowledge, the impact of TA-SUA exposure, as opposed to single measurements, on renal outcomes remains inadequately characterized in multicenter, long-term follow-up studies of patients with T2DM with CKD exhibiting high etiological heterogeneity in real-world settings. Based on this, we conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study to explore the association of TA-SUA with renal outcomes in T2DM with CKD, providing evidence for the risk stratification and intervention of this heterogeneous population.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted with the data from the Southern Chinese Registration Network of Diabetic Kidney Disease (SCRENDKD) database, which was designed to standardize the management of DKD from eight clinical sites across southern China [25]. All the patients enrolled in SCRENDKD provided written informed consent and received personalized health education at enrollment. Patients were regularly monitored according to clinical guidelines, and their management was adjusted at each scheduled visit based on home self-monitoring of body weight, blood pressure, and blood glucose, as well as laboratory assessments including biochemical parameters, 24-h urine protein, urine urea nitrogen (UUN), and urinary sodium excretion, as previously described [25].

The primary composite endpoint comprised doubling of serum creatinine, progression to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), or initiation of kidney replacement therapy such as maintenance dialysis or kidney transplantation. Survival time was measured from the time point enrolled to the endpoint, or the last time visit. The follow-up frequency was scheduled according to Standards of Medical Care in Diabetes issued by the American Diabetes Association (ADA) [1].

The outcome variable was the composite endpoint, coded as a binary variable: 0 for outcomes not reaching the endpoint, and 1 for events reaching the endpoint.

Ethical Approval

The protocol of study was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the institutional review board of the Guangdong Provincial People's Hospital [NO. GDREC2019513H(R1)]. This central ethics approval encompassed all eight clinical centers participating in the Southern Chinese Registration Network of Diabetic Kidney Disease (SCRENDKD) database. All authors received formal authorization from the SCRENDKD database committee to access and analyze the anonymized data for this study.

All participants provided written informed consent for the collection of demographic, clinical, and laboratory data; the storage and analysis of anonymized information; and the publication of aggregated results without personal identification. All data collection, biochemical testing, and clinical management adhered to routine standard clinical practice and relevant clinical guidelines. All patient data were fully de-identified and anonymized; no individual could be identified directly or indirectly from the analyzed dataset.

Study Population

All the patients diagnosed with T2DM with CKD at stages 1 to 4 in SCRENDKD were screened. Gout was diagnosed and excluded according to the 2015 ACR/EULAR gout classification criteria. We reviewed ICD-10 codes (M10), medical records, joint symptoms, and use of urate-lowering medications. We excluded patients with clinically confirmed or suspected active gout at baseline (including those with acute flares, tophi, or current use of urate-lowering medications). Patients with a history of gout (diagnosed before enrollment but inactive at baseline) were not excluded and were included in the subgroup analyses [26]. The study spanned a 10-year period from January 2013 to January 2023. T2DM was diagnosed according to Standards [1] and DKD was diagnosed based on the KDIGO guidelines [2], which were defined as T2DM combined with urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio ≥ 30 mg/g and/or eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73 m2 for more than 3 months, excluding other CKD causes, or confirmed by renal biopsy [1]. The patients were visited in the outpatient department, or by 5G Internet hospital APP, or phone as scheduled time according to protocol. Only patients with follow-up visits at least twice yearly were included in the subsequent analysis. To investigate the association between SUA levels and renal prognosis, we selected clinically relevant SUA thresholds of 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9 mg/dl (equivalent to 300, 360, 420, 480, and 540 μmol/l, respectively), based on current domestic and international clinical guidelines and consensus statements [27–29]. The distribution of SUA levels across these intervention thresholds and their correlation with renal outcomes were systematically analyzed.

Data Collection

Clinical and demographic data, usage of medications and other available information of management of DKD were collected. TA-SUA was calculated as previously described [23, 24]. Briefly, TA-SUA was defined as the area under the curve of the SUA measurements at baseline and during follow-up, divided by total follow-up duration. This approach accounts for time-dependent variables and is less influenced by diet, lifestyle, and medications, thereby providing a more accurate reflection of long-term SUA exposure during follow-up.

The body weight, blood pressure, laboratory tests, and medication data of every single visit were documented for all patients enrolled during the follow-up course. Patients with at least biannual follow-up and complete clinical documentation were considered eligible for analysis.

Covariates

Selected covariates were classified as: (1) Continuous variables including age, body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), hemoglobin (Hb), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine (SCr), estimate glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), serum uric acid (SUA), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), 24-h urine protein (24 h-UP), 24-h urine albumin (24 h-UAlb), urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR), urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), 24-h urine urea nitrogen (24 h-UUN), 24-h urine uric acid (24 h-UUA), 24-h urine sodium (24 h-UNa); (2) Categorical variables, including sex. Demographic data (age, sex) and medication details were collected through each visit to the hospital. Height, weight, and blood pressure were measured by trained staff. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2). The CKD-EPI equation was used for eGFR calculation, and a standard electronic sphygmomanometer for blood pressure measurement.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. Group comparisons were performed using Student's t test for normally distributed data or Mann–Whitney U test for skewed data. Categorical data were expressed as rates or ratios and compared using χ2 tests or nonparametric rank sum tests. One-way ANOVA and chi-square tests were used to compared clinical disparities among SUA groups. Spearman rank was used to analyze SUA and eGFR correlation.

To assess the effect of modifiable risk factors on SUA changes, we constructed four Cox proportional hazards regression models, including unadjusted model (crude model: unadjusted covariate), minimally adjusted model (model I: only adjusted for sociodemographic variables, including sex, age, SBP, BMI), partially adjusted model (model II: additionally adjusted for eGFR, Hb, HbA1c, UACR, ALB), and fully adjusted model (model III: further adjusted for lipid-lowering, hypoglycemic, antihypertensive, urate-lowering, antiplatelet medication use and history of gout). Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests were used to compare survival rates among different groups. Stratified Cox proportional hazards regression models were used for subgroup analyses for various factors (sex, age, SBP, DBP, TP, ALB, UACR, Hb, HbA1c, eGFR). For subgroup analyses, continuous variables were categorized according to clinically meaningful cutoffs: age (< 50 vs. ≥ 50 years), SBP (< 140 vs. ≥ 140 mmHg), DBP (< 90 vs. ≥ 90 mmHg), TP (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 g/l), ALB (< 30 vs. ≥ 30 g/l), UACR (< 30, 30–299, and ≥ 300 mg/g), Hb (< 110 vs. ≥ 110 g/l), HbA1c (< 8% vs. ≥ 8%), and eGFR (15–29, 30–59, 60–89, and ≥ 90 ml/min/1.73 m2). Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using a 1:1 nearest-neighbor algorithm with a caliper width of 0.2, based on sex, age, BMI, BUN, SCr, UACR, and eGFR, to assess outcomes across different SUA levels. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed for renal outcomes in the matched cohort. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R statistical software (http://www.r-project.org, The R Foundation). This study adhered to the Sex and Gender Equity in Research (SAGER) guidelines throughout the whole process of study design, data collection, statistical analysis, and manuscript reporting. Sex and gender variables were clearly distinguished and fully reported without selective reporting or gender-related bias, ensuring the fairness and authenticity of the research results. All tests were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of Participants

Of 1651 screened participants, 1072 were included in the final analysis. The mean age was 56.5 ± 10.4 years, and 700 (65.3%) were male. A total of 579 patients were excluded for the following reasons: baseline eGFR < 15 ml/min/1.73 m2 (n = 16), age > 80 years (n = 12), fewer than 2 follow-up visits (n = 498), and incomplete data (n = 53). The mean follow-up duration was 60.4 ± 5.6 months. During follow-up, 272 endpoint events occurred: 103 patients had doubling of serum creatinine, and 169 patients progressed to ESKD or initiated dialysis or underwent renal transplantation (Fig. 1).

Baseline SUA and TA-SUA values were normally distributed, with mean values of 420.8 ± 111.5 μmol/l and 407.5 ± 81.3 μmol/l, respectively (P = 0.0016) (Supplementary Fig. S1). In the hyperuricemia group, the proportions of male sex, comorbidities, and impaired renal function were significantly higher compared with the SUA < 420 μmol/l group (P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in other clinical or demographic parameters between groups (Table 1).

Correlation of SUA with Renal Outcomes

Baseline SUA levels showed a negative linear correlation with eGFR (Supplementary Fig. S4). Both baseline SUA and TA-SUA were significantly higher in patients who reached the composite endpoint compared with those who did not (Supplementary Fig. S5). The cumulative incidence rates of the composite endpoint were 16.5, 14.0, and 7.95 per 100 person-years for baseline SUA, and 18.1, 12.9, and 6.7 per 100 person-years for TA-SUA respectively (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Survival Analysis According to Different SUA levels

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that patients with baseline SUA and TA-SUA less than 360 μmol/l had significantly higher survival rates than those with more than 360 μmol/l (Fig. 2a and d). Similarly, patients with baseline SUA and TA-SUA less than 420 μmol/l had significantly higher survival rates than those with more than 420 μmol/l (Fig. 2b and e). Patients with baseline SUA and TA-SUA between 360 to < 420 μmol/l exhibited a middle survival rate (Fig. 2c and 2f). Both baseline SUA and TA-SUA were significantly associated with renal survival.

Face-to-Face Comparison of Predictive Performance Between Baseline SUA and TA-SUA

To further compare the prognostic value of baseline SUA and TA-SUA, a face-to-face predictive model comparison was performed. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and time-dependent AUC showed that the model based on TA-SUA achieved a significantly higher discriminative capacity than the model based on baseline SUA. Variable importance assessment further demonstrated that TA-SUA contributed more strongly to renal outcome prediction than baseline SUA (Supplementary Fig. S7). These findings indicated that TA-SUA serves as a more stable and reliable prognostic marker than single baseline SUA in patients with T2DM with CKD.

Cox Regression Analysis of Risk of Renal Outcomes According to Baseline SUA and TA-SUA

Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed to evaluate the association between baseline SUA, TA-SUA, and renal outcomes (Table 2). Patients with baseline SUA or TA-SUA < 360 μmol/l served as the reference group and had the lowest hazard ratios (HRs) for renal outcomes. After adjustment for confounders, the HR of renal outcomes increased progressively with each 60 μmol/l (1 mg/dl) elevation in baseline SUA and TA-SUA. During follow-up, patients with TA-SUA < 360 μmol/l had significantly better renal survival than those with TA-SUA 360 to < 420 μmol/l or ≥ 420 μmol/l (P for trend < 0.001), findings that were corroborated in multivariable-adjusted models. A non-linear relationship between SUA levels and the HR of renal outcomes was observed after adjustment of multivariable factors (Fig. 3). Specifically, the risk of renal outcomes remained stable below TA-SUA 360 μmol/l but increased sharply when TA-SUA exceeded this threshold. The lowest elevated risk was observed in the TA-SUA 360 to < 420 μmol/l subgroup (HR 1.81, 95% CI 1.24–2.65; P = 0.002) in the minimally adjusted model.

Subgroup Analyses

To further investigate the impact of confounding factors on the composite outcomes, subgroup analyses were conducted. As shown in Fig. 4, patients with TA-SUA < 360 μmol/l demonstrated significantly lower HRs for the composite endpoint in most subgroups. Patients with CKD stage 3 (eGFR 30–59 ml/min/1.73 m2) exhibited a 57% reduction in the risk of composite endpoint (HR 0.43, 95% CI 0.21–0.89; P = 0.023) when TA-SUA was maintained < 360 μmol/l compared with ≥ 360 μmol/l. However, no significant reduction in HR was observed in CKD stage 1–2 (eGFR ≥ 60 ml/min/1.73 m2; HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.36–1.69; P = 0.521) or stage 4 (eGFR < 30 ml/min/1.73 m2; HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.31–1.04; P = 0.067) groups. Additionally, no significant reduction of HR was observed in individuals with SBP < 140 mmHg, or UACR < 300 mg/g. Although patients without a history of gout exhibited a 65% decreased risk of composite outcomes (HR 0.35, 95% CI 0.23–0.54; P < 0.001) when TA-SUA < 360 μmol/l, no significant reduction of HR was observed in patients with a history of gout (HR 0.97, 95% CI 0.54–1.76; P = 0.929) unexpectedly. These findings suggest that the prognostic value of TA-SUA in patients with T2DM with CKD varies substantially by gout history. Subgroup analyses stratified by sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2i) and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1RA) use were also performed. The renoprotective association of TA-SUA < 360 μmol/l remained significant both in users and nonusers of SGLT2i or GLP-1RA (all P for interaction > 0.05), confirming that the benefit of low TA-SUA was independent of these medications.

Propensity Score Matching Analysis of the Association of SUA with Renal Outcomes

To further minimize potential selection bias and confounding, we performed Cox regression analysis in a 1:1 propensity score-matched cohort based on sex, age, BMI, BUN, SCr, UACR, and eGFR. As detailed in Supplementary Table S2, baseline characteristics were well balanced between the 291 matched pairs of patients with SUA < 360 μmol/l and ≥ 360 μmol/l. Kaplan–Meier analysis with log-rank testing revealed significant differences in survival probability among different TA-SUA levels groups. Consistent with the finding in original cohort, patients with TA-SUA < 360 μmol/l exhibited a markedly improved survival probability compared to those with TA-SUA ≥ 420 μmol/l or 360 to < 420 μmol/l (P < 0.001). Sensitivity Analyses

To assess the robustness of the primary results, four sensitivity analyses were conducted using Kaplan–Meier survival curves (Supplementary Fig. S8). After excluding patients with a follow-up duration < 12 months, the renal survival advantage of TA-SUA < 360 μmol/l remained significant (log-rank P < 0.001). When serum creatinine doubling was removed from the composite endpoint, the prognostic value of TA-SUA < 360 μmol/l was unchanged (log-rank P < 0.001). In the analysis restricted to male patients, TA-SUA < 360 μmol/l was still associated with superior renal survival (log-rank P < 0.001). After excluding patients who received urate-lowering therapy during follow-up, the association remained robust (log-rank P < 0.001). Additionally, to quantitatively assess the potential impact of reverse causality, we conducted a formal E-value sensitivity analysis. The results demonstrated that to eliminate the observed association, unmeasured confounding factors would need to increase both SUA and renal risk by more than twofold. This suggests that reverse causality is unlikely to explain our primary study findings (Supplementary Table S3).

Overall, all sensitivity analyses supported the primary conclusion that maintaining TA-SUA < 360 μmol/l is associated with favorable renal outcomes in patients with T2DM with CKD.

Discussion

In the present study, we observed that elevated TA-SUA levels were associated with a significantly increased risk of adverse renal outcomes (including doubling of serum creatinine, ESKD, initiation of dialysis or kidney transplantation) in patients with T2DM complicated by CKD during the follow-up cohort. Patients with TA-SUA < 360 μmol/l demonstrated significantly improved survival probability compared to those ≥ 360 μmol/l after adjusting for multivariable factors. PSM, subgroup analyses, and sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these results and further revealed that patients with eGFR between 30–59 ml/min/1.73 m2 were the most likely to benefit from maintaining TA-SUA < 360 μmol/l by improved renal outcomes.

Previous studies highlight the highest prevalence of hyperuricemia among the patients with T2DM [8–10]. A recent cross-sectional study in China [4] reported a hyperuricemia prevalence of 21.6% in type 2 DKD patients, who were more likely to be assigned to higher KDIGO risk categories. Our study cohort exhibited an even higher prevalence of hyperuricemia (46.1%) compared with that reported previously (32.4%) [30]. This may be attributable to the fact that the majority of our participants resided in southern coastal areas, where diets rich in purines (e.g., seafood and red meat) are common [31]. Moreover, our subjects were predominantly middle-aged and elderly, with a concomitant decline in kidney function and reduced uric acid excretion capacity compared to younger individuals [32]. Although 42.7% of enrolled patients received urate-lowering therapy and all patients received health education with regularly scheduled visits, the high prevalence of hyperuricemia in our cohort suggests poor patient compliance, which needs to be improved. Consistent with previous studies [33–35], our findings revealed a higher prevalence of hyperuricemia in males, which was attributed to the uricosuric effects of estrogen.

SUA has emerged as a modifiable risk factor for CKD and has garnered increasing recognition in recent years [19, 36]. Several large prospective observational studies have suggested that elevated SUA levels are associated with an increased risk of proteinuria and that hyperuricemia may promote DKD progression [37, 38]. Other studies have proposed that elevated SUA could be a significant predictor of the transition from microalbuminuria to macroalbuminuria in advanced DKD [39]. Increasing evidences highlight that hyperuricemia is a crucial predictor of the progression and decline of DKD [4, 5, 21, 40]. A multicenter retrospective real-world study showed that both baseline and time-averaged SUA were correlated with the risk of composite outcomes of DKD after multivariable adjustment. However, only TA-SUA, and not baseline SUA, that was significantly associated with the increased risk of DKD composite outcomes when TA-SUA exceeded 360 μmol/l. It is crucial to emphasize that our data indicate that maintaining low TA-SUA levels may help protect this high-risk patient population from rapid progression to endpoint events.

However, a recent meta-analysis of 722 patients with DKD showed that allopurinol did not significantly improve renal function or reduce albuminuria compared with placebo [41]. Two randomized controlled trials reached similar conclusions, suggesting that allopurinol urate-lowering therapy did not slow the decline of eGFR and was not clinically significant for renal outcomes [21, 22]. These inconsistent findings may stem from the dynamic nature of SUA levels during disease progression; single SUA measurements used in these studies may be insufficient to capture longitudinal changes and the cumulative burden of elevated SUA exposure. Furthermore, those studies predominantly enrolled white patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and early-stage CKD, thereby excluding T2DM populations and patients with normal uric acid levels or gout [21, 22]. These limitations restrict the generalizability of findings regarding the relationship between SUA levels and long-term outcomes.

Regardless of the underlying mechanisms linking SUA to DKD progression, lowering SUA levels in patients with T2DM has been associated with attenuated eGFR decline and reduced proteinuria [4, 21, 42–48]. According to recent studies, SUA levels should be lowered to < 360 μmol/l in patients with gouty arthritis or hyperuricemia with organ damage [49, 50]. As a matter of fact, clinical guidelines from the Chinese Endocrine Society recommend a target SUA level of < 360 μmol/l for patients with T2DM-associated CKD and suggest pharmacological intervention for individuals with asymptomatic hyperuricemia when SUA levels persistently exceed 480 μmol/l [48]. Our data exhibited that maintaining TA-SUA < 360 μmol/l yielded favorable prognostic improvements. Further subgroup analysis revealed that the significant association between favorable composite outcomes and TA-SUA < 360 μmol/l was observed only in patients with eGFR 30 to 59 ml/min/1.73 m2, but not in those with eGFR ≥ 60 or < 30 ml/min/1.73 m2. This may be because patients at early stage (CKD1-2) require longer follow-up to reach the endpoint, whereas risk factors are too multifactorial to isolate the effect of SUA at advanced stages (CKD 4). Notably, uricosuric agents such as losartan, SGLT2i, GLP-1RA, and diuretics may affect both kidney outcomes and serum uric acid levels [51–53]. The renal effects of these drugs have recently garnered considerable interest. Interestingly, the present findings indicate that none of aforementioned agents exerted a statistically significant effect on the primary renal outcomes assessed. Unfortunately, due to limited data, we could not perform subgroup analyses of the potential mixed effects of analgesics, NSAIDs, diet, alcohol intake, or other interventions on outcomes.

Consistent with previous RCTs, our data also indicated that higher TA-SUA was associated with a greater risk of eGFR decline in patients without a history of gout. In patients with gout, the use of nephrotoxic nonsteroidal analgesics and marked fluctuations in SUA levels complicate the relationship between uric acid and endpoint events. Despite the extreme complexity of renal damage in gout, post-hoc analysis of the CARES trial showed that urate-lowering therapy delayed CKD progression in patients with gout [36].

This study enrolled a relatively large sample size with a long follow-up period, ensuring a sufficient number of composite endpoint events. Moreover, the well-established multicenter design helps reduce bias in the conclusion drawn. Importantly, to address potential reverse causality, we used TA-SUA rather than single baseline SUA, which reduces bias from late renal function decline. TA-SUA, rather than single time-point measurement, provides a more comprehensive assessment of the impact of uric acid on composite outcomes. TA-SUA may act as both a causal mediator and a high-fidelity surrogate marker in renal function decline among patients with T2DM with CKD. Elevated long-term uric acid exposure induces tubular inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction. Meanwhile, TA-SUA reflects overall metabolic control stability. After multivariable adjustment, TA-SUA remained independently associated with renal outcomes, supporting its dual role [6, 50, 54, 55]. Furthermore, E-value sensitivity analysis showed that an unmeasured confounder would need to increase both SUA and renal risk by more than twofold to eliminate the observed association, suggesting that reverse causality is unlikely to explain our main findings [4, 19, 50, 56, 57]. However, we did not perform a time-dependent Cox model with time-varying eGFR, which may limit our ability to fully account for the time-dependent decline in renal function and its impact on the association between SUA and outcomes. Future studies with complete longitudinal renal function data are needed to validate our results using time-varying models. Impressively, we performed three sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings: First, we estimated the marginal effects of potential confounders (CKD stage, potential uricosuric agents [SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonist, losartan], age and sex) separately. Second, we estimated the marginal effects in 1:1 propensity score-matched cohorts with balanced baseline characteristics. Third, we estimated the marginal effect in patients with or without a history of gout. Furthermore, the face-to-face comparison of predictive models further strengthened the core advantages of TA-SUA over baseline SUA in prognostic evaluation. In the present study, the TA-SUA-based prediction model showed superior AUC levels, stable time-dependent predictive efficacy, and higher variable importance for renal outcomes relative to baseline SUA, fully demonstrating that long-term averaged uric acid exposure is a more reliable prognostic indicator than a single baseline measurement [23]. Although the current study focused primarily on exploring the independent association between TA-SUA and renal progression rather than establishing a complete clinical prediction tool, these comparative analyses confirm the robustness and superiority of TA-SUA as a prognostic biomarker. Future studies could further incorporate TA-SUA into classic renal risk prediction systems, such as the Kidney Failure Risk Equation (KFRE) and CKD-EPI-based risk models, and comprehensively evaluate the incremental predictive value using C-statistic, net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) to develop more precise individualized risk prediction tools for T2DM with CKD populations [43].

This study has some limitations. First, our analysis cannot address all potential hidden biases and confounding factors, such as the effects of specific dietary intake. Due to the retrospective design and feasibility constraints, routine dual-energy CT was not performed to screen for asymptomatic silent gout; therefore, underdiagnosed silent gout may exist in a small proportion of participants. Second, as a retrospective study, clinical data may be subject to various biases. The diagnosis of T2DM with CKD was based partly on clinical criteria rather than a definitive kidney biopsy, so the presence of non-diabetic nephropathy cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution and warrant validation in prospective studies. Finally, further mechanistic studies are needed to elucidate the prognostic relationship between hyperuricemia and T2DM with CKD. Although the prevailing view holds that higher uric acid levels are associated with worse outcomes in patients with T2DM and CKD, kidney damage per se can also increase hyperuricemia risk, making it difficult to establish causality within this vicious cycle.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this multicenter retrospective cohort study in southern China demonstrates an association between SUA levels and renal outcomes in patients of T2DM with CKD. A substantial association was observed between TA-SUA < 360 μmol/l and favorable renal composite endpoints. Although our data highlight the importance of maintaining low SUA levels in the management of high-risk patients of T2DM with CKD, these findings may have limited generalizability due to the specific study population and design characteristics. Therefore, prospective clinical studies involving more diverse populations are needed to confirm these findings.
