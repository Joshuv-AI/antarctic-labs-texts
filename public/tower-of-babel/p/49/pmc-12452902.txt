Directed evolution of a plant Rubisco chaperone with altered client recognition
Authors: Siyu Li, ByungUk Lee, Yichong Lao, Sirawit Lertwiriyapiti, Xuhui Huang, Tina Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe enzyme Rubisco plays a crucial role in photosynthesis by fixing carbon dioxide into biomass and is a major engineering target for improving crop yields. However, its biogenesis, requiring up to seven different assembly factors, complicates introducing heterologous Rubiscos into plants because endogenous chaperones often fail to recognize a nonnative Rubisco. Here, we investigated whether chaperone incompatibility can be resolved by engineering a Rubisco chaperone to assemble a foreign Rubisco that it does not natively recognize. To do so, we developed an assay in Escherichia coli to detect the activity of Rubisco chaperones in high throughput, which we employed to evolve the Rubisco chaperone Raf1 to recognize and assemble plant Rubiscos in a more promiscuous manner.

In nature, the enzyme Rubisco catalyzes the first step of the Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle to fix atmospheric CO2 into biomass. Rubisco’s relatively slow rate of reaction and propensity for reaction with O2, which generates a toxic byproduct that must be recycled through energetically wasteful photorespiration, are thought to bottleneck photosynthesis. Significant effort has been invested in trying to increase agricultural productivity through improving Rubisco activity, whether by engineering better-performing Rubisco variants or through transgenic expression of catalytically superior Rubisco orthologs in plants. However, these efforts have only yielded modest successes to date (1, 2).

Plant Rubiscos, which belong to the Form I structural class, comprise a hexadecameric complex formed from eight large subunits (RbcL) and eight small subunits (RbcS). In plants, Rubisco holoenzyme formation is mediated by up to seven assembly factors: chaperonins Cpn60α, Cpn60β, and Cpn20, and chaperones Raf1, RbcX, Raf2, and BSD2 (Fig. 1A) (3–5). After ribosomal synthesis, RbcL is folded by the Cpn60α/Cpn60β/Cpn20 chaperonin system(6), then stapled into a series of oligomeric assembly intermediates by chaperones Raf1 (7–9), RbcX (10–12), and BSD2 (3) [the role of Raf2(13) in this process remains unclear]. Some Rubisco chaperones are selective for the Rubisco they evolved to assemble and can be highly sensitive to changes to RbcL (3, 14–18). For example, substituting RbcL from Arabidopsis thaliana for the native RbcL in Nicotiana tabacum reduces assembled Rubisco by fourfold even though these orthologs share 94% sequence identity. Providing A. thaliana Raf1 partially rescued assembly, suggesting that the native tobacco Raf1 inefficiently recognizes A. thaliana RbcL (14). Similar incompatibilities between A. thaliana Raf1 and N. tabacum Rubisco have been observed in Escherichia coli systems for plant Rubisco production (3). Recent studies profiling the activity of specific Rubisco assembly factors on a range of plant Rubisco orthologs in both E. coli and tobacco models also revealed multiple instances where the assembly factors failed to assemble noncognate Rubiscos (15). Together, these precedents suggest that chaperone compatibility may pose a serious roadblock to engineering Rubisco or expressing nonnative orthologs in plants, as both of these undertakings necessitate changing RbcL from the native sequence recognized by Rubisco chaperones (1, 19).

The ability to reprogram chaperones to recognize mutated or nonnative Rubisco may address a significant bottleneck in Rubisco engineering, improve the expression of heterologous Rubiscos in plants, and aid in the study of new Rubisco orthologs. Groundbreaking work from Aigner et al. demonstrated that Rubiscos from vascular plants can be assembled in E. coli by coexpressing the corresponding chloroplastic assembly factors (3). Production of functional plant Rubisco chaperones in a genetically tractable model organism provides an opportunity to explore whether chaperone activity or selectivity can be altered using protein engineering techniques. Nevertheless, there has only been a single example where a plant Rubisco chaperone was engineered for the improved assembly of a nonnative client: A. thaliana Raf1 was rationally mutated to enable it to assemble the Rubisco from cyanobacteria Synechococcus elongatus PCC6301 in a BSD2-independent manner (16). However, the strategy employed in that study was guided by structural information on the A. thaliana Raf1/S. elongatus RbcL complex and is unlikely to work in cases where the wild-type chaperone has little starting ability to assemble the target Rubisco, or where there is insufficient information to guide rational engineering.

Directed evolution is an alternative approach for protein engineering that uses the selection of randomized libraries of a given protein of interest to identify variants with improved properties (20). Compared to rational mutagenesis, this method requires far less to be known about the protein of interest and may be therefore be better suited for chaperone engineering given the complexity and dynamic nature of the plant Rubisco assembly process. In addition, conventional directed evolution techniques can explore a large number (up to ~108) of protein variants, enabling more thorough investigation of sequence space to uncover rare mutations that confer activity upon weakly or inactive parent proteins. However, directed evolution requires the ability to detect the phenotype or activity under selection in high throughput; existing strategies that assess Rubisco chaperone function utilize biochemical techniques such as native PAGE and size-exclusion chromatography that are throughput constrained. While several scalable methods for assessing Rubisco activity have been reported (21–24), these do not provide a direct readout on Rubisco chaperone activity. Furthermore, their compatibility with plant Rubiscos and their assembly factors is yet to be demonstrated.

Here, we report a strategy for the directed evolution of Rubisco chaperones to improve their ability to assemble nonnative clients. By leveraging an oligomerization-dependent transcription factor, we developed a genetically encoded selection that correlates Rubisco chaperone activity with expression of a reporter gene. This strategy allows us to assess the ability of ~105 chaperone mutants to assemble a specific RbcL ortholog in a single experiment. We employed our selection strategy to evolve the Rubisco chaperone Raf1 from the vascular plant A. thaliana to assemble a nonnative client, N. tabacum Rubisco, on which it has minimal starting activity. Raf1s can exhibit high selectivity for their native RbcLs, presenting an obstacle to heterologous Rubisco assembly: For instance, A. thaliana Rubisco assembly factors cannot assemble N. tabacum Rubisco unless A. thaliana Raf1 is replaced with N. tabacum Raf1 (3, 18). After four rounds of directed evolution, we obtained A. thaliana Raf1 variants that exhibit a substantially increased ability to assemble N. tabacum Rubisco compared to the wild-type chaperone. Characterization of the evolved A. thaliana Raf1s suggests that they retain the ability to assemble their native client, A. thaliana Rubisco, and can furthermore assemble additional Rubiscos that they were not evolved to recognize. Our results demonstrate the feasibility of using directed evolution to alter Rubisco chaperone selectivity and may enable future engineering of chaperones that promote the assembly of high-value Rubisco orthologs in plants.

Results

High-Throughput Detection of Plant Rubisco Chaperone Activity.

We first set out to develop a strategy to detect Rubisco chaperone activity in a manner that could be adapted for use in directed evolution. We hypothesized that we could sense plant Rubisco chaperone activity by detecting RbcL assembly intermediates, which are made up of RbcL oligomers complexed with chaperones (Fig. 1A), in E. coli. To do so, we fused an oligomerization-dependent transcription factor, cCadC (25), to RbcL from A. thaliana (AtRbcL). cCadC is by itself monomeric and transcriptionally inactive, but when self-associated by a fused oligomerizing protein, activates transcription of genes under the cadBA promoter (PcadBA). Coexpression of A. thaliana Rubisco chaperones with cCadC-AtRbcL in the absence of RbcS should promote the formation of AtRbcL8 complexes, thus bringing the fused cCadC domains in close enough proximity to each other to activate PcadBA transcription (Fig. 1B). While other oligomeric states of the RbcL dimer may be transiently populated during plant Rubisco assembly, the predominant intermediates sufficiently stable for observation contain BSD2-associated RbcL8 cores (3, 17), leading us to hypothesize that the primary chaperone-assembled species sensed in this assay will consist of cCadC-RbcL octamers.

In the presence of all seven assembly factors (A. thaliana chaperonins Cpn60α/Cpn60β/Cpn20 and chaperones Raf1/RbcX/Raf2/BSD2), the cCadC-AtRbcL fusion produced robust gfp expression from the cadBA promoter (Fig. 2 A and B). Next, we assessed the chaperone dependence of assembly. Removal of chaperones Raf1, Raf2, or BSD2 significantly decreased GFP signal, while RbcX deletion had no effect. For comparison, we measured the assembly of unfused A. thaliana Rubisco generated from untagged AtRbcL and a C-terminally 6xHis-tagged A. thaliana RbcS by native PAGE. (We were unable to detect assembly of the cCadC-AtRbcL fusion using biochemical techniques, likely due to the instability of the cCadC DNA binding domain in the absence of DNA.) In excellent agreement with the GFP signal produced by the cCadC-AtRbcL fusion, robust A. thaliana Rubisco assembly is only observed when the three chaperones Raf1, Raf2, and BSD2 are all present, while RbcX appears to be dispensable (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Raf1 and RbcX are believed to share some overlap in their roles in Rubisco assembly and Raf1 can mediate assembly on its own when RbcX is deleted (3, 17). It is possible that our system expresses sufficiently high levels of Raf1 to fully compensate for the removal of RbcX. The sensitivity of cCadC-AtRbcL activity to BSD2 deletion suggests that we may be detecting a BSD2-bound RbcL8 complex, which is thought to be the final chaperone-containing intermediate prior to RbcL8:RbcS8 formation (Fig. 1A). To further verify that GFP signal depends upon BSD2 activity, we installed a W108A/L109E double mutation in BSD2 (“BSD2 mut”), which abrogates BSD2 binding to RbcL (3). As expected, the BSD2 W108A/L109E double mutant greatly reduced GFP signal from cCadC-AtRbcL transcription and also abolished A. thaliana Rubisco assembly (Fig. 2 B and C). The data in Fig. 2 B and C are thus consistent with the current understanding of the plant Rubisco biogenesis pathway and suggest that our cCadC fusions can translate chaperone-dependent assembly of AtRbcL into a signal that can be measured at scale (GFP fluorescence).

Next, we tested whether the assembly of cCadC-RbcL fusions is sensitive to Rubisco chaperone selectivity by assessing cCadC-N. tabacum RbcL (cCadC-NtRbcL) activation by cognate and noncognate assembly factors. It has been shown that A. thaliana assembly factors inefficiently assemble N. tabacum Rubisco unless the chaperone Raf1 is replaced with its N. tabacum ortholog (3). We observed similar selectivity trends in our GFP assay. A. thaliana assembly factors failed to activate cCadC-NtRbcL transcriptional activity, whereas replacement of AtRaf1 with NtRaf1 greatly increased GFP expression by cCadC-NtRbcL (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Together, these results establish an assay for linking plant Rubisco chaperone activity to the production of a signal (GFP fluorescence) that is compatible with high-throughput detection.

Directed evolution of A. thaliana Raf1 to assemble N. tabacum RbcL.

To explore whether we can alter the selectivity of Rubisco chaperones by directed evolution, we chose to evolve AtRaf1 to assemble N. tabacum Rubisco, which it does not recognize (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). AtRaf1 is composed of an N-terminal α domain (residues 75 to 264) and a C-terminal β domain (residues 288 to 434), which are connected by a flexible linker (residues 265 to 287) that is rich in charged residues (7). Although there are no reported structures of AtRaf1 bound to RbcLs of plant origin, structures using prokaryotic homologs indicate that the α and β domains of Raf1 make extensive contacts with RbcL (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S3) (9, 16), making it challenging to predict specific regions in AtRaf1 to mutagenize to change its selectivity. Similarly, alignments between the AtRaf1 and NtRaf1 amino acid sequences do not reveal any obvious regions that might be important for their selectivity for cognate RbcLs (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Thus, we decided to generate a random mutagenesis library of AtRaf1 variants to select for the ability to assemble NtRbcL.

In the first round of evolution, a library of AtRaf1 variants generated through error-prone PCR (4 × 105 transformants, ~3 mutations/gene) was selected for GFP expression by cCadC-NtRbcL using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). The other six A. thaliana Rubisco chaperones were coexpressed but not mutagenized. The sorted population (top ~1% most fluorescent cells) was then used as the template for a second round of diversification by error-prone PCR. This time, the resultant library (3 × 105 transformants) was selected for cCadC-NtRbcL activation by fluorescent colony picking (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). After these two rounds, we selected 16 clones (2a–p, Fig. 3C) for characterization. Sanger sequencing revealed significant sequence convergence across the enriched AtRaf1 variants. We observed mutations in both the Raf1α and Raf1β domains. In addition, a small number of mutations mapped to the flexible charged linker that connects the two domains. Many of the α domain mutations conferred modest changes, while in contrast, the β domain mutations were more drastic, resulting in the neutralization or inversion of several charged residues (Fig. 3C). When tested individually with cCadC-NtRbcL, 2a–p exhibited increased cCadC activation and GFP expression compared to wild-type AtRaf1 (Fig. 3 C and D). We then assessed the ability of 2a–p to assemble unfused N. tabacum Rubisco (using untagged RbcL and a C-terminally 6xHis-tagged RbcS) through native PAGE and western blotting. Several of the evolved clones exhibited increased ability to mediate N. tabacum Rubisco assembly compared to wild-type AtRaf1, with the most active showing ~fourfold higher activity estimated by gel densitometry (Fig. 3E and SI Appendix, Fig. S7).

Some variants produced over 10-fold higher GFP signal from cCadC-NtRbcL activation but failed to improve N. tabacum Rubisco assembly to the same extent. This suggests a disconnect between cCadC-NtRbcL activation and the production of fully assembled N. tabacum Rubisco, which might stem from our system detecting a RbcL8 assembly intermediate instead of the full RbcL8S8 complex. Many of these variants, including 2f, 2j, 2l, and 2m, share E314K and E336K mutations in Raf1β. To obtain insight into how these mutations might promote cCadC-NtRbcL activation, we conducted molecular dynamics (MD) simulations comparing the wild-type AtRaf1 β domain with the 2j mutant β domain (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). The wild-type system was initialized directly from an AtRaf1β crystal structure (PDB ID: 4WT4), while the 2j mutant was derived from the same structure by introducing E314K/E336K. We performed three independent 2-μs simulations for both systems (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). We observed large differences in the 2j mutant compared to wild-type AtRaf1β. In wild-type AtRaf1β dimers, E314 makes intramonomer contacts with R343 and E336 interacts intermolecularly with R343 in the other monomer, potentially through salt bridges (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B). In wild-type AtRaf1β, our simulations predicted pairwise average distances between the nearest carbon atoms of E314 and R343 within the same monomer and between E336 and R343 in the adjacent monomer of 5.5 ± 0.4 Å and 4.8 ± 0.2 Å, respectively. However, for the 2j mutant, the average distances increased markedly to 15.2 ± 0.2 Å for K314–R343 and 7.1 ± 0.2 Å for K336–R343, suggesting that the E314K and E336K mutations disrupt interactions with R343 and trigger greater flexibility in the AtRaf1β structure. Interestingly, inspection of a recently reported cryo-EM structure of AtRaf1β complexed with S. elongatus PCC 6301 RbcL (16) suggests that AtRaf1β undergoes a conformational change that increases the E336-R343 distance upon RbcL binding (SI Appendix, Fig. S8C), potentially indicating weakened interaction for this residue pair (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 B and D). This leads us to hypothesize that the E314K and E336K mutations may mimic the conformational changes taking place in AtRaf1β during RbcL binding to favor the formation of Raf1-bound RbcL intermediates. Such intermediates are expected to enable cCadC-NtRbcL activation but may interfere with displacement by RbcS to form fully assembled Rubisco.

Site-Saturation Mutagenesis of Raf1β Helix 14.

Of the active variants identified from our second round of directed evolution, 2b is notable because of its N351Y mutation, located in helix 14 of Raf1β. This helix faces a region in NtRbcL which contains several residues that differ from AtRbcL (Fig. 4A). We previously discovered that replacement of this region in NtRbcL with the corresponding sequence from AtRbcL increases assembly of the resulting chimeric RbcL by AtRaf1 (18). Thus, we wondered whether targeted mutagenesis of helix 14 would enable the identification of AtRaf1 variants with an increased ability to assemble N. tabacum Rubisco.

Alignment of the helix 14 sequences of At and NtRaf1 reveals differences at five positions (Fig. 4A). Using 2b and 2g as templates, we cloned site-saturation mutagenesis libraries (~5 × 105 transformants) randomizing these positions with degenerate NNK codons and selected them for cCadC-NtRbcL assembly by fluorescent colony picking. Interestingly, the helix 14 site-saturation library cloned from 2b yielded very few fluorescent colonies. This suggests that the N351Y mutation, which was the only 2b mutation remutagenized in this library, may play a critical role in the activity of variant 2b. In contrast, we observed many fluorescent members of the library generated from 2g and picked 14 clones (3a–n, Fig. 4b) for downstream characterization. Sanger sequencing revealed convergence at several of the randomized positions (Fig. 4B). Positions 353 and 356, which face away from the RbcL surface, strongly favored branched aliphatic sidechains. In contrast, positions 351 and 355, which face RbcL, were enriched in polar or positively charged residues, suggesting that these residues may engage in hydrogen bonding or electrostatic interactions with RbcL (Fig. 4C).

We tested the ability of the helix 14 mutants to mediate N. tabacum Rubisco assembly by native PAGE and western blotting (Fig. 4D and SI Appendix, Fig. S10). One of the clones, 3n, exhibited ~threefold increased N. tabacum Rubisco assembly compared to the parent 2g, as estimated by gel densitometry. However, none of the Raf1β helix 14 mutants were able to assemble N. tabacum Rubisco to as great of an extent as NtRaf1. It is possible that, while interactions with Raf1β helix 14 impact AtRaf1 recognition of RbcL, changes to these interactions may not by themselves be sufficient to enable robust assembly of a noncognate RbcL by AtRaf1.

Crossing α and β Domain Mutations.

Our efforts to select AtRaf1 variants from globally mutagenized libraries revealed that mutations in Raf1β domain could strongly activate the cCadC-NtRbcL biosensor without increasing N. tabacum Rubisco assembly (Fig. 3 C–E). To avoid the occurrence of such mutations in our fourth and final round of directed evolution, we opted to select a library where only the α domain of variants 2b and 2g was diversified using error-prone PCR (~2 mutations/gene). These diversified Raf1αs were fused with Raf1βs from variants 2b, 2g, and 3n to generate a library where the α and β domains from all evolved variants were scrambled (Fig. 5A). We sorted a library of ~3 × 105 variants by FACS for members that induced increased cCadC-NtRbcL-dependent GFP expression compared to 2b and 2g (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). The top 3% most fluorescent cells were then subcloned back into the parent vector and screened for cCadC-NtRbcL GFP activation as individual colonies. From this secondary screen, 10 mutants were chosen for sequencing and downstream characterization (Fig. 5 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S12). Many of the selected mutants bore α and β domains from different round 2 variants (Fig. 5B). None of the selected variants bore the 3n Raf1β helix 14 mutations, although 3n was included as a template in the library cloning. When tested individually with cCadC-NtRbcL, several of the AtRaf1 variants exhibited similar GFP signal as NtRaf1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). Additionally, variants 4h and 4p exhibited increased ability to assemble unfused N. tabacum Rubisco compared to 2b and 2g (Fig. 5D and SI Appendix, Fig. S13).

To gain insight into the origins of the AtRaf1 variants’ gain of function on NtRbcL, we mapped the mutations in 4p onto the structure of AtRaf1 bound to S. elongatus RbcL (Fig. 5 D and E). In general, mutated residues in 4p did not match the NtRaf1 sequence (Fig. 5B). Most mutated residues in both the α and β domains of 4p reside along the Raf1:RbcL binding interface, suggesting alteration of contacts between AtRaf1 and NtRbcL. The exceptions are I90V and R139H, which occur at positions that face into solvent. In addition, E275V is located in the highly charged linker between the α and β domains, which is not resolved in the structure. Interestingly, R139 and R432, which are conserved between At and NtRaf1, were both mutated in 4p. R432 is a highly conserved residue that forms intramolecular contacts with D368 and E379 (SI Appendix, Fig. S14); the R432G mutation would likely destabilize the β domain, and it is not clear to us how this mutation contributes to 4p’s activity. We also reverted the individual mutations of variant 4p to determine their contribution toward NtRbcL recognition. No reversion led to a substantial loss in N. tabacum Rubisco levels, although some appeared to slightly decrease N. tabacum Rubisco assembly (Fig. 5F and SI Appendix, Fig. S15). This suggests that the individual contribution of each mutation to 4p’s activity is modest.

Characterization of Evolved A. thaliana Raf1 Variants.

We compared variants 2b and 2g from the second round of directed evolution, 3n from the third round, and 4h and 4p from the fourth round for their abilities to mediate N. tabacum Rubisco assembly. All five evolved variants assembled N. tabacum Rubisco, albeit to different degrees, with 3n, 4h, and 4p possessing the highest apparent activity (Fig. 6A). SDS-PAGE of cell lysates also showed an increase in the levels of soluble NtRbcL in the presence of our evolved AtRaf1 variants compared to wild-type AtRaf1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S16). We estimated the yield of assembled N. tabacum Rubisco using small scale affinity purifications (SI Appendix, Figs. S17 and S18 and Table S1). In these experiments, AtRaf1 was observed to assemble only trace amounts of N. tabacum Rubisco, while the most active evolved variants produced at least 10-fold greater levels of assembled Rubisco.

We then purified N. tabacum Rubisco produced using NtRaf1 or our most active AtRaf1 variants 3n, 4 h, and 4p in large scale to assay their catalytic activities (SI Appendix, Figs. S19 and S20). N. tabacum Rubisco assembled by the evolved AtRaf1 variants displayed similar elution profiles in size-exclusion chromatography as N. tabacum Rubisco assembled using NtRaf1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S19). The purified Rubiscos migrated as a single complex by native PAGE that separated cleanly into RbcL and RbcS subunits when subjected to denaturing SDS-PAGE (SI Appendix, Fig. S20 A and B). When assessed for activity using an enzyme-coupled spectrophotometric assay for 3PG generation, the N. tabacum Rubiscos we isolated exhibited similar RuBP carboxylation kinetics regardless of whether they were assembled using NtRaf1 or one of our evolved AtRaf1 variants (SI Appendix, Fig. S20 C and D).

Finally, we assessed the ability of evolved AtRaf1s to assemble additional dicot Rubiscos (Fig. 6B). Except for 2g, all variants tested assembled A. thaliana Rubisco with comparable efficiency to that of wild-type AtRaf1 (Fig. 6C). We also tested whether 2b, 3n, and 4p could assemble dicot Rubiscos that they had not been evolved to recognize, including orthologs from Solanum tuberosum (potato), Medicago truncatula (barrelclover), Glycine max (soybean), and Gossypium hirsutum (cotton) (Fig. 6D). Here, a subset of the evolved variants displayed similar or increased activity on these Rubisco orthologs compared to wild-type AtRaf1. Compared to wild-type AtRaf1, 4p exhibited better activity in assembling G. max Rubisco, while all evolved variants produced increases in M. truncatula Rubisco, although for the latter we observed the presence of higher molecular weight bands that likely correspond to chaperone-bound assembly intermediates rather than fully assembled holoenzyme. The evolved AtRaf1s also assembled G. hirsutum Rubisco, which both At and NtRaf1 recognize, but were unable to assemble S. tuberosum Rubisco, which AtRaf1 also does not assemble. Thus, by evolving AtRaf1 to assemble N. tabacum Rubisco, we obtained chaperone variants with broadened client recognition.

Discussion

We demonstrated the use of directed evolution to reprogram a plant Rubisco chaperone, AtRaf1, to recognize and assemble a nonnative Rubisco client that the wild-type chaperone poorly recognizes. To assess libraries of AtRaf1 variants in high throughput, we developed a strategy that links the formation of chaperone-stabilized RbcL assembly intermediates to a fluorescent signal, enabling the evaluation of ~105 to 106 different Raf1 mutants by FACS or fluorescent colony picking (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). After four rounds of directed evolution, we obtained several variants that exhibited >10-fold improved ability to assemble N. tabacum Rubisco compared to wild-type AtRaf1 (Fig. 6A). Despite the large difference (~50% pairwise identity) between the wild-type AtRaf1 and NtRaf1 sequences, we found that ~5 to 7 mutations (<2% of the AtRaf1 sequence) were sufficient to confer AtRaf1 with substantial activity on N. tabacum Rubisco. The mutations in our evolved AtRaf1s span both the α and β domains, as well as the flexible and highly charged linker that connects these domains. Many of the mutations are predicted to increase hydrophobicity or result in charge neutralization (Fig. 5B), suggesting that they increase N. tabacum Rubisco assembly through strengthened hydrophobic interactions with the NtRbcL surface. Our reversion experiments show that no single mutation drives the activity of the evolved AtRaf1s (Fig. 5F). This indicates that no single interaction identified or improved by our directed evolution is very strong on its own and that the increase in NtRbcL recognition may be driven by an aggregate of modestly strengthened individual contacts. In general, the evolved AtRaf1s assembled A. thaliana Rubisco to a similar degree as the wild-type chaperone (Fig. 6C). A subset of evolved variants also assembled other dicot Rubisco orthologs to an equal or greater degree compared to wild-type AtRaf1 (Fig. 6D), although in some cases (such as for M. truncatula Rubisco) we predominantly observed higher molecular weight bands that likely correspond to assembly intermediates. This suggests that directed evolution can uncover AtRaf1 variants with broadened Rubisco recognition compared to the wild-type parent, which contrasts with the observation that plant Raf1s have naturally evolved to possess high selectivity for their cognate RbcLs (14). One caveat of this experiment is that a limited number of dicot Rubisco orthologs were tested.

Although our evolved variants assembled markedly more N. tabacum Rubisco than wild-type AtRaf1, they produced at most ~25% of the levels of N. tabacum Rubisco assembled by NtRaf1 (SI Appendix, Table S1). Further evolution is likely required to uncover AtRaf1 variants with NtRaf1 levels of activity. A challenge that arose during our study is the identification of false positives that robustly activated the cCadC-NtRbcL biosensor (Fig. 3 D and E). The interactions between Rubisco assembly factors and RbcL should be sufficiently transient to allow the final RbcS displacement to happen. Our false positives possessed mutations predicted to alter the conformation of the β domain of AtRaf1 and potentially favor the formation of AtRaf1:RbcL intermediates, resulting in an increase in cCadC-NtRbcL activity but failing to generate a concomitant increase in N. tabacum Rubisco assembly. In addition, after a certain threshold of AtRaf1 activity, the GFP signal produced by the cCadC-NtRbcL biosensor became decoupled from N. tabacum Rubisco assembly efficiency measured by native PAGE, which renders the biosensor output as a qualitative rather than quantitative assessment of assembly. We hypothesize this is a consequence of insufficient selection stringency and the reliance on detecting an assembly intermediate rather than fully assembled Rubisco. Improvements to the selection strategy, such as the addition of a more stringent secondary screen for assembly by measuring assembled Rubisco activity using a Rubisco-dependent E. coli strain (22–24), may address these limitations. Rubisco assembly factors are thought to act cooperatively, and coevolution of additional chaperones (for example, AtBSD2) alongside AtRaf1 might also enable additional boosts in Rubisco production. Finally, our experiments are limited to an E. coli model system for plant Rubisco production. While Rubisco assembly in E. coli roughly correlates with Rubisco assembly in transgenic tobacco (15), future experiments testing the activity of our evolved chaperone variants in plant models will be important validation for the strategy reported here.

For decades, it has been recognized that replacement of the native Rubiscos in crops with catalytically superior orthologs or mutants could lead to improvements in photosynthetic efficiency and yields. However, incompatibility with endogenous assembly factors often presents a major obstacle to producing foreign Rubiscos in plants. Coexpression of cognate assembly factors has been shown to promote heterologous Rubisco production in plants, but for some Rubiscos, such as those from rhodophytes that have attracted interest because of their superior CO2 vs. O2 specificities, the required assembly factors are yet to be identified (26). Our work may provide a path for overcoming these challenges through using directed evolution to reprogram plant assembly factors to act upon nonnative Rubiscos. The high-throughput nature of the cCadC-RbcL strategy could also accelerate assembly factor identification (for example, by enabling the screening cDNA libraries for gene products that act upon a given RbcL ortholog) or provide a complimentary tool to traditional, lower-throughput biochemical techniques for studying Rubisco assembly.

Materials and Methods

General Methods.

Antibiotics (Gold Biotechnology) were used at the following working concentrations: ampicillin, 50 µg/mL; spectinomycin, 100 µg/mL; chloramphenicol, 25 µg/mL; kanamycin, 50 µg/mL and tetracycline, 10 µg/mL. Inducers were diluted from stock with the following concentrations: anhydrotetracycline (aTc), 100 µg/mL; Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG, Gold Biotechnology), 1 M. Nuclease-free water (Omega Bio-Tek) was used for PCRs and cloning. For all other experiments, water was purified using a MilliQ purification system (Millipore). Taq DNA polymerase, Q5U polymerase or Q5 polymerase (New England Biolabs) were used for PCRs.

Bacterial Strains, Culture Conditions, and Media.

E. coli strain S2060 (27) was used for general plasmid cloning and library cloning. E. coli strain BL21 DE3 was used for all Rubisco expression experiments. S2060 ΔcadCBA was used for fluorescence assays and AtRaf1 variant selection to reduce background from endogenous PcadBA transcription (28). Generally, bacteria were cultured in 2xYT medium (US Biological) supplemented with 20 mM glucose (Sigma Aldrich). Davis Rich Media (DRM) (US Biological) was used for fluorescence assays.

Rubisco and Assembly Factor Expression Plasmid Cloning.

Plasmids were cloned by USER assembly or Golden Gate assembly. DNA sequences for A. thaliana Rubisco and assembly factors were obtained using expression plasmids from ref. 3, a generous gift from M. Hayer-Hartl. DNA sequences for N. tabacum assembly factors were obtained using expression plasmids from ref. 29. DNA sequences for other Rubiscos were synthesized by Twist Biosciences. A list of all plasmids used in this study is provided in SI Appendix, Table S2. Sequences of selected primers used in this work are provided in SI Appendix, Table S3. Plasmid maps (GenBank format) can be found in Dataset S1. Details on the construction of AtRaf1 libraries can be found in SI Appendix, AtRaf1 Library Generation.

GFP Fluorescence Assays.

Cells harboring the plasmids of interest were inoculated from single colonies into 2xYT media containing maintenance antibiotics and 20 mM glucose and then grown at 37 °C for 16 h with shaking to reach saturation. Saturated overnight cultures were diluted 1:100 into 1 mL of DRM with antibiotics into 96 deep-well plates. The cultures were then incubated at 37 °C with shaking (225 rpm) until they reached OD600 of 0.4. Final concentrations of 0.02 mM IPTG and 200 ng/mL aTc were added to induce the production of assembly factors and cCadC-RbcL fusions, respectively. Cells were subsequently incubated at 23 °C overnight with shaking, then 200 µL of culture was transferred into 96-well black clear-bottom plates for GFP fluorescence and OD600 measurements using a Tecan Infinite Mplex plate reader.

Detection of Rubisco Assembly by Protein Gel Electrophoresis and Western Blotting.

Chemically competent or electrocompetent BL21 DE3 E. coli cells (New England Biolabs) were transformed with assembly factor and Rubisco plasmids. Transformed cells were selected on LB agar plates with maintenance antibiotics and 20 mM glucose. Then, single colonies were inoculated into 2xYT media containing maintenance antibiotics and 20 mM glucose and incubated at 37 °C with shaking overnight. The next day, saturated cell cultures were diluted 1:100 in 2xYT media with 20 mM glucose and grown at 37 °C until the OD600 reached ~0.6 to 0.7. Cells were induced with 1 mM IPTG and grown at 23 °C overnight with shaking (255 RPM). 3 mL of culture was pelleted at 8,000×g and resuspended in 120 µL lysis buffer [B-PER (ThermoFisher 78243) supplemented with 10 mM MgCl2 (Sigma Aldrich), 1 µg/µL lysozyme (DOT Scientific), 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, RPI research products), and 2 mM Benzamidine HCl (DOT Scientific)]. The samples were incubated on ice for 25 min, and then centrifuged at 20,000×g at 4 °C for 25 min.

The protein concentration of soluble fraction of each sample was measured using a standard Bradford assay (ThermoFisher) and 10 µg-12 µg of sample was loaded on a hand cast 6%, 6.5 %, or 7% (40% acrylamide and bis-acrylamide solution), 19:1 (Bio-Rad); Ammonium persulfate (Sigma Aldrich) and TEMED (RPI research products) Tris-Glycine polyacrylamide gel.

Proteins were separated at 130 V for 25 min and then at 170 V for 3 to 4 h at 4 °C in native PAGE running buffer [25 mM Tris (Sigma Aldrich) and 190 mM glycine (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), pH 8.3]. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad 1620177) at 100 V or 350 mA for 1 h at 4 °C. The membrane was blocked using 3% BSA (Sigma Aldrich) in Tris-buffered saline (TBS, 20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.6) at room temperature for 1 h and incubated with primary antibodies (rabbit anti-RbcL; Agrisera AS03 037 and mouse anti-6xHis; ThermoFisher MA1-21315) in TBST (TBS + 0.1% TWEEN-20) at 4 °C overnight. After washing the membrane three times in TBST, the blot was incubated with secondary antibodies (horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit; Azure AC2114 and Dye-650-conjugated goat anti-mouse; Azure AC2166) in TBST buffer (1:10,000 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. Then, the membrane was washed with TBST four times. The membrane was first imaged for fluorescence signal and then treated with Radiance Q chemiluminescence substrate (Azure Biosystems) and imaged for chemiluminescence using an Azure c400 imager. A list of the antibodies used in this work can be found in SI Appendix, Table. S4. Densitometry analysis was performed using ImageJ.

Selection of AtRaf1 Variants.

General procedure.

AtRaf1 libraries were transformed into chemically competent selection cells [S2060 ΔcadCBA harboring plasmids pBL155C (cCadC-NtRbcL) and pSL012 (PcadBA mGreenLantern)]. Cells were recovered in 2xYT supplemented with 20 mM glucose at 37 °C for 1 h, then plated on LB agar containing maintenance antibiotics, 20 mM glucose, and, where indicated, 200 ng/mL aTc and IPTG (either 0.05 mM, 0.03 mM, 0.01 mM, or 0.005 mM) to induce the expression of cCadC-NtRbcL and assembly factors, respectively. Plates were incubated at room temperature for ~2 d. GFP-positive variants were either picked directly from the plate or isolated by FACS. Details on how each selection round was performed can be found in SI Appendix, Selection of AtRaf1 Variants.

MD Simulations of the Raf1 Beta Domain.

Initial structures for MD simulations of wt and 2j Raf1β domains were obtained from the crystal structure (PDB ID: 4WT4). Residues E314 and E336 were mutated to lysine to generate the 2j variant. Each protein structure was centered in a simulation box with a minimum distance of 1.5 nm between the solute and box edges and subsequently solvated with TIP3P water molecules (30). Sodium and chloride ions were added to achieve a physiological salt concentration of 0.15 M and to neutralize the overall charge of the system.

For each system, the following equilibration steps were performed. Energy minimization was initially performed using the steepest descent algorithm. Systems were then equilibrated sequentially under NVT conditions at 300 K for 1 ns, followed by NPT conditions at 1 bar for another 1 ns. During the equilibration stages, heavy atoms of the protein were restrained using a harmonic potential with a force constant of 1,000 kJ mol−1 nm−2. Temperature coupling was achieved with a V-rescale thermostat (31) (coupling constant: 0.1 ps), and pressure coupling in NPT was managed using a Parrinello–Rahman barostat (32) (coupling constant: 2 ps).

Production MD simulations were then conducted for 2 μs without any positional restraints. Electrostatic interactions were computed using the Particle Mesh Ewald (33, 34) (PME) algorithm with a short-range cutoff of 1.2 nm. Lennard–Jones interactions were truncated at 1.2 nm. Hydrogen atom bonds were constrained using the LINCS (35) algorithm, and the leap-frog integration algorithm was employed with a timestep of 2 fs. All simulations were conducted using GROMACS 2022.5 software (36) and the CHARMM36m force field (37).

Recombinant N. tabacum Rubisco Expression and Purification.

BL21 (DE3) E. coli electrocompetent cells were transformed with N. tabacum Rubisco plasmid pTW335i3 and the assembly factor plasmids of interest. Transformed cells were selected on LB agar plates supplemented with maintenance antibiotics and 20 mM glucose. Single colonies were used to inoculate overnight cultures of 2xYT supplemented with maintenance antibiotics and 20 mM glucose. Cultures grown to saturation at 37 °C overnight with shaking (250 rpm) were diluted 1:100 to inoculate fresh cultures for Rubisco expression, which were then incubated at 37 °C with shaking (250 rpm) until they reached an OD600 of ~0.6 to 0.7. Cultures were then induced using 1 mM IPTG and incubated at 23 °C overnight with shaking. Cells were lysed by sonication and N. tabacum Rubisco was isolated using Ni-NTA resin. The N. tabacum Rubisco used in activity assays were further purified by size-exclusion chromatography. Detailed purification procedures can be found in SI Appendix, N. tabacum Rubisco Purification.

Rubisco Activity Assay.

Rubisco carboxylation activity was measured using a coupled enzyme assay according to previously reported procedures (38) modified to a microtiter plate format. The coupling enzyme mix was prepared by combining the following enzymes to achieve the specified activity levels in the assay: 2.5 U of creatine phosphokinase (C3755-3.5KU), 25 U of carbonic anhydrase (C3934-100MG), 2.5 U of glyceraldehyde-3-P dehydrogenase (G2267-5KU), 2.5 U of 3-phosphoglycerate kinase (P7634-5KU), and 20 U of TPI/2 U of G3PDH (G1881-1KU), all purchased from Millipore Sigma. The enzyme mix (~2.5 mL) was dialyzed twice for 2 h and once more overnight at 4 °C in 2,000 mL of 20 mM HEPPS-NaOH (DOT Scientific, pH 8.2), 5 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT to remove ammonium sulfate. After dialysis, 20% glycerol was added to the enzyme mixture, which was then aliquoted, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at –80 °C until use.

Rubiscos were activated in activation buffer consisting of 30 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0, 20 mM MgCl2, and 40 mM of NaHCO3 (Sigma Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature. Substrates [NADH (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), NaHCO3, ATP (DOT Scientific), and phosphocreatine (Santa Cruz Biotechnology)] were dissolved in 100 mM HEPPS-NaOH (pH 8.2) buffer containing 20 mM MgCl2 and 1 mM Na2EDTA. For each 100 µL reaction, 10 µL of coupling enzyme was combined with substrate (final concentration of 268 µM NADH, 1 mM ATP, 5 mM phosphocreatine, 10 mM NaHCO3) and activated Rubisco (final concentration of 0.3 µM) to yield a 90 µL reaction mixture. Enzyme mixtures were transferred to a 96-well black clear-bottom plate (Greiner) and incubated at 25 °C in the TECAN Infinite Mplex plate reader for 15 min to equilibrate. Finally, 10 µL of 5 mM RuBP (Millipore Sigma R0878) was added to the wells to initiate the assay. Absorbance at 340 nm was measured at 25 °C. The concentration of 3-phosphoglycerate (3PG) produced was estimated from NADH depletion assuming a molar extinction coefficient of 6,220 M−1 cm−1.
