Roseburia intestinalis-derived extracellular vesicles alleviate osteoporosis through gut microbiota-regulated histidylleucine via Akt/FOXO1 signaling
Authors: Jiaxu Lu, Yuanyuan Liu, Jiaxian Chen, Sheng Yu, Shukai Yang, Chengshuo Huang, Jv Chen, Jianhao Li, Hao Lin, Jiaqi Chu, Peng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoporosis (OP), characterized by reduced bone mass and microarchitectural deterioration, significantly impairs patient health and quality of life. Conventional therapies are often limited by long-term safety concerns and adverse effects. Recent studies suggest that extracellular vesicles (EVs) play a critical role in bone remodeling by promoting osteoblast differentiation and mineralization while inhibiting osteoclast activity, offering a potential therapeutic avenue for OP. In this study, we observed a significant reduction of Roseburia intestinalis (R.intestinalis), an anaerobic, gram-positive bacterium, in both OP patients and ovariectomized (OVX) model rats by 16S rRNA sequencing. We successfully isolated and characterized R.intestinalis-derived EVs (REVs), which were found to promote the differentiation and mineralization of human bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells (hBMSCs) in vitro. Mechanistically, REVs were shown to enhance the production of histidylleucine, and the administration of histidylleucine alone was sufficient to promote hBMSCs differentiation and mineralization through the Akt/FOXO1 signaling pathway. Furthermore, both REVs and histidylleucine were effective in reversing gut microbiota dysbiosis and improving bone mineral density in vivo. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential REVs and their metabolite histidylleucine in OP treatment through modulation of the “gut-bone” axis, which providing insights into histidylleucine as a promising therapeutic agent for OP.Subject terms: Calcium and phosphate metabolic disorders, Pharmaceutics, Regeneration

Introduction

Osteoporosis (OP) is a systemic skeletal disease characterized by reduced bone mineral density and architectural deterioration of trabecular bone structures, leading to increased bone fragility and susceptibility to fracture1. With the aging of the population, the incidence of OP and osteoporotic fractures is increasing, resulting in a serious economic burden and reducing the quality of life2. The gut microbiota has gained increasing attention in recent years for its potential role in influencing bone metabolism pathways and osteoporotic pathophysiology3.

Emerging evidence highlights the relationship between the gut microbiota and bone metabolism, with microbial compositional alterations frequently undergoing skeletal changes4. The gut microbiota and its metabolites may influence bone metabolism through multiple mechanisms5. For instance, short-chain fatty acids can regulate the differentiation and function of osteoblasts and osteoclasts by modulating the immune system and inflammatory responses6. Bile acids can affect bone metabolism by binding to specific receptors and influencing the secretion of hormones and growth factors7. Additionally, the gut microbiota can impact nutrient absorption and utilization, thereby indirectly affecting bone health. For example, certain gut microbiota can enhance the absorption of calcium and vitamin D, which are essential for bone formation and maintenance8.

Roseburia intestinalis (R. intestinalis), a gram-positive, microaerophilic rod-shaped bacterium and a major butyrate-producer, is an important member of the gut microbiota9. Butyrate, a SCFA, is crucial for gut health and exerts anti-inflammatory effects10. Numerous studies have shown that R. intestinalis is associated with various diseases. In inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) patients, its abundance is significantly reduced, and it can alleviate IBD symptoms by regulating gut microbiota balance and reducing intestinal inflammation11. It also plays a protective role in diseases such as atherosclerosis, alcoholic fatty liver disease, and liver cirrhosis. For example, in atherosclerosis mouse models, R. intestinalis can lower serum cholesterol levels and reduce atherosclerotic lesions by interacting with dietary plant polysaccharides12. In alcoholic fatty liver disease, it improves gut barrier integrity, reduces gut bacterial translocation and inflammation, and mitigates liver damage13.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs), membrane-bound particles derived from cells, mediate cellular communication via protein, lipid, and nucleic acid exchange, thereby positioning them as critical regulators in OP pathogenesis14. Cell-derived EVs have been identified as key mediators in bone homeostasis15. Previous studies have demonstrated that EVs produced from Proteus mirabilis can suppress osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption by influencing apoptotic signaling pathways and mitochondrial function16. Moreover, EVs derived from Akkermansia muciniphila have been shown to prevent the loss of bone mass and strength, as well as the deterioration of bone microarchitecture17. These findings indicate that bacterial EVs play a significant role in the development of OP and offer therapeutic avenues for its treatment18.

The 16S rRNA gene acts as a unique genetic “barcode” for bacteria, while all bacteria have it, its exact sequence differs between species, allowing for precise identification. In this study, we found that R. intestinalis was enriched in the normal group compared to OP patients and OVX model rats via 16S rRNA sequencing. In vivo experiments showed that R. intestinalis reduced bone loss in OVX rats. We also successfully isolated REVs, which promoted the differentiation and mineralization of hBMSCs through the Akt/FOXO1 signaling pathway. Moreover, histidylleucine, a metabolite of R. intestinalis, enhanced hBMSCs osteogenesis differentiation and significantly elevated bone mass and ameliorated OVX mediated bone microarchitectural disorders via the Akt/FOXO1 signaling pathway. Overall, these findings may offer a promising therapeutic strategy for OP.

Results

Roseburia intestinalis is decreased in OP compared with normal control

Firstly, the fecal microbiota of OP patients and healthy controls was analyzed via 16S rRNA sequencing. As shown in Fig. 1A, the relative abundance of gut microbiota in the OP group was increased, such as Peptostreptococcaceae, Acidminococcus, and Christensenella. While Roseburia intestinalis (R. intestinalis), Erysipelotrichaceae, and Faecalibacterium were significantly enriched in normal individuals compared to OP patients. Furthermore, in an OP rat model by OVX, R. intestinalis expression in the feces of healthy rats was higher than that in OP rats (Fig. 1B), consistent with results in humans.

To investigate the impact of R. intestinalis on bone loss, the bone microstructure of the distal femur was assessed using micro-CT. Compared to the sham group, the OVX group displayed decreased bone thickness, reduced bone density, a sparser bone arrangement, and an enlarged bone marrow cavity. R. intestinalis treatment significantly mitigated these bone loss effects and improved bone microstructure (Fig. 1C). BMD, BV/TV, and Tb.N were analyzed as indicators of bone status. R. intestinalis treatment led to significant increases in BMD, BV/TV, and Tb.N compared to the OVX group (Fig. 1D). Femur sections were stained with H&E and Masson to evaluate the defect repair area and bone trichrome formation rate. Results showed a significantly higher bone formation rate in R. intestinalis-treated rats than in OVX groups. Additionally, R. intestinalis treatment led to significantly elevated RUNX2 expression compared to OVX groups (Fig. 1E). These findings demonstrate that the probiotic R. intestinalis can enhance bone strength and ameliorate microstructural loss in OVX rats.

The isolation and characterization of Roseburia intestinalis-derived extracellular vesicles

To isolate REVs, sequential ultracentrifugation was performed, and their characteristics were analyzed via TEM, which revealed a round-shaped vesicular morphology with a lipid bilayer (Fig. 2A). NTA analysis determined the REVs concentration at approximately 2.0 × 109 particles/mL and an average diameter of 117 nm (Fig. 2B). After labeling REVs with PKH26 and co-culturing them with primary hBMSCs, results confirmed successful internalization of REVs by hBMSCs (Fig. 2C), supporting the effective isolation of REVs. To investigate whether REVs could be transported to bone via blood circulation, DiR-labeled REVs were administered intraperitoneally. Fluorescent signals were detected in the bone and other tissues (heart, liver, spleen, and kidney) at 12, 24, 48, and 72 h post-administration. Mild fluorescent signals were observed in the femur at 12 h, with high-intensity signals appearing at 24 h, indicating that REVs gradually entered the femur in significant numbers. By 48 h, REVs were metabolized and cleared from the rats (Fig. 2D). Also, we administered PKH26-labeled REVs into rats via intraperitoneal injection. After 24 hours, the rats were euthanized, and rat bone marrow stem cells (rBMSCs) were isolated from the femur. Confocal microscopy confirmed the presence of PKH26-labeled REVs within the rBMSCs, demonstrating that the REVs were successfully internalized by the rBMSCs in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Additionally, intraperitoneal administration of REVs did not induce liver or kidney toxicities, as confirmed by H&E staining (Fig. 2E). These findings demonstrate that REVs can enter bone tissue and be taken up by BMSCs without causing toxicity.

REVs improved osteoblast differentiation and mineralization

Cell viability of BMSCs following REVs administration was assessed via CCK-8 assay, revealing no significant effect on hBMSC proliferation (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, the osteogenic differentiation capacity of hBMSCs in vitro under various REVs concentrations (0, 1, and 10 μg/mL) was investigated. Compared to the blank group, the intensity of ALP and Alizarin red S staining in hBMSCs increased in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3B). Western blot analysis of the osteogenic markers ALP, OPN, and Runx2 further confirmed that REVs promote osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs as the dose of REVs increases (Fig. 3C). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining for RUNX2 revealed higher expression following REVs administration (Fig. 3D). Moreover, Oil red O staining results indicated that REVs markedly suppress the adipogenic differentiation potential of hBMSCs (Fig. 3E). Given that PPARγ and C/EBPα are key transcription factors in adipogenesis and can negatively regulate osteoblast differentiation, we observed a significant decrease in their expression following REVs treatment (Fig. 3F). These findings collectively suggest that REVs promote osteoblast differentiation and mineralization.

To further investigate the activation mechanism of REVs in hBMSCs, we performed RNA-seq on total RNA samples purified from both control and REVs-treated hBMSCs. The GSEA pathway analysis revealed that the FOXO1 signaling pathway was significantly enriched in the REVs-treated group (Fig. 3G). FOXO1 plays a pivotal role in osteogenesis by promoting mesenchymal cell differentiation into osteoblasts through its interaction with Runx2, a transcription factor critical for osteoblast differentiation. The heatmap analysis also demonstrated that FOXO1 expression was upregulated in response to REVs treatment (Fig. 3H). Akt signaling is known to regulate the transcriptional activity of Foxo1. Akt can phosphorylate FOXO1, causing its nuclear exclusion and degradation, thereby inhibiting its transcriptional activity. Our subsequent western blotting analysis showed that REVs treatment inhibited the phosphorylation of Akt, thereby enhancing the expression of its downstream target FOXO1 (Fig. 3I). Collectively, these data suggest that REVs may promote osteoblast differentiation and mineralization by modulating the Akt/FOXO1 signaling pathway.

Histidylleucine promoted the osteogenesis of hBMSCs via Akt/FOXO1 signaling

Metabolomics analysis was conducted to profile the metabolic components of REVs and R. intestinalis. The results revealed that histidylleucine was one of the predominant metabolites (Fig. 4A), a finding further confirmed by LC-MS analysis (Fig. 4B). Treatment with histidylleucine resulted in elevated ALP activity and enhanced calcium salt deposition (Fig. 4C), which augmented early osteogenic marker expression and mineralization. Western blotting analysis corroborated these findings by demonstrating increased protein levels of ALP, OPN, and RUNX2 following histidylleucine treatment (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, Oil red O staining indicated that histidylleucine diminished adipogenic differentiation (Fig. 4E), which was further substantiated by reduced expression of the adipogenic transcription factors PPARγ and C/EBPα (Fig. 4F). Moreover, histidylleucine administration was associated with suppressed phosphorylation of Akt and concomitant upregulation of FOXO1 (Fig. 4G). Collectively, these data suggest that REVs augment osteoblast differentiation and mineralization through their metabolic component histidylleucine, acting the Akt/FOXO1 signaling pathway.

Both REVs and histidylleucine attenuate OVX-induced bone loss in rats

As evidenced by the micro-CT imaging of trabecular bone, OVX rats exhibited a significantly lower bone mass compared to sham-operated controls. However, treatment with both REVs and histidylleucine effectively prevented OVX-induced bone loss (Fig. 5A). Both REVs and histidylleucine not only ameliorated the OVX-induced reduction in BMD, BV/TV, and Tb.N (Fig. 5B), but also enhanced bone volume in OVX rats, as evidenced by H&E and Masson staining (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, the administration of REVs and histidylleucine increased RUNX2 expression (Fig. 5C) and upregulated FOXO1 expression in OVX rats (Fig. 5D). Collectively, these findings underscore the beneficial role of REVs and histidylleucine in mitigating bone loss in this OVX-induced osteoporosis model.

Roseburia intestinalis was decreased during the gut microbiota dysbiosis

To further confirm R. intestinalis as a key component of gut microbiota, WT rats were treated with antibiotics, after which fecal microbiota analysis was conducted via 16S rRNA sequencing. Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) revealed distinct clustering patterns between the antibiotic-treated and control groups (Fig. 6A). Further analyses, including a differential abundance analysis (Fig. 6B) and heatmap (Fig. 6C) identified a significant reduction in R. intestinalis within the antibiotic-treated group. These findings indicated that antibiotic treatment induced gut microbiota dysbiosis and suggest a potential link between R. intestinalis and bone loss.

REVs and Histidylleucine mitigated bone loss triggered by antibiotics

To further explore the roles of REVs and its metabolic component histidylleucine in WT rats with antibiotic – induced gut dysbiosis, micro-CT was used to analyze the BMD of the distal femur in rats, with the results presented in Fig. 7A. In contrast, dysbiosis rats treated with REVs and histidylleucine exhibited a restoration of bone mass (Fig. 7B). Nevertheless, treatment with REVs or histidylleucine following antibiotic administration markedly enhanced BMD, BV/TV, and Tb.N (Fig. 7B). H&E staining and Masson’s staining of rat bone tissue structure revealed a significant reduction in new bone trabeculae and mature bone tissue in the dysbiosis group than in the control group. In contrast, dysbiosis rats treated with REVs and histidylleucine exhibited significantly better levels of both trabeculae and mature bone tissue in the femur (Fig. 7C). IHC staining revealed a similar trend in the expression of osteogenesis-related protein RUNX2 (Fig. 7C). In addition, FOXO1 expression was upregulated after REVs and Histidylleucine administration in the antibiotic - induced dysbiosis model. These findings suggest that REVs and its metabolic component histidylleucine may enhance bone tissue production affected by antibiotics.

Supplementary Fig. 1A and B show the TEM images and NTA results of the reconstituted nanoparticles. Compared to the REV-like nanoparticles Histidylleucine knockout (RLNP-KO), the REV-like nanoparticles Histidylleucine knockin (RLNP-KI) significantly promoted calcium salt deposition and alkaline phosphatase activity in hBMSCs(Supplementary Fig. 1C), demonstrating that RLNP-KI enhance the osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs.

Discussion

Osteoporosis is a systemic metabolic bone disease that poses significant challenges to patient health and quality of life. Conventional therapies are often limited by long-term safety concerns and adverse effects, highlighting the need for new therapeutic approaches19. In this study, we observed a significant reduction of R. intestinalis in both OP patients and OVX model rats through 16S rRNA sequencing, which indicating that R. intestinalis may contribute to the development of OP. REVs promoted the differentiation and mineralization of hBMSCs in vitro, suggesting a promising therapeutic avenue for OP. Mechanistically, REVs were shown to contain histidylleucine, and the administration of histidylleucine alone was sufficient to promote hBMSC differentiation and mineralization through the Akt/FOXO1 signaling pathway. Our study highlights the therapeutic potential of REVs and their metabolite histidylleucine in OP treatment, providing a better understanding of the role of the gut microbiota in bone metabolism.

Numerous studies have provided substantial evidence supporting the significant role of the gut-bone axis in the treatment of bone loss. For instance, gut microbiota can ameliorate postmenopausal bone loss by modulating tryptophan metabolism to repair the AhR-mediated gut-bone signaling pathway20. Gut microbiota-derived indoleacetic acid has been shown to alleviate postmenopausal bone loss by inhibiting RANKL-induced activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway and ROS generation, thereby suppressing osteoclastogenesis21. Gut microbiota often exert their therapeutic effects by participating in the regulation of intestinal metabolite levels, such as short-chain fatty acids22, polyamines23, bile acids, and bacterial-derived enzymes22.

Bacterial extracellular vesicles (BEVs) are nanosized, lipid bilayer-enclosed spheres secreted by bacteria24. With increasing research focus on gut microbiota, a growing body of evidence has highlighted the important role of BEVs in mediating bacterial regulation of host homeostasis, including in conditions such as intestinal inflammation, neurological diseases, metabolic disorders, and autoimmune diseases25–27.

Bacterial extracellular vesicles are complex nanostructures composed of diverse components, including nucleic acids, lipids, proteins, and metabolites28. Current research primarily focuses on the holistic functions of BEVs. However, there is a lack of sufficient investigation into the specific vesicle components responsible for their regulatory effects. This study reveals the significant role of BEVs derived from the probiotic R. intestinalis in mitigating postmenopausal bone loss. Accumulating evidence suggests that bacterial-derived peptides, particularly oligopeptides which are more readily absorbed than polypeptides29, play a crucial part in mediating probiotic functions11. Accordingly, utilizing omics approaches, this study identified a oligopeptide, histidylleucine. In future research, we need to further investigate the transport mechanism of histidylleucine, which is of great significance for elucidating their therapeutic mechanism in postmenopausal osteoporosis. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that this dipeptide alleviates postmenopausal bone loss by promoting the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow stem cells via activation of the AKT/FOXO1 signaling pathway.

The Akt/FOXO1 signaling pathway plays a crucial role in the development of OP30. FOXO1, a transcription factor in the FOXO family, is abundantly expressed in osteoblasts and is crucial for osteoblast differentiation, bone formation, and skeletal homeostasis31. Akt phosphorylation can inactivate FOXO1, reducing its transcriptional activity and thereby affecting osteoblast differentiation and bone formation32. Our study demonstrated that REVs and histidylleucine significantly suppressed AKT expression and activation in hBMSCs, while enhancing FOXO1 expression, which further promotes osteogenic differentiation. Targeting the Akt/FOXO1 signaling pathway may offer strategies for OP prevention and treatment.

Given the biocompatibility, targeted bone accumulation, and lack of toxicity in vivo, REVs represent a safe and effective approach for enhancing bone regeneration. Furthermore, the discovery that a microbiota-derived metabolite such as histidylleucine can significantly enhance osteogenic differentiation provides a new therapeutic strategy beyond conventional interventions. This may be especially beneficial for postmenopausal women and elderly patients, who often cannot tolerate systemic hormone replacement or bisphosphonates due to their side effects. In addition, the demonstrated efficacy of REVs and histidylleucine in antibiotic-induced dysbiosis models suggests potential for treating secondary osteoporosis associated with gut microbiota disruption, such as that caused by long-term antibiotic use, inflammatory bowel disease, or aging. Thus, these findings support future translational development of microbiota-derived EVs or histidylleucine as therapeutic strategies for different forms of osteoporosis, including postmenopausal and secondary osteoporosis.

Our study showed that extracellular vesicles from gut microbiota R. intestinalis boosts hBMSCs’ osteogenic differentiation and mineralization, and enhances bone mass in OVX-induced rat models. REVs, via metabolite histidylleucine, activates the Akt/FOXO1 signaling pathway to promote osteoblast osteogenic differentiation and bone formation. These findings offer new ideas for preventing and treating OP.

Methods

Ethical statement

Postmenopausal women with osteoporosis were recruited from the Orthopedic Center of the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University. Inclusion criteria for the patient group were: (1) age 50–55 years; (2) natural menopause for at least 2 years; (3) bone mineral density T‑score ≤ –2.5 at the lumbar spine or femoral neck. The control group consisted of premenopausal non‑osteoporotic women, with inclusion criteria of: (1) age 35–40 years; (2) regular menstrual cycles and no clinical signs of menopause; (3) bone mineral density T‑score > –1.0. Common exclusion criteria for both groups included: history of metabolic bone diseases other than osteoporosis, use of medications affecting bone metabolism within the past 6 months (e.g., bisphosphonates, glucocorticoids, hormone replacement therapy), pregnancy or lactation, and any chronic disease that could interfere with bone metabolism. All participants provided written informed consent after receiving a full explanation of the study purpose, procedures, potential risks and benefits. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University (approval no. PJKT2024-157). All animal experiments and patient sample collection were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University (PJKT2024-157 and AHGDMU-LAC-B-202310-0075, respectively). All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed, and we have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Animal experiments

Eight-week-old Sprague Dawley (SD) rats were used in this study, which were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Liaoning, China). For 16S rRNA sequencing, the rats were randomly divided into two groups: a control group (non-ovariectomized) and an OVX group. The OVX surgery was performed in accordance with our previously established protocol33. After anesthesia was induced in Sprague‑Dawley rats by intraperitoneal injection of 1.25% tribromoethanol (250 mg/kg; Nanjing Aibei Biotech, China), bilateral ovariectomy was performed to establish an estrogen‑depleted model by making a ~1 cm incision in each flank and carefully detaching the ovaries from the tips of the double‑horned uterus for removal. Fecal samples were collected and stored at −80°C before metabolome analysis. To evaluate the effects of gut microbiota R. intestinalis on the osteoporotic process, rats were further divided into three groups: a control group, an OVX group and an OVX plus R.intestinalis group (n = 5). R. intestinalis was administered via oral gavage once daily at a concentration of 10⁹ CFU/mL (1 mL per rat) for six weeks. The control group received an equal volume of the sterile bacterial culture medium without bacteria on the same schedule. After the experiment, the SD rats were euthanized according to the approved euthanasia procedure. The rat carcasses were fixed in a supine position on a dissecting board, and the hind limbs were disinfected by spraying or wiping with 75% ethanol. An incision was made along the midline of the abdomen to expose the hind limbs. The femoral head was separated at the hip joint, and the ligament and tendon connections at the hip and knee joints were cut. The entire hind limb was then removed. Using a scalpel and scissors, the muscles and connective tissues around the femur were carefully removed, taking care not to damage the periosteum, while retaining some soft tissue to prevent drying. After dissection, any residual muscle, fascia, and blood stains were gently wiped off with sterile gauze soaked in PBS. The femur was then rinsed with PBS two to three times. Fixation was performed within 24 hours after sampling.

To further investigate the effects of REVs and the metabolite histidylleucine in dysbiosis rats, 20 female SD rats were randomly divided into four groups: a control group, a dysbiosis group, a dysbiosis + REVs (10 mg/kg) group, and a dysbiosis + histidylleucine (1 mg/kg) group. Dysbiosis was induced by treating the rats with broad-spectrum antibiotics (Ampicillin and neomycin were administered at doses of 160 and 80 mg/kg/day, respectively, in the sterilized drinking water.) for two weeks to deplete Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. REVs and histidylleucine were suspended in PBS and administered intraperitoneally every three days for five doses. Rats were placed in an induction chamber with 5% isoflurane in 100% oxygen (1 L/min) until breathing stopped, and isoflurane exposure was continued for an additional 2 minutes. Death was confirmed by loss of heartbeat, respiration, and pedal reflex. After the experiment, the SD rats were euthanized according to the approved euthanasia procedure. Micro CT analysis

Rat femurs isolated from the female OVX rat with or without REVs treatment were scanned using a micro-CT system with the following settings: pixel size 8.82 μm, working voltage 80 kV, working current 500 μA, and exposure time 1500 ms. The data were analyzed using Inveon Research Workplace software (Siemens) to assess trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), and trabecular number (Tb.N).

Histological staining

Rat femurs from female OVX rat with REVs or histidylleucine treatment were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Sections (4 μm) were dehydrated using dimethylbenzene and graded ethanol, followed by antigen retrieval with EDTA. After washing three times with PBS, the sections were blocked with 3% BSA and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. They were then treated with streptavidin-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 30 minutes, followed by visualization with DAB and counterstaining with hematoxylin. Additionally, femurs were stained with H&E (G1120, Solarbio, China) and Masson’s trichrome (G1346, Solarbio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Fecal 16S rRNA gene sequencing

Place the SD rat into a sterile, high-pressure sterilized empty cage (no bedding) and allow the rat to defecate naturally. After defecation, use sterile forceps to collect fresh feces and immediately place them into a sterile EP tube. For each rat sample, collect at least one fecal pellet (2–3 pellets recommended), avoiding contact with the surface of the feces to prevent contamination.

Before human sample collection, explain the study purpose, procedures, potential risks, benefits, and withdrawal mechanism to the subject, ensure full understanding, and obtain written informed consent. Use a sterile disposable fecal collector. The subject should defecate directly into the sterile container, avoiding contact with toilet water and urine. During sampling, take an internal portion from the middle section of the feces, use a sterile spoon to collect approximately 1 g, Fecal pellets from humans or rats were delicately collected into individual sterile tubes and immediately stored at a frigid temperature of −80°C until analysis. Total genomic DNA was extracted from microbiome samples using the DNeasy PowerSoil Kit (Mo Bio/QIAGEN). DNA concentration was measured with a Quantifluor-ST fluorometer (Promega) using the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen), and DNA quality was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The 16S rRNA gene variable region was then amplified by PCR (Pyrobest DNA Polymerase, TaKaRa), followed by gel extraction and purification using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen). Each sample was quantified with a BioTek Flx800 microplate reader (Quant-iT PicoGreen assay), and sequencing libraries were constructed following the standard Illumina TruSeq DNA Sample Preparation protocol.

Isolation and purification of REVs

Roseburia intestinalis (TS253874) was obtained from ning bo testobio Co., Ltd. and revived in anaerobic broth at 37 °C for two passages (each 48 h), then concentrated by centrifugation and resuspended in pre‑reduced sterile water to a desired density measured by the McFarland standard. Culture supernatants were harvested at an optical density (OD600 nm) of 2.0. The culture was continued for 72 h, then centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min to remove bacterial deposits and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter. The supernatant was then subjected twice to centrifugation at 10,000 g for 30 min, followed by filtration through a 0.45 filter and 0.22 μm filter, respectively. Finally, ultracentrifugation at 150,000 g for 90 min with 0.22 μm filter was performed, and the resulting REVs were purified with the EXODUS system (Shenzhen Huixin Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd., China) and suspended in PBS and stored at −80°C until further use.

Characterization of REVs

REVs were fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 minutes and subsequently dropped onto EM grids that had been pretreated with ultraviolet irradiation. After an additional 30 minutes incubation on the grids, the samples were stained using 1% uranyl acetate, followed by rinsing with deionized water. The morphology of the REVs was then visualized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL-2100F, Tokyo, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. The concentration (particles/mL) and size distribution of REVs were determined by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) using a NanoSight NS300 system (Malvern Panalytical, Amesbury, UK). REVs were labeled with PKH26 following the manufacturer’s protocol: (1) Staining: Add PKH26 dye solution to REV suspension. Mix thoroughly and incubate at 37 °C for 30 minutes in the dark. (2) Reaction Termination: Add exosome-depleted fetal bovine serum (FBS) to bind and quench any excess dye. (3) Washing: Dilute the mixture with a larger volume of PBS. Perform ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 70 minutes at 4 °C to pellet the labeled REVs. (4) Wash Cycles: Carefully discard the supernatant containing unincorporated dye and serum. Resuspend the pellet in fresh PBS. Repeat the ultracentrifugation wash step three times total to ensure complete removal of residual, unbound PKH26. 5. Final Product: Resuspend the final pellet in PBS to obtain the labeled REV preparation for downstream experiments), and the red fluorescent signals were visualized using confocal microscopy (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Biodistribution and toxicity of intraperitoneally administrated REVs

To assess in vivo toxicity, female SD rats were randomly divided into two groups: a control group and a REVs group. REVs suspended in PBS were administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 10 mg/kg every 3 days. Histopathological evaluation of the liver and kidneys was performed using H&E staining to examine tissue morphology.

To evaluate the biodistribution of REVs, female SD rats were orally administered with DiR fluorescent dye-labeled REVs. At designated time points, the rats were euthanized, and the heart, liver, spleen, kidneys, and femur were collected for ex vivo imaging analysis using the IVIS Lumina Series III In Vivo Imaging System (PerkinElmer).

Cell culture and osteogenic differentiation

Primary human bone mesenchymal stem cells (hBMSCs, Male, RRID: CVCL_A9JT) were purchased from OriCell® Company (Cyagen Biosciences, Guangzhou, China). Cell culture contamination can be identified by performing tests for bacteria, fungi, and mycoplasma. For routine culture, hBMSCs were maintained in α-MEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1% glutamine at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. For osteogenic differentiation, hBMSCs were induced using osteogenic induction medium (OIM) containing 50 μM ascorbate-2-phosphate, 100 nM dexamethasone, and 10 mM β-glycerophosphate for 2 weeks, with medium changes every 3 days. For adipogenic differentiation, hBMSCs were induced using adipogenic induction medium containing 500 nM dexamethasone, 50 µМ Indometacin,100 ug/ml insulin, and 0.5 mM 3-isobutyl-1-methylxantine for 14–17 days, with medium changes every 3 days.

CCK8 assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions. hBMSCs (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured in growth medium at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 24 hours. The cells were then treated with REVs at concentrations of 0, 1, or 10 μg/mL for 24 or 48 hours. After treatment, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 1 hour. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA).

ALP activity assay and Alizarin red S staining

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity was determined by measuring the conversion of p-nitrophenyl phosphate (pNPP) to p-nitrophenol (pNP) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For Alizarin red S staining, cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4.0% paraformaldehyde after differentiation. The cells were then stained with Alizarin red S for 30 minutes at room temperature, and images were captured using a phase-contrast microscope.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed on ice using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST (TBS with 0.1% Tween-20) and then washed three times with TBST. Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system. The primary antibodies used were ALP (381009, ZEN-Bio Science, China, concentration: 1:1000), RUNX2 (860139 & 660207, ZEN-Bio Science, China, concentration: 1:1000), OCN (614487, ZEN-Bio Science, China, concentration: 1:1000), OPN (SC-21742, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA, concentration: 1:1000), PPAR-γ (R30029, ZEN-Bio Science, China, concentration: 1:1000), C/EBPα (222172, ZEN-Bio Science, China, concentration: 1:1000), p-AKT (#4060, CST, USA, concentration: 1:1000), AKT (#3653, CST, USA, concentration: 1:1000), FOXO1 (#2880, CST, USA, concentration: 1:1000), β-actin (380624, ZEN-Bio Science, China, concentration: 1:1000)

Immunofluorescence assay

hBMSCs with or without REVs treatment were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes at room temperature and permeabilized with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 (PBST) for 10 minutes. The cells were blocked with 3% BSA in PBST for 30 minutes at room temperature, then incubated with anti-Runx2 (ZEN-Bio Science, #660207, concentration:1:200) or anti-FOXO1 primary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Inc. #2880, concentration:1:200) overnight at 4 °C. Afterward, the cells were incubated with anti-DyLight 568 secondary antibody (1:200) or DyLight 488 secondary antibody (1:200) for 1 hour and counterstained with DAPI for 5 minutes. Finally, the cells were visualized using a laser-scanning confocal microscope (Olympus IX83, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). In this study, immunofluorescence (IF) images were quantified based on mean fluorescence intensity using ImageJ. All images for a given experiment were acquired under identical acquisition settings. Prior to quantification, images were converted to 8-bit. For each image, the mean fluorescence intensity was measured using the Analyze tool.

Oil Red O staining

hBMSCs with REVs or histidylleucine treatment or not were washed twice with PBS and fixed in 10% formalin for 30 minutes. The cells were then washed twice with water and incubated with 60% isopropanol for 5 minutes, followed by staining with Oil Red O working solution for 10 minutes. After staining, the cells were washed three times with water, and images were captured using a phase-contrast microscope.

Untargeted metabolomics

For UHPLC-MS/MS analysis, REVs were dissolved in 1 mL of extraction buffer (methanol:acetonitrile:water = 2:2:1). The samples were quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen for 5 min and homogenized three times. The mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was vacuum-concentrated until completely dry. UHPLC separation was performed using a Waters HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm). The mobile phase consisted of water with 0.1% formic acid (A) and 100% methanol (B), with a flow rate of 300 μL/min. Metabolites were identified by matching molecular weights, substance names, and formulas against the Human Metabolome Database.

Polypeptide sequencing was analyzed by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Polypeptides were dissolved in 0.1% formic acid, vortexed for 15 minutes, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 minutes. The supernatant was re-dissolved in Nano-LC mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid/water) and loaded onto a nanoViper C18 column (3 μm, 100 Å). The liquid phase was performed using an Easy nLC 1200 nanoliter system (ThermoFisher, USA) with an analytical C18 reversed-phase column (Acclaim PepMap RSLC, 75 μm × 25 cm, 2 μm, 100 Å). Mobile phase B consisted of 80% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid. Raw mass spectrometry data were analyzed using PEAKS Studio 8.5 software (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc., Waterloo, Canada).

AST and ALT measurement

The rat tail was immersed in warm water (45–50 °C) for 1–2 minutes to engorge the tail vein. After drying and disinfecting with 70% ethanol, the tail tip was rapidly cut 0.2–0.5 cm from the end using sterile scissors. Blood was allowed to drip naturally into a centrifuge tube without anticoagulant and left upright at room temperature for 30–60 minutes to clot. The clotted blood was then centrifuged at 4 °C and 1,500 × g for 10–15 minutes, yielding three layers: the uppermost clear yellowish layer being rat serum. Rat serum was used for the measurement of Aspartate aminotransferase (AST; Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd) and Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT; Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd) using ELISA kits in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols.

The preparation REV-like nanoparticles Histidylleucine knockout and knockin (RLNP-KO and RLNP-KI)

TBME extraction of REVs membrane components, followed by lyophilization and resuspension in anhydrous ethanol to form the lipid phase, was carried out. A citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 4.0) with or without Histidylleucine was used as the aqueous phase. Microfluidic technology was employed to reassemble the components, yielding RLNP‑KO and RLNP‑KI. Specific parameters included an aqueous‑to‑lipid phase volume ratio of 3:1 and a total flow rate of 12 mL/min. Unencapsulated dipeptide was removed using a 35 kDa dialysis membrane, and the suspension was centrifuged at 3,500 × g for 10 min. The filtrate was discarded, and the concentrated liposome suspension was retained. The product was washed several times with PBS and centrifuged to obtain the final target preparation (RLNP-KO and RLNP-KI). The RLNP‑KO and RLNP‑KI were characterized using TEM and NTA.

Statistics and Reproducibility

All experiments were performed with at least three biologically independent samples (n ≥ 3), and the exact sample sizes are detailed in the corresponding figure legends.

All data are shown as mean ± SD. Significant differences between groups were determined by the t-test, while multiple group comparisons were performed using one‑way ANOVA. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.
