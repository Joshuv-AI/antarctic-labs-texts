Systematic Search of Height and Weight Changes of Exclusively Breastfed Infants Until 1 Year of age: A Contribution from the ConcePTION Project
Authors: Martje Van Neste, Julia Macente, Nina Nauwelaerts, Lieveke Ameye, Annick Bogaerts, Anne Smits, Pieter Annaert, Karel Allegaert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modelling and simulation allows prediction of drug exposure in specific populations, such as infants during lactation. However, the influence of feeding type (e.g., human milk vs formula) on physiology has not yet been implemented in current PBPK platforms. We conducted a systematic search to compile datasets during the first year of life of infants who were exclusively breastfed for at least 4 months to incorporate in the virtual breastfed infant populations of PBPK platforms. Physiological data in exclusively breastfed infants were extracted from 223 included articles. This article reports the results on sex-specific height and weight data, collected from 35 and 43 articles, respectively, and assesses these data for girls and boys separately. The datasets were converted to pooled means ± standard deviation and subsequently to mathematical equations describing height and weight trajectories for exclusively breastfed infants. For the purpose of external verification, the novel function was compared with Flemish height and weight profiles stratified by maternal origin, revealing the most similarity with breastfed infants from European mothers. Furthermore, to assess the differences in current functions from PBPK software, data from the literature showed that current PBPK height and weight equations often overestimate relative to the novel equations for breastfed infants from 6 months onwards. These overestimations may result in differences in PBPK predictions. Systematic searches to assess maturational processes of other physiological parameters (e.g., body composition) in exclusively breastfed infants is likely warranted. These patterns should be incorporated in PBPK platforms to more adequately represent infant exposure to medicines, specifically for lactation-related medicine systemic exposure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40262-025-01583-4.

Introduction

Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) models integrate population-specific data with medicine- and trial design-specific data to enable simulation-based prediction of exposure to medicines in specific populations [1]. These models are widely used in drug development, for example to help identify the starting dose in paediatric trials or to develop clinical trials with smaller sample sizes [2, 3]. The prevalence and impact of this approach is expected to increase over time as it allows predictions when data are scarce or inclusion of specific populations are avoided because of approval [3–5].

Infant PBPK models have already been developed, applying extrapolation from ‘healthy’ adult PBPK models [6]. Various PBPK platforms have included a general infant population in their software.1,2 To adequately predict infant systemic exposure, accurate physiological data are essential as the predictive performance of PBPK models depends on the quality of implemented data. However, these platforms do not yet distinguish between the physiology of infants fed with human milk and those fed with formula, even though differences in maturational physiology have been described [6]. Consequently, the generalisation of infant physiology, independent of feeding type, compromises the predictive performance of PBPK models for the breastfed infant population. This concern is particularly relevant, but not limited to, PBPK efforts to predict lactation-related medicine systemic exposure in infants [7, 8].

Multiple global organisations, such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations International Children's Emergency Fund (UNICEF), recommend that infants should be exclusively breastfed up to 6 months of age and partially breastfed until at least 2 years of age. Because of these recommendations, breastfeeding rates are rising and are expected to rise further [9, 10]. As the prevalence of infants ever breastfed was 95% globally, there is an increased demand for and higher relevance of simulating breastfed infant populations, for example, in case of exposure to medicines via human milk [11]. Breastfeeding offers many advantages for the infant and mother, such as lower infant morbidity and mortality, a reduced risk of breast and ovarian cancers for mothers, and a decreased risk of obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus for both [9, 12, 13]. The effects of breastfeeding also influence infant physiology and, subsequently, pharmacokinetic parameters in breastfed infants.

Gastric emptying is affected by diet (human milk vs formula milk), with a shorter gastric residence time and faster gastric emptying for human milk [14, 15]. In addition, differences have been observed for gastric emptying rate, serum biochemistry, and first-pass-related metabolizing enzyme activity (e.g., cytochrome P450) [16].

Variations in height and weight have also been observed in infants with different types of feeding. For instance, the initial weight loss in breastfed infants is more pronounced because of a higher loss of fat tissue than of fat-free tissue and, therefore, require more time to recover their birth weight than formula-fed infants [17, 18]. Weight gain later in life is also modulated, as breastfed infants exhibited faster weight gain up to 2 months of age and slower weight gain between 3 and 12 months compared with the 2000 Centers for Disease Control and Prevention growth charts based on a formula- and breastfed infant population [19].

These physiological changes might have a significant impact on pharmacokinetics in infants and should therefore be reflected in the physiological databases of PBPK platforms that generate virtual populations of breastfed infants. To fully capture the maturational physiology and its effect on pharmacokinetics, a systematic search was conducted to compile novel datasets on various physiological parameters of exclusively breastfed infants. These datasets were subsequently used to derive parameter values of mathematical functions that can be used for accurate and precise generation of age-specific physiology in virtual populations of breastfed infants. Future PBPK modelling and simulation expeditions in the breastfed infant population can rely on these improved population descriptions to more accurately predict systemic exposure to medicines in this population group.

This review aimed to characterize the growth trajectories of infants exclusively breastfed for ≥4 months worldwide collected through a systematic search and subsequently to generate sex-specific height and weight equations that more accurately reflect growth trajectories during the first year of life. Furthermore, the newly developed dataset and equations were compared with external data on height and weight from a Flemish cohort comprising both breastfed and formula-fed infants, stratified by maternal origin. Lastly, these novel equations were compared with existing height and weight equations available in currently used PBPK software platforms (Simcyp and PK-Sim), with WHO and 1977 National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) growth charts, and with equations found in literature to establish the adequacy of these current equations for breastfed infants [20, 21].

Methods

Systematic Search and Dataset Compilation

A systematic search, developed in consultation with the Biomedical Sciences Library of KU Leuven (Leuven, Belgium), was conducted on 22–23 February 2022 as registered at open science framework (https://osf.io/mhgf2/). Concepts regarding “infant”, “breastfed”, and physiological parameters influencing pharmacokinetics were searched in four different databases: PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, and Web of Science (see the electronic supplementary materials [ESM]). Using Covidence, the results were deduplicated and were manually screened by two independent reviewers on in- and exclusion criteria. Articles were included if they were written in English, described term infants up to 1 year of age and exclusively breastfed up to 4 months or longer without any medical conditions such as very low birth weight or congenital malformations (see the ESM). There were no restrictions on year of publication. A third reviewer resolved any disagreements.

Relevant data regarding physiological parameters during the first year of life of infants exclusively breastfed for ≥ 4 months were extracted from the included articles and compiled using Microsoft Excel (version 16.96). Data from infants who were exclusively breastfed for < 4 months were also collected up to the timepoint of discontinuation of exclusive breastfeeding. Data from figures were extracted using WebPlotDigitizer (version 4.6).

This article reports the height (cm) and weight (kg) data collected via this systematic search. If not mentioned otherwise, reported data were extracted assuming mean ± standard deviation (SD). Furthermore, standard errors and confidence intervals (CIs) were converted to SD, according to chapter 6 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [22]. Identical cohorts described in separate articles were processed as one cohort.

Data were stratified per longitudinal time points during the first year of life. A pooled mean ± SD was calculated for the longitudinal time points using Equation 1 and 2, respectively, for each sex. For each individual article, the largest sample size (n) of each cohort was used at all time points for this study. The pooled mean ± SD were segmented as monthly time points with optional intermittent time points based on the availability and volume of the data. In other words, if the number of included infants was greater than the month with the lowest number of included infants (month 11; n at interval ≥ n of month 11) (Table 1). Flemish Cohort of Breastfed and Formula-Fed Infants

The newly derived height and weight functions were compared with the height and weight data of a Flemish infant population cohort collected by Kind and Gezin3 (Agentschap Opgroeien) and the Study centre of Perinatal Epidemiology. This way, the applicability of the novel equations to real-world data was explored to generate more confidence. These datasets described the height and weight pattern during the first year of life (up to 60 weeks) of Flemish infants born between 2009 and 2017. These Flemish data were stratified by maternal origin (Europe, Africa, Asia, and South and Central America) and feeding type, more specifically, no breastfeeding (n = 7478), ≥ 3 months exclusive breastfeeding but < 6 months (n = 8248), and ≥6 months exclusive breastfeeding (n = 4287). Growth and feeding type were assessed at 24 h, 6 days, 6 and 12 weeks, and 6 months of age. Data on feeding type could be ‘not completed’ or ‘not applicable’ for, at most, two time points if interpretation of exclusive breastfeeding duration was still possible. Locally weighted smoothing function methodology was used to estimate the centre of the weight/length distribution for every week.

Results

The systematic search yielded 15,770 articles from the different databases. After deduplication, 9447 articles remained, which was reduced to 1216 articles after screening of titles and abstracts. Eventually, 223 articles were included after full-text assessment (Fig. 1).Fig. 1PRISMA flowchart of the systematic search

PRISMA flowchart of the systematic search

The included articles reported studies mainly conducted in Europe (35%), Asia (22%), and North America (20%), with some articles in Central and South America (11%), Africa (6%), and Australia (6%). In total, data were reported on 176,284 infants, primarily from North America (63%). Other population groups were from Europe (7%), Asia (4%), Central and South America (2%), Africa (1%), and Australia (0.4%).

From these 223 included articles, 106 and 147 articles reported on height and weight characteristics in exclusively breastfed infants, respectively (see the ESM) [18, 19, 24–172]. When looking at sex-specific data, 34 articles reported on height and 43 articles on weight [19, 24–65].

When dividing the articles by region of study (Europe, Africa, Asia, and South and Central America), the numbers of articles describing height and weight for sexes separately were limited. More specifically, no article reported height and weight from 6 to 12 months of age for girls and boys separately in South and Central America. Furthermore, only one article described weight, and no article described the height of exclusively breastfed African girls and boys separately.

Compatibility with Population Groups

The compiled datasets from the systematic search were divided into four different population groups (European, African, Asian, and South and Central American), and the weighted means were compared with the newly derived equations. The height function for exclusively breastfed girls was most compatible with the European cohorts (MAE 0.4 vs 0.67 and 0.78) (Fig. 4). For exclusively breastfed boys, the height function was most compatible with the South and Central American cohort with an MAE of 0.49 (vs 0.71 and 1.52). Discussion

This review reported the evolution of height and weight in infants exclusively breastfed for ≥ 4 months, based on a systematic search to determine growth parameters during the first year of life. This search focused on the physiological parameters influencing lactation-related medicine systemic exposure. Furthermore, these data were used to parametrize mathematical functions for age-specific height and weight in this specific breastfed population over the first year of life. The resulting equations showed low variance, with the smallest R2 being 0.96, whereas SER values for height were 1.21 and 1.43 cm in girls and boys, respectively. The SER values for weight were 0.38 kg in both sexes. At certain time points during the first year of life (e.g., birth and 3, 6, 9, and 12 months), more data points were available. This is in line with the follow-up in clinical practice and the faster growth in the first months of the first year of life. This was evident when observing the range for height in breastfed infants, as the range and—more specifically—the 95% CI became narrower for these time points. Since the weight functions were generated using an exponential function rather than a polynomial function, this effect was not apparent for weight.

Height and weight of infants are considered interdependent and are implemented accordingly in PBPK software, such as the inclusion of the height function in the weight function in the Simcyp platform (V21). Based on this principle, we a priori chose to adopt the functions of this PBPK platform in the present analysis to reflect the dependency of weight on height. Furthermore, this approach will facilitate future implementation in the PBPK software to assess the impact of the newly derived equations for (exclusively) breastfed infants in lactation-related PBPK predictions.

The applicability of these newly developed equations was assessed for different population groups (Figs. 4, 5). The novel equations seem to be appropriate for exclusively (≥ 4 months) breastfed infants of the European, Asian and South and Central American population. More particularly, the height equation for girls is most applicable to the European cohort, whereas the height equation for boys is most compatible with South and Central American populations. The weight equations were most applicable with the European population. However, it was illustrated that the newly developed weight equations might be less accurate when studying the African breastfed infant population. This might be partly explained by the large number of studies conducted in Europe included in this review.

Similarly, the same trend was observed when the novel equations were compared with the Flemish dataset to assess the comparability with real-world data. Compared with different populations, a considerable difference in weight trajectories was observed for infants from African origins and the novel weight equations. However, the multicentre growth reference study by the WHO reported similar growth patterns in well-nourished, healthy children from diverse ethnic groups [173]. Therefore, it is likely that these differences in weight are not only explained by genetic variation but are also mainly affected by the environmental and cultural conditions of the infants’ habitat [174, 175]. Furthermore, the Flemish data considered only maternal origin to determine the population group of the infant. Consequently, it is possible that infants WHO have a mixed genetic background, since parents with different origin countries, were not accounted for.

When comparing these novel equations specifically parametrized based on breastfed infant data with already existing equations and datasets for infants, these existing equations overestimate growth, specifically weight, of exclusively breastfed infants from 6 months onwards relative to the novel equations [176, 177]. These currently used equations and datasets do not differentiate between feeding type in infants, illustrated by weight trajectories compatible with the growth and weight gain in formula-fed infants. As weight and size affect pharmacokinetic processes, such as volume of distribution and drug clearance, this misestimation suggests that the predictive performance of PBPK models using these existing equations will be biased for breastfed infants [178].

Apart from a minimal difference on the height of boys, the WHO growth charts are the most consistent with our newly compiled dataset. Since the WHO growth charts are based on healthy infants who were predominantly breastfed for ≥4 months (with the inclusion of water-based liquids such as juice and sugar water) [173], this similarity was expected. However, these growth charts are still not completely suited for PBPK modelling for exclusively breastfed infants who are fed only human milk and no other liquids. Additionally, to our knowledge, the WHO growth charts do not provide an equation describing growth over age to implement in PBPK software.

A separate weight function for breastfed infants was included in the lactation module of the Simcyp software (V21). This function is highly comparable to the current datasets (Fig. 8) and newly developed equations in this review. Unfortunately, this function in Simcyp is only generated for weight and is solely used to predict infant systemic exposure to maternal medicine through human milk for lactation PBPK models. Therefore, this weight function cannot be used to simulate separate infant PBPK models to predict systemic exposure in breastfed infants needing direct pharmacotherapy.

Some limitations were noticed while conducting this review, including our inability to assess the quality of the selected articles because of the vast number of articles. Therefore, this review is registered and reported as a semi-systematic review. Moreover, the number of infants of the article was generalised for all time points during the first year of life. The sample size was extracted according to the intention-to-treat principle, which did not account for the dropout rate. Consequently, the statistical weight of articles might be overestimated in the later months of the first year of life. Additionally, this review did not incorporate the weight loss during the first days of life, as models have already been described for this neonatal phenomenon [177]. Therefore, the newly derived functions are expected to be most accurate from 1 week up to 12 months of age. Furthermore, data on infants from African or Central and South American countries to compare with the Flemish models were limited. Particularly when examining girls and boys separately, articles describing height and weight were limited, with only one article describing the weight patterns and no articles describing the height patterns in breastfed African infants. Lastly, the Flemish dataset displayed heavier formula-fed infants than infants exclusively breastfed for ≥ 6 months, albeit this was a small difference. This difference may be explained by missing data on feeding type resulting in omission of the data or the smaller volume of this dataset as this was divided by maternal origin.

The strength of this review lies in the systematic search that was conducted to collect physiological data of this special population. Since knowledge on the physiological maturation in breastfed infants (e.g., growth, fat mass, albumin) is scattered and non-standardized or based on assumptions because of the limited data, systematic searches are relevant methodological approaches to enable thorough assessment of current literature. Through an in-depth systematic search, these assumptions could be reduced and accurate incorporation of physiological data in PBPK models can be supported. We claim that this workflow, also illustrated in a previous article [179], should also be applied to compile data on other physiological parameters, such as body composition (e.g., fat mass and fat-free mass), and to report these data as mathematical functions suitable for integration into PBPK models.

In conclusion, this semi-systematic review with a systematic search reported novel parametrized equations describing the growth trajectories during the first year of life in an infant population exclusively breastfed for ≥4 months. These new equations should be used when simulating for this population rather than the currently used height and weight equations in various PBPK software to predict lactation-related exposure to maternal medicine. Existing equations show bias towards significant overestimation of weight gain from 6 months onwards relative to the novel equations. Even though limitations have been described, this workflow has the potential to compile datasets and parametrize equations on other physiological parameters that can be incorporated in PBPK models for exclusively breastfed infants.
