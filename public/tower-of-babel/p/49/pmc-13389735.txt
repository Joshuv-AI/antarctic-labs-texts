Pathological fracture of the fifth metatarsal bone from an extraosseous ganglion cyst
Authors: Mon Gabriel Distor, Keiichi Muramatsu, Gayle Krystle Grey, Yo Morita, Yuto Ichimiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective: Ganglion cysts are benign soft-tissue tumors that are relatively rare in the foot. Although most foot ganglia are asymptomatic, cases of structural compromise and pathological fractures are rare.Patient: A 65-year-old woman presented with right lateral foot pain without direct trauma. Physical examination revealed a soft, non-erythematous mass between the fourth and fifth metatarsals. Imaging demonstrated a scalloping depression at the fourth metatarsal and a pathological fracture of the fifth metatarsal shaft.Results: A surgical excision was performed by tracing the cyst to the fourth metatarsophalangeal joint. The fifth metatarsal was stabilized using a four-hole plate. Bony union was achieved at six weeks with pain resolution.Conclusion: This case highlights the importance of considering the mechanical compression exerted by the cyst and possible osteoporosis as contributing factors. Complete excision with stalk removal combined with fixation is recommended to prevent recurrence and to optimize functional outcomes.

Introduction

Ganglion cysts are benign soft-tissue tumors that are usually multilocular and filled with a viscous translucent fluid. These are composed of proteins and hyaluronic acid. Capsular walls are composed of compressed collagen fibers sparsely lined with flattened cells. These are usually found adjacent to joints and, less frequently, along tendon sheaths and bone1, 2).

First described by Hippocrates as knots of tissue with mucoid flesh, they are more commonly found in the hand and wrist. In the foot, recorded incidence vary from 8% to 10%, with most occurring at the dorsum1, 2). In this area, stalks connecting the cysts occur more frequently in the tendon sheath than in the joint1, 2).

Mucoid degeneration is the most widely accepted theory regarding the origin of ganglion cysts. However, connective tissue proliferation, as well as synovial herniation, have also been proposed1). McEvedy supported the theory that bursal expansion was the primary cause. As frequent motion and friction occur in these structures, capsular weakness inevitably occurs, causing fluid inflow into the superficial sacs of fibrous tissue and distending the cysts1). Direct and indirect trauma play an important role in its pathology. Most theories are still in development and subject to scrutiny, often reserved for academic debate. Even with established evidence for treatment, it is prudent to consider each theory when presenting with ganglion cysts.

To the best of our knowledge, there are very few reports on ganglion cysts of the foot, including subsequent pathologic fractures. Here, we report a case of pain in the lateral side of the foot with no apparent direct trauma.

Case description

A 65-year-old woman presented with right lateral foot pain for at least one month. No particular trigger was identified. As the pain worsened, the patient consulted a local doctor and was referred to our institution. The patient had begun treatment for osteoporosis at a previous clinic and underlying bone fragility was present. Discussion

Ganglion cysts at the foot and ankle are more commonly found in the dorsum (62%) and rarely in the plantar aspect (2%)2). Conservative treatment has been described in the form of aspiration and steroid injections; however, surgical excision remains the most beneficial course with lower recurrence rates.

Very few reports have described ganglion cysts that cause structural compromise in the metatarsal bones of the foot. Serigano et al. described a periosteal ganglion compressing the neck of the third metatarsal3). Radiography revealed cortical erosion of the neck of the third metatarsal. Shimozono et al. first reported a case of an extraosseous ganglion cyst compressing the two metatarsals that caused stress fractures4). All patients presented with pain in the forefoot while weightbearing and were eventually treated with surgical excision.

To the best of our knowledge, this report uniquely describes a complete pathological fracture from a foot ganglion. The scalloping found in the fourth metatarsal appeared to mimic the pathogenesis of previously reported cases. Given the patient’s age and sex, osteoporosis may have also contributed to this condition.

Traditionally, operative fixation of fifth metatarsal fractures has been reserved for patients presenting with angulations greater than 10° and displacements of 3–4 mm. However, the benefits of adhering to these measurements lack supporting evidence5, 6). Furthermore, displacement, although not apparent at the initial consultation, may eventually lead to shortening and elevation of the metatarsal head. Furthermore, patients face prolonged healing time and functional impairment when treated nonoperatively6). Thompson et al. reviewed 64 patients who were surgically treated fifth metatarsal fractures. They reported a mean healing time of 7.73 ± 4.74 weeks and a non-union rate of 1.56%6). Faster healing time may contribute to better functional outcomes, which can be prevented by prolonged immobilization.

In a retrospective review by Steinweg, persistent pain was reported in 11% of conservatively treated fifth metatarsal shaft fractures7). This is consistent with the findings of Bigsby where 39% still reported pain after 4 months of non-operative treatment. These findings persisted at the 1-year follow-up, with 33% still reporting pain and 10% reporting restrictions in daily activities8).

Given the age and sex of the patient, osteoporosis may have contributed to the fracture of the fifth metatarsal. Lateral foot pain may have caused guarding of the foot and modified the gait mechanics of the patient. Compression from the ganglion may have eventually overcome the structural integrity of the metatarsal.

Studies by Varenni et al. and Tomczak and VanCourt have shown that female post-menopausal patients presenting with low-energy metatarsal fractures have an increased risk of spinal osteoporosis9, 10). In 113 metatarsal fractures, 54.9% had bone mineral density below 0.759 g/cm2, significantly higher than that of the unfractured control10). The implications of these findings are vast, spanning clinical and prognostic sequelae. However, the findings remain controversial. Alternatively, Bridges and Ruddick found no correlation between low-energy metatarsal fractures in post-menopausal women and low bone mineral density11).

Pathological fractures of the metatarsals of the foot due to extraosseous ganglion cysts are rare. As indicated in previous studies, the mechanism by which this condition occurs appears to be consistent with structural weakness resulting from persistent compression combined with underlying osteoporosis. Complete cyst resection is necessary to prevent recurrence. This entails isolating the stalk up to the source and then resecting it. Finally, proper stabilization with plates and screws is crucial for improving functional outcomes through early mobilization and preventing complications such as metatarsalgia and non-union.

Conclusion

Ganglion cysts are benign soft-tissue tumors that are relatively rare in the foot. Most foot ganglia are asymptomatic and pathological fractures are exceedingly rare. Herein, we presented the case of a 65-year-old woman with a pathological fracture of the fifth metatarsal shaft caused by an extraosseous ganglion cyst of the fourth metatarsal. This case underscores the importance of considering mechanical compression exerted by a ganglion cyst and possible osteoporosis as contributing factors. Complete excision combined with fixation is recommended to prevent recurrence and to optimize functional outcomes.
