Comparative physiological and transcriptomic analyses reveal different stress adaptation strategies controlling buckwheat chilling tolerance
Authors: Hanmei Du, Wen Zheng, Runqi Liu, Sijing Wang, Changhe Wei, Lu Tan, Yan Yi, Qiuzhu Yu, An’hu Wang, Xianjun Lai, Yonghong Zhou, Houyang Kang, Dandan Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTartary buckwheat is the most important traditional minor crop in the high and cold mountain area of southwest China. Its growth and development were seriously inhibited by chilling stress. However, the molecular and physiological adaptive mechanisms of chilling stress tolerance in Tartary buckwheat are still not well understood.ResultsThis study performed physiological and transcriptome analyses on the seedlings of two low-temperature resistance (LT-R) and three LT sensitive (LT-S) Tartary buckwheat cultivars under normal (22℃) and low (4℃) temperature conditions. The results revealed that LT-R cultivars exhibited smaller changes in oxidative damage and photosynthetic pigment levels (chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b contents) compared to LT-S cultivars. They also exhibited significant increases in antioxidant enzyme activity. A total of 2932, 4114, 3975, 2499, and 5905 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in the five materials after chilling stress, respectively. DEGs were enriched in photosynthesis, glutathione (GSH) metabolism, cell wall synthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, carbon metabolism, peroxisome, and membrane stability-related pathways. Several AP2/ERF, bHLH, bZIP, DOF, GATA, HSF, MYB, NAC, and WRKY transcription factors also responded to chilling stress in both LT-R and LT-S cultivars.ConclusionThe results increase our understanding of the chilling stress tolerance in Tartary buckwheat seedlings and identify multiple key regulatory factors in the stress response, and also indicate that chilling stress affects photosynthesis and GSH metabolism of and that changes in these pathways might contribute to mechanisms of chilling stress tolerance in Tartary buckwheat.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07533-3.Keywords: Tartary buckwheat, Chilling stress, Physiological, Transcriptome, Photosynthesis, Glutathione

Background

Plants frequently encounter a range of abiotic stresses, including cold, heat, high salinity, drought, and heavy metals, in the natural environment, with temperature being a primary factor that influences the geographical distribution and growth duration [1–3]. Cold stress is categorized based on temperature into two types: chilling (0–15 °C) or freezing (< 0 °C) stress. Chilling stress leads to a series of slow comprehensive physiological and biochemical reactions in plants, particularly those related to enzymes and membrane transport activities, photosynthesis, and metabolic processes [4, 5]. Besides, chilling stress can also interfere with photosystem I (PS I) and PS II, electron transport chain (ETC), chlorophyll (Chl) biosynthesis, destroy the structure of cell membranes, affect the synthesis of osmotic regulating substances and antioxidant capacity, which negatively affects seed germination, plant growth and development, yield and quality [3, 6, 7].

Plants have developed various physiological and molecular mechanisms including the synthesis of substances or protective proteins involved in low temperature (LT) sensing and conduction [8, 9], cold response signaling pathways such as ABA-dependent pathway (ICE-CBF-COR) and ABA-independent signaling pathway [10–12], transcription factor regulation [13–15], and epigenetic regulation [16] to avoid or to minimize damage caused by chilling stress. COLD1 is a LT-sensing protein that could interact with the α subunit of G-protein or form a complex with RGA1 to activate the Ca2+ channel or calcium/calmodulin-regulated receptor-like kinases 1/2 (CRLK 1/2), and ultimately enhance the chilling tolerance in rice [17, 18]. Therefore, Ca2+ is an important second messenger in response to LT in plants. In addition, reactive oxygen species (ROS) and nitric oxide (NO) have also been reported to play signal transduction roles in plant response to chilling stress [19, 20].

Moreover, some transcription factors (TFs), such as CBF (DREB1), MYB, NAC, WRKY, bHLH, bZIP, AP2/ERF, and HSF, not only act as calmodulin-binding proteins during cold sensing, but also play important roles in elucidating the transcriptional regulatory networks of cold acclimation [21–25]. Besides, numerous genes associated with LT-tolerance have been identified in various crop species, and their underlying molecular mechanisms have been studied extensively. In rice, COLD1, CTB4a, and OsMADS57 play crucial roles in maintaining fertility and regulating ATP levels under LT conditions [17, 26, 27]. Similarly, maize possesses genes like ZmCDPK1, ZmSEC14p, and ZmMPK5, which contribute to its tolerance to LT [28, 29]. It is worth noting that previous studies have demonstrated a significant increase in the activity of antioxidant enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), in response to chilling stress, enhancing plants’ resistance to potential injury [30, 31].

Tartary buckwheat (Fagopyrum tataricum (L.) Gaertn), belonging to the Fagopyrum genus within the Polygonaceae family, is one of the most widely cultivated coarse grain crops in the alpine mountains of southwest China, and is believed to have originated in the Himalayan region [32, 33]. Recently, Tartary buckwheat has become a rich source of numerous health-benefiting compounds such as rutin, quercetin, dietary fiber, vitamins, and other nutrients [34, 35]. Although buckwheat can grow rapidly, adapt to harsh and infertile environments, and thrive in harsh and infertile environments, its growth and development are still affected by environmental factors, especially being highly sensitive to cold stress in the early development stages [36, 37]. Previous studies found that the seed germination rate as well as the value of average plant height of buckwheat decreased significantly when exposed to cold stress [38, 39]. In addition, exposure of buckwheat to LT during the seedling stage will slow down the accumulation of biomass and accelerate root growth, and affect seed development and grain yield in the later stage [40]. A previous study found that the flavonoid biosynthesis, some amino acids and their derivatives, organic acids derived from the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and the contents of anthocyanins and proanthocyanidins in Tartary buckwheat were affected by cold stress [41]. However, plant chilling stress tolerance is a complex trait, its genetic regulation mechanism is poorly understood. Therefore, continued efforts to understand the cold stress responses of different genetic materials are essential to further explore the pathways of Tartary buckwheat resistance to cold stress.

In previous research, we evaluated various Tartary buckwheat cultivars and landraces from southwest China during their germination stage. We selected two low-temperature resistant cultivars (LT-R), Chuanqiao No.10 (CQ10) and Xiqiao No.8 (XQ8), and three low-temperature sensitive cultivars (LT-S), which are Chuanqiao No.2 (CQ2), Xiqiao No.3 (XQ3), and Jiujiang (JJ) [39]. In this study, we attempted to investigate the transcriptomes of these five materials under chilling stress. The objective was to detect the physiological effects of chilling stress on different genotypes of Tartary buckwheat and to elucidate the molecular responses occurring at the transcriptome level in these materials under chilling stress. We sought to elucidate the complex regulatory mechanisms and identify key genes/TFs and pathways involved in the Tartary buckwheat response to chilling stress by using RNA-seq technology.

Results

The changes in physiological parameters under chilling stress at the seedling stage

To characterize the cold response mechanism of five Tartary buckwheat cultivars (LT-R: CQ10 and XQ8; LT-S: CQ2, XQ3, and JJ), we measured ten important physiological traits including the content of malondialdehyde (MDA), three key photosynthetic pigments (chlorophyll a (Chla), chlorophyll b (Chlb), and carotenoids), osmotic stress-related parameters (proline, soluble sugars, and soluble proteins), and antioxidant enzyme activity (SOD, CAT, and POD), in eight-day-old seedlings after three days of chilling stress at 4 °C. The results showed that low temperature (LT) induced changes in most indicators, with slight differences between the LT-R and LT-S cultivars. The increase in MDA content was less pronounced in the LT-R cultivars (CQ10, XQ8) compared to the LT-S cultivars (CQ2, XQ3, and JJ) (Fig. 1A). Compared with normal temperature (22℃, NT), the average increase of proline, soluble sugars, and soluble protein in the LT-R cultivars was 43.4%, 57.1%, and 43.5% higher than those in the LT-S cultivars under LT conditions, respectively (Fig. 1B-D). The content of photosynthetic pigments, specifically Chla and Chlb, decreased less in the LT-R cultivars compared to the LT-S cultivars (Fig. 1E-F). However, carotenoid levels were significantly reduced in the LT-R cultivars than in the LT-S cultivars (Fig. 1G). Antioxidant enzyme activity, including SOD, CAT, and POD was induced and increased in five buckwheat cultivars under cold stress. Notably, the increase was more significant in the LT-R cultivars for SOD and POD (Fig. 1H-J). All cultivars could activate protective mechanisms against chilling stress. The LT-R cultivars (CQ10 and XQ8) exhibited a slight increase in oxidative damage (MDA), and a slight decrease in photosynthetic pigment content (Chla and Chlb). In contrast, the LT-S cultivars (CQ2, XQ3, and JJ) showed a more moderate decrease in carotenoid content and a slight increase in antioxidant enzyme activity (SOD and POD).

Transcriptome analysis of different genotypes of Tartary buckwheat under NT and LT

To more comprehensively understand the gene expression profiles of five different genotypes of Tartary buckwheat at the seedling stage exposed to chilling stress, 30 cDNA libraries were constructed, which included three biological replicate libraries treated with NT and LT conditions. A total of 40,904,885, 41,941,225, 41,835,951, 41,737,866, 41,172,227, 41,444,971, 41,061,157, 42,135,668, 40,574,306, and 40,219,048 raw reads were generated from three replicate libraries for CQ10_NT, CQ10_LT, XQ8_NT, XQ8_LT, CQ2_NT, CQ2_LT, XQ3_NT, XQ3_LT, JJ_NT, and JJ_LT, respectively (Table 1). After stringent quality assessment and data filtering, the clean reads in all 30 samples were all above 91% of the raw reads, and each library consisted of more than 5.79 Gb of clean data. More than 91% clean bases from all samples successfully mapped to the reference genome of Pinku No.1 (Table 1). Based on the gene expression data from RNA-seq of each sample, Pearson correlation analysis indicated that the correlation coefficients (R2) among samples were distributed between groups but clustered within groups (R2 > 0.89, Fig. S1).

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identification

To determine the cold stress-responsive genes in five buckwheat cultivars, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified (Fig. 2, Table S1). In total, we identified 2932 (1305 up-regulated, 1627 down-regulated), 4114 (1589 up-regulated, 2525 down-regulated), 3975 (1386 up-regulated, 2589 down-regulated), 2499 (839 up-regulated, 1660 down-regulated), and 5905 (2562 up-regulated, 3343 down-regulated) DEGs from CQ10_NT vs. LT, XQ8_NT vs. LT, CQ2_NT vs. LT, XQ3_NT vs. LT, and JJ_NT vs. LT under LT (4℃) and NT (22℃), respectively (Fig. S2). Our analysis revealed that 605 DEGs (217 up-regulated, 388 down-regulated) were commonly altered in all cultivars, while 1741 DEGs (785 up-regulated, 966 down-regulated) and 866 DEGs (299 up-regulated, 567 down-regulated) were shared among the LT-R and the LT-S cultivars, respectively (Table S2). Large number of DEGs in the LT-R cultivars were considered important candidates for further investigation. In addition, 23 transcription factors (TFs, four up-regulated and 19 down-regulated), including AP2/ERF, bHLH, bZIP, DOF, GATA, HSF, MYB, NAC, and WRKY, were also differentially expressed under LT conditions in all five cultivars (Table S2). Combined with DEGs, we utilized the STRING database to explore and analyze protein-protein interactions and functions. In total, 14 TFs interacted with 41 DEGs in all five varieties (Table S2).

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis

To analyze the biological functions of the screened DEGs, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis for the DEGs. The DGEs were categorized into three categories, including biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC), based on the GO database. All cultivars showed enrichment in key GO terms (Fig. S3, Table S3). For the CQ10_NT vs. LT comparison, 2196 DEGs were divided into 20 functional groups, most of which were enriched in the “cytoplasm”, “cellular amide metabolic process”, “peptide metabolic process”, “cytoplasmic part”, and “amide biosynthetic process” (Table S3). In XQ8_NT vs. LT, CQ2_NT vs. LT, XQ3_NT vs. LT, and JJ_NT vs. LT comparisons, 844, 386, 1062, and 677 DEGs were divided into 17, 12, 12, and 18 functional groups, respectively (Table S3). The shared DEGs in the LT-R and LT-S cultivars were enriched in biological process, such as cellulose metabolism (GO:0030243), cellulose biosynthesis (GO:0030244), outer membrane (GO:0019867), polysaccharide biosynthesis (GO:0000271), and dextran biosynthesis (GO:0009250) (Fig. 3A). The unique DEGs identified in the LT-R cultivars were enriched in the photosynthesis (GO:0015979), photosystem (GO:0009521), photosynthetic membrane (GO:0034357), photosystem Ⅱ (GO:0009523), and oxidoreductase activity (GO:0016705) (Fig. 3B, Table S3). Meanwhile, the unique DEGs in the LT-S cultivars were enriched in sulfate transport (GO:0008272), outer membrane (GO:0019867), cellulose metabolism (GO:0030243), cellulose biosynthesis (GO:0030244), peptidase complex (GO:1905368), and sulfate transmembrane transporter activity (GO:0015116) (Fig. 3C). These results indicated that Tartary buckwheat cultivars are tolerant of low temperatures due to their distinct metabolic processes. The LT-R gene regulates the process of photosynthesis, while the LT-S gene regulates cell wall synthesis and membrane stability.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs

To further investigate the metabolic pathways enriched in these cultivars under LT conditions, we mapped them to the reference pathways in the KEGG database. A total of 1783 (CQ10_NT vs. LT), 1980 (XQ8_NT vs. LT), 1953 (CQ2_NT vs. LT), 1679 (XQ3_NT vs. LT), and 2483 (JJ_NT vs. LT) DEGs were assigned to 115, 118, 115, 116, and 116 KEGG pathways, respectively (Table S4). Based on the number of enriched DEGs, we identified the top 20 pathways. All cultivars showed enrichment in flavonoid biosynthesis (bvg00941), proteasome (bvg03050), DNA replication (bvg03030), and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (bvg00940) (Fig. 3D, Fig. S4). The unique DEGs in the LT-R cultivar were enriched in photosynthesis (bvg00195), photosynthesis-antenna proteins (bvg00196), DNA replication (bvg03030), proteasome function (bvg03050), and flavonoid biosynthesis (bvg00941) (Fig. 3E). Meanwhile, the unique DEGs in the LT-S cultivar were enriched in flavonoid biosynthesis (bvg00941), proteasome function (bvg03050), galactose metabolism (bvg00052), glycerol metabolism (bvg00561), and glutathione metabolism (bvg00480) (Fig. 3F). This indicates that different varieties exhibited distinct regulatory modules. Notably, the number of upregulated genes was predominant in the LT-R cultivar, while the number of downregulated genes was predominant in the LT-S cultivar. The photosynthesis pathway was upregulated in LT-R cultivars, whereas downregulated in LT-S cultivars. Besides, glutathione metabolism (bvg00480) and flavonoid biosynthesis (bvg00941) pathways were common in both LT-R and LT-S (Fig. 3E, F).

Identification of modules in response to cold stress by weighted gene co-expression network analysis

To obtain a comprehensive understanding of gene regulatory networks closely associated with buckwheat chilling tolerance, we performed a WGCNA using transcriptome data derived from five buckwheat cultivars subjected to cold stress, along with their corresponding controls at normal temperature. A total of 31,561 genes were effectively expressed (FPKM ≥ 1) in at least one sample for WGCNA (Table S5). Within the same cluster, genes exhibited similar expression patterns and high correlation coefficients (Fig. 4A). The dynamic tree-cut method identified 36 distinct co-expression modules, with module sizes varying from 44 to 4992 genes (Fig. 4B, Table S6). The grey and turquoise modules were the largest, containing 4992 and 4768 genes, respectively, while the yellow-green module contained the fewest genes. We evaluated the connectivity degree among genes in each module using eigengene connectivity (kME) values. Genes with high kME values (kME > 0.7) were designated as representative genes within the modules—known as module eigengenes (MEs) —and were used for further analysis (Table S5).

The Heatmap Eigengene Expression method was utilized to visualize the co-expression network, highlighting distinct trends within the turquoise module (Fig. 4C). Under low-temperature stress conditions, this module exhibited an overall positive expression, whereas under normal control conditions, it displayed a negative trend. The KEGG enrichment analysis showed that the module was significantly enriched in several pathways, including carbon metabolism (bvg01200), peroxisome (bvg04146), alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism (bvg00250), homologous recombination (bvg03440), and mismatch repair (bvg03430).

Differential expression of photosynthesis and carbon metabolism related genes induced by chilling stress

Based on the GO and KEGG enrichment analysis, we found that the highest enrichment biological processes (BP) and KEGG pathways were related to photosynthesis (GO:0015979, bvg00195) and carbon metabolism (bvg01200) in the LT-R group. A total of 31, 38, 13, 7, and 38 DEGs related to photosynthesis were identified in CQ10_NT vs. LT, XQ8_NT vs. LT, CQ2_NT vs. LT, XQ3_NT vs. LT, and JJ_NT vs. LT comparisons, respectively (Fig. 5, Table S7). Among them, 9, 13, 5, 3, and 13 DEGs were photosynthesis antenna protein genes. Most of these DEGs, two genes related to Ferrdoxin-NADP+ reductase (FNR, EC 1.18.1.2), namely FNR and PETH, along with three ATP synthase genes: ATPG, ATPC1, and ATPD, were significantly upregulated in LT-R cultivars under chilling stress, whereas downregulated in LT-S varieties except for in JJ_NT vs. LT. We observed the downregulation of the novel.602, FER6, SAPK3, PSBS, FD3, and FENRO genes in all five varieties, indicating that it is a common chilling stress response in Tartary buckwheat. Notably, the CBA36 and Psaeb gene expression was significantly upregulated in LT-R but downregulated in LT-S.

In the carbon metabolism pathway, a total of 77, 90, 58, 60, and 110 DEGs were identified in CQ10_NT vs. LT, XQ8_NT vs. LT, CQ2_NT vs. LT, XQ3_NT vs. LT, and JJ_NT vs. LT comparisons, respectively (Table S8). Among them, 19 DEGs were identified in all five varieties under LT conditions. Enolase (EC 4.2.1.11, ENO1) can catalyze the conversion of 2-phospho-D-glycerate (Glycerate-2P) to phosphoenolpyruvate and water, which is also a key step in the glycolytic pathway (Fig. S5). Three downregulated expression ENO1 genes, including the novel.1080, FtPinG0003207000.01, and novel.5321, caused negative regulation of enolase, which regulated the energy balance in the sugar metabolism pathway of plants under LT stress by reducing the activity of the glycolytic pathway, reducing oxidative stress damage, and protecting cell structure and function. These results indicated that photosynthesis and energy metabolism in carbon metabolism pathways play key roles in the response to chilling stress in Tartary buckwheat.

Glutathione metabolism pathway is involved in the chilling stress response in Tartary buckwheat

Based on WGCNA and KEGG enrichment analyses, two KEGG pathways, glutamate metabolism (bvg00250) and glutathione (GSH) metabolism (bvg00480), were found to be significantly enriched during chilling stress, the important metabolic pathways and related genes involved in GSH metabolism were investigated further (Fig. 6, Table S9). Notably, in the conversion of GSSH to GSH, several differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) were upregulated in LT-R, including G6PD (glucose-6-phosphate 1-dehydrogenase, G6PD and G6PD6), GS (glutathione synthetase, GSH1 and GSHRP2), and γ-ECS (γ-glutamylcyclotransferase, GCT23), whereas downregulated expression of IDH (isocitrate dehydrogenase, ICDH-1 and ICDHP) and GST (glutathione S-transferase, GSTFD, GSTU8-1, GSTU18, GSTU9-1, GSTF, GSTF13, GSTU9, GSTU8, GSTXC, GSTFB, GSTF11, GSTL1, GSTXC-1, MGST3, and UBP25). Additionally, the expression of GPX (glutathione peroxidase, GPX4, GPX6, and GPX8) which is a DAP for the conversion from GSH to GSSG, APX (ascorbate peroxidase, APX1, APX2, APX4, APX6, APXT, and MAA3), and DHAR (dehydroascorbate reductase, DHAR2) which were the DAPs for the conversion from GSH to monodehydroascorbate (MDHA) were also downregulated after exposing to chilling stress that resulted in enhanced GSH/GSSG signaling (Fig. 6B). Further, the above genes, combined with the upregulated OXP (5-oxoprolinase, OXP1) gene, (Fig. 6), can increase the level of GSH in LT-R Tartary buckwheat, thereby effectively improving the ability to scavenge ROS and enhancing chilling stress tolerance.

Validation of gene expression data by qRT-PCR

To verify the reliability of RNA-seq data, we randomly selected 13 DEGs related to photosynthesis (FtPinG0008168200.0, FtPinG0008444600.01, FtPinG0000127100.01, and FtPinG0002658600.01) and carbon metabolism pathways (FtPinG0003207000.01 and novel.1080) involved in chilling stress tolerance in Tartary buckwheat for qRT-PCR analysis (Fig. 7). The results obtained with qRT-PCR and transcriptome sequencing were highly correlated. The correlation coefficients between RNA-seq and qRT-PCR of 5 varieties ranged from 0.54 to 0.97, with an average of 0.72 (Fig. 7N), indicating that the transcriptome data are reliable.

Discussion

Chilling stress affects the plant biomembrane system, cellular osmoregulatory substances, and antioxidant system [42], and even threatens the productivity and yield reduction of many important crops such as wheat, rice, and maize in temperate climates [43–45]. Our results showed that chilling stress increased the accumulation of MDA, and reduced the synthesis of Chla, Chlb, and carotenoids in Tartary buckwheat (Fig. 1A, E, F, G), which will lead to lipid peroxidation, inhibit photosynthesis, and ultimately result in yield decline. Researchers found that plants could manage to overcome chilling stress through specific genetic characteristics related to their internal physiological and biochemical processes, including antioxidants, antioxidant enzymes, phytohormone, inorganic ions, and osmotic regulation substances [46–49]. For instance, the accumulation of osmoregulation substances, including vitamin C, soluble sugars, and free amino acids, could prevent the damage of chilling stress in spinach, wheat, and Prunus mume [50–52]. In this study, all five cultivars showed significant accumulation of stress-responsive metabolites, including proline, soluble sugars, and soluble proteins (Fig. 1B, C, D). Proline plays several important roles in helping plants withstand abiotic stress, such as osmotic adjustment, membrane protein stabilization, induction of osmotic stress gene expression, and scavenging ROS [53]. Particularly, proline can regulate cytosolic acidity level, maintain the NAD/NADH ratio, enhance the photochemical efficiency of PS II, and reduce lipid peroxidation [54]. We speculated that the reason why the LT-R varieties are more cold-tolerant than the LT-S varieties is that the increase of osmoregulation substances in the former is much greater than that in the latter. Additionally, the reduction in CO2 assimilation efficiency under LT stress leads to the accumulation of excessive reducing power on the PS I acceptor side, causing more electrons to be transferred to O2 and generating ROS. Several studies have shown that the expression and activity of antioxidative enzymes such as SOD, CAT and POD were significantly increased under chilling stress, which could help maintain cellular homeostasis [55, 56]. Similar to these reports, our study found an identical trend with a substantial enhanced activity of the antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POD, and CAT) during chilling stress (Fig. 1H, I, J). These results suggested that the negative impact of chilling stress on LT-R was reduced compared with LT-S, and osmoregulation substances, photosynthetic pigments, and antioxidant enzymes play important roles in chilling stress.

Transcription factors (TFs) could not only bind to cis-acting elements upstream of stress-responsive genes to activate or inhibit gene expression, but also participate in signal transduction under chilling stress [22–24]. In cucumber, the MYB-like TF CsHHO2 activated by cold signals could positively regulate the chilling tolerance of fruit by enhancing CsGR-RBP3 expressin [57]. Similarly, WRKY, NAC, AP2/ERF, bHLH, bZIP, and HSF were also involved in the regulation of chilling stress in rice [58, 59], wheat [60], buckwheat [61], melon [62], and peanut [63], etc. In the present study, we have identified a total of 23 TFs, including AP2/ERF, bHLH, bZIP, HSF, WRKY, NAC, and MYB, involved in the chilling stress response in Tartary buckwheat (Table S2). Among them, only four TF genes (one WRKY, one Dof, and two AP2/ERF) were induced in all five germplasms, while the other 19 TFs were significantly downregulated by chilling stress.

Chilling injury could decrease the photosynthetic rate of plants, impact the photosynthetic parameters, including the carbon reduction cycle, transpiration rate, and thylakoid electron transport, and ultimately inhibit photosynthesis [64, 65]. In this study, we found that FNR and PETH gene which were involved in the synthesis of Ferrdoxin-NADP+ reductase, which is part of the NADP redox family and promotes the reduction of NADP+ to NADPH [66], were significantly upregulated in the LT-R under chilling stress (Fig. 5). During the initial stages of photosynthesis, protein complexes and pigment molecules work together to convert solar energy into chemical energy stored in the form of NADPH and ATP. However, chilling stress could destroy the chloroplast structure, reduce light energy conversion efficiency, result in insufficient energy supply, and decrease the levels of the coenzyme NADPH [67]. To enhance the rate of NADPH production and maintain the energy and supply required for photosynthesis and growth, LT-R buckwheat varieties might increase the synthesis of NADP+ reductase by reducing the expression of FNR and PETH. Conversely, Chla and Chlb in LT-S materials were greatly consumed. Furthermore, ATP synthase (EC 7.1.2.2) plays a crucial role in photosynthesis by catalyzing the conversion of ADP and phosphate to ATP. To meet the energy demands of photosynthesis, plants may boost the expression and activity of ATP synthase under low temperatures [68, 69]. Our study found that the ATP synthesis genes ATPG, ATPC1, and ATPD were significantly upregulated in LT-R materials, increasing the rate of ATP synthesis and enhancing energy supply. Enolases play crucial roles in multiple metabolic pathways under chilling stress [70]. In Arabidopsis, enolase responded to a cold environment by regulating the transcription of cold-responsive genes [71]. In addition, this enzyme was regulated by cAMP-mediated signal cascades to adapt to LT in Botrytis cinerea [72]. While in the RNA degradation pathway, enolase could be used as a component of the RNA degradosome at LT to combine with the cold shock protein CsdA (DEAD-box RNA helicase) to acclimate plants to cold stress [73]. In this study, three ENO1 genes encoding enolases were simultaneously enriched in the carbon metabolism pathway and downregulated by LT stress (Fig. S5, Table S8). These results indicated that photosynthesis and energy metabolism help Tartary buckwheat acclimate to cold.

Plant cells produce increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) under conditions imposed by external stresses [74]. Fortunately, plants can scavenge the damage caused by ROS through antioxidant enzymes and antioxidants [23]. Glutathione (GSH) is a widely found substance in plant cells, which has antioxidant effects, maintains cellular redox balance, and regulates the osmotic pressure of plants under environmental stress. GSH/GSSG is composed of reduced glutathione (GSH) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG), which is one of the most abundant redox pairs in plant cells and can participate in intracellular redox balance, and could participate in plant signaling and resistance to adversity stress as signals [75]. Previous studies found that strawberries and grafted cucumbers could alleviate the damage caused by LT stress through the combined action of antioxidant enzymes and antioxidants such as GSH and ascorbic acid (AsA) [76, 77]. Meanwhile, the expression of related genes in the glutathione metabolic pathway might affect plant tolerance to LT stress [78]. In this study, we found that glutathione metabolic pathway was enriched in LT-R materials after chilling stress, and the differentially expressed proteins were mainly enzymes during the mutual transformation of oxidized glutathione (GSSG) and reduced glutathione (GSH), including: G6PD, IDH, and GST promoted significant upregulation of the production of reduced GSH, whereas GPX glutathione peroxidase (GPX4, GPX6, and GPX8), which promoted the production of oxidized glutathione, was significantly downregulated, thus promoting the ratio of GSH/GSSG and the accumulation of the antioxidant GSH (Fig. 6), thereby reducing oxidative damage caused by chilling stress.

Conclusion

We used physiological index measurement and RNA-seq technology to investigate the dynamic changes of five Tartary buckwheat varieties, CQ10, XQ8, CQ2, XQ3, and JJ, under normal and low temperature conditions. Physiological data analysis found that chilling stress affects the photosynthetic pigment content, membrane structural stability, antioxidant enzyme activity, and osmotic substance content of Tartary buckwheat. Transcriptomic analysis detected 2932, 4114, 3975, 2499, and 5905 DEGs after chilling stress in five materials, respectively. The expression of many TFs from the AP2/ERF, bHLH, bZIP, DOF, GATA, HSF, MYB, NAC, and WRKY gene families responded to chilling stress. We speculate that photosynthesis and glutathione metabolism are critical pathways for different Tartary buckwheat cultivars under chilling stress. Meanwhile, the energy metabolism and the maintenance of cell membrane integrity are also important for it to deal with cold (Fig. 8).

Materials and methods

Plant material, growth conditions, and chilling treatment

Five cultivars of Tartary buckwheat with different cold tolerance (low temperature sensitive, LT-S; low temperature-resistant, LT-R), including CQ10 (LT-R), XQ8 (LT-R), CQ2 (LT-S), XQ3 (LT-S), and JJ (LT-S), were used in this study (Table 2) [39]. Seeds were disinfected with a 15% NaClO solution for 5 min, washed five times with deionized water, and placed in culture dishes for germination in a growth chamber at 22 °C for 48 h. Seedlings were transferred to a controlled growth chamber for five days of growth (size: 45 cm × 32 cm × 28 cm, 1/2 Hoagland’s solution with pH = 6.0, temperature: 22 °C, light cycle: 16/8 h).

Materials used in this study

Young seedlings with uniform size were selected for control (treatment condition the same as the growth chamber above) and chilling treatment (except the temperature at 4 °C, treatment condition is the same as the growth chamber). After three days of cold treatment, seedlings were collected for physiological and biochemical measurements, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and kept at −80◦C for subsequent RNA extraction. Three biological replicates were performed for each treatment, having three technical replicates.

Determination of physiological indexes of seedlings under cold stress

The free proline content was measured using acid ninhydrin colorimetry using pure proline as a standard [79]. The soluble sugar was determined by anthrone colorimetry using glucose as a standard [80]. The soluble protein content was determined using the method of Coomassie brilliant blue G-250 staining at an absorbance of 595 nm with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as a standard [81].

The MDA content was determined by the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) method [82]. Plant photosynthetic pigment content (Chla, Chlb, and carotenoids) was estimated by 470 nm, 649 nm, and 665 nm using 95% ethanol isolated for 6 h with shaking in the dark conditions from fresh leaves. The activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT) was determined according to previous research methods [83].

RNA extraction, cDNA library construction, and sequencing

Total RNA was isolated from the buckwheat seedling after 3 days of chilling stress using the Trizol reagent kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, United States). RNA was examined for quality and quantified using agarose gel and the NanoDrop 2500 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, United States). RNA integrity was assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit of the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). The cDNA library was constructed and sequenced by Novogene company (Beijing, China). Sequencing was conducted using the Illumina Novaseq platform and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated.

We first processed raw data using fastp (v0.19.7) to obtain clean data by removing poly-N, adapters, and low-quality reads. Simultaneously, the Q20, Q30, GC-content, and sequence duplication levels of clean data were determined [84]. The high-quality clean dataset that was filtered from the raw reads was mapped to the F. tataricum cv. “Pinku1” [85] using Hisat2 (v2.2.0) [86]. Bowtie2 (v2.2.5) and StringTie (v2.1.2) were used to align the clean reads and calculate gene expression profiles, respectively [87].

Transcriptomic data analysis

The transcript abundance value (fragments per kilobase of transcripts per million mapped fragments, FPKM) of each gene was calculated using Feature Counts (v1.5.0-p3) [88]. We performed differential expression analysis using DESeq2 (v1.26.0) [89], which provided statistical routines for identifying differential expression in the digital gene expression dataset. Benjamini and Hochberg’s method of controlling the false discovery rate was then applied to adjust the resulting P-values. Genes with padj < 0.01 and |log2(foldchange)| ≥ 2 were found by classifying as differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using DESeq2 [90]. The Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis and the database of Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were conducted using the cluster Profiler (v3.8.1) based on the selected DEGs. For Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), we use the local version of the GSEA analysis tool (http://www.broadinstitute.org/gsea/index.jsp).

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

Gene co-expression networks were constructed using the WGCNA package (v1.69) in the R package [91]. A power value of 9 was selected to meet the scale-free topology criterion. The similarity matrix was transformed into the adjacency matrix according to the formula Amn = [(1 + Smn)/2] β, where Amn indicates the adjacency value, Smn means Pearson’s correlation between gene m and gene n, and β represents the power value. The resulting adjacency matrix was further converted to a topological overlap matrix (TOM) using the TOM similarity algorithm. The hierarchical clustering of genes in the dissimilarity matrix was performed by the hclust function in the WGCNA package, and the resulting clustering tree was cut by the dynamic tree cut to define the modules [92]. Module-sample associations were estimated using the correlation between the module eigengenes (MEs) and samples. The visualization of co-expression networks and the identification of hub genes in each module were implemented by Cytoscape software (v3.7.2) [93].

Validation of quantitative real-time PCR

Gene-specific primers were designed using Primer 5.0 software (Premier Biosoft International, Palo Alto, CA, USA) and were listed in Table S10. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the SYBR Green Master Mix kit, and the expression of genes was detected by CFX96Touch fluorescence quantitative PCR (Bio-Rad, USA). FtH3 was selected as the internal standard to calculate the relative expression levels as follows: FC = 2−ΔΔCt [94]. Three independent biological replicates for each sample for each gene were performed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using ANOVA, and then post-hoc multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey’s test in SPSS (v26.0) (Chicago, IL, USA). A significance level of P < 0.05 was applied to indicate significant values. Graphs were drawn and merged using Origin 8.0 (v9.1) (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA) and Adobe Photoshop CC 2018 software (San Jose, California, USA), respectively.
