Prevalence and risk factors of latent tuberculosis infection in healthcare workers in Eastern China
Authors: Lili Zhen, Shichao Shangguan, Jing Zhou, Shulei Wang, Liyong Lu, Yuelei Wang, Jingyu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealthcare workers (HCWs) are at an increased risk of latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) and active TB due to their occupational exposure to tuberculosis (TB). There are limited studies to assess the baseline prevalence of LTBI among HCWs in coastal eastern China. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the prevalence and risk factors of LTBI among HCWs.MethodsA cross-sectional study with stratified cluster sampling method was conducted. All HCWs with TB exposure from the selected healthcare institutions were recruited to complete a standardized questionnaire and to undergo testing by the creation tuberculin skin test (C-TST). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression was employed to identify determinants associated with LTBI.ResultsA total of 342 HCWs were included in the analysis. The prevalence of LTBI was found to be 13.16% (95% CI: 9.76–17.21%). The results of multivariate regression analysis showed that healthcare institutions level (OR = 4.131, 95% CI:1.687–10.118, p = 0.002); physician (OR = 3.132, 95% CI:1.132–8.662, p = 0.028), laboratory technician (OR = 5.545, 95% CI:1.385–22.200, p = 0.016); working duration 10–14 years (OR = 5.721, 95% CI: 1.435–22.809, p = 0.013), 15–19 years (OR = 4.305, 95% CI: 1.028–18.032, p = 0.046) and 20 years above (OR = 4.047, 95% CI: 1.079–15.171, p = 0.038); household contact with TB (OR = 4.488, 95% CI: 1.051–19.166, p = 0.043) were significantly associated with a higher prevalence of LTBI.ConclusionsThe LTBI prevalence among HCWs in Yantai is relatively low. TB screening should be included in the annual health check-ups for HCWs, especially for high-risk groups such as frontline HCWs, long-serving workers, and those with household contact with TB, to reduce the risk of TB infection. These findings highlight the need to enhance infection control measures and active surveillance for at-risk HCWs, even in low TB incidence settings. Background

Tuberculosis (TB) continues to pose a significant global health challenge, with an estimated 10.8 million new cases and 1.25 million deaths reported globally (WHO, 2023). China accounted for 6.8% of the global TB burden, with approximately 741,000 new cases reported in 2023, and an incidence rate of 52 per 100,000 population (WHO, 2023) [1]. Latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) represents a precarious equilibrium between the bacteria and the host, characterized by a detectable immune response to Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) antigens, yet without any clinical manifestations of active TB disease [2–4]. Approximately 5% to 10% of the LTBI population are likely to develop active TB disease during their lifetime, with a higher probability of onset within the first 2 years of infection, making it a constant source of infection [5, 6]. Globally, the prevalence of LTBI has been estimated to be around 23.0%, which corresponds to approximately 1.7 billion individuals. With prevalence rates above 20%, the South-East Asia, Western Pacific, and Africa regions accounted for about 80% of global LTBI cases. Meanwhile, the Eastern Mediterranean, Europe, and Americas regions had lower rates, below 17% [7]. Additionally, China has the highest global burden of LTBI, with approximately 350 million individuals at risk of developing active TB [7, 8]. In China, a nationwide estimation utilizing the B-SHADE model and data from Interferon-Gamma Release Assays (IGRAs) revealed that the prevalence of LTBI was 20.3% among those aged 15 years and older [9]. WHO has proposed to achieve the goal of the End TB Strategy by 2035, and advocates for active screening and preventive treatment of TB infection in high-risk populations to effectively reduce the incidence of TB [10].

The diagnosis of LTBI is pivotal for the implementation of preventive therapy, which can significantly reduce the risk of progression to active TB disease. The methods for diagnosing LTBI, such as the tuberculin skin test (TST) and interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs), have limitations in terms of specificity, sensitivity and cost-effectiveness, particularly in populations with high rates of BCG vaccination and non-tuberculous mycobacterial infections [2, 11–13]. The TST has a reported sensitivity of approximately 75%−90%, but lower specificity, which often leads to false-positive results in individuals vaccinated with BCG or exposed to non-tuberculous mycobacteria. IGRAs are blood tests that use TB-specific antigens, achieving a specificity of 98%−100%. They are less impacted by BCG vaccination but are expensive and require advanced labs [14]. The positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) of the TST are 95% and 86%, respectively, while those of IGRAs are 98% and 84%, respectively [15]. The emergence of new diagnostic tools, such as the creation tuberculin skin test (C-TST), offers improved specificity and sensitivity while maintaining affordability, potentially enhancing the accuracy of LTBI detection [14, 16, 17]. This dual advantage of high diagnostic precision and low implementation cost positions C-TST as a scalable solution for TB screening in low-and middle-income countries.

Healthcare workers (HCWs) are at an increased risk of LTBI and active TB due to their occupational exposure to tuberculosis disease [18, 19]. The risk of TB among HCWs is estimated to be two to five times higher than that of the general population [19, 20]. An updated systematic review shown that the mean prevalence of LTBI among HCWs is 49% in low-and middle-income countries [18]. Previous studies revealed that the epidemiology of LTBI in HCWs varies by region, influenced by factors such as the prevalence of TB in the community, occupational exposure, the quality of healthcare infrastructure, and the implementation of infection control measures [21]. In high TB-burden countries, HCWs often work in settings with limited resources and suboptimal infection control practices, leading to increased risk of TB infection. Moreover, the high turnover and job mobility among HCWs in these regions contribute to the spread of TB both within and outside healthcare facilities [22]. WHO has identified medical personnel as a high-risk group for TB screening, whereas China has not yet established a systematic monitoring and risk assessment system for TB infection in healthcare institutions [23, 24].

Despite the substantial proportion of individuals latently infected with TB in China, a significant gap remains in understanding the true prevalence of LTBI, particularly in high-risk groups like HCWs and in areas with varying TB prevalence. Without accurate prevalence data, it is challenging to identify high-risk populations and implement timely preventive therapy in different TB-endemic regions, leading to an increased risk of active TB cases. Additionally, the lack of information on LTBI prevalence among HCWs can result in inadequate infection control measures, leading to the potential spread of TB within healthcare facilities and to vulnerable patients. Comprehensive studies addressing these needs are still lacking across regions with differing TB burdens in China. Existing research on LTBI has predominantly focused on HCWs in tertiary public hospitals and county-level hospitals in high-incidence areas in China. Notably, epidemiological data on LTBI prevalence in primary healthcare settings, particularly in low-incidence eastern coastal regions like Shandong Province, remain scarce. Therefore, we aimed to assess LTBI prevalence among HCWs and identify risk factors in Yantai, a low-incidence TB coastal city of China. By providing accurate prevalence data and identifying risk factors, our study will support evidence-based policies and national strategies for TB control and prevention, thereby accelerating progress towards TB elimination through targeted interventions and improved infection control measures.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study with stratified cluster sampling method was conducted from October 2023 April 2024 in this study. First, we classified counties as high- or low-incidence TB areas in 2022 based on their incidence rates relative to the municipal average, using data from Tuberculosis Information Management System of China. Second, within each stratum, we selected one county-level TB-designated hospital and one primary healthcare institution as clusters. Third, all eligible HCWs employed at the selected healthcare institutions were invited to participate in the study. The inclusion criteria were: HCWs who have worked in a medical institution for more than 6 consecutive months; HCWs who were not currently in the active tuberculosis disease. The exclusion criteria were: HCWs with active TB disease; HCWs who are pregnant or lactating; HCWs who refused to participate in this study. Figure 1.

Flowchart of selection procedures. Yantai City’s counties were divided into high- and low- TB incidence areas based on 2022 data. One CTD and one PHC were selected from each stratum. Eligible workers were invited to participate. Note: CTD = county-level TB designed hospital; PHC = primary healthcare center

Yantai City covers a land area of 13,930 km2 and had a gross domestic product of 1,078.28 billion RMB in 2024. The city comprises 11 counties, each of which has one county-level TB-designated hospital that serves as the central facility for TB diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up. These hospitals must be officially designated by the local health authorities as the primary institutions for TB diagnosis and treatment in their respective counties. Each county has 6 to 18 primary healthcare centers (PHCs). PHCs should be the main providers of basic healthcare services, including TB screening and management, within their communities. The following four institutions were involved in our survey: (1) County-level TB-designated hospitals: Laizhou Chronic Disease Prevention and Treatment Hospital and Muping TB Prevention and Treatment Hospital; (2) Primary healthcare centers: Daxindian Town Central Health Center and Chenggang Road Community Health Service Center.

The sample size was determined using an anticipated LTBI prevalence of 28% [25], with a margin of error set at ± 5% and a type I error rate (α) of 0.05. Thus, a sample size of 310 was calculated. Considering a potential 10% non-participation rate among HCWs, it was determined that about 340 HCWs should be invited to participate in the survey.

Data collection and testing measures

Infection detection and questionnaire survey were carried out for all eligible HCWs from four selected healthcare institutions. Each informed-consent HCWs was recruited by their departmental supervisor and invited to complete a standardized questionnaire autonomously using the Questionnaire Star online survey platform. The survey questionnaire comprises two main components: (1) Self-Designed Section: This section was designed based on an extensive review of relevant literature. It captured sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., gender, age, height, weight, educational level, marital status, et al.), health-related lifestyle behaviors, practice category, history of TB, and household contact with TB. (2) Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS): The SDS is a widely recognized, brief self-report questionnaire developed by psychiatrist Dr. William W.K. Zung in 1965 [26, 27]. It consists of 20 items designed to assess the presence and severity of depressive symptoms over the past week. The SDS is particularly useful for screening depression in various settings, including clinical practice, research studies, and public health surveys, facilitating large-scale mental health assessments while providing clinically meaningful insights [28–31]. Prior to the formal investigation, a preliminary survey was conducted in HCWs in Yantai City. This preliminary survey aimed to assess the clarity, feasibility, and appropriateness of the questionnaires. The questionnaire has been validated and demonstrated good reliability and validity. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.855, indicating good internal consistency of the scale. The KMO value of validity analysis was 0.894, and the Bartlett’s spherical test was significant (p < 0.001), so the scale was suitable for factor analysis. The full questionnaire is available as online supplementary file.

The C-TST was used for the infection detection among the respondents. The test reagent was Recombinant Mycobacterium Tuberculosis Fusion Protein (1.0 ug/0.1 ml, product batch no.: 202308039) produced by Zhifei Longcom Biologic Pharmacy Co. Ltd., China. The test was performed by an experienced nurse using the Mantoux technique, and participants viewed the results 72 h later after receiving the test, which was confirmed by two clinicians. The size of the horizontal and longitudinal diameters of the skin reactions were measured using a standardized ruler, and the average diameter was calculated. The C-TST results were considered positive when the mean diameter of the nodules or erythema was more than 5 mm [14, 32]. Previous studies have demonstrated that an induration or erythema diameter of not less than 5 mm can be used as a diagnostic indicator for detecting TB infection, even in individuals who have received the BCG vaccine [13, 33]. In this study, LTBI was defined by a C-TST induration ≥ 5 mm as the cut-off point for positivity. For participants with a positive C-TST result, we conducted a clinical evaluation, collected at least two sputum samples to test for acid-fast bacilli (AFB), and performed a chest X-ray (CXR) to rule out active disease.

Quality control measures

A trained investigative team from the city’s CDC conducted surveys at selected hospitals. The questionnaires were pre-surveyed and adjusted among HCWs who were not included in the actual study. Multiple quality control measures were adopted in questionnaire surveys: (1) automated validation rules (e.g., forced-choice questions, logic jumps, the maximum and minimum values for height and weight) were embedded to minimize missing data and illogical responses, (2) post-collection data scrubbing was performed to remove duplicate submissions (identified by ID card number).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using Stata, version 18.0. The descriptive statistics are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and n (%) for categorical variables. Variance inflation factor (VIF) values and Pearson’s correlation coefficients were measured to detect the degree of multicollinearity among the independent variables. VIF value of more than 5 or correlation coefficient greater than 0.7 indicated the presence of significant multicollinearity [34]. The influencing factors were analyzed using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis. The dependent variable was defined as a binary indicator of LTBI status (1 = yes, 0 = no). Independent variables included healthcare institution level, gender, age, marital status, BMI, education, profession, working years, annual family income, BCG vaccination, history of TB, household contact with TB, diabetes, smoking, drinking, and depression status. Univariate logistic regression was applied to analyze the association between the factors and LTBI. Factors with p < 0.2 were included in the multivariate logistic regression model. A p-values of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and all tests were calculated two-tailed.

Definitions

Latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI): A state of persistent immune response to stimulation by Mycobacterium tuberculosis antigens without clinical, radiological, or bacteriological evidence of active TB. There is no diagnostic gold standard for LTBI. The vast majority of infected people have no signs or symptoms of TB but are at risk for active TB disease. In this study, LTBI was determined using a C-TST induration ≥ 5 mm as a cut-off point for C-TST positivity. If a participant tested positive C-TST result, a clinical evaluation was conducted, at least two sputum samples were collected to investigate acid-fast bacilli (AFB), and a chest X-ray (CXR) was performed to rule out active disease.

Tuberculin skin test (TST): A test used to detect TB infection by injecting 0.1 ml of tuberculin purified protein derivative (PPD) intradermally. However, false positive results occur in patients who have received a Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccination and those with non-tuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infection.

Interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs): IGRAs are blood-based in vitro tests that measure the interferon-gamma released by circulating lymphocytes in response to specific antigens of Mycobacterium tuberculosis. It requires a well-equipped laboratory and trained technicians to perform and interpret the tests.

Tuberculosis antigen-based skin tests (TBST): A new class of tests to diagnose TB infection, have been evaluated by the WHO. The TBST class is defined as skin tests that use Mtb-specific antigens (ESAT6 and CFP10) to detect infection. The following technologies were included: C-Tb (Serum Institute of India, India); C-TST (formerly known as ESAT6-CFP10 test, Anhui Zhifei Longcom, China); and Diaskintest (Generium, Russian Federation).

Chest X-ray (CXR): Primary imaging tool for detecting pulmonary abnormalities suggestive of active TB (e.g., infiltrates, cavities) in symptomatic HCWs or those with positive C-TST.

Acid-fast bacilli (AFB): A group of bacteria that have unique cell wall properties, making them resistant to certain staining techniques. This includes Mycobacterium species like M. tuberculosis and M. leprae, which can be identified by staining methods such as Ziehl-Neelsen. These bacteria are termed “acid-fast” because they retain specific dyes even after acid treatment, facilitating their identification under a microscope.

Healthcare workers (HCWs): It refers to individuals employed in the healthcare system whose work encompasses patient care, support, or administration. Any member of staff in the healthcare facility, including physicians, nurses, laboratory technicians, and other HCWs (e.g., radiology technicians, administrative staff, pharmacists, and housekeeping personnel). Physicians and nursing staff are primarily involved in both inpatient and outpatient settings. Laboratory technicians mainly work in laboratory settings. Other HCWs work in different positions according to their specific roles.

Household contact with TB: It refers to the exposure to a person with TB within the same household, indicating that the individual has lived with or had close contact with a TB patient in their home.

Prevalence and risk factors for LTBI

A total of 45 of the 342 HCWs tested positive for LTBI, with an LTBI rate of 13.16% (95% CI: 9.76%−17.21%). HCWs working in county hospitals had a higher LTBI rate compared to those working in primary healthcare settings [3.092 (95% CI:1.319–6.872)]. Compared with participants younger than 30 years, those aged 30–39 years and ≥ 50 years had significantly higher LTBI rates, with unadjusted ORs (95% CI) of 2.724 (1.038–7.152) and 3.492 (1.207–10.105), respectively. Among HCWs who had worked in different positions, physicians and laboratory technicians had a significantly higher prevalence of LTBI compared to those who had worked in other positions. HCWs who had worked for 10–14 years, 15–19 years, and ≥ 20 years had significantly higher odds of C-TST positivity compared to those who had worked for less than 5 years, with positivity rates of 17.24%, 17.07%, and 15.52%, respectively. Participants with a history of TB had a significantly higher LTBI rate compared to those without [7.000 (95% CI: 1.368–35.821)]. Table 2.

Multivariate analysis

Variables with a p-value of < 0.2 from the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis [35]. The variance inflation factors (VIF) for each variable ranged from 1.02 to 1.66. However, the Pearson’s correlation coefficient was 0.803 between age and working years indicating that there was a linear association between these two variables. Moreover, we explored three multivariate models corresponding to working years, age, and the interaction between age and working years, with AUC values of 0.762, 0.751, and 0.738, respectively. Considering the model fit and practical significance, the variable of type of hospitals, profession, working years, history of TB, and family history of TB were incorporated into the final model.

In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, the prevalence of LTBI was higher among HCWs working in county-level TB-designated hospitals compared to those in primary healthcare institutions (OR = 4.131, 95% CI: 1.687–10.118). We found that HCWs who had worked as physicians and laboratory technicians had a significantly higher prevalence of LTBI (OR = 3.132, 95% CI:1.132–8.662 and OR = 5.545, 95% CI:1.385–22.200) compared with those working in other positions. Working duration of 10–14 years (OR = 5.721, 95% CI: 1.435–22.809), 15–19 years (OR = 4.305, 95% CI: 1.028–18.032) and 20 years or above (OR = 4.047, 95% CI: 1.079–15.171) were more likely to have TB infection. Another important factor associated with TB infection was having a household member with TB (OR = 4.488, 95% CI: 1.051–19.166). The results showed that the logistic regression analysis model was statistically significant (P < 0.05). Table 3.

Discussion

Our study found an LTBI prevalence rate of 13.16% (95% CI: 9.76%−17.21%) among HCWs in Yantai City, eastern China. The LTBI rate is lower than those reported in the other high-incidence areas in China and in other high-burden countries. Studies in China have reported LTBI rates of 28.59% among HCWs in a provincial tertiary hospital using the T-SPOT.TB assay [25], 33.9% in frontline TB HCWs in southeastern China using the ELISA [36], and 16.36% in Shanghai using IGRAs [37]. Internationally, LTBI prevalence among HCWs has been reported as 40.0% in Pakistan [38], 24.2% in Saudi Arabia [39], 34.4% in Mozambique [40], 47.2% in Afghanistan [41], and 56.2% in Peru [42]. In contrast, our study found a higher LTBI prevalence compared to developed countries and low-incidence cities. LTBI prevalence among HCWs was 10.6% in Malaysia [43], 5.2% by TST, 4.9% by QFT-GIT, and 6.0% by T-SPOT in the United States [44]. These comparisons highlight the significant variation in LTBI prevalence among HCWs across different regions and settings [45]. The lower LTBI prevalence observed in Yantai likely reflects effective regional tuberculosis control programs and a reduced burden of active cases, consistent with evidence that healthcare advancements and economic development contribute to TB mitigation [46].

Our findings suggest that certain factors may be associated with a higher prevalence of LTBI among HCWs. The present study showed that the higher LTBI rate among HCWs in county-level TB-designated hospitals compared to primary institutions may be due to their direct involvement in the diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up examinations of TB, which increases exposure to active cases. In TB prevention and control system in Shandong province, PHCs serve as critical first contacts for suspected TB cases, delivering accessible community-based screening and referrals. County-level TB-designated hospitals are higher-tier facilities specializing in advanced care such as outpatient and inpatient services, emergency treatment, complex diagnostics and therapeutic procedures, primarily handling acute, severe, complex, or undiagnosed cases referred from primary healthcare institutions [47, 48]. This underscores the importance of enhanced infection control measures in county-level TB-designated hospitals. The rate of LTBI was significantly higher among clinicians and laboratory technician who were exposed to MTB than among other HCWs in other departments, which is consistent with the findings of Joshi et al. review of related studies, suggesting that occupational exposure is an important factor in increasing the risk of MTB infection [22, 49, 50]. Our data show that HCWs with longer working years have a higher prevalence of LTBI, indicating a cumulative risk associated with increased occupational exposure. The nature of the work of medical staff determines the frequency of contact with various types of patient populations. HCWs who directly provide services such as consultation, nursing care, and testing for patients are more likely to develop TB or LTBI [51, 52]. This finding underscores the importance of regular health surveillance and infection control measures for healthcare workers, particularly those who have been in service for an extended period. Additionally, HCWs who have household contacts with TB exhibit a higher prevalence of LTBI, which underscores the importance of addressing household transmission to control the disease [53, 54]. Household settings pose a high risk for TB transmission due to prolonged close contact, confined spaces, and inadequate ventilation, especially when index cases have active pulmonary TB with high bacillary loads [55, 56]. HCWs who are also household contacts of TB patients face the critical dual risks of occupational and domestic exposure. Consequently, early identification and preventive therapy for HCWs who are household contacts of TB patients can reduce the risk of progression to active TB disease [57, 58].

The study has some limitations. First, the retrospective nature of some questionnaire information may have introduced recall bias. Moreover, non-participation of some study subjects led to missing data, which may have biased our estimates and limited the generalizability of our findings. Future studies should consider using prospective designs and strategies to minimize missing data. Second, as our sample was drawn from a limited number of specific healthcare institutions within each stratum, it potentially introduces selection bias and limits the generalizability of our findings. Future studies will expand sampling across a broader range of institutions and HCWs to validate these findings and enhance generalizability. Third, the cross-sectional study design of this study has its own limitations for association analysis. Additionally, due to the limited use of C-TST in studies involving HCWs, we compared our findings with those from TST and IGRA for broader context, but these comparisons should be interpreted cautiously. The lack of a definitive gold standard for diagnosing TB infection complicates the evaluation of the diagnostic accuracy of the C-TST. Therefore, further large-scale case-control or cohort studies are needed to confirm the results.

Conclusions

The LTBI prevalence of 13.16% among HCWs in Yantai, a coastal city in eastern China, is relatively low. We found that working at a county-level TB-designated hospital, being a physician or laboratory technician, having longer experience in medical work, and having household contact with TB were associated with a higher prevalence of LTBI. These findings highlight the urgent need for stringent infection prevention protocols and active surveillance among high-risk HCWs, even within low TB incidence healthcare settings. Tuberculosis screening should be included in annual health check-ups for HCWs, especially for those at high risk, such as frontline HCWs, long-serving workers, and those who contact with TB patients in their families. They also demonstrate that identifying these high-risk subgroups offers a strong evidence-based rationale for implementing targeted public health interventions in low-incidence settings in China. Furthermore, future research should focus on longitudinal studies to understand the dynamics of LTBI acquisition and progression among HCWs, as well as the effectiveness of various intervention strategies in reducing LTBI prevalence.
