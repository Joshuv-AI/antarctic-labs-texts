Methionine concentration regulates LSD1 acetylation in glioma cells
Authors: Jie Chang, Lude Wang, Yi Pan, Qiuwen Lou, Wenxia Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Glioma is a highly aggressive brain cancer with poor prognosis, characterized by vigorous methionine metabolism. While methionine restriction demonstrates broad anticancer effects, its mechanistic relationship with epigenetic regulators in glioma remains inadequately understood. This study reveals the molecular mechanism by which methionine restriction (0.1 mM) modulates the epigenetic regulator LSD1. Restricting methionine to 0.1 mM induced site-specific acetylation at LSD1 lysine residues K6 and K359, promoting its ubiquitin-proteasome-dependent degradation independently of transcriptional alterations. Multi-omics analysis revealed that LSD1 inhibition induced dual reprogramming: transcriptomic activation of the p38MAPK and ubiquitin-proteasome pathways, coupled with metabolomic suppression of the TCA cycle with accumulation of L-proline and other amino acid metabolites. The HDAC inhibitor TSA synergized with methionine restriction to enhance LSD1 acetylation and degradation, whereas K6R/K359R mutations stabilized LSD1, confirming the role of acetylation in proteasomal targeting. Functional validation via colony formation identified LSD1 as a key mediator of methionine restriction; LSD1 knockdown mimicked the growth inhibition of methionine starvation, while its overexpression partially restored proliferation. Collectively, these results establish the methionine-LSD1 axis as a crucial nutrient-sensing mechanism that drives glioma adaptation via epigenetic-metabolic crosstalk. This highlights potential combinatorial therapeutic strategies involving dietary methionine limitation and LSD1 acetylation-targeted therapy to disrupt tumor survival pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00726-026-03527-z.

Introduction

Glioma is characterized by aggressive proliferation, pseudopalisading necrosis, abnormal microvascular proliferation, and infiltrative growth patterns, which collectively hinder complete surgical resection(Aaron et al. 2020). Despite the implementation of multimodal therapies including maximal safe resection and radiation combined with concurrent temozolomide (TMZ), the median survival remains dismal at 12–15 months due to inevitable recurrence(Gábor et al. 2023; Jigisha et al. 2014). The metabolic adaptability of glioma cells contributes to therapeutic resistance, underscoring the urgent need to elucidate the nutrient-sensing mechanisms that facilitate tumor adaptation.

Methionine, an essential amino acid, is obtained exclusively from the diet, and serum methionine concentrations fluctuate with dietary intake(Joni and Vincent 2022). Previous research has investigated the association between diet factors and glioma risk, indicating that adherence to a healthy diet may lower the risk of developing glioma(Fatemeh et al. 2024). The dependence of glioma on exogenous methionine represents a key metabolic distinction between normal and tumor cells and offers new therapeutic insights(Lin et al. 2025; Mark and Lawrence 2022). A study demonstrated that oral recombinant methioninase combined with a low-methionine diet was highly effective in a patient with high-grade glioma undergoing radiation and TMZ chemotherapy(Motokazu et al. 2024). Methionine deprivation has been shown to induce epigenetic modifications that promote the growth and proliferation of cancer cells(Pavan et al. 2023; Serena et al. 2023). Given the high dependency on methionine of gliomas, [11 C]-methionine positron emission tomography (MET-PET) is widely used in clinical settings for glioma imaging(Asim et al. 2021; Edgardo de Jesús et al. 2025). While substantial evidence supports the role of methionine in glioma progression, the underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation.

Histone methylation, acetylation, and various other histone modifications are crucial in glioma biology(Jingyuan and Shuai 2025; Xiuhong et al. 2021). LSD1 is overexpressed in various human malignancies, including glioma(Jie et al. 2022; Lei et al. 2016), and is implicated in tumorigenesis(Annamaria et al. 2023; Mao and Shi 2023), positioning it as a promising therapeutic target(Keiko et al. 2024). Recent findings indicate that LSD1 activity is modulated by nutrient availability, energy status, and hormonal signals, suggesting that it functions as a novel sensor in the nutritional regulation of metabolic homeostasis(Dan et al. 2022; Pallavi et al. 2024). Our prior study demonstrated that the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of LSD1 is influenced by low methionine levels(Jie et al. 2022). However, the precise mechanisms through which methionine concentration regulates LSD1 ubiquitination in glioma remain largely undefined.

The objective of this research was to examine the influence of methionine concentration on the regulation of LSD1 expression and to elucidate the mechanisms underlying LSD1 acetylation in glioma. We investigated transcriptional and metabolic pathways in glioma cancer cells following treatment with LSD1-specific inhibitors, utilizing high-throughput methodologies such as transcriptomics and metabolomics. Furthermore, LC-MS analysis of post-translational modifications revealed alterations in the acetylation status of LSD1 following methionine deprivation, including modification at the K6 site and the identification of a novel acetylation site at K359. Our findings elucidate the upstream regulatory mechanisms and downstream effects of LSD1 in response to methionine deficiency, providing a multi-omics perspective on amino acid metabolism in tumor cells.

Materials and methods

Reagents and inhibitors

RPMI-1640 medium powder (31800-105) and fetal bovine serum (FBS, 11011 − 8611) were purchased from Gibco (Invitrogen, United States). Powdered RPMI-1640 medium without amino acids (R8999-04 A) was acquired from United States Biological (United States). GSK-LSD1 (final concentration 50 µM), TSA (concentrations of 0.4, 4, and 40 µM), and Cycloheximide (CHX, final concentration 25 µM) were obtained from MCE (Shanghai, China).

Cell culture

Human glioma cell lines (U251 and U87) and HEK293T cells were purchased from the Shanghai Institute of Cell Research, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin, and utilized to establish cells in the complete medium (CM) group. Cell lysates were collected at the indicated time points following methionine starvation. All cell cultures were maintained in a humidified CO2 incubator at 37 °C.

RNA-sequencing analysis

Total RNA was extracted from U251 cells and U251 cells treated with the LSD1 inhibitor GSK-LSD1 (MCE, Shanghai, China) using Trizol. Three independent biological replicates were analyzed. The methodologies for RNA-sequencing (RNA-Seq) have been detailed in our previous publications(Jie et al. 2022; Lude et al. 2021).

Metabolomic analysis

Metabolomic analysis of glioma cells involved the culture of cells, preparation of samples, extraction of quality control samples, data processing, biomarker identification, and metabolic pathway analysis as previously documented(Jie et al. 2022; Lude et al. 2021). The methodology for data preprocessing and metabolite identification has been detailed in our earlier publication(Lude et al. 2021). In summary, raw HPLC-MS data were extracted as matrix data and imported into SIMCA14.1 for multivariate statistical analysis. Orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was conducted to elucidate the metabolic differences between the U251-Con group and the U251-GSK-LSD1 group. Identified differential metabolites were required to satisfy three criteria simultaneously: (1) VIP > 1, (2) p|corr| value > 0.58, (3) the confidence interval crossing zero in the jack-knifed loading plot. Potential biomarkers were identified using MS2 in conjunction with the LIPIDMAPS and HMDB databases. Pathway analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (http://www.MetaboAnalyst.ca/).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells using Trizol reagent (Carlsbad, CA, United States) and cDNA was synthesized by Quantscript RT kit (Takara, Osaka, Japan). qRT-PCR was performed with SYBR green dye using Roche LightCycler 480Ⅱ qPCR system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Relative fold changes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with the β-actin gene as a standard, the results were normalized to the experimental control. The primer sequences are as follows:

LSD1: Forward: 5’-TTTGGAAGCCAGGGATCGTG-3’

Reverse: 5’-GACAGTGTCAGCTTGTCCGT-3’

β-actin: Forward: 5’-ACTCTTCCAGCCTTCCTTCC-3’

Reverse: 5’-CGTCATACTCCTGCTTGCTG-3’

LC-MS analysis of post-translational modification

Sample preparation of HEK293T cells for post-translational modification analysis

Primary methods for post-translational modification (PTM) analysis were established as previously reported (Zhihui et al. 2023). Briefly, SDT buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM DTT, 150 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0) was added to HEK293T cells transfected with the Flag-LSD1 plasmid (LSD1 cDNA ORF Clone, Sino Biological, China). The lysate was sonicated and then boiled for 15 min. After centrifugation at 14,000 g for 40 min, the supernatant was quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). A total of 20 µg of protein from each sample was combined with 5× loading buffer and heated in a metal bath for 5 min. The denatured proteins were then individually loaded onto 12% SDS-PAGE gels and electrophoresed at a constant current of 14 mA for 90 min to achieve protein separation. Proteins were isolated and purified within the gel to obtain purified LSD1 protein. Protein bands were visualized using Coomassie Blue R-250 staining. The gel containing LSD1 protein (~ 100 kDa) was subsequently dissected for digestion.

In gel digestion of proteins

HEK293T cells (1 × 108) were transfected with Flag-tagged human LSD1 under CM or MSM conditions. Flag-tagged LSD1 was immunoprecipitated and desalted. The gel containing LSD1 protein was excised from the SDS-PAGE. Gel pieces were treated with 30% ACN (100 mM NH4HCO3) until destained. The destained gels were then dried using a vacuum centrifuge. The protein was reduced with dithiothreitol (10 mM DTT, 100 mM NH4HCO3) for 30 min at 56 °C. Alkylation was performed with iodoacetamide (200 mM IAA, 100 mM NH4HCO3) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Following rinsing with 100 mM NH4HCO3 and ACN, the gel was digested overnight with trypsin (12.5 ng/µL, 100 mM NH4HCO3). Peptides were extracted using 60% ACN (0.1% TFA) in triplicate. The extracts were combined and completely dried in a vacuum centrifuge.

HPLC-MS for post-translational modification

Each fraction underwent nano LC-MS/MS analysis. Samples were evaluated by Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd., utilizing a methodology established in a prior study(Shang-Zhi et al. 2019).

Data analysis for post-translational modification

The mass spectrometry data were processed using MaxQuant software version 1.5.3.17 (Max Planck Institute of Biochemistry, Martinsried, Germany). The MS data were queried against the UniProtKB Escherichia coli database (2,585,998 total entries, downloaded 06/07/12). The precursor mass search was conducted within a tolerance of 6 ppm. The search allowed for a maximum of two missed cleavage sites, adhering to the enzymatic cleavage rules for trypsin/P, with a mass error tolerance of 20 ppm for fragment ions. The enzyme was set to trypsin, with a maximum of two missed cleavages. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was specified as a fixed modification, and the decoy database pattern was set to reverse. The global false discovery rate (FDR) threshold for peptide and protein identification was established at 0.01.

Immunoblotting analysis

Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 1% PMSF (phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride), and protein concentrations were normalized using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Samples were boiled with SDS loading buffer and separated via SDS-PAGE. Proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane (BioRad, Hercules, CA, United States) and probed for LSD1 (1:1000, AF5216, Beyotime), ubiquitin (1:1000, ab134953, Abcam), acetylated-lysine (1:1000, ab80178, Abcam), H3K27Ace (1:1000, 8173, CST), H3 (1:1000, AF0009, Beyotime), Flag (1:1000, 20543-1-AP, Proteintech), and β-actin (1:1000, 23660-1-AP, Proteintech). Immunoblotting was performed using the ECL kit (Fdbio, Zhejiang, China) and visualized on the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc XRS system.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

Cells were lysed using an immunoprecipitation lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with a 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Co-IP assays were conducted utilizing a Pierce Direct Magnetic IP/Co-IP kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, United States) and were subsequently processed for immunoblotting analysis as described above.

Plasmid constructs and transfection

The Flag LSD1-WT, Flag LSD1-K6R, and Flag LSD1-K359R plasmids were obtained from Sino Biological (Beijing, China), where K6R and K359R denote the substitution of Lysine residues at positions 6 and 359 with Arginine. All plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells employing Lipofectamine 2000 for experimental purposes. All experiments were conducted two days post-transfection. RNA interference and gene transfection were validated by immunoblotting and/or quantitative PCR analysis.

Colony formation assay

To investigate the role of LSD1 in methionine restriction (MSM)-mediated growth inhibition, colony formation assays were performed in human glioma U251 and U87 cells. Cells were cultured under the following conditions: control medium (CM), methionine-starved medium (MSM), CM combined with LSD1 knockdown (si-LSD1), and MSM combined with LSD1 knockdown. For LSD1 knockdown, cells were transfected with small interfering RNA targeting LSD1 (si-LSD1, Ribobio, China) or negative control siRNA (si-con) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For overexpression experiments, U251 cells were transfected with Flag-tagged LSD1 plasmid (Flag-LSD1) or empty vector (Flag-con) using the same transfection reagent. At 24 h post-transfection, cells were trypsinized, counted, and seeded into six-well plates at a density of 500 cells per well. Cells were cultured for 10–14 days, with the corresponding medium refreshed every three days. After visible colonies formed, the culture medium was removed, and cells were gently washed twice with PBS. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, followed by staining with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 20 min. After staining, plates were rinsed with distilled water and air-dried. Colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted manually under Image J. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are presented as mean±standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Statistical analysis

For RT-PCR assays, an unpaired t-test was analyzed. The results for western blotting assays were representative of at least three biological independent experiments. All statistical analyses and visualization were performed by GraphPad (Prism 9) or R (version 3.6.1). Data were reported as the mean ± SD and P values are indicated as follows: ns (P > 0.05), *(0.01 < P < 0.05), **(P < 0.01). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

LSD1 expression exhibits a positive correlation with methionine concentration

To systematically identify amino acids that modulate LSD1 expression, we examined LSD1 protein levels under conditions of deprivation of individual amino acids. Notably, deprivation of lysine or methionine resulted in the most significant reduction in LSD1 protein levels compared with other amino acid restrictions (Fig. 1A). Quantitative analysis indicated a greater than 50% decrease in LSD1 abundance after 24 h of lysine or methionine deprivation (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, complete deprivation of all 20 amino acids similarly diminished LSD1 levels, which were specifically restored by methionine supplementation (Fig. 1C). The methionine-dependent regulation displayed a dose-dependent response, with LSD1 protein levels progressively increasing across methionine concentrations ranging from 0 to 0.4 mM (Fig. 1D). Temporal analysis revealed a gradual depletion of LSD1 during extended methionine restriction (up to 5 days) in both U251 and U87 cell lines (Fig. 1E). Importantly, methionine deprivation did not affect LSD1 mRNA levels (Fig. 1F), indicating that the reduction in LSD1 protein occurs via translational or post-translational mechanisms.

Methionine starvation induces the acetylation of LSD1

After confirming that methionine starvation primarily downregulates LSD1 expression through translational mechanisms (Fig. 1), we proceeded to examine the post-translational modifications (PTMs) of LSD1 under methionine restriction. Utilizing mass spectrometry analysis of Flag-LSD1 immunoprecipitated from HEK293T cells, we identified two evolutionarily conserved acetylation sites: K6 and K359 (Fig. 2A-D). Notably, methionine starvation (-Met) led to significant alterations in acetylation patterns: the acetylation site at K6 persisted under both complete medium and methionine starvation conditions, while methionine starvation resulted in the emergence of a new acetylation site at K359 (Fig. 2B-C). This site-specific acetylation dynamic indicates a potential functional shift of LSD1 in response to methionine levels. To further elucidate this relationship, we treated U251 cells with Trichostatin A (TSA), a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor, across varying methionine concentrations. Notably, TSA treatment further intensified the methionine starvation-induced decrease in LSD1 protein levels in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2E). Collectively, these results imply that acetylation-mediated regulation may play a crucial role in governing LSD1 stability under conditions of methionine starvation.

Methionine starvation induces LSD1 acetylation at residues K6 and K359 to promote LSD1 ubiquitination and degradation

Building on our prior findings that proteasome inhibitors mitigate LSD1 degradation while autophagy inhibitors have no impact(Jie et al. 2022), we explored the dynamics of ubiquitination under conditions of methionine restriction. Co-IP assays indicated that methionine starvation (-Met) works in concert with the HDAC inhibitor TSA to amplify LSD1 acetylation and ubiquitin conjugation (Fig. 3A-B). To establish a causal link between site-specific acetylation and protein stability, we created lysine-to-arginine mutants (K6R/K359R) that mimic deacetylated forms. CHX assays revealed significantly extended half-lives for both mutants compared to wild-type LSD1 during methionine deprivation (Fig. 3C-D). This epistatic interaction indicates that acetylation at K6 and K359 functions as a molecular switch that facilitates ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal degradation. These results elucidate a methionine-sensing mechanism wherein nutrient stress induces site-specific acetylation (K6/K359), thereby enabling LSD1 for ubiquitination-mediated turnover in glioma cells, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting this acetylation-degradation pathway.

Transcriptomic profiling reveals LSD1 inhibition-mediated regulatory networks

LSD1 is aberrantly overexpressed in numerous cancers, including gliomas, with its heightened expression linked to unfavorable clinical outcomes(Salvador et al. 2023). The therapeutic potential of targeting this epigenetic regulator has gained momentum, with GSK-LSD1 emerging as a highly effective inhibitor that exhibits enhanced target specificity through covalent interaction with the FAD cofactor(Wang et al. 2021). To mechanistically explore LSD1-dependent transcriptional networks, we performed RNA sequencing on U251 cells post-pharmacological LSD1 inhibition. Our comparative transcriptomic analysis revealed 1097 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with statistical significance (|fold change|>1, FDR < 0.05, Student’s t-test), comprising 296 upregulated and 801 downregulated genes (Figs. 4A). Functional annotation revealed distinct pathway alterations: KEGG enrichment analysis underscored significant modulation of the GABAergic synapse pathway, synaptic vesicle cycle and cell adhesion molecules (Fig. 4B). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further highlighted a pronounced upregulation of the p38MAPK signaling pathway, the ubiquitin-proteasome system (including ubiquitin-protein transferase activity and ubiquitin binding), and cholesterol metabolism. Conversely, pathways related to GABA-ergic synapse and G protein-coupled receptor activity were significantly suppressed (Fig. 4C). Collectively, these multi-faceted findings indicate that LSD1 inhibition triggers extensive transcriptomic reprogramming in glioma cells, particularly influencing pathways linked to proliferative signaling (p38MAPK), cellular homeostasis (ubiquitin-proteasome system), and metabolic adaptation (cholesterol metabolism). Moreover, in order to avoid the off-target effects of LSD1 inhibitors, we used siRNA to specifically inhibit LSD1 (Fig. 4D). Then, we conducted pathway enrichment analysis on all differentially expressed genes (P < 0.05 and FDR < 0.05) in U251-GSK-LSD1 cells compared to the control group. Figure 4E showed similar pathway changes as in Fig. 4B, the KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that the expressions of glutamatergic synapse, cell adhesion molecules, cholesterol metabolism and GABAergic synaptic pathways had significant changes.

Metabolic analysis of LSD1 inhibition in U251 glioma cells

To elucidate the metabolic ramifications of LSD1 inhibition, we conducted a comparative metabolomics study. OPLS-DA effectively differentiated the two cell populations, revealing distinct metabolic signatures (Fig. 5A). Pharmacological inhibition of LSD1 resulted in significant alterations across various metabolic pathways, with the most notable effects observed in L-amino acid metabolism, particularly influencing downstream metabolites such as L-proline (Fig. 5A). Pathway enrichment analysis of significantly altered metabolites (P < 0.05, FDR < 0.05) indicated substantial disruptions in aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, central carbon metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, and D-amino acid metabolism (Fig. 5B). In alignment with these observations, GSEA demonstrated upregulation of aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis (Fig. 5C) and central carbon metabolism (Fig. 5D), alongside downregulation of the citrate cycle (TCA cycle) pathway (Fig. 5E). These coordinated metabolic shifts suggest that LSD1 inhibition may facilitate glioma progression by reconfiguring central carbon metabolism while inhibiting mitochondrial energy production through TCA cycle suppression. Further analysis of pathway-associated metabolites revealed L-proline and L-asparagine as the most significantly elevated metabolites in GSK-LSD1-treated cells (Fig. 5F). In contrast, critical TCA cycle intermediates—including succinic acid, phosphoenolpyruvate, fumaric acid, malic acid, oxoglutaric acid, citric acid, isocitric acid, and 2-(A-hydroxyethyl) thiamine diphosphate—were markedly diminished in U251-GSK-LSD1 cells (Fig. 5G). Importantly, the accumulation of amino acid metabolites (L-proline and L-asparagine) in conjunction with TCA cycle suppression suggests that LSD1 inhibition may drive metabolic reprogramming towards the accumulation of biosynthetic precursors, potentially supporting tumor growth under conditions of impaired oxidative phosphorylation.

LSD1 functions as a critical downstream mediator of methionine restriction in suppressing glioma cell proliferation

To investigate the role of LSD1 in MSM treatment-mediated growth inhibition in glioma cells, we performed colony formation assays in U251 and U87 cells under the following conditions: CM, MSM, CM combined with LSD1 knockdown (si-LSD1), and MSM treatment combined with LSD1 knockdown. Additionally, U251cells were transfected with Flag-con or Flag-LSD1 and subsequently cultured in either CM or MSM medium. The results showed that, compared with the CM group, methionine restriction significantly suppressed the colony formation ability of both U251 and U87 cells (P < 0.05). Knockdown of LSD1 under CM conditions also led to a marked reduction in colony formation (P < 0.01), phenocopying the effect of methionine restriction (P < 0.01) (Fig. 6A-B). Furthermore, combined treatment with methionine restriction and LSD1 knockdown exhibit a stronger inhibitory effect than either treatment alone. In contrast, overexpression of LSD1 partially rescued the MSM-induced inhibition of colony formation (P < 0.05 vs. MSM + Flag-con group) (Fig. 6C-D), indicating that restoring LSD1 levels alleviates the growth-suppressive effect of methionine starvation. These findings confirm that LSD1 is a critical downstream mediator through which methionine starvation regulates glioma cell proliferation, and provide experimental support for therapeutic strategies targeting this metabolic-epigenetic axis.

Discussion

A variety of malignancies exhibit a reliance on methionine for proliferation, with increased serum methionine levels being clinical associated with gastric (C et al. 1997) and prostate cancers(Adriana et al. 2012). This metabolic reliance stems from the inability of tumor cells to synthesize methionine via the homocysteine salvage pathway, making them susceptible to strategies aimed at methionine deprivation. Emerging data indicate that restricting methionine effectively inhibits tumor progression(Desiree et al. 2020; Peng et al. 2024), while the administration of methioninase safely reduces circulating methionine levels without significant adverse effects(Y et al. 1996). These findings underscore the therapeutic promise of targeting methionine metabolism in the treatment of gliomas.

As a pivotal epigenetic modulator, LSD1 facilitates cancer progression through various mechanisms: enhancing invasion and metastasis(Yu et al. 2024), stabilizing HIF1α through K391 demethylation to promote VEGF-mediated angiogenesis (Lee et al. 2017), and maintaining cancer stemness(Guo et al. 2024). Although LSD1 mRNA levels remain consistent across different malignancies, its activity is intricately modulated by PTMs(Kim et al. 2023; Yinrui et al. 2025). While the deacetylation of histones and transcription factors by LSD1 is well-documented, the autologous acetylation of LSD1 itself is not well understood, representing a significant knowledge gap that this study aims to address.

Our research demonstrates that LSD1 stability in U251/U87 glioma models is regulated in a methionine concentration-dependent manner. Methionine deprivation (< 0.1 mM) triggers site-specific acetylation at K6 and K359 residues, alongside increased ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. This contrasts with the stabilizing effect of K117 acetylation noted in previous studies(Suli et al. 2022), highlighting the functional dichotomy in PTM site selection. The methionine-LSD1 axis may represent a metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk mechanism that contributes to gliomagenesis. The destabilization of LSD1 induced by methionine starvation could interfere with HIF1α/VEGF signaling and epigenetic reprogramming, potentially elucidating the tumor-suppressive effects of methionine restriction.

In terms of gene function and cellular behavior, our study mechanistically links LSD1 inhibition by GSK-LSD1 to extensive transcriptomic and metabolic reprogramming, offering critical insights into its dual role as an epigenetic and metabolic regulator in glioma progression. Transcriptomic profiling revealed widespread alterations in gene expression, with pathway analyses highlighting LSD1’s influence on proliferative signaling (e.g., p38MAPK), proteostasis (e.g., ubiquitin-proteasome system), and metabolic adaptation (e.g., cholesterol metabolism). Several studies have highlighted that cancer cells experiencing metabolic stress exhibit increased p38 MAPK pathway and rely on carbon sources or lipd metabolites, other than glucose, to facilitate cellular adaptation to metabolic stress and to maintain cancer cell survival and progression(Sukumaran and Baddireddi Subhadra 2024; Xiaohong et al. 2024). Metabolomic analyses further revealed that LSD1 inhibition induces a striking metabolic shift characterized by accumulation of L-proline and L-asparagine alongside depletion of TCA cycle intermediates. L-proline, an important amino acid metabolite, can increase the production of proteins needed for cell proliferation(Di et al. 2024; James et al. 2014), suggesting that LSD1 inhibition may influence energy metabolism. This metabolic rewiring—marked by upregulated aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis and central carbon metabolism but downregulated mitochondrial energy production—implies a compensatory mechanism favoring anabolic processes over oxidative phosphorylation. Such reprogramming may enable glioma cells to sustain biosynthetic demands under metabolic stress, a hallmark of tumor adaptation. The depletion of TCA intermediates, including succinate and citrate, suggests mitochondrial dysfunction, potentially driving reliance on alternative pathways like glutaminolysis(Ryuichi et al. 2023) or other amino acid metabolism pathways(Pasquale et al. 2020) to meet energy requirements. These studies support the idea that the increased animo acid levels under metabolic stress may act as a molecular switch to reprogram transcriptional and metabolic networks to drive cancer progression. Phenotypic rescue and colony formation assays in U251 and U87 glioma cells demonstrate LSD1 as a crucial functional mediator of methionine restriction. LSD1 knockdown replicates methionine starvation’s growth inhibition, and their combination synergistically enhances colony suppression. LSD1 overexpression partially rescues proliferation, confirming axis specificity while suggesting broader methionine effects. These findings establish the methionine-LSD1 nutrient-sensing mechanism, supporting dual-targeting therapies for glioma.

Conclusion

This work elucidates a novel nutrient-sensing mechanism whereby methionine availability governs LSD1 stability through competing acetylation/ubiquitination pathways. The residue-specific effects of acetylation expand our understanding of PTM coding in cancer epigenetics, offering dual therapeutic strategies: dietary methionine modulation combined with LSD1 acetylation-site targeting. Future studies should explore tissue-specific variations and develop selective LSD1 acetyltransferase inhibitors.
