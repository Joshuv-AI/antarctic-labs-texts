Unified FPGA framework for secure satellite image transmission: adaptive orthogonal moments with integrated confusion-diffusion cryptography
Authors: Ismail Mchichou, Mohamed Amine Tahiri, Hicham Amakdouf, Mhamed Sayyouri, Eman Abdullah Aldakheel, Doaa Sami Khafaga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Satellite image transmission systems face a dual challenge: efficient compression and robust encryption. Existing approaches treat these requirements sequentially, resulting in doubled latency and energy overhead, while no FPGA implementation of orthogonal moments exists in the literature. This work presents the first unified FPGA framework transforming adaptive orthogonal moments into dual compression-encryption vectors. The system implements four moment families (Charlier, Hahn, Meixner, Krawtchouk) on Xilinx ZedBoard Zynq-7000, with real-time adaptive selection (93.8% accuracy) and confusion-diffusion chaotic encryption applied directly to coefficients. Three major contributions are demonstrated: (i) first FPGA hardware architecture for orthogonal moments via hybrid PC/FPGA strategy, (ii) adaptive multi-family framework optimized for satellite energy constraints, (iii) unified pipeline eliminating intermediate stages. Implemented at 125 MHz on ZedBoard, the system achieves PSNR of 27–36 dB for Sentinel-2 images of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$64 \times 64$$\end{document} pixels (4.6:1 compression), 728 ns latency, and robust security (7.99 bits entropy, correlations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 0.021$$\end{document}).

Introduction

Context

The transmission of satellite imagery faces a critical dual challenge: spatial bandwidth constraints impose efficient compression (typical ratios of 4:1 to 10:1) to make transmission viable from orbit to ground stations, while the growing geopolitical sensitivity of Earth observation data demands robust cryptographic protection against unauthorized interception and analysis.

Traditional approaches address these challenges sequentially: image compression followed by conventional encryption (AES, RSA). This separation generates major systemic inefficiencies: double processing latency, increased energy consumption, architectural complexity, and underutilization of embedded hardware resources. For satellite systems where every millisecond of latency and every watt of consumption are critical, this separate approach constitutes an operational bottleneck.

Orthogonal moments have been widely used in image processing and analysis for many years due to their ability to extract characteristic information from images in both local and global manners. They are applied with excellent results in different domains such as image reconstruction1–5, image compression6–8, edge detection9, correction of geometric distortions10, and image classification11,12. The basic idea of moments is the projection of the data space onto often orthogonal bases, forming two main families: continuous orthogonal moments (COMs) based on continuous polynomials13 such as Legendre14, Zernike15, Gegenbauer16, and Fourier-Mellin17, and discrete orthogonal moments (DOMs) based on discrete polynomials13,18 such as Tchebichef19, Krawtchouk4,20,21, Charlier4,22, Hahn23, and Meixner24.

Discrete orthogonal moments offer significant computational advantages over continuous moments by avoiding discretization issues and associated calculation errors20,21,23,25–27. Recent work has explored fractional versions of these moments to improve precision and region localization in images. X. Liu et al.28 derived new fractional Krawtchouk moments (FrKT), while other research has developed fractional moments of Legendre29, Fourier-Mellin30, and Chebyshev31. Work on Charlier moments23,32–35 demonstrates their effectiveness for image reconstruction and classification. However, despite these theoretical advances, no hardware implementation of orthogonal moments exists in the literature, and their cryptographic potential remains largely unexplored in the spatial context where bandwidth constraints (a few Mbps) and energy constraints (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$<10$$\end{document} W) are particularly critical.

Problematic

Contemporary satellite imaging systems face convergent constraints that demand a revolutionary approach. Satellite-ground links, typically limited to a few Mbps, require efficient compression techniques to transmit high-resolution images within acceptable operational timeframes, while Earth observation generates strategically sensitive data (critical infrastructure, military activity, natural resources) requiring robust cryptographic protection. These requirements must be satisfied in a hostile space environment where limited transmission windows (8–12 minutes per pass) impose real-time processing with latency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$<5$$\end{document} ms, the radiative environment can corrupt data in transit, and the constrained energy budget (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$<10$$\end{document} W for image processing on a total power of 100–500 W) dictates every architectural decision.

Existing optimization attempts remain fragmented and insufficient. Although discrete orthogonal moments offer demonstrated computational advantages and fractional moments have revealed superior reconstruction and localization capabilities7,28,30,31, no hardware architecture exploits these properties in an operational context. This technological gap reveals the absence of a unified system simultaneously meeting requirements for efficient compression, robust security, and spatial optimization in a hardware architecture exploiting adaptive orthogonal moments. Current solutions present major limitations: absence of a unified hardware architecture exploiting the dual properties of discrete orthogonal moments, lack of multi-family adaptability (Charlier, Hahn, Meixner, Krawtchouk) according to image content, and inadequate performance for satellite constraints (latency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$>3$$\end{document} ms, consumption \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$>5$$\end{document} W).

Theoretical framework

This section establishes the mathematical foundations of the four orthogonal moment families integrated into our adaptive FPGA architecture. We present the theoretical framework that enables dynamic selection among Charlier, Hahn, Meixner, and Krawtchouk moments according to local image characteristics.

The process begins with the calculation of orthogonal moments of the image, producing a compact matrix of coefficients faithfully representing the image. This matrix is then encrypted in two stages: a confusion phase to permute the coefficients and a diffusion phase to propagate modifications. The keys used for these operations are generated from a Hénon map, guaranteeing a high level of security while preserving the reconstruction capability during ground decryption.

Satellite images present a diversity of contents (urban, forest, oceanic, desert, cloudy), where each moment family demonstrates differential performance. Some images benefit optimally from the Krawtchouk family, others from Charlier, Hahn, or Meixner. This variability justifies an adaptive multi-family approach rather than a universal single solution.

Optimal selection constitutes a methodological problem: no a priori criterion exists in the literature. Traditional quality metrics (PSNR, MSE) require complete image reconstruction, creating a circular dependency incompatible with embedded satellite constraints. The developed machine learning system (detailed in Section "Development of the Adaptive Selection System") resolves this limitation via a decision tree predicting the optimal family from intrinsic characteristics, eliminating prior reconstruction.

The calculated moment coefficients constitute the basis of the combined cryptographic process (detailed in Section "Cryptographic Process Based on Coefficients"). This approach optimizes space constraints while guaranteeing secure transmission to ground stations.

Decision tree construction and optimization

The training data collected during the offline phase enables construction of the adaptive prediction system. Analysis of labeled Sentinel-2 EuroSAT images reveals distinctive patterns correlating fast image features with optimal moment families, achieving 93.8% classification accuracy on the validation set.

A decision tree classifier was selected for its two advantages: (i) interpretability via explicit decision rules easily translatable into hardware logic, and (ii) minimal computational overhead compatible with FPGA real-time constraints. The tree was trained via supervised learning using grid search on hyperparameters (criterion, depth, splitting conditions) on 24,300 training samples with 5-fold cross-validation.

The optimized tree structure, presented in Figure 4, employs a hierarchical decision cascade based on five rapidly computable image features. Cryptographic process based on coefficients

The cryptographic process directly exploits orthogonal moment coefficients to combine compression and encryption. The compact and mathematically stable structure of the coefficients eliminates the need for a separate compression stage, while facilitating the application of chaotic transformations to secure the data.

Adapted Hénon chaotic system

The encryption relies on the two-dimensional Hénon chaotic map, adapted to generate key sequences for the coefficient matrix rather than the raw image. The system produces two distinct sequences enabling confusion and diffusion of coefficients.

Adopted solution: Hybrid PC/FPGA approach

Faced with these technical limitations, we adopted a hybrid strategy separating responsibilities according to each platform’s capabilities. The PC phase (offline) performs pre-computation of complete polynomial matrices \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P^{F^*}_n(x)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q^{F^*}_m(y)$$\end{document} for all orders n, m in double precision via validated mathematical libraries. The pre-computed matrices are then quantised to Q11.4 format and transferred to ZedBoard BRAM, occupying a minor fraction of available memory. All four polynomial matrices (Charlier, Hahn, Meixner, Krawtchouk) are transferred simultaneously to the FPGA BRAM during system initialisation and remain permanently stored, enabling instantaneous selective activation of the optimal family without loading latency during runtime. This quantisation introduces negligible residual error that does not significantly affect image reconstruction quality. The main advantage of this approach is guaranteed exact correspondence with PC reference values, eliminating any divergence observed during direct FPGA computation attempts. The FPGA phase (real-time) then exploits pre-stored polynomials to compute moments in real time with deterministic and reproducible precision, microsecond latency, and simplified validation through direct verification of stored values.

Online phase (FPGA): The FPGA exploits pre-stored polynomials to perform real-time moment computation via a selective activation pipeline. Only the optimal family determined by the decision tree is activated, avoiding redundant calculations of the three other families. This organisation guarantees deterministic precision in fixed-point arithmetic (Q11.4 format), maximum parallelism via dedicated DSP48E1 blocks.

Pipeline control and optimization

The state machine coordinates the execution of the optimized pipeline according to five main states: IDLE (awaiting input image), FEATURE_EXTRACTION (decision tree analysis), SELECTIVE_COMPUTATION (computation of selected family only), ENCRYPTING (encryption of selected coefficients), and DONE_MAIN (data ready for transmission).

The selective activation logic guarantees that only one module is active per cycle through conditional start signals. Each moment module receives its start signal only when both the global selective start is asserted and its corresponding enable signal is active. For example, the Charlier module starts only when both start_selective and enable_charlier are high, and similarly for the other families. The active module is detected by monitoring the completion signals of all enabled modules through a priority-based selection mechanism. This logic optimizes energy efficiency by ensuring that only the selected family performs computations while the others remain idle. The controller communicates with each module through a handshake protocol using start signals to initiate operations and done signals to confirm completion. Each module implements its own internal state machine that responds to the global controller’s commands, ensuring deterministic pipeline sequencing.

The architecture uses a Q11.4 fixed-point numerical format (12-bits total) with 11 bits signed integer part and 4 bits fractional part, offering a range from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-1024.9375$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+1023.9375$$\end{document} with a resolution of 0.0625. Accumulators are extended to 20-bits to prevent overflows, with strict control of variable widths and a partitioned pipeline for complex multiplications.

Results and validation

This section presents the experimental results obtained during the evaluation of the proposed unified framework. Adaptive selection system for orthogonal moments

Training and validation of the decision tree were performed on the Kaggle platform using the EuroSAT dataset, a reference database for satellite image classification. This dataset comprises over 27,000 RGB images at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$64 \times 64$$\end{document} pixel resolution from the Sentinel-2A satellite, distributed across 10 land cover classes: forest, crops, grassland, residential areas, industrial areas, rivers, lakes, highways, permanent pastures, and annual croplands. For our experimentation, these RGB images were converted to greyscale.

The developed adaptive selection system demonstrates a remarkable accuracy of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$93.8\%$$\end{document} on a validation subset comprising 2,700 EuroSAT images (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10\%$$\end{document} of the total dataset) representing equitably the 10 land cover classes. Analysis of classification errors reveals that the main confusions occur between the Hahn and Meixner families (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5.4\%$$\end{document} of cases), particularly for transition images between similar land cover classes (e.g., dense forests vs textured grasslands, or residential vs industrial areas). This limitation remains acceptable given the complexity of the a priori selection problem, which is non-existent in the current literature for orthogonal moments.

The principle of adaptive selection consists of computing moments of the four orthogonal families (Charlier, Hahn, Meixner, Krawtchouk) for each image, then automatically selecting the family offering the best PSNR value for that specific image. Temporal architecture of chaotic encryption:

The 4-cycle latency for chaotic encryption corresponds exclusively to the real-time encryption phase, whilst the 1,000 transient iterations of the Hénon map form part of an offline initialisation phase. The 1,000 transient iterations are executed only once during satellite system start-up or when changing the cryptographic key KEY1. This phase generates stabilised chaotic sequences that are stored in dedicated BRAM. For each transmitted image, encryption exploits pre-generated sequences stored in BRAM via a 4-cycle pipeline: reading the confusion sequence from BRAM (cycle 1), applying chaotic permutation (cycle 2), reading the diffusion sequence (cycle 3), and applying recursive XOR mask (cycle 4). Initialisation iterations are amortised over thousands of images transmitted during the satellite mission, rendering the initialisation overhead negligible compared to total processing time. Chaotic sequences are regenerated only when changing KEY1, periodically for security reasons, ensuring that temporal overhead remains imperceptible throughout the mission. This strategy of pre-generation and reuse of chaotic sequences follows principles similar to cryptographic pseudo-random number generators (PRNG), where expensive initialisation is performed once whilst generated values are rapidly consumed during operation.

Adaptive selection latency (3 cycles) represents a small fraction of total satellite-side latency, validating the efficiency of the proposed approach. Processing throughput demonstrates the system’s capability to process satellite image streams in real time.

Clarification on 666 MHz frequencies:

References45 and46 both mention a frequency of 666 MHz on Zybo Z20 platform (Zynq-7020). This frequency corresponds to the Zynq ARM Cortex-A9 processor, not to the FPGA logic which typically operates between 50 and 200 MHz. For46, the article explicitly states “the Zynq processor is clocked at the frequency CPUFreq = 666.67 MHz”, confirming that this refers to the ARM processor. Encryption requires tens of millions of cycles per image, suggesting sequential software implementation rather than FPGA hardware acceleration. In comparison, our system exploits pure FPGA logic at 125 MHz with an optimised hardware pipeline, without ARM processor intervention for compression and encryption operations.

Discussion: limitations and perspectives

Current limitations

The proposed architecture relies on PC pre-computation of polynomial matrices with transfer to FPGA BRAM. While guaranteeing bit-exact precision and strict software compatibility, this hybrid approach presents limitations for fully autonomous space systems. Direct evaluation of orthogonal polynomials on FPGA encounters significant barriers: the Gamma function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma (x)$$\end{document} and the hypergeometric function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}_2F_1$$\end{document} have no native VHDL implementation, and fixed-point approximations (Q11.4) generate cumulative errors during polynomial recurrences, causing PSNR degradation of 3 to 5 dB. The system cannot dynamically generate new moment orders nor integrate new families without ground station intervention, acceptable for current missions with regular communication windows but limiting for future autonomous deep space exploration.

Research perspectives

Pure FPGA implementation: The long-term objective for autonomous systems includes adaptation of the CORDIC algorithm for Gamma/hypergeometric approximation (error \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 10^{-6}$$\end{document}), piecewise polynomial interpolation with coefficient storage in BRAM, and hybrid tables combining pre-computed values and dynamic computation. These approaches would enable complete autonomy while maintaining required precision.

Fractional orthogonal moments: Extension to fractional-order moments (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M \in \mathbb {R}^+$$\end{document}) enables fine control of compression ratio and superior noise resilience compared to integer orders, particularly relevant for sensor noise and atmospheric disturbances. The hybrid approach extends naturally through pre-computation of fractional bases for discrete values. Future work will investigate new families (Zernike for circular images, Legendre for rectangular regions) enabling finer geometric adaptation.

Conclusion

This study presents the first unified hardware framework combining compression through adaptive orthogonal moments and chaotic encryption for secure satellite image transmission. FPGA implementation on Xilinx Zynq-7000 demonstrates exceptional performance: adaptive selection accuracy of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$93.8\%$$\end{document}, end-to-end latency of 728 ns, and robust cryptographic security (entropy 7.99 bits, spatial correlations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 0.021$$\end{document}).

The three major contributions—first FPGA architecture for multi-family orthogonal moments, real-time adaptive selection system via decision tree, and exhaustive validation on Sentinel-2 data—establish a new benchmark for embedded space systems where energy efficiency, real-time constraints, and data security converge toward specialized hardware solutions.

The adopted hybrid PC/FPGA strategy guarantees bit-exact precision while opening perspectives toward future pure FPGA implementations via optimized hardware approximations. The identified extensions—multispectral imaging, radiation hardening, robustness to noisy channels—position this work as a foundation for future generations of secure Earth observation systems (Sentinel-Next, Copernicus-2030) requiring autonomous high-performance processing in constrained environments.
