Can rural elderly groups be supported by increasing the pension contribution rate?
Authors: Haibo Lin, Yihan Xu, Yueming Song
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
As a typical East Asian country transitioning from a production-based social insurance system to a modern free social insurance system, South Korea has set a precedent for other Asian countries. This study adopts data from 2006, 2010, and 2012 Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA) and uses a fixed effect analysis of the quasi-natural experiment to conclude that the increase in the Korean pension contribution rate has a limited negative impact on social security participation. The degree of filial piety of children, including the financial support of those children, rather than the number of children, affects the participation of the elderly in social security insurance. Thus, an alternative relationship between family pensions and social pensions presents itself. This paper discusses the mechanism that factors influence the willingness to participate in pension insurance with reduced replacement rates in an extended family of a multigenerational population. After systematically delineating the background of South Korea's welfare system, this paper compares the similarities between China and South Korea in this regard and proposes that China and other East and Southeast Asian countries can learn from South Korea's example.

Introduction

Many countries are faced with the problem of low pension substitution rates [1]. The so-called substitution rate refers to the ratio that the income of pension insurance after retirement may account for the reasonable part of the income before retirement [2]. And the relevant financial authorities are challenged by the task of increasing the contribution rate [3]. However, the existing survey data in various countries are insufficient to analyze the policy-related consequences of the quasi-natural experiments aimed at increasing the contribution rate [4]. Nevertheless, the practice of South Korea in increasing the contribution rate and appropriately reducing the originally excessive substitution rate during the financial crisis in the early 21st century serves as a useful reference [5]. To address this issue, this paper will explore the applicability of South Korea's experience to other countries. By examining South Korea's successful strategies, we aim to identify practical lessons that can be applied to improve pension systems in countries facing similar challenges.

Historically, East Asia has leveraged its productivity to build a comprehensive social security system [6,7]. However, given the insufficiency of the accumulation of social security funds in East Asian countries, it is crucially important to explore effective approaches for enhancing the participation and replacement rates of the welfare systems [8]. The pay-as-you-go system relies on the ratio of the younger generation population to the elderly population [9,10]. Once this ratio decreases, increasing the contribution rate becomes an inevitable option. South Korea's experience demonstrates how strategic policy adjustments can effectively manage this dependency ratio. Countries implementing a complete accumulation system, rather than relying on pay-as-you-go, must face the challenge of increasing contribution rates too. This makes studying the impact mechanisms of replacement and contribution rates particularly important [11]. East Asian countries or regions, such as mainland China and Taiwan, along with Eastern European countries [12,13], many African countries [14], and other underdeveloped countries, are facing similar challenges [15]. For instance, in China, the participation rate has increased rapidly in response to deeper implementations of rural insurance [16]. However, only an average of approximately 1200 yuan of pension can be received each year. The replacement rate is only five to ten percent of the income of farmers as standard laborers. In recent years, the annual average income of rural residents in China is approximately between 15,000 and 29,000 yuan [17]. Therefore, China is faced with the practical task of increasing the participation rate on the premise of raising the substitution rate of rural residents' endowment insurance to a reasonable level. This paper will explore the impact of South Korea's related experiences and policies on increasing participation rates [18]. and focus on how these countries and regions can learn from South Korea's experience to address similar challenges.

Many studies discuss the consequences of the 2007 national pension system (NPS) reform in South Korea, including macro-level measures of budget balance [19] and the impact on welfare [20]. Additionally, while many studies have discussed intergenerational support [21], we find that very few studies have discussed the mechanisms for the increase in participation rate and contribution rate in the family economy under the condition of relatively reasonable insurance returns for the insured. Hence, the first contribution is using Korean data to discuss the impact of detailed family factors on effective participation rate (rather than the nominal participation rate in the pattern of very low contributions and very low substitution rates like rural social security in China). This analysis will help better understand the role of family factors in increasing participation and contribution rates.

The second contribution of this article is a comprehensive analysis of the similarities between South Korea and China. This study will first examine the participation of South Korea in the increase of pension contributions after the financial crisis. Then, taking China as an example, the study will illustrate the comparability of the old-age security system with the cultural and economic background of both countries. By analyzing the similarities and differences, we aim to highlight specific policy elements that can be adapted by China and other nations to improve their pension systems. Through this method (there are many studies on the comparison of welfare systems in Asian countries, but some of articles are too broad [22,23] or depth of family history and culture [24,25], we gain a better balance between depth and width of comparison), we have strengthened the persuasiveness of international comparison in the field of endowment insurance. Therefore, we can provide some supporting evidence for other countries in like need.

After discussing to the limitations of the findings in this article, South Korea's experience in increasing the payment rate can still be used for reference by countries with similar backgrounds and needs, including China. Careful discussion on the feasibility (limitation) of drawing lessons from international experience as third contribution.

The structure of this paper is arranged as follows: The first part introduces the research background and problem; the second part conducts a literature review, summarizing the theory of productive welfare insurance systems in East Asia, the importance of family support in elderly care, and the comparability between China and South Korea on this issue, and presents the hypotheses, exploring whether having more children increases family dependence for the elderly, and the impact of children's filial piety and economic support on pension insurance substitution, as well as the impact of the financial crisis on participation rates under the conditions of rising contribution rates and declining replacement rates; the third part describes the research methods and data; the fourth part analyzes the empirical results; the fifth part discusses the findings, their policy implications, the limitations of the study, and future research directions; and finally, a brief conclusion. Literature review and hypothesis

Basic production theory of the welfare system and the importance of the family in East Asian countries

By applying production theory to East and Southeast Asian contexts, we aim to extend the usefulness of South Korea's experience to other countries with similar characteristics. As a generalization of the welfare development of East Asian countries after World War II, productivity is fairly well codified [26,27]. As for why the productive welfare system emerged in the postwar climate, some discussions attribute it to the family structure and cultural characteristics of East and Southeast Asian countries [28,29]. It is beyond the scope of the present study to debate whether agricultural production produced the Confucian spirit or whether the Confucian spirit strengthened the role of the family [30,31]. Either way, because the efficiency of industrialization has yet to reach a sustainable level in such cultures, the accumulation of social wealth and national finances is not enough to support generous old-age insurance benefits. Therefore, as a tradition, family support structures are worth preserving [32,33]. The family bears the responsibility of caring for the elderly, but in addition to immediate relatives, the economic and educational support of the entire clan still carry practical leverage [34]. The “clan” here indicates a tree of relationships across multiple generations and family ties. Thus, kinship continues to play an important role in the economy and one's survival within it.

In this article, we attempt to extract more economic, cultural, and political characteristics that affect the development of social insurance in Korea based on general East Asian trends. We compare these characteristics with other countries, especially China, to illustrate the applicability of South Korea's experience beyond its borders. We then analyze the impact of South Korea's policy of increasing social insurance contribution rate and reducing the expected insurance income for participants. When other countries follow suit, the changes in payment rates during and after the financial crisis should provide an empirical reference for decision-making endeavors elsewhere.

The applicability of the Korean experience to China

Homogeneity of the family and pension tradition

China, Japan, and South Korea are all influenced by Confucianism to varying degrees. As these cultures have bowed to the pressure of rapid industrialization and aging, doubts about the role of filial piety in the welfare system in East Asia have been inevitable, but in any case, it is meaningful to compare the family pension systems in each. South Korea's practice is to emphasize the role of family protection [35]. Its Confucian tradition emphasizes the family as the epicenter of financial stability. The government encourages filial piety to parents and family stability [36], prioritizing healthy couples over single parents and single-parent families, and rewarding families that respect their parents. The Chinese government is also unable to provide comprehensive social care for the elderly in rural areas in the short term [37]. Both countries hope to combine family and social pensions [38,39].

The urgency of an aging population

East Asian countries such as Singapore, South Korea, and China are dealing with the challenges of accelerated aging. South Korea is expected to become the third-highest average population in the world in 2050. It is estimated that by 2045, China's elderly population aged over 65 will account for 20 % of its total population [40,41]. Due to the widespread development of the productive welfare system, the insufficient financial resources of the welfare fund will lead to similar policy measures. The transformation of the productivity welfare system and social productivity policy constitute a major issue to cope with in the context of an aging society. Both Korea and China are designing and implementing a joint pension system, including defined-contribution (DC) and defined-benefit (DB) plans, as well as a broader form known as the “four pillars” [23]. These measures are designed to cope with the acceleration of aging under the condition of insufficient resources.

Similarities of the development stages

Japan, China, and South Korea all exhibited rapid economic growth in roughly the same period. In the 1970s, the productivity of Japan and South Korea exploded, and China's growth rate is expected to reach synchronization later. Korea has successfully broken through this middle-income trap. This is due in part to the fact that the Korean government is committed to narrowing the gap between the rich and the poor through its establishment of a solid welfare system and rural economic development [42]. The Chinese government has also realized the importance of welfare and consumption for crossing the middle-income trap. Because according to the per capita income indicator, China has entered the middle-income stage by United Nations standards, with a per capita income of 5450 US dollars. The southeastern coastal areas have even reached 10,000 US dollars. Although the per capita income in coastal areas of China is high, this does not mean that China's rural population has reached the same income level [43,44]. Therefore, improving the income levels of rural residents, especially older adults, is an important step in reducing the wealth gap.

Similarities of governmental behavior and power

Both Korea and China have strong systems of government. Their economies and government revenues are mainly supported by large enterprises (at least at this stage), and the government's intervention in large-scale enterprises is more effective than in small and medium-sized enterprises. While the Chinese government is more powerful [45], the implementation of various policies in Korea is likewise due to its concentration of large-scale enterprises. Many discussions have further mentioned the impact of democracy on social security in East Asia. For example, some political parties in Taiwan have proposed non-accumulation rural social insurance plans [46,47]. Whether such plans are sustainable or not, regardless of suspicious measures to please the electorate, their initial implementation still depends on strong and effective governmental operations [48].

Hypothesis about the Korean experience

Some research articles suggest that the Korean model should be adopted in rural areas and that the Japanese model should be adopted in urban areas. Given Korea's success in economic development and its relevance to middle-class countries, it serves as a valuable case study. Before we propose our hypotheses, it is crucial to understand the underlying dynamics of family support and pension systems in Korea. Specifically, we need to address the following key questions: First, what is the relationship between the support of children and the elderly and social pension replacement or improvement? Second, what is the impact of Korea's contribution rate and replacement rate on NPS participation or compliance? Third, while financial support from children is essential, it may not be the most decisive factor; the income level of the elderly themselves plays a critical role [49]. If the income level of the elderly exceeds a value, the intention to support the children is stronger. In contrast, at this stage, with the disagreeable or bad relationship among children and the balance of the elderly to maintain a multi-child relationship is a difficult task [50]. In East Asian families, although there is a tendency for families to be smaller and smaller, there is traditional retention of unified planning of finances in the extended family. Therefore, the extended family will have a choice between contributing to the pension insurance and expenditure planning within the family. In addition, whether or not the family agrees on the elderly's participation in the NPS is largely a matter of the elderly's will, which comes from a more complex concept of Subjective Well-Being (SWB). This is because SWB is the most important psychological factor in determining whether to reduce the financial burden on their children or to have their children provide financial support. Here we propose “well-being” as the answer to the last question and that the number of children has a positive impact on the well-being of the elderly. Methodology and data

Method

Under the above four hypotheses, the fixed effect model, intermediate effect model, and fixed effect model with annual effect were used. In the robustness test, the GEE model and random effect model are used to compare the fixed effect model. In this study, statistical analysis software stata17 was used to obtain data results.

The basic unit-level fixed effect regression is as follows:(1)Yit=γ0+ρi+γ1Exposureit+γ2Xit+γ3Tt+ωitwhere Yit is the outcome of interest, Expossureit is the independent variable which affects dependent variable, Xit are observed time-varying covariates, Tt denotes the time period capturing common secular trends, and ωit is the error term. The estimate of γ1 is the “effect” of dependent variable on independent variable. and ρi is a unit-specific intercept (“fixed effect”), capturing all time-invariant factors that are correlated with the outcome. Because ρi controls for all time-invariant characteristics of the individual, the estimated effect of independent variable which we interested Yit is based on individuals who change their exposure status over time. All individuals contribute to the coefficient estimates on the other time-varying covariates Xit, but only individuals whose exposure status changes contribute to the estimate of γ1 [51,52].

The generalized estimating equation (GEE) model extends generalized linear regression with continuous, categorical or count outcomes to correlated observations within cluster [53].

Data

Data resources

The Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLoSA) is organized by the Korea Employment Information Service (KEIS) [54]. The first survey began in 2006. The survey sample consists of adults over 45 years old (similar samples in other countries do not include ages between 45 and 49 years old). The database contains 11,796 survey samples from 7574 families in 999 communities across the country. The baseline survey was conducted four times in 2006, 2008, 2010, and 2012. It includes basic information about the respondents, including children, income, property, health, social security, and details of self-assessment satisfaction.

The KLoSA data are publicly available and can be downloaded from the employment survey site with personal information removed. This study was conducted following the approval of the Yonsei University Health System Research Ethics Committee (Y-2017-0007) [55].

Dependent variables, independent variables, and control variables

The first dependent variable is participation in the national pension system (NPS). The value for participation is 1 and the value for non-participation or no response is 0 (C301). The second dependent variable is the self-evaluation of well-being. The self-evaluation of life satisfaction in the database is used(G039), and the original data are equidistant (from 0 to 10 divided into 11 levels, so no further classification processing is needed).

In the first three propositions and the fifth proposition, the main independent variables are the number of children, the degree of filial piety of children, and the economic support of children. The independent variable of the fourth proposition is “year”. The number of children is directly selected in the questionnaire. It can be seen from the questionnaire that the degree of children's filial piety is based on the number of children's meetings (Ba019_01), the number of children's telephone calls (Ba020), and their satisfaction with children's self-evaluation (G038; for the sake of brevity, the original questionnaire is divided into 11 levels and redivided into 3 levels: 0–30 points are assigned “1,” 40–70 points are assigned “2,” and 80–100 points are assigned “3”), as well as the amount of children's daily or monetary support (original database E002 and children's non-daily gift amount E006).

Control variables include age, age squared, family assets, family income, education level, health status, and child satisfaction (11 levels from low to high) and participation in medical insurance. Education level is divided into seven levels from low to high while health status (A005; divided into five levels: excellent, very good, good, normal, and poor) and child satisfaction are taken as fixed distance variables. Family assets include real estate appraisal, assets of family members (spouses), financial assets, stocks, bonds, deposits, and other financial assets. Family income includes wages, rental income, self-employment income, social security income, and other family income (it is impossible to distinguish whether children's economic support is included in the questionnaire, so this item is not included in this item). Unanswered questions are treated as 0. The number of children's meetings, telephone calls, income, and assets are logarithmic.

The fixed effect model: the stepwise regression method is used to analyze assumptions 1, 2, and 3

For the present analysis, we used the fixed effect model to obtain an estimation of the “average treatment effect” [56] The fixed effect model automatically eliminated 5644 samples that did not change across the three surveys (When fixed effect is estimated with conditional logistic regression for a categorical outcome, individuals with no change in the outcome will be dropped from the regression, and therefore do not contribute to any of the estimated coefficients. [51]) (see Table 2). From the perspective of the fixed effect model, except for Model 1, the impact of age on enrollment is the opposite. Since the NPS of Korea stipulates the relationship between the payment terms and the amount received, age is a basic factor, which affects the enthusiasm of payment. However, there is a positive correlation between age squared and participation, indicating that there is a U-shaped nonlinear relationship between age and participation in insurance. The relationship between education level and NPS participation is not significant, which is consistent with the Chinese-language literature. There is no obvious relationship between the number of children and social security participation, indicating that family size is not the decisive factor in participating in NPS. So it did not support Hypothesis 1. Income is positively correlated with the participation rate. According to Model 3, if the income increases by 1 %, the probability of participating in NPS increases by 0.016 in NPS. However, the impact of children's economic support on NPS participation is opposite to the direction of family income and has a larger coefficient. This supports Hypothesis 2. Why does children's financial support harm their participation in NPS? One possible reason is that South Korea generally emphasizes extended family-based economic integration. When funds are limited, that is, when there is a need to allocate limited funds within an extended family, family mutual support or saving on social security expenses tends to be a consensus in Korean households. This becomes the basic motivation to give up NPS. Therefore, Model 3 introduces the child relationship factor, which is the same as the impact of children's economic support on NPS participation. This supports Hypothesis 2. This result raises a question: Does children's economic support determine children's relationships? We have completed the work of the fixed effect model and conclude that the participation of age growth in NPS is first high, then middle-low, and then high. Families with increased income are more willing to participate in NPS, families with filial children are more willing to participate in NPS, and there is no significant correlation between the number of children and pension plan participation. Therefore, in the following robustness test, these two variables are deleted.

Robustness test: comparison of three models

The negative effect of age on social security participation and the positive effect of age squared are supported by the GEE model and random effect model [57,58](the age squared coefficient in the GEE model is not significant, indicating that the conclusion of the fixed effect model is not opposed). Health status is a factor often mentioned in previous studies affecting social security participation. Health status was included in the robustness test to avoid duplication. In the fixed effect model used for comparison, the better the health status of respondents, the lower the willingness to participate in social insurance. Although the GEE and random effects TS models do not support it, there is no clear objection. Since health is a quintile equidistant variable, it cannot be compared with the positive impact of income factors. The positive impact of family income on social security participation can be compared with the negative impact of child support funds. The negative impact of child health support funds has a greater positive impact on insurance participation than family income. This shows that the elderly in Korea pay more attention to family pensions rather than participating in a national pension plan (see Table 3).

Empirical study of hypothesis 4: the impact of the economic crisis on pension insurance participation

Using the first survey (2006), the second survey (2008), the third survey (2010), and the fourth survey (2012) to carry out logistic fixed effect regression, we added time variables and checking the time variables of two model coefficients, which are also annual effects. From the perspective of the time effect [61,62](Table 5), from 2006 to 2012, the results were negative (−74.73), which means that over time, the probability of unwillingness to participate in social insurance increased by 74.73 %/6 = 12.45 %, but we found, if we only observe the data of 2010–2012, the annual effect drops to 3.6 %. In response to the negative impact of time variables, the participation rate of social security has been greatly reduced. According to the fixed effect model, although the sample size is small, if the problem of missing variables can be solved, the explanation of the annual effect should be concise and clear: the annual effect from 2006 to 2012 can only explain the impact of the financial crisis along with the amendment to the Korean social security law determined in 2007, which instituted new payment and substitution rates. From 2010 to 2012, the impact of the financial crisis was mitigated. It is generally believed that due to the lag in policy intervention, the effects of the new social security law would also take time to manifest. Therefore, the average annual negative impact from 2006 to 2012 was 12.45 %, corresponding to an average annual negative impact of 3.6 % between 2010 and 2012. We can roughly attribute the difference between these two periods to the impact of the financial crisis, while the remaining part was due to the decline in the social security replacement rate. Additionally, we can infer that other macroeconomic factors (such as employment rates, wage growth, and demographic changes) might have a greater impact on participation rate [63,64]. In comparison, the impact of increased contribution rates and decreased replacement rates is smaller. Discussion

The contribution of this paper lies in several points. First, different from previous studies, we use a fixed effect model and intermediary effect test to discuss the mechanisms of changing participation and contribution rates in terms of extended household economies (intergenerational mutual assistance) for the first time, focusing on intergenerational mutual assistance. Second, from the perspective of economy and culture, this paper summarizes some commonalities in elder care in East Asia and offers a revision of the debate on the weakening of Confucianism or family relations in East Asia. Third, our empirical analysis of South Korea's increase in contribution rates during the financial crisis provides valuable insights for countries aiming to enhance their substitution rates. Most importantly, we build a compelling narrative suggesting that South Korea's experience can serve as a reference for other countries and regions facing similar challenges.

Family pension support has a strong substitution effect on social pension support. This substitution effect is primarily realized through close contact between children and parents, coupled with economic support. However, economic support alone does not fully capture the impact of close familial interactions.

To address the sustainability of the pension system, many countries need to avoid the low contribution model that still affects South Korea's reform. The strategy of the Korean government has been to gradually expand coverage, increase the contribution rate, and reduce the substitution rate (the coverage of public pension increased rapidly from 5.6 % in 1982 to 61.3 % in 2000, although there were many problems along the way). Official data show that in 2009, 27.1 % of the insured and 58.2 % of the individual insured were exempted from contributions (different from the company channel). The contribution rate of the declared income group is 92 % and the contribution coverage rate of the insured paying the premium through personal channels is 62.1 %. The empirical analysis of this paper also shows that the Korean government could increase the contribution rate alone without impacting to participation rate. This experience also means that it is not very difficult to increase the payment rate or reduce the substitution rate to improve the participation rate based on financial constraints.

For China, centralized planning makes for heavier financial burdens. This is why South Korea has increased the payment rate and coverage while reducing the substitution rate several times over. Will this reform ultimately improve the sustainability of social insurance? In addition, the difference of urban and rural management systems caused by China's urban-rural divide is the biggest obstacle to the realization of a Korean-style social security goal. Although the merger of urban and rural medical insurance since 2007 marks a sharp decline in the quality of health insurance in Korea, the reform of the registered residence system needs to be intensified with the progress of urban-rural integration. At the present stage, the background of the dual differentiation of social structure between urban and rural areas should be taken into consideration in policy design.

Insufficiencies and further work

As Paul D. Allison said, “Of course, some statisticians believe that talking about the causal effects of these variables is meaningless” [56]. Therefore, whether the results of these curious developments can justify causal inferences from non-experimental data remains a point of future discussion.

Since South Korea can improve its contribution rate without reducing its participation rate, this paper only analyzes this relationship and discusses its results and possible causes. The deeper reasons may involve more complex political and religious reasons, which are expected to be explained by future work. Moreover, it is worth mentioning that the following reason may diminish the robustness of the conclusions of the causal analysis in this paper. One possible reason is that the economic crisis has made South Koreans feel insecure about their income. Therefore, it is possible that this is one reason for the increase in contribution rates [65,66]. And another reason is that the pension replacement rate in South Korea is higher than Europe and the United States, the 60 % pre-reform rate in 2007 is the upper limit for European and U.S. welfare states (the people can find this advantage through comparison [67]), while the 50 % replacement rate in 2007 is also the median for European and U.S. welfare states. As for 40 % in 2028, it is within the range of the replacement rate of European and U.S. welfare states [68]. Besides, “policy-makers encouraged and exploited negative public opinion to legitimize the implementation of substantial cuts in pension benefits” [69]. That is, there is more consensus that makes the replacement rate lower. The third reason is that the economic crisis may have weakened family solidarity so that the elderly feel that pension is more and more important than the financial support from their adult children [70]. However, these reasons have not been revealed in the empirical study of this paper which further limits the applicability of the paper's finding. Therefore, it is the next step to continue searching for appropriate data related to the impact of the economic crisis on the payment rate so as to conduct a mechanism analysis.

Post-Communist countries in Central and Eastern Europe are also facing the challenges of aging societies, and their pension replacement rates are generally not high. Therefore, in addition to improving the efficiency of pension operation (safely improving the rate of return) and increasing public trust, the practice of South Korea's cumulative pension system stands for now as a helpful reference point. The logical next step would be to discuss the common points of some Asian countries and Central and Eastern Europe to provide the possibility of learning from each other.

The empirical research in this paper relied on data from specific years (2006, 2010, 2012), which may not capture recent changes or long-term trends in social security participation or family dynamics. Similarly, the joy and anxiety associated with either multiple or single children will change as traditional intergenerational relations evolve, especially in the case of ultra-low fertility [66,71]. Therefore, the mechanism analysis based on the economic and emotional interaction between the elderly and their children will also change. However, based on the different development stages of similar paths in different countries or regions, this study still strives to provide cross-temporal reference value.

Conclusion

Despite the financial crisis, South Korea has gained some experience in improving the payment rate of old-age insurance. One of the most important conclusions is that increasing the contribution rate will not affect the participation rate in social security; it may even have a positive impact on social security participation during financial crises or economic downturns. We analyzed in detail of the mechanism of family intergenerational support in East Asia, along with the similarities and differences between South Korea and other East and Southeast Asian countries. This paper provides more quantitative empirical analysis for the experience of South Korea, so that these experiences can be used for reference more convincingly by other countries and regions with weak pension foundations in East and Southeast Asia.

But on the other hand, the South Korea's experience is based on the tradition of the extended families (intergenerational mutual assistance), which is declining but still influential. This tradition comes from the experience of long-term agricultural production, and as the tradition fades away or diminishes, the prospect of intergenerational support for pension payment needs to be examined.

Although South Korea has a population of 50 million, but this is not a large volume compared to some economies that are much larger in terms of land and population scale. Therefore, as Sen, A. argued, the Four Asian Tigers (Chinese Taiwan, Chinese Hong Kong, South Korea and Singapore) in 1980s in general can not be used for reference by an economic giant in terms of overall system [72]. In other words, some of their specific experiences also need to be examined more carefully.

Therefore, we have discussed in detail the common points between China and South Korea. Even though China, as a large economy, is not so suitable for comparison with South Korea in terms of size, but in some regions, it has a similar level of economic development path and social life pattern as South Korea. It can learn from the experience of South Korea and strive to gradually increase the payment rate in more developed regions such as the Yangtze River Delta [73].
