Alteration of epidermal lipid metabolism by 2-linoleoylglycerol in a 3D skin model
Authors: Andréa Tremblay, Mélissa Simard, Angélina Larouche, Élizabeth Dumais, Pierre Julien, Nicolas Flamand, Roxane Pouliot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSkin modeling is a crucial tool in dermatology, cosmetics, and pharmaceutical research. However, replicating the skin barrier function in these models remains challenging. Recent advances in lipidomics have highlighted significant differences between skin models and native human skin. Notably, self-assembled tissues exhibit an accumulation of 2-linoleoylglycerol, a monoacylglycerol containing a linoleic acid moiety. This study investigated the impact of 2-linoleoylglycerol on the skin lipidome and testosterone permeability in skin substitutes produced via the self-assembly method. The treatment of skin substitutes with 2-linoleoylglycerol resulted in higher levels of linoleic acid in epidermal phospholipids. Testosterone permeability remained largely unaffected by 2-linoleoylglycerol supplementation, and there was a modest impact on the expression of tight junction proteins. Although monoacylglycerol degradation by monoacylglycerol lipase was unchanged, its synthesis by diacylglycerol lipase β appeared to be influenced by exogenous 2-linoleoylglycerol, since the level of RNA gene transcription was decreased. In conclusion, these results show that the accumulation of 2-linoleoylglycerol in the epidermis of skin substitutes could be one of the factors that affect the lipid metabolism of the skin model.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-60336-w.Keywords: Skin model, Epidermis, Barrier function, Phospholipids, Fatty acids, Lipid mediators, Lipid metabolism, Tight junctionsSubject terms: Biochemistry, Chemistry

The three-dimensional modelling of tissues, such as skin, is an important tool for better understanding the roles and interactions of diverse cell types. Skin models are recognized as important models in the dermo-pharmaceutical industry1. Their structural similarities to native human skin (NHS) make them a key model for studying the interactions of putative active ingredients2. Still, an important difference between the in vitro skin models and NHS remains, which is the barrier function of the skin. Skin models are more permeable than NHS3,4. This barrier function is mainly the consequence of an efficient Stratum corneum (SC), which is the outermost layer of the epidermis. This specific layer mostly consists of flattened corneocytes surrounded by a lipid matrix that seals the intercellular space. The formation of an efficient lipid matrix mainly depends on the lipid metabolism that occurs during the differentiation process of keratinocytes. In the basal layer, phospholipids are predominant, providing structural integrity and fluidity to the cell membranes. As keratinocytes differentiate, the lipid composition shifts, with ceramides becoming more abundant in the SC5.

Many studies have attempted to clarify the composition and the biomechanical mechanism of the lipid matrix assembly that seals the SC. Of note, linoleic acid (LA) incorporation into ceramides, which leads to the formation of a cornified lipid envelope, appears to be important6,7. In light of this key role of LA, we previously attempted to enrich our skin model with LA and α-linolenic acid (ALA) by increasing their concentrations in our culture media8. However, this strategy was somewhat unsuccessful, as the incorporation of LA in skin remained modest and below what is found in healthy/normal skin8,9. Furthermore, we also found that LA was mostly transformed into its monoacylglycerol (MAG), namely 1/2-linoleoyl glycerol (2-LG)9. It is important to note that MAGs are naturally subjected to the acyl migration from sn-2 to sn-1(3). In consequence, the data presented in this study is the combination of both MAG isomers (1/2-MAG).

Given that our previous LA-supplemented culture media did not enrich LA levels in the epidermis (versus those of NHS) but rather increased the levels of its MAG, we sought to determine whether a supplementation of 2-LG would better enrich our skin model in LA, by bypassing the equilibrium between LA and its MAG. This study was therefore designed to further explore this unexpected accumulation and to clarify how MAG metabolism, specifically 2-LG, impacts epidermal lipid pathways within the model. As such, skin substitutes that were enriched with either LA, 2-LG, or ALA were compared. Data indicate that 2-LG supplementation provides better enrichment of LA in our skin model than LA itself.

Results

Integrity of the skin model

We initially assessed the integrity of our skin models that were treated (or not) with either 2-LG, LA or ALA. The histological and macroscopic characteristics of the skin substitutes remained consistent across all tested conditions. Each culture condition yielded a fully differentiated epidermis, as illustrated in Fig. 1 (e-h). A well-formed SC was evident in all conditions, with a clearly stratified living epidermis. Additionally, the skin surface appeared smooth and uniform, as depicted in Fig. 1 (a-d). Measurements of the epidermal and dermal thickness, presented in Fig. 1 (i-j), revealed no significant differences between the conditions. These findings indicate that the addition of LA, 2-LG, or ALA does not compromise the integrity of the skin substitutes, as all conditions were comparable to the controls.

Assessment of the barrier function

Testosterone permeability assay

The percutaneous absorption of lipophilic molecules was determined using a testosterone permeability assay, a reference molecule in such studies. Moreover, the use of testosterone is particularly relevant in the present study, as lipid supplementation is expected to primarily affect the lipid matrix. Testosterone permeability was not affected by 2-LG supplementation compared with unsupplemented conditions, as shown in Fig. 2 (a) and the corresponding calculated flux values can be found in Table 1 for all tested conditions. The quantification of the testosterone found in the receiver compartment of the Franz cells indicated that the flux of testosterone rapidly increased in the first three hours after testosterone deposition. However, ALA supplementation led to a significantly lower flux of testosterone through the skin substitutes at the third time point. After the fourth hour, the fluxes started to decrease until the last timepoint, 24 h after molecule deposition in the donor compartment. When looking at the cumulative amount of testosterone found in samples (see Figure S2), it is clear that the amount of testosterone that went through the substitutes is lower in ALA-supplemented conditions.

Tight junction expression

The expression levels of Cldn-1, ZO-1, and Ocln were semi-quantified using western blot analysis (Fig. 2b), and normalized to that of β-actin in each sample. When assessing the expression of the primary tight junction (TJ) protein associated with skin barrier function, Cldn-1, no significant increase was observed in any of the supplemented skin substitutes as compared with the control (Fig. 2c) although the mean expression was increased compared with other conditions. ZO-1 expression (Fig. 2d) was not affected by any of the supplementations compared with controls. In contrast, Ocln was the only TJ protein tested whose expression decreased in LA-supplemented samples (Fig. 2e). Additionally, the ratio of Cldn-1 to Ocln expression was calculated. Although not statistically significant, the Cldn-1/Ocln ratio was higher in the LA- and 2-LG-supplemented conditions compared with controls.

Epidermal lipidome of the skin substitutes

Fatty acid contents of epidermal phospholipids

The epidermal phospholipids of the skin substitutes were analyzed for each condition. The most abundant n-6 PUFAs in the skin substitutes were LA and AA (Fig. 3). As expected, both LA and 2-LG supplementation significantly increased the levels of LA-containing phospholipids in the respective substitutes compared with those of untreated substitutes− (Fig. 3). Furthermore, an increase in the levels of most n-6 PUFAs, many being statistically significant, including AA, was also observed in 2-LG- and LA-supplemented skin substitutes. Of note, the supplementation of skin substitutes with ALA did not increase but rather decreased the levels of some n-6 PUFAs versus the other experimental conditions.

In contrast, n-3 PUFA levels largely remained unchanged in 2-LG- and LA-supplemented substitutes while those treated with ALA effectively and significantly incorporated ALA into epidermal phospholipids, as shown in Fig. 3. In addition, the levels of most n-3 PUFAs were much higher in the phospholipids of ALA-supplemented substitutes, with ALA predominantly being converted to EPA and n-3 DPA, the most abundant n-3 PUFAs in the epidermis. Moreover, the ratio of n-3 PUFAs to n-6 PUFAs was significantly higher in ALA-supplemented substitutes compared with LA or 2-LG supplementation, as expected (Figure S1 b). Notably, the levels of SFA-containing phospholipids were significantly higher in substitutes2−LG+ compared with controls, a trend not observed in other conditions. As shown in the supplementary materials (Figure S1 a), the ratio of PUFAs to SFAs is higher in ALA-supplemented substitutes compared with controls, with no significant differences observed between controls and 2-LG or LA supplementation. The levels of different MUFAs were generally higher than the levels of other fatty acids analyzed, indicating that while their levels do not vary between conditions, their presence in the epidermis remains noteworthy (Fig. 3).

Free fatty acids in the epidermal compartment

Fatty acids and lipid mediators were analyzed using liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (Fig. 4). The most abundant n-6 free fatty acid in the substitutes independent of conditions was LA. While free n-6 PUFA levels, including LA, tended to be increased in 2-LG- or LA-supplemented conditions, it did not reach statistical significance. In line with this, there was no change in the quantities of LA-derived 9- and 13-HODE either. In contrast, substitutesALA+ had significantly increased levels of most of the n-3 PUFAs quantified. Moreover, ALA culture media supplementation decreased the levels of 15-HETE compared with 2-LG supplementation, and increased levels of 15-HEPE compared with LA supplementation.

MAG species tend to vary with lipid supplementation

The MAGs found in the epidermal layer of the substitutes were also quantified. The relative abundance of the different MAGs was 2-OG > 2-PG > 2-LG > 2-AG, then followed by the n-3 PUFA-containing MAGs (Fig. 5). Surprisingly, there was no significant change in either 2-LG or 2-AG levels despite the supplementation with LA or 2-LG itself. We observed the same pattern for n-3 PUFAs with the exception of 2-SDG, despite a significant increase in SDA. As for the documented or putative 15-lipoxyenase metabolites of MAGs, the changes (increases) were modest for 13-HODE-G and not significant. However, 15-HETE-G was significantly increased in substitutes2−LG+ compared with the other ones.

N-acyl-ethanolamine profile of the epidermis

N-acyl-ethanolamine (NAE) levels in the epidermis are shown in Fig. 6. Few NAEs were significantly affected by lipid supplementation of the culture media. In fact, only the ALA supplementation tended to increase the levels of n-3 PUFA-derived NAEs compared with unsupplemented or 2-LG-supplemented conditions. OEA, derived from the MUFA OA, was the most abundant NAE in the substitutes for all conditions with mean values higher than 1000 pmol/g of epidermis, followed by SEA, derived from SA. However, the quantity of NAEs derived from many of the FAs found in Fig. 6 is generally lower than the quantity of MAGs found from the same FA precursor. All n-3 PUFA derived MAGs (Fig. 5) were found at significantly higher levels in substitutesALA+ compared with substitutes−. All the lipid results show that MUFAs, specifically OA, and their derived metabolites are present in particularly high levels in skin substitutes.

Single-cell RNA-seq datasets from human skin (Fig. 7b) were analyzed to investigate the spatial distribution of lipid-metabolizing enzymes (as seen in Fig. 7a) across the epidermis (Fig. 7e), with particular attention to keratinocyte differentiation states (Fig. 7c-d). Genes involved in MAG metabolism displayed relatively restricted expression patterns. MGLL expression was enriched in suprabasal keratinocytes, consistent with increased lipid turnover during differentiation. DAGLβ was expressed across keratinocytes but was not restricted to a specific differentiation state, indicating a widespread capacity for DAG conversion into MAG within the epidermis. In contrast, DAGLα, MOGAT1, and MOGAT2 showed minimal expression in keratinocytes.

Enzymes involved in glycerolipid synthesis and phospholipid remodeling exhibited distinct yet partially overlapping expression patterns across keratinocyte populations. AGPAT1, AGPAT4, and LPIN1 were preferentially enriched in basal keratinocytes. In contrast, GPAM, GPAT4, AGPAT2, AGPAT3, AGPAT5, LPIN3, and PLA2G4A were broadly expressed across keratinocytes, suggesting a general role in PL synthesis and remodeling throughout the epidermis. GPAT2 and GPAT3 were not detected in keratinocytes. Enzymes involved in TAG synthesis, including DGAT1 and DGAT2, were expressed across all keratinocyte populations, suggesting sustained lipid synthesis throughout differentiation.

Lipoxygenase (LOX) and cyclooxygenase (COX) expression revealed clear compartmentalization between keratinocyte populations and immune cells. ALOX15B was preferentially expressed in basal keratinocytes, while ALOX12 and PTGS1 were enriched in suprabasal keratinocytes, consistent with their established roles in epidermal differentiation. In addition, PTGS2, ALOX5, and ALOX15 were predominantly expressed in skin macrophages.

Discussion

The present study provides a detailed lipid analysis of the epidermis of reconstructed skin substitutes following three different supplementations: ALA, LA, or 2-LG, an LA-containing MAG. Given the lipidome’s critical role in forming the lipid matrix that seals the SC, this study aimed at highlighting the differences observed in the metabolism of FAs in skin substitutes with exogenous MAG levels and the differences concerning skin permeability. While previous studies focused on the importance of triacylglycerols (TAGs) or ceramide ratios in the skin, very few have explored bioactive lipid mediators.

Since 1929, it has been well established that LA plays a critical role in skin health, as deficiency leads to enhance skin permeability10. Subsequent studies have further demonstrated its importance in ceramide formation and the establishment of skin barrier function11. While skin substitutes displayed suboptimal barrier function and significant LA deficiencies compared with NHS, supplementation of the culture medium with LA did not increase phospholipid levels nor improved skin barrier function, suggesting impaired LA incorporation in skin substitutes8. In the present study, supplementation with LA or 2-LG slightly increased LA levels in phospholipids and did not influence the percutaneous absorption of testosterone through the skin substitutes. First, these findings further support the presence of a defect in LA incorporation in skin substitutes, which appears to be specific to n-6 PUFAs, as the n-3 PUFA counterpart, ALA, increased n-3 PUFA levels and reduced testosterone absorption. The inability to restore LA incorporation in skin substitutes highlights a significant gap in our understanding of LA metabolism in the skin. A review of previous studies showed that increasing the dietary levels of ALA and decreasing those of LA results in higher levels of long-chain n-3 PUFAs in plasma or erythrocyte phospholipids12. ALA addition to the culture media significantly affected the phospholipidic PUFA levels of DHA, EPA, and n-3 DPA compared with ALA levels (Fig. 3). This was expected given that ALA is preferred over LA by the Δ-6 desaturase13. Fatty acid chain length distribution is also important, as it affects the permeability of skin lipid model membranes14. The addition of ALA to culture media increased total chain length distribution by increasing levels of the most elongated PUFAs of the n-3 PUFA family (Fig. 3), especially in the epidermis. ALA supplementation of the self-assembled skin model has been shown to improve the barrier function of skin substitutes against testosterone in previous studies8,9. Hence, it is used in this study as a positive control for increased barrier function.

While the lipid profile of the skin substitutes was more representative of human skin, the results concerning barrier function show that exogenous levels of 2-LG do not modulate percutaneous absorption of testosterone in the skin model. The literature suggests that MAGs increase the fluidity of and even destroy the barrier in ceramide bilayers15. On the contrary, our observations indicate that the 3D model’s barrier against testosterone remains intact in 2-LG-supplemented substitutes compared with unsupplemented controls, these results showed that exogeneous provision of LA in its free fatty acid form versus its MAG form did not differentially affect its uptake and/or incorporation in the skin substitutes phospholipids. This finding contrasts with previous reports suggesting that uptake of MAG is more rapid than free fatty acids. A recent clinical study further showed that n-3 PUFAs (EPA + DHA) are more efficiently incorporated into plasma when supplemented as MAGs or free fatty acids, both yielding similar results compared with TAGs16. Diets containing a mixture of MAG, TAG, and diacylglycerol (DAG) have been shown to enhance the incorporation of DHA and EPA into serum phospholipids compared with free fatty acid supplementation, while both approaches result in similar incorporation into serum TAGs17. However, it is not possible to clearly determine from the present study whether cellular uptake is enhanced, as 2-LG may be taken up and subsequently metabolized into other lipid species. Future studies using radiolabeled or deuterated lipids would be valuable to directly assess uptake of LA18,19.

In the present study, levels of 2-LG did not increase, therefore suggesting its further metabolism. However, the lipid species which were incorporated by either 2-LG or LA could not be clearly identified.. Interestingly, supplementation with 2-LG did not increase free LA levels, thus showing that the supplementation did not affect 2-LG hydrolysis by MAGL. Indeed, LA:1/2-LG ratios and AA:1/2-AG remained similar across all conditions. Therefore, LA could be accumulating into lipids species not assessed in the present study, such as DAG, TAG, or ceramides, although the lack of improvement of the barrier function suggests that LA is not incorporated into ceramides. Ceramide profiling, including ω-O-acylceramides (Cer[EOS]), was not included in the present study, as these analyses are addressed in a separate work focusing specifically on the impact of varying concentrations of LA in this reconstructed skin model39. To date, studies involving the enrichment of skin samples with LA remain scarce. A study of particular interest showed increased TAG synthesis in submerged keratinocytes when LA was added to the culture media20. Although a wide variety of lipids and bioactive lipid mediators were analyzed in the current study, many other lipids could not be analyzed due to method limitations, such as many octadecanoids known as trihydroxy-octadecenoic acids (TriHOMEs). Their absence in the present study cannot be ignored since they are partly synthesized from epidermal lipoxygenase-3, a member of the LOX family recognized as a key enzyme in the formation and integrity of the skin barrier21. DAG levels are also absent from the present study due to method limitations, although their quantification would greatly help to understand the lipid metabolism dynamics between TAGs, MAGs, and free fatty acids. As seen in Fig. 8, exogenous 2-LG levels decreased RNA expression of DAGLβ, potentially indicating lower DAGLβ transcription and activity. Consequently, if DAGLβ is less active in skin models, this could reduce MAG synthesis, providing a larger pool of DAGs that can be metabolized into TAGs, phospholipids or more. Interestingly, although several lipid-metabolizing enzymes displayed a layer-specific expression pattern across the epidermis (Fig. 7), this was not the case for all enzymes. In particular, DAGLβ expression was not restricted to a specific epidermal layer but rather detected in a subset of keratinocytes irrespective of their differentiation state. This observation suggests that the use of whole-epidermis RNA in the reconstructed skin model is unlikely to substantially bias the expression reported for this enzyme (Fig. 8). One hypothesis highlighted in our current work is that DAGLβ activity could be a key factor explaining the more efficient incorporation of PUFAs into TAGs and phospholipids in the epidermis when derived from MAGs, although the lack of protein expression quantification limits the validation of this hypothesis.

The results of the present study suggest that 2-LG has a limited impact on the lipid content. In this context, 2-LG likely acts primarily as a bioactive lipid mediator rather than a structural component of the SC. Research on the effects of 2-LG on the organism is still very limited, with the few studies focusing on its interaction with endocannabinoid signalling in the brain and nervous system22–25. MAG levels may influence keratinocyte differentiation or lipid processing through signalling pathways without directly contributing to the extracellular lipid matrix.

Supporting this, 2-LG was identified as a major product of DAGL activity in Drosophila and shown to act as a CB1 receptor agonist, reinforcing its role as a signalling lipid25. Moreover, one study of particular interest proved that the human cannabinoid type 1 (CB1) receptor is essential for the differentiation process of keratinocytes and barrier recovery in mice skin. This study used CB1 receptor knockout mice to prove this point, with opposite results for the CB2 receptor26. The use of EPEA in a self-assembled psoriatic skin model suggested that CB1 receptor activity may contribute to phenotype modulation, as the effect was prevented by the CB1 antagonist rimonabant27. These findings suggest that CB1 receptors are expressed in skin models and can be functionally engaged under certain conditions. Known as a low-affinity partial agonist of the CB1 receptor, 2-LG exerts its activation of the CB1 receptor most efficiently when its hydrolysis is blocked using a MAGL inhibitor23. Since the accumulation of 2-LG was previously observed in skin models and was shown to be higher in substitutes than in NHS, it is therefore conceivable that 2-LG could interact with CB1 signalling pathways in this context, although this was not directly assessed in the present study9. Moreover, increased levels of EPEA in ALA-supplemented substitutes may also contribute to this signalling environment. These findings suggest that CB1-mediated signalling may play a role in epidermal homeostasis and therefore provide a strong rationale for future studies aimed at directly investigating CB1 expression and activity in skin models.

To date, studies showed that medium supplemented with essential fatty acids, including 10 µM LA, increased keratinocyte differentiation and CLE formation28. A study by Beguin et al. showed that under normal culture conditions, incubating Caco-2 cells with n-6 PUFAs (including LA) decreased Ocln expression compared with controls or n-3 PUFAs incubation29. Similar results were observed when studying TJs in the context of intestinal epithelial barrier dysfunction caused by hemorrhagic shock and resuscitation in rats30. This is also reflected in the present study, where Ocln expression decreased in the presence of exogenous LA compared with controls and ALA-supplemented substitutes. Under such conditions, the supplementation of the cells with several PUFAs affected trans-epithelial electrical resistance (TEER) results29. While Beguin et al. also determined that 30 µM DHA was sufficient to increase Ocln expression, higher levels were required to see an effect on ZO-1 expression, which could explain our results for this key protein29. Since many studies highlighted the importance of Cldn-1 expression in skin permeability, the anticipated result was that all conditions would have decreased Cldn-1 expression compared with ALA supplemented substitutes. However, this was not observed, as no differences existed . between the studied conditions (Fig. 2c). In the same skin model, a previous study demonstrated that while 10 µM DHA supplementation increased Cldn-1 expression, it did not affect the expression of ZO-1 or Ocln. Additionally, DHA reduced skin permeability to testosterone, similar to the effects observed with ALA in the present study, indicating that ALA might be a more palatable source of n-3 PUFAs than DHA31. Mechanisms by which lipid species such as MAGs and free fatty acids can indirectly modulate TJ integrity were recently proposed in the literature. These lipids can alter intracellular signaling pathways involved in TJ regulation, notably through phospholipase C (PLC)-dependent mechanisms leading to DAG production and subsequent activation of protein kinase C (PKC). PKC activation was shown to influence TJ dynamics by promoting phosphorylation of proteins such as Cldn-1 and Ocln, altering their membrane localization, and affecting actin cytoskeleton organization32. In addition, MAGs and fatty acids can interact with extracellular calcium (Ca²⁺), reducing its availability, which is essential for TJ stability33. Together, these mechanisms suggest that lipid metabolites such as 2-LG or LA may influence TJ organization and paracellular permeability through indirect signaling and biophysical effects.

Other interesting trends observed in the present study are the levels of MUFAs and SFAs found in epidermal phospholipids in 2-LG supplemented models compared with other conditions (see Fig. 3). The use of 2-LG tended to increase the levels of MUFAs and SFAs in phospholipids, whereas LA supplementation did not affect these levels compared with unsupplemented models. It has often been noted in the literature that high levels of LA in culture media lead to a better uptake of LA at the expense of OA20 . Although our current results show that exogenous LA and 2-LG did not affect OA incorporation in keratinocytes compared with unsupplemented substitutes, this can likely be attributed to the low doses used in the present study.

In conclusion, supplementation of the culture media with ALA increased the amount of n-3 PUFAs in phospholipids and improved the barrier function of skin substitutes. Supplementation with 2-LG induced an increase in the amounts of LA in the skin substitute phospholipids, leading to a more representative lipid profile. However, such an improvement did not affect the percutaneous absorption of testosterone through the skin substitutes. Lipid analyses were performed on whole epidermal extracts and therefore did not allow discrimination between the SC and the viable epidermis, which limits conclusions regarding spatial lipid distribution. In future studies, emerging techniques such as mass spectrometry imaging can provide the spatial resolution necessary to accurately map lipid distribution across epidermal layers.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The institutional review board of Université Laval approved this study. Consent forms were signed by the donors according to the Helsinki declaration and the guidelines of the Research Ethics Committee of the CHU de Québec–Université Laval. Skin biopsies from three healthy Caucasian women aged 46 to 49 years old were used for the extraction of keratinocytes and fibroblasts.

The biopsies were cut into tiny sections before being placed in a solution of thermolysin at 4 degrees for 14 h according to the method described elsewhere34. The epidermis was mechanically separated from the dermis using forceps. Keratinocytes were extracted from the epidermis using a trypsin solution for 30 min while fibroblasts were extracted from the dermis using a collagenase H solution for 4 h. Freshly extracted keratinocytes were seeded on a feeder layer of irradiated fibroblasts for 5 days, while freshly extracted fibroblasts were cultured until confluence.

Production of tissue-engineered skin substitutes

Skin substitutes were produced according to the self-assembly method previously published35. Amplified fibroblasts were seeded at 1.25 × 104 cells/cm2 in 6-well plates at passage 6 and were cultured for 26 days in Dulbecco-Vogt Eagle’s medium (DME) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Seradigm, Radnor, PA, USA) with 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid (Sigma, Oakville, ON, CAN), 0.06 mg/mL penicillin G (Sigma) and 25 µg/mL gentamicin (Schering, Pointe-Claire, QC, Canada), at which point the manipulable dermal sheets were superimposed in pairs. After two additional days of culture, primary keratinocytes were seeded on the dermal compartments previously formed at 1.2 × 106 cells/dermal equivalent. The culture medium was replaced by DME mixed with Ham’s F12 medium (3:1) (Gibco, Life Technologies, New York, NY, USA) supplemented with 5% FetalClone II serum (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) with 50 µg/mL of ascorbic acid (Sigma), 5 µg/mL insulin (Sigma), 0.4 µg/mL hydrocortisone (Galenova, St-Hyacinthe, QC, Canada), 0.4 µg/mL isoproterenol (Sigma), 10 ng/mL human epidermal growth factor (EGF) (Ango Inc., San Ramon, CA, USA), 60 µg/mL penicillin and 25 µg/mL gentamicin. After 7 days of submerged culture, the skin substitutes were raised to the air-liquid interface and maintained for 21 days, during which EGF was omitted from the culture medium. All throughout the experiment, cell culture media were changed thrice a week, and cells were incubated at 37 °C in an 8% CO2 atmosphere. After a total of 56 days of culture, skin substitutes were complete with a differentiated epidermis, and sections were sampled for further analysis.

Lipid supplementation of cell culture media

All of the supplementations used in this study were carried out according to previous work8. Stock solutions of lipids were diluted in 99% ethanol (EtOH) to obtain a concentration of 100 mg/mL and conserved at -20 °C. The required volumes of stock solutions were calculated to obtain a final concentration of 10 µM of either 2-LG, LA or ALA, in accordance with our previous work8. The EtOH was evaporated before the addition of serum to the lipid-containing Falcon tubes. Unsupplemented skin substitutes with evaporated EtOH were also produced as vehicle controls.

Histological analyses

Biopsies were taken from the skin substitutes once epidermal differentiation was complete. Small sections of skin were removed from the substitutes and were embedded in paraffin. The samples were then cut with a microtome to obtain 5 μm thick cross sections that were stained with Masson’s Trichrome staining. The thickness of the living epidermis, total epidermis and dermis was measured with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health (NIH), Bethesda, USA). The mean of three sections of tissue, including ten measurements per section, was used for each technical replicate (N = 3, n = 6).

Percutaneous absorption

The percutaneous absorption of testosterone was assayed using a Franz cell static diffusion system. The assay was conducted in conformity with the Organization for Economic Co-operation Development (OECD) guidelines. Whole skin substitutes were placed onto the receptor chamber with the epidermis facing up, then the donor chamber (5 mL volume, 0.63 cm2 surface area; Crown Glass, Somerville, NJ, USA) was placed onto the epidermis and kept in place using clamps. The receptor chambers were filled with a 0.1 M phosphate buffered saline (PBS) solution at pH 7.4 and stirred with a magnetic stirrer (VWR, Radnor, PA, USA).

At the initial time point (0), 100 µL of a 4 mg/mL testosterone solution in EtOH/water (1:1, v/v) was deposited onto the epidermis of each donor compartment before the chamber was sealed with parafilm. The receptor chambers’ temperature was set at 37 °C. Sample collection was performed through the sampling port at 7 time points (1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 4 h, 6 h, 8 h and 24 h) using a 5 mL syringe lengthened with a catheter (3 ½ Tom Cat Length 4½). Collected samples were immediately filtered with a 0.22 μm syringe filter (Sigma-Aldrich, Oakville, ON, Canada). Samples were finally kept at − 20 °C until their quantification with a Waters Acquity UPLC system using a Waters photodiode array (PDA) detector and a thermostatted autoinjector (Acquity UPLC H-Class System, Waters, Mississauga, ON, Canada).

An in-house-developed UPLC-UV method was used at 248 nm to successfully separate and detect the testosterone present in each sample. The separation was carried out using a BEH C18 Waters column (50 mm × 2.1 mm, 5 μm) kept at 50 °C. The mobile phase was a concentration gradient of acetonitrile in water with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) eluted at a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min. Testosterone peaks eluted at 0.91 min under these conditions. Data were collected and peak integration was performed using Empower 2 software (Waters).

Gas chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry

Fatty acids found in epidermal phospholipids were analyzed by gas chromatography coupled with a mass spectrometer as previously published36. Briefly, the epidermis of the skin substitutes was separated from the dermis using forceps and scalpels, and wet weight was noted. Then, the lipids were extracted using a modified Folch method with a mixture of chloroform/methanol (2/1 v/v). From this extract, phospholipids were isolated on a thin layer chromatography plate using an isopropyl ether-acetic acid mixture (96/4 v/v). Methylation of the fatty acids in the isolated phospholipid fraction was performed, using methanol/benzene 4:1 (v/v) mixed with acetyl chloride, before samples were quantified with an HP5890 gas chromatograph (Hewlett-Packard, Toronto, ON, Canada) composed of an HP-88 capillary column (100 mm x 0.25 mm internal diameter x 0.20 μm film thickness; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) coupled with a flame ionization detector using helium as carrier gas (split ratio: 1:80).

Liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry

The epidermis of the skin model was isolated with the same technique as in the GC-MS methodology section. Samples were ground using a Cryomill MM400 (Retsch, Haan, Germany) and suspended in a mixture of Tris-50 mM HCl (pH 7) and methanol (1/1). The extraction of the epidermal layer of the skin substitutes was performed according to the Bligh and Dyers technique and then quantified using a LC-MS/MS method as formerly described37.

Western blot analyses

Western blot analyses were performed on protein extracts obtained from the ground epidermal compartment of the skin substitutes (see Sect. 3.6). The protein extraction was performed using RIPA buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Ocomplete, Roche, Diagnostics GmbH, Germany). The total protein of each sample was quantified with a Pierce BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific, Ottawa, ON, Canada), and polyacrylamide (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) gels containing 2,2,2-trichloroethanol (Sigma-Aldrich) were loaded with 20 µg of protein per sample. Gels were activated with a Gel Doc EZ Imager (Bio-Rad). Proteins were transferred from the gel to a fluorescent PVDF membrane (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) overnight, and the membrane was imaged using a Gel Doc EZ Imager. Membranes were blocked with TBST-5% milk for 1 h. Primary antibodies were diluted in TBST-milk according to Table S1 and incubated for an additional hour. Three washes were done before incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h. Membranes were washed thrice before using ECL detection reagents (Cytiva) and band densities were detected using a Fusion FX7 MBI chemiluminescence imager (MIB Lab Equipment, Kirkland, QC, Canada). Blot density quantification was performed using ImageJ Software (NIH), with protein band densities normalized to β-actin band densities.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Epidermal samples from skin substitutes were separated as described in Sect. 3.5 and lysed with a 0.5 mg/mL proteinase K (Bio Basic Inc., Markham, ON, Canada) solution in 10 mM Tris-HCl, 10 mM EDTA 2% SDS (pH 9.0), using a mortar in 1 mL microtubes (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). DNA was eliminated using QIAzol and RNA was precipitated with ethanol prior to RNA isolation with the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Toronto, ON, Canada). A QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen) was used to produce the complementary DNA. A quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed for MGLL (Forward: 5′- GGAAACAGGACCTGAAGACC-3′; Reverse: 5′-ACTGTCCGTCTGCATTGAC-3′), DAGLβ (Forward: 5′-CAGCTCAACTGTCACTTCGGCT-3′; Reverse: 5′-CAGAGCCACTAAAAACGGCAGC − 3′) and GAPDH (Forward: 5′-TGCACCACCAACTGCTTAGC-3′; Reverse: 5′-GGCATGGACTGTGGTCATGAG-3′) and primers were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc. (IDT, Coralville, IA, USA). Real-time PCR was performed using a SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Scientific) in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations, with the LightCycler 480 Real-time PCR system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Values were normalized using the GAPDH housekeeping gene.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as means ± standard error of the mean, unless otherwise stated. Statistical analyses for lipid, western blot, and qPCR were performed using either a paired one-way ANOVA or the Friedman test (a non-parametric paired statistical test), followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test. Paired analyses were performed by matching each donor across experimental conditions, thereby accounting for interindividual variability. The multiple comparison test was used across the different experimental conditions; no comparisons were performed across lipid species. The choice of test was determined by assessing the normality of the datasets using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The ROUT method (Robust regression and Outlier removal) from Prism version 10 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) was used on technical replicate values to identify and remove any outliers. Percutaneous absorption statistics were analyzed using a one-way ANOVA. Only values with p < 0.05 were considered significant. All statistical calculations were performed using Prism version 10 software.

Single-cell RNA-sequencing analysis

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were obtained from the publicly available dataset GSE147424, originally generated by He et al. (2020), which provides a high-resolution transcriptomic atlas of human skin including healthy and diseased conditions38. In this study, skin biopsy specimens were dissociated into single-cell suspensions and processed using droplet-based scRNA-seq (10x Genomics platform). To minimize biological variability and focus on baseline skin homeostasis, only healthy samples were included in the present analysis. Eight independent samples were selected from the GEO database (GSM4430462, GSM4430464, GSM4430466, GSM4430467, GSM4430468, GSM4430470, GSM4430471, and GSM4430475).

Raw expression matrices were imported and converted into Seurat objects. Stringent quality control filtering was applied to remove low-quality or potentially apoptotic cells, including removal of cells expressing fewer than 100 genes or more than 5000 genes, cells with greater than 25% mitochondrial gene content and non-informative or ubiquitously expressed genes (ribosomal genes, long non-coding RNA and microRNAs and small nucleolar RNAs). These filtering criteria are consistent with those used in the original study38.

Data were normalized using the LogNormalize method with a scaling factor of 10,000. Highly variable genes were identified using the variance-stabilizing transformation (VST) method and used for downstream integration. Batch effects across samples were corrected using a Reciprocal PCA (RPCA)-based integration approach. This strategy aligns datasets across conditions and is conceptually derived from canonical correlation analysis methods described in the original study38.

Following integration, a principal component analysis (PCA) was performed. The first 20 principal components were used for neighbor graph construction, Clustering (Louvain algorithm, resolution = 0.4) and UMAP embedding. To improve cluster compactness and visualization, UMAP parameters were further optimized (dims = 1:10, n.neighbors = 25, min.dist = 0.5). Keratinocyte clusters were annotated based on canonical marker gene expression, including KRT5, KRT14, KRT1, KRT10. This approach is consistent with the original publication, which defined cell populations based on differential gene expression and lineage-specific markers38. Expression patterns of genes involved in lipid metabolism and bioactive lipid mediator pathways were investigated. Cellular distributions were visualized using global UMAP embeddings. For each gene, expression values were normalized per gene (0–1 scaling) to enhance visualization of spatial expression patterns and gene-specific color palettes were applied based on functional categories. All figures were generated using ggplot2 and exported as high-resolution PNG images.
