Coronary vasodilator capacity in obesity and morbid obesity – divergent flow responses with left ventricular hypertrophy
Authors: Elgin Ozkan, Liya Dai, Farrokh Dehdashti, Kan Liu, Thomas H Schindler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the relationship between coronary vasodilator capacity, left ventricular hypertrophy, and regional myocardial function in two different disease entities of obese (OB) and morbidly obese (MOB) individuals.Methods13N-ammonia PET/CT determined myocardial blood flow (MBF) at rest and during pharmacologically induced hyperemia, and corresponding myocardial flow reserve (MFR) with 13N-ammonia PET/CT. Left ventricular mass (LVM), early diastolic flow (E), relaxation (e’) velocities, and global longitudinal strain (GLS) were acquired with 2D, trans-mitral Doppler and tissue Doppler, and speckle tracking echocardiography, respectively. Patients were then grouped according to the body mass index (BMI) into normal weight (NW: BMI 20.0–24.9 kg/m2, n = 27), overweight (OW: BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/ m2, n = 31), obesity (OB: BMI 30.0–39.9 kg/m2, n = 71), and morbid obesity (MOB: BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2, n = 97).ResultsMFR progressively decreased from NW, OW, to OB (2.71 ± 0.84 vs. 2.50 ± 0.67 and 2.33 ± 0.63; p ≤ 0.04 by ANOVA), while it increased again in MOB comparable to NW (2.51 ± 0.51 vs. 2.71 ± 0.84, p = 0.70). In OB and MOB, MFR was inversely correlated with E velocity (cm/s), respectively (r = 0.32, SEE = 0.58, p = 0.02; and r = 0.29, SEE = 0.47, p = 0.02). Conversely, LVM, and GLS associated significantly and inversely with the MFR in OB (r = 0.27, SEE = 0.59, p = 0.05; and r = 0.31, SEE = 0.61, p = 0.04), but not in MOB, respectively (r = 0.13, SEE = 0.49, p = 0.27; and r = 0.05, SEE = 0.54, p = 0.73). Notably, GLS, E-velocity, and LVM remained independent predictors of MFR.ConclusionDivergent associations of coronary vasodilator capacity with left ventricular mass and early myocardial contractile dysfunction outline OB and MOB to affect left ventricular remodeling differently.Keywords: Blood flow, Circulation, Coronary circulatory function, Coronary microvascular function, Left ventricular hypertrophy, Myocardial flow reserve, Myocardial perfusion, Obesity, PET, Vasomotion

Introduction

Obesity has been widely recognized as a risk factor of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [1]. The rising tide of obesity in adults, but also among children and adolescents, poses a major public health concern [2,3]. Increasing visceral or abdominal obesity has been demonstrated to associate inversely with mortality [4]. Large cohort studies, however, outline the relationship between obesity and cardiovascular outcome by far more intertwined and even paradoxical [5,6]. Positron emission tomography (PET) non-invasive assessment of myocardial flow reserve (MFR) for the detection of coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) unraveled the highest major adverse event rate in obese individuals with a body mass index (BMI) ranging from 30 to 39 kg/m2, while significantly less in patients with more severe obesity despite comparable cardiovascular risk profile [7]. In more recent investigations, PET determined flow denoted a U-turn of abnormal hyperemic MBFs or MFR from patients with obesity to those with morbid obesity outlining a functional “obesity paradox” putting forth less vulnerability of coronary vascular function with morbid obesity [8,9]. The transition from abdominal obesity to morbid obesity in fact is paralleled by a switch from predominant atherogenic effects of visceral adipose tissue to a less atherogenic lipid profile, marked changes in the inflammatory-metabolic environment, and adipocytokine pattern related to an enormous increase in subcutaneous adipose tissue in morbid obesity [1,8,10]. The downside of the observed normalization of coronary circulatory function in morbid obesity presumably is a more enhanced left ventricular remodeling process paralleled by regional myocardial dysfunction, and interstitial fibrosis. Thus, it is intriguing to speculate that obesity and morbid obesity as different disease entities may not only affect coronary circulatory function differently but also left ventricular remodeling.

In this respect, we aimed to investigate the relationship between coronary vasodilator capacity, left ventricular mass (LVM) or left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), and early myocardial contractile function in two different disease entities of obese (OB) and morbidly obese (MOB) individuals.

Methods

Study population

The study population included 226 symptomatic patients with predominantly medically treated traditional cardiovascular risk factors such as dyslipidemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and/or arterial hypertension (Table 1), who were referred for myocardial rest-stress myocardial perfusion imaging with 13N-ammonia and PET/CT (Biograph mCT PET-CT scanner, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) for evaluation of obstructive CAD and/or coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) between May 2023 and March 2025. Patients with normal rest-stress myocardial perfusion imaging and wall motion analysis, as determined by gated 13N-ammonia PET/CT, that widely precluded the presence of significant flow-limiting obstructive CAD, served for study purposes. Patients were then grouped according to the body mass index (BMI) into normal weight (NW: BMI 20.0–24.9 kg/m2, n = 27), overweight (OW: BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/ m2, n = 31), obesity (OB: BMI 30.0–39.9 kg/m2, n = 71), and morbid obesity (MOB: BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2, n = 97). Vasoactive medications such as calcium channel blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers, statin as well as b-blockers, and diuretics were withheld at least 24 h before PET/CT myocardial perfusion-flow assessment. All patients refrained from caffeine-containing beverages for at least 24 h, and from smoking for at least 12 h prior to the cardiac PET/CT study. The study was approved by the Washington University in St. Louis (No.201812037), and each participant signed a clinical approved informed consent form.

Cardiac 13N-ammonia PET/CT study

After performing the topogram, to denote the axial field of view and a low-dose CT scan (120 kV, 30 mA) for attenuation correction, 13N-ammonia PET concurrently determined myocardial perfusion and myocardial blood flow (MBF) in ml/min/g with dynamic image acquisition (Biograph mCT PET-CT scanner, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) and a two-compartment tracer kinetic model at rest and during hyperemic stimulation with regadenoson, respectively [8,11]. PET ten-minutes list mode data acquisition at rest was started immediately after injection of ≈ 259 MBq 13N-ammonia as a bolus and subsequent infusion of 10 mL saline solution over 30 s (0.33 mL/sec) via infusion pump (e.g. Aitecs Syringe Pump). Following, the same imaging protocol during regadenoson-stimulated hyperemia (0.4 mg intravenous bolus injection over 10- and 20-seconds interval) was repeated immediately after injection of ≈ 518 MBq 13N-ammonia.

Myocardial perfusion at rest and during pharmacologic-stimulated hyperemia was analyzed visually on reoriented short-and long-axis myocardial slices and semiquantitatively on the corresponding polar map from the last static 10-min transaxial PET image. Semiquantitative evaluation of 13Nammonia PET perfusion images were conducted with a standard 17-segment model and a five-point grading system by two expert observers. Summed stress score (SSS), summed rest score (SRS), and summed difference score (SDS) were obtained. Only patients with a SDS < 2, excluding myocardial ischemia, and normal wall motion on gated PET were included for study evaluation. For the assessment of MBF in ml/g/min, left ventricular (LV) contours and input function region was obtained automatically with minimal operator intervention using Corridor4DM software (Cardiac; 4DM PET CFR) version 2016 (INVIA Medical Imaging Solutions, Ann Arbor, MI [8,11]. Initiating intravenous 13N-ammonia i.v. injection, serial transaxial emission images were acquired (dynamic frames: 1 × 5 sec, 12 × 10 sec, 4 × 30 sec, 4 × 60 sec, 1 × 120 sec) with PET/CT, and time-activity curves from the first 2 min in conjunction with a two-compartment tracer kinetic model were applied to quantify regional and global MBF in ml/g/min [8,11]. On the polar map of the last 600-s image set, regions of interest (ROIs) were aligned to the territories of the main three coronary arteries using the 17-segment model. Using the Corridor4DM software package, MBF was determined in ml/ g/min. Regional MBFs of the LAD, LCx, and RCA territory were averaged on a polar map, and the calculated mean MBF of the left ventricle was defined as global MBF. Heart rate, blood pressure, and a 12-lead electrocardiogram were assessed continuously during each MBF measurement. From the average heart rate and systolic blood pressure during the first 2 min of each image acquisition, the rate-pressure product (RPP) was derived as an index of cardiac work. To allow for possible interindividual variations in coronary driving pressure, an index of global coronary vascular resistance (CVR) was determined as the ratio of mean arterial blood pressure (mmHg) to MBF (ml/g/min) [8,11].

Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiograms were performed using GE Vivid E9 machine (GE Healthcare, Vingmed Ultrasound A/S, Horten, Norway) and 3.5 MHz phased-array transducers for standard comprehensive two dimensional (2D), Doppler/tissue Doppler, and speckle tracking echocardiography. LV ejection fraction (LVEF) was calculated by biplane Simpson’s method from apical four- and two-chamber views. Other echocardiographic parameters included LV end diastolic dimensions (LVEDD), septal wall thickness (SWT), posterior wall thickness (PWT), and these parameters were evaluated according to American Society of Echocardiography guidelines [12,13]. LV mass was calculated as 0.8*(1.04*((LVEDD + PWT + SWT)3 –(LVEDD)3)) + 0.6. Left ventricular (LV) diastolic function was evaluated following updated guidelines / consensus from the American Society of Echocardiography recommendations [12]. Early blood flow velocity across the mitral valve (E) and mitral annulus (both septal and lateral) diastolic myocardial motion velocity (eʹ) were measured with pulse wave Doppler and tissue Doppler study, respectively. The ratio of mitral E peak velocity and averaged e’ velocity (E/e’) was calculated subsequently. Speckle tracking analysis automatically tracked myocardial motion throughout the cardiac cycle and allowed a rapid generation of regional myocardial strain curves. We traced the endocardial border at the end of ventricular systole and adjusted the width of the ventricular walls to include the entire myocardium. Built-in software automatically accepts segments with good tracking quality and rejected segments with poor tracking quality. Finally, an automated function imaging based on speckle tracking analysis integrated the quantitative data of ventricular peak systolic longitudinal strain into a standard 17-segment model with a “bull's-eye” figure, helping to detect both regional and global ventricular dysfunction. The 17 LV segments were grouped as basal (6), middle (6), and apical (5) segments for statistical analysis, and the strain value of each group was calculated by averaging the peak systolic longitudinal strain [12]. Two board certified echocardiographic readers blinded to patients’ clinical and biochemical data independently performed 2D, Doppler and strain analyses to maintain the measurement reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± SD for quantitative and absolute frequencies for qualitative variables. For comparison of differences, appropriate t-tests for independent or paired samples were used. A comparison of MBF at rest, MBF during hyperemia, and corresponding MFR among the different groups was performed by 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed

by Scheffe multiple comparison test. Pearson's correlation coefficients (r), assuming a linear regression and the standard error of the estimate (SEE), were calculated to investigate possible associations among BMI, MBFs and CVR, hemodynamic and cardiovascular risk parameters. Multivariate analysis was performed by means of a linear stepwise regression model adjusting for the following a priori selected predictors of the MFR: gender, BMI, age, parameters of global and regional myocardial function, left ventricular mass, SBP, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, triglycerides, glucose, and high-sensitive C-reactive protein. All test procedures were 2-tailed, and p ≤ 05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS for Windows 25.0 (SPSS). The inter- and intra-observer coefficient of variation for echocardiography wall motion assessment was determined to be less than 3 % and 5 %, respectively. Based on a standard deviation of 0.17 of hyperemic MBF [8], and a minimum clinically relevant difference in hyperemic MBF of 0.22 ml/g/min, ɑ = 0.05, and a power (1-β) of 0.8, the number of patients necessary for the cross-sectional analysis was calculated to be twenty-three. As regards the reproducibility or coefficient of variation (COV) of PET-determined MFR, it has been reported of about 21 % for day-to-different-day, reflecting both biological and methodological variability, for repeat assessment of MFR [13].

Results

Clinical characteristics

Table 1 denotes the clinical characteristics of the study population. The sex distribution was widely balanced among groups except for MOB. There was a continuous increase in BMI from NW, OW, OB, to MOB, while age was comparable. Regarding the cardiovascular risk profile, the prevalence of medically treated dyslipidemia and arterial hypertension was similar among OW, OB, and MOB, but lowest in NW. There was a continuous increase in diabetes mellitus from NW, OW, OB to MOB. The prevalence of a history of smoking and family CAD, respectively, was comparable among groups.

With respect to the lipid profile, total- and LDL-cholesterol did not differ significantly between groups, respectively (p = 0.848 and p = 0.571 by ANOVA). Further, HDL-cholesterol was within normal range, while it was significantly less in OB and lowest in MOB, when compared to NW and OW, respectively (p ≤ 0.002). Regarding triglyceride levels, they demonstrated a non-significant increase increased from NW, OW, to OB, and dropped again to normal in MOB (p = 0.189 by ANOVA). Plasma glucose and high-sensitive CRP levels were significantly higher in OB and MOB compared to NW, respectively. The group comparison of plasma glucose and high-sensitive CRP levels, however, did not reach significance, respectively (p = 0.253 and p = 0.649 by ANOVA). As regards HbA1c, it was highest in MOB, while it did not differ significantly among groups (p = 0.308 by ANOVA). As denoted in Table 2, preventive medical intervention for cardiovascular risk factors widely increased from NW, OW to OB, while it was somehow comparable between OB and MOB except for angiotensin II receptor blocker and diuretics that were more frequent in MOB.

Hemodynamics and myocardial flow

Resting heart rate, was comparable among groups, except for MOB with mildly but significantly higher rate when compared to NW, while SBP was similar between groups (Table 3). Accordingly, the corresponding RPP at rest was highest in MOB. During pharmacologic vasodilation with regadenoson to stimulate hyperemic flow increase, the heart rate significantly increased in all study groups, while SBP and DBP decreased, respectively. The RPP during pharmacologic stress and change in RPP from rest (ΔRPP) during regadenoson stimulation did not differ significantly between groups, respectively (p = 0.066 and p = 0.087 by ANOVA).

As regards the assessment of MBFs, resting MBF did not differ significantly among groups, respectively (p = 0.461 by ANOVA) (Table 3). There was a nonsignificant decrease in regadenoson-stimulated hyperemic MBFs from NW, OW to OB, respectively, while it increased again in MOB like NW (Table 3). This was paralleled by a significant and progressive decline in MFR from NN, OW to the OB, and it increased again in the MOB group comparable to NW (Table 3). The group comparison of MFR in the NW group was significantly different from those in the OW and OB group (p = 0.049 by ANOVA) but not from the MOB group (p = 0.126 by ANOVA). To account for possible interindividual variations in coronary driving pressure, the coronary vascular resistance (CVR) during regadenoson-stimulation was calculated. The CVR increased from NW, and OW, to OB, while it was comparable between OB and MOB (Table 3). The group comparison of CVR during regadenoson application in NW, OW was significantly different from those in OB (p = 0.010 by ANOVA), while it was not the case compared to MOB (p = 0.077 by ANOVA).

Determinants of MFR

In the entire study population, on univariate analysis age, GLS, regional myocardial relaxation parameters, LVM, LVM-index, systolic blood pressure, total- and LDL-cholesterol correlated inversely with MFR (Table 4). By multivariate analysis, only age, GLS, E-velocity, LVM, LDL- cholesterol and triglycerides remained independently associated with MFR. As regards the OB group, the univariate analysis demonstrated GLS, E velocity, averaged E/e’, LVM, LVM-index, systolic blood pressure, and triglycerides to be significantly and inversely associated with MFR (Table 5, Fig. 1). By multivariate analysis, only age, averaged E/e’, systolic blood pressure, and triglycerides were independently associated with MFR (Table 5, Fig. 1). Conversely, the univariate analysis in MOB unraveled age, E velocity, averaged E/e’, to be significantly and inversely associated with MFR. By multivariate analysis, only age was associated with MFR in an independent fashion.

Discussion

The current findings are unique as they demonstrate divergent associations of coronary vasodilator capacity with left ventricular mass and early myocardial contractile dysfunction that signify and characterize OB and MOB to affect left ventricular remodeling differently. Furthermore, obesity related increases of left ventricular mass were paralleled by disturbances of myocardial relaxation that did not differ significantly between OB and MOB. In keeping with prior PET flow studies in obesity [9], current findings also denote a U-turn of the MFR from OB to MOB outlining contrasting impact of abdominal versus subcutaneous adipose tissue on coronary vasodilator capacity, but expands these observations to a larger cohort across obese and morbidly obese individuals.

As regards the PET-assessment of the myocardial flow reserve or coronary vasodilator capacity, it is important to consider that the study population consisted of predominantly metabolically unhealthy obesity as many patients underwent preventive medical care to control cardiovascular risk factors such as dyslipidemia, arterial hypertension, and/or diabetes mellitus. Cardiovascular risk factors, despite being medically treated, therefore may have added to the observed progressive worsening of impaired coronary vasodilator capacity in patients with overweight and obesity. The exact underlying mechanisms may remain uncertain, while components of the metabolic syndrome, commonly associated with abdominal obesity, such as dyslipidemia, increases in triglycerides, plasma glucose levels, systemic microinflammation, and arterial hypertension may have added to the observed progressive worsening of coronary vasodilator capacity with increasing body weight [8].

Conversely, major differences in adipocytokine profiles, inflammation, lipid and glucose metabolism, and yet unknown factors may account for the observed relative offset of impaired vasodilator capacity of the coronary circulation from obesity to morbid obesity or so-called U-turn [8,9].

The observed U-turn paradox of the coronary vasodilator capacity with its relative normalization in individuals with severe and even unhealthy obesity denotes less vulnerability of the coronary circulation and its function. The exact reasons for the observed functional U-turn paradox of the coronary circulation remains uncertain, but likely is related to a shift from the prevalent pro-atherosclerotic effect of visceral adipose tissue in OB to a less atherogenic lipid profile, changes in the adipose tissue related inflammatory environment, alterations in adipocytokine and endocannabinoid plasma level patterns, and still-unknown mechanisms in function to a marked elevation in subcutaneous adipose tissue in individuals with MOB [6,10]. Such a shift of metabolic risk from OB to MOB has been reported to be associated with preserved endothelium-dependent function of the coronary and peripheral circulation, respectively [14]. Another consideration may be seen in reported obesity-related increases in circulating progenitor cells derived from the bone marrow that are likely to contribute to vascular repair and regeneration via direct and/or paracrine mechanisms maintaining normal coronary artery function [6]. All these factors may indeed translate into less vulnerability of coronary arterial walls and its function associated with substantial less CAD burden in MOB compared to OB as reported with CT-assessment of coronary artery calcification burden [[8], [9], [10]].

As abnormalities in coronary vasomotor function are commonly seen as functional precursor of CAD, the described functional “obesity paradox” of the coronary vasodilator capacity may indeed denote less vulnerability of the coronary arterial wall in patients with advanced morbid obesity than in those with less obesity. The downside of the observed relative normalization of the MFR in individuals with morbid may be adipose tissue-released and seven-fold increase in plasma leptin levels leading to increases in left ventricular mass and thus left ventricular hypertrophy associated with subclinical and/or clinically manifest diastolic and systolic dysfunction [4,15]. Adipose tissue stimulated production of myocardial matrix metalloproteinase leading to interstitial fibrosis may also account for this [8]. In the current study, increasing body weight was indeed accompanied by a continuous and marked increase in left ventricular mass or left ventricular hypertrophy responsible for observed alterations in diastolic function in obesity. Furthermore, we investigated early myocardial systolic function, as reflected by measures of global longitudinal myocardial strain. And as it was observed, the (early) loss of myocardial contractile function continuously worsened with increasing body weight despite comparable global left ventricular ejection fraction among groups.

In the current study, myocardial relaxation parameters were inversely associated with decreasing coronary vasodilator capacity in both OB and MOB, outlining a disturbance of regional myocardial relaxation likely due to obesity-stimulated increases in left ventricular mass as an underlying cause for HFpEF, frequently observed in obesity and as described more recently [15]. In the latter study [15], impaired MFR was independently associated with disturbances of diastolic function and with HFpEF rehospitalization. The current investigation accords with the reported PET flow study in symptomatic patients with overt CAD [15] denoting a mechanistic link between alterations in coronary vasodilator function and diastolic dysfunction, while it expands these observations to increases in left ventricular mass in a large cohort across unhealthy obesity and morbid obesity. Notably, obesity-related increase in left ventricular mass and alterations in early myocardial contractile dysfunction correlated inversely with disturbances in coronary vasodilator capacity in OB, but not in MOB. Thus, while decreases in MFR are coupled to increase in left ventricular mass and associated alterations in regional systolic function in OB, the current data suggest an uncoupling of these relationships in advanced MOB. The reason for this novel and discordant observation may remain uncertain but likely is related, at least in part, to metabolically different disease entities of OB and MOB impacting coronary vascular function and/or left ventricular remodeling differently. In respect of coronary vascular function, lipid profile, glucose and hs CRP plasma levels were comparable between OB and MOB, except for higher triglycerides levels in OB. Someone could argue therefore that related offset of an impairment of coronary vasodilator capacity from OB to MOB is predominantly related to major alterations in metabolic and/or adipocytokine profile with increasing body weight. For example, while the predominance of visceral adipose tissue in OB mediates predominantly adverse metabolic effects via high fatty acid supply from the abdominal region stimulating atherogenic metabolic alterations favoring coronary vascular dysfunction and likely the initiation and progression of CAD, this is likely counterbalanced by increasing subcutaneous adipose tissue component in MOB [10]. The increase in subcutaneous adipose tissue may mediate a lower lipolytic response to catecholamines, a higher antilipolytic sensitivity to insulin, and an enhanced lipoprotein lipase activity [9]. With increasing body weight, there is also a marked increase of specific visceral adipose tissues, like the mesenteric and/or round ligament adipose tissue tissues, that has a higher sensibility to insulin in stimulating lipoprotein lipase activity and lipogenesis and in inhibiting lipolysis [16,17]. Thus, with increasing adipose tissue accumulation, there is indeed a metabolic shift from predominant atherogenic alterations of the visceral adipose tissue to a less atherogenic metabolic- and/or lipid profile in an inflammatory surrounding, conferring rather protective metabolic profiles for the arterial wall. In particular, the metabolic shift from predominant abdominal to subcutaneous adipose tissue is also paralleled by increases in insulin sensitivity and decreases in metabolic stress with reductions in catecholamine and glucocorticoids releases, that again leads to increased releases of adipose tissue derived and athero-protective adiponectin plasma levels [18,19]. There is also evidence of a 7-fold increase in leptin plasma levels predominantly released from subcutaneous adipose tissue in MOB individuals [10]. These marked increases in leptin plasma levels, but also micro-inflammation with advanced adipose tissue accumulation, have been demonstrated to be indeed associated with maintained coronary vascular function in MOB [10]. This paradoxical conservation of peripheral and coronary vascular function in individuals with advanced stages of obesity may be related to direct effects of leptin to stimulate endothelial-derived and athero-protective nitric oxide release and/or to an increase in inflammatory adipose tissue surroundings accompanied by enhanced mobilization of endothelial progenitor cells and decreased stimulation of the immune system, needing further investigations [9,10,14].

In the current study population, however, including predominantly patients with unhealthy obesity, age, regional systolic function and relaxation parameters, left ventricular mass, rest systolic blood pressure, total – and LDL-cholesterol were predictive of global MFR. By multivariate analysis, age, early diastolic relaxation, left ventricular mass, LDL-cholesterol, and triglyceride plasma levels emerged as independent predictors of the coronary vasodilator capacity. The independent predictive value of global longitudinal myocardial strain, as a reflection of regional systolic function, and E velocity, as a probe of early diastolic relaxation, and left ventricular mass outlines complex interactions of left ventricular hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunction, and early myocardial contractile dysfunction clustering effects of increasing left ventricular mass, apart from components of dyslipidemia and triglycerides of abdominal obesity, on coronary vasodilator capacity. The observations may accord with findings from Zupo et al. [20] investigating the role of higher plasma glucose levels, but still within normal range, as preclinical marker affecting cardiac structure in obese individuals. And indeed, higher fasting plasma glucose levels were an independent predictor of interventricular septum wall thickening in the pre-diabetic but obese state. Thus, elevations in fasting plasma glucose levels in the pre-diabetic state may indeed contribute to stimulating left ventricular wall thickening in obesity, independent of effects of adipocytokines, such as marked increases in leptin, and yet-unknown mechanisms on the left ventricular wall [10]. Components of metabolic syndrome, however, such as dyslipidemia and increases in plasma triglyceride levels, as independent predictors of decreases in MFR emphasize abdominal obesity, a risk factor clustering adverse effects of the metabolic syndrome on coronary vasodilatory function. In line with prior PET flow studies in obesity [8,9], age also emerged as an independent predictor of MFR. In this direction, a mild but progressive decline in hyperemic MBFs owing to a certain age −related impairment of epicardial vasomotor function and/or diffuse CAD, may account for this observation [21]. Further, advancing age is commonly accompanied by mild increases in resting arterial blood pressure leading to a relative increase in resting flows with a commensurate decrease in MFR or coronary vasodilatory capacity.

Taken together, the observed divergent associations of coronary vasodilator capacity with left ventricular mass and early myocardial contractile dysfunction signify OB and MOB to impact left ventricular remodeling differently. Given that pharmacological intervention with e.g. glucagon-like peptide-1 agonists or gastric- bypass surgery appears to be effective in the short −term, while it may fail to maintain long-term weight loss with relapsing disease owing to the interaction between individual predispositions and an obesogenic environment [22], current observations may gear research and clinical investigations in new directions for the identification of different key components of adipose-tissue stimulated adverse left ventricular remodeling process as new treatment target for the prevention of heart failure manifestation and worse clinical outcome in different forms of obesity.

Limitations

There are limitations worthy of consideration in interpreting these findings. First, the population studied included predominantly metabolically unhealthy obesity. Since current observations may reflect complex interactions between changes in metabolic profile, alterations in left ventricular mass and function with altered MFR in unhealthy obese and morbid obesity, further investigations in metabolically healthy obesity are desirable. Second, albeit that BMI correlates well with the population level and it can define the range of obesity, measures of BMI in the individual may not catch important differences such as adipose tissue/muscle mass pattern, obesity distribution, sarcopenic form of obesity, cardiorespiratory fitness, and metabolic risk that may have affected the observed but divergent association of coronary vasodilator capacity with left ventricular mass and early myocardial contractile dysfunction in OB and MOB. Given such limitations and small in numbers, current findings may be regarded as preliminary and hypothesis-stimulating, necessitating further large-scale and well-elaborated clinical investigations. Third, the clinical findings reported are derived from a highly selected and symptomatic patient study population with predominantly medically controlled cardiovascular risk factors like dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, and/or arterial hypertension that may have biased rest-stress 13N-ammonia PET/CT determined MFR. Preventive care with medically controlled cardiovascular risk factors, however, was comparable among groups, in particular, between OB and MOB individuals that should have widely excluded confounding effects of differences in cardiovascular risk profile and medical treatment on the described U-turn of coronary vasodilatory capacity with increasing body weight as also reported previously [8,9]. Fourth, smoking and any vasoactive medication were withheld at least 24 h before rest-stress 13N-ammonia PET/CT myocardial perfusion-flow study so that potential confounding effects on coronary vasodilator function should have been widely avoided . Fifth, well designed and elaborate investigations are needed assessing potential microvascular or metabolic adaptations in morbid obesity that might explain current findings. Finally, elaborated cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was not conducted that could have provided even more specific insights into early stages of obesity-related left ventricular remodeling process.

Conclusions

Divergent associations of coronary vasodilator capacity with left ventricular mass and early myocardial contractile dysfunction outline and characterize OB and MOB to affect left ventricular remodeling differently, that may gear research and clinical investigations in new directions.
