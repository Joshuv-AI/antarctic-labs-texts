Farmers’ perception of termite infestation, economic importance, and control practices in Dodota district, Southeast Ethiopia
Authors: Sisay Taddese, Yeshi Asaye, Annissa Muhammed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTermites play important ecological roles in soil formation and nutrient cycling, but they also cause significant damage to crops and rural infrastructure in sub-Saharan Africa. Despite their economic importance, information on farmers’ perceptions, indigenous knowledge, and local termite management practices remains limited in many termite-prone areas of Ethiopia. Therefore, this study assessed farmers’ perceptions of termite infestation, its impacts, and indigenous management practices in Dodota district, Southeast Ethiopia.MethodsA household questionnaire survey was conducted using 120 randomly selected households determined through Cochran’s sampling formula at a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error. Field data were collected from April to May 2025 through household interviews and focus group discussions. Descriptive statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26 software. The study assessed farmers’ knowledge of termite biology, perceptions of termite impacts, crop susceptibility, indigenous control practices, and the ecological importance of termites in the agricultural landscape.ResultsMost respondents were male (88.3%), married (95.8%), and within the middle-aged category of 34–60 years (65.8%). All farmers were aware of termites, and 91.7% correctly associated mound construction with termite activity. Farmers used three major criteria to identify different termite castes: body size (66.7%), body structure (16.7%), and feeding tunnels (10.0%). The major negative impacts reported by farmers included land consumption due to mound formation (43.3%), crop destruction (36.7%), and damage to homes (13.3%). Farmers also recognized several beneficial effects of termites, including improvement of soil porosity (22.5%), soil moisture retention (15.8%), and soil fertility (5.8%). Indigenous termite control practices included application of wood ash (6.7%), smoking termite mounds (13.3%), addition of organic matter (16.7%), destruction of mounds and removal of queens (6.7%), and promotion of natural predators (9.2%).ConclusionsFarmers possessed substantial indigenous knowledge of termite occurrence, impacts, and management practices. Integrating this knowledge with scientifically validated and environmentally sustainable approaches could improve termite management in smallholder farming systems. Future research should focus on evaluating indigenous practices and developing locally adaptable integrated termite management strategies.Keywords: Cochran’s sampling, Termite impacts, Indigenous knowledge, Soil fertility, Sustainable approaches

Introduction

Termites (order Isoptera) are among the most ecologically significant soil macrofauna in tropical ecosystems, where they play essential roles in decomposition, nutrient cycling, soil formation, and ecosystem functioning. Their activities enhance soil structure, porosity, and fertility, thereby contributing positively to agricultural productivity and environmental sustainability [1, 2]. However, despite these ecological benefits, termites are widely recognized as major agricultural pests, causing substantial damage to crops, rangelands, forestry plantations, and rural infrastructure across sub-Saharan Africa, including Ethiopia [3, 4].

Globally, more than 2,600 termite species have been identified, of which only a small proportion are considered economically important pests affecting crops and woody materials. In Africa, fungus-growing termites, particularly those belonging to the subfamily Macrotermitinae, which includes the genera Macrotermes, Odontotermes, and Microtermes, are responsible for over 90% of crop damage and yield losses [2, 5]. These species construct extensive mound systems that alter soil physical and chemical properties, sometimes reducing arable land and interfering with farming operations. At the same time, termite-induced soil modifications may improve soil fertility and water infiltration, demonstrating the dual ecological role of termites as both beneficial organisms and agricultural pests (Jouquet et al. 2020; [6]).

This dualistic nature of termites, which provides ecosystem service and causes harm to crops, poses a great challenge to rural farmers who have to weigh the benefits of termite activity against the direct crop losses and reduced land area. While the soils used for termite mounds have been shown to have high nutrient values because of the accumulation of organic matter, clay, and base cations [1, 7], the presence of termite mounds in farmlands reduces the available land area for farming and the efficiency of farming operations like plowing and harvesting crops [6]. Crop losses caused by termite activity in Africa have been estimated at 20–45% for different food crops, with crops like maize, sorghum, groundnuts, and cassava being the most vulnerable to termite activity [4].

In Ethiopia, termites are widely distributed and constitute a serious constraint to agricultural production, particularly in the western, southern, and southeastern lowland and semi-arid regions (Abdurahman et al. 2010). The threat of termites is more critical in Wollega, Benishangul-Gumuz, Gambella, Sidama, Gamo Goffa, and Rift Valley regions, where high densities of Macrotermes mounds are landscape features [6, 8]. Investigations in the western part of Ethiopia indicated that maize yield losses ranged from 20% to complete crop failure due to termite damage, with Macrotermes subhyalinus and Microtermes spp. as the dominant pest species [8]. In the Central Rift Valley, Debelo and Degaga , [9] reported that farmers estimated pre-harvest yield losses of 18.0% for haricot bean and 10.6% for maize due to termite damage, while maize lodging ranged from 3% to 33% across the surveyed fields.

Indigenous termite management strategies in Africa involve the destruction of mounds, removal of the queens, use of wood ash and plant extracts, animal manure, intercropping, crop rotation, and proper harvest time [2]. In the case of Ethiopia, the strategies involve the use of wood ash around the base of the crops, smoking the mounds with pepper pods and eucalyptus, dusting ashes in the fields, and removing the residues to deny the termites food [6, 10]. The efficiency, sustainability, and environmental impacts of the strategies, however, need to be evaluated. Despite the recognized impact of termites on agricultural productivity in Ethiopia, there is limited empirical evidence on farmers’ perceptions, indigenous knowledge, and locally adapted termite management practices in specific agro-ecological contexts, particularly in termite-prone areas such as Dodota district. Existing studies in Ethiopia have largely focused on the biological aspects and general impacts of termites, with insufficient attention given to integrating farmers’ experiential knowledge into context-specific management strategies. Farmers’ perceptions play a vital role in the development of termite management strategies that match local socioeconomic and environmental conditions [2, 11]. Farmers in termite-infested areas have gained considerable empirical knowledge about termite biology, susceptibility of crops, and management practices over the years; however, this knowledge is often under-documented and poorly integrated into formal research and extension systems [12, 29].

This study, therefore, aims to bridge this gap by examining farmers’ perceptions of termite infestation and documenting indigenous termite management practices in Dodota district, Southeast Ethiopia. The specific objectives of the study were: (i) to assess farmers’ knowledge of termite biology, diversity, and impacts; (ii) to evaluate farmers’ perceptions of termite infestation and crop susceptibility; (iii) to document indigenous termite management practices used by farmers; (iv) to identify perceived benefits of termites in the agroecosystem; and (v) to examine the major constraints to effective termite management. The findings of this study are expected to contribute to the development of integrated, locally adapted, and sustainable termite management strategies by linking indigenous knowledge with scientific approaches.

Materials and methods

Study area description

The study was carried out in Dodota district, which lies in the Arsi Zone of Oromia National Regional State, Southeast Ethiopia (Fig. 1). The district lies in the southeastern highlands of Ethiopia with altitudes ranging from 1,500 to 2,500 m above sea level. The district has a bimodal rainfall pattern with the major rains falling from June to September and a short rains season from February to April. The mean annual rainfall varies from 800 to 1,200 mm, and the mean annual temperature varies from 15 to 25 degrees centigrade. The agricultural system in Dodota District is a mixed farming system focused on both crop and livestock production. Major crops grown in Dodota district include maize (Zea mays L.), teff (Eragrostis tef), wheat (Triticum aestivum), barley (Hordeum vulgare), haricot beans (Phaseolus vulgaris), and other legumes and oil crops. Dodota district was purposively selected for this study due to the high density of termite mounds on agricultural land and reports from farmers and agricultural extension workers of significant agricultural problems caused by termites.

Location map of the study area

Reconnaissance survey and site selection

A preliminary reconnaissance survey was carried out in collaboration with experts from the Dodota district agricultural office to gather information regarding the severity of infestation, patterns of infestation, and impacts of termites on agricultural practices. This provided insight into areas of high densities of termite mounds as well as areas of farmer complaints regarding termite damage. Based on the reconnaissance survey results, three kebeles of Dodota district, being the lowest administrative unit, were purposively selected based on the following criteria: (1) high density of termite mounds in farmlands, (2) history of crop damage by termites, (3) ease of accessibility, and (4) representation of agro-ecological zones of the district.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

The target population consisted of households involved in agricultural activities in the selected kebeles. A comprehensive list of households in the kebeles was obtained from the kebele administration offices and used as the sampling frame. The total sample size for household respondents was calculated using the probability proportional to sample size sampling technique with a 5% sampling error and a 95% confidence interval. Data collection instruments and procedures

Prior to data collection, the purpose and objectives of the study were clearly explained to all selected respondents, and their informed consent was obtained. Participation was entirely voluntary, and no financial or material incentives were provided. With the assistance of local agricultural extension workers and community leaders, all selected households were successfully contacted and agreed to participate in the survey. Semi-structured questionnaires were used as the primary data collection tool through face-to-face interviews with selected respondents from the target population of household heads. The interview was conducted from April to May 2025. The questionnaire was developed initially in English, which was then translated into the local Afan Oromo language. Before the actual survey, the questionnaire was pre-tested with ten non-sample farmers to ensure its clarity and appropriateness. The household interviews were conducted over approximately one and a half months, while focus group discussions were carried out over a period of about two weeks. Each focus group consisted of eight to twelve participants drawn from the selected kebeles.

Further qualitative data were obtained through interviews with elderly farmers who have extensive experience in termite management (> 60 years old), as well as through direct field observations.

For this study, key terms were operationally defined to ensure clarity and consistency. Termite control practices refer to measures undertaken by farmers to reduce, manage, or eliminate termite populations and their impacts (e.g., mound destruction, smoking, application of wood ash, and use of organic materials). In contrast, termite deterrence mechanisms refer to practices that discourage termite activity or reduce the likelihood of infestation without necessarily eliminating termite colonies (e.g., habitat modification or use of organic amendments to divert termite feeding). In this study, the term control practices is used as an overarching concept, while distinctions are made where necessary. Moreover, the term agricultural landscape refers to smallholder-managed farmland systems in the study area, primarily consisting of crop fields and associated land-use components relevant to agricultural production.

Termite collection and identification

During field surveys, termite samples were obtained from mounds as well as infested crops, and taxonomic identification was conducted (Fig. 2). For every sampling location, soldiers and workers of termites were preserved in 80% ethanol in vials labeled clearly with sampling location, date, habitat, and type of mound. Termite identification was conducted at Arsi University up to the genus level using standard identification keys for termites in Ethiopia [14]. Specimens of termites were also deposited at the reference collection of the Department of Natural Resource Management.

Data analysis

The survey data were coded, entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, cleaned to check for errors in data entry, and corrected accordingly. The analysis was carried out using SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics of various socio–demographic characteristics, knowledge–related variables, perception–related variables, and management related variables were computed. Cross–tabulation was carried out to check the associations between various farmer characteristics and their perceptions, as well as management practices. Qualitative data from key informant interviews and focus group discussions were also analyzed thematically.

Results

Socio–demographic characteristics of respondents

A total of 120 households participated in the study, achieving a 100% response rate. The socio-demographic characteristics show that most respondents were male-headed households (88.3%) and married (95.8%) (Table 1). The predominance of male respondents reflects the socio-economic and cultural characteristics of the study area, where agricultural land ownership and farm management responsibilities are largely vested in male household heads. The majority of respondents (65.8%) were within the economically active age group of 34–60 years, while only a small proportion (7.5%) were above 60 years. Educational attainment was generally low, with more than half of the respondents having no formal or only informal education, and relatively few reaching secondary (10.0%) or tertiary (6.7%) levels. The average household size was 7.2 persons, with most families (51.7%) consisting of 6–10 members. In terms of farming experience, half of the respondents (50.0%) had more than 20 years of practice, indicating strong familiarity with local agricultural conditions and termite-related challenges. The average landholding size was 1.6 ha, with 75.0% of households owning less than two hectares, reflecting the dominance of smallholder farming systems in the study area.

Termite species identified

Taxonomic identification revealed the presence of several termite genera in the agricultural landscapes of the study area (Table 2). The genus Macrotermes was the most dominant and widely distributed termite group, characterized by the presence of large epigeal mounds commonly observed in cultivated and grazing areas. Microtermes was also frequently encountered, particularly in maize fields and crop residue sites, although its subterranean nesting habit made it less conspicuous. Other termite genera identified included Odontotermes and Pseudacanthotermes, which were moderately distributed across croplands and grasslands and were associated with crop damage. In contrast, Amitermes and Microcerotermes were mainly associated with woody debris, dead wood, and building materials. Trinervitermes was primarily recorded in grazing lands, where it occurred as a grass-feeding termite species.

Land access and termite mound distribution

The majority of respondents (97.5%) had access to agricultural land for cultivation, reflecting the predominance of smallholder farming systems in the study area (Table 3). Termite infestation was reported on all cultivated farmlands owned by respondents, indicating the widespread occurrence of termites across the agricultural landscape of the study area. Considerable variation was observed in the perceived density of termite mounds among farms. Most respondents (44.4%) reported medium mound densities ranging from five to ten mounds per hectare, while 38.5% perceived termite infestation levels as high, with more than ten mounds per hectare. Only a relatively small proportion of respondents (17.1%) reported low mound densities of fewer than five mounds per hectare.

Farmers’ awareness and knowledge of termites

All respondents were aware of termites and their occurrence on agricultural lands, indicating widespread familiarity with termite infestation in the study area (Table 4). The majority of farmers (91.7%) correctly identified termites as the primary agents responsible for mound formation, whereas only a small proportion associated mound formation with other animal activities. Most respondents (71.7%) also recognized the existence of different termite types or groups within and among mounds, suggesting considerable local knowledge of termite diversity. Farmers primarily distinguished termite castes and groups based on differences in body size and body structure. Other respondents identified termites through observable indicators such as mound characteristics, feeding tunnels, and damage to wooden materials.

Crop susceptibility to termite attack

Farmers identified eight crops as susceptible to termite attack, although the degree of susceptibility varied among crop types. Maize (Zea mays L.) and haricot beans (Phaseolus vulgaris) were perceived as the most susceptible crops, each being reported by 87% of respondents (Fig. 3). Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor) was also frequently identified as susceptible, whereas teff (Eragrostis tef), wheat (Triticum aestivum), and barley (Hordeum vulgare) were considered moderately affected. In contrast, potatoes (Solanum tuberosum) and vegetables were perceived as relatively less susceptible to termite infestation. Overall, the findings indicate that cereal and legume crops constitute the major targets of termite attack in the study area, particularly maize and haricot beans, which were consistently identified as highly vulnerable by farmers.

Perceived termite impacts on agriculture and livelihoods

Farmers perceived termites as having both negative and positive effects on agricultural production and rural livelihoods, reflecting their dual ecological and economic roles (Table 5). Among the negative impacts, the reduction of cultivable land due to termite mound occupation was the most frequently reported problem, followed by yield reduction, crop destruction, interference with cultivation activities, and soil hardening. Maize and haricot beans were identified as the crops most severely affected by termite infestation. Respondents also reported structural and livelihood-related damages associated with termite activity, including destruction of fence posts, wooden furniture, houses, and stored products. In addition, farmers indicated that termite infestation increases labor requirements for control activities and reduces farm income.

Despite these negative impacts, farmers also recognized several beneficial ecological and socio-economic roles of termites. The most commonly mentioned benefits were improvements in soil structure and porosity, the use of termite mound soil for house painting and construction materials, and enhanced soil moisture retention. Farmers further indicated that termite activities contribute to soil fertility improvement, soil aggregation, new soil formation, and the reduction of soil erosion. Some respondents also acknowledged the cultural and medicinal value of termite mound soil, while others recognized termites as contributing to waste decomposition and serving as food sources for predators within the ecosystem.

Factors favoring termite abundance and damage

Farmers identified several environmental and management-related factors that favor termite abundance and increase the severity of termite damage in the study area (Fig. 4). Drought and prolonged dry conditions were the most frequently reported factors, identified by the majority of respondents (78.3%). Continuous monocropping and retention of crop residues in the field were also commonly perceived as important contributors to termite infestation. Other factors reported by farmers included low soil organic matter, the presence of weeds, nearby abandoned lands, application of animal manure, and shallow plowing practices. Overall, the findings suggest that both climatic conditions and poor land management practices contribute to increased termite occurrence and damage in agricultural fields.

Indigenous termite control practices

Farmers in the study area employed various indigenous termite control practices to minimize termite damage to farmland and household properties (Table 6). Burning termite mound soil was the most commonly reported practice, followed by the application of organic matter and the smoking of termite mounds. Some farmers also relied on natural predators and on the physical destruction of termite mounds, including the removal of termite queens.

Constraints to effective termite management

Farmers identified several challenges that limit the effectiveness of termite management practices in the study area (Table 7). The most frequently reported constraint was the lack of effective termite control methods, followed by the shortage of labor required for termite management activities. Many respondents also indicated that termites rapidly reconstruct their mounds after treatment, reducing the long-term effectiveness of existing control practices. Economic and institutional constraints were also commonly reported. More than half of the respondents considered chemical control methods too expensive, while many farmers reported limited knowledge of modern termite management approaches. Lack of extension support and inadequate availability of control materials were additional challenges affecting termite management efforts. Some respondents further expressed concerns regarding the toxicity and environmental risks associated with chemical control methods. Small landholding size was also reported as a limiting factor, particularly because it restricts the possibility of fallowing and other land management options that may help reduce termite infestation.

Discussion

Farmers’ knowledge and perception of termites

Farmers in the Dodota district demonstrated strong indigenous knowledge of termite occurrence, behavior, and impacts on agriculture. Most respondents could identify termite presence, mounds, and damage on crops, grazing lands, and wood materials, reflecting long-term experience with termite-infested environments. This experiential knowledge supports local decision-making and aligns with findings from Ethiopia and other African countries, where farmers show a good understanding of termite ecology and infestation patterns [6, 12, 15]. Similar studies in Kenya and Uganda also reported farmers’ ability to recognize termite species, nesting behavior, and seasonal dynamics based on indigenous experience [15, 16], while studies in Benin and Ethiopia highlighted the role of indigenous knowledge in termite identification and management [17–19].

Farmers perceived termites mainly as agricultural pests due to crop and structural damage, but also recognized their ecological benefits, particularly in improving soil fertility, aeration, and organic matter decomposition. This dual perception is consistent with findings from other African agroecosystems where termites are viewed as both pests and ecosystem engineers [2, 12, 19]. Similar patterns have been reported in Uganda, Kenya, and Benin, where farmers associate termites with yield losses while also valuing their role in soil health improvement [15, 16, 19]. Recent evidence further emphasizes the importance of indigenous knowledge in sustainable termite management in sub-Saharan Africa [20].

Termite genera identified

The present study identified several termite genera belonging mainly to the subfamilies Macrotermitinae, Termitinae, and Nasutitermitinae. Among these, Macrotermes was the dominant and most widely distributed genus in cultivated and grazing lands. Similar findings have been reported from Ethiopia and other parts of sub-Saharan Africa, where Macrotermes species were identified as dominant mound-building termites associated with agricultural damage and reduction of cultivable land [6, 8]. Microtermes was also commonly recorded, particularly around maize roots and crop residues, which agrees with studies conducted in Uganda and Kenya that identified Microtermes as important subterranean termites affecting cereal crops [15, 16]. The occurrence of Odontotermes and Pseudacanthotermes further corroborates previous reports that these genera are common in African agricultural ecosystems and may contribute to crop damage, especially under dry conditions [4, 19].

In addition, Amitermes and Microcerotermes were mainly associated with woody debris, dead wood, and building materials, consistent with earlier ecological studies that describe these genera as important decomposers of lignified organic matter [21, 22]. Trinervitermes was primarily observed in grazing lands, reflecting its ecological specialization as a grass-feeding termite genus, as also reported by Badenhorst et al., [23] and Adam et al., (2018).

Termite impacts on agriculture and livelihoods

Farmers’ perceptions of termite impacts in the study area reflect the dual ecological and economic roles of termites in smallholder farming systems. Reduction of cultivable land due to termite mound formation was identified as a major constraint on agricultural productivity, consistent with findings from Ethiopia and other sub-Saharan African countries where mound proliferation reduces arable land and limits cultivation [6, 16]. Crop damage was also widely reported, with termites affecting roots, stems, and mature plants, particularly under dry and moisture-stressed conditions. Similar impacts have been documented across Ethiopia, Uganda, Kenya, and Nigeria on crops such as maize, sorghum, teff, beans, and groundnuts [12, 18, 20]. Termite damage was further noted to intensify during drought periods, leading to lodging, wilting, and yield losses [15, 24]. In addition, farmers reported structural damage to wooden posts, fences, and household materials, as also observed in Kenya and Benin, where termites increase maintenance costs and labor demand for control measures [16, 19].

Despite these negative effects, farmers also recognized important ecological and socio-economic benefits of termites. They acknowledged their role in improving soil structure, porosity, moisture retention, and nutrient cycling, consistent with studies describing termites as key ecosystem engineers in tropical environments [25, 26]. Similar perceptions have been reported in Benin and Uganda, where termite activity is linked to enhanced soil fertility and land restoration [11, 19]. Farmers also indicated the use of termite mound soil for construction, plastering, and other local uses, with additional evidence from African communities showing termites as food and livestock feed resources [12]. This coexistence of positive and negative perceptions highlights a holistic understanding of termites, suggesting that sustainable management should balance pest control with conservation of their ecological functions. Integrated termite management approaches are therefore essential to ensure both agricultural productivity and ecosystem sustainability in termite-affected landscapes [12, 18].

Crop susceptibility to termite attack

The identification of maize and haricot beans as the most susceptible crops to termite attack in the present study aligns with results from Ethiopia and other sub-Saharan African countries. Studies from the Central Rift Valley of Ethiopia and central Uganda similarly reported maize and beans as highly vulnerable crops [6, 12]. Farmers in Dodota district linked this susceptibility to the soft stems, high moisture content, and greater palatability of these crops, particularly during dry periods when alternative food sources for termites are scarce. This agrees with broader evidence showing maize vulnerability in African farming systems due to limited co-evolution with termite species and increased stress under drought conditions [2, 15]. Similar observations in Kenya and Nigeria also identified maize as highly prone to termite damage, especially during maturity stages [16, 20], while haricot beans were similarly noted for their nutrient-rich and moisture-attracting characteristics [4].

Other crops such as sorghum, teff, wheat, and barley were perceived as moderately susceptible, whereas potatoes and vegetables were considered relatively less affected. These variations in susceptibility are consistent with studies from Benin and Uganda, where differences in crop structure, rooting depth, and moisture content influenced termite preference and damage levels [15, 19]. The lower susceptibility of potatoes and vegetables may be related to their morphological and physiological traits that reduce exposure or attractiveness to termites. Farmers also reported that termite damage intensifies during crop maturity stages and prolonged dry conditions, which is supported by studies in Uganda, Zambia, and Ethiopia showing increased termite activity during tasseling and maturation when crops experience moisture stress and tissue hardening [18, 27]. Under such conditions, termites typically attack roots and basal stems, leading to lodging, wilting, and significant yield losses.

Indigenous termite control practices

Farmers in the study area employed a range of indigenous termite control practices, including mound destruction, smoking of nests, application of wood ash, flooding, use of organic materials, and removal of termite queens. These practices are grounded in long-term experiential knowledge developed through continuous interaction with termite-infested farming systems. Similar reliance on indigenous termite management strategies has been widely reported in Ethiopia and other African countries, where smallholder farmers depend mainly on locally available and low-cost control options [12, 18, 19]. Among these, mound destruction was the most frequently used method due to its perceived immediate effect in reducing termite populations. Comparable practices have been documented in western Kenya and Uganda [15, 16], although farmers also noted in the present study that termite colonies often rebuild mounds, limiting long-term effectiveness. This challenge has also been reported in Ethiopia and elsewhere, where rapid mound regeneration reduces the sustainability of physical control methods [6, 12].

The use of wood ash, smoking, and organic materials reflects farmers’ dependence on accessible and affordable resources for termite management. Similar indigenous approaches involving ash, smoke, plant materials, manure, and other organic residues have been reported in Benin, Uganda, and Kenya as traditional means of repelling or suppressing termite activity [15, 16, 19]. These methods are generally low-cost and environmentally compatible, although their effectiveness varies with termite species, infestation intensity, and environmental conditions. Only a small proportion of farmers reported using chemical insecticides, mainly due to cost constraints, limited availability, and concerns over toxicity. This pattern is consistent with findings from Ethiopia and central Uganda, where reliance on synthetic chemicals remains low among smallholder farmers [12, 18].

Constraints to effective termite management

Farmers identified several key constraints that limit the effectiveness of termite management practices in the study area, including labor shortages, lack of effective control technologies, rapid re-establishment of termite colonies after treatment, high costs of chemical alternatives, and weak extension support. Similar constraints have been widely reported in Ethiopia and other termite-prone regions of sub-Saharan Africa, where smallholder farmers rely heavily on traditional practices due to limited access to improved pest management technologies and technical assistance ([18]; [12]).

The labor-intensive nature of indigenous termite control was highlighted as a major challenge, especially during peak agricultural seasons when labor demand for crop production is already high. Similar findings from Uganda and Kenya show that repeated mound destruction, ash application, and manual control methods require substantial time and effort [15, 16]. Farmers also reported that termite colonies quickly rebuild mounds after treatment, making indigenous methods only temporarily effective, a problem also documented in Ethiopia and Uganda [6, 12]. Economic constraints further limit termite management, as chemical control options are often costly, difficult to access, and associated with environmental and health concerns. Comparable observations have been reported in Benin and Uganda, where farmers prefer indigenous methods despite their lower effectiveness due to affordability and safety considerations [15, 19]. In addition, limited knowledge of improved termite management and weak extension services further reduce effective control in the study area, consistent with earlier findings from Ethiopia [18].

Conclusion

This study was conducted to address the limited empirical evidence on farmers’ perceptions of termite infestation, indigenous knowledge, and local termite management practices in termite-prone areas of Ethiopia. The findings revealed that farmers in Dodota district possess substantial indigenous knowledge regarding termite occurrence, infestation patterns, crop susceptibility, and management practices. Farmers recognized termites as both agricultural pests and ecologically beneficial organisms. They identified land reduction through mound formation, crop damage, yield loss, and destruction of wooden structures as the major negative impacts of termites. Maize and haricot beans were perceived as the most susceptible crops, particularly under drought conditions and during crop maturity stages. At the same time, farmers acknowledged the positive contributions of termites to soil structure, porosity, moisture retention, fertility improvement, and the provision of mound soil for construction and other local uses.

The study further showed that farmers rely on various indigenous termite management practices, including mound destruction, smoking, ash application, organic amendments, and queen removal. However, these methods were often considered labor-intensive and only temporarily effective because of rapid colony recovery. Major constraints to termite management included labor shortages, limited extension support, lack of effective technologies, and the high cost of chemical control methods. Indigenous knowledge remains an important component of local farming systems and is transmitted across generations through observation, practical experience, and community knowledge sharing, guiding daily decisions on crop production, land management, and pest control. Therefore, integrating indigenous knowledge with scientifically validated and environmentally sustainable termite management approaches could improve crop protection while maintaining the beneficial ecological functions of termites in agricultural landscapes.
