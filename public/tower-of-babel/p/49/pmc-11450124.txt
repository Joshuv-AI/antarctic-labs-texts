Identification of key genes participating in copper-diethyldithiocarbamate-related cell death process and predicting the development of prostate cancer
Authors: Xin’an Wang, Chengdang Xu, Junjie Ma, Xiao Wang, Xi Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCopper (Cu) is used as a cofactor in all organisms, and yet it can be toxic at high intracellular concentrations, causing cell death. Diethyldithiocarbamate (DDC) is a Cu ionophore that can transport Cu effectively into the cell. Copper-diethyldithiocarbamate (Cu-DDC) can treat prostate cancer (PCa) and may correlate with the cell death process. However, the specific Cu-DDC-related cell death genes in PCa are still unknown. Information about the Cu-DDC-related cell death genes was obtained from a previous study. Concurrently, the RNA expression profiles and clinical data were downloaded from public databases such as GEO, TCGA, and CPGEA. Using data from TCGA database, the logistic and lasso regression models were generated using R software. The influence of these genes in affecting PCa progression and prognosis was analyzed. Finally, the expression of these genes was verified in clinical samples. We found five Cu-DDC-related cell death genes associated with the occurrence of PCa from GSE35988, a gene dataset, namely, CDKN2A, PRC1, CDK1, SOX2, and ZNF365. CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 are known to influence PCa patients’ disease-free survival (DFS) status and were overexpressed, whereas SOX2 and ZNF365 were under-expressed in PCa in the different databases. Some of these genes can affect PCa progression. Consistent with the database results, the mRNA and protein expression of CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 was also higher in clinical samples. In conclusion, we identified five hub genes which are important for Cu-DDC-related cell death process that can predict the development of PCa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-024-01390-6.Keywords: Hub genes, Copper-diethyldithiocarbamate, Cell death, Prostate cancer, Cancer development

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) has a rapidly increasing incidence worldwide. It had the highest incidence with the second-highest death rate among all malignant tumors in men, in 2021 [1]. Additionally, the morbidity and mortality of PCa in China are increasing [2], especially in older men, seriously threatening their health. Many studies have reported that genetic change and heredity are the main causes of PCa [3–6]. In the past decades, lots of genetic alterations have been widely reported that are important for patients suffered cancer [7]. With the development of many cancer genetic databases, such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), detailed characterization of genetic and epigenetic alterations in cancer can be performed [8, 9]. Therefore, bioinformatic methods can help find the potential genes which maybe important for predict the development of PCa.

Copper (Cu) is an essential cofactor for enzymes in all organisms of human [10]. However, the level of Cu in cells is maintained at an extraordinarily low level by many complex mechanisms [10, 11].This indicates that high concentrations of Cu can be harmful. A previous study has reported that diethyldithiocarbamate (DDC) can effectively transport Cu into PCa cells and cause cell death [12]. Another study reported that Cu caused unique cell death, different from transitional apoptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis [13]. This new type of cell death is correlated with many genetic variations and may have an important role in PCa occurrence. However, the mechanism of PCa progression induced by Cu-DDC-related cell death genes is still unclear. This study aimed to identify and analyze the function of Cu-DDC-related cell death genes in PCa.

From a previous study [13], we collected 310 Cu-DDC-related cell death genes. With the RNA sequence data from GSE35988, we identified five genes, CDKN2A, PRC1, CDK1, SOX2, and ZNF365 that were associated with both PCa occurrence and Cu-DDC-related cell death. Then, we analyzed the expression of these genes in different databases. We found that CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 were upregulated, and SOX2 and ZNF365 were downregulated in PCa patients from TCGA database. The same results were found in the Chinese Prostate Cancer Genome and Epigenome Atlas (CPGEA) database. Using logistic and lasso regression, we found these five genes can affect PCa occurrence and prognosis. Using the survival and immunologic information from TCGA, we found the three upregulated genes, CDKN2A, PRC1, and, CDK1 affected patients’ disease-free survival (DFS) status, and all the five hub genes were associated with immune cell infiltration in PCa. Finally, an analysis of the three upregulated genes, CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1, in clinical samples showed that they were highly expressed in tumor tissues. The experimental procedure was shown in supplemental Fig. 1 (Figure S1). Thus, we identified five Cu-DDC-related cell death genes important for PCa occurrence and development.

Materials and methods

Microarray and RNA sequence data

We used GSE35988, a gene dataset, which includes the sequence data of both PCa and normal prostate tissues from the GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Then, we downloaded the microarray data and clinical information of patients with PCa from the TCGA database (http://cancergenome.nih.gov/). Further, we downloaded the RNA sequence of Chinese PCa patients of 186 paired data from the CPGEA database (http://www.cpgea.com).

Data handing

The primary microarray data from the different databases were normalized using R software “limma” package (version 4.0.3). According to comments on the documents, the expression matrix, including probe ID, was substituted by the corresponding gene ID. If multiple probes corresponded to the same gene, the average value was calculated using the R software for further study [14]. The Cu-DDC-related cell death genes analyzed were those reported previously [13]. An adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |log2-fold change (FC)|>1 were considered as hub genes. Next, we used an online web tool, bioinformatics (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/), to draw a Venn map to identify the potential genes correlating with both PCa and Cu-DDC-related cell death.

Online web tool

The following are a few of the online web tools used in the study: UALCAN (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/) and gene expression profiling interactive analysis (GEPIA, http://www.gepia.cancer-pku.cn/).

Construction of risk prediction model

Logistic regression was performed to screen the hazard ratios of the changes in the hub genes resulting in PCa. A forest map was made to directly reflect the results of the logistic regression model. A nomogram was built to predict the risk value of the hub genes. A forest map was utilized to show the hazard ratios more intuitively. Both the nomogram and forest map were built by R software “rms” package.

Survival analysis

Lasso regression model was used to estimate the roles of these genes in affecting the prognosis of patients with PCa. The Lasso regression was built by R software “glmnet” package. The risk score was calculated by the following formula: risk score = Σjn = 1 Coef j∗Xj, with Coef j referring to the coefficient calculated by Lasso and Xj referring to mRNA expression of key genes. Log(λ) was also shown. In addition, the effect of these genes on patients’ DFS was validated by an online web tool, GEPIA.

Tumor progression analysis

To confirm whether these hub genes can influence PCa progression, the clinical data of patients were downloaded from TCGA. These genes expression was analyzed in different tumor stages according to the Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) tumor classification system of malignant tumors.

Clinical specimen collection

Clinical PCa specimens were collected from Tongji Hospital, School of Medicine, Tongji University. The methods used for collecting the samples were approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, School of Medicine, Tongji University (SBKT-2021-220). All patients who provided the samples were informed of the experimental process and wrote informed consent for participation. Finally, total 18 patients included in the study. In addition, the clinical information of these patients could be found in supplemental Table 1 (Table S1.)

Antibodies

Rabbit monoclonal antibodies against CDKN2A (Catalog No. ab185620), PRC1 (Catalog No. ab51248), and CDK1 (Catalog No. ab201008) were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK); Goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Catalog No. ab6721) was purchased from Abcam, too. (Cambridge, UK).

Cell culture and transfection

LNCaP PCa cell line was purchased from the Chinese Academy of Science Cell Bank (Shanghai, China). The cell culture and transfection process was carried out as previous study described [15]. The CDKN2A, PRC1 and CDK1 overexpression (oeCDKN2A, oePRC1, and oeCDK1) plasmids were constructed by Youze Biotechnology Company (Guangzhou, China).

Cell invasion and proliferaion assay

After 48 h of transfection, we carried on the cell invasion experiment. The process of cell invasion and proliferation was done as previous study depicted [16]. Then, the cell number in each field was acquired by ImageJ.

Western blot

Western blot (WB) experiment was down as previousl bewrited [17]. In addition, the primary antibodies dilutions were as follow: anti-CDKN2A antibody (1:1000), anti-PRC1 antibody (1:1000), anti-CDK1 antibody (1:1000), and anti-β-actin antibody (1:1000, Catalog No. ab8226) (Abcam UK, Cambridge, UK).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The expression of CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 in clinical patients’ specimens was detected by an IHC. The tumor samples were fixed by formalin and embedded in paraffin; 4-µm thick sections were used for the studies. The sections were antigen retrieved and immunostained as previous described [18], at the following dilutions: anti-CDKN2A antibody (1:200), anti-PRC1 antibody (1:100) and anti-CDK1 antibody (1:500). Two experienced pathologists (unaware of tissue information) independently evaluated and scored the IHC intensity.

Statistical analysis

The matrix data were handled using R version 4.0.3 (Institute for Statistics and Mathematics, Vienna, Austria; https://www.r-project.org). For descriptive statistics, mean ± standard deviation was used for continuous variables with normal distributions, whereas the median (range) was used for continuous variables with abnormal distributions. Student t-test was used to perform the statistical analysis. P values were used as statistical metrics. Two-tailed P < 0.05 was deemed as statistically significant.

Results

Five Cu-DDC-related cell death genes associated with PCa occurrence

First, we tried to find the Cu-DDC-related cell death genes which differentially expressed in PCa samples. We used a GSE dataset, GSE35988, from the GEO database, which included detailed microarray and clinical data of patients with both PCa and castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), a finally stage of PCa after treating by androgen deprivation therapy, and constructed a volcano map to reflect the differentially expressed genes in GSE35988. In total, we found 362 upregulated genes and 1258 downregulated genes when PCa occurred from GSE35988 (Fig. 1A). Next, using the information about the 310 Cu-DDC-related cell death genes reported previously [13] and the differentially expressed genes identified in GSE35988, we identified five Cu-DDC-related cell death genes, CDKN2A, PRC1, CDK1, SOX2, and ZNF365 that were associated with the occurrence of PCa (Fig. 1B). The expression pattern of these five genes in different samples from GSE35988 is represented in a heat map and found that CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 were upregulated, and SOX2 and ZNF365 were downregulated (Fig. 1C). Further, we verified the expression of these five Cu-DDC-related cell death genes in the TCGA database. We downloaded the RNA-sequence data from the TCGA database and built a heat map to reflect the expression of these five genes (Fig. 1D). Then, using an online web tool, UALCAN, we re-verified the expression of these genes in the TCGA database. We found that CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 were upregulated, and SOX2 and ZNF365 were downregulated in patients with PCa (Fig. 1E-I). In addition, we also found the same expression trend in CRPC patients from GSE35988 dataset (Supplementary Fig. 2). As methylation modifications can affect gene function, we next compared the methylation level of these genes in PCa. We found that with the exception of CDK1, the methylation level of the other genes was modified in PCa (Supplementary Fig. 3). The results indicated that these Cu-DDC-related cell death genes could be important in PCa occurrence.

Cu-DDC- related cell death genes leading the occurrence of PCa

As we found the expression of these five Cu-DDC-related cell death genes would change when PCa happened, we guessed they are important for PCa occurrence. So, using logistic regression model, we analyzed the risk of these genes in casuing PCa occurrence. Both binary and multivariant logistic analysis revealed that these five genes were risk factors for PCa (Fig. 2A-B). Further, a nomogram constructed to reflect the value of these genes in leading to PCa and PRC1 had the highest risk of PCa occurrence (Fig. 2C). Finally, a calibration curve built to reflect the accuracy of the nomogram (Fig. 2D). These results indicated that these genes could be risk factors for PCa.

The expression of Cu-DDC-related cell death genes in chinese patients with PCa

As the data from the TCGA database mainly includes the data of Western populations, we further analyzed the expression of Cu-DDC-related cell death genes in a Chinese population which can make our results more comprehensively. Using the CPGEA database, we downloaded the RNA-sequence data of Chinese patients with PCa. Results showed that CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 were upregulated (Fig. 3A-C) and SOX2 and ZNF365 were downregulated in Chinese patients with PCa (Fig. 3D-E). Thus, the CPGEA results were consistent with the results from both TCGA database and GSE35988 dataset.

Prognostic value of Cu-DDC-related cell death genes in PCa

After verifying the importance of Cu-DDC-related cell death genes in causing PCa, we further analyzed whether these Cu-DDC-related cell death genes can affect the prognosis of PCa. Using microarray and clinical data from the TCGA database, we constructed a lasso regression model, wherein we found that these genes affected the patients’ DFS status. The risk score=(0.0106 × the expression of CDKN2A)+ (0.1755 × the expression of CDK1) + (0.8063 × the expression of PRC1) + (−0.0221 × the expression of SOX2) + (−0.0117 × the expression of ZNF365) (Fig. 4A). The Log(λ) was reflected as well (Fig. 4B). Then, we constructed a Kaplan–Meier (KM) curve to determine whether these Cu-DDC-related cell death genes affected the DFS in patients with PCa. We found that these genes effectively influenced the DFS in patients with PCa with a P = 1.1e-06 (Fig. 4C). Finally, a time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve to perform the accuracy of the model verified high accuracy (Fig. 4D). These results indicated that these Cu-DDC-related cell death genes affected the prognosis of patients with PCa.

Cu-DDC-related cell death genes’ value in the survival status of patients with PCa

As the Cu-DDC-related cell death genes were found to affect prognosis, we analyzed whether these genes affected patients’ survival status. Using an online web tool, GEPIA, we found that the three upregulated genes, CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 affected patients’ DFS (Fig. 5A-C), whereas the two downregulated genes, SOX2, and ZNF365 did not (Fig. 5D-E). These results indicated that these genes can also affect PCa patients’ survival status.

The correlation between five Cu-DDC-related cell death genes and the prognosis of PCa in DFS status got from GEPIA online tool. A CDKN2A (B) PRC1 (C) CDK1 (D) SOX2 (E) ZNF365

Cu-DDC-related cell death genes in influencing PCa progression

Because the Cu-DDC-related cell death genes affected PCa occurrence and prognosis, we assessed whether these genes also affected PCa progression. The Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) staging classification is widely used to grade PCa severity [19]. Therefore, we analyzed the expression of Cu-DDC-related cell death genes in different TNM stages of PCa, using the data from the TCGA database. As there was an insufficient number of patients with metastasis, we did not analyze the metastasis stage. We found differential expression of CDKN6A, CDK1, SOX2, and ZNF365 in PCa patients with different tumor stages. The results indicated that these genes influenced PCa primary tumor progression (Fig. 6). However, none of the genes affected the node metastasis stage in PCa (Supplementary Fig. 4). The results reflected that the Cu-DDC-related cell death genes affected PCa progression.

Expression of Cu-DDC-related cell death genes in clinical PCa specimens

Finally, we verified the expression of Cu-DDC-related cell death genes in clinical PCa samples. The results from GEPIA had indicated that the three upregulated genes, CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1, were differentially expressed in patients with PCa and affected their DFS. Therefore, we verified these three upregulated genes’ expression in clinical PCa samples. So, IHC experiments to detect the protein level of these three genes in PCa specimens and showed that the three genes were upregulated in PCa samples compared to that in normal prostate tissues (Fig. 7A-F). These results indicated that CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 were truly upregulated in patients with PCa .

CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 increase PCa cells invasion and proliferation ability

After finding that CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 that were upregulated in patients with PCa. We next tried to confirm whether these Cu-DDC-related cell death genes can affect cell function. So, we first constructed CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 overexpression (oeCDKN2A, oePRC1, and oeCDK1) plasmids and transfected them in to LNCaP PCa cells. We found that these plasmids can improve the protein level of CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 in LNCaP cells (Fig. 8A-C). Then, we next to investigate whether the cell function would change when these genes upregulated in LNCaP cells. We found that when CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 overexpression in LNCaP cells, the cell invision would increase rapidly (Fig. 8D-I). The same results were also found in cell proliferation. When CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 was upregulated in PCa cells, the cell proliferation would increase at the same time (Fig. 8J-L). These results indictated that CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 are oncogenes for PCa. Then, we tried to confirm whether these three genes can affect the function of Cu-DDC in PCa cells. We brought Cu-DDC from MedChemExpress (MCE company) and treated LNCaP cells with 200nM/ml as the previous study reported [13]. Then, we further transfected plasmids with CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 overexpression into these cells and then tested cell invasion ablity after 3 days. We found that Cu-DDC can effectively decrease cells invasion and the overexpression of these three genes can reverse the ability of Cu-DDC in reducing cell aggressiveness (Supplementary Fig. 5). These results detected that CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 can prevent Cu in suppressing PCa progression.

Discussion

Cu is a double-edged sword for organisms: it is a critical cofactor for enzymes in the body, and yet, excess intercellular concentrations can be toxic [20]. A change in the Cu homeostasis can lead to the occurrence of many diseases such as Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, and Wilson’s disease [21, 22]. Regarding the role of Cu in PCa, previous studies have found that Cu is cytotoxic and can inhibit PCa cell proliferation [23, 24]. In addition, Cu can also be used in image diagnosis in PCa [25, 26]. Furthermore, Cu is also used clinically to treat PCa [27]. These studies indicated that Cu could play an important role in PCa.

Cuproptosis is a new concept proposed recently for a Cu-triggered modality of cell death [28]. Several studies have found that cell death can enhance the efficacy of antiandrogen therapy in PCa, release drug resistance, and cause PCa cell death [29–31]. These studies indicate the critical role of cell death in PCa. A recent study determined cuproptosis to be a unique cell death process, regulated by the lipoylated tricarboxylic acid cycle proteins and genetic factors [13]. Therefore, we speculated that genetic alteration of cuproptosis-related genes could play a central role in PCa.

DDC is an effect Cu ionophore that transports Cu into cells without causing cell toxicity. Cu-DDC can decrease PCa cell proliferation and induce PCa cell death [12], although the underlying mechanism of Cu-DDC for treating PCa is unclear. Previously, a study focusing on cuproptosis used whole-genome CRISPR-Cas9 technology to identify the potential genes that correlated with Cu-DDC-related cell death [13]. Using the data from this previous study and different dataset, we analyzed the potential Cu-DDC-related cell death genes in PCa. In the study, we identified five Cu-DDC-related cell death genes that were differentially expressed between normal prostate tissues and PCa samples from the GSE35988 dataset. Three genes, CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1, were upregulated and two genes, SOX2 and ZNF365, were downregulated. The regression model revealed PRC1 to be the highest risk factor for PCa progression and prognosis. Further, we found that the three upregulated genes affected the DFS in patients with PCa. In addition, some of these genes affected PCa progression and all these genes were also associated with immune infiltration in PCa. Finally, we determined that CDKN2A, PRC1, and CDK1 were truly upregulated in PCa samples.

The cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2 A (CDKN2A) gene is located on chromosome 9p21 and has three exons encoding the tumor suppressor protein p16; CDNK2A is frequently mutated or deleted in a wide variety of tumors [32–34]. Regarding its role in PCa, a study has found that CDKN2A affects the survival status of patients with PCa [35]. Protein regulator of cytokinesis 1 (PRC1) variably resides in different tissue types and affects cell division [36]. Changes in the copy number and missense mutation-mediated disruption of PRC1 can dysregulate cytokinesis [37]. The abnormal regulation of PRC1 can lead to many carcinomas such as breast cancer and gastric cancer [37, 38]. Regarding the role of PRC1 in PCa, a study found that patients with overexpressed PRC1 would have a higher risk of biochemical recurrence [39]. Cyclin-dependent kinases 1 (CDK1) is an essential factor in the management of the cell cycle and tumor development including PCa [40]. Loss of 17q22 of CDK1 in PCa has been linked with the development of CRPC and enzalutamide resistance [40]. CDK1 is also associated with the stabilization of the androgen receptor (AR) in PCa [41]. Moreover, androgen-independent AR activity is driven by CDK1-mediated AR Ser81 phosphorylation in CRPC [42]. Furthermore, CDK1 can affect the SPOP signaling pathway and enhance the effect of programmed death-ligand 1 in treating PCa [43]. Some studies reported the association of SRY-box transcription factor 2 (SOX2) with PCa [44, 45]; and although no study has reported the association of zinc finger protein 365 (ZNF365) with PCa, it is known to play a role in the occurrence of breast and ovarian cancer [46, 47]. These studies indicate that these five Cu-DDC-related cell death genes may play an important role in carcinomas including PCa.

Our study has a few shortcomings. First, the identity of the Cu-DDC-related cell death genes was obtained from a previous study. The results derived from a single study may be incomplete and biased. Second, the Cu-DDC-related cell death genes associated with PCa were obtained from a gene dataset, GSE35988. This screening process is not very rigorous and may have led to some Cu-DDC-related cell death genes not being included in the study. Third, the number of clinical samples was small. Therefore, studies with larger sample sizes are needed to be carried out to prove the results from this study. Fourth, although we identified Cu-DDC-related cell death genes in PCa, we did not analyze the underlying mechanism of these genes in causing PCa. Future studies should aim to investigate the underlying mechanisms of Cu-DDC-related cell death genes in causing PCa.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we identified three hub genes that are participated in Cu-DDC-related cell death genes process and can predict the development of PCa.
