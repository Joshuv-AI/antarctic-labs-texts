Symmetric Dimethylarginine Predicts Previously Undetected Atrial Fibrillation in Patients With Ischemic Stroke
Authors: Juliane Hannemann, Katrin Wasser, Yoana Mileva, Fiona Kleinsang, Mario Schubert, Edzard Schwedhelm, Kaomei Guan, Rolf Wachter, Rainer Böger
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is a stroke risk factor that often remains undetected at hospital admission. Long‐term Holter monitoring helps to identify patients with previously unrecognized AF. Asymmetric (ADMA) and symmetric dimethylarginine (SDMA) are elevated in AF in cross‐sectional studies. We analyzed ADMA, SDMA, and other L‐arginine metabolites to assess their association with AF in the Find‐AF trial.Methods and ResultsWe included 280 patients presenting with acute cerebral ischemia. Patients presenting in sinus rhythm received 7‐day Holter‐ECG. Biomarkers were quantified by ultra‐performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. We also analyzed protein methylation and L‐arginine‐related metabolites in human induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived cardiomyocytes in vitro. ADMA and SDMA were elevated in 44 patients who presented with AF. SDMA, but not ADMA, was significantly elevated in patients newly diagnosed with AF in Holter‐ECG as compared with those in sinus rhythm. SDMA plasma concentration >0.571 μmol/L significantly predicted presence of AF in Holter‐ECG (area under the curve=0.676 [0.530–0.822]; P=0.029; sensitivity 0.786, specificity 0.572). SDMA levels further increased in patients with AF during the first 24 hours in hospital, and ADMA levels remained stable. In vitro, induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived cardiomyocytes showed increased symmetric protein methylation and elevated SDMA during rapid pacing (2.0 Hz versus 0.5 Hz), whereas asymmetric protein methylation and ADMA were unchanged.ConclusionsSDMA at admission was significantly elevated in stroke patients presenting with AF and showed a moderate but significant prospective association with previously unrecognized AF. Thus, stroke patients with elevated SDMA concentration at admission may specifically benefit from extended Holter‐ECG monitoring.

Dimethylarginines are metabolites of L‐arginine that are derived from protein L‐arginine dimethylation, a posttranslational process of protein modification. 1 A family of enzymes named PRMTs (protein arginine N‐methyltransferases) catalyze the mono‐ or dimethylation of L‐arginine residues within proteins; during physiological protein turnover, free asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA) and symmetric dimethylarginine (SDMA) are released and passed from cytoplasm into the extracellular fluid and plasma (for review, cf. 2 ). ADMA interferes with vascular endothelial nitrous oxide (NO) synthesis by directly and competitively inhibiting NO synthase (ADMA 3 ). In addition, both ADMA and SDMA have been shown to inhibit cellular L‐arginine uptake and thereby deprive NO synthase from its natural substrate. 4 Despite these apparent similarities in biochemistry, the influence of SDMA—in contrast to the clear inhibitory effect of ADMA—on vascular NO synthesis is minimal, so that the main biochemical function of SDMA has remained elusive. In addition, differences in clinical significance have been reported: ADMA causes endothelial dysfunction and is primarily associated with high blood pressure and cardiovascular diseases, including systemic and pulmonary hypertension 3 , 5 , 6 ; it is a marker of cardiovascular and total mortality. 7 By contrast, we found SDMA to be a risk marker of short‐term and long‐term outcome after ischemic stroke. 8 , 9

Therefore, we hypothesized that the differential risk factor profiles of ADMA and SDMA, which have been observed despite their apparent similar biochemistry may relate to differential roles of both compounds in atrial fibrillation (AF). We undertook the present study to assess the relationship of ADMA and SDMA with AF in the Find‐AF trial, which was designed to detect subclinical AF in patients presenting with acute cerebral ischemia by using long‐term Holter monitoring. 10 We also quantified the plasma concentrations of L‐arginine, the substrate of the NO synthase and arginase enzymes, as well as L‐citrulline, the (by‐)product of NO synthase, and L‐ornithine, the product of arginase, to more completely assess these L‐arginine metabolic pathways.

METHODS

Study Participants and Protocol

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The details of the Find‐AF study design have been described before. 10 Briefly, consecutive patients presenting to the emergency department with symptoms of acute cerebral ischemia were included and underwent initial blood sampling. Patients who were found during inpatient treatment to have other diagnoses than cerebral ischemia causative of their symptoms were excluded from the analysis. Find‐AF was a single‐center prospective observational trial performed between March 2009 and February 2010. The study protocol was approved by the responsible Ethics Committee, and all patients gave written informed consent in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients underwent cerebral computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging scan, carotid ultrasound, and echocardiography to confirm the diagnosis of cerebral infarction and to determine its cause. The definitions of transient ischemic attack (TIA), minor stroke, and major stroke were based upon the Trial of ORG 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment classification scheme. Stroke severity was approximated by video‐trained physicians applying the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale and the modified Rankin Scale. Presence of AF was defined as at least 1 period of >30 seconds' duration of an absolute arrhythmia without detectable P waves and without a pattern more consistent with an alternative diagnosis. These diagnostic criteria have been reported previously. 10 Furthermore, serial venous blood sampling was performed at admission (0 hour), at 6 hours, 24 hours, and at 3 months after discharge. Blood samples drawn from an antecubital vein were centrifuged, and EDTA plasma samples were stored at −80 °C until analysis. Patients who did not present with AF at admission were asked for their consent to perform a 7‐day Holter‐ECG. Holter monitors (CardioMem CM 3000, getemed, Germany) were applied and analyzed by trained study personnel. Briefly summarized, prolonged Holter‐ECG revealed an increased number of AFs and led to a substantial change in therapy toward oral anticoagulation. 10

We also recruited 87 apparently health blood donors as a control group. Blood donors were included after they had given their informed consent. Their individual health status was assessed by a structured questionnaire that was filled at the time of blood donation. Individuals with underlying cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease were excluded. Residual blood samples taken during regular routine blood chemistry checks from an antecubital vein were centrifuged, and EDTA plasma samples were stored at −80 °C until analysis according to a separate approval by the Ethics Committee of the Hamburg Board of Physicians (2022‐300 225‐WF). Controls were individually matched according to sex and age to Find‐AF study participants; however, due to age restrictions for blood donors, we were unable to include controls aged ≥75 years.

Quantification of ADMA, SDMA, L‐Arginine, and Related Metabolites by Ultra‐Performance Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry

ADMA, SDMA, L‐arginine, L‐citrulline, and L‐ornithine were quantified in plasma samples and in conditioned cell culture media using an ultra‐performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC‐MS/MS) method established in our laboratory as described elsewhere. 11 Briefly, 25 μL of plasma or conditioned medium were diluted in 100 μL methanol to which stable isotope labeled internal standards had been added. Subsequently, the compounds were converted into their butyl ester derivatives. Quantification of the analytes was performed on a Waters UPLC‐MS/MS platform (Xevo TQ‐S cronos, Waters GmbH, Eschborn, Germany) applying an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1×50 mm, 1.7 μm, Waters GmbH) for chromatographic separation. The coefficient of variation for the quality control samples was <6% for all analyzed compounds. The investigators performing and analyzing the mass spectrometric measurements were blinded to the clinical status of the patients.

Assessment of Protein Methylation and L‐Arginine‐Related Metabolite Concentrations in Induced Pluripotent Stem Cell‐Derived Cardiomyocytes In Vitro

Human induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived cardiomyocyte (iPSC‐CM) lines (iWTD2.1/2.3, isWT7.22, iBM76.3) used in this study were reprogrammed from somatic cells of 3 healthy individuals (approval number of the Ethics Committee Göttingen: 21/1/11 and 10/9/15) and characterized previously. 12 , 13 The iPSCs were cultured in E8 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and directed differentiation of iPSCs into iPSC‐CMs was described in detail previously. 5 , 12 From day 8 post initiation of differentiation spontaneously beating iPSC‐CMs were cultivated in RPMI/27 medium, composed of RPMI 1640 with Glutamax and HEPES supplemented with 2% B27 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 56 to 60 days, iPSC‐CMs were replated into Geltrex‐coated 6‐well plates at a density of 0.6 million cells/well and recovered in RPMI/B27 medium for 7 days. 13 , 14 Cells were randomly subjected to electric field stimulation (2 milliseconds pulse duration, 10 V) at 0.5 Hz (control) or 2 Hz (to mimic tachyarrhythmia) for 72 hours using a C‐Pace EP (Ionoptix). Afterwards, cell pellets were harvested and frozen at −80 °C for protein analysis, and conditioned media of the iPSC‐CMs from the past 48 hours were collected and immediately frozen at −80 °C until analysis of L‐arginine‐related metabolites by UPLC‐MS/MS.

Protein Methylation Analysis By SDS‐PAGE and Western Blot

Cells were lysed in TritonX100/Proteinase Inhibitor Cocktail (cOmplete, EDTA‐free Proteinaseinhibitor tablets, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer's instructions. A total of 25 μg of total protein containing supernatant was loaded onto a 10% polyacrylamide gel and denaturing SDS‐PAGE was performed. Subsequently, proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane for 25 minutes at 0.3 A/15 V in a semidry Western blotting system (Bio‐Rad Laboratories Inc., Hercules, CA). The membrane was blocked for 1 hour at room temperature in 3% milk. Symmetric and asymmetric methylation was detected by the methylation‐specific antibodies SYM10 (07‐412, Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and ASYM24 (07‐414, Sigma‐Aldrich), respectively. Both antibodies were used in a 1:500 dilution. A horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated goat‐anti‐rabbit IgG (A0545, Sigma‐Aldrich) was used as a second antibody according to the manufacturer's instruction. Protein methylation was detected by measuring chemiluminescence using a Chemidoc XRS+ system (Bio‐Rad Laboratories Inc.).

Statistical Analysis

We calculated the ratio of L‐arginine over ADMA as an index of NOS (NO synthase) substrate, as well as the ratio of L‐citrulline over L‐arginine as a potential surrogate of NOS activity and the ratio of L‐ornithine over L‐arginine as a potential surrogate of arginase activity. All biomarker concentrations are given as mean±SD. Differences in biomarker concentrations between patients and controls and between subgroups of patients according to stroke severity or AF were calculated by Mann–Whitney U test for the comparison of 2 groups, or by ANOVA with adjustment of P for multiple testing (Dunnett's multiple comparisons test) when more than 2 groups or time points were compared. Receiver‐operated curve analyses were performed to analyze the associations of SDMA and ADMA with the risk of having previously undetected AF. The Youden index was calculated to assess the optimal biomarker cutoff. 15 Bootstrapping was performed for internal validation of the cutoff value, and sensitivity and specificity are reported including their 95% CIs. Metabolite concentrations in vitro were compared for statistically significant differences by the Mann–Whitney U test. All data are presented as mean±SD. Statistics were performed using SPSS (version 27; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and GraphPad Prism (version 6.01, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). A P<0.05 after adjustment for multiple testing was considered significant.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

This analysis included 280 patients from the original Find‐AF cohort after exclusion of 1 patient with missing biomarker data. Of these, 155 (55%) were men. Patients presented with TIA (n=87 [31.1%]), minor stroke (n=68 [24.3%]), or major stroke (n=117 [41.8%]); in 8 patients (2.8%) no cerebral ischemia could be confirmed. A total of 202 patients had neither AF at admission nor on 7‐day Holter‐ECG during the trial (72.1%); in 29 patients who presented in sinus rhythm at admission, AF was found during 7‐day Holter‐ECG (10.4%), and in 5 patients who presented with sinus rhythm at admission, 7‐day Holter‐ECG was inconclusive. Association of Plasma Concentrations of L‐Arginine and Its Metabolites With Atrial Fibrillation at Admission

Patients who presented with AF at admission had significantly higher SDMA plasma concentrations than patients who presented in sinus rhythm (median [interquartile range] 0.590 [0.520–0.801] versus 0.585 [0.447–0.697] μmol/L; P=0.020; Table 2). Patients with AF also had significantly higher ADMA (0.632 [0.558–0.724] versus 0.585 [0.527–0.645] μmol/L; P=0.016) and lower L‐arginine concentrations (38.1 [26.3–61.4] versus 49.0 [35.1–70.2] μmol/L; P=0.036); consequently, the median L‐arginine/ADMA ratio was significantly lower (61.9 [48.9–101.1] versus 85.1 [57.5–125.0], P=0.004) (Table 2). When we adjusted the regression analysis of SDMA with AF for age, SDMA remained significantly associated with the presence of AF (P=0.027), whereas the association of ADMA with AF was lost (P=0.286).

Predictive Value of SDMA for Detection of Previously Unrecognized AF in Patients Admitted to Hospital in Sinus Rhythm

We further analyzed if the plasma concentrations of L‐arginine and its metabolites at admission to hospital were associated with newly detected AF during 7‐day Holter monitoring. This prospective analysis was restricted to 195 patients with biomarker levels available and no AF at admission. We found a significant difference in baseline SDMA concentration between patients who were identified as having newly detected AF in Holter‐ECG versus those not having AF (0.718±0.390 versus 0.595±0.268 μmol/L; P=0.042; Figure 1B). By contrast, no significant difference was found for ADMA in patients with or without AF in Holter‐ECG (0.591±0.102 versus 0.606±0.106 μmol/L; P=0.492; Figure 1C). None of the other metabolites or metabolite ratios were significantly associated with the detection of AF during Holter monitoring (data not shown).

Receiver‐operated curve analysis was performed for SDMA and ADMA for newly detected AF in 7‐day Holter monitoring in this prospective cohort. SDMA had an area under the curve of 0.676 (0.530–0.823; P=0.029; Figure 2A), whereas ADMA was unable to discriminate patients at risk of previously undetected AF (area under the curve=0.490, P=0.911). The positive and negative predictive values were 0.028 (0.006–0.081) and 0.877 (0.783–0.934), respectively. The optimal SDMA cutoff to discriminate patients with or without previously undetected AF was 0.571 μmol/L with a sensitivity of 0.786 (0.492–0.953) and a specificity of 0.572 (0.497–0.647).

A total of 107 patients (54.9%) had low SDMA plasma concentration (≤0.571 μmol/L) with a mean of 0.459±0.064 μmol/L, as opposed to 88 patients (45.1%) who had high SDMA with a mean of 0.800±0.341 μmol/L (P<0.001). Among 14 patients with newly diagnosed AF in 7‐day Holter monitoring after stroke, 11 patients had high but only 3 patients had low baseline SDMA (P=0.008; Figure 2B).

The odds ratio for patients admitted with sinus rhythm and SDMA plasma concentration >0.571 μmol/L to have unrecognized AF was 2.98 (1.09–8.20; P=0.0315). The sensitivity to detect previously unrecognized AF in patients with SDMA >0.571 was 0.579, the specificity was 0.684, and positive and negative predictive values were 0.946 and 0.144, respectively.

Time Course of Plasma Metabolites

We finally analyzed the time course of biomarker plasma concentrations during the first 24 hours of inpatient treatment as well as 3 months after discharge. We found that SDMA plasma concentration increased during the first 24 hours of inpatient treatment in all patients; SDMA plasma concentration was significantly higher in patients with AF at admission and in patients with AF recognized in Holter monitoring than in patients with sinus rhythm throughout the study (Figure 3A). Accordingly, there was a significant effect of groups with AF on SDMA in 2‐way ANOVA (P<0.0001) and a significant effect of time on SDMA (P=0.038), with no significance of the interaction term of group and time (P=0.458). By contrast, ADMA plasma concentration was significantly higher only in patients with AF at admission; its concentration did not further increase during hospital treatment (Figure 3B). Accordingly, 2‐way ANOVA showed a significant effect only of groups with AF (P=0.0002) but not of time (P=0.080) on ADMA plasma concentration and no significant effect of the interaction of group and time (P=0.605). At 3 months after discharge, there were no longer any significant differences in SDMA or ADMA plasma concentrations between patient subgroups.

Effect of Rapid Pacing on Protein Methylation and Dimethylarginine Release in iPSC‐Derived Human Cardiomyocytes In Vitro

We observed elevated staining of total cellular proteins for symmetric dimethylation of L‐arginine in iPSC‐CMs stimulated at 2.0 Hz when compared with cells cultured under identical conditions but stimulated at 0.5 Hz, whereas no clear difference was detected for asymmetric dimethylation (Figure 4A). Parallel analysis of conditioned media by UPLC‐MS/MS showed a significantly higher release of SDMA by the rapidly paced iPSC‐CMs, whereas no significant differences were observed for ADMA (Figure 4B), L‐arginine, L‐citrulline, and L‐ornithine (data not shown).

DISCUSSION

The present study shows that patients presenting as emergencies with signs of cerebral ischemia have elevated plasma concentrations of ADMA and SDMA, 2 dimethylated L‐arginine derivatives that interfere with NO production. The plasma concentrations of SDMA but not ADMA were higher with increasing severity of ischemic stroke. This observation could be explained by higher SDMA plasma concentrations in patients with AF, a major risk factor for ischemic stroke. Most important, in patients with ischemic stroke or TIA presenting in sinus rhythm and with no known history of AF, elevated SDMA concentrations were prospectively associated with AF detected during 7‐day Holter‐ECG. Finally, rapid pacing of iPSC‐CMs in vitro caused a selective increase of symmetric dimethylation of total cellular proteins and release of SDMA into conditioned media.

The analyses in our present study confirm previous results of our group 8 , 9 by showing elevated SDMA plasma concentrations in patients with ischemic stroke. By comparison with an age‐ and sex‐matched healthy control group, we found that ADMA and SDMA concentrations were significantly higher; L‐arginine concentration was lower and L‐ornithine was higher in patients with stroke. These differences were maintained throughout all AF subgroups of patients with stroke, and they resulted in a significant and major reduction of L‐arginine/ADMA ratio in patients with stroke including their AF subgroups.

In more detailed analyses, we found that the association of SDMA with the severity of acute cerebral ischemia was related to the significantly higher number of patients with AF among those with major stroke as compared with patients with TIA or minor stroke. As AF is a major risk factor of ischemic stroke but not of myocardial infarction, this association of SDMA with AF might explain the differential clinical risk factor profile of SDMA (mainly with cerebrovascular ischemic disease) as compared with ADMA (mainly with cardiovascular ischemic disease). We noted that baseline SDMA was increased likewise in patients with AF present at hospital admission and in those who presented in sinus rhythm but had previously unrecognized AF detected during 7‐day Holter‐ECG. By contrast, ADMA was solely elevated in patients with AF present at admission but not in those with AF detected during Holter monitoring. In line with our previously published observations in healthy reference cohorts, 16 , 17 both SDMA and ADMA correlated significantly with age. In multivariate regression analyses, the association of ADMA with AF was lost in a model including age, whereas that of SDMA retained significance, further underlining the relevance of the association of SDMA with AF.

Previous studies have analyzed the concentrations of ADMA and SDMA in patients with AF; however, as these studies had cross‐sectional designs, they allowed the establishment of associations of dimethylarginine concentrations only with present AF. Both ADMA and SDMA were elevated in a cross‐sectional analysis of individuals with AF within the population‐based Gutenberg Health Study. 18 In a study of patients with anticoagulated AF in the ARISTOTLE (Apixaban for Reduction in Stroke and Other Thromboembolic Events in Atrial Fibrillation) trial, both ADMA and SDMA were prospectively associated with mortality. 19 A study of 241 patients admitted with AF revealed significantly elevated SDMA plasma concentration in AF as compared with published reference cohorts. 20 In the latter study, restoration of sinus rhythm was associated with significant reduction in ADMA and SDMA concentrations. However, neither ADMA nor SDMA was prospectively associated with incident AF during up to 10 years of follow‐up in the Framingham Offspring study. 21

The main question of Find‐AF was to assess the percentage of patients with stroke with paroxysmal AF shortly after stroke. This usually leads to a switch of antiplatelet therapy to anticoagulation. 22 The Find‐AF trial showed that 7 days of extended Holter monitoring allowed the detection of a significant number of otherwise unrecognized AF cases, which leads to a relevant change in therapy toward anticoagulation. 10 However, if this translates into a reduction of recurrent ischemic strokes is unknown and investigated in the ongoing Find‐AF2 trial. 23 We analyzed here whether SDMA or ADMA might be suitable biomarkers to identify patients presenting with cerebral ischemia who have a high probability of previously unknown AF. We found that an elevated plasma concentration of SDMA, but not ADMA, above a cutoff threshold of 0.571 μmol/L allowed the prediction of the presence of previously unknown AF. Although this association of SDMA with unrecognized AF was moderate (area under the curve=0.676), SDMA may be a useful biomarker aiding in the decision of Holter equipment allocation to patients with stroke. An observation similar to ours has been reported by Ziegler and coauthors 24 in a smaller patient cohort. These investigators determined a cutoff concentration for SDMA of 0.515 μmol/L to discriminate patients with previously undetected AF versus sinus rhythm.

In the present study, we further aimed at better understanding the molecular mechanisms behind the elevation of ADMA and, specifically, SDMA in AF. In a rapid pacing model of iPSC‐CMs, we showed increased symmetric dimethylation of total cellular protein in parallel with an increased release of SDMA into the conditioned media. This experimental finding strongly suggests that tachyarrhythmia may cause upregulation of the activity and possibly the expression of certain protein arginine methyltransferases that mediate symmetric dimethylation of cellular proteins. PRMTs are a family of at least 9 distinct enzymes; PRMT5 and PRMT9 have been shown to induce symmetric demethylation which ultimately leads to release of SDMA upon protein turnover of dimethylated proteins, whereas PRMTs 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 8 induce asymmetric dimethylation of proteins and, accordingly, release of ADMA (for review, cf. 1 , 25 ). Remarkably, protein arginine methylation has originally been thought to be a posttranslational process exclusively affecting histones and other transcription‐regulatory proteins; only recently has arginine methylation of nonhistone proteins been described. 26 Based on the results of our present study, we speculate that (symmetric) dimethylation of proteins involved in the development or maintenance of tachyarrhythmia in cardiomyocytes may occur during AF episodes. Possible targets for such posttranslational modification might be calcium‐handling proteins or ion channels. Further studies will be needed to assess whether inhibition of protein arginine methylation modulates the electrical activity of cardiomyocytes.

Our study included a comparatively high number of patients with cerebral ischemia. We applied state‐of‐the‐art techniques by using 7‐day Holter monitoring to detect previously unrecognized AF and by using UPLC‐MS/MS for biomarker quantitation. Further, we performed additional in vitro experiments to find hints toward potential biochemical mechanisms for the elevation of SDMA in AF. Nonetheless, our study is limited by the fact that we were unable to fully match patients with stroke with healthy controls due to the unavailable of healthy blood donors of advanced age. Multivariate adjustment of the regression analyses of SDMA and ADMA with AF was performed to partly overcome this limitation. There are options to analyze SDMA and ADMA with less sophisticated equipment than that used in our study, for example, enzyme immunoassay. This will allow others to more easily reproduce and possibly extend our current findings.

CONCLUSIONS

Our core finding from this study, the predictive role of elevated SDMA to assist in the detection of previously unrecognized AF in patients admitted with clinical signs of acute cerebral ischemia, may allow biomarker analysis to be added to clinical patient checkup in order to more easily and rapidly identify the cause of stroke of unknown origin in patients presenting in sinus rhythm. Our data suggest that patients with stroke with elevated SDMA concentration at admission may specifically benefit from extended Holter‐ECG monitoring. Thus, in combination with prolonged Holter monitoring, this biomarker may thus help to optimize patient management; whether it may stand alone in the absence of sufficient equipment for prolonged Holter monitoring and how SDMA may be positioned relative to other potential biomarkers remains to be determined in future clinical studies.
