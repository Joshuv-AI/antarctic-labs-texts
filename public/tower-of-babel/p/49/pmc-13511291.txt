Effect of Implementing a Dashboard With or Without a Best Practice Alert on HLA‐B*58:01 Testing Rates Among Allopurinol Users at VA Medical Centers
Authors: Abimbola Fadairo‐Azinge, Gabriela Schmajuk, Sristi Sharma, Colleen P. Flanagan, Sara Faghihi‐Kashani, Ning Zhang, Mary A. Whooley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe American College of Rheumatology recommends HLA‐B*58:01 allele testing before the initiation of allopurinol, specifically among Asian and African American/Black patients, due to their increased risk for severe hypersensitivity reactions. However, testing rates remain low at many health care facilities. This study aimed to determine whether the introduction of a clinician‐facing dashboard, with or without a best practice alert (BPA), would increase HLA‐B*58:01 testing rates among eligible patients at two VA medical centers.MethodsIn October 2022, we launched a clinician‐facing dashboard that displayed HLA‐B*58:01 testing results for all patients prescribed allopurinol at two VA medical centers. In January 2023, we added a synchronous BPA that appeared at one of the two medical centers whenever a provider entered a prescription for allopurinol. The BPA was presented as blue text on the medication order entry form and stated “HLA‐B*58:01 genotyping recommended before rx for Asian or African American.” Using data from the electronic health record, we compared the change in proportion of patients receiving allopurinol prescriptions who had HLA‐B*58:01 genotype testing done after implementation of the dashboard (±BPA) at the two VA medical centers.ResultsFrom October 2022 through December 2023, the number of Asian or African American/Black patients who filled one or more prescriptions for allopurinol was 262 at the BPA + dashboard site and 330 at the dashboard only site. The percentage of Asian or African American/Black patients who had HLA‐B*58:01 testing before or during the month allopurinol was prescribed increased from 8.8% in October 2022 to 35.5% in December 2023 at the dashboard + BPA site and from 2.1% to 4.5% at the dashboard only site (P < 0.0001 for difference in difference). The cumulative percentage of prescribers whose patient(s) completed HLA‐B*58:01 testing increased from 11.7% to 46.8% at the dashboard + BPA site and from 6.9% to 10.8% at the dashboard only site (P < 0.0001 for difference in difference).ConclusionImplementing a dashboard plus simple text BPA was associated with a greater increase in the proportion of patients who completed guideline‐recommend HLA‐B*58:01 testing compared to implementing a dashboard alone.

INTRODUCTION

Allopurinol is a commonly prescribed medication for the treatment of gout. However, patients carrying the HLA‐B*58:01 allele are at increased risk of developing severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARs) such as Stevens–Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis. 1 The mortality rate of allopurinol‐induced SCARs can be as high as 25%. 2 Individuals that report Asian and African American/Black descent have a particularly high prevalence of the HLA‐B*58:01 allele (7.4% and 3.8%, respectively) compared with those who identify as White (<1%). 1 , 3 The American College of Rheumatology (ACR) guidelines recommend HLA‐B*58:01 allele testing before initiating allopurinol treatment in patients of Asian or African American/Black descent. 4

Despite these recommendations, testing rates at major medical centers have remained low. 5 Previous studies have shown that electronic health record (EHR)–based interventions, such as dashboards and best practice alerts (BPAs), can influence clinician behavior and improve adherence to guidelines. 6 This study aimed to determine the effectiveness of implementing a dashboard alone or a dashboard combined with a BPA in increasing HLA‐B*58:01 testing rates at two VA medical centers.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The Veterans Health Administration (VHA) is the largest integrated health care system in the United States, providing comprehensive medical services to over 9 million veterans across its network of medical centers and outpatient clinics. The VHA operates as a closed health care system, meaning that patient care, including prescriptions, is primarily managed within the network. All prescriptions ordered by VA providers are processed and dispensed through VA pharmacies, ensuring a complete and centralized record of medication use.

The VA health care system uses the Computerized Patient Record System as its EHR, which allows for standardized documentation, order entry, and clinical decision support across all VA facilities. The closed nature of the system, combined with robust prescription tracking, ensures high data reliability for assessing medication prescribing patterns and adherence.

This quality improvement project was conducted at the San Francisco and Palo Alto VA Medical Centers. In October 2022, a clinician‐facing dashboard was launched at two VA medical centers. This web‐based dashboard can be viewed at any time and is not embedded in the EHR. The dashboard was viewable within the VA intranet. The dashboard displayed HLA‐B*58:01 testing results for all patients prescribed allopurinol and compared the proportion of patients who had or had not completed testing at each facility with the national average (Supplemental Figure 1A). When the dashboard was launched, links to educational information and a recorded webinar were provided via SharePoint. Use of the dashboard was optional for providers.

At one of the medical centers, a synchronous clinical alert was implemented in January 2023. When clinicians entered an electronic order for allopurinol, they would encounter a message saying “HLA‐B*58:01 genotyping recommended before rx for Asian or African American” patients (Supplemental Figure 1B). The message was truncated in this way because of limits on the number of characters. The BPA was purely informational; it did not require any acknowledgment or further action.

Data from both medical centers were collected from the VHA Clinical Data Warehouse during the 12 months before and following implementation of the BPA. Included patients were veterans alive during the study period that self‐identified as of Asian or African American/Black descent and had an active prescription for allopurinol. Race and ethnicity were determined by self‐selecting from a fixed set of categories. Patients were excluded if we could not verify Asian or African American/Black descent.

During each month, we calculated the proportion of patients receiving allopurinol prescriptions at each facility for whom HLA‐B*58:01 genotype testing had been completed. We used difference‐in‐differences analysis to compare the change in proportion of patients being prescribed allopurinol who had completed HLA‐B*58:01 testing between October 2022 (when the dashboard was launched) and December 2023 (12 months after the BPA was launched) at the two medical centers. We also examined the subset of patients receiving new prescriptions, defined as the absence of any previous allopurinol prescription recorded in the VA medical record. Basic characteristics of the two facilities, including facility complexity, types of prescribers, and types of patients being prescribed allopurinol, were compared using t‐tests for continuous variables and chi‐square tests for dichotomous variables. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

From January 2022 through December 2023, the number of Asian or African American/Black patients who filled one or more prescriptions for allopurinol was 262 at the dashboard + BPA site and 330 at the dashboard only site. The two sites were similar in complexity, patient volume, location, and academic affiliation. As compared with the dashboard alone site, the dashboard + BPA site had a higher percentage of African American/Black patients and a lower percentage of Asian patients (Table 1). Patients at the dashboard + BPA site were slightly older and more likely to have had HLA‐B*58:01 testing before the study (8.8% vs 2.1%; P = 0.0002).

Between October 2022 and December 2023, the cumulative percentage of prescribers whose patient(s) completed HLA‐B*58:01 testing increased from 11.7% to 46.8% at the dashboard + BPA site and from 6.9% to 10.8% at the dashboard only site (P < 0.0001; Figure 1A). Allopurinol prescribers at the dashboard + BPA facility were more likely to order at least one HLA‐B*58:01 test during the observation period than those at the dashboard only medical center (46.8% vs 10.8%, P < 0.00001; Figure 1B). There were five patients (four at dashboard + BPA site and one at dashboard only site) who had positive tests during the study period, and in all five patients, allopurinol was discontinued following the test.

DISCUSSION

This study found that implementation of a simple text BPA increased the proportion of Asian and African American/Black patients on allopurinol who received guideline‐recommended HLA‐B*58:01 testing. The improvement in testing rates was substantially greater at a BPA + dashboard medical center than at a dashboard only medical center. In addition, the simple text BPA increased the proportion of allopurinol prescribers who ordered HLA‐B*58:01 genotyping. Taken together, these results demonstrate that a simple text BPA visible on a medication order entry screen provided a timely reminder to clinicians without requiring any extra clicks and emphasized the importance of following ACR guidelines. 4

The increased testing rates observed at the BPA medical center suggest that synchronous tools that provide immediate prompts may be more effective in changing provider behavior compared to tools that require clinicians to seek out information independently. Further, the significant improvement associated with the roll‐out of the clinical alert suggests that this effect outweighs that of an asynchronous dashboard. These findings align with prior studies that have shown EHR‐based clinical alerts to be effective in improving adherence to evidence‐based practices when delivered at the point of care. 7 Health systems that are investing in clinical decision support tools may find these alerts more impactful than an asynchronous tool. Alternatively, increased interoperability within EHR systems may unlock greater potential from asynchronous dashboards.

The implications of this study are particularly relevant for health systems seeking to optimize the use of clinical decision support tools. Future research should focus on evaluating the long‐term outcomes of implementing such interventions, including the impact on patient safety and adverse event reduction, as well as exploring the potential for similar approaches in other areas of clinical practice. Additionally, we hope to expand this alert to other VHA sites to further its positive effect on veterans.

Some limitations of this study include the self‐identification of race, which may have led to inappropriate exclusion of “mixed race” individuals for whom we could not verify Asian or African American/Black descent. Also, although the two sites had similar characteristics, variations in provider practices between the locations may have contributed to differences in outcomes. For example, greater engagement with the dashboard (at the dashboard + BPA site than at the dashboard only site) could have contributed to the increased screening rates at the dashboard + BPA site. Because this was an observational study, we cannot exclude the possibility that greater engagement with the dashboard resulted in higher rates of testing.

In summary, the combination of a clinician‐facing dashboard and a synchronous BPA was associated with a significant increase in HLA‐B*58:01 testing rates among high‐risk patients prescribed allopurinol compared to the dashboard alone. These findings suggest that, BPAs, when used in conjunction with dashboards, can effectively influence clinician behavior and improve adherence to guideline‐recommended testing, ultimately enhancing patient safety. These findings have implications beyond HLA‐B*58:01 testing and could possibly be used in broader clinical settings across other specialties.
