Effects of dietary supplementation with copra meal on growth performance, meat quality, and intestinal health of Wenchang chicken
Authors: Kun Ouyang, LiMin Wei, ChengJun Hu, Qi Qi, Jie Liu, Ruiping Sun, GuiPing Zhao, Yongliang Zhang, QuanWei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe purpose of this study was to investigate the effects of dietary supplementation with copra meal on growth performance, meat quality, and intestinal development in Wenchang chickens. A total of 480 healthy, 80-day-old female Wenchang chickens were randomly allocated to 4 groups, each with 6 replicates of 20 birds. The chickens in the control group (Con) received a basal diet, whereas the 3 experimental groups were fed the basal diet supplemented with 1.5%, 3.0%, or 6.0% copra meal for 40 days. The results indicated that the 1.5% group exhibited higher average daily gain (ADG) and lower feed-to-gain ratio (F/G) compared with the Con group. Additionally, dietary supplementation with 1.5%, 3.0%, or 6.0% copra meal reduced drip loss in both thigh and breast muscles. In the 1.5%, 3.0% and 6.0% group, the contents of C14:0 were significantly induced, and C14:1 were significantly increased in thigh muscle, compared with the Con group. Moreover, the 6.0% group demonstrated significantly greater villus height and villus-height-to-crypt-depth ratio in the jejunum, along with higher levels of propionic and butyric acids in the ileum. At the genus level, Turicibacter abundance was significantly lower in the 6.0% group, whereas Bacteroides and Barnesiella were significantly higher. These findings suggest that dietary supplementation with 6.0% copra meal reduces F/G and enhances meat quality and intestinal development in Wenchang chickens.Keywords: Copra meal, Wenchang chicken, Growth performance, Meat quality, Intestinal health

Introduction

The broiler farming industry is an important component of global animal husbandry and one of the key industries to ensure human protein supply. Chicken meat, a valuable source of protein, is well-received by consumers, accounting for over 40% of global meat consumption, it plays an irreplaceable role in global food security [1, 2]. Due to the shortage of feed ingredients, key components in chicken diets such as corn and soybean meal, are largely dependent on imports in China, causing feed costs to increase. Therefore, developing new feed resources is essential for the poultry industry’s long-term growth.

The growing global demand for coconut oil has resulted in large quantities of copra meal. It was reported that production of this by-product surpassed 1.98 million metric tons (MMT) in 2023 [3]. Copra meal is rich in fiber, fats, and amino acids, and the average crude protein is 17.15%, crude fat is 22.39%, crude fiber is 16.20% [4]. Moreover, the ileal digestible energy, metabolizable energy, and nitrogen-corrected metabolizable energy values of copra meal in broiler chickens were 10.27, 15.35, and 14.61 MJ/kg, respectively [5]. Study reported that no effect of diluting commercial broiler feed with 5% Copra meal on carcass traits [6]. These findings suggest that copra meal can be a valuable source of energy and protein in animal diets. Evidence has shown that copra meal plays a key role in regulating body metabolism, including reducing blood lipids and cholesterol, as well as inhibiting hyperlipidemia [7, 8]. Furthermore, dietary supplementation with copra meal has been reported to decrease abdominal fat and feed costs in chickens [9]. However, copra meal contains a high concentration of dietary fiber, especially mannan, galactomannan and cellulose, and mannan accounts for 30% of non-starch polysaccharides [4].This increases the viscosity of digestates and impairs the digestion of nutrients, making it difficult to use on a large scale in chicken feed [5]. If copra meal is added to broiler feed in quantities of more than 10%, this leads to a significant reduction in average daily weight gain and feed conversion efficiency [9]. Wenchang Chicken is a local excellent broiler breed in Hainan Province, China. It belongs to slow yellow feathered broiler, reach a market weight of 1.8 ~ 2.0 kg after a 120-day breeding cycle [10], and it has the characteristics of rough feeding resistance and heat resistance [11]. Further research is needed to determine whether Wenchang chickens can efficiently utilize miscellaneous meal diets and unconventional local feedstuffs. In addition, copra meal impact on meat quality and intestinal development in Wenchang chickens remains unclear.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate the optimal dosage of copra meal in the diet of Wenchang Chicken and its effect on meat quality and intestinal health, aiming to provide further insights for incorporating copra meal in broiler diets.

Materials and methods

Animals and experimental design

Copra meal was sourced from a local oil extraction company (Yongfa Oil Extraction Plant, Hainan). Its main nutritional components include 16.31% crude protein, 13.30% crude fat, 15.20% crude fiber, 0.11% calcium, and 0.43% phosphorus. All the Wenchang chickens were sourced from Hainan (Tanniu) Wenchang Chicken Co., Ltd.

A total of 480 healthy, 80-day-old female Wenchang chickens were randomly assigned to 4 groups, each with 6 replicates of 20 birds per replicate. The control group (CON) was fed a basal diet, whereas the 3 experimental groups were offered a basal diet supplemented with 1.50%, 3.0%, or 6.0% copra meal, respectively. Diet composition and nutrient levels are shown in Table 1. All chickens were housed in cages with 12 h of light per day, and feed and water were provided ad libitum. Daily feed intake was recorded. The experiment lasted for 40 days, during which initial and final body weights of all the chickens were measured. The average daily weight gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed-to-gain ratio (F/G) were then calculated.

Sample collection

At 120 days of age, two chickens near the average body weight in each replicate were selected for sample collection, the chicken was euthanized by cervical dislocation after blood collection from the wing vein. The left breast and thigh muscles were collected and promptly frozen at −80 °C for further analyses. Approximately 2 g of jejunum and ileum contents were collected, transferred into sterile 2 mL tubes, and stored at −80 °C. And the jejunum and ileum were also harvested and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, then stored at −80 °C.

Serum biochemical parameters

Serum biochemical parameters were determined using an automatic biochemical analyzer (LABOSPECT008AS, HITACHI, Japan). The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), plasma glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), malondialdehyde (MDA), and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) were analyzed using spectrophotometric kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Biotechnology Institute, Nanjing, China).

Meat quality

The pH values of breast and thigh muscles were measured at 45 min postmortem (pH 45 min) and after 24 h (pH 24 h) using a FE28 Standard pH meter (METTLER TOLEDO, Zurich, Switzerland). To determine drip loss, approximately 30 g of fresh breast and thigh muscle were manually trimmed and weighed at 45 min postmortem, then stored at 4 °C. for 24 h before being reweighed [12]. Drip loss was calculated as follows: drip loss (%) = [(initial weight − final weight)/initial weight] × 100%.

To measure shear force, approximately 250 g of breast and thigh muscle were placed in a plastic bag and cooked to an internal temperature of 75 °C. After cooling the cooked samples to room temperature, they were cut into pieces measuring 1 cm × 1 cm × 2 cm, sheared parallel to the muscle fiber direction as described in a previous study [21]. The shear force (N) of the sample cores was then measured using a C-LM36 digital-display muscle tenderness determination device (Harbin, China).

Inosine monophosphate (IMP), amino acid profiles, and fatty acid

The breast and thigh muscle samples were sliced, vacuum-freeze-dried, and then ground into powder. The inosine monophosphate (IMP) content was measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (LC-1100, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) following the method described by Huang et al. [13]. Briefly, 50 mg of freeze-dried sample were homogenized with 2 mL of phosphate buffer (pH 7.2). Next, 500 µL of acetonitrile were added to the homogenate in a 2 mL centrifuge tube, subjected to ultrasonic extraction for 30 min, and then centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. Chromatographic separation was conducted using an Agilent Technologies ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (model 1,100) system and a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) under specified conditions.

The amino acid profiles in breast and thigh muscles were measured as described previously [14]. Briefly, samples were thawed and minced, and 0.05 g of sample was mixed with 500 µL of a 70% methanol - water solution. The mixture was vortexed for 3 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, then the supernatant was collected. Free amino acid composition was determined by liquid chromatography- mass spectrometry (LC-20AD, Shimadzu, Japan; 5500 Q TRAP LC - MS/MS, AB SCIEX, USA). For fatty acid analysis, 100 mg of tissue were homogenized and 2 mL of n-hexane were added. The mixture was shaken at 50 °C for 30 min, followed by the addition of 3 mL of a 0.4 mol/L KOH–methanol solution. The mixture was then shaken for an additional 30 min at 50 °C. Next, 1 mL of water and 2 mL of n-hexane were added and mixed thoroughly. The solution was allowed to stand until layers separated. The upper layer was collected, and fatty acids were detected using gas chromatography- mass spectrometry (GC-MS).

Intestinal morphology measurements

The jejunum and ileum samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned into 5 μm-thick slices, and subsequently stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Eight fields per slide were captured using a panoramic slide scanner (PANNORAMIC, 3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary). The villus length, crypt depth, and villus height-to-crypt depth (V/C) ratio were measured using ImagePro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics, Maryland, United States) as described by Schneider et al. [15].

Short-Chain fatty acid (SCFA) concentrations

Approximately 0.5 g of cecal content was mixed with 200 µL of 0.5% phosphoric acid (H3PO4) and centrifuged at 25,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was then subjected to ultrasonic disruption at 4 °C for 5 min and subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min. Next, 100 µL of the supernatant was transferred to a new tube and combined with 250 µL of Methyl Tert-Butyl Ether (MTBE) extract containing an internal standard. This mixture was centrifuged again at 25,000 × g for 10 min, followed by ultrasonic disruption at 4 °C for 5 min, and a final centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting supernatant was collected and analyzed using gas chromatography - mass spectrometry (GC-MS/MS) as described by Lotti [16].

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) were analyzed using an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph coupled to a 7000D mass spectrometer with a DB-FFAP column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm film thickness, J&W Scientific, USA). Helium served as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.2 mL/min. Samples were injected in split mode with a split ratio of 5:1 and an injection volume of 1 µL. The oven temperature was maintained at 50 °C for 1 min, then increased to 220 °C at a rate of 18 °C/min and held for 5 min. All samples were analyzed in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. The injector inlet and transfer line temperatures were set to 250 °C and 230 °C, respectively [17].

Cecal microbial sequencing

Based on previous research findings, dietary supplementation with 6% coconut meal significantly improved both feed conversion ratio and intestinal morphology in Wenchang chickens. Therefore, the control group and the 6% supplementation group were selected for intestinal microbial sequencing analysis. The microbial community composition in the cecal contents of Wenchang chickens was analyzed via high-throughput sequencing performed by Qunati Health Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Cecal contents were placed in Ribonuclease (RNase) - and Deoxyribonuclease (DNase)-free tubes, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then stored at − 80 °C. Genomic DNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and its purity and concentration were verified by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. The DNA was fragmented using a Covaris M220 ultrasonic disruptor, and fragments of approximately 400 bp were selected. Library construction was then conducted using the NEBNext® Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA). Samples were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq Xten platform (Illumina, USA), and Unigenes were mapped to the NCBI NR database using DIAMOND software. Gene abundances were used to calculate species abundance and generate abundance profiles across different taxonomic levels.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference test in GraphPad Prism 9.3. All data are presented as means ± Standard Error of the Mean (SEM), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Copra meal, a byproduct of coconut oil production, has long been used in ruminant feed. However, despite its low cost and wide availability, due to copra meal contains a high concentration of dietary fiber, mainly includes mannan which is non-starch polysaccharides, it remains underutilized in poultry diets, with large portions still going to waste [3]. Therefore, we investigated the effects of copra meal on meat quality and intestinal development in Wenchang chickens, aiming to provide further guidance for its incorporation into broiler diets.

One study reported that dietary supplementation with 5%, 10%, and 15% copra meal in weanling pigs led to a linear decrease (P < 0.05) in final body weight, overall ADG, and ADFI, whereas F/G was unaffected [18]. Another study showed that adding 10–20% copra meal to finisher pig diets improved feed conversion ratio (FCR) compared with a control diet [19], suggesting that copra meal may be suitable for finisher animals. However, in Ross broilers, diets supplemented with more than 10% copra meal decreased weight gain, feed consumption, and feed conversion efficiency [20]. In the present study, supplementing 1.50% copra meal in the diets of 80-day-old Wenchang chickens increased ADG and lowered F/G; notably, the F/G remained significantly reduced in the 6.0% group, and there was also a decrease in the 3.0% group compared with the control. These findings indicate that incorporating up to 6% copra meal may enhance F/G in finisher Wenchang chickens.

Common indicators for evaluating the physicochemical properties of muscles include pH, drip loss, cooking yield, and shear force, all of which are closely related to muscle tenderness and flavor [21]. A previous study reported that copra meal improves meat quality by increasing both the variety and quantity of volatile compounds in chicken meat [22]. In our study, compared with the Con group, the 24 h drip loss in both breast and thigh muscles was significantly reduced in the 1.5%, 3.0%, and 6.0% groups, indicating that copra meal plays a key role in enhancing chicken meat quality. However, few studies have examined the effects of copra meal on chicken meat quality, and further research is needed to explore the underlying mechanisms. In conclusion, adding 1.5% −6% copra meal to the diet of Wenchang Chicken had no negative effect for its muscle physical and chemical properties.

The composition and content of amino acids play a key role in determining the nutritional value and flavor of meat, with higher levels of free amino acids often associated with better meat quality and flavor [23, 24]. Flavor amino acids such as serine, alanine, glycine, and arginine, can serve as precursors that subtly enhance meat flavor [25]. In this study, compared with the Con group, the flavor amino acid profiles in both breast and thigh muscles did not differ significantly in the 1.50%, 3.0%, or 6.0% groups, indicating that adding up to 6.0% copra meal does not negatively affect the nutritional quality or flavor of Wenchang chicken.

Nutritional intervention is an effective way to offer consumers healthier meat, egg, and milk products by modifying the fatty acid profile in livestock and poultry products. Copra meal, a byproduct remaining after coconut endosperm oil extraction, is rich in oil dominated by saturated fatty acids (SFA) [26], lauric acid alone accounts for approximately 44% to 52% [8, 27]. Previous research has shown that the fat content and composition of feed can influence the fatty acid profile in chicken meat [26]. Also, study had shown that dietary fiber (such as mannan) may indirectly change fatty acid metabolism by regulating the structure of intestinal flora [28], and Copra meal is rich in mannan [3]. In this study, supplementing 1.5%, 3.0% and 6% copra meal significantly reduced C14:0 and increased C14:1 in thigh muscle, this indicated that copra meal can change the fatty acid composition of Wenchang Chicken muscle. But the total saturated fatty acids (SFA) and total unsaturated fatty acids (UFA) remained unchanged. Therefore, including 1.50% to 6.0% copra meal in the diet does not adversely affect the fatty acid composition in muscles.

Villus height is intimately linked to nutrient absorption [29], while crypts function as villi factories, producing and rapidly migrating cells to the villi surface to maintain homeostasis [13]. A previous study reported that adding high levels of copra meal did not improve broiler performance but did increase intestinal surface area and support digestive health [30]. Another study showed that copra meal hydrolyzed by mannanase in broiler diets also increased intestinal surface area and enhanced nutrient absorption [31]. Studies have shown that supplementation with the mannan improving the indoleacetic acid, which can fortifies intestinal barrier function via activation of intestinal aryl hydrocarbon receptor [28], also, supplementation with mannan improved the intestinal VH and VH: CD of broiler [32]. In the present study, different levels of copra meal supplementation increased the villus-height-to-crypt-depth ratio (V/C) in both the jejunum and ileum, thus improving nutrient absorption, with the 6.0% group displaying the most pronounced effect, which is consistent with previous results. These findings suggest that copra meal has a favorable impact on intestinal morphology, helping to boost nutrient uptake and facilitate chicken growth.

The abundant fiber in copra meal can be metabolized by gut microbiota to produce SCFAs [33]. Studies indicate that SCFAs (especially butyric acid) can enhance intestinal morphology [34, 35] including increased jejunal villus height and V/C ratio [34]. In this study, we observed that 6.0% copra meal supplementation elevated propionic acid, butyric acid, and caproic acid levels. Therefore, the observed improvements in intestinal morphology and structure also may be attributed to the beneficial effects of copra meal on intestinal SCFAs, which influence enterocyte differentiation and proliferation.

The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in nutrient digestion and immune function [36, 37]. In this study, we used metagenomic sequencing to compare the cecal microbiota composition and structure in Wenchang chickens fed 6.0% copra meal with those fed a control diet. Although alpha diversity did not differ significantly between the two groups, indicating that high-level copra meal supplementation did not affect microbial diversity or richness, and beta diversity analysis showed that the microbial community structure in the 6.0% group was distinctly different from that of the control group. This finding is consistent with Kim et al. [38], who reported that dietary fiber can significantly alter gut microbiota composition. Thus, high-level copra meal addition may reshape the cecal microbiota structure in Wenchang chickens without reducing community diversity.

Previous work has linked elevated Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratios to microbial dysbiosis, which can adversely affect an animal’s physiological state [39, 40]. In a healthy gut, Bacteroides is considered a keystone genus capable of digesting various dietary fibers and producing butyric and propionic acids [41], and it has been shown to increase intestinal villus length [42]. Likewise, Barnesiella has been associated with rendering the intestinal environment less prone to inflammation [43]. In our study, compared with the Con group, the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio and Turicibacter were significantly reduced in the 6.0% group, while Bacteroides and Barnesiella were significantly increased. Our correlation analysis further revealed that Barnesiella abundance positively correlates with propionic and butyric acids, whereas Bacteroides abundance positively correlates with butyric acid. Therefore, supplementing 6.0% copra meal in Wenchang chicken diets beneficially enhances the production of propionic and butyric acids by regulation gut microbiota, thereby improving intestinal morphology.

Conclusion

In summary, our study reveals that that dietary supplementation with 6.0% copra meal increases the growth performance and decreases the drip loss of muscle of Wenchang chicken. Moreover, dietary supplementation with 6.0% copra meal improves the morphology of the jejunum and ileum by regulating intestinal microbiota composition and metabolites. Based on these findings, we recommended that dietary supplementation with 6.0% copra meal is beneficial for 80-day-old Wenchang chicken growth.
