From nonagentic large language models to multiagent systems in emergency medicine: a scoping review
Authors: Hyesung Kim, Seunghoon Jo, Min Hyuk Lim, Dong Hyun Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis scoping review aimed to examine studies of nonagentic large language models (LLMs), LLM-based agents, and multiagent systems in emergency medicine and to identify current research trends and major gaps by analyzing their scope of clinical application, system structures, evaluation approaches, and input data characteristics.MethodsThe Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, and CINAHL were searched for literature published from March 8, 2021, to March 7, 2026. English-language full-text studies published during this period were included if they addressed the application, evaluation, or benchmarking of nonagentic LLMs, LLM-based agents, or multiagent systems in emergency medicine or the emergency department. Additional studies were identified through reference tracking and supplementary searching. In total, 35 studies were analyzed.ResultsOf the 35 included studies, 26 were application studies, 6 were framework studies, and 3 were benchmark studies. Nonagentic LLMs were the most common system type (n=25), followed by LLM-based agents (n=7) and multiagent systems (n=3). Inputs were predominantly text-based, and evaluation mainly relied on expert comparison, retrospective record review, vignette-based comparison, and task-specific performance metrics. In contrast, workflow-level, prospective, and safety- and trustworthiness-oriented evaluations were limited.ConclusionLLMs in emergency medicine have shown potential for task-level decision support and documentation. However, the current literature remains focused on nonagentic LLM-based task support, whereas studies that reflect the dynamic workflow of real emergency departments remain limited. Future research should expand toward workflow-aware design, operational evaluation, multimodal data integration, multiagent-based role coordination, and validation of safety and trustworthiness.

INTRODUCTION

The emergency department (ED) is a high-pressure clinical environment in which limited time, incomplete initial information, simultaneous patient arrivals, and rapidly changing patient conditions coexist. In this setting, clinicians must continuously perform tasks such as triage, diagnostic and treatment decision-making, handoff, discharge, and documentation, all of which can create substantial cognitive and operational burdens [1–4]. Accordingly, emergency medicine is a clinical domain in which rapid, context-adaptive decision support and workflow-level assistance are particularly important.

Recently, an increasing number of studies have explored the use of large language models (LLMs) to support selected tasks in emergency care [5,6]. Some studies have reported the potential utility of LLMs for tasks such as generating clinical recommendations, assessing severity, predicting admission, and drafting handoff and discharge documentation. Others have suggested their applicability to ED decision support, imaging recommendations, patient guidance, and diagnostic support. These early studies generally focused on individual tasks without iterative interaction with the environment; that is, they primarily involved the use of nonagentic LLMs.

More recently, LLM-based agents have been developed to support interactive information gathering and task execution. An LLM agent is a system that uses an LLM to autonomously plan, reason through complex tasks, use memory, and access external tools to achieve specific goals [7–10]. By contrast, multiagent systems involve two or more LLM-based agents with distinct roles, such as a triage agent, a diagnostic agent, and a documentation agent, that collaborate through coordinated interaction to accomplish complex tasks, thereby enabling role division and iterative communication that a single agent cannot achieve alone. These approaches may be better suited to ED settings characterized by pronounced temporal dynamics. Furthermore, some studies have extended this line of work to multiagent systems intended to reflect the division of roles and collaborative structure of the ED at the system level (Fig. 1) [11–15]. Approaches involving collaboration among multiple agents with distinct roles, such as a triage agent and a charting agent, have also been reported [12–17].

The ED is an environment in which sequential decision-making, repeated information updates, interaction across roles, and workflow-level support are tightly interconnected, and decisions regarding testing, diagnosis, and disposition affect not only patient outcomes but also resource allocation and operational efficiency [18]. Nevertheless, most current studies have focused on a limited set of tasks, such as triage, diagnostic or disposition support, and documentation using nonagentic LLMs. In addition, inputs have been largely text-centered, and evaluation has mainly relied on expert comparison, retrospective record review, vignette-based comparison, and task-specific performance metrics [5,6].

Several reviews have described recent trends and potential applications of LLM-based agents and multiagent systems [19–22]. However, these studies have mainly focused on general medical scenarios, and reviews that jointly examine nonagentic LLMs, LLM-based agents, and multiagent systems in the ED context remain limited.

Accordingly, this scoping review aimed to systematically organize studies on nonagentic LLMs, LLM-based agents, and multiagent systems in emergency medicine and to identify their application scope and key characteristics. To this end, the review included not only studies involving ED patients but also simulation and benchmark studies reflecting the ED environment. The core research question was as follows: In emergency medicine and ED settings, to which clinical tasks have these systems been applied, and what are their characteristics in terms of system type, evaluation strategy, input modality, and safety and trustworthiness? In addition, this review aimed to identify recurrent trends, major gaps, and implications for future LLM-based system design in emergency medicine.

METHODS

This scoping review was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews) checklist (Suppl. 1) [23].

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met all of the following criteria: (1) they were directly related to emergency medicine or the ED setting; (2) they addressed the application, evaluation, or benchmarking of LLMs, LLM-based agents, or multiagent systems; (3) they involved ED-related tasks that were directly integrated into, or had clear implications for, ED clinical workflow; (4) they were published in English and available in full text; and (5) they were published from March 8, 2021, to March 7, 2026, a period during which LLM-based approaches emerged and became prominent.

Studies were excluded if they met one or more of the following criteria: (1) the context of emergency medicine or the ED was unclear, or ED data were used solely as a data source for disease-specific prediction or analysis without clear relevance to ED clinical workflow; (2) they addressed traditional machine learning, rule-based systems, or general clinical decision support without an LLM-based structure; (3) they were not original research, including review articles, editorials, commentaries, letters to the editor, conference abstracts, case reports, guidelines, or webpages; (4) they were not published in English or were not available in full text; or (5) they were published before March 8, 2021.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using the Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, and CINAHL. The search strategy was developed using key terms related to emergency medicine, LLMs, LLM-based agents, and multiagent systems and was adapted to the search syntax and indexing structure of each database (Suppl. 2). The final search was performed on March 7, 2026.

To identify relevant studies that were not captured through database searching, the reference lists of candidate included studies were reviewed, and additional manual searches of related topics were conducted as needed.

To ensure objectivity in study selection, two researchers independently reviewed titles, abstracts, keywords, and, when necessary, full texts according to the eligibility criteria. The search and selection process was documented using a PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 2), and bibliographic information and screening records for retrieved studies were managed using Zotero (Digital Scholar), a reference management software. Disagreements arising during the review process were resolved through discussion between the two researchers, and when consensus could not be reached, a final decision was made after additional review.

Data charting for the included studies was performed using predefined extraction items. Two researchers reviewed the main characteristics of each study and organized information relevant to the study objectives in a structured format (Table 1) [24–58]. Extracted items included study role, ED task, input data modality, primary output or function, system type, added features, and evaluation approach. Full texts were rereviewed as needed to supplement the extracted information.

In addition, because studies with the same clinical objective could differ in design and interpretation according to input data type, system structure, and evaluation approach, these items were categorized to facilitate cross-study comparison.

Synthesis and data analysis

To reduce heterogeneity in terminology across studies and ensure consistent classification, this review established operational definitions in advance that prioritized actual functional structure over author-reported labels. Structures that performed tasks without external tool calling or iterative execution were classified as nonagentic LLMs, including not only single-model applications but also noninteractive pipeline-type LLM applications. In contrast, structures that performed tasks while interacting with the environment through retrieval, tool use, planning, memory, or iterative action were classified as LLM-based agents. Structures in which two or more agents engaged in explicit communication and coordination based on role division were defined as multiagent systems. When boundaries were ambiguous, actual functional structure was prioritized over author-reported naming, and rule-based processing, static auxiliary modules, and simple retrieval augmentation were distinguished from agentic behavior and recorded separately as added features.

Study role was classified into three categories: application study, framework study, and benchmark study. Application studies were defined as studies whose primary contribution was the evaluation of performance or usefulness for a specific emergency medicine task. Framework studies were defined as studies whose primary contribution was the design and implementation of system structure, orchestration, and tool integration methods. Benchmark studies were defined as studies whose primary contribution was the development of datasets and benchmark or benchmark-style evaluation frameworks, or the systematic comparison of models. When boundaries were unclear, final classification was determined on the basis of the study’s primary objective and main contribution.

The studies were then narratively synthesized across the categories of clinical applications, system characteristics, evaluation and benchmarking, data modality, and safety and emerging directions. In this process, comparisons focused on relevance to ED tasks and workflows, the composition of system types and added features, the static and dynamic characteristics of evaluation, and feasibility for actual clinical application.

RESULTS

A total of 475 studies were initially identified through database searching. Of these, 274 underwent title- and abstract-level screening, and 41 were selected for full-text review. Thirty-two studies were included through the primary screening process, and three additional relevant studies were identified through reference-list review. Ultimately, 35 studies were included in the final analysis (Fig. 2).

Among the 35 studies, application studies were the most common (n=26), followed by framework studies (n=6) and benchmark studies (n=3). In terms of system type, nonagentic LLMs were the most common (n=25), followed by LLM-based agents (n=7) and multiagent systems (n=3). Inputs were predominantly text-centered, and evaluation mainly consisted of expert comparison, retrospective real-world record evaluation, and vignette-based comparison. In contrast, more extensive evaluation designs, such as prospective evaluation, robustness assessment, and workflow-level evaluation, were identified in only a few studies. Clinical applications

Studies of LLMs in emergency medicine were concentrated on a limited set of tasks, including triage, diagnostic and treatment decision support, patient guidance, and documentation (Table 1) [24–58]. Within the decision support category, recurrent tasks included ED acuity assessment, diagnostic or disposition support, and outpatient guidance [24–31,36–38,48]. For example, several studies evaluated LLMs in a decision support role by presenting clinical vignettes or structured patient data, thereby simulating the function of a clinical decision support system. However, integration with electronic medical records (EMRs) was rarely reported; most studies used curated or synthetic inputs rather than live clinical data streams. In the documentation category, the major applications included handoff note drafting, discharge note generation, discharge summary drafting, and documentation automation [46,47,55]. This distribution indicates that current LLM research in emergency medicine is focused primarily on supporting specific clinical tasks rather than addressing the entire ED process as a single integrated system.

System characteristics

The dominant pattern in current LLM research in emergency medicine was an output-centered structure based on nonagentic LLMs, whereas studies adopting agent-based or multiagent structures were relatively uncommon. Overall, many included studies were designed around nonagentic LLMs that generated outputs such as classifications, suggestions, predictions, or document drafts, indicating that recommendation-oriented design remained central at the system level (Table 1) [24–58].

In contrast, studies incorporating added features or more complex components were relatively limited [30,32,45,46,50–58]. Among these studies, some took the form of LLM-based agents or multiagent systems through the incorporation of retrieval, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG; combining model outputs with external knowledge sources), handbook grounding, web search, RxNorm API integration (US National Library of Medicine), and agent coordination [30,32,50–58]. For example, Han and Choi [54], Lu et al. [52], and Sharma et al. [50] presented multiagent structures involving role division and coordination, whereas Moser et al. [55], Glicksberg et al. [30], Karamanlıoğlu et al. [51], and Niset et al. [32] incorporated retrieval-based or RAG-based augmentation structures. Taken together, the current literature was centered on recommendation-oriented or generative structures based on nonagentic LLMs, whereas studies of agentic architectures incorporating role division, tool integration, and multistep processing remained relatively limited.

Evaluation and benchmarking

Evaluation strategies have become increasingly diverse; however, they remained centered overall on expert comparison, case-based comparison, retrospective record review, and task-specific performance evaluation. Triage-related studies were often evaluated comparatively against widely used validated triage systems or actual ED triage cases [24,25,29,31,36,37,39–43,49–53,56–58]. Studies related to diagnosis or disposition generally used retrospective designs centered on physician comparison, discharge-diagnosis comparison, retrospective case evaluation, or comparison with actual disposition [26,27,30,32,34,35,38,51,57]. In documentation studies, quality was evaluated using methods such as ROUGE (recall-oriented understudy for gisting evaluation; a lexical overlap-based metric), BERTScore (bidirectional encoder representations from transformers score; a semantic similarity-based metric), 4C (coherence, consistency, correctness, and completeness) evaluation, and physician review [45–47,55].

Meanwhile, studies classified as benchmark studies presented more structured evaluation frameworks, including dataset-level benchmarking, baseline comparison, distribution shift, and robustness to missing data [56–58]. This pattern indicates that evaluation in emergency medicine LLM research is being developed in increasingly diverse ways beyond simple accuracy comparisons.

Nevertheless, many studies remained at the stage of validating model performance within static cases or limited task settings. Although some designs were closer to real-world application, such as prospective multicenter evaluation [33], evaluations that broadly reflected the overall ED workflow or operational context were still rare.

Data modalities

Input data were concentrated largely in text-based formats, whereas studies using both structured data and multimodal input were relatively limited. Many studies used text-based inputs such as triage summaries, electronic health record (EHR) notes, clinical vignettes, discharge-related text, and handoff notes [24–29,32,34–41,46–49,52–55]. Overall, the current literature therefore reflects a heavily text-dominant input environment.

Some studies also used structured variables such as vital signs, laboratory results, and triage metadata [30,31,42,43,45,50,51,56–58], whereas only a small number incorporated voice input [33] or combined image and text input [44]. To date, broad integration of speech, imaging, and structured data has been reported only to a limited extent.

Safety and trustworthiness

Safety and trustworthiness were explicitly addressed in only some studies, whereas the overall evaluation was oriented more strongly toward task performance and output quality. Prior work has discussed safety, deferral, and trustworthy agent behavior [59–61], but only a limited number of studies directly evaluated these elements in the context of emergency medicine. For example, individual studies reported on the safety of lay-rescuer guidance for basic and advanced life support [28], triage accuracy and reproducibility [29], omissions or hallucinations in documentation output [47], and patient safety–focused physician review [45]. In contrast, most studies presented performance-centered metrics such as accuracy, agreement, recommendation quality, and documentation qual [24–30,32–49,51–56,58].

Although some studies introduced agent-based or multiagent structures [50,52,54], few explicitly evaluated safety-related behavior, uncertainty handling, traceability, and human oversight in the ED context. Thus, although the literature has expanded beyond nonagentic LLM-based task support to include retrieval-augmented, agent-based, and multiagent directions, studies directly validating safety and trustworthiness in the ED remain limited. DISCUSSION

Summary of evidence

By organizing studies on nonagentic LLMs, LLM-based agents, and multiagent systems reported in the context of emergency medicine and the ED, this scoping review identified overall trends in the current literature with respect to application scope, system structure, evaluation strategy, input data modality, and safety and trustworthiness. The included literature consisted primarily of single task-specific application studies using nonagentic LLMs and focused on a limited set of tasks, such as triage, diagnostic and treatment decision support, patient guidance, and documentation. Input data were predominantly restricted to a single modality, typically text, and evaluations were often limited to narrow, task-specific performance metrics.

This pattern indicates that the current literature remains at a stage of exploring the applicability of LLMs to individual tasks that are relatively comparable and easy to evaluate, rather than addressing the ED as a complex system as a whole. Indeed, many studies examined relatively well-bounded tasks, which may represent a natural developmental pathway during the early stage of evidence accumulation. However, actual ED operations cannot be explained solely by the generation of a correct answer at a single time point; rather, the ED is an environment in which reassessment in response to changes in patient condition, information updates, external tool integration, and coordination across roles occur continuously [2]. Therefore, although the current literature demonstrates the potential for prediction or generation at the level of individual tasks, it still provides only limited evidence regarding continuous workflow support and action-oriented system design.

At the system level, current research has been dominated by static recommendation or generation structures based on nonagentic LLMs. In contrast, studies of agents and multiagent systems involving retrieval, tool use, planning, iterative execution, and role-based coordination have been limited, and research on workflow-level orchestration tailored to the ED remains at an early stage. In other words, LLM research at the system level has continued to be characterized predominantly by output-centered design.

Evaluation mainly relied on case-based comparison, expert agreement, task-specific performance, and benchmark-based evaluation. Time sensitivity, resource constraints, escalation, and traceability, all of which are critical in real-world ED operations, were not adequately reflected. In addition, consideration of safety and trustworthiness was limited. Although some studies addressed the safety of lay-rescuer guidance, triage accuracy and reproducibility, documentation omissions or hallucinations, and patient safety–focused physician review, performance-centered metrics remained dominant overall, and only a small number of studies explicitly evaluated uncertainty handling, traceability, and human oversight in the ED context.

In terms of input data, text-based input was dominant. Many studies used relatively curated text inputs, such as triage summaries, EHR notes, clinical vignettes, handoff notes, and discharge-related text, whereas studies integrating structured data, multimodal input, and time-sensitive signals were relatively limited. Given the expanding use of multimodal generative artificial intelligence (AI) [61], further investigation of multimodal and structured data integration in emergency medicine is warranted.

Taken together, current LLM research in emergency medicine shows meaningful potential at the level of individual tasks, but important gaps remain in continuous workflow support, dynamic system design, operational-level evaluation, multimodal data integration, and the assurance of safety and trustworthiness required for real ED application.

Implications for future ED-specific LLM system design

The gaps identified in this review have implications that extend beyond simply indicating the need for more studies and instead offer more specific guidance regarding the directions in which ED-specific LLM systems should be designed (Fig. 3). First, in terms of clinical application, support structures are needed that extend beyond an individual task-centered approach to provide continuous support from patient reassessment through disposition planning, handoff, and discharge preparation. The current literature has shown a degree of potential for judgment and document generation at specific time points; however, because real-world ED workflows operate in the context of changing patient conditions and ongoing work-process continuity, future systems need to reflect workflow continuity more actively. Beyond these research gaps, practical barriers to real-world ED deployment also warrant attention. These include response latency in time-critical triage scenarios, regulatory considerations surrounding AI-assisted clinical decision-making, and technical challenges involved in integrating LLM outputs into existing EMR workflows.

From the perspective of system structure, a shift is warranted away from nonagentic LLM-centered design toward approaches that reflect workflow-aware agents, dynamic orchestration, and role coordination. For example, a role-based multiagent architecture in which a triage agent handles initial acuity assessment, a diagnostic agent integrates clinical reasoning, and a documentation agent generates structured notes could enable workflow-aware support while maintaining human oversight at key decision points [15]. Pilot implementations in controlled ED settings, combined with RAG linked to EMR data, may represent a feasible starting point for validating such approaches [55]. In the ED, multiple roles, including triage nurses, emergency physicians, consulting clinicians, and discharge coordinators, are involved, and decisions are iteratively revised on the basis of test results, patient responses, and interprofessional communication. Therefore, LLM-based agents and multiagent systems should be considered not merely technical extensions but potentially suitable structures for more closely reflecting actual clinical workflows and role division.

Changes are also needed in evaluation frameworks. The current literature mainly relies on task-specific accuracy and retrospective comparisons. However, to determine feasibility for ED implementation, operational evaluation, real-world validation, and safety-aware benchmarking need to be strengthened. In particular, a useful ED system is not simply one with a high correctness rate; it must also be possible to evaluate when the system should intervene, when it should defer to humans, and how incorrect recommendations affect the overall workflow.

From a data perspective, it is important to move beyond text-centered input toward temporal data integration, multimodal fusion, and the use of structured ED data. Real-world ED data are characterized by the combination of multiple modalities over time, including vital signs, physician orders, laboratory results, imaging, voice interactions, bedside conversations, and nursing observations. Therefore, future research needs to expand beyond a simple text-dominant paradigm toward approaches that jointly reflect temporality and multimodality.

Finally, safety and trustworthiness should be treated not as auxiliary considerations but as core design elements of emergency medicine-specific LLM systems. The ED is a high-risk environment, and even small errors can have cascading effects on patient safety, resource allocation, waiting times, and clinician workload. Therefore, designs and validation frameworks that explicitly incorporate traceability, uncertainty handling, and human oversight are needed. These elements will become even more important as agent-based structures continue to expand.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, because only English-language full-text studies were included, non-English literature, gray literature, technical reports, conference abstracts, and web-based materials were excluded, and some relevant studies may therefore have been missed.

Second, although the search included the Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, and CINAHL, the diversity of terminology used in emergency medicine and LLM-based systems research, as well as differences in database indexing, may have limited search sensitivity. In particular, some studies may have incorporated agentic structures without explicitly describing them as “agent,” “LLM-based agent,” or “multi-agent.”

Third, to distinguish among nonagentic LLMs, LLM-based agents, and multiagent systems, this review applied operational definitions based on actual functional structure rather than author-reported labels. However, the recent literature uses concepts such as RAG, tool-calling pipelines, planning-based systems, memory-augmented workflows, role-based prompting, and multi-LLM collaboration in overlapping and inconsistent ways, so the same system may be classified differently depending on the level of interpretation.

Fourth, in the process of organizing heterogeneous elements such as ED task, primary input data, primary output or function, system type, added features, and evaluation approach into a comparable framework, some information was necessarily abstracted. As a result, the complex structure or detailed context of some studies may have been simplified.

Fifth, in accordance with the purpose of a scoping review, this review focused on mapping the research landscape and identifying conceptual gaps and did not perform a formal critical appraisal or meta-analysis of the included studies. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted not as evidence for quantitatively judging the effectiveness or superiority of individual systems, but rather as a synthesis of the current scope, structure, methodological trends, and limitations of the literature.

Finally, because many of the included studies were based on retrospective evaluation, simulated scenarios, curated benchmarks, or vignette-based assessment, evidence directly demonstrating prospective implementation, real-time operational performance, patient safety, and workflow impact in actual ED settings remains limited.

Conclusions

This scoping review showed that although LLM research in emergency medicine and ED settings is expanding rapidly, the current literature remains focused largely on task-level support based on nonagentic LLMs. In contrast, studies of LLM-based agents and multiagent systems that reflect the dynamic workflows and operational complexity of real EDs remain limited. Existing research demonstrates meaningful potential at the level of individual tasks, such as triage, diagnostic and treatment decision support, patient guidance, and documentation. However, key elements required for real-world ED implementation, including continuous support, dynamic orchestration, multimodal data integration, operational evaluation, and validation of safety and trustworthiness, have not yet been adequately addressed. Future research on emergency medicine-specific LLM systems needs to move beyond individual task-level performance toward integrated design and evaluation frameworks that reflect real clinical workflows, operational contexts, and safety and trustworthiness requirements.
