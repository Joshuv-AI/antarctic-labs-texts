Undiagnosed hemophilia presenting as orbital hemorrhage in a 32-year-old male
Authors: Henry Bair, Sandy Samuel, Charlotte L. Marous
Published: 2025

Case report

A 32-year-old man presented to the emergency department with right periorbital pain, swelling, and vision loss 10 h after reportedly being struck in the face by a cardboard box. The patient, a recent immigrant from Togo, reported no past medical history or medication use. Examination of the right eye revealed a visual acuity of hand motion, intraocular pressure (IOP) of 65 mm Hg (reference range, 10–21), and a relative afferent pupillary defect. Visual acuity and IOP of the left eye was 20/25 and 20, respectively. Computed tomography showed right-sided retrobulbar hemorrhage, proptosis, optic nerve stretching, and posterior globe tenting (Fig. 1A), consistent with orbital compartment syndrome. Despite appropriate lateral canthotomy and inferior/superior cantholysis (Fig. 1B), IOP remained elevated. Laboratory studies revealed a prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time of 113 s (reference range, 25–35) with normal prothrombin time, and subsequent targeted factor assays confirmed a diagnosis of hemophilia A. Upon thorough questioning, the patient denied any history of easy bruising, epistaxis, or gingival bleeding. Factor VIII replacement was initiated and hemorrhage stabilized. While surgical decompression — including anterior orbitotomy for clot evacuation or bony decompression — were considered, it was deferred given that the patient's vision in the right eye had decreased to no light perception. The patient was discharged 6 days after presentation. At ophthalmology follow-up 4 weeks post-discharge, the wound had healed appropriately and IOP was normal. Discussion

This case highlights a rare and vision-threatening initial presentation of hemophilia A. Although hemophilia typically manifests as hemarthroses or soft tissue bleeds, ocular and orbital hemorrhages are uncommon and often underrecognized. Previous reports have described similar presentations in children,1,2 including a case of bilateral retrobulbar hemorrhages in an 11-year-old girl, which led to the diagnosis of acquired hemophilia A following minor head trauma.3 In our case, the extent of orbital hemorrhage was strikingly disproportionate to the reported mechanism—a low-impact facial blow from a cardboard box. After conducting a literature review on June 1, 2025, utilizing PubMed and Google Scholar with the key words “orbital hemorrhage,” “orbital compartment syndrome,” “hemophilia,” and “occult coagulopathy,” we could not find any prior reports of undiagnosed hemophilia A presenting as orbital hemorrhage in patients older than 18 years. Our case therefore expands the demographic scope of such presentations. Because imaging does not distinguish coagulopathy, hemorrhage that is disproportionate to mechanism or demonstrates poor hemostasis should prompt expedited aPTT/PT testing and targeted factor assays.

Early recognition of orbital compartment syndrome (OCS) is critical. OCS arises when intraorbital pressure rapidly increases, often due to hemorrhage, compromising perfusion from the ophthalmic or central retinal artery to the optic nerve. Hallmark clinical signs include acute proptosis, resistance to retropulsion, tight eyelids, elevated IOP, and a relative afferent pupillary defect. Emergent lateral canthotomy and cantholysis should not be delayed for imaging or lab results, as orbital hemorrhage carries a high risk (∼30–50 %) of permanent vision loss within 120 min if not promptly decompressed.4,5 Although the patient's coagulopathy was ultimately identified and treated, the duration of optic nerve compression resulted in irreversible vision loss. This case demonstrates the dual urgency of decompressing the orbit and correcting systemic coagulopathy.

Conclusion

Orbital hemorrhage disproportionate to trauma should prompt consideration of an occult coagulopathy such as hemophilia A. This case highlights the need to keep a broad differential when encountering severe bleeding after minor injuries. It also underscores the importance of recognizing orbital compartment syndrome and performing timely canthotomy/cantholysis to preserve vision. Imaging signs of OCS often reflect severity rather than etiology. Prompt hematologic evaluation and factor replacement therapy are essential to address the underlying disorder and prevent recurrence.
