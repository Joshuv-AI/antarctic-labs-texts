Tetrahedral DNA nano-PROTACs enable enhanced ocular penetration and efficient nucleolin degradation for choroidal neovascularization therapy
Authors: Jiayi Song, Xu Wei, Kai He, Ruixin Ge, Mengxuan Li, Tinghui Bai, Han Han, Caiyun You, Mengyu Liao, Wei Pan, Miao Chen, Rubens Belfort Jr, Rodrigo Antonio Brant Fernandes, Songbo Xie, Hua Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the paradigm shift brought about by anti-VEGF therapy against neovascular age-related macular degeneration, significant challenges persist, including the risk of intraocular infection and retinal structural damage caused by frequent intravitreal injections, suboptimal long-term outcomes in some patients, as well as economic and psychological burdens. Attempts to identify novel therapeutic targets with more compliant drug delivery strategies are warranted. In this study, we identify that nucleolin (NCL) is upregulated in choroidal neovascularization lesions and mobilized to the endothelial cell surface upon VEGF stimulation. Inspired by this localization change and the critical role of NCL in angiogenesis, we developed an integrated delivery and degradation platform, named dNCL@tFNAs, which leverages tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNAs) to decorate an aptamer-based proteolysis-targeting chimera for NCL through a simple Watson-Crick pairing. dNCL@tFNAs exhibits notable stability, improved ocular penetration and degradation efficiency. Mechanistic study reveals that membrane-associated NCL promotes the uptake of dNCL@tFNAs into the endothelial cells, followed by engaging the cytosolic PROTAC machinery to degrade intracellular NCL via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. In vivo study demonstrates that dNCL@tFNAs enables administration via a minimally invasive subconjunctival injection to suppress choroidal neovascularization with a favorable safety profile. Collectively, our work establishes a programmable delivery and degradation modality for the treatment of choroidal neovascularization and other ocular neovascular diseases.Subject terms: Drug development, Therapeutics, Molecular medicine

Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a leading cause of irreversible vision loss among individuals aged over 60, resulting in severe visual impairment.1,2 It is estimated that by 2040, approximately 288 million people worldwide will be affected by AMD, representing a substantial public health challenge.3 Neovascular AMD (nAMD), characterized by the formation of choroidal neovascularization (CNV), accounts for nearly 90% of cases of severe vision loss associated with AMD.4 Intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy remains the cornerstone of first-line management for nAMD.5 However, the need for repeated, frequent injections imposes considerable psychological and economic burdens on patients, which often compromises long-term treatment adherence. Furthermore, cumulative exposure to both the pharmacological agent and the intravitreal procedure elevates the risk of adverse events, including drug-related complications and procedure-related sequelae, such as intraocular infection and retinal structural damage.6 Moreover, approximately 60% of patients exhibited a suboptimal response to anti-VEGF therapy.7 And among the initial responders, many were unable to maintain sustained visual gains and eventually acquired drug resistance. Thus, there is an urgent clinical need to identify novel therapeutic targets for nAMD and to develop optimized drug delivery strategies that enable efficient treatment with high patient compliance.

Nucleolin (NCL), a multifunctional protein primarily localized in the nucleolus, has been regarded as a promising therapeutic target for cancer treatment.8,9 It is translocated from the nucleus to the endothelial cell surface during VEGF-induced tumor angiogenesis.10 Strikingly, its presence on the cell surface is not found on mature endothelial vessel cells; thus, it may act as a specific marker for angiogenic endothelial cells within the tumor microenvironment. However, whether this distinct surface localization also occurs in CNV remains unclear. Supporting its therapeutic relevance to cancer biology, the NCL-targeted aptamer AS1411 has been assessed in clinical Phase I/II trials for the treatment of advanced solid tumors.11,12 Nevertheless, these clinical trials have been suspended or limited probably due to the requirement of high dose of AS1411 to maintain its sustained anti-tumor activity, highlighting a potential challenge in translating NCL-targeted strategies into clinical practice.

Targeted protein degradation (TPD) has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy, demonstrating broad application prospects in drug development in recent years. Among the various TPD approaches, proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) stand out as the most representative and leading example.13–15 PROTACs are bifunctional molecules composed of a binder for a protein of interest and a recruiter for an E3 ubiquitin ligase, connected via a linker.16 Their mechanism of action involves simultaneously binding the protein of interest and an E3 ubiquitin ligase, thereby inducing ubiquitination of the target protein, which is subsequently recognized and degraded by the intracellular ubiquitin-proteasome system.17–19 Distinct from the “occupancy-driven” mechanism of action employed by traditional small-molecule inhibitors and antibody drugs, PROTAC technology operates through an “event-driven” catalytic mechanism.20 Conventional drugs require sustained occupation of the target protein’s active site to exert their effects, which not only limits the range of druggable targets but also readily leads to drug resistance. In contrast, PROTACs need only transiently bind the target protein to induce its degradation catalytically and potently, thus obviating the need for high affinity, high concentration, or prolonged drug occupancy. This catalytic degradation mechanism significantly reduces the likelihood of drug resistance. Our previous study revealed that an AS1411-based PROTAC permitted significantly lower administration than AS1411 in cancer.21 The therapeutic potential of this PROTAC in CNV, however, has yet to be characterized.

In this study, we found that NCL is upregulated in CNV lesions and localized on the cell surface of angiogenic endothelial cells, providing a rationale for the targeted delivery and degradation of NCL to inhibit CNV. To address the formidable ocular barrier that limits effective drug delivery to the posterior segment, we leveraged tetrahedral framework nucleic acids (tFNAs), a class of biodegradable and programmable DNA nanostructure22 widely used in biosensors23 and drug delivery systems,24 as a versatile platform to enhance both ocular barrier penetration and the degradation efficiency of NCL-targeted PROTACs. Consequently, we developed a DNA-based tetrahedral nano-PROTAC platform. By integrating targeted delivery, protein degradation, and the capacity to traverse extraocular barriers, this platform harbors great potential as a novel and promising therapeutic strategy, opening new avenues for the treatment of CNV-related ocular diseases.

Results

Characterization of NCL expression in CNV lesions and dNCL@tFNAs

To examine the delivery and therapeutic potential of NCL for CNV diseases, we used Cy5-labeled AS1411, the NCL-specific aptamer, as a probe to examine its biodistribution in ocular tissues. Staining of ocular sections revealed robust accumulation of the probe specifically within retinal neovascular lesions in the laser-induced CNV mouse model group compared to that in controls (Fig. 1a, b). Further immunoblotting analysis of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)-choroidal tissue confirmed the elevated expression of NCL (supplementary Fig.1a, b). Notably, in neovascular lesions, Cy5-labeled AS1411 was predominantly colocalized with CD31-positive neovessels (Fig. 1c), indicating its translocation to the endothelial cell surface during the angiogenesis process. Although the VEGF challenge did not affect total NCL levels (supplementary Fig. 2a, b), it induced significant cell surface translocation of NCL, as demonstrated by non-permeabilized immunofluorescence staining of VEGF-stimulated human umbilical vascular endothelial cells (HUVECs) and non-stimulated quiescent cells (Fig. 1d). Immunoblotting of membrane proteins from VEGF-stimulated HUVECs further verified the elevated expression of membrane-localized NCL. (Fig. 1e, f). Collectively, these findings suggest the targeted delivery and therapeutic potential of NCL for CNV-related disease therapy.

The AS1411 aptamer was internalized into HUVECs within 3 h (supplementary Fig. 2c), suggesting its potential for both targeted delivery and degradation. Thus, we synthesized NCL-targeted PROTAC, named dNCL, via a click chemistry reaction using azide-modified AS1411 aptamer and thalidomide-O-amido-propargyl (a cereblon ligand, CRBNL) (Fig. 1g). Native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) analysis confirmed the successful synthesis of dNCL and dCTRL, a negative control PROTAC in which AS1411 was replaced with a scrambled aptamer (supplementary Fig. 3a). Transfection of dNCL into HUVECs triggered NCL degradation in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 1h, i & supplementary Fig. 3b–f), whereas direct addition of dNCL into the culture medium did not (Fig. 1h, i), probably due to the inefficient uptake or endosomal/lysosomal escape.

tFNAs, as a programmable and biodegradable nanocarrier, can penetrate the ocular barrier.25 We thus employed them for dNCL delivery. The self-assembled tFNAs containing a complementary sequence to AS1411 were hybridized with dNCL, generating dNCL@tFNAs (Fig. 1g). Successful conjugation was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig. 1j). AFM confirmed the tetrahedral structure of tFNAs with dNCL binding outside of the tetrahedron (Fig. 1k). The zeta potential shifted from −9.42 mV to −13.10 mV, further confirming the successful loading (Fig. 1l). Physicochemical characterization revealed that the complexation increased the hydrodynamic diameter from approximately 11.78 nm (tFNAs) to 19.61 nm (dNCL@tFNAs) (Fig. 1m), as measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS). Agarose gel electrophoresis demonstrated that the serum stability of dNCL@tFNAs was significantly enhanced compared to that of tFNAs (Fig. 1n, o).

dNCL@tFNAs is efficiently internalized into endothelial cells to degrade NCL

Next, we assessed cellular uptake of dNCL@tFNAs. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the uptake efficiency of dNCL@tFNAs was significantly enhanced compared to that of dNCL (Fig. 2a, b & supplementary Fig. 4). A rapid and efficient uptake was observed as early as 1 h, and as the time prolonged, more dNCL@tFNAs entered the HUVECs and escaped from the lysosome (Fig. 2c, d & supplementary Fig. 5). Furthermore, comparative treatment of adult RPE cells (ARPE-19) and HUVECs demonstrated that dNCL@tFNAs possessed a strong targeting specificity toward endothelial cells (Fig. 2e, f). And for HUVECs stimulated by VEGF, the endocytosis of dNCL@tFNAs could be mediated by membrane NCL (supplementary Fig. 6).

We next evaluated the penetrating ability of dNCL@tFNAs using an in vitro RPE permeability model. The confluent ARPE-19 monolayer cultured on semi-permeable inserts was established to recapitulate the blood-retinal barrier. Cy5-labeled-dNCL or -dNCL@tFNAs was applied to the upper chamber, and the fluorescence intensity in the lower chamber was measured over time (Fig. 2g). A significantly higher fluorescence intensity was detected in the dNCL@tFNAs group compared to that in dNCL (Fig. 2h), indicating the ocular barrier penetration potential of dNCL@tFNAs.

To further elucidate the endocytic mechanisms triggered by dNCL@tFNAs, we employed three specific inhibitors: chlorpromazine (CPZ, an inhibitor of clathrin-mediated endocytosis), Methyl-beta-cyclodextrin (M-β-CD, an inhibitor of lipid raft-mediated endocytosis), and Ethylisopropylamiloride (EIPA, an inhibitor of macropinocytosis). Immunofluorescence staining (supplementary Fig. 8a) and flow cytometry analyses (supplementary Fig. 8b, c) revealed that cellular uptake of dNCL@tFNAs was significantly reduced in the presence of EIPA, suggesting that the internalization process of this delivery system may be associated with the macropinocytosis pathway. It should be noted that commonly used endocytosis inhibitors exhibit some degree of cross-reactivity and incomplete inhibition. Therefore, the precise endocytic pathway of dNCL@tFNAs requires further systematic investigation using multiple experimental approaches.

We next assessed the efficacy of dNCL@tFNAs in degrading NCL. Direct addition of dNCL@tFNAs into the culture medium effectively induced NCL degradation in both dose- and time-dependent manners (Fig. 2i–l). Moreover, it could degrade the membrane NCL stimulated by VEGF (supplementary Fig. 9a, b). Additionally, cycloheximide (CHX) experiments revealed that dNCL@tFNAs markedly shortened the half-life of intracellular NCL (Fig. 2m & supplementary Fig. 10a). Control experiments confirmed that NCL degradation was specific to dNCL@tFNAs, as neither tFNAs nor dNCL (direct addition) produced this effect (Fig. 2n, o & supplementary Fig. 10b). As anticipated, dNCL@tFNAs-induced NCL degradation was blocked by the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Fig. 2p) but not the lysosome inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) (Fig. 2q), suggesting that dNCL@tFNAs mediates NCL degradation primarily through the proteasomal pathway.

dNCL@tFNAs significantly inhibits in vitro angiogenesis

The abnormal neovessels induced by elevated VEGF are the major pathological feature of wet AMD. It is supposed that VEGF mobilizes NCL from the nucleus to the cell surface, which promotes endothelial cell migration, proliferation, and angiogenesis (Fig. 3a). Thus, we evaluated the functional impact of dNCL@tFNAs on these cellular activities. In the absence of VEGF stimulation, neither dNCL nor tFNAs significantly affected HUVEC migration. By contrast, dNCL showed an inhibitory effect on cell migration upon VEGF stimulation, probably due to the enhanced uptake enabled by VEGF-facilitated NCL translocation to the cell surface (Fig. 3b, c). Although dNCL@tFNAs markedly inhibited endothelial cell migration even without VEGF stimulation, its inhibitory effect was more pronounced in the presence of VEGF (Fig. 3b, c). Consistently, further tube formation assays confirmed that dNCL@tFNAs markedly inhibited angiogenesis with or without VEGF stimulation, and a stronger inhibitory effect was observed under VEGF-stimulated conditions (Fig. 3d–f). Similarly, the cell proliferation assay revealed comparable trends (Fig. 3g, h). These data indicate that dNCL@tFNAs displays enhanced inhibitory activity on VEGF-induced angiogenesis.

Subconjunctivally injected dNCL@tFNAs penetrates ocular barrier and accumulates at the CNV lesions

Having identified the penetration ability of dNCL@tFNAs across RPE monolayer barrier, we then examined its delivery efficiency of two non-intravitreal routes: topical eye drops and subconjunctival injection (Fig. 4a). Fluorescence intensity measurements in enucleated eyeballs at 6 h post-administration revealed that subconjunctival injection led to lower drug loss (Fig. 4b), and the fluorescence signals retained for up to 48 h in mice receiving the subconjunctival injection, demonstrating its superior delivery profile (Fig. 4c, d).

We then assessed the in vivo penetration kinetics of subconjunctivally injected dNCL and dNCL@tFNAs (2 μΜ, 2 μL). Cy5 fluorescence signals were detectable in the retina in the dNCL@tFNAs group as early as 6 h post-injection, accumulated as the time prolonged, and persisted for up to 48 h, confirming successful penetration. In contrast, Cy5 fluorescence signals in the retina were hardly detectable in the dNCL group, suggesting its inability to traverse the blood-retinal barrier (Fig. 4e). We then proceeded to evaluate the targeting specificity of dNCL@tFNAs for CNV. The dNCL@tFNAs (2 μΜ, 2 μL) was administered via subconjunctival injection to both control mice and CNV model mice 7 days after laser induction. Analysis of eyeballs collected 6 h later revealed that dNCL@tFNAs preferentially accumulated within the CNV lesions and exhibited strong colocalization with CD31-positive neovessels (Fig. 4f, h), demonstrating excellent targeting efficacy toward pathological angiogenesis. Furthermore, no significant Cy5 signal accumulation was detected in the retinas of normal mice or in the non-lesion areas of CNV model mice (Fig. 4f–h). Taken together, these findings demonstrate the targeting specificity of dNCL@tFNAs for pathological neovascularization.

dNCL@tFNAs displays superior anti-angiogenic activity in vivo without noticeable toxicities

We next evaluated the therapeutic effects of dNCL@tFNAs on angiogenesis in a laser-induced mouse CNV model (Fig. 5a). On day 5, the laser-treated mice received subconjunctival injection of vehicle group (TM buffer, 2 μL), dNCL (2 μΜ, 2 μL), tFNAs (2 μΜ, 2 μL), or dNCL@tFNAs (2 μΜ, 2 μL), and intravitreally injected anti-VEGF drug Conbercept (2 μL) was used as a positive control. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) was performed to measure CNV lesion height and area on day 7. Both dNCL@tFNAs and intravitreally-injected conbercept significantly reduced CNV lesion height and area, whereas dNCL and tFNAs did not, suggesting the therapeutic potential of dNCL@tFNAs (Fig. 5b–d). We next assessed its therapeutic effects on vascular leakage. Fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) indicated that both dNCL@tFNAs and intravitreal conbercept significantly reduced CNV-induced vascular leakage, whereas neither dNCL nor tFNAs alleviated vascular leakage (Fig. 5e, f). Consistently, further choroidal flat mounts revealed that CNV volume was significantly decreased by dNCL@tFNAs and intravitreal conbercept treatment but not by dNCL and tFNAs (Fig. 5g, h, i).

Next, we assessed the potential toxicity of dNCL@tFNAs. Comprehensive ocular evaluations, including corneal fluorescein staining, fundus photography, OCT imaging, retinal thickness analysis, TUNEL staining of retinal tissues, inflammatory factor: interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) detection in RPE-choroidal tissue and electroretinogram were examined 7 days post-subconjunctival administration, and non-noticeable toxicity was observed in all the subconjunctival groups as well as intravitreal conbercept (Fig. 6a–j). Similarly, H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) showed no obvious pathological alterations (supplementary Fig. 11). The levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) in the serum were all within the normal range (Fig. 6k–m). Collectively, these results demonstrate that dNCL@tFNAs exhibits a high level of both ocular and systemic safety profile.

Discussion

Despite the proven efficacy of anti-VEGF therapy in inhibiting CNV progression, its long-term application remains challenging. Prolonged anti-VEGF therapy may disrupt retinal vascular homeostasis26–29 and carries a risk of systemic adverse events due to potential drug entry into the circulation.30–32 These limitations indicate that strategies solely targeting VEGF cannot address all clinical needs in CNV patients, underscoring the urgent need for therapies aimed at novel targets beyond VEGF. Furthermore, the efficient delivery of therapeutics to the posterior segment of the eye remains a formidable technical bottleneck. Intravitreal injection, the current standard of care, is invasive and entails risks of complications, including endophthalmitis, elevated intraocular pressure, and retinal detachment. Subconjunctival injection and other alternative routes, though less traumatic, still require that drugs negotiate physiological barriers to access the lesion site, with the blood–retinal barrier being the most notable among them.33 Hence, the development of safe, non-invasive, or minimally invasive administration strategies that facilitate efficient drug accumulation in CNV lesions is of paramount importance for advancing CNV therapy. To overcome these hurdles, we adopted tFNAs, a highly programmable nanocarrier capable of penetrating various biological barriers, such as the skin34 and blood-retinal barrier,25 to load an AS1411-based PROTAC and achieved selective CNV delivery via subconjunctival injection and efficient NCL degradation, presenting a straightforward and less-invasive means to deliver aptamer-based degraders into posterior eye lesions.

VEGF stimulation induces the translocation of NCL from the nucleus to the cell membrane in angiogenic endothelial cells.10 The membrane translocation of NCL is closely associated with increased tumor malignancy35 and enhanced angiogenesis.36 As a result, cell surface-expressed NCL has been recognized as an important therapeutic target for suppressing angiogenesis and treating various angiogenesis-related diseases.37 In addition, overexpressed NCL on cancer cell surface has been widely used as a targeting marker for payload delivery.38 In this study, we found that NCL is up-regulated and translocated to the endothelial cell surface within CNV lesions, providing a strong rationale for targeted NCL delivery and degradation as a means to inhibit CNV. To test this hypothesis, we developed a nano-PROTAC platform referred to as dNCL@tFNAs, which is based on DNA tetrahedral framework nucleic acids and functionalized with an NCL-targeting moiety. Notably, dNCL@tFNAs exhibited specific accumulation at the CNV lesions and promoted the degradation of NCL in angiogenic endothelial cells. In contrast, direct addition of dNCL into the cell culture did not result in significant NCL degradation, a finding that may be explained by inefficient cellular uptake or limited endosomal/lysosomal escape. Collectively, these findings affirm the critical contribution of the tFNAs delivery platform to intracellular PROTAC delivery and enhanced degradation efficacy, thereby corroborating the promise of this strategy as a therapeutic approach for CNV.

It is canonically characterized that tFNAs promotes payload uptake via a corner-attack mode and caveolae-mediated endocytosis.39 In this scenario, we found that dNCL@tFNAs facilitates rapid cellular uptake and efficiently circumvents lysosomal entrapment, thereby confirming its promising potential for drug delivery. And our results further demonstrate that dNCL@tFNAs undergo internalization via macropinocytosis, which mediates the initial clearance of membrane-associated NCL. Following escape from lysosomal entrapment, the system further engages the cytosolic PROTAC machinery through CRBN-bound ligands, thereby promoting the degradation of intracellular NCL via the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway. These findings describe a dual-step internalization mechanism of dNCL. The initial step involves macropinocytosis to clear membrane-associated NCL, which is succeeded by the proteasomal degradation of intracellular NCL. This sequential mechanism not only highlights the cooperative roles of distinct endocytic pathways but also affirms the sophisticated capacity of the dNCL@tFNAs platform for targeted protein degradation. Importantly, the ability to rapidly enter cells, avoid lysosomal degradation, and harness the endogenous ubiquitin–proteasome system positions dNCL@tFNAs as a versatile and efficient nanoplatform for therapeutic applications aimed at pathogenic membrane and intracellular proteins.

In addition, dNCL@tFNAs is serum stable even for 24 h, probably due to the inherent negative charge and structural rigidity of tFNAs that confers resistance to enzymatic degradation in biological fluids.40,41 This enhanced stability is particularly advantageous for in vivo applications, as it ensures sufficient bioavailability of the nanoplatform following administration. Although previous studies have suggested that tFNAs may intrinsically influence cell proliferation42 and angiogenesis,43 we observed in this experimental setting that their role as a carrier did not compromise the core functionality of the dNCL. Specifically, the degradation activity and targeting specificity of dNCL were preserved upon conjugation with tFNAs, indicating that the tFNA platform functions primarily as a delivery vehicle without interfering with the therapeutic payload. This carrier–payload independence is critical for the rational design of future tFNA-based delivery systems, as it allows the modular incorporation of various therapeutic agents without compromising their intrinsic activities. Although a clear therapeutic effect was observed in the CNV mouse model following dNCL@tFNAs treatment, whether a single administration can produce sustained efficacy requires further validation. The durability of protein degradation-mediated therapeutic outcomes may depend on multiple factors, including the turnover rate of the target protein, the intracellular half-life of the PROTAC, and the potential for target re-expression following treatment. Therefore, future studies incorporating repeated dosing regimens and extended observation periods will be required to evaluate the durability of the therapeutic response and to determine the optimal treatment schedule for potential clinical translation. Additionally, systematic pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic analyses would further inform the translational potential of this platform.

To conclude, herein we identified NCL as an optimal delivery and therapeutic target for CNV and thus developed a tFNAs-based delivery and degradation platform, which presents a viable and less-invasive alternative to conventional intravitreal injections. dNCL@tFNAs improves the ocular barrier penetration and enhances therapeutic efficiency with a favorable safety profile, establishing a novel therapeutic modality for the treatment of CNV and other ocular neovascular diseases.

Materials and methods

Materials

The following antibodies were used: anti-NCL (#D4C70, Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA), anti-CD31 (#AF3628, R&D Systems, MN, USA), and anti-β-actin (#UM4001, Utibody, China). The aptamers and their modified versions were synthesized by BGI (Beijing, China). Sequences used in this study can be found in supplementary Table 1.

Animals

All animal protocols were approved by the Ethical Committee of Tianjin Medical University General Hospital (IRB2025-DWFL-188), in accordance with the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology guidelines for the use of animals in vision research. All the mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories Inc. (Beijing, China). Mice were housed under a 12 h light/dark cycle at a temperature of 21–25 °C with 30–70% humidity, and had free access to water and standard diet. CD-1 mice were utilized for in vivo optical imaging system (IVIS) imaging, and wild-type C57BL/6 mice were used for all other experiments. Mice were randomly assigned for different treatments. No animals were excluded from the study. All experimenters were blinded both during the execution and analysis of experiments.

Cell culture

Primary HUVECs were isolated as described44 and approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Medical University General Hospital. Cells were cultured in Collagen (#354236, Corning, USA)-coated dishes using endothelial cell medium (#1001, ScienCell, USA) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% endothelial cell growth supplement, and 1% antibiotic solution. Cells between passages 3 and 8 were used in the experiments. For VEGF (#293-VE-010, R&D Systems, USA) stimulation, HUVECs were cultured in endothelial cell medium containing 2% FBS and treated with 100 ng/mL VEGF for 12 h. ARPE-19 (#h020, iCell Bioscience Inc, China) were cultured in F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Synthesis of Apt-PROTACs

Alkynyl-modified thalidomide (CRBN ligand, CRBNL) was synthesized as previously described.21 Thalidomide was employed as a ligand to selectively recruit the CRBN E3 ubiquitin ligase, for the purpose of promoting the ubiquitination of the target protein. CRBNL and azide-modified aptamers were mixed in a reaction buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 10 mM CuSO₄, 10 mM sodium isoascorbate) to perform a click reaction. The reaction was carried out at 37 °C for 4 h, followed by ultrafiltration with a 3 K ultrafiltration tube (Millipore, Billerica, USA) to remove excess CRBNL.

Synthesis and characterization of dNCL@tFNAs

Four single-stranded DNAs (S1, S2, S3, S4) were mixed at equimolar ratios in TM buffer (20 mM Tris, 50 mM MgCl2, pH = 8.0), heated to 95 °C for 10 min, and then rapidly cooled to 4 °C for 20 min. Agarose gel electrophoresis was used to verify the formation of dNCL@tFNAs. The zeta potential and hydrodynamic diameter of tFNAs and dNCL@tFNAs were measured by dynamic light scattering (Nano ZS90, Malvern, UK). The dispersion and morphology of tFNAs and dNCL@tFNAs were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Hitachi HT7700, Japan) and atomic force microscopy (AFM; Park NX12, South Korea).

Serum stability

The stability of tFNAs and dNCL@tFNAs were assessed by incubation in ECGM medium supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C for 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 24 h. Following incubation, the integrity of the samples was analyzed using agarose gel electrophoresis.

Cellular uptake

HUVECs were incubated with 250 nM dNCL-Cy5@tFNAs in ECGM medium containing 2% FBS for varying durations. After incubation, the cells were washed with PBS and co-stained with a Lyso-Tracker and Hoechst for 20 min at 37 °C. Following another PBS wash, confocal fluorescence imaging (LSM 800, Carl Zeiss, Germany) was performed.

Permeability assay

ARPE-19 cells were seeded into Transwell inserts with 0.4 μm pore size (Corning Costar, USA) at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well. Monolayer integrity was verified by monitoring the transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) every 48 h until values stabilized. Following the confirmation of a tight monolayer barrier, the cells were treated with dNCL-Cy5 or dNCL-Cy5@tFNAs (50 nM) and incubated at 37 °C for specified time points. After incubation, the supernatant in the lower chamber was collected, and Cy5 fluorescence intensity was quantified using a microplate reader (EnVision 2105, PerkinElmer, USA).

Scratch assay

HUVECs were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured under standard conditions to form a confluent monolayer. After a 12 h pre-incubation in low-serum ECGM (2% FBS) with or without 100 ng/mL VEGF, a scratch was made with a sterile pipette tip. Subsequently, the cells were treated with vehicle, dNCL, tFNAs, or dNCL@tFNAs (50 nM) for 24 h. The wounds were imaged at 0- and 24-h post-scratch using an inverted microscope.

Tube formation assay

HUVECs were pre-treated with or without 100 ng/mL VEGF, followed by a 9 h treatment with vehicle, dNCL, tFNAs, or dNCL@tFNAs (50 nM). Prior to seeding, 24-well plates were coated with 50 μL of Matrigel (#354277, Corning, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to allow polymerization. Subsequently, 5 × 10⁴ pre-treated HUVECs from each group were plated onto the solidified Matrigel. Following 6 h of incubation, tube formation was imaged using microscopy and analyzed with ImageJ software.

Cell proliferation assay

HUVECs were pre-treated with or without 100 ng/mL VEGF, followed by a 9 h treatment with vehicle, dNCL, tFNAs, or dNCL@tFNAs (50 nM) in 12-well plates. And then labeled with 10 μM EdU at 37 °C for 2 h. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature for 20 min and then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min. The cells were incubated with a 1x Click-iT reaction solution for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After washing with PBS, the cells were stained with Hoechst solution in the dark for 15 min, washed again with PBS, and finally imaged using a confocal microscope.

Laser-induced CNV model

Following full anesthesia, pupils of the C57BL/6 mice were dilated with 1% tropicamide. Four laser photocoagulation spots (532 nm, 200 mW, 0.15 s, 100 μm, Lumenis, USA) were applied to the eye at positions approximately two optic disc diameters from the optic disc center. Successful laser injury was identified by the immediate formation of a bubble at Bruch’s membrane without significant hemorrhage under a slit lamp, with care taken to avoid major retinal vessels during laser application.

Subconjunctival injection

Mice were anesthetized via isoflurane inhalation. A 2 μL volume of either the vehicle, dNCL, tFNAs, or dNCL@tFNAs was administered via subconjunctival injection. The injection was performed 1–2 mm posterior to the corneal limbus using a 33-gauge metal needle and a 5 μL micro-syringe. Following the procedure, levofloxacin ophthalmic ointment was applied to prevent infection.

Fluorescein angiography

Following full anesthesia, pupils of the C57 mice were dilated with 1% tropicamide. 100 μL of 10% sodium fluorescein was injected intraperitoneally, and fluorescein angiography images were captured using a digital fundus camera (Heidelberg Confocal Retina Angiography, Germany) 5 min after the injection. We delineated the high fluorescence areas corresponding to the laser spots. For each mouse, we quantified the lesions in both eyes and calculated the average value for each mouse.

Fundus photography and OCT

Following full anesthesia, pupils of the C57BL/6 mice were dilated with 1% tropicamide. Fundus images and OCT scans were acquired using an animal retinal imaging system (Micron IV, Phoenix Research Labs, USA). The thickness of the entire retinal layer or the CNV area was measured using the analysis software provided with the instrument.

Western blotting

Proteins were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto a PVDF membrane. The membrane was then incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or anti-mouse secondary antibodies at room temperature. Target proteins were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate kit (Millipore, Billerica, USA).

Immunofluorescence of retinal sections

The mouse eyes for histological analysis were immersed in a 4% PFA solution and fixed for 2 h. The eyes were then dehydrated, embedded in OCT compound, and sectioned. The sections were washed at room temperature to remove OCT, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and blocked with 3% BSA in 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1 h. Immunofluorescence staining was performed by incubating with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h, followed by DAPI counterstaining. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal microscope (LSM800, Carl Zeiss, Germany). For each set of experiments, the same settings were used for collecting confocal images. The images were quantified by ImageJ software. Three images from each tissue were used for analysis.

RPE-choroid flat mount staining

The mouse eyes were fixed in 4% PFA for 2 h. Extraocular tissues were carefully removed, followed by the removal of the anterior segment (cornea, iris, ciliary body, and lens). The eyecups were permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100 for 12 h and subsequently blocked with 3% BSA in 0.3% Triton X-100 for 12 h. Incubation with primary antibodies was performed at 4 °C for 36 h. After washing, the samples were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h, followed by DAPI counterstaining. To create flat mounts, four radial incisions were made from the edge of the retinal cup towards the optic nerve. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope, and CNV volume was quantified using Imaris software. For each mouse, we quantified lesions in both eyes and calculated the average value per mouse.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data were analyzed using an unpaired Student’s t-test for comparisons between two groups, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used to determine statistical significance among multiple groups. A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A minimum of three independent cell culture replicates (or three animals per group) were used for all analyses, as detailed in the Methods section.
