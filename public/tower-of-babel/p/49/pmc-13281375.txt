Caffeic Acid Ameliorates l‑Methionine-Induced Hyperhomocysteinemia, Leading to Decreased Adult Neurogenesis and Oxidative Stress Status in Adult Rats
Authors: Oabnithi Dornlakorn, Apiwat Sirichoat, Wanassanan Pannangrong, Nataya Sritawan, Anusara Aranarochana, Ram Prajit, Peter Wigmore, Jariya Umka Welbat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic l-methionine (l-met) treatment induces hyperhomocysteinemia, which impairs neurogenesis and memory by increasing oxidative stress. Caffeic acid (CA) possesses antioxidant properties that reduce brain oxidative damage and promote neurogenesis. This study investigated the neuroprotective effects of caffeic acid against the impact of l-met on hippocampal neurogenesis. Forty male Sprague–Dawley rats were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 10 per group): Control, l-met, CA, and CA+l-met. Rats received daily oral gavage of l-met (1.7 g/kg) and/or CA (40 mg/kg) for 28 days. The results demonstrated that CA ameliorated l-met-induced decreases in DCX and Sox-2-positive cells in the SGZ of the dentate gyrus, as shown by immunofluorescence staining. CA also reversed the l-met-induced decreases in hippocampal protein expression of Nrf2, Sox-2, and BDNF, as determined by Western blotting. Furthermore, CA alleviated l-met-induced oxidative damage and decreased antioxidant enzyme activity in the prefrontal cortex (PF) and hippocampus. These findings demonstrate CA’s ability to counteract neurotoxicity induced by l-methionine.

Introduction

Currently, the prevalence of dementia is increasing worldwide, affecting millions of people. Dementia is becoming more prevalent globally and a significant public concern, affecting learning, memory, and cognitive function. Several studies have demonstrated that the production of free radicals leads to oxidative stress, causing damage to lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. This damage contributes to the acceleration of neurodegenerative diseases associated with dementia. , l-methionine (l-met) is an essential, nonpolar amino acid commonly found in foods such as meat, fish, eggs, sesame oil, and cereal grains. l-Met serves as a substrate for several important biomolecules, including cysteine and taurine. Through a methylation pathway, it is converted to homocysteine (Hcy) via a methylation pathway. Excessive levels of Hcy are associated with the production of oxidative agents that cause neuronal damage through highly reactive free radical agents. This, in turn, disrupts the redox system in neuronal cells, inducing and accelerating lipid peroxidation events. Chronic l-met treatment at 1.7 g/kg of animal weight elevates Hcy levels, inducing hyperhomocysteinemia (HHcy). This can lead to cerebrovascular disorders such as stroke and vascular dementia. Furthermore, administering l-met 1.7 g/kg for 28 days can lead to decreased cell proliferation, cell survival as well as increased cell cycle arrest, resulting in spatial and recognition memory deficits.

Neurogenesis continuously generates newborn neurons in the hippocampal dentate gyrus throughout life; however, it declines with age and is disrupted by negative factors affecting the neurogenesis process. Conversely, hippocampal neurogenesis and neuroprotective qualities are enhanced through exposure to an enriched environment and compounds derived from plant-based sources, including hesperidin, asiatic acid, chrysin, and CA. ,−

Caffeic acid (CA), a hydroxycinnamic acid, is a natural phenolic compound present in plants and foods such as tomatoes, berries, tea, coffee, and red wine. − CA exhibits antioxidant properties and effectively scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to the neutralization of lipid peroxidative markers. A previous study investigated the effects of CA at the doses of 20 and 40 mg/kg on oxidative stress and antioxidant enzyme activities. CA 40 mg/kg demonstrates its ability to improve memory deficits associated with cell proliferation and survival, decrease cell cycle arrest, and effectively enhance antioxidant enzyme activities and reduce neuronal apoptosis and oxidative stress in both l-met and aging models. , This study aims to explore how CA 40 mg/kg affects the oxidative state, antioxidant enzyme activity, and the expression of specific protein markers linked to neurogenesis and neural stem cells. This research may ultimately uncover a new way that CA mitigates the detrimental effects of hyperhomocysteinemia.

Materials and Methods

In all experiments, male Sprague–Dawley rats aged 4–5 weeks and weighing 200–220 g were purchased from Nomura Siam International Co., Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand, and used in all experimental procedures. They were housed in a stainless-steel cage (28 × 47 × 18 cm) and maintained under a 12 h light/dark cycle at 28–30 °C. The rats had unlimited access to food and water. The experiment methods were accepted by the Khon Kaen University Ethics Committee in Animal Research (reference number AICUC-KKU-87/65). Before any drugs were administered, the rats were randomly housed for 7 days to acclimate to their surroundings. The CA and l-met supplier was Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co. (Saint Louis, MO, USA). The rats were randomly assigned to four groups: the vehicle group; the l-met (l-Met) group (l-met 1.7 g/kg/day, dissolved in 0.1% w/v Carboxyl Methyl Cellulose [CMC]); the CA group (CA40 mg/kg/day, a dose from previous study, dissolved in propylene glycol); and the CA combined with l-met (CA+l-met) group. Oral gavage was used to administer both CA and l-met. The vehicle (propylene glycol and 0.1% w/v CMC) was given to the animals in the vehicle group by oral gavage. All treatments, including l-met, CA, and vehicle, were initiated on the same day and continued for 4 weeks (Figure ).

Immunofluorescence Assessment

Tissue Preparation

Rat brains were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Sigma-Aldrich, Inc., USA). The brains were then serially sectioned at a thickness of 40 μm in the coronal plane using a cryostat (Cryostat Series HM550, Microm International, A.S. Science Co. Ltd., Walldorf, Germany). The sections were kept in the cryoprotective buffer at 4 °C. A systematic random sampling method was used and every eighth section along the length of the dentate gyrus was selected to obtain nine sections per brain for DCX and Sox-2 staining. Immunofluorescence staining was then used to assess hippocampal neurogenesis.

Determination of Hippocampal Neural Stem Cell Marker

The sex-determining region Y-box 2 (Sox-2) immunofluorescence staining method was used to investigate cell development, self-renewal, and pluripotency. Forty-micron sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-Sox-2 (1:400, Abcam, USA). On the second day, the sections were treated with Alexa 568 goat antirabbit IgG (1:500, Invitrogen, USA) at room temperature for 60 min and counterstained with DAPI (1:6000, Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR, USA). Finally, each section was mounted with glycerol.

Determination of Immature Neurons (DCX)

Cell differentiation into immature neurons was analyzed using the doublecortin microtubule-associated protein staining method. Serial sections of frozen brains were prepared along the length of the dentate gyrus in the coronal plane. The antimouse monoclonal IgG primary doublecortin antibody (1:100, Santa Cruz, USA) was applied to the sections and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the sections were incubated with rabbit antimouse IgG Alexa Fluor 488 antibody (1:300, Invitrogen, USA) for 60 min and then counterstained with propidium iodide (1:6000, Sigma-Aldrich, Inc., St. Louis, USA) for 30 s. Ultimately, each section was mounted using glycerol and coverslipped for fluorescence microscope examination using the free-floating approach. Staining was applied to each section, and three cell layers in the subgranular zone of the upper and lower blades of the DG were counted to determine the presence of DCX-positive cells.

Biochemical Assessment

After homogenizing tissue samples from the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex on ice with cold deionized water to prepare a 100 mg/mL solution, the mixture was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was then carefully collected.

Measurement of Malondialdehyde (MDA)

MDA level is the end product of lipid peroxidation, and its levels are measured using the thiobarbituric acid reactive substance (TBARS) assay. , A 100 μL aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with a solution of 20% acetic acid (pH 3.5), 0.8% thiobarbituric acid (TBA), and 8.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS). The mixture was heated to 95 °C for 1 h. Once cooled to room temperature, deionized water was added. Afterward, the mixture was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min at room temperature. A 15:1 pyridine and n-butanol solution was then added to the mixture. The supernatant was collected to measure absorbance at 540 nm using a microplate reader. The data were expressed in nmol/mg protein, and the reaction was performed in triplicate.

Measurement of Catalase (CAT) Levels

The CAT enzyme activity measurement technique is based on the method described by Weydert. A 96-well plate containing 10 μL of the supernatant and 30% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in a 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) was used to initiate CAT enzyme activity. The reaction was then rapidly stopped using 2.5 M sulfuric acid (H2SO4). After gently mixing the solution with potassium permanganate (KMnO4), absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader.

Measurement of Glutathione Peroxidase (GPx) Levels

The procedure described by Eyer, Podhradsky, and Weydert was adapted to measure GPx enzyme activity. , A cocktail solution was prepared using sodium azide (NaN3), 50 mM glutathione, β-nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (β-NADPH), and glutathione reductase (GR). To initiate the reaction, 60 μL of the supernatant was combined with 30% H2O2 and the cocktail containing 5,5′-dithiobis­(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB). After incubating the mixture in a dark chamber for 10 min, absorbance was measured at 405 nm using a microplate reader.

Measurement of Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) Levels

SOD activity was measured using a method adapted from Weydert. The cocktail included 216 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 1.1 mM cytochrome C solution, 0.108 mM xanthine solution, and 10.7 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid solution (EDTA). Each measurement was performed at room temperature. To initiate the reaction, 700 μL of the cocktail and 25 μL of the supernatant were mixed in a 96-well plate, followed by the addition of xanthine oxidase solution to stop the cytochrome C reaction. Absorbance at 540 nm was measured using a microplate reader from minute 0 to minute 5. The data were reported in protein units per mg. The biochemical assessment reaction was performed in triplicate.

Western Blotting Analysis

Hippocampal tissue samples were homogenized on ice in lysis buffer to a concentration of 100 mg/mL solution. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected. Protein concentrations were measured using a Nanodrop spectrometer. For the analysis of BDNF, Nrf2, and Sox-2, proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE using a 12% separating gel. Following separation, the samples (30 μg proteins per lane) were transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Freiburg, Germany). The membrane was then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: Sox-2 (1:1000, ab97959, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Nrf2 (1:1000, ab31163, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), BDNF (1:1000, ab108319, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA), and anti-GAPDH (1:20,000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). The membranes were incubated for 60 min at room temperature with the following secondary antibodies: antirabbit (1:2000, DAKO, Glostrup, Denmark), antigoat (1:2000, DAKO, Glostrup, Denmark), or antimouse (1:2000, DAKO, Glostrup, Denmark), depending on the primary antibody used. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) Western blot detection system (GE Healthcare, Buckingham, UK). The relative optical density of the protein bands was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.53a, Wayne Rasband, National Institutes of Health, USA). The data were expressed as the optical density of each protein band relative to that of the GAPDH housekeeping protein. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the protein bands of interest were compared to this internal control.

Homocysteine Serum Levels

Blood samples were collected from the lateral tail veins. The serum homocysteine level was estimated using a Roche Cobas homocysteine enzymatic analyzer to determine l-met levels using a method based on the principle that assesses the cosubstrate conversion product instead of Hcy. First step: free hydrogen is created by reducing oxidized hydrogen bonds. Next step: Hcy reacts with SAM, a cosubstrate, in a reaction catalyzed by Hcy S-methyltransferase to produce l-met and SAH. SAH is measured via a series of coupled enzyme reactions at the Immunology and Clinical Chemistry Laboratory, Faculty of Medicine, Khon Kaen University.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical parameters were calculated using GraphPad Prism software (Version 7.04). Results are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk test (p > 0.05) with IBM SPSS Statistics 29. A One-way ANOVA was used to analyze Hcy serum level followed by Dunnett post hoc testing, Sox-2 and DCX immunofluorescence-positive cells, protein expression (from Western blotting analysis), antioxidant enzyme activities, and MDA followed by Bonferroni post hoc testing. Animal weight gain was analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc testing.

Discussion

The major finding of this current study is that l-met-treated rats exhibited a decrease in hippocampal neurogenesis, as evidenced by a reduction in the expression of the Sox-2 and DCX-positive cells in the hippocampal dentate gyrus. In this study, chronic administration of l-met caused a significant increase in Hcy serum levels compared to the vehicle group. These findings confirm previous studies reporting that excessive l-met results in hyperhomocysteinemia, which in turn induces neuroinflammation and impairs neurogenesis. The main mechanism of l-met-induced neurogenesis impairment is likely related to increased oxidative stress in the hippocampus. This stress develops during HHcy as the thiol groups generate ROS, including H2O2, superoxide, and hydroxyl radicals. These impair redox and homeostasis, resulting in increased lipid peroxidation and neuronal damage. CA possesses potent antioxidant properties that may neutralize MDA levels, thereby increasing antioxidant enzyme activity and reducing serum Hcy levels by eliminating free radicals caused by elevated Hcy accumulation. Furthermore, l-met increased MDA levels and decreased the expression of Nrf2, Sox-2, and BDNF in the hippocampal region, while also lowering the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as CAT, GPx, and SOD. On the other hand, CA improved antioxidant activity by lowering MDA levels and increasing the expression of DCX and Sox-2-positive cells, as demonstrated by immunofluorescence staining. Additionally, CA administration ameliorated the l-met-induced decrease in Nrf2, Sox-2, and BDNF expression.

Throughout life, adult neurogenesis occurs in the subventricular zone of the lateral ventricles and in the SGZ of the dentate gyrus. According to our current research, rats treated with l-met exhibited a significant reduction in the expression of Ki-67 and BrdU-specific cells. The Sox-2 transcription factor can increase cell survival and proliferation by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway through its connections with tyrosine kinase pathways. In this interaction, AKT phosphorylates Sox-2, affecting its stability, nuclear localization, and activity, while Sox-2, in turn, acts as a downstream target that encourages stemness and therapeutic resistance. Sox-2 moderates Notch1 signaling to maintain the precursor pool in the adult SGZ, and there is a positive correlation between Sox-2 and nestin expression. , Previous studies have reported that deletion of Sox-2 in adult rats results in a loss of hippocampal neurogenesis, because Sox-2 is required for maintaining the proliferation of NSCs and intermediate progenitor cells (IPCs), and Sox-2 in adult rats disappears as these cells differentiate into neuroblasts.

In the present study, the l-met group showed a significant decrease in the neuronal stem cell marker Sox-2 and the immature neuron marker DCX compared to the vehicle group. These results demonstrate that l-met administration contributes to a reduction in hippocampal neurogenesis. This finding is consistent with a previous study on brain aging induced by D-galactose, which reported that reduced Sox-2 expression negatively affects cell proliferation associated with the neurogenic niche. However, CA treatment led to a significant increase in Sox-2-positive cells compared to the vehicle group. Interestingly, the combined administration of CA and l-met can improve Sox-2-positive cell levels compared to both the vehicle and the l-met groups, as well as enhance cell differentiation compared to the l-met group in the SGZ of the hippocampal dentate gyrus. These findings suggest that CA can mitigate the negative effect of l-met by preventing the reduction of neuronal stem cell expression and promoting cell differentiation. The current study supports previous findings that hyperhomocysteinemia causes apoptosis, neuronal damage, and inflammation. However, CA can also lessen the adverse effects of l-met through strong antioxidant properties, which protect against free radical-induced oxidative stress. ,,,

Oxygen-centered free radicals are known as ROS. Oxidative stress refers to the biological damage caused by an excess of ROS. An imbalance between ROS production and the cell’s antioxidant capacity leads to oxidative stress. According to a previous study, chronic l-met administration showed a significant increase in Hcy serum levels associated with oxidative agent production via homocysteine thiolactone, which is a highly reactive radical unstable electron atom. In this study, rats that received l-met exhibited increased ROS production, leading to oxidative stress and a significant rise in MDA levels due to lipid peroxidation in the hippocampal and prefrontal cortex regions compared to the vehicle group. Moreover, increased oxidative stress in the hippocampus is likely the primary cause of neuronal damage and memory impairment, resulting from the sulfur-containing property of Hcy. Oxidative stress, brain defects, and cognitive and memory dysfunction are all significantly influenced by HHcy. Recent findings indicate that chronic l-met consumption is associated with a significant decline in the enzymatic activities of GPx, CAT, and SOD. However, these declines were prevented with the administration of CA. Furthermore, treatment with CA mitigated these effects, suggesting that oxidative stress plays a role in the interaction between CA and l-met.

Interestingly, the current study found that the increase in Nrf2 expression in the prefrontal cortex and the hippocampal regions was associated with a rise in antioxidant enzyme levels. In order to preserve cellular redox equilibrium, Nrf2 plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular redox balance by regulating the production of antioxidants. During oxidative stress, the disruption of Keap1 and Nrf2 interaction triggers Nrf2 translocation into the nucleus, where it binds to the antioxidant response element (ARE). Following this, Nrf2 stimulates the transcription of numerous genes related to detoxification and antioxidants, including those encoding enzymes like heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), CAT, SOD, and GPx. These enzymes lessen free radicals and oxidative stress in cells. Consequently, the Nrf2/ARE signaling pathway serves as a defense mechanism for antioxidants. This study found that the l-met group exhibited a significant decrease in Nrf2 expression compared to the vehicle and combined groups. In contrast, both the CA group and the CA with l-met group showed a significant increase in Nrf2 expression in the hippocampus. Similarly, a prior study demonstrated that the CA micronutrient protects the brains of mice from Aβ-induced neurodegeneration and memory impairment through the Nrf2-HO-1 signaling pathway in an AD rat model. Additionally, a CA polyphenol ester derivative prevents oxidative stress-induced neuronal cell apoptosis and scopolamine-induced cognitive impairment by activating Nrf2/HO-1 signaling.

Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) is a neurotrophin essential for processes such as neurogenesis and neuronal survival. BDNF also has significant affects both presynaptic and postsynaptic levels, making it a crucial modulator of synaptic plasticity. The BDNF signaling pathway enhances the activity of the cAMP response element binding protein (CREB) transcription factor, which regulates nerve cell survival and promotes synaptic plasticity and neurogenesis. , This study found that l-met administration was associated with a significant decrease in hippocampal BDNF expression compared to the vehicle and combined groups. However, treatment with CA prevented the decreases in BDNF expression in both the CA and combined groups compared to the vehicle group. Supporting this, previous studies have shown that increasing BDNF expression and reducing neuroinflammatory reactions can help mitigate cognitive impairment. Dietary methionine restriction may reduce cognitive impairment in sleep-deprived mice and CA significantly increased the expression of these markers in the brains of mice given Aβ injections. Moreover, the binding of BDNF to its TrkB (Tropomyosin receptor Kinase B) receptor facilitates the dimerization and autophosphorylation of cytoplasmic tyrosine residues, initiating the activation of a diverse array of signaling pathways, including ERK, PI3K, and the generation of second messengers such as cAMP, inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3) and Ca2+ crucial for neural development, survival, and plasticity that promote neuronal growth. A study in hippocampal neurons revealed that BDNF activates Nrf2, evidenced by BDNF-induced increases in Nrf2 gene targets. Conversely, Nrf2 activation with fluoxetine raises BDNF expression levels in a mouse model of anxiety and depression. Furthermore, a recent study found that while antioxidant therapy restores redox equilibrium, lower hippocampal levels of BDNF in a model of depressed rats result in decreased nuclear translocation of Nrf2, causing persistent oxidative stress. These findings demonstrate that CA effectively increased BDNF expression via bidirectional crosstalk, thereby improving Nrf2 translocation to the nucleus and enhancing oxidative enzyme activities after binding with TrkB receptor. This process is essential for memory, neurogenesis, and synaptic plasticity in the hippocampus. Numerous investigations have demonstrated that individuals with AD and other neurodegenerative diseases exhibit ectopic expression of stem cells, such as Sox-2 and immature neurons like DCX in the hippocampal dentate gyrus. BDNF can enhance the migration of NSCs expressing Sox-2. Conversely, Sox-2 regulates BDNF gene expression, as well as NSC survival, proliferation, and differentiation into neurons. These interactions frequently cooperate to create a synergistic loop for neurogenesis. This study showed that Sox-2 expression in the CA and combined groups was significantly higher than in the vehicle group in the hippocampus. The interaction between the Nrf2 and Sox-2 pathways is crucial for maintaining cell pluripotency, stemness, and survival, particularly in cancer and development. Nrf2 shields cells from oxidative stress, while Sox-2 controls stem cell identity. Nrf2 can affect Sox-2 expression, and both pathways work together to regulate genes essential for self-renewal and stress resistance.

Conclusions

The neuroprotective effects of CA against hyperhomocysteinemia in adult rats are demonstrated by the increase in neural stem cells, an immature neuron marker, as well as a decrease in MDA overproduction. Additionally, a rise in GPx, CAT, and SOD activity was observed in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex. These discoveries may lead to future therapies for cognitive impairment.
