Long-term survival outcomes following total shoulder arthroplasty: An implant-level systematic review and meta-analysis of case series and registry data with more than 13 years follow-up
Authors: Faheem Bhatti, Saima Waseem, Hemant Singh, Robert Scott, Jenna Shepherd, Pip Divall, Niel Kang, Harvinder Pal Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsIt is known that survival of primary total shoulder arthroplasty at 10 years is excellent; however, evidence beyond this time-point is limited. We aimed to evaluate the long-term survival of anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (aTSA) and reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) implants, at ≥13 years follow-up, using case series and international joint registry data.MethodsCENTRAL, Embase, and MEDLINE databases were searched for studies reporting TSA survival data at ≥13 years follow-up. National joint registries were contacted for implant data. Pooled survival at 13, 15, and 20 years was calculated for case series and registry data using a random-effects model.ResultsData from four national joint registries and seven studies were analysed. Pooled aTSA registry data showed survival of 91.50% (89.36–93.64) at 13-years and 90.82% (88.25–93.38) at 15-years. Pooled rTSA registry data showed survival of 93.19% (91.78–94.60) at 13-years and 90.74% (85.73–95.74) at 15-years. Data from case series gave pooled aTSA survival estimates of 88.69% (76.97–100.42), 90.23% (85.87–94.58) and 82.08% (70.30–93.88) at 13, 15 and 20 years, respectively.ConclusionOur analysis suggests approximately 90% of shoulder replacements last up to 15 years without revision. Variation between individual implants highlights the need for granular implant-level data to guide patient and clinician decision making.Keywords: Shoulder, arthroplasty, joint replacement, survival, long-term, registry

Introduction

Total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) is a treatment for end-stage glenohumeral osteoarthritis. 1 The two main types of TSA are the anatomic (aTSA), requiring an intact rotator cuff, and reverse (rTSA), preferred in cuff deficiency. 2 The number of TSAs performed is rising; 8221 primary procedures were performed in the UK in 2023 compared to 7354 in 2022, with similar growth seen in Australia and forecasted to continue.3–5

Short- and mid-term aTSA and rTSA outcomes (survival, clinical, and patient-reported outcome measures [PROMs]) are well reported, but a paucity of data beyond 10 years exists.6–8 A meta-analysis of case series and registry data in 2020 estimated the 10-year revision-free survival of TSA to be 92–94.7%. 6 As life expectancy increases and total shoulder replacements are increasingly performed in younger, active patients, there is understandable angst regarding how long these implants last.9,10 Particularly given revision surgery is associated with a high risk of post-operative complications and need for re-operation. 11 Understanding the long-term outcomes following aTSA and rTSA is therefore vital towards guiding patient and clinician decision making. It also contributes to evaluating whether patients with shoulder arthritis are best treated with aTSA or rTSA; a research priority identified in the 2015 James Lind Alliance. 12

Ongoing implant development means survival outcomes should be assessed at the implant level; two aTSA implants, despite sharing an anatomic design, may achieve different long-term outcomes. National arthroplasty databases offer real-world, implant-specific survival data over increasing follow-up periods and complement data from published case series. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the long-term (≥13 years) survival aTSA and rTSA implants using case series and international registry data. The 13-year time-point was selecting in line with the Orthopaedic Data Evaluating Panel benchmarking process. 13

Methods

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of case series and joint registry data, registered with PROSPERO on 2 October 2024 (CRD42024594803) and reported according to PRISMA guidelines. 14

Information sources and search strategy

National joint registries were contacted between September and December 2024 and requested to provide implant-level survival data, with confidence intervals, at 13 and 15 years in a structured form. Data was obtained from the Kaiser Permanente Shoulder Arthroplasty Registry, New Zealand Orthopaedic Association Joint Registry, and Norwegian Arthroplasty Register. Data from the Australian Orthopaedic Association National Joint Replacement Registry was obtained from the most recent Annual Report. 4 The most recent reports from the aforementioned registries, and the UK National Joint Registry (NJR), were reviewed to determine whether the implants were still in use.

Embase, MEDLINE, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) databases were searched on 24 June 2024 for studies reporting survival outcomes at ≥13 years after TSA. In view of a similar systematic review published by Evans et al. in 2020, the search results were filtered to only include studies published after the systematic search performed in that review. 6 The review was screened for relevant studies which were then included in this analysis. 6 Full search terms are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Eligibility criteria

Studies reporting implant-specific survival outcomes following aTSA or rTSA at ≥13 years follow-up were included. Procedures such as hemiarthroplasty, resurfacing, and revision arthroplasty were excluded. Studies which did not specify the implant used, reported outcomes on a mixture of implants, or did not provide confidence intervals or standard error values were excluded. The indication for surgery in at least 50% of the study population had to be osteoarthritis. Studies in which the indication for over 50% of all procedures was fracture, avascular necrosis, inflammatory arthritis, or post-infectious arthritis were excluded. Conference abstracts were excluded but checked for any following full publications. Reference lists of systematic reviews were checked for relevant studies. Screening and data extraction

Titles and abstracts were screened in Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Australia) by two blinded reviewers with conflicts resolved through consensus discussion. Full texts were similarly reviewed. Data was extracted from included studies into Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Excel for Mac, Version 16.88, Microsoft Corporation) by two blinded reviewers. Items extracted included author, publication year, study design, number of TSAs, demographics, indication for surgery, implant used, and follow-up duration. The key outcome was survival data at ≥13 years post-surgery. Data was not extracted from figures (e.g. Kaplan–Meier plots) to avoid inaccuracies. The data extraction of the two reviewers (FB and HS) was compared, and discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion.

Quality assessment

The non-summative four-point system of study quality assessment developed by Wylde et al. was used in keeping with similar reviews on arthroplasty survival.6,15,16 The following parameters were assessed: inclusion of consecutive cases, representativeness (i.e. multicentre), percentage follow-up >80%, and minimisation of potential confounding (i.e. multivariable or univariable analysis). 15 Each parameter was scored as adequate, inadequate, or not reported.

Statistical analysis and synthesis methods

Meta-analysis was performed to estimate survival of anatomic and reverse shoulder replacements at 13 years, 15 years, and 20 years, analysing case series and registry data separately. These time-points are in keeping with those used in the Orthopaedic Data Evaluation Panel rating system. 13 The patient populations of case series originating from the same institution were reviewed, specifically in terms of (1) the number of patients, (2) the dates between which arthroplasty procedures took place, and (3) demographics to ensure studies included in the meta-analyses were not reporting on the same population of patients. Cumulative revision rates reported by national joint registries were used to approximate survivorship. A random-effects model (restricted maximum likelihood method) was used and forest plots were generated. All analyses were performed in STATA (Stata Statistical Software: Release 18. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC).

Results

Registry results:

The Australian, Kaiser, New Zealand, and Norwegian national joint registries reported 44 aTSA implant/component series (13259 shoulders) and 21 rTSA implant/component series (36525 shoulders) with survival data at ≥13 years of follow-up. Of the 44 aTSA series, survival was reported for 40 implant/component series at 13 years (number of shoulders at risk = 1246), three implant/component series from the Australian registry at 14 years (number of shoulders at risk = 120), and 31 implant/component series at 15 years (number of shoulders at risk = 528). Of the 21 rTSA implant/component series, survival was reported by 17 implant/component series at 13 years (number of shoulders at risk = 333), four implant/component series from the Australian registry at 14 years (number of shoulders at risk = 120), and six implant/component series at 15 years (total number of shoulders at risk = 135). For meta-analysis, the three aTSA implant series and four rTSA implant series reporting outcomes at 14 years were pooled with those studies reporting survival analysis at 13 years. No significant difference was noted between aTSA and rTSA implants at 13 or 15 years (p = 0.20 and p = 0.97, respectively). Heterogeneity was high (I2 > 75%) across the analyses. Case series results

Database searches yielded 928 unique studies, of which four were eligible for inclusion in this review.18–21 The reference list of a recent systematic review on this topic revealed a further three eligible studies,6,22–24 giving a total of seven studies published between 2009 and 202318–24 as shown in Figure 5. All seven studies provided survival analysis of aTSA implants. No studies reporting the survival of rTSA implants, at a minimum of 13 years were identified. Across the seven studies, eight unique aTSA implant series were reported, encompassing 711 aTSAs. The mean age of patients was 65.7 years, with 51.5% being female, and 94.3% of all procedures being performed for a primary diagnosis of osteoarthritis. Survival was reported at 13 years (3 implant series),19,23,2415 years (5 implant series),18,20,22,23 and 20 years (2 implant series).20,21 The Aequalis implant was used in three series,20,23,24 the Bigliani/Flatow Complete Shoulder Solution TSA was used in three series,18,21 whilst the Eclipse 19 and Neer II 22 implants were each used in one series. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis is the first to utilise case series and international arthroplasty registry data to provide survivorship estimates for aTSA and rTSA at 13 and 15 years. Our results demonstrate good implant longevity for both aTSA and rTSA implants with pooled registry survival estimates exceeding 90% at 13 and 15 years.

Case series reporting survivorship at a minimum of 13 years were only available for aTSA implants. The pooled survival estimates from case series for aTSA were 88.69% (95% CI 76.97–100.42) at 13 years, 90.23% (85.87–94.58) at 15 years, and 82.08% (70.30–93.88) at 20 years. Notably, relatively few studies, with small sample sizes (34–308), contributed to these estimates which may potentially explain the inconsistency between the 13- and 15- year survival estimates. Furthermore, several implants contributing to the pooled estimates are now less commonly used. Whilst the Eclipse implant remains in use, the Aequalis implant has been modified, the Bigliani/Flatow implant remains in use but now comprises a smaller proportion of all TSAs performed, and Neer II implants are seldom used.3,4,17 Additionally, all but one study utilised single-centre patient recruitment, thus results could have been influenced by each individual centre's approach towards deciding when to revise or not revise implants.18–22,24 In the context of a previous review of the topic, in which 10-year TSA survival in case series was estimated at 94.6%, our results suggest a gradual reduction in implant survivorship occurs over time. 6

The analysis of registry data provides a more robust estimate of implant survival due to a greater number of shoulders at risk and wider implant representation. Pooled survivorship of aTSA implants was estimated at 91.50% (95% CI 89.36–93.64) at 13 years and 90.82% (95% CI 88.25–93.38) at 15 years based on 1366 and 528 shoulders at risk, respectively. For rTSA implants, the corresponding pooled survival estimates were 93.19% (95% CI 91.78–94.60) at 13 years and 90.74% (95% CI 85.73–95.74) at 15 years from analysis of 493 and 135 shoulders at risk, respectively. Thus, the previously reported registry TSA survival of 92% at 10 years appears to be relatively well maintained through to 15 years. 6 Our analysis revealed no significant differences between aTSA and rTSA survival (p = 0.20 at 13 years, p = 0.97 at 15 years).

Substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 75%) was noted within the meta-analyses of registry implant series for both aTSA and rTSA. This may be explained by the differences in implant design resulting in different implant survival within the aTSA and rTSA groups. Innovation within shoulder arthroplasty has led to implant development and changes in implant utilisation. Several implants included in the analysis are no longer in clinical use, having been superseded by newer designs. This is particularly relevant for the aTSA analysis in which 26 of the 44 included implant/component series are no longer routinely used.3,4,17 One example of this is the Lima SMR implant which had 13-year survival of 73.50% and 77.94% in the Australian and New Zealand registries, a vastly lower survival rate compared to those reported for other implants. This was determined to be due to the polyethylene liner separating from the metal-backed portion and resulted in its recall from use. 25 Further outliers noted were the Nottingham Anatomic Total Shoulder system and the Tornier Affiniti Anatomic Shoulder system from the American Kaiser registry; however, this may in part be due to a low number of implants at risk at 13 years (4 and 7 shoulders, respectively). Collectively this demonstrates the nuances in interpreting overall aTSA and rTSA survival figures reported in registry reports, as they may be skewed by a subset of particularly poorly performing implants and/or implants which are no longer in use today. Thus, prior implant survival performance may not be representative of what patients can expect from the implants used in current practice, highlighting a need to review survivorship at implant level.

Our analysis is limited by 13-year survival data only being available from four national arthroplasty registries and relatively small numbers of shoulders with follow-up to this time-point. This is notable for rTSA, as only 493 and 135 shoulders were followed to 13 and 15 years, respectively. Furthermore, as discussed, the pooled survival estimates incorporate implants which are no longer commonly used and may be skewed by these implants. Over the coming years, future registry data will support long-term comparisons between aTSA and rTSA. For example, the UK NJR currently reports TSA survival outcomes to 11 years. 3 One area of interest will be the performance of stemless aTSA implants. Stemless aTSA implants account for the most used aTSA implants in 2023 in Australia (Affinis (n = 442) and Simpliciti (n = 257)) and New Zealand (Affinis short stem (n = 43) and SMR stemless (n = 34) as per the respective most recently published annual registry reports.4,17 A similar trend is seen in the UK, with the Affinis implant (n = 232) being the second most used aTSA implant after the stemmed Aequalis Ascend Flex implant (n = 277). 3 Stemless aTSA implants are currently out to 10 years of follow-up in the AONJRR and 11 years in the UK NJR.3,4 Both registries report lower revision rates amongst stemless aTSA compared to stemmed aTSA, and future implant-level registry data will help better analyse the differences between these implants.3,4 Due to a low number of stemless implant series in our dataset, this could not be explored here.

Similarly, future implant-level data on rTSA will be telling. The Comprehensive and Equinoxe implants were the two most used rTSAs in 2023 in Australia (1648 and 1316 shoulders, respectively), whilst the SMR and Perform, and Equinoxe and Delta Xtend implants were the two most used rTSA implants in 2023 in New Zealand and the UK, respectively.3,4,17 A total of 134 Delta Xtend implants were followed to 13 years in our analysis; however, only 3 Comprehensive, 1 Equinoxe, and no Perform implants were at risk at this time-point. Thus, the long-term survival outcomes of some of the most used rTSA implants, at and beyond the 13-year time-point, remain awaited.

Further limitations include case series being identified which did not report survival outcomes with confidence intervals or standard errors values, therefore precluding their inclusion in this meta-analysis.26,27 Effort was made to ensure that studies included in the meta-analysis, which originated from the same institutions,18,21 were not reporting on duplicate shoulders through review of the number of patients included in each series, the dates between which procedures occurred, and patient demographics. Whilst deemed to be sufficiently different, there does exist a risk of overlap in the patients included in the case series from Brochin et al. and Duey at al. which is of relevance to our pooled 15-year survival estimate that both studies contribute towards.18,21 Additionally, a pragmatic decision was made to include studies in which at least 50% of TSA procedures were performed for either glenohumeral osteoarthritis or rotator cuff arthropathy, in keeping with the methodology of similar studies.6,16 However, it must be acknowledged that the indication for surgery may influence the survival outcomes following TSA. Patients undergoing TSA for glenohumeral osteoarthritis or rotator cuff arthropathy represent a distinct cohort from those treated for proximal humerus fractures, with lower rates of surgical complications and reoperation.28,29 Furthermore, outcomes following rTSA for primary osteoarthritis may differ from those for rotator cuff arthropathy.30,31 A retrospective cohort study of 414 shoulders demonstrated rTSA revision rates were five times higher when performed for osteoarthritis secondary to a rotator cuff tear, and three times higher for primary osteoarthritis with an accompanying rotator cuff tear, compared to primary osteoarthritis alone (5.4% vs 3.1% vs 0.8%, respectively). 30 Similarly, a larger retrospective cohort of 1611 shoulders reported a 2-year revision rate of 3.5% for rTSA performed for primary osteoarthritis compared to 5.0% for cuff tear arthropathy. 31 Due to an absence of data stratified by indication for surgery, it was not possible to explore this within this systematic review and meta-analysis.

Finally, implant revision-free survival is just one tool for assessing TSA outcomes. A paucity of data on clinical outcome measures and PROMs at long-term follow-up means that survival data is currently the most readily available proxy. However, using survival has its limitations given the decision for revision is one influenced by both patient, surgeon, and implant factors. Moreover, implant survival is not always concordant with patient satisfaction. 32 With PROMs increasingly being recorded in national arthroplasty databases, this data may emerge over the coming years and be more informative in helping patients to better understand how their shoulder may feel after surgery and therefore guide perioperative consultations between patients and clinicians.

Conclusion

Our analysis of national arthroplasty registry data has shown that approximately 90% of aTSA and rTSA survive to 15 years without need for revision suggesting good implant longevity. No significant differences were noted between the pooled survivorship estimates of aTSA or rTSA at 13 and 15 years. Significant heterogeneity exists within the two groups of TSA emphasising the importance of evaluating survival at implant level. There exists a need for further implant-level registry data, which may emerge over the coming years, to better understand the differences between implants and ultimately guide perioperative consultations between patients and clinicians.
