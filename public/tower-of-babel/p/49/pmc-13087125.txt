Study on establishment of cardiovascular interventional disease database and prediction of postoperative mortality risk
Authors: Pengjia Qi, Canjie Hu, Yong Li, Yang Chen, Yanyun Dai, Huaqing Zhang, Jijun Tong, Shudong Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn order to solve the problems of missing, discontinuous, and unstructured data in past cardiovascular interventional studies, this work constructed a database of specific cardiovascular interventional diseases. Within one year of its implementation in a top-three hospital in Zhejiang Province, the database collected a total of 728 cases of cardiovascular interventional patients, realizing the structuring of patient data and 360° whole-cycle management. With the support of a specific disease database, we proposed an improved LSTM-BLS model to predict the risk of death after cardiovascular interventions. Compared with the traditional long-short term memory (LSTM) model, the parallel learning structure-width learning system (BLS) was introduced to calculate the weights directly, which can solve the problems of overfitting and delay caused by the deep structure of LSTM, so as to improve the accuracy of prediction. The experimental results showed that the accuracy rate of the proposed model was 87.46%, the precision was 90.74%, and recall rate was 93.61%, which can objectively reflect the postoperative death risk of patients, and help doctors to make timely medical intervention for patients with high death risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-39788-7.Keywords: Cardiovascular intervention, Postoperative death risk prediction, Long-short term memory (LSTM), Broad learning system (BLS)Subject terms: Biomedical engineering, Information technology, Cardiology, Medical research, Risk factors, Engineering, Health care

Introduction

Cardiovascular interventional therapy refers to the minimally invasive diagnosis and treatment of cardiovascular diseases through percutaneous puncture with puncture needle, special catheter, guide wire and other precision instruments introduced into blood vessels in the body under the guidance of medical imaging equipment1,2. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the number of deaths attributed to cardiovascular disease in 2016 was 17.9 million, which is 31% of the total global deaths, and it is expected that up to 23 million people will die from cardiovascular disease by 20303,4. According to research in China, the total number of cardiovascular patients has reached 330 million, with an annual death toll of over 4 million. Among them, the mortality rate of cardiovascular intervention patients ranks first among various intervention treatments, with an average mortality rate of 12.7%5,6. Therefore, in many postoperative evaluation indicators of patients with cardiovascular intervention, the risk of postoperative death has always been an important indicator for doctors and patients, because it reflects the quality of interventional treatment, the survival status of patients after surgery and the effect of medical intervention7. However, most hospitals have not yet established cardiovascular intervention disease databases, and patient data generally suffers from issues such as missing, discontinuous, and unstructured data. Therefore, the prediction of postoperative mortality risk by doctors is still based on the personal clinical experience or relevant statistical methods of medical personnel, and the accuracy of prediction is relatively low. The above methods are no longer applicable in today’s digital healthcare era. Therefore, it is very important to establish a cardiovascular intervention disease database, assist doctors in using information technology to record patients’ full cycle medical data, and track their postoperative health status8. In addition, it is also very meaningful to establish a postoperative mortality risk prediction model by mining the intrinsic correlation of patient data, assisting doctors in timely medical intervention for patients with high mortality risk, and reducing patient mortality rate9.

The specialized disease database plays a crucial role in the medical development of various countries. For example, the United States collects, integrates, and analyzes national disease data through national disease monitoring systems such as the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the National Cancer Institute (NCI), providing support for disease monitoring, epidemiological research, and policy-making10. In addition, the Canadian chronic disease monitoring system includes cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer and other disease fields11. Its construction is dedicated to monitoring and reporting the epidemic trend of chronic diseases. The UK National Health Service (NHS) electronic health record system collects clinical data from patients nationwide, providing data support for researchers and clinical physicians12. The European Society of Cardiology has promoted the establishment of the European cardiovascular research program, which integrates cardiovascular patient data from multiple European countries for research and clinical practice of cardiovascular disease13. Under the guidance of the “Healthy China 2030” plan, many hospitals in China have begun to establish specialized disease databases for specific diseases such as coronary heart disease and cerebrovascular disease14–16. These specialized disease databases collect and manage patient data in specific fields, playing a driving role in the research and treatment of related diseases. Researchers and doctors can use clinical databases as data sources to extract, transform, and load them to form disease research databases, which can greatly reduce the workload of data processing in the early stages of scientific research and improve research efficiency.

On the basis of establishing a specific disease database and combining information technology to predict the condition of patients and analyze risk factors, it can not only help researchers to study the disease, but also help doctors to timely medical intervention for high-risk patients to reduce the risk of death. For example, Bhera et al. combined support vector machines and improved particle swarm optimization models to create a hybrid model for heart and liver data classification to perform death risk factor analysis for patients with heart disease and liver disease17. Theerthagiri et al. adopted a gradient lifting method based on recursive feature elimination, using patients’ health records to evaluate the results, and identified that mortality could be reduced by avoiding or reducing the severity of cardiovascular diseases18. Singh et al. discussed the common reasons behind the reduced prediction of drug targets based on the support vector machine model, so as to predict the incidence of tumors19. Annamalai et al. proposed to use convolutional neural networks based on the optimal auction mechanism to predict lung diseases, and finally proved through performance analysis that the proposed method could extract features from X-ray images and accurately predict lung diseases20. Sudha et al. used a hybrid CNN-LSTM model to predict heart disease, and verified it with K-fold cross-validation technology, and the accuracy of the final hybrid model reached 89%21. Liang et al. proposed tBNA-PR, a temporal bidirectional neural network model with attention mechanism, to select patients’ electronic health record data for heart failure disease prediction and stratification22. Sun et al. proposed to use multi-strategy optimized nuclear extreme learning machine to study the mortality of heart disease and liver disease, and achieved good prediction effect in clinical application23. Islam et al. selected 12 different machine learning classifiers to study and analyze chronic kidney disease in the supervised learning environment, and concluded that the accuracy rate of XGBoost classifier was the highest, reaching 0.98324.

This study validates the feasibility of disease-specific database-enabled AI prediction for cardiovascular intervention, focusing on scarce high-quality structured full-cycle datasets and traditional AI models like LSTM with overfitting and prediction lag. We developed an innovative targeted cardiovascular intervention database with standardized multi-source data and 360° full-cycle management, offering a valuable clinical research resource. We proposed a novel LSTM-BLS hybrid architecture. Patient lifecycle data enters LSTM for temporal feature capture, passes through a Dropout layer to mitigate overfitting and a full connection layer, then feeds into BLS with support from dedicated LSTM training and BLS forecast modules. This design resolves LSTM’s flaws while leveraging BLS’s fast inference. Comparative experiments with DNN, RNN and traditional LSTM confirm its superior postoperative mortality risk prediction. It provides clinicians a reliable decision-support tool for high-risk patient intervention and informs similar studies in other disease-specific interventional fields.

Database and methods

Construction and implementation of cardiovascular interventional disease database

User demand analysis of special disease database

The users of the cardiovascular interventional database are divided into medical staff, scientific researcher and managerial personnel. The Unified Modeling Language (UML) use case diagram for all users is shown in Fig. 1.

UML use case diagram for medical staff, scientific researcher and management personnel.

(1) Medical staff.

As the daily users and maintainers of the database, medical staff in the cardiovascular intervention department are assigned their account information by their supervising doctors, which specifically includes the following functional requirements:

(a) Integrated information retrieval. The database provides convenient retrieval functions, so that medical staff can quickly search relevant information according to keywords such as patient physiological information and medication information.

(b) Clinical decision support. medical staff need databases to provide comprehensive and accurate clinical information to support their decision during diagnosis and treatment. Therefore, the database will provide pathophysiological characteristics, clinical manifestations, diagnostic criteria and other information of cardiovascular diseases, so that medical staff can better understand the characteristics of such diseases and give the best treatment plan.

(c) Quality improvement and evaluation. medical staff need to collect and analyze clinical data through the database to evaluate the effectiveness, safety, and quality of medical practices, to make recommendations for improvement, and to develop better care programs.

(d) Statistical analysis of data. The database contains advanced statistical tools that support a variety of statistical methods, data modeling and visualization to help medical staff analyze disease trends, prognostic factors and treatment effects, providing a scientific basis for clinical decision making.

(2) Scientific researcher.

For scientific researchers, the need for cardiovascular interventional disease database is mainly reflected in the collection and collation of data, which mainly includes:

(a) Large-scale access to clinical data. Scientific researchers need the cardiovascular interventional disease database to provide large-scale clinical data, including basic information of patients, clinical manifestations, examination results and follow-up data. Therefore, in the construction of the system, the database will be connected with HIS system, LIS system, EMR system and ICMS system of the interventional department of the hospital, and the data information from different systems will be collated into the cardiovascular interventional disease database.

(b) Data integrity and continuity. Cardiovascular interventional disease database should ensure the integrity and continuity of patient data to avoid data loss.

(c) Data diversity and multi-dimensionality. In addition to paper record information, the cardiovascular interventional disease database should collect multi-modal data information of patients, such as image information, time series information, etc.

(d) Data quality and standardization. Since the data entry standards of different information systems in hospitals are different, the standard should be unified when the data are collected into the cardiovascular interventional disease database to ensure data quality and standardization.

(3) Management personnel.

The management personnel of the cardiovascular interventional disease database are mainly responsible for the control of users and permissions, data security maintenance, etc., and the system administrator is generally composed of the director of the interventional department. Analysis and processing of specific disease database data module

(1) Data source analysis.

The primary task of building a cardiovascular interventional disease database is to collect relevant data of cardiovascular interventional patients, including various business systems of the hospital (HIS, LIS, EMR, etc.), various medical forms (outpatient medical records, outpatient medical orders, test reports, etc.) and data platforms of the hospital (demographic data, treatment data and follow-up data). The source analysis of the data in the specialized disease database is shown in Fig. 2.

Data source analysis of patients with cardiovascular intervention.

(2) Data module analysis.

The data modules of the cardiovascular interventional disease database are divided into patient baseline information, preoperative examination information, intraoperative surgery information, and postoperative follow-up information, as shown in Fig. 3.

Schematic diagram of data module of cardiovascular interventional disease database.

a. Patient baseline information.

The baseline information of patients with cardiovascular intervention is the initial data when patients enter the medical system or research project, which is generally integrated and exported through the hospital’s HIS system and PACS system. It includes personal information (sex, age, height, weight and contact information), medical information (previous illnesses, surgical history and family medical history, etc.), chief complaint and symptom information (the patient’s main problems such as chest pain, difficulty breathing and a detailed description of symptoms), lifestyle information (eating habits, exercise, smoking and drinking history, etc.), and laboratory tests results (blood, urine, biochemistry, immunology, etc.). This information is essential for diagnosing, treating, and studying cardiovascular disease, helping doctors and researchers to fully understand patients’ conditions, develop personalized treatment plans, evaluate treatment effects, and conduct scientific research and data analysis.

b. Preoperative examination information.

Preoperative patient information is a series of assessments performed before undergoing surgery or interventional therapy to determine the patient’s suitability for surgery, assess surgical risks, and develop a surgical plan. This information is mainly integrated and derived from the hospital’s HIS system, PACS system and EMR system, including clinical evaluation (detailed review of patient history, symptoms and signs, physical examination to assess overall health and surgical risk), laboratory examination (blood routine, biochemical indicators, coagulation function, etc., assessment of hemoglobin level and liver and kidney coagulation function, etc.). Make sure there are no metabolic disorders or other abnormalities), angiography or interventional tests (to determine the degree of vascular stenosis, blood flow and location of the lesion), and other specific tests (such as myocardial perfusion imaging, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, cerebral angiography, etc.) depending on the type of surgery and the patient’s needs. These comprehensive assessments help doctors gain a comprehensive understanding of the patient’s health and disease, ensuring the safety and success of the surgery.

c. Intraoperative surgical information.

Intraoperative patient information is critical data collected during surgery or interventional therapy to comprehensively monitor the surgical process and evaluate the outcome. The information was integrated and exported through the hospital’s HIS system, PACS system and nurses’ surgical records. It includes the type and time of surgery (exact name of surgery and time of operation), details of surgery (instruments used, incision location and size, instrument layout), observations and events during the procedure (such as vascular injury, arrhythmia, bleeding, etc.), and surgical conclusions (completion of surgery and postoperative management). These records provide a detailed basis for the evaluation of postoperative conditions, surgical effects, complication analysis and scientific research, and help doctors and researchers to deeply understand the performance and quality of surgery.

d. Postoperative follow-up information.

The key information of postoperative follow-up is to monitor the recovery of patients, evaluate the operation effect and detect the complications in the early stage. This information is usually integrated and derived through the hospital’s post-hospital follow-up system, including postoperative observation (pain degree, temperature, heart rate, respiration, etc.), vital signs monitoring (blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate and other indicators are regularly measured), and drug treatment (including drug name, dose, and frequency of medication). By recording and analyzing this follow-up information in detail, doctors and medical teams are able to assess the patient’s post-operative recovery progress, adjust treatment protocols accordingly, and implement necessary interventions to optimize personalized treatment and care strategies.

(3) Data storage processing.

In the process of constructing the database of cardiovascular interventional patients, we adopted strict data processing measures to unify the data standards and structure in the face of the differences in the data entry standards of various business systems and medical forms in hospitals. In order to ensure data consistency and interoperability, this work adopts the cardiovascular disease guidelines of the Chinese Society of Cardiology, Health Grade 7 (HL7) standards and CDISC (Clinical Data Exchange Standards) alliance standards for data standard processing of various data sources under the advice of doctors. For the field design of the data, this work refers to the health information data element value field code standard, and sets 8 attributes for the data unit (Chinese name, English abbreviation, field type, field length, value range, allowed null value, source, remarks).

General design of specific disease database

As a medical information system, the database of cardiovascular interventional disease is designed by using enterprise Web technology stack. The system architecture adopts the Browser/Server (B/S) mode25 to build the system. Compared with the traditional C/S (Client-Server, server-client mode) mode, it has the advantages of lightweight, multi-concurrency, strong operation, etc., and is more suitable for the application of current medical information system. Choose Spring Boot to build background microservices, Vue framework to design front-end pages, and realize the development mode of separating front and back end development. This development mode can ensure the stability and maintainability of the system, and reduce the later system update work.

Because the disease database involves a large amount of medical data, and with the increase of the number of follow-up patients, the response speed and security of the disease database are put forward higher requirements. Therefore, this work chooses Oracle database to build the background data service of special disease database. Oracle Database26 is a relational database management system of Oracle Corporation. It has always been a leading product in the field of database, with its advantages of fast processing speed and high security level, which meets the needs of the construction of specialized disease database. Data processing

Dataset extraction

The extraction process of data set for postoperative death risk prediction study of cardiovascular interventional patients is shown in Fig. 5. Specifically, it can be divided into: (1) Formulate screening rules, screen patients who meet the criteria from the data of cardiovascular intervention specific diseases, and export patient data from the specific disease database. (2) Determine the starting point and end point of this work. (3) At the suggestion of doctors, the predictive features were extracted from patients’ baseline information, intraoperative information, preoperative examination information and postoperative follow-up information according to the data modules of the specialized disease database.

Sample diagram of data extraction.

In view of the complexity and specificity of patients with different cardiovascular interventions, after exchanging views with physicians in cardiovascular intervention departments, this work formulated screening criteria for patient data included in the database of cardiovascular intervention specific diseases according to the following criteria:

(a) The patient must be at least 18 years old;

(b) The patient was recorded in the hospital and completed cardiovascular interventional surgery;

(c) The patient’s preoperative, intraoperative and post-hospital follow-up information was continuous without interruption, and there was no information error entry;

(d) All pathological data were structured and complete, and physiological signs were recorded during post-hospital follow-up;

(e) Patient’s personal information shall be protected in accordance with the privacy regulations.

The starting point of this work was the establishment of personal information profiles upon admission, and the end point was death or non-death during the 6-month follow-up. After rigorous screening, the whole-cycle case data of 638 patients meeting the criteria were derived from the cardiovascular interventional disease database, with a total of 42,746 data items, including 41 deaths and 597 non-deaths six months after surgery.

Data cleaning and transformation

Specialized disease databases often contain missing values or outliers. They also include numerous non-numerical categorical features that cannot be directly processed. Therefore, this work adopted various data preprocessing methods to improve data quality, adapt to model input requirements and enhance feature representation.

(1) Handling of Missing Values and Outliers.

This work adopted a differentiation strategy according to the characteristics of variables and missing ratio27. For variables with high missing rate (such as LVPWD, missing rate reached 87.4%), they were directly eliminated. The category variables with a low proportion of missing variables (e.g., “hospitalization mode”) were filled in with mode. Continuous variables (e.g., aortic diastolic blood pressure, white blood cells, and hemoglobin) were imputed with means because of very low missing rates. Outliers were removed when the number of outliers was small, and replaced with values close to the normal range when the number was large to reduce the interference with the model. The matrix of missing values of baseline information features is shown in Figure S1 in the appendix.

(2) Feature Vectorization.

One-Hot encoding was used to convert discrete character-type data into numerical data. For binary classification problems, a 0/1 encoding scheme was adopted: male patients were encoded as 1 and female as 0; diabetic patients as 1 and non-diabetic as 0; intraoperative bleeding as 1 and no bleeding as 0; smokers as 1 and non-smokers as 0, etc. Additionally, ordinal scores in the medical records were classified using English letter grades. The numerical mapping table for major categorical variables is shown in Table S1 of the appendix.

(3) Handling of Comorbidity Features.

In addition, patients with cardiovascular interventions often have multiple chronic diseases, which will lead to high-dimensional sparsity if they are infused one by one. To integrate the information, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was introduced in this work28. By weighting and summing each comorbidity, a comprehensive index reflecting the disease burden of patients was generated, simplifying the feature dimension while retaining clinical significance. The table of CCI scoring criteria is detailed in Table S2 in the appendix.

Predictive models development

Feature selection based on Relief-F algorithm

This work used the improved Relief-F algorithm to select predictive features. Compared with the traditional Relief algorithm, the Relief-F algorithm introduces the weight variance when calculating feature weights and evaluation scores. Following data preprocessing, feature screening via the Relief-F algorithm, and integration with clinical expert input, this study ultimately retained 30 feature variables. These features are distributed across four categories including 10 patient baseline variables, 7 preoperative examination variables, 5 intraoperative surgical variables, and 8 postoperative follow-up variables. Figure S2 displays the feature weight curve, while Table 1 presents the complete list of these variables. Most of these 30 features such as BMI, CCI and Mitral Regurgitation exhibit significant group differences with p < 0.05 and strong associations with postoperative mortality as indicated by OR values whose 95% confidence intervals do not include 1. These findings validate their utility as robust predictors. Several features including Sex and Hypertension demonstrate weaker univariate associations but are retained in the model. Such features enhance comprehensive risk stratification in the multivariate model by complementing information from other variables and align with clinical consensus. Hypertension for instance is recognized as a core baseline condition in cardiovascular intervention patients. Incorporating all 30 features therefore balances high predictive accuracy with strong clinical applicability for the final model.

Architecture description

LSTM model29,30 is a special type of recurrent neural network (RNN). By introducing gating mechanism, LSTM model can better capture and deal with long-term dependencies in time series data, and can solve the problems of gradient disappearance and gradient explosion in traditional RNN. Although the LSTM model has shown good prediction effect in many disease prediction studies, the prediction model built based on LSTM has not solved the problem of overfitting, which also leads to the lag of prediction. The main reason for this problem is that LSTM needs to adjust the weight in the training process, while the Broad Learning System (BLS)31,32 can directly calculate the weight, which has the advantages of simple and fast operation. By combining the width learning system with LSTM, this work proposes a LSTM-BLS postoperative death risk prediction model. By introducing BLS, the LSTM-BLS model overcomes the limitation of weight adjustment in the traditional LSTM model, thus making more effective use of data information and improving the accuracy and efficiency of prediction. Moreover, BLS eliminates the disadvantages of long training time and provides good generalization ability on the basis of traditional stochastic vector function linked neural network.

Model training workflow

This study adopts the cross-entropy loss function to optimize model performance, with specific emphasis on addressing the class imbalance inherent in postoperative mortality prediction tasks. The cross-entropy function not only quantifies the discrepancy between predicted outputs and true labels with high precision but also provides robust gradient signals to guide model parameter updates. For imbalanced datasets, this loss function inherently assigns higher penalties to misclassifications of the minority class (postoperative deaths), effectively mitigating model bias toward the majority class (non-deceased patients) and ensuring the model prioritizes identifying high-risk cases. Performance indicators

The evaluation of algorithmic models is an important indicator to verify the effect of model construction, which is used to determine the performance and reliability of the model33,34. The LSTM-BLS model incorporates BLS, which features direct weight calculation. This avoids the problem of frequent weight adjustment, effectively alleviates overfitting, and improves the model’s generalization ability. Additionally, BLS has stronger data representation capabilities, enabling more efficient extraction of underlying information from data. Among the five evaluation indicators, LSTM-BLS demonstrates the best performance, with an accuracy of 87.46%, a precision of 90.74%, and a recall rate of 93.61%. The model exhibits excellent predictive effectiveness for postoperative death risk in patients, objectively reflecting their postoperative death risk and helping doctors timely implement medical interventions for patients at high death risk.

Conclusion

In this study, we proposed an improved LSTM-BLS hybrid model for postoperative mortality risk prediction in cardiovascular interventional patients, leveraging clinical data from 638 patients who underwent cardiovascular interventional procedures at a Grade A tertiary hospital in Zhejiang Province. Guided by MeSH medical standards and cardiovascular disease guidelines, we standardized the data processing and used the Relief-F algorithm to screen 30 clinically relevant key features associated with postoperative mortality risk, ensuring the model inputs align with clinical medical principles. The proposed LSTM-BLS model integrates LSTM’s capability to capture temporal dependencies in sequential clinical data with the fast convergence and efficient decision-making advantages of the BLS module, effectively addressing the overfitting and lag issues of traditional LSTM models. Comparative experiments with DNN, RNN, and traditional LSTM models demonstrate that our LSTM-BLS model achieves superior performance, with an Accuracy of 87.46%, Precision of 90.74%, and Recall of 93.61, enabling it to objectively assess postoperative mortality risks and support clinicians in timely interventions for high-risk patients. We acknowledge that this study has certain limitations, including the single-center nature of the dataset and the need for enhanced model interpretability to better align with clinical translation requirements. In future work, we will address these limitations by expanding to multi-center clinical data to improve the model’s generalizability and applying advanced interpretability techniques such as SHAP to clarify the model’s decision-making mechanisms, thereby further promoting the clinical applicability of our findings.
