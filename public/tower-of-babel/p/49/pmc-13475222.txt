What Resources Are Available for Parents Online? A Readability, Quality, and Source Evaluation of Website and AI-Generated Content on Inherited Pediatric Orthopaedic Conditions
Authors: Daniel S. Hayes, Elizabeth H. Blank, Ryan D. Muchow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndividuals frequently consult the internet when seeking information about their health. However, patient education materials (PEMs) published online are often written above the recommended sixth-to eighth-grade reading level. The readability and quality of online resources for inherited conditions are poorly studied. Additionally, little is known about the reading level of responses from artificial intelligence (AI) models when prompted with healthcare inquiries. We aimed to assess the readability and quality of online health information encountered when searching for inherited pediatric orthopaedic conditions, including outputs from popular large language model (LLM) chatbots.MethodsTwenty-three inherited pediatric orthopaedic conditions were queried using Google in three common search formats. First-page search results, including Google AI Overviews, were analyzed. Searches were replicated in three chatbots: Microsoft Copilot, ChatGPT, and Google Gemini. Readability was assessed using Flesch Reading Ease (FRE) and Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL).ResultsOf 706 identified webpages, 355 met inclusion criteria. Most webpages were hosted by government agencies, academic hospitals, or nonprofit organizations. Mean readability levels were high (FRE 38.2; FKGL 12.1), substantially exceeding the recommended levels for patient-facing health information. Only 18% of webpages met eighth-grade readability standards. AI-generated outputs demonstrated similar readability (FRE 36.1; FKGL 12.3). Only 2% of AI responses met eighth-grade readability recommendations. However, when the input prompt included a request to improve readability, AI models lowered average output readability by nearly six grade levels (FRE 69.0; FKGL 6.8).ConclusionsOnline information for inherited pediatric orthopaedic conditions is frequently written above recommended reading levels, and AI-generated content does not meaningfully improve accessibility unless specifically prompted to do so. Despite generally reputable website sources, limited readability and accountability highlight opportunities for orthopaedic surgeons and organizations to advocate for improved clarity, accessibility, and patient-centered communication.Key Concepts(1)Online patient education materials for inherited pediatric orthopaedic conditions are written at a higher reading level than the recommended 8th grade level.(2)Only 18% of websites and 2% of AI-generated responses meet the ≤8th grade readability standard.(3)Most online resources come from reputable sources (government, academic hospitals, nonprofits) but still have poor readability.(4)AI-generated health information has similar readability to websites but is significantly shorter.(5)Improving readability, accessibility, and patient-centered communication is a key opportunity for orthopaedic providers and organizations.Level of EvidenceV

Introduction

Families of children diagnosed with an inherited condition often turn to online sources to learn more about the condition. It is estimated that 2.8 million children in America have an inherited condition, and as many as 25% of new patients at pediatric orthopaedic hospitals may have a disease of genetic etiology [[1], [2], [3]]. Diagnosis can occur at any stage of childhood from in utero to adolescence and can have varying clinical courses throughout a child's life. More than half of orthopaedic patients will use the internet to research their condition, presenting both a valuable tool and a unique challenge for physicians, as information is often of poor quality or written at a reading level too difficult for patients [3]. One new tool at the forefront of medicine today is AI, which has broad utility in medical education. Notably, AI can be used to provide patient education that ranges from short summaries to curated and detailed health information [4]. While little is known about how orthopaedic patients utilize AI, it is crucial to understand its benefits and limitations so that it can best serve the patient population.

Patient education materials can help both patients and families understand the etiology, treatment, and prognosis of their diagnosis. However, these materials often contain extensive jargon and prove difficult for patients and their families to understand [3]. Evaluating the readability of online materials available to patients is essential to understanding patients’ ability to evaluate and apply that information to their own health. A misinformed patient may be more likely to self-diagnosis/treat, request improper treatment, or question their physician, which can influence their satisfaction and weaken trust in their physician [[5], [6], [7]]. There is evidence that poor parent and child health literacy can lead to worse pediatric health outcomes, which solidifies the need for access to more readable PEMs [8]. In recent years, there has been a push from professional medical societies to increase the readability of PEMs. In addition, the National Institutes of Health (NIH), Centers for Disease Control (CDC), and American Medical Association recommend that public-facing materials for all funded projects be published at a sixth-to-eighth-grade reading level [[9], [10], [11], [12]].

Prior studies investigating PEMs for pediatric orthopaedic conditions have found that most resources are written above the recommended reading level. Prior studies on pediatric PEM readability have focused on acquired or older-onset pediatric orthopaedic conditions, such as sports injuries or scoliosis, and little is known about material related to inherited conditions [13,14]. The gravity of inherited and early-onset conditions emphasizes the importance of easily accessible and understandable PEMs. Furthermore, there is a paucity of studies investigating the readability of AI-generated health information, which is ever more prevalent today.

The primary goal of our study was to assess the readability of online health information that families are likely to encounter when researching an inherited pediatric orthopaedic condition. Our secondary aims included understanding where patients would likely find information when searching the internet and assessing the quality of these materials. We hypothesized that most information would be at a higher reading level than the national average of seventh- to eighth-grade level and be found on high-quality academic hospital websites.

Methods

Website selection and content analysis

This study involved no human participants and was exempt from review by our institutional review board. A list of 23 clinically significant inherited disorders with orthopaedic manifestations in infants through adolescents was selected based on relevance in orthopaedic literature and practice, population prevalence, and expert review (Table 1) [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. Internet search parameters closely followed the methodology employed by Thomas et al. [19] including use of private browsing, disabled location filters, and exclusion of sponsored results, but inclusion was not restricted by website host. Google (Alphabet Inc., Mountain View, CA, USA) was queried using each condition in three formats: (1) condition name alone, (2) preceded by “What is … ?”, and (3) preceded by “My child was diagnosed with …”. This process was repeated for common abbreviations for the condition name as listed in Table 1. Websites on the first page of search results were included in the study. If the search resulted in the generation of a Google Gemini (Alphabet Inc., Mountain View, CA, USA) “AI Overview,” this was also included. Websites were classified into different categories based on website type and ownership. Because search results yielded journal articles across a variety of platforms (e.g., PubMed, ResearchGate, and publisher websites), they were categorized separately as their own category. If the search queries resulted in the same journal article appearing on different hosts, it was counted only once.

The search procedure was also replicated using three large language model-based generative AI models in December 2025: Microsoft CoPilot 4.0 (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA), ChatGPT-4o (OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA), Google Gemini 2.0 (Alphabet Inc., Mountain View, CA, USA). For all AI models, a new user account with no prior chat history was created. Conversation history and/or memory settings were turned off, and chat history was cleared between inputs. For models capable of internet searching, this feature was enabled to simulate a realistic use environment and allow information retrieval to support reasoning. An additional query of AI models was performed in June 2026, with the prompts ending with “Explain this to me like I'm in 5th grade” and “Please ensure your response meets Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level readability standards at or below the 8th U.S. school grade level.”

Readability analysis

The running text from each webpage and AI response was copied and pasted as plain text into Microsoft Word (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA), where it was formatted as described by Badarudeen and Sabharwal [20]. To determine reading difficulty, the two most widely utilized formulas were applied through the built-in Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level and Flesch Reading Ease calculators in the document Readability Statistics. Both metrics use average sentence length and syllables per word to calculate a readability score: FKGL = (11.8 X (number of syllables/number of words)) + (0.39 X (number of words/number of sentences)) −15.59 and FRE = (206.835 - (84.6 X (number of syllables/number of words)) - (1.015 X (number of words/number of sentences)). The FRE is scored from 0 to 100, with lower scores indicating increased reading difficulty, and the FKGL equals the corresponding U.S. grade level of readability. We recorded whether websites included visual aids, such as images, charts, videos, or other media. Presence of advertisements, including intrusive advertisements that interrupted reading flow, was recorded. We also recorded if the websites included a reference, bibliography, or similar citation section and if references to peer-reviewed literature were present.

Accountability analysis

We utilized the JAMA Benchmark criteria to assess the quality of each website. This instrument is designed as a strategic quality assessment tool for online material to check the most basic components of information transparency and reliability [21]. The JAMA benchmark assigns one point for the presence of each of four quality indicators: authorship, attribution (references/sources), disclosure of ownership or conflicts of interest, and currency (date of creation or update. Scores range from 0 to 4, with higher scores reflecting greater transparency and adherence to established standards for online health information quality.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were utilized for this investigation.

The average readability scores for all included websites were FRE 38.2 and FKGL 12.1 (Table 2). Text from public and private medical education websites and journal articles had the highest readability, with mean FKGL scores over 14. Medical news was a small portion of search results (n = 2) but had the lowest readability scores with an FKGL mean of 9.4. There were 64 websites (18.0%) with FKGL scores at or below the eighth-grade reading level (FKGL ≤8.9). The majority of these were hosted by pediatric or academic hospitals (n = 43, 67%). Readability on pages from common hosts varied greatly, with average FKGL scores of 12.4, 13.7, and 9.5 for MedlinePlus, Wikipedia, and Cleveland Clinic, respectively. Over 40% of websites included some form of media related to the searched condition, with fewer than one-third of hospital-owned websites including a media component. Website quality was low, with an average JAMA Benchmark score of 2.0, with the lowest quality websites hosted by pediatric academic hospitals (JAMA Benchmark average 0.8).

Readability scores across AI models were similar to those obtained from the text on the websites from the first page of Google search results, with an average FRE of 36.1 and FKGL of 12.3 across all models and all input prompts (Table 3). The lowest readability score came from ChatGPT4o with an average FKGL of 11.3, corresponding to text that can be understood at the 11th grade level. Only six of 304 (2%) AI outputs had a readability score at or below the eighth-grade reading level, with five (83%) being generated by ChatGPT4o and one from the Google AI Summary generated alongside search results. AI models generated outputs that were nearly six times shorter in length than the text found on websites, with average word counts of 302 and 1,716, respectively. Discussion

Our review of 23 pediatric inherited orthopaedic conditions identified 355 unique websites that patients were likely to encounter on the first page of Google search results. Most websites were hosted by government agencies and academic hospitals. Readability of online materials was poor, with an average reading grade level over 12, which is well above the national average reading level and recommended guidelines from leading health organizations. Website quality was low and readability varied widely, with only a few pages meeting recommended reading levels. Similarly, AI-generated content had comparably high levels of readability but was shorter in length than most website materials. When input prompts included specific readability thresholds, the readability of AI-generated outputs greatly improved.

Inherited pediatric orthopaedic conditions affect many children in the US, and there is a lack of accessible and easily readable PEMs for patients and their families. Poor parent health literacy contributes to worse pediatric health outcomes, emphasizing the need for increased ease of readability for PEMs [8]. Our study found that, for all included websites, the average FKGL was 12.1, which is notably higher than the AMA's recommendation for PEMs to be written around a fifth- or sixth-grade reading level (FKGL of 5.0–6.9) [22]. These findings are similar to other studies that investigated the readability of PEMs for other pediatric orthopaedic conditions. In 2008, Sabharwal and Badarudeen investigated the readability of 426 PEMs available on the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) website “Your Orthopaedic Connection” and found a mean FKGL of 10.43 [23]. Specific to pediatric orthopaedics, Thomas et al. [19] analyzed six websites identified via internet search engine for pediatric ACL tears and found the mean FKGL to be 8.6. In 2021, Ó Doinn et al. [13] review of 176 educational materials on pediatric orthopaedic conditions from different professional societies, including AAOS, POSNA, and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) identified a mean FKGL of 9.1. In a systematic review of 11 studies containing 893 PEMs for pediatric orthopaedic conditions, Hecht et al. [14] found that academic resources had mean readability scores of at least an FKGL of 8, and search engine results varied significantly. Notably, in Hecht et al.’s review, less than 50% of studies (n = 5) assessed PEMs accessed through search engines, noting the need for improved understanding of what patients are likely to encounter when researching their health online. The results of our study highlight the complex nature of inherited conditions, with average reading levels exceeding those identified in prior studies across a range of orthopaedic subspecialties. While many of the studied conditions are far rarer than common orthopaedic conditions, such as arthritis or ACL tears, the high reading level required to understand educational materials on these conditions makes this a potential area for advocacy and awareness for individuals and organizations producing educational material for inherited conditions.

In recent years, artificial intelligence has become a topic of immense interest among healthcare professionals with the release of advanced large language models (LLMs). Top models have achieved passing scores on medical licensing examinations and scored higher than the averages of graduating orthopaedic residents on the In-Training Orthopaedic Examination (OITE), raising interest in their potential benefit in healthcare education and professional development [24,25]. However, little is known about how the public utilizes AI in researching their health. An investigation of over 600 ChatGPT users found that over three-quarters of users (78.4%) were willing to use ChatGPT for self-diagnosis [26]. Studies of the general public have found that many patients are unfamiliar with AI chatbots, use chatbots less than internet searches for health information, and would prefer a real person in most health scenarios [[27], [28], [29]]. Our study found that popular AI models, including ChatGPT, provided responses above the national average reading level, and only 2% of responses were written at an eighth-grade reading level. These findings are consistent with AI model output in other specialties, with high reading level outputs (FRE = 13.19) from ChatGPT-4 in response to patient questions related to ophthalmologic surgery [30]. While little is known about how orthopaedic patients use AI, its potential benefits in patient education may be undermined by overly complex language inhibiting patients’ understanding and application to their health. Further research is needed to understand the role of AI in patient medical education, including demographics of users and safety/accuracy of outputs.

Developing public-facing health information is often resource-intensive and challenging, particularly when attempting to balance readability, accuracy, and completeness. Consequently, healthcare organizations and researchers are exploring the use of AI to improve the readability of existing healthcare materials. One specific area is within clinical trial research and informed consent forms (ICFs). A study using active clinical trial protocols found that AI-generated ICFs outperform human-generated ICFs in readability and understandability, with no significant changes in accuracy or completeness [31]. Similar applications of AI have been explored with PEMs, including brochures and handouts as well as online educational materials. In a study of patient handouts from plastic surgery societies, the authors used the prompt “Rewrite the following at a 6th-grade reading level” to produce revised handouts with an average 7th-grade reading level [32]. This finding is consistent with our results, where prompting to “explain this to me like I'm in 5th grade” yielded an average readability in the sixth- to seventh-grade range. Importantly, in our study, use of a specific FKGL grade target resulted in outputs with a readability score that better matched the input request. This observation underlines the importance of appropriate and specific prompt design when using AI to optimize readability. Additional studies of PEMs supplied by orthopaedic professional societies have found that AI models can improve readability by nearly 50%, decreasing readability scores from advanced high school or college level to around the sixth-grade level [33,34]. A recent study of pediatric orthopaedic PEMs (OrthoInfo) found AI models improved readability, but outputs have lower accuracy and completeness levels compared to OrthoInfo PEMs [35]. Additional research should focus on safely implementing AI to improve readability without sacrificing the accuracy of materials.

Our findings suggest that the readability of online information obtained through internet queries and popular AI models is higher than that of orthopaedic professional society websites. Notably, only two pages from POSNA or AAOS were captured in our search results, indicating a potential opportunity for orthopaedic organizations to strengthen their online visibility and accessibility. The addition of new PEMs for inherited orthopaedic conditions would ensure high-quality orthopaedic information is available to patients. However, most identified websites were from reputable sources, including government agencies and academic medical centers, suggesting that the overall credibility of available information is already high. Consequently, enhancing online presence may represent an area for strategic growth rather than an urgent deficiency. Additionally, many of the evaluated webpages were produced by healthcare organizations and government agencies, yet still demonstrated reading levels above recommended standards. As such, continued awareness and internal advocacy with colleagues and institutional communications teams remain important for orthopedists related to health literacy.

AI has the potential to assist with improving the readability of PEMs. To maximize effectiveness, users should ensure the use of a clear and specific prompt, preferably with a request for a specific FKGL score, and review all outputs to ensure accuracy and completeness. Additionally, PEM writers may choose to focus on media or visual aids as these were not generated by AI models in our study and have been shown to improve reader comprehension and understanding [36,37]. Outside of the academic setting, Wikipedia appeared on the first page of search results for 21 (91.3%) of queried conditions. This highlights a potential opportunity for orthopaedic professionals to engage in grassroots efforts to enhance both the quality and readability of publicly available information.

There are several limitations to this study. Readability metrics were limited to FKGL and FRE, which may limit interpretation of the readability scores, as both metrics utilize average sentence length and syllables per word in readability calculations. A prior study in pediatric orthopaedics, included additional readability metrics and found similar readability scores were generated across different formulae [38]. We limited our findings to the first page of results from a single search engine. This approach was taken to identify the most likely websites patients will realistically encounter when searching their condition, as at the time of writing, Google accounted for >90% of the global search engine market and 97% of user clicks occur on the first page of search results [39,40]. However, this approach limited the number of websites reviewed and may not reflect the experience of all internet users. We queried three different popular AI models and other models may output text at a lower reading level. Additionally, by limiting AI memory and inputting single questions, it is unlikely our study mimicked how individuals naturally converse with AI chatbots and may have limited the model's ability to garner conversational context clues and alter the future outputs as needed. Several studies have reported promising results for AI systems to convert higher-grade-level text into more accessible language by reducing the FKGL [41,42]. These studies, along with the improved readability scores found in our study, suggest that AI systems are capable of producing text with lower readability levels when appropriately prompted. However, without specific prompting, the readability of AI-generated responses is similar to that of information commonly found through standard internet searches. We found that over 40% of websites and 0% of AI responses contained media. Media, including visual aids, have been shown to improve reader comprehension, but our study design was unable to capture the benefit of these visual aids in PEMs for pediatric orthopaedic conditions. Additional research is needed to better understand the role of AI in health communication, particularly to understand how patients interact with and interpret AI-generated health information.

Conclusion

Online educational materials for inherited pediatric orthopaedic conditions are predominantly written at reading levels that exceed national recommendations and the average U.S. reading level, despite being largely hosted by reputable organizations such as government agencies and academic medical centers. Contemporary AI models generate responses with readability scores comparable to those of commonly encountered internet resources. However, when prompted with explicit grade-level targets, AI-generated responses showed substantial improvements in readability, suggesting a potential role for AI as a tool to improve the accessibility of healthcare education materials. These findings highlight the continued need for healthcare organizations, professional societies, and digital content creators to prioritize health literacy when developing educational resources. Future efforts should focus on improving the readability and accessibility of high-quality patient education materials while ensuring that simplification does not compromise accuracy or completeness. Additionally, further research is needed to evaluate the real-world use, utility, and accuracy of AI-generated health information by patients and families seeking information about inherited pediatric orthopaedic conditions.
