Different association of psychological distress with socioeconomic, migration, and parenting factors in first-time fathers and mothers among migrants in China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jie Li, Shuqin Jiang, Xinjie Hu, Chengchao Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFirst-time parents among young internal migrants are at high risk for psychological problems, especially during the early years of parenting. We aimed to explore the factors associated with psychological distress of first-time fathers and mothers among young migrants.MethodsA cross-sectional analysis was performed using the Chinese Internal Migrant Dynamic Monitoring Survey of 978 fathers and 731 mothers with children aged three years or younger. The main outcome variable was psychological distress, which was measured using the Short Form of the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale. Potential independent factors related to sociodemographic information, migration, and parenting. Univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses were conducted.ResultsFathers and mothers had similar levels of psychological distress (9.28 ± 3.21 vs. 9.41 ± 3.22, t = -0.81, p = 0.417), and similar severity distribution (χ2 = 0.88, p = 0.644) with prevalence of serious psychological distress of 11.86% and 13.27%. Fathers and mothers with higher subjective socioeconomic status (B = -0.29, 95% CI=-0.41, -0.17; B = -0.27, 95% CI=-0.43, -0.12), greater willingness to engage in social integration (B = -0.55, 95% CI=-1.05, -0.04; B = -0.59, 95% CI=-1.13, -0.05), and who often spent time with their children (B = -0.52, 95% CI = -1.03, -0.02; B = -0.85, 95% CI = -1.39, -0.31) were less distressed. A high school education (B = 0.67, 95% CI = 0.20, 1.14), with family monthly income of > = 8000 (B = 0.55, 95% CI = 0.003, 1.10), be unemployed (B = 1.46, 95% CI = 0.11, 2.81), and lower levels of acceptance by local residents (B = -0.56, 95% CI=-0.90, -0.22) were associated with more severe distress in fathers. Additionally, mothers with children aged 1 (B = 1.16, 95% CI = 0.12, 2.19) and 2 years (B = 1.05, 95% CI = 0.02, 2.08) had more distress than mothers with children under one-year old.ConclusionsIn conclusion, common factors were associated with psychological distress in first-time parents among young migrants; however, differences were found between fathers and mothers. Parental differences should be considered when developing policies to improve young migrants’ mental health.

Introduction

The migrant population has been increasing worldwide [1], especially in China, as reform policies began in the 1980s. According to the 2018 Report on China’s Migrant Population Development, China had 244.5 million internal migrants, more than half of whom were under 40 years of age [2]. For the large group of young migrants, having their first child is an important life event, combining happiness and challenges, especially during the early childrearing years [3]. According to life span theory, both migration and parenting are defined as major life events that can affect an individual’s mental health [4]. The challenges of parenting combined with stress from migration can lead to elevated psychological distress in parents, which impairs the parent-child relationship [5] and leads to negative child outcomes [5, 6]. For instance, elevated distress in parents has been associated with impaired emotion expression and parent–child interactions, which increased the risk of child neglect [7, 8]; meanwhile, it was also associated with reduced parental sensitivity, and difficulties in forming secure attachment relationships [9, 10]. These adverse effects are especially salient during the first years of life, when children are highly dependent on caregivers for emotional regulation and psychosocial development. Therefore, identifying factors associated with psychological distress among first-time parents during migration is a critical step in developing appropriate public services to improve the health of both parents and their children.

Potential factors include migration- and parenting-related factors, in addition to sociodemographic characteristics. Socioeconomic status is the primary sociodemographic determinant of psychological distress [11]. The theory of health and social position indicates that the effect of socioeconomic disparities on health are resulted primarily from subjective status, whereas objective indicators (e.g., family income) play lesser roles [12, 13]. Most studies on internal migrants have focused on objective indicators of socioeconomic status with less attention paid on subjective indicators. Preliminary evidence has shown that subjective socioeconomic status plays a more important role than absolute indicators of subjective well-being among migrants [14]. However, the association between subjective socioeconomic status and mental health in this population remains unclear.

Social integration is a key migration-related factor with a protective effect against psychological distress among international [15] and internal migrants [16]. However, less is known about the association between different types of social integration and psychological distress, including willingness to integrate, belongingness to the local society, and acceptance by local residents. Willingness reflects individuals’ attitudinal orientation toward integrating into the host society, belongingness captures subjective identification and emotional attachment to the local community, and acceptance represents perceived recognition and validation by local residents, which represented different subjective aspect of social integration [17, 18]. In addition, research on first-time parents among migrants is limited. Parenting-related factors affect not only children’s behavioral problems but also parents’ distress [19]. Thus, these factors should be considered when exploring factors associated with psychological distress in migrating parents, especially population-specific indicators such as whether these parents live with their children and the frequency they spend time with their children.

Gender differences have been found in factors associated with mental health in the general population and migrants, including socioeconomic characteristics, social relationships, and parenting-related factors [20–23]. For example, immigration was associated with psychological distress in women but not in men [23], work satisfaction was more strongly associated with distress among men than women, and time spent on childcare and activities was more strongly associated with distress in women than men [22]. Previous studies also have reported gender differences in parenting experiences between mothers and fathers [6, 24], suggesting that parenting-related factors may have different effects on psychological distress. Furthermore, these gender differences are supported by gendered cultural expectations. In traditional Chinese families, fathers are more likely to assume primary responsibility for economic provision, whereas mothers are more heavily involved in childcare and domestic labor [25]. Among migrant families, these gendered roles may be intensified by migration-related constraints, such as employment insecurity, limited childcare support, and reduced extended family assistance [15]. From a role strain perspective, psychological distress may arise when individuals experience overload or conflict between competing social roles, which may differ systematically for fathers and mothers [26].Thus, it is reasonable to examine gender differences in the socioeconomic, migration, and parenting correlates of psychological distress among young internal migrant first-time fathers and mothers. However, such gender-specific analyses remain scarce in existing literature.

In summary, the psychological distress of young migrant parents is influenced by multiple factors, with migration-related and parenting-related factors representing the two domains that most distinctly differentiate this population from others grounded in life span theory. Specifically, based on the theories and empirical evidence of health and social position and social integration, this study posits that post-migration socioeconomic status (particularly subjective socioeconomic status) and social integration are key potential migration-related predictors of their psychological distress. Therefore, this study utilized a large sample of migrant first-time parents from the Internal Migrant Dynamic Monitoring Survey in China to investigate factors related to psychological distress in fathers and mothers, focusing on subjective socioeconomic status, social integration, and parenting-related factors.

Methods

Participants

This cross-sectional study used data from the 2014 Internal Migrant Dynamic Monitoring Survey in China [27]. This is a nationally representative study on the social integration and mental health of migrants from different families. A total of 15,999 migrants aged 15–59 years were included, selected based on a three-stage stratified probability proportionate-to-size sampling method in eight cities in China. Details of the sampling process are presented in another study [28]. The inclusion criteria for the sample in the current study were (1) being a first-time father or mother, (2) having a child aged ≤ 3 years, (3) being younger than 40 years, which is the criterion for young to middle age in Chinese culture, and (4) migrating for a job for themselves or their partner. Parents of twins were excluded to reduce potential confounding bias arising from the substantially greater and qualitatively different caregiving burden associated with multiple births, which may independently affect psychological distress and obscure the relationships of interest in this study. The current study included 1,709 participants. The procedures for sample selection are presented in Fig. 1.

Measures

Psychological distress was measured using the Short Form of the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6) [29, 30]. The K6 comprises six items that assess six symptoms of psychological distress. The severity of each symptom was scored on a 5-Likert scale, ranging from 0 (none of the time) to 4 (all of the time). The total score was calculated by summing the six items, ranging from 0 to 24; higher scores indicated higher levels of psychological distress. A total score of < 8, 8–12, and ≥ 13 was used to define low, moderate, and serious psychological distress, in accordance with the well-established and widely used cutoffs [30–32]. The scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity [29, 30]. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.851 for this study.

Data on parents’ sociodemographic characteristics, including age, gender, ethnicity, education, family income, marital status, occupation, and subjective socioeconomic status, were collected during the interviews. Subjective socioeconomic status was measured using an adapted version of the MacArthur Scale [33]. Rather than comparing themselves to society as a whole, participants were asked to rate their perceived social position relative to local relatives, friends, and colleagues in the destination area. This contextualized reference frame was adopted to better reflect migrants’ everyday social comparisons, which are often shaped by proximal social environments following migration. Such adaptations have been used in previous research to capture context-specific perceptions of social standing [33–35]. The lowest rung (1) indicated the lowest income and worst occupation, whereas the highest rung (10) indicated the highest income and best occupation.

Migration-related factors, including migration range (across or within provinces) and psychological indicators of social integration, were collected. Psychological indicators of social integration were measured using a 13-item Social Integration Scale, which included three factors: willingness to integrate, sense of belonging in local society, and acceptance by local residents [18]. Each item was rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree), and higher scores indicated higher levels of social integration. The scale has good reliability and validity for use with Chinese migrants [36]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.908 for the total scale and 0.910, 0.892, and 0.912 for the three factors, respectively.

Parenting-related factors, including age of children, gender of children, whether living with children, and frequency of spending time with children, were collected through self-report. The ages of the children were divided into < 1, 1, 2, and 3 years. Living with children was a binary variable coded as “yes” and “no.” Frequency of spending time with children was categorized as “often,” “sometimes,” and “rarely/never.”

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to assess participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, migration- and parenting-related factors, and psychological distress, using frequencies (proportions) for categorical variables and means (standard deviations) for continuous variables. Differences in psychological distress between fathers and mothers were assessed using independent sample t-test for scores and chi-squared tests for severity distribution. Univariate and multivariate linear regressions were used to examine the relationship between these factors and severity of psychological distress among fathers and mothers, respectively. Variables such as age, education, income, and migration range have been shown in previous research to be associated with psychological distress among migrant populations [11, 37], therefore, they were included in the multivariate linear regression models regardless of their statistical significance in the univariate analyses. Unstandardized regression coefficients and 95% confidence interval (CI) were reported. In supplementary analyses, we conducted logistic regression models to examine the associations between relevant factors and serious psychological distress. Interaction terms between gender and key variables were additionally included to formally test whether the associations between these factors and psychological distress differed by gender in the total sample. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). All VIF values were below the conventional threshold of 5 (1.02–1.79 for fathers, 1.05–4.75 for mothers), indicating no serious multicollinearity. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p-value of 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 24.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Participants’ characteristics

The participants were 1,709 young migrants with a single child, aged between 0 and 3 years. Among the total sample, 978 (57.2%) were fathers and 731 (42.8%) were mothers from different families. The average age of the participants was 28.06 ± 3.56 years, ranging from 18 to 39 years. Table 1 shows the sample characteristics and comparisons between the fathers and mothers. Most participants were ethnic Han (97.2%), were in their first marriage (99.5%), migrated across provinces (58.2%), and had children migrating with them (72.7%). Of the participants, 491 (28.7%) often spent time with their children and 524 (30.7%) did so rarely or never. The fathers and mothers had comparable sociodemographic characteristics, except for age and occupation status. No significant differences were found in migration- and parenting-related factors, except for the frequency of spending time with children.

Description and comparison of psychological distress between fathers and mothers

The mean psychological distress scores were 9.28 ± 3.21 for fathers and 9.41 ± 3.22 for mothers (Fig. 2a). The frequencies and proportions of low, moderate, and serious psychological distress were 323 (33.03%), 539 (55.11%), and 116 (11.86%) in fathers; and the numbers and percentages were 232 (31.74%), 402 (54.99%), and 97 (13.27%) in mothers (Fig. 2b). No significant differences in psychological distress scores (t = -0.81, p = 0.417) and severity distribution (χ2 = 0.88, p = 0.644) were observed between fathers and mothers.

Association of psychological distress severity with socioeconomic, migration, and parenting factors among fathers and mothers

In the univariate linear regression analyses among fathers, education, occupational status, subjective socioeconomic status, three social integration factors, and frequency of spending time with one’s children were significantly related to psychological distress severity (Table 2). Including them together with age, family monthly income, and migration range as independent variables in multivariate linear regression analysis, all of them except age and migration range were significantly associated with fathers’ distress (Fig. 3). Fathers who had a high school education (B = 0.67, 95% CI = 0.20, 1.14), with family monthly income of ≥ 8000 (B = 0.55, 95% CI = 0.003, 1.10), be unemployed (B = 1.46, 95% CI = 0.11, 2.81) experienced higher levels of psychological distress than their reference group; higher subjective socioeconomic status (B = -0.29, 95% CI=-0.41, -0.17), greater willingness (B = -0.55, 95% CI=-1.05, -0.04) and acceptance (B = -0.56, 95% CI=-0.90, -0.22) of social integration, and frequently spending time with their children (often vs. sometimes: B = -0.52, 95% CI = -1.03, -0.02) were associated with lower psychological distress in fathers.

Among mothers, subjective socioeconomic status, all three social integration factors, age of children, and frequency of spending time with one’s children were significantly correlated with psychological distress severity in the univariate regression analyses (Table 2). Including them and age, education, family monthly income, and migration range to the multivariate analysis, the results showed that higher subjective socioeconomic status (B = -0.27, 95% CI=-0.43, -0.12), greater willingness to engage in social integration (B = -0.59, 95% CI=-1.13, -0.05), and frequently spending time with their children (often vs. sometimes: B = -0.85, 95% CI = -1.39, -0.31) were associated with lower psychological distress in mothers, which was also observed for fathers. In contrast to fathers, age of the children and rarely/never spending time with children were also associated with psychological distress (Fig. 3). Specifically, mothers with children aged 1 (B = 1.16, 95% CI = 0.12, 2.19) and 2 years (B = 1.05, 95% CI = 0.01, 2.08) reported higher levels of psychological distress than those with children aged younger than 1 year. Mothers who rarely/never (B = -0.86, 95% CI = -1.45, -0.26) spent time with their children reported lower distress than those who sometimes spent time with their children.

Supplementary analyses

Using serious psychological distress cases (total score ≥ 13) or non-cases (total score < 13) as the dependent variable, logistic regression analyses were conducted among fathers and mothers, separately. Only higher levels of subjective socioeconomic status (odds ratio (OR) = 0.78, 95%CI = 0.70, 0.89) and acceptance by the local residents (OR = 0.62, 95%CI = 0.44, 0.85) were associated with lower risk of serious psychological distress in fathers. Among mothers, subjective socioeconomic status (OR = 0.81, 95%CI = 0.70, 0.94), willingness to engage in social integration (OR = 0.46, 95%CI = 0.28, 0.74), age of children (age of one year relative to < 1 year: OR = 8.89, 95%CI = 1.13, 69.80), and frequency of spending time with children (often relative to sometimes: OR = 0.46, 95%CI = 0.27, 0.79) were associated with risk of serious psychological distress. In addition, the interaction of gender and other independent variables were included to test the interaction effects in the total sample. The results showed that only the interaction effects of gender with education (p = 0.029) and gender with occupation status (p = 0.022) on psychological stress were statistically significant, while the interaction effects between gender and other factors that differed between fathers and mothers were not statistically significant (family monthly income: p = 0.378, acceptance by the local residents: p = 0.484, age of the children: p = 0.317). These findings suggest that gender may moderate the associations of education and occupational status with psychological distress, whereas gender differences observed for other factors may reflect differences in association patterns rather than statistically significant effect modification.

Discussion

In a nationwide sample of internal migrants who were first-time parents, we found that over two thirds of these young internal migrants reported at least moderate levels of psychological distress, and fathers and mothers had similar levels of psychological distress. The psychological distress in fathers and mothers were both associated with subjective socioeconomic status, social integration, and frequencies of spending time with their children; in addition, fathers’ distress was associated with education and occupational status, while mothers’ distress was associated with age of the children. This study is the first to investigate the levels and factors related to psychological distress among internal migrants who were first-time parents and examine the differences between fathers and mothers.

The results of this study showed comparable levels of psychological distress between young migrant fathers and mothers, however, the related factors differed. Subjective socioeconomic status, willingness to engage in social integration, and frequency of spending time with one’s children were associated with psychological distress in both fathers and mothers. Higher subjective socioeconomic status than that of local residents remained an independent protective factor against psychological distress in both fathers and mothers. The results were in accordance with previous theories regarding the effects of socioeconomic disparities on health, resulting primarily from subjective status, while objective indicators (e.g., family income) had a lesser role [12, 13]. Studies on adolescents [38] and migrants [14] have also found that subjective socioeconomic status plays a more important role than objective indicators. A possible reason is that a much stronger relationship between subjective socioeconomic status and response to stressful events was found in individuals with less social support than in those with high social support [39], and migrants always perceived low levels of social support [40].

Fathers and mothers with higher willingness to engage in social integration were less likely to experience psychological distress. Fathers with higher levels of perceived acceptance by local residents were also less likely to experience distress; however, the same effect was not observed in mothers. The results indicate that the effective domains of social integration in this relationship differed between fathers and mothers. This is in line with prior research that found a different effect of social integration on mental health between male and female migrants [41]. However, the different aspects of social integration have not been examined in previous studies. To our knowledge, this is the first study to consider the protective effects of different social integration domains on the psychological well-being of migrant fathers and mothers. This difference may have resulted from the different social roles of fathers and mothers. Fathers spend more time than mothers working and communicating with local residents; therefore, their willingness to integrate into local society and perceived acceptance by local residents play an important role in psychological outcomes. In contrast, mothers spend more time on domestic work than fathers do, resulting in their willingness to engage in social integration, which has a significant effect on psychological outcomes.

In the present study, how frequently parents spent time with their children, rather than whether their children migrated with them, was associated with psychological distress. Fathers and mothers who often spent time with their children reported lower levels of psychological distress than did those who sometimes spent time with their children. Spending time with one’s children has many benefits, including improving their healthy development, creating happy memories, and strengthening family connections, which could further decrease parents’ psychological distress [42]. Notably, unlike fathers, mothers who rarely or never spent times with their children perceived lower psychological distress. Several potential explanations may account for this unexpected finding. First, in some migrant families, grandparents or other relatives often provide substantial childcare, which may reduce mothers’ caregiving burden and associated stress even when they spend less direct time with their children [43]. Second, mothers who are more engaged in employment or community activities may have less time with their children but may benefit psychologically from social and economic engagement [44]. Third, reverse causality is possible: mothers who spend less time with their children may have more discretionary time for personal activities, which can reduce role conflict and contribute to lower psychological distress [26]. These interpretations highlight the complex interplay between caregiving, social support, and mental health among migrant mothers and warrant further investigation.

Mothers with children older than 1 year perceived more psychological distress than those with younger children. However, fathers’ psychological distress was not associated with their children’s age. This pattern may reflect gender differences in parenting roles within traditional Chinese families. Mothers are generally expected to be the primary caregivers, particularly for younger children, and bear the majority of daily childcare responsibilities, whereas fathers’ involvement tends to be less intensive and less influenced by the child’s developmental stage [25, 45, 46]. Such expectations may increase maternal stress when children are younger, but have little effect on fathers’ psychological distress [45]. Another possible reason is that mothers’ psychological well-being was more likely to be affected by their children than that of fathers’ [24]. Consistent with this, our results suggest that fathers’ psychological distress is primarily associated with their own individual characteristics (e.g., education and occupation) rather than the age of their children, whereas maternal distress appears less related to their personal socioeconomic factors but more influenced by child-related responsibilities. The finding that fathers’ psychological distress was associated with higher school education attainment may reflect status inconsistency or occupational mismatch. In traditional Chinese families, the gendered division of labor positions fathers as the primary economic providers, and higher educational attainment is often accompanied by expectations of elevated occupational status and financial returns [25]. Fathers with higher education may experience stress if their employment or income does not align with expectations [47]. Although formal interaction tests were statistically significant only for education and occupational status, gender-stratified analyses revealed distinct patterns of associated factors for fathers and mothers. This suggests that, beyond statistical interaction, different psychosocial pathways may underlie psychological distress among migrant fathers and mothers, warranting further investigation in future studies. Together, these findings underscore the complex interplay of family support, social roles, and gendered expectations in shaping parental mental health among migrant families, suggesting that interventions should consider both gender-specific pathways and the broader socio-cultural context.

This study has two strengths. First, we focused on young internal migrants, who account for a large proportion of this population during the stressful period of being first-time parents, since they have different factors related to psychological distress compared to general migrants. Second, the consideration of the differences between fathers and mothers in factors related to psychological well-being will help to develop targeted interventions to improve internal migrants’ mental health. Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences; observed associations between parental characteristics, migration factors, and psychological distress cannot be interpreted as causal, and longitudinal studies are needed to examine the temporal dynamics of these relationships. Second, young parents during pregnancy could not be included in this study, although pregnancy represents a critical early period for the mental health of both mothers and fathers [3, 9]. This limitation arises from the absence of pregnancy-related information in the survey. Future studies incorporating pregnancy-related information are needed to more comprehensively capture mental health risks across the transition to parenthood among young migrant families. Third, self-reported psychological distress was used which may be subject to recall bias and social desirability. However, this is reasonable for national surveys because of the feasibility and economy of the measurements. In addition, the self-report measure of psychological distress (K6) has shown good validity and reliability, and has been widely used to screen for mood disorders in many national-based epidemiological surveys worldwide [29, 30, 36]. Fourth, potential unmeasured confounders, such as marital quality, work hours, childcare support, and housing conditions, were not captured in the survey [11, 37]. These factors may influence parental psychological distress and could partially explain the observed associations. Finally, the data were collected in 2014, which may limit their relevance to current migrant conditions given recent changes in internal migration patterns, labor markets, and social policies in China. Future studies using more recent datasets are needed to assess whether these findings remain applicable in contemporary contexts.

In conclusion, the mental health of young internal migrants is an important public health issue, especially for those who are new parents. Findings from the current national-based sample confirmed the important role of subjective socioeconomic status compared to local residents, frequency of spending time with children, and willingness for social integration in the psychological well-being of both young fathers and mothers among the migrants. We also found different factors of psychological distress between fathers and mothers, in that education, family income, occupation status, and acceptance by the local residents affected psychological well-being in fathers, while age of the children had this effect in mothers.

These findings suggest several strategies to support the mental health of young internal migrants, particularly those transitioning into parenthood. Interventions aimed at improving subjective socioeconomic status, such as employment assistance, vocational training, and access to stable work opportunities, may help reduce psychological distress by enhancing perceived social standing and financial security [48–50]. Parenting education and family-based support programs have also been shown to alleviate stress during early parenthood by improving parenting confidence and work–family balance, which may be particularly beneficial for migrant families with limited informal support networks [48, 51]. In addition, promoting social integration through community-based activities, peer support groups, and policies that foster social inclusion and reduce discrimination may strengthen migrants’ sense of belonging and acceptance, thereby improving psychological well-being [52]. Given the observed gender differences, such interventions may need to be tailored to address fathers’ socioeconomic pressures and mothers’ caregiving-related stressors. Together, these approaches highlight the importance of integrated, family- and community-oriented interventions to improve the mental health of young migrant parents.
