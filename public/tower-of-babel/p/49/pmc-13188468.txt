Prevalence, molecular species, and associated factors for Cryptosporidium infection among dairy-farm workers and HIV-positive individuals in central Ethiopia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Manyazewal Anberber Zeleke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) positive patients and dairy farm workers are at increased risk of contracting Cryptosporidium infection due to compromised immunity and occupational exposure. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of Cryptosporidium infection and identify associated factors and molecular species of the parasite in HIV positive patients and dairy farm workers in central Ethiopia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted using a two-stage random sampling technique to select 300 study participants. Approximately 5 g of stool was collected aseptically from each participant, transported to the laboratory, and examined using the modified Ziehl–Neelsen staining, Nested Polymerase Chain Reaction, Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism, and sequence analysis. A pretested questionnaire was employed to collect data on infection-associated factors. Data were analyzed with the SPSS software using the univariable and multivariable logistic regression, with a 95% confidence interval and a precision level of p < 0.05.ResultsThe overall prevalence of Cryptosporidium was 9% (95% CI: 5.7–12.3). Among dairy farm workers prevalence was 10.8% (95% CI: 5.7–16.0), while HIV positive individuals had 7.8% (95% CI: 3.6–12.0). Cryptosporidium parvum (C. parvum) and Cryptosporidium hominis (C. hominis) were identified at proportions of 81.5% and 18.5%, respectively. Phylogenetic analysis confirmed that C. parvum isolates belonged to the bovine genotype family. Infection was significantly associated with low education level, presence of animals at home, frequent contact with animal excrement, and drinking untreated well or stream water (P < 0.05). HIV positive patients on antiretroviral therapy (ART) with Cluster of Differentiation 4 (CD4+) counts of 200–500 cells per microliter (cells/µL) were significantly less likely to be infected than those who were pre- ART or had CD4 + counts below 200 cells/µL.ConclusionsThe predominance of bovine genotype C. parvum, its association with untreated well or stream water, and the higher risk among HIV-positive individuals with low CD4 + counts underscore the public health importance of the infection and the need for integrated “One Health” control strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13267-w.Keywords: Antiretroviral therapy, CD4 + count, Cryptosporidium, Ethiopia, HIV positive, PCR

Background

Human Cryptosporidiosis is caused by several Cryptosporidium species; but most cases worldwide are attributable to the zoonotic C.parvum and the anthroponotic C.hominis species [1]. Cryptosporidiosis has a global distribution, with prevalence rates ranging from 15% to 25% in children [2–4]. Transmission occurs through multiple routes, including person-to-person, animal-to-human, direct or indirect contact, waterborne (drinking or recreational water), and foodborne pathways [5]. The environmental stability and low infectious dose of Cryptosporidium facilitate its spread via water and food; as few as ten viable oocysts can establish infection [6]. Animal waste and human sewage are major sources of surface water contamination with C. parvum, while large outbreaks of human cryptosporidiosis have been linked to municipal drinking water contaminated with C. hominis [5, 7]. Detection poses a major epidemiological challenge, as Cryptosporidium is not identified by routine ova and parasite examinations, and is frequently underdiagnosed [8]. It has been reported as the second most common pathogen associated with increased risk of death and the only pathogen independently linked to mortality in children under five with diarrhea [9, 10]. Cryptosporidiosis shows seasonal variation. European studies reported that C. parvum was more common in rural populations, associated with animal exposure, and peaked in spring, whereas C. hominis was more common in young children and peaked in late summer and autumn [10–15]. In Ethiopia, human Cryptosporidium has been reported from several locations, with prevalence among HIV/AIDS patients ranging from 12.1% to 43.9% [16–21]. Molecular investigations identified C. parvum and C. hominis as the most common species infecting humans, while other species (C. viatorum, C. felis, C. meleagridis, C. canis, and C. xiaoi) were highlighted as potentially zoonotic [21, 22].

The socioeconomic consequences of Cryptosporidium infection include healthcare costs and economic impacts on farming communities’ livelihoods and food security [8]. Epidemics result in financial losses due to reduced animal and human productivity, increased medical and veterinary expenses, and long-term health consequences that substantially reduce labor capacity [1–4]. HIV-positive patients and dairy farm workers are particularly vulnerable: farm workers are exposed through direct contact with infected cattle excrement, contaminated water sources, and poor farm hygiene [10–13], while HIV-positive patients with low CD4 + counts face increased risk, severity, and duration of illness [6–8].

Previous studies in Ethiopia relied largely on conventional microscopy with acid-fast staining, which cannot differentiate species because oocysts are morphologically indistinguishable [8]. Moreover, most studies excluded dairy farm workers and did not investigate infection-associated factors. Comprehensive assessment of prevalence, associated factors, and molecular species in these vulnerable populations is therefore essential to clarify transmission dynamics at the human–cattle–environment interface and to strengthen understanding of the global epidemiology of cryptosporidiosis [1].

Methods

Study area and period

This study was conducted in Addis Ababa (Kality, Bole, Akaki, Kolfe, Nifas silk lafto, Yeka) and surrounding Oromia districts (Akaki, Sebeta, Barak, Walmara, and Sululta) in central Ethiopia between June 2016 and May 2018. The study area is located between latitudes 80 46’ 34’’ and 90 23’ 00’’ N and longitudes 38032’ 27’’ and 390 14’43’’ E and is situated at an altitudinal range of 1950–2600 m.a.s.l. The area has approximately 5,627,934 inhabitants [23] and approximately 5200 large-, medium-, and small-scale dairy farms with 58,500 cattle (approximately 50% crossbred) [24].

The geographical coverage of the study extended up to 80 km from Addis Ababa, encompassing intensive dairy farms where crossbred cattle are concentrated. Addis Ababa and its surrounding urban and peri-urban districts (Sululta, Welmera, Sebeta, Holota, and Adea) represent the country’s primary dairy farming hub. This region hosts the majority of Ethiopia’s dairy farms and serves as a crucial “milk shed” due to strong market demand. The livestock production system is characterized as urban and peri-urban milk production, a system commonly found in and around major cities and towns with high demand for milk. It is based predominantly on crossbred dairy stock, mainly Friesian × Zebu, and consists of small- to medium-sized farms [24]. The main feed sources include agro-industrial byproducts (such as oilseed cakes and bran) and purchased roughage, while many farmers also cultivate homegrown feed on their land. The primary objective of this production system is milk sales, which provide an important supplementary source of household income [24].

A cross-sectional study design using a two-stage random sampling technique was used to select the study participants. Accordingly, the calculated sample size to estimate prevalence was 150, however, to adjust the sample size required for the present two-stage random sampling method and to make the prevalence estimates more precise, the sample size was inflated twice and set to 300. After the sample size was defined, it was allocated on the basis of the proportion of the population within the sampling frame, which was 1761 for HIV patients and 1174 for farm workers (0.6:0.4). Thus, 180 and 120 samples were assigned to be collected from the HIV patients and farm workers, respectively.

To reduce sampling bias in this two-stage random sampling technique, stringent probability sampling procedures were used throughout both rounds of selection (primary units: dairy farms and health facilities; secondary units: farm workers and HIV patients) to ensure that the study design was properly implemented. Random numbers generated via online resources were employed for ensuring randomization. Academics and colleagues conducted formal and informal reviews of the study design and procedures to identify any blind spots or sources of bias, methodological rigor, credibility and transparency, as well as ethical responsibility.

Study population and sample population

The study population consisted of dairy farm workers (farm owners, attendants, and their children) in the selected 13 dairy farms, and people living with HIV/AIDS (receiving and not receiving ART) who were on follow-up at the selected 9 health facilities in the study districts. Following the setup of the sampling frame, the sample population was drawn at random from the study population using online-generated random numbers.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

The study included all dairy farm employees (farm owners, attendants, and their children) who work or live on the selected dairy farms, as well as all HIV-positive patients on follow-up at the specified ART health facilities who expressed an interest in participating.

Exclusion criteria

Individuals with cognitive impairments preventing informed consent, as well as mentally ill or severely ill patients, were excluded from participating in the study.

Questionnaire survey

A pretested questionnaire, developed specifically for this study, was used to collect data on factors associated with Cryptosporidium infection among dairy farm workers and HIV-seropositive participants). The instrument consisted primarily of closed-ended (categorical) items to facilitate data processing, minimize variation, and improve response precision. The same questionnaire was administered to both groups, with a small number of items tailored to HIV-positive participants; these were coded as “missing data” for farm workers during analysis. This approach maximized the amount of information obtained, allowing the study to address broad research questions applicable to the entire population while also capturing subgroup-specific issues. Clinical history and stool sample data were collected in a standardized manner across all participants.

Prior to the main survey, the questionnaire was piloted on a subset of the study population to identify and correct potential flaws, improve clarity and comprehension, and refine the flow of questions. This process enhanced the validity and reliability of the instrument. Demographic information—including age, sex, and education level—was collected, along with potential associated factors for Cryptosporidium infection such as the presence of animals at home, type of animal contact, exposure to animal excrement, drinking water sources (tap, well, river, or spring), and water treatment practices.

Medical record data

Data on the medication history of the HIV-positive study participants, including ART medication status (pre-ART or on-ART), length of ART medication, and the CD4 + count on the sampling dates, were gathered from the medical records of each participant. (Samples were gathered in 2016, before the implementation of the “Treat All” policy in Ethiopia in October 2017).

Sample collection and preparation

Approximately 5 g of stool was collected aseptically from each participant in a sterile stool cup, labeled, preserved in an icebox, and transported to the pathobiology laboratory of the Aklilu Lemma Institute of Pathobiology (ALIPB), Addis Ababa University.

Laboratory examinations

Each stool sample was portioned into two halves immediately on the sampling day. The first portion was processed using the modified Ziehl–Neelsen acid–fast staining method, and the remaining portion was preserved in 2.5% potassium dichromate (w/v) solution at a 1:1 ratio and stored at -20 °C for molecular analysis.

Microscopy

Thin stool smears were stained using the modified Ziehl–Neelsen method [27]. A small amount of stool was spread over the surface of a clean slide on an area of approximately 2 cm × 1 cm. Smears were air-dried and fixed in absolute methanol for 5 min. For HIV-positive participants, smears were fixed in formalin vapor (37% formaldehyde solution) for 15 min by placing slides in a Petri dish containing a cotton wool ball soaked in formalin. Slides were flooded with carbol fuchsin stain (3% fuchsine and 4% phenol) for 5 min, rinsed with water, and decolorized with acid–ethanol solution (concentrated HCL and 95% ethanol) until faint pink. Slides were then rinsed with water, counterstained with 0.33% malachite green solution for 2 min, washed again, and placed in a slide rack to drain and dry.

A sample was considered positive for Cryptosporidium spp. if an oocyst with the correct morphology, optical properties, internal structure, size, and shape was detected as previously described [28]. When Cryptosporidium oocysts were identified microscopically, positive results were recorded, and the corresponding potassium dichromate-preserved samples were identified and labeled for use in molecular studies. The intensity of infection was estimated semi quantitatively according to the average number of oocysts in 20 randomly selected fields observed at 1000x magnification, following the standard criteria [29]: 0 (0 oocyst); I (1 oocyst); II (2–5 oocysts); III (6–10 oocysts); and IV (> 10 oocysts).

Molecular techniques

Extraction of genomic DNA

DNA was extracted from all stool samples using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (QIAGEN, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration of eluted DNA was measured spectrophotometrically on a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA), and the DNA was stored at -20 °C until it was used for molecular analysis.

Nested PCR

Molecular analysis was done on all stool samples, a two-step nested PCR protocol was used to amplify a fragment of the SSU rRNA gene of Cryptosporidium species oocysts (840 bp), as described previously [30]. For primary PCR, a 1325 bp PCR product was amplified via the forward and reverse primers SSU-F2 (5’-TTCTAGAGCTAATACATGCG-3’) and SSU-R2 (5’-CCCATTTCCTTCGAAACAGGA-3’), respectively. In the secondary PCR, a product size of 819–825 bp was amplified via the forward primer SSU-F3: 5’-GGAAGGGTTGTATTTATTAGATAAAG-3’ and the reverse primer SSU-R4: 5’- CTCATAAGGTGCTGAAGGAGTA-3’ [30]. The secondary products of the nested PCRs were analysed via 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis and visualized via Gel Red staining. Each sample was analysed at least twice using reagent water as the negative control and DNA from C. baileyi as the positive control to reduce the likelihood of PCR contamination by any of the bovine species. All PCRs were run on a Thermocycler (PCR-Gene Amp PCR System 9700, Applied Biosystems).

Purification of the nested PCR product

Prior to genotyping and sequencing, the 18 S rRNA gene nested PCR product was purified via a QIAquick PCR purification kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism (RFLP)

The purified nested products were digested via the SspI, AseI (VspI), or MboII (New England BioLabs Inc. USA) restriction enzymes [31–33]. Briefly, 10 µl of the purified secondary PCR product was digested with 5 units of each enzyme and 2 µl of the corresponding 10x buffer in a final volume of 20 µl. All restriction digestions were carried out at 37 °C overnight, after which the proteins were separated on a 2% agarose gel and visualized after red gel staining using ultraviolet (UV) transillumination under a UV transilluminator.

Sequencing

Sequencing of the purified secondary nested PCR products was performed in both directions using the secondary PCR primers and the Big Dye Terminator V3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Applied Biosystems, CA, U.S.A.) on an ABI 3730‒48 Capillary Genetic Analyser (Applied Biosystems Sequencer, Foster City, CA). The obtained sequences were analysed via the CLC main workbench (CLC version 7.6.4, QIAGEN Aarhus) and compared with the GenBank sequences of Cryptosporidium via BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool, NCBI http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/blast/Blast.cgi) to identify species and determine the percent homology. Sequencing was performed at the Segolip unit of the Biosciences Eastern and Central Africa-International Livestock Research Institute Hub (BecA-ILRI Hub), Nairobi, Kenya.

Statistical analysis

All data were entered into Microsoft Excel and then imported into SPSS (version 20.0; IBM SPSS Statistics, Chicago, IL, USA) for analysis. The prevalence of Cryptosporidium was estimated using descriptive statistics. Logistic regression was performed to determine the importance of various related factors on Cryptosporidium prevalence. Univariable logistic regression was first performed to assess crude associations between Cryptosporidium prevalence and each exposure variable. This step produced crude odds ratios (ORs), confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values. Variables with p < 0.25 were flagged for further consideration. Variable selection for the multivariable model was based on: Statistical screening (p < 0.25 in univariable analysis), Biological plausibility and prior evidence, Confounding assessment (≥ 10% change in ORs), Multicollinearity checks (VIF), Model fit and parsimony (AIC/BIC, likelihood ratio tests), Multivariable logistic regression was then conducted to estimate adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% CIs and p < 0.05, controlling for confounders.

Results

Questionnaire results

Prior to the main study, pilot surveys confirmed the questionnaire’s structure, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85. This result indicates that the measurement tools used demonstrated high internal reliability. Consequently, the questionnaire achieved a 92% response rate, as it was administered in person by the researcher, who directly interacted with participants. This approach proved effective in reducing non-response bias and enhancing the validity of the collected data.

Demography of the study participants

The study included a total of 300 participants. The distribution of demographic characteristics showed distinct differences within the cohort. Most participants (67.3%) lived in Oromia, while 32.7% resided in Addis Ababa. The study population consisted mainly of HIV-positive individuals (60.0%), with 40.0% being dairy farm workers. Age distribution was skewed toward adults. The largest group was 21–50 years (56.0%), followed by children aged 1–10 years (21.7%). Participants over 50 years comprised the smallest group (4.3%). Gender distribution was approximately equal, with slightly more women (53.0%) than men (47.0%). In terms of education, most Participants had lower levels of formal schooling. The majority (70.3%) had no formal education or only elementary education, while 29.7% had completed high school or college (Table 1).

Prevalence of Cryptosporidium infection

A total of 300 stool samples were collected from study participants across 13 dairy farms and 9 health centers in Addis Ababa and its surrounding areas. Each sample was examined using both modified Ziehl–Neelsen (MZN) microscopy and nested PCR techniques, with both methods yielding an equal detection rate of 27 positive cases out of 300 samples (9%). Cryptosporidium infections were detected at all 13 dairy farms (100%), with prevalence ranging from 4.3% to 18.8%, and at eight of 9 health centers (88.9%), with prevalence ranging from 0% to 15%.

Overall prevalence of Cryptosporidium infection among participants was 9% (95% CI: 5.7–12.3). Prevalence among dairy farm workers, 10.8% (95% CI: 5.7–16.0), was higher compared with 7.8% (95% CI: 3.6–12.0) among HIV-positive patients; however, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.369). No statistically significant differences were observed between females and males (p = 0.780), Prevalence was highest in the 1–10 year age group (15.4%, 95% CI: 8.4–22.4), but differences among age groups were not statistically significant (p = 0.128) (Table 1).

Prevalence of Cryptosporidium species

Among the 27 positive cases, C. parvum accounted for 22 (81.5%), while C. hominis represented 5 (18.5%). The prevalence of C. parvum infection was 7.33% (22/300), which was higher than that of C. hominis at 1.66% (5/300). Of the 22 C. parvum infections, 13 occurred in dairy farm workers and 9 in HIV-positive individuals. In contrast, all C. hominis infections (100%, 5/5) were detected exclusively in HIV-positive patients, with none observed among dairy farm workers.

Intensity of Cryptosporidium

Twenty-seven Cryptosporidium oocyst-positive samples were semi-quantitatively examined for infection intensity. Of these, 7 (26%) had > 10 oocysts, 5 (18%) had 6–10 oocysts, 7 (26%) had 2–5 oocysts, and 8 (30%) had 1 oocyst (Fig. 1). Higher infection intensity was observed in children under 5 years and in HIV-positive participants not yet on-ART.

Factors associated with Cryptosporidium infection

Significant associations were found with presence of animals at home (p = 0.024), type of animal contact (p = 0.015), frequency of contact with animals and feces (p = 0.008), duration of animal contact (p = 0.014), and source of drinking water (p = 0.004) (Table 2). Participants with no formal or only elementary education were 3.30 times more likely to be infected than those with high school or college education (OR = 3.30, 95% CI: 1.10 − 10.0). Participants with animals at home were 2.1 times more likely to become infected than those without (OR = 2.1, 95% CI: 1.1–4.0) Participants who owned only cattle were 3.1 times more likely to acquire Cryptosporidium (OR = 3.1, 95% CI: 1.1–8.9), and those with both cattle and pets were 2.85 times more likely (OR = 2.85, 95% CI: 1.0–8.2), compared with participants without animals at home. Participants with frequent contact with animals and their feces were 3.2 times more likely to be infected (OR = 3.2, 95% CI: 1.2–8.6) compared with those with no contact. Participants with more than one year of animal contact were 3.0 times more likely to be infected (OR = 3.0, 95% CI: 1.1–8.2) compared with those with no animal contact. Participants who drank well or stream water were 2.85 times more likely to acquire Cryptosporidium (OR = 2.85, 95% CI: 1.2–6.8) compared with those who drank piped water (Tables 2 and 3).

Clinical features associated with Cryptosporidium infection

Additionally, the associations between Cryptosporidium infection and the frequency and duration of diarrhea, consistency and color of feces, and presence of mucus or blood in stools were investigated (Table 4). Cryptosporidium was more common in people with a history of diarrhea than in people without a history or a current episode of diarrhea (OR = 4.43, 95% CI: 1.0–10.5). People who had 5 diarrhea episodes per day had a greater infection rate than those who did not have diarrhea (OR = 3.27, 95% CI: 1.5–7.4). Compared with those without diarrhea, those with diarrhea for a week had a significantly greater prevalence (OR = 3.91, 95% CI: 1.7–8.9). Individuals with mucoid feces (14.7%) had a higher prevalence of Cryptosporidium than those without mucoid feces (6.3%) (OR = 2.55, 95% CI: 1.2–5.7). People on-ART had a lower prevalence, 0.7%, than those not on-ART (30.2%), implying that pre-ART people were about 33 times (OR = 32.92, 95% CI: 4.3–58.71) more likely to be infected than those on-ART. Individuals with a CD4 + count below 200 cells/µl had a prevalence of 30.8%, whereas those with a CD4 + count between 200 and 500 cells/µl had a prevalence of 1.7%. These findings imply that those with a CD4 + count of < 200 cells/µl were about 25 times (OR = 25.33, 95% CI: 5.4–69) more likely to acquire Cryptosporidium infection than people with a CD4 + count of 200–500 cells/µl. HIV-positive study participants with CD4 + cell counts > 500 cells/µl had not shown Cryptosporidium infections (Tables 4 and 5).

Results of molecular analysis

All 27 microscopy-positive samples analyzed using nested PCR targeting the Cryptosporidium SSU rRNA gene yielded the expected amplicon of approximately 834 bp (Fig. 2), whereas none of the microscopy-negative samples produced this predicted PCR product.

Cryptosporidium SSU rRNA gene nested PCR secondary product. Lane L: 100bp ladder; lanes N1-N6 positive samples (834 bp) (Full-length gels are presented in Supplementary Figure 1)

RFLP restriction of the purified secondary PCR products by the SspI enzyme revealed C. parvum bands at 449, 254, and 108 base pairs, as previously described [31]. Further RFLP restriction with the AseI (VspI) enzyme revealed bands of C. parvum ‘bovine genotype A’ at 628, 104, and 102 base pairs [31] (Fig. 3). Bovine genotype A was identified in both farm worker and HIV-positive patients.

Sequence analysis

The clear SSU rRNA gene sequences acquired from the 27 microscopy and PCR–positive samples were successfully sequenced and analysed. A GenBank homology search revealed that 22 of the 27 isolates (81.5%) were C. parvum and that 5 of them (18.5%) were C. hominis. These findings validated the C. parvum species previously revealed by RFLP analysis, whereas the C. hominis species, which had not been detected by RFLP, was confirmed by sequence analysis. The partial SSU rRNA gene sequences of the 22 C. parvum-positive samples were found to be 100% comparable to the GenBank reference sequences for C. parvum: AF040725.1, EU660038.1, AB513880.1, AY204238.1, and AB089290.1. The five C. hominis-positive samples were 100% identical to the GenBank reference sequences for C. hominis: EU186156.1, KM085019.1, JQ313989.1, and GQ183510.1. The nucleotide sequence data presented in this paper are available in the GenBank database under the accession codes KX856000 to KX856002 and KX855997 to KX855999.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study assessed the prevalence of Cryptosporidium infection and identified associated factors and molecular species in HIV-positive patients and dairy farm workers in central Ethiopia. The overall prevalence was 9%, with 10.8% among farm workers and 7.8% among HIV positive individuals. The higher prevalence in farm workers is consistent with greater exposure to cattle herds, recognized as potential sources of infection. The 9% prevalence observed here is comparable to earlier Ethiopian reports (8.1–9%) [20], and to findings in children from Pakistan (9%) and Cameroon (8.9%) [34–35]. The 10.8% prevalence among farm workers falls within the 7.3–12.2% range reported in apparently healthy children [19, 20, 36], though it is lower than the 14.8% prevalence reported in diarrheic children [37]. The 7.8% prevalence in HIV-positive participants was lower than earlier Ethiopian reports (20.1%) [38], 17.7% [39]), as well as findings from Indonesia (12%) [40] and Nigeria (52.7%) [41]. Variations across studies may reflect differences in parasite distribution, water treatment practices, parasite strains, diagnostic sensitivity, and control measures against intestinal parasites and HIV/AIDS.

The higher prevalence among dairy farm workers is consistent with zoonotic risk linked to high oocyst exposure. Breeders may encounter feces containing oocysts when personal protective equipment (PPE), hand hygiene, or effective disinfectants are not consistently used [36, 42]. The lower prevalence among HIV-positive individuals in this study may be associated with ART use, improved urban infrastructure with access to boreholes or piped water, and safer hygiene practices such as consistent handwashing and vegetable washing, which reduce oocyst contamination [13].

A key observation is the predominance of C. parvum, accounting for 81.5% of infections. Nearly 60% of these cases occurred in dairy farm workers, with no C. hominis detected in this group, suggesting an association with zoonotic transmission. This is reinforced by associations with cattle ownership, frequent contact with animal feces, and prolonged animal exposure. Conversely, C. hominis was detected only in HIV-positive patients, highlighting that while zoonotic spillover drives much of the burden, anthroponotic transmission remains a particular threat to immunocompromised individuals. The distribution of C. parvum and C. hominis varies geographically. C. parvum predominates in Europe and the Middle East [43–46], especially in rural areas with high ruminant density [13], while C. hominis is more common in urban centers. In Iran, 76% of isolates were the bovine genotype [47]. Ethiopian studies reported C. parvum and C. hominis in 95% and 2.5% of infections, respectively [21], with similar findings in Sweden [48] and Thailand [49]. Conversely, C. hominis predominates in the United States [50], Australia [51], Kenya [15], and South Africa [52]. UK and Netherlands studies also reported higher C. hominis prevalence [53–54], though earlier UK data showed more bovine C. parvum [11]. Recent Chinese data identified both species in cattle, suggesting bidirectional transmission [55]. Overall, distribution reflects local oocyst sources (animal feces, human stool, sewage, food), transmission mode (zoonotic vs. anthroponotic), and environmental factors such as water quality, sanitation, hygiene, and animal contact.

The striking disparity, 30.8% prevalence in those with CD4 + counts < 200 cells/µl and 0% in those > 500 cells/µl, highlights the parasite’s opportunistic association with weakened immunity.

The study also indicates that Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) may have a protective role, as untreated patients were more frequently infected than those on ART. This aligns with evidence that immune reconstitution through ART is associated with improved clearance of the parasite [16, 26, 38, 40], whereas low CD4 levels are associated with persistent, high-intensity infections as observed in the semi-quantitative oocyst counts. These findings reinforce the global consensus that CD4 + T-cell counts are reliable predictors of cryptosporidiosis in HIV-positive populations [16, 56].

Our study highlighted associations between Cryptosporidium infection and ownership of animals at home, as well as high level of animal and fecal contact. The association was particularly evident in people with high contact to cattle. Our findings are consistent with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia and other countries, which have identified close contact with cattle and exposure to their feces as major risk factors for human Cryptosporidium infection [22, 36, 49–57]. Outbreaks of cryptosporidiosis have been reported in Sweden following public visits to dairy animals on spring pasture, with calf contact identified as a likely associated factor [58]. Likewise, a study from Pakistan found that most infected children had a history of animal contact, suggesting that animals serve as reservoirs for human infection [34]. A study in Thailand showed that stool samples from HIV-positive patients and cattle were all identified as C. parvum, suggesting zoonotic transmission between cattle and humans [49]. Contact with animals or contaminated environment provides a direct route for oocysts to enter the body. Because oocysts are resistant to environmental stressors and disinfectants, they can remain infectious in soil, bedding, or on animal fur for extended periods [1, 4]. Duration of animal contact also appeared to be a strong predictor of infection: those exposed for more than one year showed increased prevalence, consistent with cumulative exposure to contaminated surroundings.

In this study, no associations were found between human infections and contact with pet animals or their feces. However, reports from India documented C. canis and C. felis oocysts in pets and humans living in the same household [59]. Similarly, a study in Thailand reported that 30–40% of infections in dogs and cats were due to C. parvum, suggesting possible zoonotic transmission between pets and humans [60]. Detection of zoonotic C. parvum in dogs and cats supports findings that Cryptosporidium parasites are not host-specific and may spread from animals to humans [61]. The absence of association between human infections and pet ownership in the study area could be due to limited interaction between humans and their pets, since pets are usually kept as guardians rather than companions.

Individuals relying on well or stream water showed higher Cryptosporidium prevalence than those using piped water, which may be linked to greater exposure of untreated sources to animal and human waste. A study reported 77.8% of infections in children using well water [34], and similar associations with polluted sources were noted in Kuwait [43]. Urban residence near large cities appears protective, with reduced livestock exposure and access to treated water [62]. In Scotland, improperly treated private supplies have posed public health risks [13]. Most sporadic infections in rural areas are linked to farm animal contact and contaminated surface or groundwater [10]. Cryptosporidium oocysts resist standard disinfectants such as chlorine, while piped water is typically filtered or UV treated; untreated streams and wells remain vulnerable to contamination from animal waste or sewage [28, 30].

Our findings showed that individuals with a high school or college education had lower infection rates, which may reflect improved personal hygiene and greater awareness of disease prevention. Higher education is often associated with better handwashing practices, safer food preparation, and greater resources for improved sanitation [63].

Cryptosporidium prevalence was markedly higher in HIV patients not receiving ART compared to those on treatment, with infection rates greatest in individuals with CD4 + counts < 200 cells/µl versus 200–500 cells/µl. Studies consistently report that low CD4 + T-cell levels (< 200 cells/µl) are associated with increased cryptosporidiosis prevalence [64–65], while ART has been associated with reductions in opportunistic infections, likely through restoration of immune responses to viruses, fungi, and protozoa. Recovery of CD4 + T lymphocytes has been associated with resolution of cryptosporidiosis and diarrhea [64], and ART remains the only therapy consistently associated with extended and improved quality of life in HIV-positive individuals [56]. Disease severity and complications appear to correlate with CD4 + counts, with mild infections typically seen at > 500 cells/µl [40]. ART has also been linked to lower prevalence of pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus, Salmonella, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis [66], alongside reductions in Cryptosporidium among ART-treated patients in Ethiopia [17, 26, 67]. When CD4 + counts fall below 200 cells/µl, cytokine production declines, which has been associated with persistent parasite replication and prolonged diarrhea [49, 63]. By inhibiting viral replication, ART supports immune reconstitution, which may allow CD4 + T cells to clear opportunistic parasites [66]. This pattern explains the significantly lower prevalence in ART-treated patients (0.7%) compared to ART-naïve individuals (30.2%).

Our findings revealed higher Cryptosporidium prevalence among individuals with frequent (≥ 5/day) or prolonged (≥ 1 week) diarrhea, consistent with the chronic nature of infection. Diarrhea and abdominal pain were the most commonly reported symptoms [68], while oocyst shedding was linked to prolonged diarrhea, vomiting, and cramps [34]. Chronic diarrhea has been widely reported in HIV-positive patients, including 100% of seropositive cases in Nigeria [41] and in Indonesia [40]. In Mozambique, HIV infection was a major risk factor for mortality in children under five with diarrhea, malnutrition, and tuberculosis [9]. Clinical features of C. hominis and C. parvum are largely similar, though C. hominis has more often been reported in association with nausea, vomiting, and malaise [69–70]; in HIV/AIDS patients, diarrhea was linked to both species, but vomiting only to C. hominis [22]. Pathogenesis has been described through two mechanisms: malabsorptive diarrhea, caused by enterocyte loss and villus blunting, reducing nutrient and water absorption [71, 72]; and secretory diarrhea, driven by prostaglandin-induced chloride and carbonate secretion with reduced sodium chloride absorption, creating osmotic pressure that forces water into the lumen [69].

Conclusion

Cryptosporidium is an endemic public health concern in Addis Ababa and surrounding peri-urban areas, with an overall prevalence of 9%. The transmission landscape is complex, involving zoonotic spillover from dairy farming, environmental contamination of water sources, and heightened vulnerability among immunocompromised individuals. C. parvum is the predominant species, with dairy farm workers at greatest risk. Detection across all surveyed farms indicates that urban agriculture serves as a significant reservoir. Reliance on unprotected water sources and frequent exposure to animal waste are key environmental drivers. This study underscores the need for a One Health approach integrating veterinary management, improved water infrastructure, and expanded ART coverage to effectively reduce Cryptosporidium infections in Ethiopia.

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design restricts our ability to establish causal relationships between the identified risk factors and Cryptosporidium infection. Second, although a two-stage random sampling method was employed, selection bias cannot be entirely excluded. Third, unmeasured confounding variables—such as specific household sanitation practices and HIV viral load levels—may have influenced the observed associations. Fourth, the relatively small number of positive cases in certain subgroups resulted in wide confidence intervals for some odds ratios, limiting the precision of these estimates.

Finally, due to resource constraints, drinking water samples could not be analyzed for oocyst contamination, which restricted our ability to directly assess the role of contaminated water in transmission. These limitations highlight the need for future studies with larger sample sizes, inclusion of viral load data, and expanded molecular screening that incorporates environmental sampling. Such approaches would strengthen causal inference and clarify transmission dynamics.

Recommendations

On the basis of the findings of this study both preventive and control measures are recommended Ensuring access to safe drinking water is critical, particularly for communities that rely on untreated wells and streams, which were strongly associated with infection.

Public health education should be strengthened through targeted campaigns on hygiene, sanitation, and zoonotic disease prevention, especially among individuals with lower educational attainment.

Dairy farm hygiene practices must also be improved by training workers in safe handling of cattle and manure, encouraging the use of protective equipment, and enhancing waste management.

These measures should be supported by occupational health standards that recognize the zoonotic risks faced by farm workers.

Integration of HIV care with parasitic disease control is essential. Routine screening for Cryptosporidium should be considered within HIV programs, particularly for patients with low CD4 + counts, while strengthening ART coverage and adherence will reduce susceptibility to infection.

At the policy level, a “One Health” approach is recommended, fostering collaboration between veterinary, medical, and environmental health sectors. Surveillance systems that monitor Cryptosporidium in humans, animals, and water sources would provide valuable insights into transmission dynamics and guide evidence-based interventions.
