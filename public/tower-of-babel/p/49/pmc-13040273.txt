Prognostic significance of serum pyroptosis-related factors in acute coronary syndrome: a propensity score analysis
Authors: Pei Shi, Qiangsun Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis study investigated the clinical significance of pyroptosis-related factors, including NLRP1, NLRP3, and HMGB1, in patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS), and analyzed their prognostic value.MethodsA total of 231 ACS patients (unstable angina, STEMI, and NSTEMI) treated between March 2020 and August 2022 were included. Serum levels of NLRP1, NLRP3, HMGB1, IL-6, IL-1β, and CRP were measured using ELISA, and demographic and clinical data were recorded. Patients were followed for one year, and those who experienced major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) were classified into the poor prognosis group. Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to minimize baseline differences between groups.ResultsAfter PSM, 61 matched pairs (n = 122) were included for analysis, and baseline characteristics between groups were balanced. Activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) and prothrombin time (PT) were significantly shorter in the poor prognosis group. In contrast, serum levels of NLRP1, NLRP3, and HMGB1 were significantly higher in the poor prognosis group and correlated positively with inflammatory markers. Among the factors studied, NLRP1 demonstrated the highest prognostic accuracy (AUC = 0.799, cutoff = 31.04 pg/ml, sensitivity = 70.49 %, specificity = 70.49 %). Multivariate analysis identified lower APTT and PT, as well as higher NLRP1, and NLRP3 as independent risk factors for poor prognosis.ConclusionsElevated serum levels of NLRP1, NLRP3, and HMGB1 are associated with poor prognosis in patients with ACS. These factors may serve as potential biomarkers for risk stratification and therapeutic targets in clinical practice.Keywords: Coronary syndrome, Pyroptosis, NLRP3, Risk factors, Prognosis

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading global cause of death. From 1990 to 2019, annual CVD cases rose from 31.31 to 55.45 million, and deaths increased to 18.56 million, with the burden growing fastest in low- and middle-SDI regions [1]. Acute coronary syndrome (ACS), primarily caused by myocardial ischemia due to coronary artery narrowing or blockage, is a major contributor to CHD [2]. ACS is a significant global health burden, leading to nearly 7 million deaths and 129 million cases of disability annually [3,4]. Known risk factors for ACS include age, gender, smoking, psychosocial stress, diabetes, hypertension, body mass index (BMI), alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, and poor dietary habits [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. Given the substantial societal and economic impact of ACS, there is a critical need to improve risk stratification and identify patients with poor prognosis. This differentiation can help optimize prevention and treatment strategies in clinical practice.

Pyroptosis is a form of inflammatory programmed cell death characterized by the formation of membrane pores, cellular content leakage, and the subsequent activation of an inflammatory response [9]. This cell death pathway has been implicated in various diseases, including cardiovascular diseases such as ACS [[10], [11], [12]]. Recent findings suggest that endothelial cell pyroptosis plays a critical role in coronary artery endothelial injury in Kawasaki disease. Specifically, this NLRP3-dependent pyroptosis is activated via the HMGB1/RAGE/cathepsin B signaling pathway [13]. Additionally, a clinical study reported significant upregulation of NLRP1 in atherosclerotic patients compared to healthy controls [14]. These findings highlight the potential involvement of pyroptosis-related factors, including NLRP1, NLRP3, and high mobility group Box 1 (HMGB1), in ACS pathophysiology. However, while existing studies provide strong evidence from basic research, clinical investigations remain limited.

In this prospective observational cohort study, we aimed to evaluate the serum levels and clinical significance of pyroptosis-related factors (NLRP1, NLRP3, and HMGB1) in patients with ACS and identify risk factors associated with their prognosis. To minimize bias and confounding variables, propensity score matching (PSM) was applied for patient selection. This study provides insight into the prognostic value of NLRP1, NLRP3, and HMGB1 in ACS and highlights potential therapeutic targets for its treatment.

Materials and methods

Patients

This prospective cohort study included 231 patients with ACS, including unstable angina (UA), ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), and non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), who were treated at our hospital between March 2020 and August 2022. Sample size was not based on a priori power calculation, as all eligible ACS patients within the defined period were consecutively enrolled and subsequently matched using PSM to minimize selection bias.

The diagnostic criteria for UA were typical angina symptoms, ischemic electrocardiographic changes (new or transient ST depression ≥ 0.1 mV or T-wave inversion ≥ 0.2 mV), and normal levels of myocardial injury markers (cardiac troponin T, cardiac troponin I, or creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB)) [15]. NSTEMI was diagnosed using the same criteria as UA but with elevated myocardial injury markers [15]. For STEMI, diagnosis required cardiac biomarkers (preferably cTn) > 0.1 ng/ml or showing a rise and fall pattern, along with at least one of the following: ischemic symptoms lasting ≥20 min, new ST-segment elevation (V2–V3 ≥ 0.2 mV for men ≥40 years, ≥0.25 mV for men <40 years, or ≥0.15 mV for women; ≥0.1 mV in other leads and limb leads), pathological Q waves, or imaging evidence of new myocardial viability loss or regional wall motion abnormalities [16]. Exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with stable angina, myocardial bridges, coronary malformations, cardiomyopathy, valvular heart disease, congenital heart disease, or heart failure; those who had received hormones, anti-inflammatory drugs, antibiotics, NSAIDs, or immunosuppressants within the past month; those with rheumatologic or hematologic disorders; and those with severe infections, advanced liver or renal disease, malignancies, or significant cardiovascular dysfunction. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, approved by the hospital Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Blood sampling measurement

Fasting cubital venous blood samples (5 mL) were collected within 24 h of admission for all patients. Samples were centrifuged at 2000 g for 15 min, and serum levels of NLRP1, NLRP3, HMGB1, interleukin (IL)-6, IL-1β, and C-reactive protein (CRP) were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (MyBioSource, USA). The specific kits used were as follows: NLRP1 (Cat. No. MBS924094), NLRP3 (Cat. No. MBS167228), HMGB1 (Cat. No. MBS3804267), IL-6 (Cat. No. MBS175877), CRP (Cat. No. MBS177184), and IL-1β (MBS175901 MyBioSource) [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. All assays were conducted following the manufacturers’ protocols, using standard sandwich ELISA procedures including sample dilution, incubation with capture antibodies, washing, detection with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, and optical density measurement at 450 nm. According to the manufacturers, the assay sensitivities were as follows: NLRP1, 1.0 pg/mL; NLRP3, 1.06 pg/mL; HMGB1, 0.1 ng/mL; IL-6, <0.3 pg/mL; IL-1β, <0.15 pg/mL; and CRP, <10 pg/mL. Specificity information provided by the manufacturers indicated that these assays detect natural and recombinant human proteins, with no reported cross-reactivity to other cytokines.

Analytical validation of the ELISA kits for NLRP1, NLRP3, and HMGB1 was performed in our laboratory, including assessments of detection limits, linearity, reproducibility, and recovery. Detailed validation procedures and results are provided in the supplementary materials.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, BMI, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and other relevant parameters, were collected. Routine blood tests were performed using an automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi 7600, Hitachi Corporation, Japan), and the following laboratory markers were recorded: fasting plasma glucose (FPG), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDLC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDLC), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), prothrombin time (PT), D-dimer (D-D), creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), troponin I (TnI), and brain natriuretic peptide (BNP).

All patients were followed for one year. Poor prognosis was defined as the occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including cardiac death, heart failure, cardiogenic shock, recurrent myocardial infarction, or hemodynamically significant arrhythmias.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (range). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using the Student’s t-test for normally distributed data or the Mann-Whitney test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. PSM was applied to minimize baseline differences between groups. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression based on the following variables: age, gender, BMI, TC, TG, HDLC, LDLC, and FPG. Matching was performed using the nearest-neighbor algorithm with a 1:1 ratio and without replacement, under default settings in SPSS version 26.0 (no caliper specified). Matched pairs were used for all subsequent analyses. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to evaluate associations between variables. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the diagnostic value of pyroptosis-related factors for predicting poor prognosis. Binary multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent risk factors for poor prognosis. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic characteristics of all patients

A total of 231 patients with ACS were enrolled in this study and followed for one year. Based on the occurrence of MACE, patients were divided into a good prognosis group (n = 142) and a poor prognosis group (n = 89). At admission, TC, FPG, D-D, and the levels of NLRP1, NLRP3, HMGB1, IL-1β, and IL-6 were significantly higher in the poor prognosis group compared to the good prognosis group (P < 0.05, Table 1). In contrast, APTT and TT were significantly longer in the good prognosis group (P < 0.05). No other significant differences were observed between the two groups.

A 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was conducted using default settings in SPSS (no caliper specified). All subsequent analyses were performed on the matched cohort. Diagnostic value of pyroptosis-related factors for poor prognosis in ACS

The diagnostic value of serum NLRP1, NLRP3, and HMGB1 levels for predicting poor prognosis in ACS patients was evaluated using ROC curve analysis after PSM. Among these markers, NLRP1 demonstrated the highest diagnostic accuracy, with an AUC of 0.799, a cutoff value of >31.04 pg/ml, and sensitivity and specificity both at 70.49 % (Fig. 1). NLRP3 showed AUC of 0.758, with a cutoff value of >25.94 pg/ml, sensitivity 72.13 % and specificity 60.66 %; while HMGB1 showed AUC of 0.704, with a cutoff value of >42.61 pg/ml, sensitivity 65.57 % and specificity 67.21 %.

Logistic regression analysis for risk factors of poor prognosis in ACS

Binary multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify risk factors associated with poor prognosis in ACS patients. Three models were constructed using the entry method: Model 1 included clinical data (age, sex, BMI, SBP, DBP, TC, TG, HDLC, LDLC and FPG), Model 2 included cardiac enzyme profiles and coagulation factors (CK-MB, TnI, BNP, APTT, PT, and D-D), and Model 3 included inflammatory markers and pyroptosis-related factors (CRP, IL-6, IL-1β, NLRP1, NLRP3, and HMGB1). The results identified lower APTT and PT, as well as higher levels of NLRP1 and NLRP3, as independent risk factors for poor prognosis (Table 5).

Discussion

Coronary syndromes can lead to severe complications, including myocardial infarction and sudden cardiac death, and are a major cause of global mortality, responsible for up to 17 million deaths annually [21]. In China, the 2018 China Cardiovascular Disease Report revealed a continuous rise in CVD prevalence since 2006, with approximately 290 million individuals affected. Mortality rates from CVD now surpass those of cancer and other diseases [22]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify risk factors for coronary heart disease (CHD), develop new biomarkers, and adopt comprehensive strategies for early diagnosis, intervention, and improved prognosis management. In this study, we identified NLRP1 and NLRP3 as significant risk factors for poor prognosis in patients with ACS.

The accurate assessment of ACS prognosis is crucial for guiding treatment decisions and reducing the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). Recent studies have explored various biomarkers for prognostic evaluation. Wang et al. reported that a high high-sensitivity CRP-to-albumin ratio (CAR ≥ 0.114) was associated with increased MACE incidence in ACS patients, with CAR, hs-CRP, and albumin identified as independent predictors of MACE [23]. Similarly, Zhang et al. demonstrated that a coronary angiography-derived microcirculatory resistance index (caIMR ≥ 25) was associated with significantly higher MACE rates (20.6 % vs. 8.2 %, P = 0.002), highlighting its utility in predicting patient outcomes [24]. Additionally, a recent meta-analysis found that elevated IL-6 levels in ACS patients were associated with critical illness, higher cardiovascular event risk, and worse prognosis [25]. Interestingly, our study did not identify IL-6 as an independent risk factor after PSM, which may be attributable to differences in sample size, study populations, or assay methods. Nonetheless, we observed that patients with elevated IL-6 and IL-1β levels tended to have worse prognoses.

Pyroptosis, a specific form of programmed cell death, plays a critical role in the development of atherosclerosis by exacerbating inflammation and promoting the clearance of necrotic cells [26,27]. Furthermore, pyroptosis has been implicated in myocardial infarction and heart failure by triggering inflammatory responses, cardiomyocyte death, and myocardial injury [28,29]. Mechanistically, Zhang et al. found that reducing NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis and inhibiting endoplasmic reticulum stress in vascular endothelial cells (VECs) exerted anti-atherogenic effects [30]. Zeng et al. demonstrated that HMGB1 inhibition disrupted the balance between autophagy and pyroptosis, leading to neutrophil dysfunction during inflammation [31]. These studies support a close link between pyroptosis, inflammation, and the progression of coronary syndromes, where pyroptosis-induced inflammation may further exacerbate atherosclerosis and thrombosis [27,32]. Recent studies have also highlighted a mechanistic link between elevated NLRP3 activation in cardiomyocytes and the development of atrial fibrillation, which may further contribute to adverse outcomes in patients with cardiovascular disease [33,34]. Consistent with these findings, our study revealed elevated serum levels of NLRP1, NLRP3, and HMGB1 in ACS patients with poor prognosis, with significant correlations observed between pyroptosis-related factors and inflammatory markers IL-6 and IL-1β.

In addition to their prognostic significance, we also evaluated the diagnostic value of pyroptosis-related factors using ROC curve analysis. Among them, NLRP1 demonstrated the highest predictive accuracy for poor prognosis, with an AUC of 0.799, which is comparable to established biomarkers. For instance, previous studies have reported AUC values of 0.780 for NT-proBNP and 0.849 for Lp-PLA2 in predicting adverse outcomes following acute myocardial infarction [35], and an AUC of 0.85 for NT-proBNP in patients with NSTE-ACS [36]. Although the predictive value of NT-proBNP remains high, it may be affected by factors such as age, renal function, and comorbidities. In contrast, pyroptosis-related markers such as NLRP1 may offer complementary prognostic insight by reflecting inflammatory and cell death pathways that are not captured by traditional cardiac biomarkers. These findings suggest the potential utility of pyroptosis markers in conjunction with conventional indicators to enhance risk stratification in ACS.

Besides, the observed association between shorter APTT and PT and poor prognosis is consistent with the hypercoagulable state typically seen in ACS patients, and may reflect a greater thrombotic tendency in those with adverse outcomes.

Limitations

Despite its valuable findings, this study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of our results. Second, this was a single-center study without external validation, and thus the findings may not be directly applicable to other populations or clinical settings. Third, although we applied propensity score matching to reduce baseline differences between groups, potential residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out. Fourth, we only measured a limited number of inflammatory markers. Finally, while our study identified associations between pyroptosis-related factors and prognosis, further research is needed to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms of pyroptosis in ACS.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the prognostic value of pyroptosis-related factors (NLRP1, NLRP3, and HMGB1) in ACS patients, emphasizing their relationship with inflammatory markers IL-6 and IL-1β. These findings provide new insights into the role of pyroptosis in ACS progression and may inform the development of novel therapeutic strategies for improving patient outcomes.
