A wireless power transfer system for leadless endovascular electrocorticography
Authors: Zhangyu Xu, Nhan Duy Truong, Arman Ahnood, Armin Nikpour, Omid Kavehei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWireless power transfer (WPT) for stent-based neuroprosthetic devices, such as endovascular electrocorticography (endoECoG) systems, is typically constrained by the need for long lead wires to subcutaneous chest implants. This study presents a method for delivering power directly to an unmodified medical stent. The proposed system employs a subcutaneous relay that converts inductive coupling to capacitive coupling, thereby improving power transfer efficiency, reducing invasiveness, and mitigating instability in skin-contact capacitance. Experimental validation using skin, bone, and vessel tissues, combined with finite element simulations, demonstrated over 45 mW of delivered power, sufficient for endoECoG and biosignal sensing. The proposed system achieved 7.26% DC-to-DC efficiency, the highest reported for stent-based implants without custom stents or auxiliary transceivers. Measured results closely matched simulations, validating the experiment results. Safety assessments, including specific absorption rate and thermal analysis, confirmed compliance with regulatory limits. While the experimental results indicate robust performance, further theoretical analysis is required to establish a complete mechanistic understanding of the underlying coupling processes. The proposed architecture enables efficient, safe, and fully wireless power delivery to endovascular implants without requiring close skin contact, supporting long-term implantation, enhancing patient comfort, and facilitating future clinical translation.Subject terms: Biomedical engineering, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

Notable advancements have been made in developing stent-based biomedical implants, a minimal-invasive device that offers relatively lower-risk surgical delivery procedures1. With progress in electronics, stents now provide mechanical support and are equipped with sensing and stimulation capabilities2,3. This evolution could enable stents to function beyond passive interventions, expanding their application domains to monitoring and potentially to active interventions3,4.

These stent-based biomedical implants open new avenues for disease management and treatment. For example, endovascular electrocorticography (endoECoG) devices offer a relatively lower invasiveness method for long-term brain monitoring and high-performance brain-computer interfaces4,5. The current generation of endoECoG devices includes a stent with electrodes in the superior sagittal sinus (SSS), a companion unit in the chest area that receives power from an external unit and houses the electronics for signal acquisition and data communication. A long cable connects the stent in the brain to the companion unit, which carries the signal collected from the electrodes in the stent area. This device functions as a monitoring system and has the potential for brain stimulation to treat epilepsy and other neurological diseases5,6. However, the lead cable is the most critical challenge for the current device. The cable traverses through the blood vessel and requires an opening in the vessel wall for exit. These factors introduce risks, especially for long-term use, which lowers recipients’ acceptance and limits applications7.

With the advancement of miniaturized electronics, embedding sensing and communication circuits into a stent has been achieved8,9. Once there is a way to deliver energy directly into the stent, the endoECoG can be a fully wireless device without limitations related to lead cable. While substantial progress has been made in wireless power transfer (WPT) systems, delivering power directly to a leadless stent remains a substantial and open problem, not to mention a great deal of effort around making data telemetry wireless. Challenges include limited space, particularly around the unusually elongated form factor, the specialized mechanical structure of the stent and issues for electrical requirement adjustments, energy signal attenuation by biological tissues, and biocompatibility and safety requirements9,10. By addressing this challenge, endoECoG devices can become fully wireless, and after eliminating the lead-wire-related risks, these devices can reach their full potential and widespread application.

Various WPT techniques to address the challenge of powering stent-based biomedical implants have been explored in recent years. These techniques include near-field inductive coupling11, magnetic resonance coupling12, far-field radiofrequency (RF) energy transfer13, optical power delivery14,15, and acoustic power transfer14.

Near-field inductive coupling relies on a pair of coils nearby (less than one wavelength apart). Designing an efficient receiver coil within the limited space of a stent is especially challenging16. Some studies have attempted to use the entire stent as a receiving coil, showing promising results9. However, this approach requires specially designed stents with coil-like structures, as standard stents lack effective inductive properties8. Using such specialized stents introduces challenges for clinical approval due to unproven long-term mechanical stability, safety and the use of non-standard materials.

Magnetic resonance coupling faces similar obstacles when applied to stent-based implants. The requirement for resonant structures and efficient coupling over small volumes makes it difficult to implement within the confined space of a stent12,17. Achieving sufficient power transfer efficiency while meeting safety regulations remains problematic.

Far-field RF energy transfer allows for smaller antennas fitting within a stent. However, at the high frequencies required for effective far-field transmission, tissue absorption increases substantially18. This could reduce power transfer efficiency and raise concerns about tissue heating and subsequent compliance with regulatory safety limits on electromagnetic exposure19. Therefore, using far-field RF energy transfer to power a stent-based implant struggles to provide enough power without reaching the safety limit.

Optical links are promising for data transfer due to high bandwidth capabilities20 but are less suitable for energy transfer in implantable devices. When considering the light scattering by the biological tissues, the optical-to-electrical power conversion efficiency at the implant site is too low to meet the energy demands20,21.

Acoustic Power Transfer emerges as a promising alternative due to its ability to penetrate deep tissues with a lower attenuation window with the skull. It allows for small-sized receivers compatible with the dimensions of the stent and offers higher safety margins concerning tissue heating22. However, the primary challenges for acoustic power transfer in stent-based implants include the biocompatibility of piezoelectric materials used in acoustic transducers and the development of packaging technologies that can integrate these materials into stents without compromising mechanical properties or causing adverse biological reactions14.

Although various WPT techniques offer potential solutions, each presents a unique set of challenges when applied to stent-based implants. Overcoming these obstacles requires innovative approaches to accommodate the spatial constraints of stents and anatomical features for surgical implantation, maintain biocompatibility, and comply with required safety regulations.

Capacitive Coupling WPT is a technology that uses electric fields to transmit power between two conductive materials separated by a dielectric medium23. In this system, the transmitter and receiver each consist of a pair of plates that form capacitors, with the intervening space or material acting as the dielectric. When an alternating current (AC) is applied to the transmitter’s plate, it generates an oscillating electric field that induces a corresponding current in the receiver’s plate, thereby transferring power23.

Capacitive coupling WPT has gained attention for implantable medical devices due to its potential for miniaturization and reduced sensitivity to alignment compared to other WPT technologies24,25. The thin and flat nature of capacitive plates makes them suitable for integration into small implants where space is at a premium. Moreover, it is compatible with implants with an elongated form factor. In recent years, capacitive coupling WPT has been utilized to power various wearable and implantable devices because of its simplicity and flexibility26. In addition, capacitive coupling can be advantageous in scenarios where magnetic fields need to be minimized, such as in patients undergoing magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)27.

Although capacitive coupling WPT offers benefits for powering biomedical devices, it faces major challenges when applied to implantable devices. The contact impedance between the capacitive plates and biological tissue is a major issue, which can greatly affect power transfer efficiency28, especially for moving human body, the contact impedance to the skin can not stay stable. The sudden changes in this contact impedance lead to power losses and generate high electric fields that may unintentionally stimulate nerves or muscles26. Additionally, capacitive plates placed directly against the skin for long-term applications can cause tissue irritation and damage29. These factors limit the practical usability of capacitive coupling for implants that require consistent, long-term power delivery30.

This work presents a WPT system capable of transmitting power directly into a standard medical stent in the brain. The system provides a power budget exceeding 100 mW before reaching safety limits, sufficient for high-fidelity neural activity recording circuits, multiple biosignal recordings, and neuromodulation circuits. Designed for long-term applications, the external unit can maintain a naturally loose contact with the skin. Based on our research, this work may offer the highest WPT efficiency for a stent-based biomedical implant without requiring additional space or modifications to the structure of a typical stent. By providing these benefits, the proposed system is an ideal power transfer solution for an endoECoG device. Using the proposed WPT system, the endovascular electrocorticography device can be fully wireless without a lead wire or an extra implant unit for power and data. The proposed system utilizes a high-efficiency inductive link and a more flexible capacitive link to deliver power into the brain area. By employing a subcutaneous relay, the system effectively addresses the challenge of plate-to-tissue contact impedance and offers a relatively minimally invasive solution for the endoECoG device.

Figure 1 shows the structure of the proposed WPT system within the endoECoG application. The system comprises three main components: an external unit, a subcutaneous passive relay, and the receiver stent. The external device includes a power source, e.g., a battery, control circuits, and a coil for near-field inductive WPT to the subcutaneous relay. The external unit is worn over the head and uses a magnet for precise alignment with the subcutaneous relay coil beneath the scalp, ensuring efficient power transfer.

Conceptual design of the proposed WPT system for endoECoG device.

The relay is under the periosteum and above the skull. The relay is a passive device embedded in a thin silicon film. There is a magnet pair to align the external coil and the relay. The external unit aligns with the relay using a magnet. The stent has three sections: the two ends are the power-receiving stent (marked as receiving), and the optional middle section (marked as spacing) provides extra space for electronics.

The mechanically flexible subcutaneous passive relay is formed as a thin metal film with a silicone coating, which can be inserted under the periosteum layer of the scalp. Its structure consists of two metal plates serving as capacitive pole pairs and a flat coil in the gap between the two plates. The flat coil receives power through inductive coupling from the external device’s coil, where the elongated form factor consideration is not present, and then the relay delivers energy using the plates to the stent beneath the relay through the skull via capacitive coupling. This design overcomes challenges related to contact impedance and skin-related issues that plague traditional capacitive coupling methods and brings the transmitter plates closer to the receiver in a minimally invasive manner. Given the absence of any active electronics and the minimal encapsulation requirements, the subcutaneous passive relay can be very thin. The total thickness of this relay can be less than 0.2 mm (excluding the alignment magnet), using conventional manufacturing, which already offers a high comfort level for the recipients.

The receiver stent is a standard stent with three sections: two sections at the ends acting as the power receiving stents and a middle section between them to separate the two receiving pairs and provide space to house the functional circuits. The middle section is unnecessary for obtaining power, as it only provides a gap between the receiving pairs. The two end sections of the stent act as capacitive poles corresponding to the two capacitive plate poles in the subcutaneous relay. The harvesting circuits can reside in the middle section or be embedded in one end of the stent section, using a short lead wire to connect to the other section. Depending on the stent’s length requirements and the depth of its position, the passive relay plate pairs need to have matching lengths and sizes to maximize power transfer efficiency. This design does not require structural modifications to the stent, preserving its validated long-term safety, mechanical integrity, and medical functionality. The power-harvesting circuits for capacitive coupling contain only diodes and capacitors. These components have a very small volume and size, allowing them to be integrated with sensing or stimulation circuits and miniaturized to fit within the stent, enabling a fully functional smart stent without a lead wire.

This work demonstrates that the proposed system can deliver up to 100 mW of power to the receiving side without exceeding established safety limits. This power level is well above the requirements for continuous brain signal monitoring and provides a sufficient energy margin for on-demand stimulation applications. The experimental results indicate that the inclusion of a subcutaneous relay mitigates practical challenges associated with unstable contact impedance between capacitive pole plates and biological tissue. As the capacitive plates are positioned beneath the scalp and fully embedded within a biocompatible material validated for long-term implantation, the system avoids direct skin contact, thereby reducing the risk of skin irritation or damage during extended use.

The external device operates using classical near-field inductive coupling and employs a single pair of alignment magnets to ensure reproducible positioning relative to the implanted relay. The observed power transfer performance is consistent with an interpretation in which the relay facilitates effective coupling between the external inductive field and the intravascular stent receiver. However, other coupling mechanisms or the intertwining of the inductive link and capacitive link in the vicinity may also contribute to the measured results, and a complete theoretical description requires further investigation.

The alignment magnets are planned to be coated with parylene C, a polymer widely used in implantable medical devices, and are embedded within a silicone encapsulation to ensure compatibility with surrounding tissues. The compact size and lightweight design of the external device are readily achievable using established manufacturing techniques, supporting both functional performance and patient acceptance for daily wear.

Method

System architecture

Figure 2 illustrates the functional circuit blocks of the proposed system, which represent what circuits are included in the external unit, subcutaneous relay, and the implanted stent-based device.

System functional circuits block diagram.

MCU Microcontroller Unit, PMU Power management unit, Booster Direct current (DC) to DC voltage booster circuits, Driver Gate driver circuits, AC-DC Convert AC to DC to supply to the PMU, FU is a functional circuit unit providing signal sensing, data transmission, and optional stimulation functions, CSF cerebrospinal fluid, SSS superior sagittal sinus.

The external unit sits outside the body. It consists of a rechargeable battery, a power management unit (PMU), a microcontroller unit (MCU), a direct current (DC) to DC booster, a Class-E amplifier, and gate driver circuits for the GaN transistor in the Class-E amplifier, and a transmission coil. The rechargeable battery serves as the system’s energy source. Depending on the required battery life and weight considerations, the battery may be placed at another location to ensure that the external unit worn over the head remains lightweight. The PMU provides multiple DC rails for other components in the external unit and manages the charging and discharging of the battery.

The MCU acts as the control centre of the system. It provides control signals to the DC-DC booster to dynamically adjust the transmission power and generates a pulse-width modulation (PWM) signal for the driver circuits used in inductive power transmission. Instead of using high-frequency signal generation circuits, we also use this MCU to generate the PWM signal as it reduces the complexity and offers an easy way to adjust the signal frequency and duty cycle. The distance between the transmission and receiving coils in near-field inductive coupling power transmission affects the resonant frequency. For a typical inductive coupling system, we can add a tuning capacitor to improve the efficiency, but for our proposed system, the receiving coil is in the passive relay and cannot be fine-tuned to match variations in coil distance. Therefore, we equipped the external unit with the ability to adjust the frequency on the fly to maximize power transfer efficiency. Utilizing this MCU-based design, we can achieve high efficiency by dynamically fine-tuning the waveform and frequency and reducing the external unit’s size and weight.

The DC-DC booster provides the power amplifier’s boosted DC voltage, with the MCU’s voltage level controlled. This design could enable the system to dynamically adjust the output power to meet the requirements of the implant. The driver circuits receive the PWM signal from the MCU and match the impedance and voltage levels to drive the power amplifier. The power amplifier is a Class-E amplifier consisting of a pair of GaN transistors to ensure high efficiency at the desired frequency. The subcutaneous relay contains no active components and can be manufactured using a single metal film layer. This relay serves as an energy conduit, converting inductive coupling to capacitive coupling to allow typical stents to receive power. On the implant side, the two sections of the stent serve as the capacitive poles. Simple rectifier circuits with ultra-low-capacitance Schottky diodes convert the AC to DC and supply it to a buck-boost converter that provides regulated DC power for the sensing or stimulation circuits.

This design achieves high-efficiency power transfer with simplified circuits, making the external unit small and lightweight. With the current manufacturing ability of integrated circuits, all components in this design for the implant part can be miniaturized to fit within the stent without affecting blood flow, enabling a fully functional endoECoG device without a lead wire.

Experiment setup

To validate the design and test the performance of our WPT system, we built a testing platform using several sections of human coronary stents (SYNERGYTM XD from Boston Scientific), a section of bovine bone, and a section of bovine vessel. The bovine tissues were freshly purchased from a local butcher and replaced every 24 h to maintain their water content and freshness. We 3D-printed a container, see Fig. 4c, equipped with fixtures to hold the bone tissue securely and control the stent’s distance and position. The container was filled with saline solution to simulate the dura and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) properties. The first step of the experiment involved determining the characteristics of the power transfer system from the relay to the stent. This step allowed us to identify the optimal frequency and the optimal width and length of the capacitive plates to match specific lengths of the stent at certain distances for efficient capacitive coupling power delivery. Based on the results from this initial step, the second step was to verify the performance of the entire power transfer system using the optimal parameters.

In Experiment Step One, we used 50 μm-thick copper film as the capacitive plates. We cut the film to the designed dimensions (shown as length Lcp and width Wcp in Fig. 4) and soldered a 50 Ω coaxial cable to each pair of film plates. We then coated one side of the film with an electronics-grade silicone adhesive (Chip Quik EGS10C) and attached the plates to the bone. The silicone adhesive ensured good and stable contact between the plates and the bone, provided electrical insulation, and maintained stable dielectric properties, simulating the silicone coating in our real-world application. We also tested the optimal gap distance (shown as Dpgp in Fig. 4) between the plates during the experiment. The coaxial cables were connected to impedance-matching networks to match the impedance to 50 Ω, then connected to port A of the VNA (Rohde & Schwarz ZNLE6).

We soldered coated gold wires separately to each section for the stent sections, with the other end of the short wire soldered to a 50 Ω coaxial cable. After soldering, we coated all the solder joints with epoxy to prevent shorting and strengthen the connections. The open ends of the coaxial cables were also sealed with epoxy to prevent saline from entering and altering their properties. These coaxial cables were connected to impedance-matching networks to match the impedance, then connected to port B of the VNA. As we plan to do a board band measurement, a single impedance-matching network cannot match the impedance to 50 Ω for all frequencies, we used four different groups of matching networks for different frequency bands 1–20 MHz, 20–200 MHz, 200 MHz–2 GHz, and 2–6 GHz to improve the measurement accuracy.

After preparation, we inserted the stent into the bovine vessel and placed it into our 3D-printed container filled with saline. The container featured marker points and fixtures to ensure proper alignment and control of the stent’s position. We recorded the VNA readings at different stent positions. We repeated these steps for each stent length to determine the optimal parameters for different stent lengths; the detailed testing procedure is described in the testing protocol section.

In Experiment Step Two, we selected the plate size and gap distance that demonstrated good performance based on the results from Step One. We then connected the coaxial cable attached to the copper film to a flat coil with an inductance close to the calculated optimal value. This configuration simulated the entire relay and implant part of the proposed system.

Before constructing the experimental testing, we performed a systematic three-stage optimisation of the receiving and transmitting coils to ensure that the subsequent tests would be meaningful. First, we designed the receiving coil that resides within the subcutaneous relay. This component has to fit into the narrow cavity created by the capacitive plates, so its diameter and the spacing between its turns were varied to achieve resonance at the operating frequency without exceeding the available space. By iterating the number of turns and the inter-turn gaps, we arrived at an inductance and capacitance combination that maximised coupling efficiency under these geometric constraints. With the receiving coil fixed, we turned to the transmitting coil outside the body. We wanted its magnetic field to be tightly concentrated on the receiving coil, so we explored different inner and outer diameters, non-uniform spacing between turns (closer spacing at the centre and wider spacing near the edges), and multilayer arrangements. The addition of a ring-shaped ferrite sheet helped to further confine the magnetic field and reduce eddy current losses in the surrounding metal. Finally, to maximise power transfer into the load, we tuned a series compensation capacitor connected to the transmitter so that the coil would resonate at the desired frequency and present an impedance matched to the driver circuit. This three-stage process provided a well-behaved link for the subsequent experimental validation.

For this proof-of-concept demonstration, we did not fabricate the relay in its final miniaturized form. Instead, to allow rapid adjustments during testing, the inductive coils were realized as flat spiral traces on FR-4 printed circuit boards and the capacitive plates were cut from copper film and coated with silicone adhesive. These modular components could be assembled and reconfigured easily, which was essential for exploring different parameter sets. However, they also introduced mechanical constraints: the bovine bone sample used for capacitive coupling tests has a curved surface and sits in a saline-filled compartment, making it impractical to mount a rigid PCB coil directly behind the plates. To minimize losses, both the coils and the plates were wired using coaxial connectors and cables, which increase the overall bulk of the assembly. Consequently, the inductive and capacitive links could not occupy the same physical location inside a single tissue model. In our experimental arrangemen,t the inductive link rested horizontally on a separate soft-tissue phantom while the capacitive link was mounted on the bone, and the two were connected with short lengths of coaxial cable. Although such separation omits some possible interactions between the two coupling mechanisms, any interference is likely to be small because the diameter of the inductive coil is already smaller than the gap between the capacitive plates Dpgp. The transmitting coil used in this work will also be refined in future designs to further suppress residual coupling. Despite these practical limitations, the prototype clearly demonstrates the principle of relay-based wireless power transfer and provides a valid basis for evaluating overall efficiency.

For the external part, we used an MCU development board (NXP KV5x) to generate the desired PWM signal. We manufactured a custom PCB featuring a gate driver (LMG1020) and a Class-E amplifier with a GaN transistor (EPC2019). The output of the amplifier was connected to a flat PCB coil serving as the transmission coil.

On the implant side, the receiving coil was connected via a coaxial cable to a bridge rectifier formed by four low-capacitance Schottky diodes (NSR01F30). A programmable current source (Stanford Research Systems CS580) was used to emulate the electrical load and to measure the received power. The input power to the wireless power transfer system was supplied by a DC power supply (Rigol DP832) driving the Class-E power amplifier. The power supply was operated in constant-voltage mode at a fixed DC voltage, and the corresponding RMS input current reported by the supply was recorded to determine the input power.

The current source was operated as a variable load to identify the maximum received power under each experimental configuration. A computer-controlled multifunction instrument (Digilent Analog Discovery 2, AD2) was used to generate a ramped control voltage that set the output current of the CS580 to a known value. The output voltage was simultaneously read from the monitoring port of the current source. The received power was calculated as the product of the programmed load current and the measured output voltage. The maximum received power was then used to calculate the power transfer efficiency using Eq. (8).

Accurate characterization of power transfer requires careful control of the measurement setup. In all experiments, devices under test were connected using high-quality, shielded coaxial cables and connectors to minimize electromagnetic interference and unintended coupling. A full two-port calibration of the vector network analyser was performed at the cable ends using a standard calibration kit, thereby removing the effects of cable and connector losses, reflections, and phase delays from the measured data.

Particular attention was paid to cable routing. Cables were kept as short as practicable, arranged along reproducible paths, and positioned away from the inductive coils and capacitive plates to prevent the formation of unintended loops that could act as parasitic antennas. All instruments and fixtures were properly grounded to eliminate ground loops and to reduce measurement noise. These precautions ensure that the measured scattering parameters and power transfer characteristics reflect the intrinsic behaviour of the wireless power transfer system, rather than artifacts introduced by the measurement cabling.

In our testing platform, we used off-the-shelf products that met our requirements but included some features which did not accurately reflect real-world applications. So, for the system power transmission efficiency calculation, we excluded the power consumption of the MCU and the gate driver circuits and listed the results separately.

Testing protocol

Figure 5 illustrates our testing procedure, experimental steps, and the methods used for recording results. The testing procedure begins with the selection of stent dimensions. For the application of an endoECoG device, the ideal position for the stent is the SSS, the largest vessel in the brain area. When we position the stent close to the visual cortex and motor cortex regions, the stent length can range from 30 mm to 80 mm, and the diameter is ~5–8 mm. In our experiments, we used a coronary stent as the material, with a 5.75 mm diameter close to the vessel diameter of the intended application site. For stent length, we tested sections of 10 mm and 30 mm. We tested distances of 1 mm, 3 mm, 5 mm, 10 mm, 15 mm, and 20 mm for the gap length to determine the optimal performance distance. The total length of the stent area is calculated as (stent section size ×2) plus the gap distance, resulting in tested total lengths ranging from 21 mm to 80 mm, covering the intended application site.

Flowchart illustrating the testing procedure and parameters evaluated during the experimental phase.

The testing variables were analysed to determine the optimal transmission parameters. The green numbers next to each variable represent the number of tests conducted for that specific variable. As the complex of the combinations, a total of 344 experiments were performed.

As shown in Fig. 5, we have several variables in our experiment: two different stent lengths (Lst = 10 mm and 30 mm), eight capacitive plate sizes (Lcp and Wcp), six gap distances between the two capacitive plates (Dpgp), eight distances from the stent to the bone (Dt), six gap distances between the two sections of the stent (Dsgp), and frequencies from 1 MHz to 6 GHz. We designed the procedure and protocol to find the optimal parameters for this proposed power transfer system based on the theoretical modelling shown in Fig. 3. To ensure the accuracy of the VNA measurements, impedance matching is performed for the specified frequency bands (1–20 MHz, 20–200 MHz, 200 MHz–2 GHz, and 2–6 GHz) (shown in Fig. 5).

First, we separately perform a one-port VNA measurement for the stents and capacitive plate. From this step, we obtain rough readings to estimate the impedance and then adjust the matching network to bring it as close to 50 Ω as possible at each band’s central frequency. For this step, we only consider the plate size and the gap distance between the two plates on the capacitive plate side. For the stent side, we consider the length of the stent and the gap between the stent sections. Other variables are ignored in this step to simplify the testing.

After this step, we fix the inductance and capacitance values in the matching network for each frequency band and for each stent and capacitive plate size, preparing for the next two-port measurement step. During the two-port measurement, each frequency band and each combination of stent and plate size has a matching network configured to ensure the device under test’s (DUT’s) impedance is close to 50 Ω.

System-level transmission measurements were performed using the optimal parameters identified in the preceding characterization steps. No additional matching networks were connected to the transmitter or receiver during these tests. On the transmitter side, impedance matching was achieved at the output of the Class-E power amplifier. On the receiver side, a programmable current source was used to emulate a variable electrical load and to identify the operating point corresponding to maximum received power.

To characterize performance over a range of input power levels, the DC supply voltage driving the Class-E amplifier was incrementally increased in steps of 0.5 V. Because the amplifier operates from a fixed supply rail, increasing the supply voltage results in a corresponding increase in RF output power delivered to the wireless link. Using this approach, the input power was swept from ~200 mW to 1 W. At each supply voltage step, the load current was adjusted to obtain the peak received power, which was recorded and used to calculate the power transfer efficiency.

This work uses capacitive coupling to deliver power through bone to a stent-based receiver, including bone tissue in the testing. Previous works using capacitive coupling for power delivery to deep implants have used large plates as receivers or only performed modelling without progressing to bone tissue experiments. Testing different stent lengths and matching transmitter capacitive plate lengths and widths provides insight into capacitive coupling with bone tissue in between. The recorded data can be used for this capacitive power transfer system and aid in capacitive data communication or other transcranial power delivery or data communication systems. Following this experiment procedure can ensure the accuracy of the measurement and record correct data.

Finite element model and analysis

All FEM-based modelling and simulations in this study were performed using Sim4Life (Version 8.0, ZMT Zurich MedTech AG), which provides a multi-physics platform for anatomically realistic electromagnetic and thermal analyses. The simulation theory provided by the software is described in the Supplementary Appendix D that explains the details of the computational methods, governing equations, and parameter definitions used in the FEM model.

To test the system’s performance under a more controllable environment and using a realistic skull and scalp structure, we designed the proposed system model and performed finite element simulations and analyses to verify the bench-top testing results. Figure 6 shows the setup of the finite element analysis. The human head model was purchased from the Population Head Model (PHM) Repository (10.13099/ViP-PHM-V1.0), a collection of 50 de-identified head models including skin, skull, grey matter (GM), white matter (WM), CSF, cerebellum, and ventricles. We chose a head with average dimensions as the simulation model. We placed our relay model in the middle of the head above the skull. The relay model has the same dimensions as the one in our full-system efficiency benchtop experiments, allowing us to verify the experimental and simulation results.

Transmission efficiency finite element analysis and safety verification setup.

The bottom left corner shows the head model we used for the simulation and the position of our system. The main graph shows the dimensions of the external coil, relay, and stents.

The capacitive plate size in the simulation is 10 × 12 mm2, and the gap between the two plates is 15 mm. The coil in the relay has an outer diameter of 11 mm, 5.5 turns, a pitch of 0.4 mm, and a line width of 0.3 mm. Considering a parasitic capacitance of 0.9 pF and a 9 pF capacitance associated with the connecting plates, this coil is optimized for a 40–50 MHz inductive link without an extra tuning capacitor. For the simulation, we chose gold as the material for the relay and embedded it in a 0.4 mm thick silicone film. We modelled two stent sections with a diameter of 5 mm and a section length of 10 mm, and the gap between the two sections is 15 mm. We set the stent’s material to platinum-iridium, a typical material for an endovascular stent.

In our experimental measurements, we used a commercially available human coronary stent (SYNERGYTM XD from Boston Scientific) so that the material properties and mechanical structure reflect real-world clinical devices. However, intellectual property restrictions and the lack of a vectorised CAD model prevented us from using the exact commercial geometry in simulations. Instead, we constructed a stent-like model with the same diameter, length, material composition, and overall metal density as the experimental stent. The simulated stent uses a double helical pattern that matches the metal coverage ratio of the commercial device while avoiding the formation of a continuous conductive loop that could behave like a large coil. Although the mechanical structure differs, this surrogate ensures that the electromagnetic behaviour, coupling, shielding and SAR characteristics remain consistent with the experimental stent.

The transmitter coil has an outer diameter of 14 mm, 6 turns, a pitch of 0.5 mm, and a line width of 0.4 mm. The material for the external coil is silver to minimize resistance.

As this simulation involves complex structures with small curved parts, we manually set up an adaptive grid to maintain the fine structure of our simulation object while balancing simulation time and result detail. We used a 3 × 3 mm2 grid as the global mesh grid and then set up a 100 mm (length) × 80mm (width) × 100mm (depth) region of interest (ROI) box with a 1 × 1 mm2 grid for this region. We set a grid with 0.02 mm resolution for the relay to ensure all delicate structures are captured. For the silicone film, we intentionally increased the layer thickness from 0.05 mm to 0.4 mm to reduce the requirement of a fine grid for the curved surface. Then, we set a 0.04 mm grid for the stent film. For the external coil, we used a 0.05 mm grid. In total, we have 194 million cells for this simulation.

The receiving power is measured through an edge sensor across the two sections of the stent. We recorded frequency and time domain data for analysis for field and edge sensors.

Safety

To verify the safety of this power transfer system in practice, we conducted a specific absorption rate (SAR) analysis using finite element simulation results. We performed a temperature rise simulation using Sim4Life software. We extracted the SAR values according to IEEE/IEC 62704-1 for each tissue type and obtained volumetric SAR values in the head area for the SAR analysis31. Both peak spatial SAR and mass-averaged SAR (10 g) were extracted for analysis. We set a unified 1 × 1 mm2 grid for the temperature analysis and used the SAR analysis’s electromagnetic (EM) field as the heating source. We set the initial thermal conditions for the skin, skull, and subcutaneous relay to 37 ∘C, and for the tissue below the skull to 38.5 ∘C, which is the average internal temperature of a healthy person. The environmental temperature was set to 25 ∘C. We configured the simulation to allow the tissue to reach a steady-state temperature equilibrium or run for a maximum of 14,400 s. We recorded the temperature changes in the grid at 30-s intervals.

Results

To test and verify this proposed WPT system’s feasibility, performance and safety, we conducted four stages of experiments, including relay-to-stent capacitive WPT efficiency testing, whole system WPT power budget and efficiency testing, finite element simulation verification and safety simulation. The experiments setup and testing protocols are detailed in Section “Method”, and the main results are described here.

Relay to stent capacitive WPT efficiency

Figure 7 shows the relay to stent capacitive WPT efficiency testing results using the setup described in Section “Experiment setup”. Figure 7a shows the optimal parameters for a 10 mm stent section length at different depths (Dt). From the results, we observe that for capacitive coupling WPT through bone, two frequency windows can transmit power from the relay to the stent with higher efficiency: one window between 34 MHz and 47 MHz and another window around 190–700 MHz. When the stent depth is Dt = 1 mm, we achieve a peak efficiency of 20.86% with a stent section gap of Lsgp = 15 mm, capacitive relay plate size of Lcp × Wcp = 10 × 12 mm2, plate gap distance Lcgp = 15 mm, at a frequency of 46.56 MHz. For other stent implantation depths, the optimal plate size, plate gap, and stent gap are shown in Fig. 7.

The results suggest that when the stent depth is less than 10 mm, the plate gap should equal the stent gap, and the gap distance should be longer than the stent section length. On the other hand, when the stent implantation depth is greater, a larger capacitive plate is needed to achieve higher efficiency. For deeper implanted stents, we need a larger gap at the stent side and a smaller gap at the plate side. When the stent depth reaches 30 mm, the peak efficiency drops to 12.37% with a much larger plate (40 × 18 mm2) and a stent gap distance of 20 mm. Although our results suggest that further increasing the plate size may help the system achieve higher efficiency at this stent implantation depth, doing so would make the implantation of the relay more challenging for the surgical procedure and may not be practical via minimally invasive methods in clinical settings. Therefore, in our experiment, we limited the maximum plate size to 40 × 20 mm2.

Figure 7b shows the optimal design parameters for a 30 mm stent section length at different depths (Dt). Compared to the 10 mm stent sections, the 30 mm stent sections exhibit lower efficiency. When the stent depth is 1 mm, the maximum efficiency is 7.9% with a plate size of 30 × 18 mm2, plate gap of 5 mm, and stent gap of 10 mm. The peak efficiency is achieved at 35.81 MHz. We also observe two high-efficiency windows for these longer stent sections: the first window around 36 MHz and the second between ~220 MHz and 400 MHz. The average efficiency is much lower than the 10 mm stent sections, but the parameter variance is smaller. When the stent depth increases from 1 mm to 30 mm, the efficiency only drops to 7.47%. When the stent section length is 30 mm, the plate sizes are close to our set maximum size, and when the depth is 30 mm, a smaller plate can achieve high efficiency. For the 30 mm section length, the peak efficiency frequency is in the higher frequency window, but we prefer to use the lower frequency window for our proposed system. The reasons we chose the lower frequency window are that for endoECoG, the stent is in the SSS, and the depth usually is less than 2 mm. When the depth is shallow, the peak in the higher frequency window is very close to the peak in the lower frequency window. Additionally, waveform-generating circuits and power amplifier circuits have higher efficiency for our proposed system when operating at frequencies below 100 MHz.

Figure 7c shows the efficiency change when the stent depth is fixed at 1 mm, illustrating the change of stent length and the gap with varying capacitive plate size and gap. From the results, we observe that when the stent section length is 10 mm, the best efficiency is achieved when the stent gap equals the capacitive plate gap. When the stent gap becomes shorter, the maximum efficiency drops substantially, but the maximum efficiency frequency remains similar. Suppose the stent gap is kept unchanged and the capacitive plate gap is shortened. In that case, the maximum efficiency drops less than when changing the stent gap, but the maximum efficiency frequency decreases. If we keep the capacitive plate gap unchanged but increase the capacitive plate size, we find that the maximum efficiency is close to the maximum efficiency with a similar plate gap but different plate sizes; moreover, a larger plate size compared to the stent size results in lower efficiency.

For a 30 mm stent section length, similar to the 10 mm stent section length, the efficiency is high when the capacitive plate length equals the stent section length. Unlike the 10 mm stent, the efficiency for the 30 mm stent section drops less when the gap distance changes, but when the plate size becomes smaller, the efficiency drops sharply with the frequency shifting to higher frequencies. From the results, we can estimate that when using a 30 mm stent section, if we further increase the stent section gap and the plate gap, we may achieve higher efficiency, but this would exceed the dimensions of our testing platform and a larger plate may not be practical in clinical applications.

The relay-to-stent capacitive coupling power transfer results indicate that the capacitive plate in the relay should have a length equal to the length of the stent section and a width about 2–3 times the diameter of the stent. The gap distance of the stent section should be greater than 10 mm, and longer stent sections do not increase power transfer efficiency.

discussion

This work presents a system that can transfer power directly to any metal-based, commercially available medical stent without the need for an extra receiving transceiver. The proposed system can deliver over 45 mW of power without reaching safety limits, sufficient to power an endoECoG device and multiple biosignal sensing devices. To our knowledge, this system may achieve the highest WPT efficiency for delivering power into a standard stent in the brain area, providing the first proof that a fully wireless endoECoG is feasible in the near future. Incorporating a subcutaneous relay in the proposed system effectively addresses the challenge of low efficiency in WPT to stent-based bioelectronic devices. The subcutaneous relay enhances power transfer efficiency while maintaining a minimally invasive operation. Moreover, since the external device does not require close contact with the skin, the system is suitable for long-term applications, improving patient comfort and compliance.

Our experimental results, obtained using real skin, bone, and vessel tissues, closely match the detailed simulations, validating the accuracy of our models and demonstrating the practicality of further developing this technology. This high degree of correlation between experimental and simulation results underscores the reliability of our approach. In practical applications, the alignment between the relay and the stent can be achieved using medical imaging techniques before and during the surgical procedure, ensuring precise placement. A pair of magnets facilitates the alignment between the external device and the relay. Notably, the proposed system would likely be MRI-compatible. The stent is a standard device already proven safe for MRI, and the relay is a passive device made of non-magnetized material. When the alignment magnets are temporarily removed, the device can undergo MRI scanning without issues, ensuring patients can still benefit from MRI diagnostics.

Based on our research, no stent-based power transfer system is designed for use in the brain area that has been tested in vivo. One reason for this is the low practicality of previous works. For example, the study33 achieves over 15% efficiency but requires a 20 × 60 mm2 plate attached to the side of the implant, which is not feasible in the brain area without damaging surrounding tissue. The work24 reports 35% AC-AC efficiency; however, the stent used in this study is essentially a metal tube without a stent structure. In practice, implanting such a long tube into the vessels in the brain area is impossible. The research32 is designed to use a typical stent to receive power, but bone tissue was not considered in the design and experiments, and an efficiency of 2.6% is too low for real-world applications. Inductive links generally offer higher efficiency, but all the works listed here utilize specially designed stents to function as antennas.

A key consideration in our design is the trade-off between surgical practicality and power transfer efficiency. The sizes of the capacitive plates and the spacing between them (Dpgp) were carefully optimised to balance these objectives. While increasing the plate area and widening the gap can improve capacitive coupling and raise overall efficiency, larger plates introduce practical challenges such as difficulty in subcutaneous placement, conformation to curved anatomical surfaces, and greater tissue coverage, which may lead to increased attenuation or non-uniform field distribution. They may also raise regulatory and safety concerns due to elevated local SAR. The final plate dimensions selected in this work, therefore, represent a compromise: they deliver sufficient power for the implant while remaining surgically feasible and within safety limits.

From a mechanistic perspective, while the observed performance is consistent with the relay facilitating a transition between inductive coupling at the external interface and capacitive coupling at the intravascular receiver, the electromagnetic interactions in the vicinity of the relay are likely more complex. In particular, the close spatial arrangement of the inductive coil and capacitive plates may result in intertwined or hybrid coupling mechanisms, in which inductive and capacitive field components coexist and interact. Additional parasitic capacitances, displacement currents, and near-field interactions within biological tissues may also contribute to the effective power transfer. As a result, multiple coupling pathways may play a role in the observed system behaviour, and further theoretical and experimental investigation is required to fully disentangle and quantify these mechanisms.

Despite the promising advantages of the proposed system, several challenges remain unaddressed. Firstly, manufacturing the relay may present difficulties. Our goal is to make the insulation layer on the bone-facing side as thin as possible, with the total relay thickness kept below 0.5 mm. However, the mechanical properties of such an ultrathin structure may complicate fabrication and increase the risk of damage during surgical implantation. Secondly, although our system demonstrates the highest reported efficiency of over 10%, it remains relatively low compared to wired systems. These challenges also arise from the strict size constraints: the optimal position for the inductive link coil is at the centre between the two capacitive plates, but the limited space restricts the coil dimensions and introduces additional complexity in balancing the loading effects caused by interference between the two links. In this work, we have carefully optimized all parameters to maximize efficiency while maintaining a practical device size. The lower efficiency affects the overall system performance, particularly with respect to the power requirements of the external unit. For long-term use, achieving a balance between battery life and the weight of the external device will be challenging. For instance, assuming an endoECoG device continuously consumes 15 mW, operating it for more than 24 h would require a battery with a capacity exceeding 15 Wh on the external side, weighing ~100 g with current battery technology. Such a configuration could impact the user’s long-term comfort and willingness to wear the device.

From a mechanistic perspective, while the observed performance is consistent with the relay facilitating a transition between inductive coupling at the external interface and capacitive coupling at the intravascular receiver, the electromagnetic interactions in the vicinity of the relay are likely more complex. In particular, the close spatial arrangement of the inductive coil and capacitive plates may result in intertwined or hybrid coupling mechanisms, in which inductive and capacitive field components coexist and interact. Additional parasitic capacitances, displacement currents, and near-field interactions within biological tissues may also contribute to the effective power transfer. As a result, multiple coupling pathways or other coupling mechanisms may play a role in the observed system behaviour, and further theoretical and experimental investigation is required to fully disentangle and quantify these mechanisms. Importantly, the presence of such hybrid coupling effects does not alter the practical performance metrics reported in this work, which are experimentally validated under realistic anatomical conditions.

Future work should focus on addressing both the engineering and theoretical challenges identified in this study. Improving the manufacturing process of the relay, possibly through the development of new materials or fabrication techniques, could enhance mechanical robustness while maintaining the desired thinness. Additionally, further optimizing the system’s efficiency, perhaps through circuit design improvements or advanced materials, could reduce power requirements and consequently decrease battery size and weight. Exploring energy-efficient operational modes or duty cycling could also help mitigate battery constraints. In addition, we plan to fabricate the fully integrated thin-film relay and evaluate the system in live animal models. Testing in vivo will allow us to assess the mechanical robustness and biological response of the implant, and to refine the design for long-term operation in a physiological environment. Overall, this work could provide a foundation for the development of fully wireless, minimally invasive endovascular neuroprosthetic systems with potential for clinical impact.

Conclusion

This work presented a wireless power transfer (WPT) system capable of delivering power directly to a standard medical stent in the brain area without modifying its structure. The proposed system utilizes a subcutaneous relay to convert inductive coupling to capacitive coupling, effectively overcoming challenges associated with power transfer efficiency and biocompatibility in stent-based biomedical implants. Our system achieved over 45 mW of power delivery without reaching safety limits, sufficient to power an endoECoG device and multiple biosignal sensing devices.

Experimental results obtained using real skin, bone, and vessel tissues closely matched our detailed simulations, validating our models’ accuracy and demonstrating our approach’s practicality. The system maintains minimal invasiveness and does not require close contact with the skin, making it suitable for long-term applications while improving patient comfort and compliance.

While the observed performance is consistent with an interpretation in which the subcutaneous relay facilitates efficient coupling between inductive and capacitive elements, the electromagnetic interactions underlying the power transfer are likely to involve multiple, potentially intertwined coupling mechanisms. Further theoretical and experimental investigations are therefore required to fully establish a comprehensive mechanistic understanding of the system’s operation.

Compared with existing approaches, the proposed system achieves the highest reported WPT efficiency for powering a standard medical stent without the use of additional intravascular transceivers or specially designed stents. This work demonstrates the feasibility of efficiently and safely powering stent-based brain implants and represents an important step toward fully wireless, minimally invasive endovascular neuroprosthetic systems. By combining robust experimental performance with a practical implantation strategy, this study could provide a foundation for future research and clinical translation in wireless neurotechnology and implantable biosensing.
