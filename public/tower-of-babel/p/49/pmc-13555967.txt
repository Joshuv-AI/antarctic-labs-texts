Catastrophic health expenditure associated to bellwether surgery care in Northcentral Ethiopia
Authors: Atalel Fentahun Awedew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBellwether procedures—caesarean section, laparotomy, and open fracture management- are core indicators for monitoring safe, equitable, and affordable surgical care in a country. Although effective surgical care is essential for achieving universal health coverage and the Sustainable Development Goals, spending on surgical care leading catastrophic health expenditure among surgical patients, particularly low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs). This study assesses catastrophic health expenditure (CHE) associated with bellwether surgery to quantify financial risk and inform future equitable health financing reforms.MethodsA prospective institutional-based observational cohort study was conducted at Debre Tabor Specialized Hospital, South Gondar. Using systematic random sampling, 425 patients undergoing bellwether procedures were enrolled. Catastrophic expenditures assessed with the validated Financial Risk Protection tool developed by the Harvard Program in Global Surgery and Social Change. Categorical variables were compared using χ² tests, while continuous data were evaluated for normality with the Shapiro–Wilk test and histograms. Means (± SD) and medians (± IQR) were reported accordingly. Bivariable and multivariable binary logistic regression identified predictors, with adjusted odds ratios (95% CI); p < 0.05 to declare statistical significance.ResultsA total of 425 patients undergoing bellwether procedures were included, representing all woredas and city administrations of South Gondar, Northwest Ethiopia. The median age was 36 years (IQR 24–52), and the majority were classified as ASA I/II. Emergency laparotomy accounted for the largest proportion of procedures (55.8%). Within the three-delay framework, the median duration of Delay 1 (care-seeking) was 12.0 h (IQR 6–24). The overall postoperative complication rate was 21.3%, while in-hospital mortality was 0.9%. At the 10% threshold, catastrophic health expenditure (CHE) was observed in 76.0% of households lacking community-based insurance or free health initiatives. CHE at this threshold was significantly associated with referral delay (Delay 2; adjusted odds ratio [AOR] 1.06, 95% CI 1.01–1.10 per hour), emergency bone fixation (AOR 22.9, 95% CI 7.4–70.4), and emergency laparotomy (AOR 3.3, 95% CI 1.6–6.9).ConclusionApproximately two-thirds of patients undergoing bellwether surgery experienced catastrophic health expenditure, declined to one-third among those enrolled in community-based or family health insurance. Catastrophic health expenditure was greatest with delayed presentation, rural residence, long travel distance, and prolonged hospital stay. Expanding insurance coverage, improving access, and integrating surgical services into primary health care are essential to achieve safe, timely, and affordable surgery in line with global targets.

Introduction

Bellwether surgical procedures -emergency laparotomy, caesarean delivery, and open bone fixation [1]- core indicator of surgical care preparedness of country capacity. Bellwether surgical conditions have accounted a sizable portion of global morbidity, mortality, economic loss and disability worldwide, disproportionately affecting low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [2]. Surgical health care has been neglected from global health agenda for decades [3] despite surgical amenable conditions account for an estimated 28–32% of the global disease burden, with bellwether conditions representing a substantial proportion [4].

Following the adoption of World Health Assembly resolution WHA 68.15 in 2015 and the publication of the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery (LCoGS) [4], global surgical care has gained increased visibility as a global public health priority, particularly in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs). These initiatives have catalyzed progress through advocacy, the development of surgical system indicators, policy formulation, and capacity building. The Lancet commission on Global surgery proposed six core indicators to measure safe, affordable and equitable of surgical care by 2030: perioperative mortality, protection against catastrophic expenditure, protection against impoverishment, surgical workforce, and bellwether 2 h accessibility [4]. The 68th World Health Assembly (WHA) approved a resolution WHA68.15 [5], formally recognizing emergency and essential surgical care and anesthesia as a component of universal health coverage (UHC).

Financial protection in surgical care has emerged as a global and national priority, recognized as a core pillar to monitor safe, equitable and affordable surgical care [4]. The Lancet Commission on Global Surgery set a benchmark that by 2030, no individual should experience catastrophic health expenditure or be pushed into poverty as a result of undergoing surgery [4]. Yet, this vision remains far from reality in many low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Ethiopia, where patients continue to face substantial out-of-pocket (OOP) payments for essential surgical services. Globally, an estimated 3.7 billion people are at risk of financial catastrophe if surgical care is needed, and approximately 81 million individuals incur catastrophic expenditures annually due to surgical costs, with the burden concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia [6]. Current estimates suggest that between 31% and 57% of the global population is vulnerable to impoverishing expenditure from surgical care, underscoring the urgent need for financial risk protection in health systems worldwide [6].

The 68th World Health Assembly (WHA) resolution WHA68.15 marked a pivotal moment in global surgical health policy, as governments worldwide acknowledged the role of surgical and anaesthetic as component UHC and to achieve SDGs) [5]. It is a high-level government leader’s commitment to address longstanding gaps in inequitable access, safe, affordable, and timely surgical care through a coordinated and integrated approach. Following global movement and initiatives on global surgery, the government of Ethiopia has taken a noble step to strength its national surgical health system and delivery policy. The ministry of health of Ethiopia launched the ‘’Save Life through Safe Surgery’’ initiatives - SaLTS I and SaLTS II—aimed at improving access to safe, affordable, and timely surgical services by 2025 [7].

Surgical health care financing for bellwether surgery in Ethiopia comprises three mechanisms: community-based insurance (CBI), free health initiatives (FHI), and out-of-pocket payment. Community based insurance is a voluntary scheme available to all citizens, with an annual premium set by the government [8]. The free health initiative is a government programme providing free care exclusively to pregnant women. Coverage is limited to specific services, including investigations, medications, caesarean section, and anaesthesia fees; but the beneficiaries of this initiative are ineligible for CBI. Out-of-pocket payment applies to citizens who are not enrolled in CBI and are not eligible for FHI, who must pay directly for surgical services.

Despite a growing global and national recognitions of surgical care as a core components UHC and effective intervention to achieve the SDGs, significant evidence gaps remain in the financial protection in relation to bellwether procedures in Ethiopia. This study aims to generate evidences on financial risk protection and associated factors after bellwether surgery and inform future equitable surgical health finance in the stud area,

Methods

Study area, design and period

We conducted a prospective cohort study at Debre Tabor Specialized Hospital in South Gondar Zone, Ethiopia, where bellwether surgical procedures are routinely performed. The study period spanned from September 1, 2025, to February 28, 2026. All patients aged 14 years and above who were admitted to the surgical ward following emergency bellwether procedures—caesarean section, laparotomy, or open fracture management—were eligible for inclusion. Patients who were lost to follow-up during the study period were excluded from the final analysis.

Sampling size and technique

The sample size to assess the catastrophic health expenditure was determined by a single proportion formula with the assumption that the proportion of financial risks of catastrophic health expenditure after bellwether surgery in Ethiopia is unknown (p = 50%), a margin of error to be 5%, a 95% confidence interval, and considering a 10% non-response rate, resulting in a maximum sample size become 422 patients.

Data collection tool and procedure

The study used the validated Financial Risk Protection (FRP) tool developed by the Harvard Program in Global Surgery and Social Change for data collection. Data collection was done by trained medical interns and Nurses (each will be assigned to general surgery, obstetrics, and orthopedics). Out-of-pocket expenditure for medical care (admission, procedure, diagnostic, and consultation fees) and non-medical expenses (travel and food expenses) for all patients who underwent bellwether surgery was collected. Data were collected until the 30th day after discharge. A local language prepared notebook was given to every patient to record daily medical and non-medical expenses to avoid recall biases. Catastrophic health expenditure considered when treatment expenses surpass 10% of annual expenditure.

Data processing and analysis

Upon completion of data collection, we reviewed each questionnaire for the completeness and consistency. Data was analyzed using SPSS version 25 software.

Categorical variables presented using frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons for categorical variables were conducted using the chi-square test. The normality of the continuous data checked using Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of histogram. The mean ± SD was reported for normal distributed variables while median ± IQR was reported for skewed data.

The multi-collinearity test was performed before multivariable binary logistics regression to evaluate potential correlations between categorical, continuous, and binary variables. Multicollinearity assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values. Variables exhibiting a VIF below 5 and a tolerance above 0.1 were considered sufficiently independent and were subsequently included in the multivariable binary logistic regression analysis. Conversely, variables with a VIF score ranging from 5 to 10 or higher, and a tolerance value below 0.1, will be excluded from the final model to avoid issues arising from multicollinearity.

To identify potential predictors, a univariate binary logistic regression analysis initially performed to examine factors associated financial risks of catastrophic health expenditure after bellwether surgery. Variables demonstrating a p-value of 0.25 or less in the bivariable binary logistic regression analysis then entered into a multivariable binary logistic regression model. This model assessed the independent associations between the various factors and financial risks of catastrophic health expenditure after bellwether surgery.

The results of the logistic regression analysis will be reported as adjusted odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. A p-value less than 0.05 will be used as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

A total of 425 patients who underwent emergency bellwether procedures at Debre Tabor Specialized Hospital were included in the analysis. Participants were drawn from all woredas and city administrations across South Gondar Zone. The median age was 36 years (IQR 24–52). Just over half of the cohort (51.1%) were farmers, and the majority were married (82.4%). The median travel distance to reach the hospital was 58 km (IQR 30–91) (Table 1).

Factors associated with catastrophic health expenditure

Several factors were significantly associated with catastrophic health expenditure (CHE) at the 10% threshold following bellwether surgery. Increasing age was associated with higher risk (adjusted odds ratio AOR1.04, 95% CI 1.01–1.06 per year). Similarly, greater distance from the health facility was linked to increased CHE (AOR 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.02 per km). Delay 2 in the three-delay model was also significantly associated with CHE (AOR 1.06, 95% CI 1.01–1.10 per hour). The study found that emergency bone fixation was associated with markedly increased risk (AOR 22.9, 95% CI 7.4–70.4), while emergency laparotomy was also a significant factor (AOR 3.3, 95% CI 1.6–6.9). These findings highlight that both patient-level and system-level factors contribute to catastrophic health expenditure risk (Table 4).

Discussion

Our findings present a high burden of catastrophic health expenditure following bellwether surgical procedures, with rural residents, delayed presentation, long travel distance, open bone fixation, large dependent family size, and prolonged hospital stay each associated with greater financial risk. Enrolment of community-based insurance (CBI) or Free health insurance (FHI) was associated with a marked reduction in catastrophic expenditure, from roughly two-thirds of uninsured households to about one in three among the insured. These findings highlight that access to basic and essential surgical care remains financially precarious in this setting, disproportionately affecting patients who are already geographically and socioeconomically disadvantaged.

Financial risk protection is a central core to monitor safe, affordable and equitable surgical care in a country [4]. The Lancet Commission on Global Surgery sets an ambition target, no patient should face catastrophic health expenditure from accessing surgical care by 20,230 [4]. Despite this commitment, achieving these goals remains disproportionately challenging in in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where out-of-pocket payments continue to dominate health financing and insurance coverage remains limited. In this study, two-third households incurred catastrophic health expenditure following bellwether surgical procedures, a burden that declined to approximately one in three among household enrolled in CBI or FHI at 10% threshold. Although no prior study has specifically examined the financial risks following bellwether surgery, this study findings are broadly consistent with existing evidences [9, 10]. A study in Ethiopia reported a comparable magnitude of catastrophic health expenditure, assessing a board range of surgical procedure [9]. In contrast, another study reported a lower burden, with approximately one in four patients affected across all surgical procedure types [10]. Our findings showed lower rates in Malawi [11] and Madagascar [12], but substantially higher compared with some LMICs—exceeding 59% in Vietnam [13], 31% in Uganda [14], and 12% in Sierra Leone [15]. This variation likely reflects differences in procedure severity and the emergent nature of bellwether procedures, which-unlike elective surgery-often involve trauma, obstetric emergencies, or acute abdominal conditions requiring urgent, unplanned care that limits patients’ capacity to mitigate cost through advance planning.

Many factors contribute to the high incidence of catastrophic health exposure in after bellwether surgical procedures. In this study, each additional kilometer of distance from patients’ home was associated with a 1% increase in CHE risk while each hour of referral delay from first health care provider to treatment center increased the CHE by approximately 6%. This study findings consistent with previous study in Ethiopia, where travel long distances were associated with increased financial toxicity after surgery. These evidences a clearly highlights inequities in access to bellwether surgery. The Lancet Commission on Global Surgery set the benchmark that all patients should have access to essential surgery within two hours. Yet, this target is achieved in 94% of patients in high-income countries, compared with 47% in upper-middle-income countries, 22% in lower-middle-income countries, and only 18% in low-income countries [16]. Our findings underscore the urgent need to strengthen surgical systems by improving geographic accessibility and referral pathways to ensure safe, equitable, and affordable surgical care.

Caesarean section was associated with a lower risk of CHE compared with laparotomy and open bone fixation. These findings consistent with previous study in Ethiopia [9] and Uganda [14] which might be due to short duration of hospital stay after caesarean section. Despite the support of Free health initiatives (FHI) covering selected medications, investigations, and surgical and anaesthesia services, approximately 12% obstetric patients remained exposed to CHE at 10% threshold, underscoring gaps in financial protection. This finding is consistent with a free healthcare initiative that provided protection against catastrophic health expenditure for obstetric surgical care in Sierra Leone [15]. Nonetheless, the level of protection observed in this study is lower than that reported in Ghana, where 58–87% of insured patients faced catastrophic health expenditure for obstetrics surgical care [17], and in a previous Ethiopian study (57%) [9]. Despite this free healthcare initiative, it remains unclear why households enrolled in health insurance schemes continue to incur out-of-pocket expenses and catastrophic health expenditure. This may be explained by frequent stock-outs of medicines and medical supplies in government facilities, which likely force patients to purchase these items from private retail pharmacies. Open bone fixation carried the highest CHE burden, consistent with previous studies in Ethiopia [9, 10]. This association likely reflects the complex nature of trauma surgery, including high rates of contamination, prolonged hospitalization, and the cost of fixation materials.

Inequitable impacts of bellwether surgical costs were evident in this study. In this study, CHE was approximately twice as high among rural residents compared with urban residents, indicating that rural households are disproportionately burdened by the cost of surgical care. This may be explained by higher transport costs and longer distances to access care, delayed presentation with more complicated disease, and lower total household consumption among rural residents. This finding is consistent with previous studies in Ethiopia [9, 10] and Zambia [18], which reported that the highest proportion of catastrophic health expenditure occurred among the rural residents and low economic social class, despite all economic status groups experienced high catastrophic costs. Another inequity observed in this study was the age-related gradient in catastrophic health expenditure, with older surgical patients experiencing a disproportionately higher incidence of catastrophic health expenditure than their younger counterparts. This likely reflects the greater need for major, resource-intensive surgical interventions among older adults, often accompanied by longer hospital stays, higher complication rates, and greater need for perioperative support. Comorbidities common in this age group further amplify surgical risk and drive-up costs through extended postoperative care. These findings suggest that age functions not only as a clinical risk factor but as a structural determinant of financial vulnerability in surgical care, and highlight a gap in current financial protection schemes, which may not adequately account for the higher cost burden associated with age-related surgical complexity. Age-sensitive financing mechanisms, such as expanded coverage for perioperative care and targeted subsidies for older and rural resident patients undergoing high-cost procedures, may be necessary to close this gap.

This study has some limitations. First, as a single-centre study, findings from one hospital may not be generalizable to national CHE patterns, given regional and national variation in poverty levels, insurance coverage, and health infrastructure. Second, the 30-day post-discharge follow-up window captured only immediate post-operative costs and likely missed expenditures arising from late complications, rehabilitation, or repeat visits, potentially underestimating the true burden of catastrophic health expenditure. Third, out-of-pocket spending was based on patient and household self-report, which is susceptible to recall bias, particularly for non-medical costs such as transport, food, and lodging that are more difficult to itemize than medical bills. Fourth, indirect costs, including lost income and productivity losses for patients and caregivers, were incompletely captured, which may have led to an underestimation of the total economic burden of surgical care. Finally, annual household expenditure used to determine catastrophic health expenditure thresholds was estimated from short recall periods, which may have introduced measurement error and affected the precision of CHE estimates.

Conclusion

This study provides important evidence of a high incidence of catastrophic health expenditure following bellwether surgical procedures. Although enrollment in community Bases Health Insurance or Free Health initiatives reduce the incidence from approximately two-third to one -third of patients, substantial proportion of surgical patients continued to incur catastrophic health expenditure despite coverage. This burden was disproportionately concentrated among rural residents, those who experienced delays in seeking, referral or accessing care, patients residing at greater distances from health treatment center, older age, and those undergoing laparotomy and open fracture fixation. These findings alarm the need for strengthened financial risk protection policies, expanded insurance coverage, and targeted interventions for vulnerable and underserved populations. Achieving comprehensive financial risk protection for surgical care is essential to achieve the Lancet Commission on Global Surgery’s 2030 vision of safe, affordable, and timely surgical care for all.
