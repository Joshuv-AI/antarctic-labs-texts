Association between triglyceride-glucose-frailty index and metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease: Mediation analysis involving obesity indicators in the NHANES
Authors: Shixian Ruan, Shuanglong Liu, Xinmei Zhang, Jiawei Zhang, Changxin Chen, Huilin Lu, Kexin Zheng, Siyi Fang, Xiangbo Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The triglyceride-glucose frailty index (TyGFI), integrating metabolic dysfunction and physiological decline, has been proposed as a novel marker of cardiometabolic risk. Its association with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), however, remains unclear. Our objective was to assess TyGFI’s predictive value for MASLD risk and to explore the mediating role of obesity indices in the relationships among insulin resistance, aging, and MASLD. Using data from 988 participants aged ≥45 years in the 2017–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, we examined the relationship between TyGFI and MASLD. MASLD was defined by controlled attenuation parameter (CAP ≥ 278 dB/m) and at least 1 cardiometabolic risk factor. Multivariable logistic regression, restricted cubic spline, subgroup, and receiver operating characteristic analyses were conducted. Mediation analysis was applied to assess the role of obesity indicators, including body mass index, waist circumference, lipid accumulation product, and visceral adiposity index. Elevated TyGFI was independently associated with an increased prevalence of MASLD, with a significant dose–response relationship across quartiles. The association was consistent across demographic and clinical subgroups. receiver operating characteristic analysis indicated modest predictive ability (AUC = 0.62). All 4 obesity indicators correlated strongly with TyGFI and MASLD; mediation analyses showed that body mass index, waist circumference, and lipid accumulation product significantly mediated the TyGFI–MASLD association, whereas visceral adiposity index did not. TyGFI is independently associated with MASLD risk in middle-aged and older adults, with much of its effect mediated through obesity-related pathways. These findings highlight the potential of TyGFI as a risk assessment tool and underscore the importance of obesity control in MASLD prevention.

1. Introduction

The rapid development of society and shifts in lifestyle, particularly the rising prevalence of obesity, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome,[1-3] have contributed to a substantial global increase in nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), a trend that continues to escalate.[4] Current estimates suggest that NAFLD affects more than one-third of the world’s population,[5] imposing a considerable health and economic burden.[6] To address these trends and better capture the underlying etiology, on June 24, 2023, 3 leading international hepatology societies – the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD), the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL), and the Latin American Association for the Study of the Liver (ALEH) – in collaboration with 53 global experts, issued a multi-society Delphi consensus that redefined fatty liver nomenclature. They recommended replacing the term “nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)” with “metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD).”[7] This updated terminology more accurately reflects the pathophysiological features of the disease and underscores the need for clinical and public health strategies focused on its metabolic determinants.[8]

At present, noninvasive approaches are increasingly regarded as viable alternatives to liver biopsy for the evaluation of MASLD.[9] Among these, transient elastography (TE) has emerged as a rapid, noninvasive bedside tool that plays a central role in both the diagnosis and clinical management of MASLD.[10] The principal advantage of TE lies in its capacity to provide accurate, noninvasive quantification of liver stiffness measurement, thereby enabling reliable staging of fibrosis – the most critical determinant of prognosis and therapeutic decision-making.[11] Moreover, TE devices incorporate the controlled attenuation parameter (CAP), which allows simultaneous quantification of hepatic steatosis. This feature adds important value for initial screening and longitudinal monitoring, with CAP values ≥268 dB/m serving as a clinically relevant threshold suggestive of MASLD.[12]

The core pathogenic mechanism of MASLD is insulin resistance (IR), which enhances hepatic de novo lipogenesis while suppressing fatty acid β-oxidation. This imbalance results in abnormal triglyceride (TG) accumulation within hepatocytes, serving as the initial driving force of the disease.[13] Aging further aggravates IR through multiple pathways, with the accumulation of cellular senescence playing a pivotal role. Senescent cells release proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and proteases that disrupt local insulin signaling and foster a chronic proinflammatory milieu.[14] Within the liver, age-related impairment of insulin signaling, together with persistent inflammation, accelerates the activation of hepatic stellate cells and deposition of extracellular matrix, thereby advancing fibrosis.[15] Thus, MASLD can be conceptualized as a prototypical disease model initiated by IR and sustained by aging-associated inflammation and metabolic derangements.[16]

IR is the principal driver of the development and progression of MASLD.[17] IR contributes to hepatic fat accumulation (steatosis) through multiple mechanisms, including altered rates of adipose tissue lipolysis and de novo lipogenesis, impaired mitochondrial β-oxidation of fatty acids, changes in fat distribution, disruptions of the gut microbiome, and dysregulation of adipokines and cytokines.[13,18,19] Accordingly, interventions targeting IR are regarded as critical strategies for preventing and managing MASLD and its progression to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) and cirrhosis. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, which shows a strong inverse correlationwith glucose disposal rate and exhibits high diagnostic accuracy, has been widely adopted as a simple and cost-effective surrogate measure of insulin sensitivity. It is derived from routine biochemical parameters-fasting TG and fasting glucose levels.[20,21] A growing body of evidence has demonstrated its predictive value for various metabolic disorders, including diabetes, coronary artery disease, heart failure, and stroke.[22-24.

Frailty is a prevalent and clinically significant geriatric syndrome, defined by an age-related decline in physiological reserves across multiple organ systems, which markedly increases susceptibility to adverse health outcomes.[25] Although no universally accepted gold standard for frailty assessment exists, numerous screening instruments have been developed for both risk stratification and epidemiological investigation.[26] Among them, the frailty index (FI) is one of the most widely applied and validated measures of biological aging. It quantifies health status through the cumulative burden of deficits across diverse domains, including physical function, comorbidities, cognition, and psychological well-being, thereby capturing reduced physiological reserve and increased vulnerability to stressors. The FI is constructed using the standardized 10-step methodology proposed by Theou et al, ensuring consistency in variable selection, coding, and index development.[27] Expressed as a numerical score, the FI reflects the overall degree of frailty, with higher values denoting more severe frailty. The FI has been consistently linked to a wide spectrum of adverse outcomes, including cardiovascular disease (CVD), stroke, falls, and cognitive decline.[28-30] Given its high prevalence among older adults and robust prognostic relevance, frailty represents a critical component of geriatric risk assessment models.

Although the TyG index and frailty have each been recognized as predictors of MASLD, their effects have typically been assessed in isolation, and little is known about their combined impact. In clinical practice, however, metabolic impairment and physiological frailty frequently coexist in older adults, and this coexistence may exert synergistic effects – mediated by mechanisms such as chronic inflammation and oxidative stress – that accelerate lipotoxic accumulation and cell death. To address this gap, Zhao et al[31] proposed a novel composite risk metric, the triglyceride-glucose frailty index (TyGFI), which integrates metabolic stress and physiological vulnerability to capture their dual burden.

Obesity is closely linked to MASLD and is widely recognized as a major risk factor for the disease.[1,32] Aging and IR act synergistically to promote fat redistribution, resulting in excessive deposition of visceral and ectopic fat, including intramuscular fat – a key nexus connecting aging, IR, and obesity.[33,34] Furthermore, obesity represents a state of chronic low-grade inflammation, in which dysfunctional adipose tissue secretes inflammatory mediators that exacerbate hepatic inflammation and further elevate the risk of MASLD.[35] Beyond conventional anthropometric measures such as body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC), 2 novel indices – the lipid accumulation product (LAP) and the visceral adiposity index (VAI) – have recently been developed. Based on simple anthropometric and biochemical parameters, these indices provide more accurate assessments of visceral obesity and related metabolic risks than traditional metrics.[36,37] The integration of these indices offers valuable insights and enhances the robustness of analyses and study conclusions.

In summary, IR, aging, and obesity are intricately intertwined with MASLD. To further elucidate these interrelationships, we investigated the association between TyGFI and MASLD in individuals aged 45 years and older, drawing on data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).[38] We examined the predictive utility of TyGFI for MASLD risk and performed mediation analyses to evaluate the potential mediating roles of multiple obesity indices in the links among IR, aging, and MASLD. These analyses aim to provide novel insights that may inform preventive and therapeutic strategies for MASLD.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and population

The NHANES is an ongoing, nationally representative, cross-sectional survey conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics, a division of the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/). NHANES is designed to evaluate the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States. Employing a complex, multistage probability sampling strategy with stratification, the survey ensures representativeness across all 50 US states and the District of Columbia. The sampling framework incorporates key demographic factors – including age, race/ethnicity, and socioeconomic status – to generate cohorts that are nationally generalizable to the noninstitutionalized civilian population. Data collection encompasses structured interviews, standardized physical examinations, and laboratory testing, making NHANES one of the most comprehensive population health data sources in the United States. All survey procedures, including sampling design and data collection protocols, were approved by the NCHS Ethics Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

NHANES provides nationally representative data suitable for investigating the relationship between TyGFI and MASLD. In the present study, a CAP threshold of ≥278 dB/m was applied to define significant hepatic steatosis.[12] MASLD was further defined as the presence of significant steatosis accompanied by at least 1 cardiometabolic risk factor, while excluding individuals with excessive alcohol intake (>140 g/wk for women and >210 g/wk for men) or other secondary causes of hepatic steatosis.[39] The participants included in our study comprised all adult individuals aged 45 years or older from the NHANES 2017–2018 cycle. Participants were excluded if they were younger than 45 years; lacked MASLD or TyGFI data; had incomplete obesity indicator data; or had missing demographic (gender, race/ethnicity, education level), lifestyle (smoking, alcohol use), or medical history information (including diabetes, hypertension, CVD, and chronic kidney disease [CKD]). After applying these criteria, the final analytic sample comprised 988 participants, representing a weighted population of 39,350,760 US. adults, which formed the basis for our statistical analyses (Fig. 1).

2.2. Exposure variable

TyG was calculated as Ln [triglycerides (mg/dL) × glucose (mg/dL)/2].[20] This index has been widely adopted in metabolic research, and its reliability has been consistently validated in subsequent studies.[21]

Frailty was assessed using the FI, a comprehensive tool that quantifies vulnerability to adverse health outcomes, particularly in the context of aging. The FI incorporates 49 health deficits, including limitations in physical activity, cognitive impairment, deficits in physical performance (e.g., reduced grip strength, difficulty walking), comorbid conditions, self-rated health, and emotional or depressive symptoms.[40] It integrates symptoms, signs, functional impairments, and diseases into a single metric.[41] Each deficit is scored from 0 to 1 according to severity. The FI is calculated as the ratio of the total score of observed deficits to the total number of potential deficits. For example, if 40 potential deficits are assessed and an individual exhibits 10, the FI would be 10/40 = 0.25.[40] A detailed list of variables and scoring criteria is provided in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/R662.

To capture the combined impact of metabolic and functional deficits, Zhao et al proposed a multiplicative model: TyGFI = TyG × FI. This composite index has been shown to be a robust and independent predictor of CVD and stroke.[31]

2.3. Covariates

Based on previously published studies and clinical rationale, several variables were identified as potential confounders, including age, gender, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, smoking status, alcohol consumption, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, CVD, and CKD. Age was classified as <65 years or ≥65 years, and gender was categorized as female or male. Race/ethnicity was grouped into Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and Other Race. Educational attainment was categorized as less than high school, high school or equivalent, or greater than high school. Smoking status was self-reported and categorized as never, former, or current smokers, while alcohol consumption was classified as yes or no based on self-reported current drinking behavior.

Diabetes status was determined by meeting at least one of the following criteria: self-reported physician diagnosis, current use of insulin, fasting blood glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, or hemoglobin A1c levels consistent with the clinical diagnosis of diabetes. Hypertension was defined as an average systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg, or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHgHg (based on 3 measurements), or by self-reported physician diagnosis, or current use of antihypertensive medications. CVD was defined as self-reported history of congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, or myocardial infarction. CKD was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, and was based on Scr levels.

2.4. Assessment of mediators

In accordance with standardized protocols, trained healthcare professionals at the mobile examination center obtained anthropometric measurements using validated techniques and calibrated equipment. Body height, body weight, and WC were measured for all participants. During height and weight assessment, participants wore minimal clothing, consisting of underwear, disposable paper gowns, and foam slippers. WC was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm at the midpoint between the lowest rib and the iliac crest using a flexible measuring tape.

After an overnight fast of at least 8 hours, venous blood samples were collected for laboratory testing, including TG and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels. 2.5. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the sampling weights provided by NHANES to account for the complex survey design and ensure nationally representative estimates. Continuous variables were summarized as weighted means ± standard errors, while categorical variables were reported as unweighted counts with weighted percentages. TyGFI was categorized into quartiles, and its association with MASLD was assessed via 3 multivariable logistic regression models, with the lowest quartile (Q1) serving as the reference group. Results were reported as odds ratio (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). Model 1 was unadjusted; model 2 was adjusted for age, gender, race/ethnicity, and education; and model 3 further included additional adjustments for smoking status, alcohol consumption, diabetes, hypertension, CVD, and CKD. Potential confounding was addressed by conducting subgroup analyses that were stratified by age, gender, race/ethnicity, smoking status, alcohol use, diabetes, hypertension, CVD, and CKD. Potential nonlinear associations were explored using restricted cubic spline (RCS) models, adjusting for the same covariates included in model 3. The predictive performance of TyGFI for MASLD was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the area under the curve (AUC) calculated to quantify sensitivity and specificity. Correlations among the 4 obesity indicators were examined, and weighted multivariable regression models were applied to evaluate their associations with both TyGFI and MASLD. Mediation analyses with bootstrapping were then used to estimate the direct effect of TyGFI on MASLD risk and the indirect effects mediated by obesity indicators. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2, R Core Team [2021], Vienna, Austria), and 2-tailed P-values < .05 were considered statistically significant.

3.2. Association of TyGFI with MASLD

Table 2 presents the association between TyGFI and MASLD. After adjustment for potential confounders, a significant positive correlation was found between higher TyGFI levels and increased prevalence of MASLD. In the unadjusted model (model 1), TyGFI was positively correlated with prevalence of MASLD (OR = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.36–1.92, P < .001) with the OR indicating that a 1-unit increase in CTI was associated with a 62% increase in prevalence of MASLD. This association persisted after adjustment for age, gender, race/ethnicity, and education (model 2; OR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.44–2.34, P < .001). In the fully adjusted model (model 3), which also controlled for smoking status, alcohol consumption, diabetes, hypertension, CVD and CKD, the association was further strengthened (OR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.26–2.59, P = .020). In model 3, a 1-unit increase in CTI was independently associated with a 80% increase in prevalence of MASLD.

When TyGFI was analyzed as a categorical variable based on quartiles, participants in Q2, Q3, and Q4 exhibited significantly higher prevalence of MASLD across all models compared to the reference group (Q1). Overall, these findings indicate that elevated TyGFI levels are independently and positively associated with a higher prevalence of MASLD.

3.3. RCS analysis

RCS analysis was employed to determine whether a nonlinear relationship existed between TyGFI and MASLD. As illustrated in Figure 2, a significant nonlinear association between TyGFI and MASLD was observed (P = .071). Notably, the inflection points were identified at TyGFI values of 7.327. Below the threshold, the OR for MASLD remained approximately 1, whereas above these values, the risk of MASLD increased substantially with higher TyGFI levels, indicating a potential dose-dependent effect beyond the identified inflection point.

3.4. Subgroup analysis

Stratified multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine the association between TyGFI and MASLD across different population subgroups (Fig. 3). Interaction tests in the NHANES cohort indicated that there were no statistically significant effect modifications by gender, age, smoking status, alcohol consumption, diabetes, hypertension, CVD, or CKD (all P for interaction > .05). These findings indicate that the positive association between TyGFI and MASLD was consistent across all examined subgroups.

3.5. ROC analysis

Figure 4 illustrates the ROC curve and corresponding AUC values for the prediction of MASLD. The AUC for TyGFI was 0.6217 (95% CI: 0.5862–0.6572), indicating modest predictive ability. Although TyGFI demonstrated some value in predicting MASLD, its discriminatory performance remained limited (AUC < 0.7). Accordingly, TyGFI may hold clinical relevance as a reference indicator, but future studies should investigate its integration with other clinical measures to establish more robust predictive models.

3.7. Mediating role of obesity indicators

As shown in Figure 6, mediation analyses revealed that higher TyGFI values were significantly associated with increased risk of MASLD, even after adjustment for major covariates. Importantly, BMI, WC, and LAP emerged as significant mediators of this relationship. Specifically, BMI and WC accounted for 86.4% and 100.8% of the total effect, respectively (both P < .001), indicating that measures of general and central obesity substantially explained the TyGFI–MASLD association. Likewise, LAP mediated 109.5% of the effect (P < .001), underscoring the contribution of lipid accumulation to this pathway. In contrast, VAI demonstrated no significant mediation, with only 61.2% of the effect explained (P = .002), suggesting that the VAI may not independently mediate the TyGFI–MASLD link in this model. Collectively, these findings indicate that the adverse metabolic impact of TyGFI on MASLD is largely transmitted through body fat accumulation and distribution, particularly general, central, and ectopic fat depots.

4. Discussion

With ongoing population aging, IR, physiological decline, and age-related frailty are becoming increasingly prevalent among older adults, heightening their susceptibility to disease and adverse health outcomes. To our knowledge, this study is the first to explore the association between TyGFI, a composite marker integrating IR and aging, and MASLD prevalence in middle-aged and older populations. We found that higher TyGFI values were independently associated with increased prevalence of MASLD, with a consistent dose–response relationship after adjusting for multiple covariates. Subgroup analyses confirmed the robustness of this association across demographic and clinical strata. RCS analysis indicated a linear relationship, while ROC analysis suggested modest discriminatory ability (AUC = 0.622), implying that TyGFI may be most useful in combination with other markers. Furthermore, obesity indicators – particularly BMI, WC, and LAP – were strongly correlated with TyGFI and mediated much of its effect on MASLD, emphasizing the central role of adiposity. Together, these findings support TyGFI as a meaningful marker of MASLD risk in middle-aged and older adults, with its influence largely conveyed through obesity-related pathways.

As a novel metric, TyGFI offers value in both research and clinical practice by capturing the potential interaction between metabolic and functional deficits. To our knowledge, this study is the first to use NHANES data to demonstrate that TyGFI levels are elevated in individuals with MASLD compared with healthy controls. In recent years, TyG and FI have become 2 important clinical markers for evaluating MASLD risk and prognosis.[44,45]

Extensive evidence supports the TyG index as a reliable surrogate for IR, showing strong positive associations with the presence, severity (including fibrosis), and progression of MASLD, often outperforming traditional measures in diagnostic and predictive accuracy. For instance, Cao et al[46] reported that higher TyG values were an independent risk factor for NAFLD, with each 1-unit increase conferring a 74.2% higher risk. Zhang et al[47] confirmed the utility of TyG as a biomarker, demonstrating a strong correlation with hepatic steatosis and a diagnostic AUC exceeding 0.80, highlighting its promise as a simple, low-cost, and effective screening tool, particularly in primary care. Zou et al[44] extended these findings by evaluating TyG and its derivatives (e.g., TyG-BMI, TyG-WC), showing that all were significantly associated with MASLD and advanced fibrosis. Furthermore, Liu et al[48] demonstrated in a longitudinal study that participants with medium-to-high or persistently high TyG trajectories had more than a 2.5-fold higher risk of incident lean NAFLD compared with those maintaining stable low levels, underscoring the role of sustained IR as a powerful driver of lean NAFLD, even among individuals with normal BMI.

Frailty has likewise garnered increasing attention in MASLD research. A meta-analysis by Khanmohammadi et al[49] found that NAFLD patients had a significantly higher risk of frailty than non-NAFLD populations (OR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.18–1.48), an association that remained after adjustment for metabolic confounders. Similarly, Zhang et al[50] demonstrated, through large-scale cohort validation and genetic analysis, a significant causal and genetic relationship between frailty (both FI and frailty phenotype) and MASLD, suggesting frailty as an independent risk factor. Prognostically, Zhong et al[45] reported that FI was associated with increased risks of MASLD, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and liver-related mortality.

From a mechanistic standpoint, the TyGFI index reflects the synergistic interaction between metabolic dysfunction and systemic physiological decline. Accumulating evidence highlights a tightly interwoven “vicious triangle” linking IR, frailty, and MASLD, each reinforcing the other to drive disease onset and progression.

Frailty is a key initiating factor,[51] representing the common endpoint of diverse health deficits.[52] With advancing age and declining physiological reserve, mitochondrial quantity and function deteriorate, leading to impaired fatty acid β-oxidation, excess generation of reactive oxygen species, depletion of antioxidant defenses such as glutathione, and defective mitophagy – all of which promote lipid accumulation in hepatocytes.[53] Concurrently, aging is accompanied by chronic low-grade inflammation (“inflamm-aging”), driven by the senescence-associated secretory phenotype and immunosenescence, resulting in elevated circulating proinflammatory cytokines.[54] These cytokines disrupt insulin signaling, directly inducing or exacerbating IR.[55,56] In the liver, IR manifests as impaired suppression of hepatic glucose output,[57] while insulin’s lipogenic actions – facilitating hepatocellular uptake of free fatty acids and stimulating de novo lipogenesis – remain preserved or even enhanced.[13,58] The net effect is increased lipogenesis, reduced β-oxidation, and accumulation of TGs within hepatocytes, establishing the foundation of MASLD.[59] Obesity further contributes through its proinflammatory state: dysfunctional adipose tissue recruits and activates macrophages, releasing cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 as well as chemokines.[60] These mediators reach the liver via the portal vein, activate resident Kupffer cells, and amplify hepatic inflammation, thereby intensifying MASLD risk.[35] Moreover, hepatic steatosis and IR reinforce one another in a self-perpetuating cycle. Fatty liver disease itself – especially upon progression to MASH, characterized by inflammation and hepatocellular injury – exacerbates insulin signaling defects, mitochondrial dysfunction, and endoplasmic reticulum stress through the release of inflammatory cytokines and adipokines.[61,62] Aging-related inflammation compounds these processes by activating hepatic stellate cells and profibrotic pathways, promoting extracellular matrix deposition, and escalating metabolic stress. The result is enhanced lipotoxicity, hepatocyte death, and accelerated progression from simple steatosis to MASH.[63-65]

In summary, aging establishes the groundwork for IR and hepatic steatosis through mitochondrial dysfunction and chronic inflammation. IR then amplifies metabolic derangements, while steatosis feeds back to worsen IR and inflammation. Together, these interdependent mechanisms form a vicious cycle that heightens the risk of MASLD onset and progression.

In our study, TyGFI was positively associated with MASLD risk, with a clear dose–response relationship that remained significant after adjustment for confounders, supporting TyGFI as an independent risk factor. This finding implies that targeting metabolic dysfunction and systemic physiological decline may be a feasible strategy for MASLD prevention. We also observed that MASLD patients exhibited higher levels of all obesity indicators (BMI, WC, LAP, and VAI) compared with healthy controls, consistent with prior reports.[66-68] Strong correlations were noted among these 4 measures, and each was significantly associated with TyGFI. Notably, increases in all 4 indicators were linked to greater MASLD risk. Mediation analyses further demonstrated that BMI, WC, and LAP acted as significant mediators in the TyGFI–MASLD association, highlighting the role of lipid accumulation in this metabolic pathway. Collectively, these findings suggest that obesity may be a critical mediator connecting metabolic dysfunction, systemic physiological decline, and MASLD.

This study offers several notable strengths. First, we leveraged a nationally representative dataset to investigate the association between TyGFI and MASLD. Second, by incorporating multiple obesity indicators (BMI, WC, VAI, and LAP), we evaluated the mediating role of obesity from different perspectives, thereby providing a more comprehensive assessment. Third, we adjusted for a broad range of confounders and performed sensitivity analyses, enhancing the robustness of our conclusions.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the observational nature of the study precludes definitive causal inference. Second, as the findings are based on US adults, their generalizability to other populations may be limited. Third, although extensive adjustments for confounders were made, the possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be ruled out. Fourth, the cross-sectional design limits the assessment of temporal relationships. Therefore, further large-scale prospective or longitudinal studies are needed to validate our findings and to establish the temporal sequence and potential causal links between TyGFI and MASLD risk.

5. Conclusion

In summary, this study explored the relationships among TyGFI, various obesity indicators, and MASLD. The results demonstrated that elevated TyGFI levels were independently associated with increased MASLD risk, and that BMI, WC, and LAP played important mediating roles in this association. These findings suggest that appropriate obesity control may effectively mitigate the impact of metabolic dysfunction and physiological decline on MASLD.
