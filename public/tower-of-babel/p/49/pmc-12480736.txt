Sebum’s effect on wound healing and pigmentation in mice
Authors: Kyung-Ju Lee, Hye-Young An, Gwang Seong Choi, Hyun-tae Shin, Jeonghyun Shin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWound healing is often followed by postinflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH), which commonly develops in sebum-poor areas.ObjectivesTo investigate the effects of sebum on wound healing, especially relative to basement membrane recovery and PIH.MethodsUsing 100% trichloroacetic acid, we created deep dermal wounds on both sides of male HRM-2 hairless melanin-containing mice. The left side served as the control group, while the right side received daily sebum application for 21 days. The wound healing status and degree of pigmentation were evaluated by visual wound assessments and image analyses. Histological analysis and electron microscopy (EM) examinations were performed on days 7, 14 and 21, to compare basement membrane recovery and pigmentation.ResultsThe sebum group exhibited faster wound healing, including quicker crust removal and wound contraction, and reduced pigmentation, although this difference was not statistically significant. Histological and EM analyses seemed to show accelerated basement membrane recovery and delayed onset of melanosome accumulation with sebum treatment. We also performed cell viability and melanin contents assays using B16F10 melanoma cells, revealing that sebum decreased melanogenesis by inhibiting tyrosinase activity, without cytotoxicity.ConclusionsOverall, our results suggest that sebum might promote wound healing, with faster basement membrane recovery, and reduce PIH.

In clinical practice, we have observed that patients exhibit more pigmentation on sebum-poor skin areas; however, studies of how sebum affects pigmentation are limited. Here we aimed to test the hypothesis that sebum promotes wound healing, leading to reduced postinflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH). Our results showed that human sebum itself might promote wound healing and basement membrane recovery, and inhibit melanogenesis. These results may explain why PIH is less common in sebum-rich skin areas. Moreover, these findings may guide the development of new treatments to promote faster wound healing and reduced PIH.

Wound healing rates vary depending on the injury depth and extent, and the patient’s age. The wound healing process involves tissue inflammation, migration, proliferation, re-epithelialization and remodelling, and requires a complex interplay of cells and mediators to restore the protective barrier and prevent infection.1 Sometimes pigmentation occurs after inflammation (i.e. PIH). The pathogenesis of PIH is not clearly defined. PIH onset is associated with melanogenesis of melanocytes in the basal cell layer of the epidermis. Melanin is derived from the enzymatic breakdown of tyrosine by tyrosinase – a key regulatory and rate-limiting enzyme in melanogenesis.2 Inflammation processes may induce melanocyte activation via inflammatory cytokines.3 During inflammation, cellular membranes become damaged, releasing oxidized derivatives of arachidonic acid, increasing tyrosinase activity and subsequently enhancing melanocyte activity.3 Notably, leukotriene C4 is known for its strong ability to activate tyrosinase activity.4 Increased melanocytic activity leads to larger melanocytes, dendritic proliferation, enhanced melanin synthesis and improved melanosome transfer to keratinocytes.3,5 Keratinocyte protease-activated receptor-2 plays a crucial role in the transfer of melanosomes from melanocytes to keratinocytes.6 Although this process occurs in the basal cell layer of epidermis, damage to the basement membrane can allow pigments to drop into the dermis, where it is phagocytosed by macrophages, creating melanophages. Thus, the extent of basement membrane damage may play a crucial role in dermal melanophage formation.3 Keratinocytes and fibroblasts can express matrix metallopeptidases, which may contribute to basement membrane damage.7

The duration of hyperpigmentation tends to be longer in patients with darker skin, those with severe inflammation and in cases of dermal-type PIH.3 In particular, dermal-type PIH tends to persist longer than epidermal-type PIH which shows basal cell hyperpigmentation only, under conditions involving interface dermatitis that damages the basement membrane, such as lichen planus. Even without severe inflammation, if the basement membrane is damaged, as in Riehl melanosis or melasma, pigmentation does not readily improve because melanin can spill from the epidermis into the dermis through the damaged basement membrane.8 Therefore, rapid basement membrane recovery may result in faster wound healing with less PIH.

Sebaceous gland number and activity vary depending on the body location, age and sex.9 There are typically <100 glands per square centimetre on the body, compared with approximately 900 glands per square centimetre on the face (especially the T-zone), upper neck and chest. Sebaceous glands are not present on the palms or soles.9,10 Among 30 predefined facial sites, sebum secretion is most abundant on the central forehead, nose and the sides of the nose, with less distribution around the lateral areas of the cheeks and around the eyes and lips.11 The region of the pilosebaceous unit exhibits highly effective wound healing, but the precise mechanism has not been elucidated.12 During wound healing in rodents, keratinocytes from the pilosebaceous unit and from the wound edge proliferate and migrate towards the centre of the wound.13

According to Voegeli et al.’s study,11 areas of lower sebum abundance are similar to areas prone to hyperpigmentation. Prolonged PIH tends to occur more prominently on the lateral sides of the face, compared with in central sebum-rich areas, even if the entire face has been inflamed (Figure 1a–c). Notably, the sebum-poor malar areas are prone to melasma, and Riehl melanosis usually occurs in the lateral face area (Figure 1d). Based on these clinical observations, we hypothesized that sebum-poor facial areas are more prone to pigmentation. However, the effects of sebum on wound healing and pigmentation are unknown. Therefore, here we investigated whether sebum could expedite wound healing, with a focus on basement membrane recovery and reduced PIH, using a mouse model with melanin pigment.

Materials and methods

Artificial human sebum

Artificial human sebum is similar to human sebum in terms of composition and proportions, and physicochemical properties, and is thus a suitable substitute for human sebum in research.14 Here we used artificial sebum, ASTM D4265-14 (Pickering Laboratories, Mountain View, CA, USA), which is composed of 17% fatty acid, 44.7% triglycerides, 25% wax monoesters (derived from jojoba oil) and 12.4% squalene. It can be stored for a maximum of 48 h at 32 °C and for a maximum of 6 months at 4 °C or −20 °C.

Animals

For this study, we used HRM-2 mice, which are hairless but capable of synthesizing melanin pigments, making them suitable for various pigmentation researches.15 Five-week-old male HRM-2 hairless mice with melanin were obtained from Central Lab Animal (Seoul, Republic of Korea). After a 1-week adaptation period, the experiments were started when the mice reached 6 weeks of age. The mice were individually marked with ear tags, and each individual was housed in a separate cage to prevent physical injuries inflicted by other mice. These mice were kept in a controlled environment (23 ± 1 °C, 55 ± 5% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) with unrestricted access to food and water. Every possible measure was taken to minimize harm to the ­animals.

Trichloroacetic acid

Trichloroacetic acid (TCA) is a widely used chemical peeling agent16 that induces necrosis in the epidermis and papillary dermis, followed by regeneration from nearby hair follicles. TCA self-neutralizes and forms a white frost, indicating completion of the peeling process. Different concentrations induce superficial (10–35% TCA) and medium-depth peeling (35–50% TCA) in human skin, with higher concentrations associated with higher risk of complications, including PIH or hypertrophic scarring.16–18 Based on preliminary experiments with 50% and 100% TCA in HRM-2 mice, we decided to use 100% TCA to generate a wound with basement membrane damage and dermal-type PIH.

Experimental design

We used 14 mice for the experiment. TCA was used to induce wounds on both sides of the dorsal skin. The left side was designated the control group and the right side as the experimental sebum treatment group. Wounds on the right side were treated with artificial sebum once daily for 21 days, using a cotton swab with two passes, starting from the day of wound creation. Wounds on the left side received no application. To visually assess the wounds, photographs were taken on days 1, 3, 5, 7, 14 and 21. Tissue biopsies were conducted on days 7 and 14 to assess the extent of basement membrane damage, and on day 21 to evaluate the degree of pigmentation (Figure 2). Histological analysis was performed on days 7 and 21 to compare the control group, the sebum group and normal noninjured mouse skin. Prior to wound induction and tissue biopsies, intraperitoneal anaesthesia was administered using ketamine (100 mg kg–1) and xylazine (10 mg kg–1). Prior to photography, inhalation anaesthesia with isoflurane was administered. The mice did not receive painkillers or antibiotics after wound induction, as they can interfere with the immune response and potentially affect the wound-healing process.19,20

Experimental design to investigate the effects of sebum on wounds. A total of 14 HRM-2 mice were used. D, day; TCA, trichloroacetic acid.

Wound creation

Before applying TCA for wound formation, we drew squares of 1.6 × 1.6 cm2 on the left and right sides of the dorsal skin of HRM-2 mice. We applied petrolatum to the edges of these squares to prevent TCA from flowing onto the surrounding skin. Then we applied 100% TCA twice with a cotton swab, using 0.2 cc of TCA per pass. We waited 1 min, until frost appeared, and then rinsed the area with normal saline.

Visual wound assessment

Photographs were taken on days 1, 3, 5 and 7 to visually compare and evaluate the wounds of both groups during the acute phase. The wounds were photographed on days 14 and 21 to compare changes in pigmentation during the late phase. Photographs were obtained using the Canon EOS M100 camera (Canon Korea, Seoul, South Korea). Visual assessments were conducted by a single researcher, using the grading scales presented in Table S1 (see Supporting Information),21 who was unaware of whether the wound was treated with sebum, to avoid any bias. Furthermore, wound areas were calculated using ImageJ software (version 1.54; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Although the induced wound area was 1.6 × 1.6 cm2, the wound area was recalculated on day 1 to account for any variation. The wound contraction rate was calculated using the wound area from day 1 as the baseline. We compared the wound contraction rates in both groups on days 3, 5 and 7. The wound contraction rate was calculated as the percentage difference between the wound edge area on day 1 (W1) and the contracted wound edge area on each measurement day (Wi).20,21

Tissue biopsy

On days 7, 14 and 21, tissue biopsies were performed in both groups to assess the extent of basement membrane damage and pigment deposition in the wound. On days 7 and 21, we also biopsied normal mouse skin to compare with both groups. Immediately upon collection, tissue samples were immersed in specialized solutions of 10% buffered formaldehyde. Specimens from 4-mm punch biopsies were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) to examine the basement membrane, and Fontana-Masson (F-M) to identify pigment deposition. We also performed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) using a Hitachi-7100 Transmission Electron Microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). At each timepoint, H&E, PAS and F-M staining were conducted on samples from three mice. TEM was performed on samples from one mouse on days 7 and 14, and from two mice on day 21. Additionally, histological examinations of samples from different mice were conducted on days 7, 14 and 21.

Measurement of the pigmentation area

To compare the extent of pigmentation between the two groups, we analysed the F-M-stained specimen from day 21, using ImageJ software (version 1.54).20 The scanned image of the specimen was converted into an 8-bit image using the ImageJ program, and the threshold was set to include pigmentation colour. Areas mistakenly identified as pigmentation were manually removed. The area selected as pigmentation was calculated using the ImageJ program (Figure S1; see Supporting Information).

Cell culture and viability assay

B16F10 melanoma cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco, NY, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic, in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 in air, at 37 °C. The culture medium was changed every 2–3 days. Upon reaching approximately 80% confluence, the cells were harvested by trypsinization, and then seeded at appropriate density into wells of cell culture plates for further experiments. Artificial sebum was diluted to 50% in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and then diluted in the culture medium to final concentrations of 0.01%, 0.05% and 0.1%.

The viability of B16F10 cells was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, Gaithersburg, MD, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were incubated with artificial sebum (0.01%, 0.05% and 0.1%) and negative control DMSO (0.1%) for 48 h, with or without α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH; Sigma, St Louis, MO, USA). Next, CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. The optical density value at 450 nm (OD450) was measured using a TECAN microplate reader (TECAN Austria Grödig, Austria).

Melanin content assay

B16F10 cells were first treated with 100 nmol L–1 of α-MSH (Sigma) in DMEM medium, followed by 0.01%, 0.05% and 0.1% artificial sebum and negative control DMSO (0.1%) for an additional 48 h. To evaluate melanin content, the cells were washed with Dulbecco’s phosphate buffered saline (D-PBS), harvested and lysed with 1 N NaOH for 1 h at 80 °C. Melanin content was measured based on the absorbance at 450 nm, and determined using a standard curve of synthetic melanin (Sigma). The melanin content was normalized to the total protein concentration using a Bradford Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Tyrosinase activity assay

To estimate tyrosinase activity, we measured the rate of dopachrome formation of L-DOPA. B16F10 cells were treated with 100 nmol L–1 α-MSH (Sigma) in DMEM, and then with different concentrations (0.01%, 0.05% and 0.1%) of sebum and negative control DMSO (0.1%) for an additional 48 h. The treated cells were rinsed with D-PBS, and lysed in a 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) containing 5 mmol L–1 ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1% Triton X-100 and protease inhibitor. Lysates were centrifuged at 13 000 relative centrifugal force (RCF) for 20 min at 4 °C, and protein concentration was determined using a Bradford Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with bovine serum albumin as the standard. We added 2 mg mL–1 L-DOPA (0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer, pH 6.) to a certain concentration of protein, followed by a 1-h reaction at 37 °C. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a TECAN microplate reader (TECAN Austria), and tyrosinase activity was measured using the following formula: tyrosinase activity (%) = (OD450 of sample/OD450 of control) × 100.

Statistical analysis

To compare and analyse the visual assessment score, wound area, wound contraction rate and pigmentation area between the two groups, we used a Student’s t-test. In vitro evaluations were performed using ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to compare values between groups. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were reported as mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.1) and the GraphPad Prism 5 package (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Morphological evaluation

The wound contracted more rapidly in the sebum group. Figure 4 shows the wound photos on days 14 and 21. Pigment deposition was observed at these timepoints. On day 14, the wounds in the sebum group appeared more pinkish. On day 21, the control group exhibited deeper pigmentation. In vitro evaluation

To investigate the effect of sebum on melanin synthesis, and the underlying mechanism, we conducted cell viability experiments using B16F10 melanoma cells. Treatment with sebum concentrations of 0.01%, 0.05% and 0.1% did not affect cell viability (Figure 13). When α-MSH was used to induce melanin synthesis in B16F10 melanoma cells, sebum treatment dose-dependently decreased the amount of melanin synthesized compared with α-MSH control (Figure 14). We further examined tyrosinase activity to examine the mechanism by which sebum inhibits melanin synthesis. Discussion

To avoid potential errors arising from varying wound recovery rates among different individuals, in the present study, we created two skin wounds on each mouse, one on each side of the back. The wounds on the right side were treated with artificial sebum, and those on the left were left untreated as controls. We generated chemical burns using 100% TCA to produce basement membrane damage and deep dermal PIH. Visual assessment revealed that sebum treatment yielded faster removal of crusts and wound contraction, compared with controls, although this was not statistically significant. Additionally, sebum-treated wounds exhibited faster basement membrane recovery and fewer melanosomes according to TEM and histological analysis, and less pigmentation observed upon image analysis. These findings may explain why we find more pigmentary disorders on sebum-poor areas of skin. Although the results were not statistically significant, the effects of sebum seemed obvious on gross photographs, and we think the results would be proven in a study including an increased number of mice. Furthermore, our cell study suggested sebum directly inhibited melanogenesis.

Sebum has several functions, including lubricating the skin and hair to shield against friction and retain moisture,9 and acting as a conduit for antimicrobial lipids and anti­oxidants.22 Antioxidants (e.g. coenzyme Q10) and vitamin E are transported to the skin’s surface via sebum.23–25 It also contributes to pheromone production and body odour.26 It has previously been suggested that sebum may play a crucial role in wound healing by influencing macrophage differentiation and activation.27 Oleic acid and linoleic acid have anti-inflammatory properties, and promote monocyte differentiation into activated macrophages. Other lipids – such as squalene, palmitic acid, stearic acid and oleic acid – also influence the secretion of interleukins.27 Sebum may possess proinflammatory properties, and it has recently been shown that in sebocytes, it plays a role in forming lipid droplets within the cell and can also trigger the secretion of proinflammatory enzymes and cytokines, such as interleukin-6 and interleukin-8.28

Although this study did not elucidate the mechanism underlying the effects of sebum on wound healing, a similar study examined the effects of specific oils on wound healing.21 Oils extracted from prickly pear, pumpkin and linseed were effective in treating partial-thickness burns. The authors stated that the high levels of polyunsaturated fatty acids – particularly linoleic acid, which serves as a precursor to arachidonic acid – suggest potential therapeutic benefits for wound healing.

To our knowledge, no prior study has examined the possible effects of sebum on melanogenesis. When sebocytes were co-cultured with melanocytes and then exposed to ultraviolet A, enhancement of melanocyte dendrites and melanogenesis were found.29 However, our results suggested that sebum itself may suppress melanin synthesis by inhibiting tyrosinase activity. It might be questioned how sebum on the skin surface could possibly reach melanocytes in the basal layer, but when there is a wound, there is a defect in the epidermis and dermis, so sebum could reach the melanocytes. In addition, it is well known that topical oils or medicine applied to the skin surface can affect both the epidermis and underlying dermis. Given that sebum is an oil-based substance, it may penetrate these layers. Future studies would be better served by directly examining tyrosinase activity in sebum-applied mouse skin tissue.

PIH is associated with various dermatological conditions, including inflammatory skin disorders, infections, trauma and procedures (e.g. chemical peels, cryotherapy and laser therapy).3 Factors associated with poor prognosis in PIH include darker skin, increased depth of inflammation or melanin pigmentation, severe basement membrane damage and the melanocyte activation level and quantity.3

PIH treatments mainly rely on inhibiting tyrosinase activity. Topical agents include hydroquinone (2–4%), topical retinoids, azelaic acid, soy, arbutin, liquorice extract, ascorbic acid, niacinamide and N-acetyl glucosamine. These treatments inhibit tyrosinase activity, thus suppressing keratinocyte protease-activated receptor-2, or affect copper, the coenzyme for tyrosinase.30,31 Oral remedies include tranexamic acid, which reduces free arachidonic acid and decreases tyrosinase activity.32 Polypodium leucotomos extract exerts antioxidative and immunomodulatory effects that can minimize PIH.33 Physical treatment methods include peeling agents and laser therapy. Superficial peeling agents, such as glycolic acid, salicylic acid, Jessner solution (salicylic acid, lactic acid and resorcinol), 20% TCA and tretinoin are used.30 Laser therapy can be applied using 1064-nm QS Nd:YAG and intense pulsed light.30 However, deep dermal PIH is difficult to treat and usually unresponsive to the aforementioned methods. Therefore, it is important to reduce basement membrane damage and facilitate its recovery to prevent deep dermal PIH.

The sebum group exhibited faster recovery of the basement membrane during wound healing, leading to a more rapid visible wound healing process and a slower onset of melanosome accumulation compared with the control group, although this was not statistically significant. Sebum may facilitate basement membrane recovery, thereby promoting wound healing and potentially reducing PIH occurrence. Additionally, in vitro evaluation revealed that sebum itself has the ability to inhibit tyrosinase activity, thus hindering melanin synthesis. However, this study was conducted on a limited sample size and in nonhuman models, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Further research involving larger, human-based studies is necessary to confirm these results.

These results indicate that it is important to exercise caution during laser therapy or cryotherapy in serum-poor skin areas, to avoid damaging the basement membrane and causing PIH. We hope that these findings will support the development of new wound healing therapies and protective remedies for hyperpigmentation, contributing to the advancement of novel therapeutic strategies.

Differences between mouse skin and human skin can result in different immune responses. Moreover, it can be challenging to precisely control the size, depth and dimensions of TCA-induced wounds in mice, such that this is not a reliable and consistently reproducible wound model. Although artificial human sebum has similar physicochemical properties to human sebum, it has the limitation that its composition is not exactly the same as that of human sebum, which consists of glycerides (30–50%), free fatty acids (15–30%), wax esters (26–30%), squalene (12–20%), cholesterol esters (3.0–6.0%) and cholesterol (1.5–2.5%).34 Additionally, we could not quantitatively assess the extent of basement membrane damage for comparison. Although wound healing in mice primarily relies on contraction, we did not use mechanical splints to prevent it. Also, the number of mice used was too small to make a definitive ­conclusion.
