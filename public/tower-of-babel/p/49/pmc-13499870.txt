Identifying and Comparing Intervention Cost Components in Remote Patient Monitoring: A Scoping Review
Authors: Anna L. Heilig, Wilbert B. van den Hout, M. Elske van den Akker-van Marle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveRemote patient monitoring has shown promising outcomes, such as improved disease awareness and reduced hospital stay. However, assessing the cost of the remote patient monitoring itself is challenging owing to the lack of standardization and transparency in economic evaluations of remote patient monitoring. This review aims to identify and compare the cost components used to estimate the remote patient monitoring intervention costs and the reported challenges in assessing them.MethodsWe performed a scoping review of studies published in PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science that conducted an economic evaluation of a remote patient monitoring between January 2019 and March 2026. Titles and abstracts were screened. Relevant articles were subjected to full-text screening to assess eligibility, and reported cost components were counted based on predefined categories. Difficulties reported by the economic evaluation authors (challenges) were extracted and analyzed using thematic synthesis.ResultsA total of 1282 articles were identified, of which 84 studies met the eligibility criteria. Forty-nine (58%) studies had an observational design, and the remaining studies had a model design. Components of remote patient monitoring intervention costs were reported in 71 (85%) of the included studies, with staff salaries (62%) and equipment/technology (56%) costs being the most frequently reported. The most commonly reported challenge was uncertainty about which remote patient monitoring cost categories to include.ConclusionsThis review highlights the disparities in the assessment and reporting of remote patient monitoring intervention costs in economic evaluations. We offer an updated checklist and guidance for assessing and reporting intervention costs in future economic evaluations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40273-026-01620-5.

Introduction

The development and adoption of digital health interventions (DHIs) was expedited by the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic [1]. Digital health interventions encompass software and technologies designed to achieve health-related goals [2]. These interventions are applied across all levels of healthcare and can aim to promote healthy behaviors, monitor individuals with chronic conditions, or provide remote access to treatments [3]. Given this broad potential of DHIs, it has been suggested that these interventions offer an opportunity to maintain effective disease management in the face of growing healthcare demands due to an aging population and an expected increase in chronic diseases [4, 5]. For example, within the Dutch healthcare system, DHIs are emphasized as a method to mitigate imminent staff shortages [6]. Moreover, DHIs can improve both subjective outcomes (e.g., disease awareness) and societal outcomes (e.g., reduced length of hospital stay) [7–9].

Given the potential of DHIs to improving healthcare, their impact can be assessed by conducting economic evaluations (EEs) [10]. Commonly used methods of EE are a cost-minimization analysis and a cost-effectiveness analysis. In a cost-effectiveness analysis, the associated costs and possibly the effects of the new intervention to those of usual care are compared. These analyses can guide policymakers in implementing more cost-effective healthcare interventions [10]. The interpretability and usefulness of these EEs are contingent on the level of standardization and transparency, and efforts have been made to increase both [11]. However, several literature reviews focusing primarily on the cost effectiveness of remote patient monitoring (RPM) concluded that the outcomes of the included EEs were affected by differences in cost components considered in the RPM interventions [12–14]. For example, Guzman and colleagues emphasized the importance of including capital costs, noting that long-term cost effectiveness may depend on capturing the delayed offset of initial investment costs, though these costs were not always reported [12].

Checklists have been developed with the aims of supporting the assessment of the (intervention) costs, increasing the transparency, and facilitating the comparison between EEs of DHIs [15–17]. These checklists are applicable to the broad spectrum of DHIs, which range from video consultations to real-time monitoring of patient’s health values. The latter are commonly referred to as RPM [18]. Remote patient monitoring is a key driver for the expansion of DHIs [19].

Although RPM interventions share similarities with other DHIs, they also have unique characteristics. Remote patient monitoring is defined as (extramural) remote interventions providing care through monitoring of the patient, which takes place in the patient’s home or in a medical service center (performed by the patient or the healthcare provider). This monitoring uses digital applications (devices, apps, and/or websites) to collect data that are then shared with relevant healthcare providers without requiring direct physical contact with the patient [8]. Where other DHIs can use passive monitoring of the patient, RPM requires active/continuous monitoring of the patient [20]. In addition, RPM has the potential to fundamentally change clinical workflows by continuously collecting health data outside of the traditional setting and reviewing data in real time [9]. Because of these differences between RPM and other DHIs in terms of characteristics and technologies, the existing cost checklists for DHIs might not capture all cost components relevant for inclusion in EEs of RPM. This scoping review aims to give an overview how the intervention costs of RPM are currently assessed in health EEs and the difficulties encountered in their assessment. Furthermore, this review provides guidance on assessing the costs of RPM interventions by presenting a checklist of cost components to consider in EEs of RPM.

Methods

Protocol and Registration

The guidelines of evidence synthesis established by the Joanna Briggs Institute were the methods used for this scoping review [21]. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic review and Meta-analysis extension statement for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist was used to report methods and results [22]. This scoping review was registered in the Open Science Framework (SD482).

Information Sources, Eligibility Criteria, and Literature Search

A comprehensive literature search was developed that included published academic literature on EEs of RPM (see Search 1 in the Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM]). Initial searches were conducted in April 2026 using Embase, PubMed, and Web of Science, covering publications from January 2020 to March 2026. The Boolean operator “AND” was used between the two main concepts of telemedicine and EE, and the Boolean operator “OR” was used for synonyms within these concepts. The concept of telemedicine was searched using the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) telemedicine and monitoring (physiologic) and keywords such as telemedicine, telehealth, and remote sensing technology. Broad terms such as telehealth and telemedicine were used next to RPM because these terms are used interchangeably in the literature [23]. The concept “economic evaluation” was searched using MeSH terms such as cost-benefit analysis and cost-effectiveness analysis, and keywords such as economic evaluation and cost-utility analysis. Search validity was ensured by checking whether relevant articles identified prior to the start were retrieved.

Following our definition of RPM, eligible RPM involved the passive or active transmission of biometric or patient-reported outcome data to clinicians from an extramural situation (e.g., home or medical service center). Digital applications used for monitoring can be devices, apps, and/or websites. The data are then reviewed either automatically or manually by a healthcare professional, and intervened when necessary. Remote patient monitoring had to be used repeatedly (e.g., daily, weekly, or monthly) resulting in multiple measurements for monitoring. Both full and partial EEs were included. Full EEs were defined as studies that compared the costs and effectiveness of at least two interventions (e.g., cost effectiveness, and cost utility). Partial EEs, which included analyses reporting only costs or evaluating an intervention without a comparator, were also eligible. This broad definition allowed for the inclusion of a wide range of study designs, for example, trial-based, model-based, cross-sectional, and case-control studies. The search was limited to English and Dutch articles with EE as a primary or secondary objective. The inclusion of published articles was restricted to the last 5 years, as the development of RPM has accelerated since coronavirus disease 2019 [1]. This has likely contributed to an increase in EEs of RPM, making the past 5 years indicative for assessing how these intervention costs were determined.

Study Selection Process

Literature search results were uploaded to the Covidence Review Manager and used to deduplicate included articles prior to title-abstract screening [24]. Title-abstract screening was performed by one researcher (AH), who checked whether the article met the predefined inclusion criteria. A second independent researcher (EA) double-checked a random subset of 50 articles. During this screening process, only the title and abstract were visible, excluding author and journal information, to minimize the risk of bias. Two researchers (AH, EA) then conducted a full-text review of an initial subset (5%) of the included articles. The remaining full-text articles were reviewed by one researcher (AH). When articles were inaccessible (e.g., behind paywalls), we tried to obtain the article from the authors. Upon completion of the full-text screening, a random subset (20%) of the excluded articles was re-reviewed by a second researcher (EA) to ensure accuracy.

Data Collection, Management, and Synthesis of Results

Prior to data collection, a data extraction form was developed in Excel (CSV) by two researchers (AH, EA), to summarize and record the included and reviewed studies. The extracted data included information on study population, geographical context, RPM type, EE perspective, description of cost assessments and reporting, quantified cost components, currency used with reference year, and difficulties reported by the EE’s authors. For the RPM type, alert-driven and conventional RPM were distinguished. Alert-driven RPM refers to systems using artificial intelligence or automated algorithms to alert professionals (and patients) in case of values exceeding a predefined value. In contrast, conventional RPM refers to a continuous or periodic review by professionals, with/without a scheduled (remote) patient consultation [25].

A review by Dávalos and colleagues was used as a starting point for categorizing cost components because it provided a useful categorization of costs (e.g., fixed vs variable), and distinguished between different stakeholders (provider, patient/client, and other stakeholders). Subsequently, the categories and cost components listed in the checklists of Dávalos and colleagues, Mitchell and colleagues, and Khan and colleagues were combined to create an initial version of the checklist provided by this scoping review [15–17]. Finally, RPM cost components identified from the EEs were added to the checklist. For each cost component the categories adopted by Xu and colleagues were used to report the methods for quantity (volume), price per individual unit (valuation), and adopted costing-approach (bottom-up or top-down) [Table 2 of the ESM] [26]. The top-down costing approach evaluates the cost of an individual service by distributing the total system cost using predefined metrics, and the bottom-up costing approach evaluates the total system cost by identifying and summing the cost of each specific resource used [27].

Information regarding limitations, difficulties, and recommendations related to assessing intervention costs reported in the articles were extracted verbatim to an Excel form. These texts were then analyzed qualitatively using “thematic synthesis” [28]. First, line-by-line free coding was used to scan the extracted text for relevant information and to code its meaning and content sequentially. Second, free codes were organized into related areas by constructing descriptive themes that summarized the main theme of each code while remaining closely aligned with the original content. Third, eventual themes were formed by grouping descriptive themes with similar meanings [28].

Risk of Bias

This scoping review aimed to identify components of intervention costs reported in EEs of RPM, not to assess their cost effectiveness or methodological appropriateness, and therefore no risk of bias was assessed. This is consistent with published guidelines for scoping reviews [22].

Results

Search

The literature search including articles published up to and including March 2026, identified 1285 articles in PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science (Fig. 1). Subsequently, an automated duplicate removal process in Covidence excluded 353 articles, followed by title-abstract screening resulting in 212 remaining articles for full-text screening. After the full-text screening phase, 84 articles met the inclusion criteria. Study Characteristics

Twenty-eight different countries were represented, with the USA and the Netherlands accounting for 14 (17%) and 12 (14%) articles, respectively (Table 1) [25, 29–111]. The characteristics of the included articles are described in Table 3 of the ESM. Forty-eight percent of the articles included patients with cardiovascular disease (n = 40), while the other half included a variety of other disease populations. Among the observational studies (n = 49), 69% adopted a randomized controlled trial design. Among the modeling studies (n = 35), the Markov design was the most prevalent (60%). Most RPM incorporated an alert function (e.g., algorithm-based alerts) [38%] or involved continuous monitoring by professionals (35%), while for a smaller proportion data were only reviewed during scheduled (online) consultations (13%). Regarding the design of the EEs, 83% of the studies conducted a cost-effectiveness analysis. Of the 84 articles, 13 (15%) articles did not describe the assessment and quantification of RPM intervention costs. Of the 71 (85%) articles that reported and quantified intervention costs, 21 (30%) only quantified total RPM intervention costs.

Combining the checklists from the studies by Khan and colleagues [16], Mitchell and colleagues [15], and Dávalos and colleagues [17], resulted in the inclusion of 21 cost components in Table 2. Twelve of these cost components were described in the EEs of RPM interventions. Seven additional cost components were identified: software development, connection fee and subscription costs, patient educational materials, patient support, data housing and transfer, consumables, and travel costs of patients. This resulted in 28 RPM intervention cost components (see Table 2); for their descriptions see Table 5 of the ESM, which is also the final checklist. The majority of the reported cost components concerned healthcare costs. Staff wages were most frequently described (n = 52; 62%) and quantified (n = 35; 42%), followed by equipment/technology (n = 47; 56% and n = 34; 40%, respectively). Most of the reported and quantified cost components were related to the RPM in use. Seven articles (8%) reported costs related to the implementation and four (5%) reported costs related to development of the RPM.

Methods Applied for Assessment of Costs

Table 3 provides an overview of the analytical approaches used to assess the costs, encompassing the quantity (volume), the price per individual unit (valuation) of the cost components, and the costing approach used (bottom-up vs top-down). This information is presented for all articles reporting cost components, with specific details for the two most frequently reported and quantified cost components (staff wages and equipment/technology), that the RPM entailed. For the equipment/technology cost component, the quantity was often inherent to the intervention (82%), for example, one blood pressure cuff per patient. For staff wages, the quantity was either inherent to the intervention (33%), based on assumptions (28%), or sourced from a study/data registry (25%). The valuation method for staff wages was less variable, with the majority of studies (75%) quantifying staff wages using gross wages. For equipment/technology, the invoice price was used in 47% of the EEs to determine the cost per unit. In total, 75 (42%) challenges addressed RPM-specific issues. The most common RPM-specific challenge, which encompassed 31% of these challenges, was related to the level of detail and/or inclusion or exclusion of specific RPM intervention costs. Four themes reflected the potential for future cost reduction: upfront investments, technology development, economies of scale, and reorganization of care pathways. Discussion

Findings and Comparisons with the Literature

Our review of the literature on the reporting of RPM intervention costs in EEs identified 84 articles that reported on 28 distinct cost components. Table 2 provides an overview of which intervention cost components can be considered for comprehensive assessment and reporting in EEs of RPM and is an update to the Dávalos telemedicine checklist [17]. Our review revealed that the inclusion and quantification of the listed cost components in EEs of RPM varied significantly. These findings are consistent with previous literature reviews [12–14, 112]. Thirteen studies (15%) did not describe the assessment or quantify the costs associated with RPM interventions. Eleven of these addressed the cost effectiveness of RPM [42, 52, 55, 60, 66, 67, 84, 91, 93, 94, 100]. This is undesirable, as cost-effectiveness analysis guidelines emphasize including all non-trivial resource costs. Even if intervention costs are low, it is important to state this explicitly. Omitting these costs reduces transparency and comparability, and can lead to misleading outcomes [42, 113]. For studies including RPM costs, it was sometimes questionable whether a full picture of the costs was given. For example, some studies did not include device costs, even though RPM interventions involve the use of devices. This could only be explained for part of them, as the intervention involved a smartphone application or was comparable to standard care. Excluding RPM costs from EEs may make RPM appear more cost effective than traditional on-site care.

In addition to identifying cost components specific to RPM that were not included in the previous checklists [14, 96], this scoping review showed that some cost components listed in the earlier checklists were not reported in the current RPM EEs, despite being included in those checklists. For instance, components such as technician travel for installation and research staff wages were mentioned in previous checklists but missing from the included EEs. This raises questions about their relevance and significance for RPM, or about potential challenges related to feasibility and measurement. Furthermore, cost components, such as on-site implementation and software development costs, were infrequently reported in the included EEs. Although these cost components were incorporated in previous checklists, these checklists did not explicitly provide operational guidance on how to measure or allocate these costs in EEs [16, 17]. As Khan and colleagues noted, the decision to include these costs depends on the EE’s perspective, time horizon, and comparator [16]. For DHIs, omitting implementation and development costs may underestimate total intervention costs, given their substantial upfront costs [114–116]. Beyond Khan et al.’s considerations, whether these costs are considered may also depend on whether the intervention was developed externally (e.g., vendor) or internally (e.g., research team). The costs of an externally developed and implemented intervention may be included as embedded in the service fee charged by the developer. To enhance transparency, we recommend describing the activities and resources (investment costs) covered by this fee for externally developed RPM (see Table 4, RPM-specific challenge 2) [117]. For internally developed RPM, we recommend explicitly reporting the development and implementation activities undertaken, depending on the scope, perspective, time horizon, and comparator. For example, if an intervention still needs to be developed and implemented, these costs should be included and distributed across the relevant patient population over the appropriate time horizon. For DHIs (except for those involving complex devices), intervention costs are generally marginal when scaling up [118]. Including these economies of scale benefits has been identified as an RPM-specific challenge and has been emphasized in the literature (see Table 4, RPM-specific challenge 3) [16, 112, 119]. When assessing this impact, we recommend distinguishing between fixed (one-time) and variable (additional) costs. Last, the adopted perspective influences the inclusion of cost components. From a healthcare/provider perspective, costs should reflect those incurred by the provider. From a societal perspective, however, costs should reflect those incurred by all stakeholders. For example, from a societal perspective, patient time costs and productivity losses associated with RPM use should be included because RPM use may burden patients [120], especially when active monitoring is required. This review only included productivity losses and travel costs as a cost component if these were directly related to the RPM intervention.

The importance of conducting iterative cost analyses has also been emphasized [16, 121] and was mentioned as a challenge (see Table 4, RPM-specific challenge 4). LeFèvre and colleagues outlined methods and considerations for iterative cost analyses at different stages of DHI maturity [121]. Research suggests that technological advances, such as replacing conventional RPM with alert-driven RPM, may reduce costs [25]. Given that staff wages were the most frequently reported cost component, highlighting their substantial cost contribution, the impact of such advances should be studied iteratively. Our checklist and recommendations can supplement LeFèvre et al.’s framework by providing guidance on which RPM cost components to include and how to include them.

Limitations and Strengths

This review has several limitations. First, the inclusion of articles was limited to those published within the last 5 years and available in Dutch and English, potentially missing other relevant articles. However, the recent rapid development of RPM suggests that the review captures most of the current technologies [1]. Second, the exclusion of gray literature could introduce publication bias by omitting null or negative results typically found in such literature [122]. Word count restrictions do not apply to gray literature, possibly increasing the details of cost reporting. Third, a single researcher primarily conducted the title-abstract and full-text screening, which increased the risk of selection bias and the unintentional omission of relevant studies. To mitigate this risk, a second researcher verified a random sample of included and excluded studies in both screening phases. Fourth, although a distinction was made between bottom-up and top-down costing, a more granular classification into micro-costing and gross-costing was not performed [123]. Finally, no quality appraisal of the reported and quantified cost components, methods, and cost approaches was performed. This is an inherent limitation of scoping reviews, as the variety of included study designs makes a systematic critical appraisal of the evidence unfeasible [22].

Strengths of the study were the use of predefined eligibility criteria, which limited the risk of selection bias and which was further mitigated by two researchers independently searching and selecting articles [124]. Additionally, conducting the search in multiple databases reduced the risk of missing eligible EEs [125]. Because of the diversity of study designs, geographical areas, diseases, and types of RPM, our results are widely applicable. Finally, we provided suggestions on how to address the reported RPM-specific challenges in EEs.

Future Implications

Remote patient monitoring is increasingly centralized in monitoring centers. For example, in the Mayo Clinic’s RPM program, professionals review data and provide healthcare from RPM targeting different health conditions [126]. These structural changes to healthcare delivery may affect the cost effectiveness. The EE by Padula et al. included an RPM monitored by a monitoring center, but the center’s costs were considered sunk costs [36]. However, staff, electricity usage, and maintenance of such a center are examples of variable costs that should be included. Future research should provide guidance on incorporating these costs into EEs (Table 4, RPM-specific challenge 5). For instance, these costs could be divided by the number of patients or minutes of use of the monitoring center, like operating room cost calculations [127].

Furthermore, the included EEs recommended that future research should focus on identifying the cost components that drive the cost effectiveness of RPM (Table 4, RPM-specific challenge 6). Our updated checklist for RPM is a first step in this direction. We recommend validating this checklist in forthcoming EEs and assessing its face validity with key stakeholders, such as patients, healthcare providers, and developers of RPM interventions [128].

Conclusions

This review highlighted the disparities in the assessment and reporting of RPM intervention costs in EEs. We offered an updated overview of intervention cost components for future assessment and reporting. Recommendations were provided on how to deal with costs of internally versus externally developed interventions, economies of scale, iterative cost analysis, and assessing costs of monitoring centers. Future research should focus on identifying additional intervention cost components for patients and other stakeholders. These efforts will improve the standardization and detail of EEs of RPM.
