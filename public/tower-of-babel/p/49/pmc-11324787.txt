The significance of upper glycolytic components in regulating retinal pigment epithelial cellular behavior
Authors: Armaan Naghdi, Nicole Oska, Thangal Yumnamcha, Shaimaa Eltanani, Mohamed Shawky, Rao Me, Ahmed S Ibrahim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCell adhesion to the extracellular matrix and its natural outcome of cell spreading, along with the maintenance of barrier activity, are essential behaviors of epithelial cells, including retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). Disruptions in these characteristics can result in severe vision-threatening diseases such as diabetic macular edema and age-related macular degeneration. However, the precise mechanisms underlying how RPE cells regulate their barrier integrity and cell spreading are not fully understood. This study aims to elucidate the relative importance of upper glycolytic components in governing these cellular behaviors of RPE cells. Electric Cell-Substrate Impedance Sensing (ECIS) technology was utilized to assess in real-time the effects of targeting various upper glycolytic enzymes on RPE barrier function and cell spreading by measuring cell resistance and capacitance, respectively. Specific inhibitors used included WZB117 for Glut1 inhibition, Lonidamine for Hexokinase inhibition, PFK158 for PFKFB3/PFK axis inhibition, and TDZD-8 for Aldolase inhibition. Additionally, the viability of RPE cells was evaluated using a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) cytotoxicity assay. The most significant decrease in electrical resistance and increase in capacitance of RPE cells were observed due to dose-dependent inhibition of Glut1 using WZB117, as well as Aldolase inhibition with TDZD-8. LDH level analysis at 24–72 h post-treatment with WZB117 (1 and 10 μM) or TDZD-8 (1 μM) showed no significant difference compared to the control, indicating that the disruption of RPE functionality was not attributed to cell death. Lastly, inhibition of other upper glycolytic components, including PFKFB3/PFK with PFK158 or Hexokinase with Lonidamine, did not significantly affect RPE cell behavior. This study provides insights into the varied roles of upper glycolytic components in regulating the functionality of RPE cells. Specifically, it highlights the critical roles of Glut1 and Aldolase in preserving barrier integrity and promoting RPE cell adhesion and spreading. Such understanding will guide the development of safe interventions to treat RPE cell dysfunction in various retinal disorders.Keywords: Electric cell-substrate impedance sensing (ECIS), Age-related macular degeneration (AMD), Glycolysis, Resistance, Barrier integrity, Capacitance, Cell adhesion, Cell spreadingSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biophysics

Introduction

The retina is a highly metabolically active tissue that relies on rich vascular circulations for support. Approximately 80% of its blood supply originates from the choriocapillaris, sustaining the intense activity of the outer retina, particularly the photoreceptors. The retinal pigmented epithelium (RPE) forms a monolayer of cells on Bruch’s membrane, positioned between the choriocapillaris and photoreceptors. Adhesion of RPE cells to the extracellular matrix in Bruch’s membrane and their spreading are fundamental behaviors of RPE cells necessary for forming a monolayer to maintain retinal integrity and function [reviewed in1]. Additionally, the RPE, along with tight junctions between neighboring RPE cells, constitutes the outer blood-retinal barrier (oBRB)2. Under physiological conditions, the RPE barrier strictly regulates the entry of nutrients, ions, proteins, and water into the retina while efficiently eliminating metabolic waste. Maintaining this barrier is vital for a stable microenvironment and normal retinal function3. Any disruption of the RPE barrier can result in severe eye diseases, such as diabetic macular edema (DME) and age-related macular degeneration (AMD). In DME, impaired RPE barrier function allows blood to leak through the compromised barrier, causing excessive fluid influx into the retina and vision loss4. Similarly, in neovascular AMD, the abnormal growth of choroidal blood vessels penetrates the compromised RPE layer to extends into the subretinal space between the RPE and photoreceptor. This process leads to an excessive influx of fluid into the retina, often accompanied by hemorrhage, and resulting in blindness5. Therefore, substantial research efforts have been directed toward understanding the operational mechanisms that preserve the cellular functionality of RPE cells under normal conditions. In recent years, there has been a notable focus on glucose homeostasis in this context.

The maintenance of glucose homeostasis relies on intricate interactions between mitochondrial processes—the Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation (OxPhos)—and the cytoplasmic process of glycolysis. Glycolysis, a highly conserved metabolic pathway in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, operates independently of oxygen or compartmentalization, distinguishing it from the Krebs cycle and OxPhos6. Serving as the primary pathway for metabolites' introduction for energy, glycolysis was elucidated by Nobel laureates Embden, Meyerhof, and Parnas by detailing the metabolic steps that convert glucose into ATP7. Following their model, glycolysis can be segmented into two parts: the upper glycolysis, involving an initial ATP investment as glucose is broken down into two trioses, glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate (GA3P) and dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP), and the lower glycolysis, where ATP is produced (Fig. 1). While the RPE relies on mitochondria for its cellular functionality8, there is limited understanding of the role of glycolysis in regulating RPE cellular behavior. Particularly, the involvement of components in the upper segment of glycolysis is of interest, as disruptions in this region have been associated with the diversion of glycolytic metabolites into harmful pathways, including the polyol, hexosamine, PKC, and AGEs pathways [reviewed in9].

The influx of glucose into the RPE through its transporters servs as the initial rate-limiting step in glucose metabolism and glycolysis. Glucose transporter (Glut)-1 plays a pivotal role as the primary glucose transporter in the RPE, facilitating the transport of glucose into the outer retina10. The recent elucidation of the crystal structure of human Glut111 enabled the discovery of the selective Glut1 inhibitor WZB11712. Nevertheless, the significance of Glut1 in regulating the cellular functionality of RPE under physiological conditions still requires further investigation.

The key regulated steps of upper glycolysis involve reactions catalyzed by Hexokinase and phosphofructokinase (PFK). Hexokinase, as the first enzyme in upper glycolysis, plays a crucial role by phosphorylating glucose into glucose-6-phosphate—an important step in glucose metabolism13. The crystal structure of Hexokinase has been elucidated, leading to the discovery of Lonidamine14,15. Lonidamine has been used as a tool to study the role of Hexokinases in diverse cellular behaviors16. However, the specific contribution of Hexokinases to the regulation of the cellular functionality of the RPE remains largely unexplored.

The second crucial enzyme in upper glycolysis is PFK, and its activity is regulated by PFKFB3 (6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase), a key enzyme that modulates the level of fructose-2,6-bisphosphate (F2,6BP), an allosteric activator of PFK17–19. PFK158 is a widely recognized inhibitor of PFKFB3, and has been utilized to investigate the role of the PFK/PFKFB3 axis in influencing cellular behavior20. While PFKFB3 has been identified as a significant player in regulating the barrier functionality and cell spreading in human retinal endothelial cells, major components of the inner BRB21, its importance in controlling the cellular behavior of RPE cells of the oBRB remains largely unknown.

The final component in upper glycolysis is Aldolase, which regulates the flow of metabolites between the upper and lower segments of glycolysis by facilitating the reversible cleavage of F1,6BP, produced by PFK, into three-carbon molecules. These molecules can then traverse the lower segment of glycolysis to produce ATP7,22. TDZD-8 (1,2,4-thiadiazolidine-3,5-dione)-8 is recognized for its potential inhibitory effects on Aldolase enzymes, as it covalently modifies the enzyme's active cysteine residue at position 239, influencing various cellular processes dependent on glycolysis23. Despite these findings, there remains limited understanding of the role of Aldolase in regulating the cellular behavior of RPE cells.

As electric cell-substrate impedance sensing (ECIS) technology proves to be a valuable tool for real-time monitoring of the behavior of barrier-forming cells, such as RPE cells24–26, we employed this technology in the current study to explore the effects of upper glycolysis inhibition on the cellular functionality of RPE cells. ECIS utilizes a continuous alternating current (AC) of 1 µA, facilitating the breakdown of overall impedance into barrier resistance and cell capacitance. Resistance represents cell barrier functions, while capacitance indicates the extent of cell layer coverage on the substrate. Our previous research using ECIS demonstrated that different mitochondrial components play differential roles in maintaining RPE cell functionality, with the uncoupling of the electron transport chain from OxPhos being a major factor in the disruption of barrier function27. In this current study, we further employed this ECIS modeling to assess the impact of inhibiting upper glycolysis on RPE cell barrier function and cell spreading across the substrate surface. Our goal is to provide valuable insights into the bioenergetic controls that regulate the integrity of RPE cells and, consequently, the oBRB.

Materials and methods

ECIS experiment

We investigated the impact of different inhibitors targeting upper glycolytic components on retinal RPE cellular behaviors by monitoring overall cellular Z using ECIS®Zθ technology (Applied Biophysics Inc., Troy, NY, USA), as previously described25,27. The selected inhibitors were: WZB117 (a Glut1 inhibitor), Lonidamine (a Hexokinase inhibitor), PFK158 (a phosphofructokinase inhibitor), and TDZD-8 (an Aldolase inhibitor). To start the experiment, a 96-well special array (96W20idf PET; Applied Biophysics Inc.) underwent treatment with 100 µM cysteine (50 µL/well; Applied Biophysics) for 30 min, followed by aspiration. Cysteine was used to clean and modify the electrode surfaces, thereby enhancing experimental repeatability and minimizing variation between ECIS wells, as recommended by the manufacturer. Subsequently, the array was coated with 0.02% gelatin (50 µL/well; Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA) for 30 min and then aspirated. Then human retinal pigmented epithelial cell line (ARPE-19) obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; CRL-2302) were seeded in (DMEM/F-12, Corning-10-090-CV) with 10% Fetal bovine serum (FBS, Corning-35011CV) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (PS; Hyclone- SV30010). Upon reaching confluence with a capacitance < 20 nF, the culture media were replaced with serum-free media for 8–12 h before applying different concentrations (1 and 10 µM) of aforementioned inhibitors. Then, the impedance of the ARPE-19 monolayer was assessed over time and frequency by applying a 1 µA AC to the electrode array at the base of the 96-well plate. Nine frequencies ranging from 250 to 64,000 Hz were employed to measure the impedance. The normalized impedance value at each time point was obtained by dividing the raw impedance value at each time point by the impedance value obtained before treatment with glycolytic inhibitors and plotted against time. Then the ECIS software calculated the capacitance and the resistance across the ARPE-19 monolayer based on the measured impedance, with data points collected either at the end or throughout the experiment represented by the area under the curve (AUC).

Assessment of RPE cells viability

We determined the impact of different concentrations of the four upper glycolytic inhibitors on ARPE-19 cell viability using the lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) Cytotoxicity Assay (CyQUANT™; Invitrogen-C20300, Waltham, MA, USA). The amount of LDH released into the medium was determined according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons among the experimental groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). To address multiple comparisons, the False Discovery Rate (FDR) was controlled using the two-stage linear step-up procedure of Benjamin, Krieger, and Yekitieli, with a threshold of < 0.05. P-values were visually depicted as follows: * for p ≤ 0.05, ** for p ≤ 0.01, *** for p ≤ 0.001, and **** for p ≤ 0.0001. The figure graphs provided the number of biological replicates for each analysis.

Results

Real-time monitoring of the effect of various upper glycolytic components on RPE cell behavior using ECIS

Given that impaired barrier function of RPE cells contributes to the pathogenesis of multiple retinal diseases1, we conducted bioimpedance analysis in vitro using ECIS technology to investigate whether different upper glycolytic components play varying roles in maintaining RPE barrier function. Selected inhibitors included DMSO (as a vehicle, Fig. 2A), WZB117 for Glut1 inhibition (Fig. 2B,C), Lonidamine to inhibit Hexokinases (Fig. 2D,E), PFK158 to inhibit the PFKFB3/PFK axis (Fig. 2F,G), and TDZD-8 to inhibit Aldolases (Fig. 2H,I). Two different concentrations of these glycolytic inhibitors were introduced to RPE cells when they reached a confluent monolayer, indicated by a plateau in the y-axis in the 3D model (black arrows in Fig. 2). Thereafter, barrier integrity was continuously monitored by measuring resistance over time (the z-axis in the 3D model) across a frequency range between 250 and 64,000 Hz (the x-axis in the 3D model). Visual representations of these 3D models (Fig. 2A–I) revealed that higher concentrations of these inhibitors (10 µM), except Lonidamine, led to dramatic reductions in the corresponding resistance curves and thus compromised barrier integrity of RPE cells compared to the vehicle-treated control group. However, at a lower concentration (1 µM) of these inhibitors, WZB117 and TDZD8 exhibited the most significant decrease in resistance (Fig. 2B,H), whereas Lonidamine and PFK158 treatments (Fig. 2D,F, respectively) did not show a clear reduction in resistance compared to the control group in Fig. 2A. These observations underline the distinct roles of Glut1, Aldolase, Hexokinase, and PFK in maintaining RPE barrier function. Effects of Glut1 inhibition on RPE cell behavior

The maintenance of barrier activity and cell spreading are key characteristics of epithelial cells, including RPE cells. These behaviors can be dynamically monitored in real-time by measuring resistance and capacitance, respectively, serving as specific indicators of cell behavior. Capacitance reflects cell spreading over the substrate, while resistance describes cell–cell junction strength, cell morphology, and cell–matrix adherence25. We initially examined in Fig. 3A the effect of pharmacological inhibition of Glut1, using WZB117, on the barrier integrity of RPE cells through monitoring the resistance across RPE monolayers. An optimum frequency corresponding to the maximum peak resistance was selected and found to be 4000 Hz, according to our previous publication25. At that frequency, WZB117-treatment resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in the RPE cellular resistance at the end of the experiment (Fig. 3B) as well as throughout the experimental period (Fig. 3C), indicated by a reduction in the area under the curve (AUC), without affecting cell viability (Fig. 4). These data indicate that inhibition of Glut1 transporter irreversibly impairs the barrier function of RPE cells in a dose dependent manner. Additionally, it is noteworthy that at the 24, 48, and 72-h intervals (Fig. 4A–C, respectively), only PFK158 and TDZD-8 (10 μM) caused a significant increase in LDH release, indicating cytotoxicity, while PFK158 (1 μM), TDZD-8 (1 μM), and Lonidamine (1 and 10 μM) did not exhibit any cytotoxic effects.

Next, we examined the impact of Glut1 inhibition on the spreading of RPE cells over the substrate through monitoring the capacitance across RPE monolayers. A frequency of 64,000 Hz was selected based on our previous publication25, which represents the maximized extent of cell spreading over the electrode. Given that capacitance decreases inversely with spreading extent, assessing the effect of inhibiting Glut1 on RPE cell spreading becomes crucial once the cells reach a state of electrical quiescence. We defined this electrical quiescence as the point at which cells attained stable minimum plateaus in capacitance curves after being placed on the electrode (Black arrow in Fig. 5A). As depicted in Fig. 5A, the capacitance of RPE cells accelerated in a dose-dependent manner following WZB117 treatment. Moreover, the capacitance of RPE cells treated with WZB117 (10 µM) exhibited a characteristic behavior of being increased from baseline to reach a temporary maximum, decelerated for a few hours, and then steadily increased for the remainder of the experiment (Fig. 5A). These alterations reflect dynamic changes in the spreading movement of RPE cells over the substrate in response to Glut1 inhibition. At the experimental endpoint, WZB117 increased capacitance dose-dependently (Fig. 5B). Additionally, the AUC was calculated for each capacitance curve to assess the effects of WZB117 on capacitance throughout the experiment's duration. As illustrated in Fig. 5C, the AUCs significantly distinct across each WZB117-treated group, indicating that Glut1 inhibition impacts the capacitance of RPE cells and hence the cell spreading dose-dependently.

Assessing the impact of inhibiting Hexokinase on RPE cell behavior

Here, we proceeded to investigate the effects of inhibiting Hexokinase on RPE cell behavior utilizing the Hexokinase inhibitor, Lonidamine. First, we evaluated Lonidamine's influence on the barrier function of RPE cells by monitoring the resistance response over time at 4000 Hz. As illustrated in Fig. 6A, there were no notable reductions observed in the resistance curves at the end of the experiment (Fig. 6B) or throughout its duration (Fig. 6C) following treatment with 1 or 10 μM of Lonidamine. Second, we examined Lonidamine's effects on the spreading behavior of RPE cells over the substrate through monitoring the capacitance over time across the RPE monolayers at a frequency of 64,000 Hz (Fig. 7A). Following the treatment with either concentration of Lonidamine (1 or 10 μM), the capacitance of RPE cells did not exhibit significant changes at the end of the experiment (Fig. 7B). Additionally, we calculated the AUC for each capacitance curve to assess Lonidamine's effect on capacitance throughout the experiment. As depicted in Fig. 7C, the AUCs for both Lonidamine groups remained insignificantly different from the control. These findings provide evidence that Hexokinase does not play a significant regulatory role in the behavior of RPE cells.

The effect of PFKFB3/PFK axis on RPE cell behavior

Next, we investigated the effects of inhibiting PFKFB3/PFK axis on RPE cell behavior through using the PFKFB3 inhibitor, PFK158. First, we determined the effect of PFK158 on the RPE barrier functionality by measuring the resistance response over time at 4000 Hz. As shown in Fig. 8A, treating RPE cells with PFK158 resulted in a decline in resistance only at a higher concentration (10 µM), where PFK158 was reaching toxic levels depicted in Fig. 4. At this concentration, it took 5–10 h for the resistance to sharply decrease to a minimum level before plateauing over time. Despite the reduction in both endpoint resistance values shown in Fig. 8B and the behavior of resistance over the experimental period as depicted in Fig. 8C, a non-toxic concentration (1 µM) of PFK158, albeit higher than the IC50 of approximately 0.2 µM, resulted in a modest (~ 6–10%) yet significant decrease in resistance throughout the experimental period, as shown in Fig. 8C, but not at its end, as depicted in Fig. 8B. This suggests that the impact of inhibiting PFKFB3/PFK on reducing the barrier integrity of RPE cells is a reversible process.

Next, we assessed the capacitance across the RPE monolayer at 64,000 Hz to determine if inhibiting PFKFB3/PFK affects the spreading behavior of RPE cells on the substrate. Effects of aldolase inhibition on RPE cell behavior

We initially examined in Fig. 10A the effect of pharmacological inhibition of Aldolase, using TDZD-8, on the barrier integrity of RPE cells by monitoring the resistance across RPE monolayers at 4000 Hz. Treating RPE cells with TDZD-8 resulted in a decline in resistance, particularly evident at a higher concentration (10 µM) where TDZD-8 reached toxic levels (Fig. 4), leading to a sharp decrease in resistance over approximately 5 h before plateauing over time. Even at a non-toxic concentration (1 µM) of TDZD-8, there was a modest (~ 6–10%) yet significant decrease in resistance at the end of the experiment (Fig. 10B) as well as throughout its entire period (Fig. 10C), indicating the involvement of Aldolase in maintaining RPE cell barrier integrity.

Evaluating the viability of RPE cells treated with various upper glycolytic inhibitors

To ensure that the observed impacts of glycolytic inhibitors on the behavior of RPE cells were not attributed to cytotoxicity, we conducted a cytotoxicity assay across all RPE cell groups, measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release within the 24–72-h post-treatment window. Results showed that only PFK158 and TDZD-8 (10 μM) induced a significant increase in LDH release at the 24-h, 48-h, and 72-h marks, indicating cytotoxicity, whereas WZB117 (1 and 10 μM), TDZD-8 (1 μM), and PFK158 (1 μM) did not manifest any cytotoxic effects (Fig. 4A–C). This contrasts with earlier observations (Figs. 3A, 5A), where substantial changes in total RPE resistance and capacitance, respectively, were already apparent in both WZB117 groups and the TDZD-8 (1 μM) group within the same 24–72-h timeframe (Figs. 10A, 11A for resistance and capacitance, respectively). These findings collectively suggest that the disruption of RPE cell barrier integrity is an early event triggered by WZB117 and Aldolase inhibition, preceding any noticeable effects on cell viability.

Discussion

The key findings of this study are that the components involved in upper glycolysis exhibit distinct effects on RPE cellular behaviors, notably emphasizing the critical roles of Glut1 and Aldolase in regulating both barrier functionality and spreading activity of RPE cells. The following findings support this conclusion: firstly, inhibiting Glut1 with WZB117 and aldolase with TDZD-8 resulted in the most significant reduction in electrical resistance, indicating barrier disruption, and the most significant increase in RPE cell capacitance, indicating compromised cell spreading and adhesion. On the other hand, inhibition of PFKFB3/PFK with PFK158 or hexokinase with lonidamine did not notably affect electrical resistance or capacitance. Secondly and importantly, the disruption of RPE barrier function and cell spreading resulting from Glut1 or Aldolase inhibition were not attributable to cell death, as evidenced by the absence of elevated levels of released LDH within 24–72 h post-treatment of their respective inhibitors. To our knowledge, this study represents the first demonstration of such temporal relationships between RPE cellular behaviors, encompassing the maintenance of barrier activity and cell spreading, in response to inhibitors targeting different upper glycolytic components using ECIS technology alongside its mathematical modeling system.

Studies have shown that RPE cells express glucose transporters from the SLC2A family, which are facilitative hexose transporters. Among the 12 members of the Glut family, RPE cells primarily express Glut1, with lower expression levels of Glut 12. Additionally, RPE cells express low levels of the Na+-glucose-linked transporter SGLT-1, suggesting that glucose enters RPE cells mainly through Glut110. Our findings indicate that inhibiting Glut1 with WZB117 in RPE cells significantly affects their cellular behavior, including epithelial barrier functionality and spreading coverage, in a dose-dependent manner. This could be attributed to Glut1 inhibition decreasing glucose uptake and glycolysis, resulting in reduced pyruvate levels and, consequently, mitochondrial ATP depletion, impacting these ATP-dependent cellular behaviors. Conversely, other RPE functions, such as transepithelial potential (monitored by recording the c-wave in ERG) and RPE differentiation, have been reported not to be disrupted by genetic deletion of Glut110. Overall, our results highlight the importance of Glut1 in controlling RPE cellular functionality, particularly those related to barrier functionality and cellular spreading.

It's noteworthy that while Hexokinase plays a pivotal role in glycolysis regulation, inhibiting it with Lonidamine in RPE cells did not yield significant changes in cell behavior. In support, a similar trend was observed in human retinal endothelial cells (HRECs) when treated with Lonidamine; they exhibited non-significant changes to their cellular behavior24. This suggests that cells forming the retinal barrier, including RPE cells, might utilize alternative pathways to circumvent Hexokinase inhibition. One potential pathway involves glycogen hydrolysis by glycogen phosphorylase, generating glucose 1-phosphate (G1P), which continues to sustain glycolysis via phosphoglucomutase conversion to G6P28. RPE cells have been found to hold glycogen reserves29, serving as an energy reservoir during periods of increased energy demand or restricted nutrient availability, as seen in conditions like diabetic retinopathy and AMD30,31. Together, our findings underscore the ability of RPE cells to maintain their barrier functions and cellular spreading even in the presence of Hexokinase inhibition, revealing insights into their adaptive metabolic mechanisms in response to Hexokinase inhibition.

Next, our findings revealed an intriguing pattern: inhibiting PFKFB3/PFK, a pivotal step in glycolysis, with PFK158 (1 µM), initially compromised the barrier integrity of the cells. However, with time, the cells managed to overcome these effects. This stands in contrast to HRECs forming the inner blood-retinal barrier (BRB), where inhibition of PFKFB3/PFK leads to irreversible damage to the barrier24. It is suggested that targeting PFKFB3/PFK slows down glycolysis and ATP production, thereby affecting ATP-dependent cellular processes, including those responsible for maintaining epithelial barrier integrity32. This could explain the initial decrease in RPE resistance by PFK158. Nonetheless, restoring ATP levels through non-carbohydrate alternative pathways, such as glutamine-fueled mitochondrial ATP production [where RPE cells predominantly rely on mitochondria33 compared to HRECs, which primarily rely on glycolysis34], can rebalance ATP levels. Glutamine undergoes conversion to glutamate and enters the tricarboxylic acid cycle, where it undergoes oxidation to produce reducing equivalents (NADH and FADH2) that fuel the electron transport chain. This process ultimately leads to the phosphorylation of ADP to ATP in the mitochondria, thereby replenishing cellular ATP levels35. This likely elucidates the observed restoration of barrier integrity in RPE cells following prolonged PFKFB3/PFK inhibition. These findings indicate a dynamic interplay between PFKFB3/PFK inhibition and the restoration of normal RPE barrier functionality.

Interestingly, our results showed that treatment of RPE cells with a non-toxic dose of an Aldolase inhibitor (TDZD-8) compromised both the barrier integrity and spreading coverage of the cells. It has been suggested that inhibiting Aldolase enzymatic activity leads to an imbalance in glycolysis, wherein upper glycolysis outpaces lower glycolysis, resulting in the accumulation of F1,6BP and depletion of its hydrolyzable product, GA3P36. Since GA3P serves as a crucial substrate for lower glycolysis, its decrease slows down the velocity of lower glycolysis and ATP production37, thereby impacting ATP-dependent cellular processes responsible for maintaining epithelial barrier integrity and spreading coverage. Our findings underscore the pivotal role of Aldolase in maintaining normal RPE cellular behavior, shedding light on its significance in both clinical and pre-clinical contexts. Notably, anti-Aldolase antibodies have been detected in the sera of patients with wet AMD, along with decreased Aldolase expression in the RPE of aging mice38. Similarly, anti-Aldolase C IgG has been identified in rabbit serum following laser-induced injury to the RPE39.

In summary, this study provides insights into the dynamic involvement of upper glycolytic components in regulating the functionality of ARPE-19 cells under normal physiological conditions. Specifically, it underlines the critical roles of Glut1 and Aldolase in preserving barrier integrity and enhancing ARPE-19 cell coverage. However, the relevance of these findings in other independent RPE sources, such as iPSC RPE or primary RPE, needs to be further investigated. Future studies targeting glycolytic pathways in retinal RPE cells across various retinopathies should consider the relative importance of the glycolytic components highlighted in this research. Understanding their contributions to maintaining the normal functions of RPE cells under normal conditions will facilitate the development of safe, targeted interventions to address dysfunctional RPE cells in retinal disorders while minimizing any adverse effects on normal RPE cells.
