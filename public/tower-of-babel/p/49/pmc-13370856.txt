Molecular Mechanisms of KIT Receptor Dimerization and Oncogenic Activation Revealed by Multiscale Simulations
Authors: Maria Gabriella Chiariello, Akash Deep Biswas, Carmen Gratteri, Ingrid Guarnetti Prandi, Siewert-Jan Marrink, Andrea Beccari, Carmine Talarico
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The receptor tyrosine kinase KIT is essential for cell proliferation and differentiation, and its aberrant activation contributes to cancers such as acute myeloid leukemia and gastrointestinal stromal tumors. KIT activation normally requires stem cell factor (SCF)-induced dimerization, whereas oncogenic mutations can drive ligand-independent signaling. Here, we employ multiscale molecular dynamics simulations based on the Martini 3 coarse-grained (CG) force field to investigate the dimerization mechanisms of full-length KIT in both wild-type (WT) and oncogenic mutant forms within a realistic membrane environment. For the WT receptor, simulations capture SCF-driven dimerization through a cooperative “zipper-like” mechanism involving sequential domain–domain interactions. In contrast, the T417I, Δ418–419 mutant forms stable dimers spontaneously in the absence of SCF, stabilized by enhanced D5–D5 hydrophobic contacts. Back-mapping and atomistic refinement yielded the first all-atom (AA) structural model of the full-length KIT (T417I, Δ418–419) mutant, consistent with the V-shaped extracellular conformation observed experimentally. This study provides mechanistic insight into KIT activation and establishes a Martini 3-based framework for simulating receptor assembly and guiding the design of antibodies or small molecules that disrupt aberrant dimerization.

Introduction

KIT protein kinase, also known as CD117 or stem cell factor receptor, is a key member of the receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) family, which plays a critical role in various cellular processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, survival, and migration. − KIT is encoded by the KIT gene and is expressed in a variety of cell types, including hematopoietic stem cells, melanocytes, and germ cells. , The activation of KIT occurs when it binds to its ligand, stem cell factor (SCF), which induces the dimerization of the receptor. − This dimerization event is followed by autophosphorylation, a process where the intracellular tyrosine kinase domain of one KIT molecule phosphorylates the other, creating binding sites for downstream signaling molecules. − The activated KIT receptor then triggers multiple signaling pathways, such as the MAPK,> PI3K/AKT, and JAK/STAT pathways, which regulate cell proliferation, survival, and migration. − This precise control of cell signaling allows KIT to play essential roles in development and tissue homeostasis.

While KIT is essential for normal cell function, mutations in the KIT gene can result in constitutive activation of the kinase, even in the absence of its ligand. , These mutations are often found in a variety of cancers, including gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs), melanoma, and acute myeloid leukemia (AML). , Due to the central role of KIT in these cancers, the receptor has become a target for therapeutic intervention. , Several small-molecule inhibitors have been developed to block KIT kinase activity and are used to treat KIT-driven malignancies. − However, resistance to these drugs can emerge over time, necessitating the development of new strategies to combat these mutations. −

Another promising approach to inhibiting kinase activation involves the use of monoclonal antibodies that target and block the dimerization process. , In this strategy, antibodies are designed to bind to specific regions of the receptor, preventing it from dimerizing and becoming activated. This approach is similar to strategies used in other cancers, such as HER-positive breast cancer, where monoclonal antibodies like trastuzumab and pertuzumab are used in combination to inhibit receptor dimerization and block downstream signaling pathways. −

To design effective therapies, it is essential to understand the mechanisms of dimerization and the driving forces that stabilize the dimeric structure of KIT. The interactions at the dimerization interface, which bring the extracellular domains of the receptor together, are crucial for proper activation. Any disruptions to this interface, whether through mutation or drug binding, can alter KIT’s function and affect its ability to drive oncogenesis. Experimental techniques like X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) have provided valuable insights into the protein regions responsible for dimer formation and stability in both wild-type (WT) and oncogenic mutant forms of KIT. ,− These high-resolution structures pave the way for the application of molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, enabling the investigation of structural flexibility, stability, and functional mechanisms of KIT. −

While several all-atom (AA) MD studies have focused on the intracellular kinase domains, offering insights into conformational dynamics and interactions with drugs, simulating the spontaneous dimerization of full-length KIT in its membrane environment presents significant challenges. This is due to the system’s size and the long time scales involved. To address this, coarse-grained (CG) models can be employed. In this study, we utilize coarse-grained simulations based on the Martini 3 force field to model the spontaneous dimerization of full-length KIT kinase in a membrane environment. The recent advancements in the Martini 3 force field have significantly improved the accuracy of CG simulations for proteins, lipids, and small molecules. − These improvements enable the study of processes such as molecular recognition, binding events, and structural rearrangements on larger time scales. − Martini 3 is especially well-suited for studying large protein aggregates in their realistic membrane environments and complex membrane compositions. , This approach shows promise for investigating antibody-protein interactions and how dimerization can be inhibited.

As a first step toward addressing this ambitious challenge, we simulate the spontaneous dimerization of both wild-type KIT and its oncogenic mutant T417I, Δ418–419, where dimerization occurs even without SCF ligand binding. Cryo-EM studies have shown that the mutant forms a ligand-free dimer with a distinct V-shaped structure. Due to the flexibility of this structure, it is not fully resolved in experimental data. Using the same dimerization procedure applied to the wild-type, we successfully obtained this V-shaped structure through CG simulations, which was then back-mapped to an atomistic resolution, providing the first full-length atomistic model of the KIT mutant.

The paper is organized as follows: in the Methods section, we provide details on the construction of both the atomistic and coarse-grained models of full-length KIT, along with the computational procedures for the simulations. In the Results and Discussion section, we analyze the flexibility and conformational changes of full-length KIT WT, as obtained from both CG and atomistic simulations. We then explore the progression of dimerization in WT and the oncogenic mutant. Finally, in the Conclusions section, we discuss how our setup can be applied to future studies, particularly in investigating KIT interactions with other molecules and potential inhibitors of its dimerization.

Results and Discussion

Analysis of the AA and CG Simulations of the WT Full-Length KIT

Figure A presents the AA structure of the full-length KIT dimer bound to SCF (depicted in green) within a POPC membrane, alongside the corresponding CG representation shown in Figure B. As with other members of the type III RTK family, the extracellular (EC) domain of KIT consists of five immunoglobulin-like (Ig-like) domains, designated D1, D2, D3, D4, and D5. , In addition, it features a single transmembrane (TM) domain and a cytoplasmic region, which includes a regulatory juxtamembrane segment (JM) and the catalytic tyrosine kinase domain. Dimeric SCF binds to the membrane-distal Ig-like domains (D1, D2, and D3), forming a 2:2 KIT-SCF complex.

In Figure C,D, we show contact maps derived from 200 ns and 4 μs simulations at the all-atom and CG resolutions, respectively. Since KIT is a homodimer, these maps are symmetric, highlighting key contacts between the D4 and D5 domains in the extracellular region (Residues 285–490), the transmembrane segment (Residues 490–525), and the interface between the two kinase domains in the intracellular region (Residues 525–900). A comparison of these maps demonstrates that the CG model accurately reproduces the interactions at the KIT dimer interface, with the primary contacts in the EC domain occurring through D4-D4 and D5-D5 interactions.

Homotypic interactions between the D4 domains of two adjacent KIT molecules are mediated by the D4-D4 interface in the SCF-KIT 2:2 complex. Notably, Arg381 and Glu386 form salt bridges across the KIT dimer. Additional D5-D5 interactions are facilitated by Tyr418 and Asn505, while hydrophobic contacts further stabilize the D5-D5 interface. The D4 and D5 domains are essential for mediating lateral D4-D4 and D5-D5 interactions, which bring together and stabilize the membrane-proximal regions of two adjacent KIT EC domains. It has been shown that the simultaneous disruption of both the D4:D4′ and D5:D5′ interfaces has a synergistic effect in impairing KIT activation.

To analyze the dynamical nature of the KIT dimer, in Figure , we compare the Root Mean Square Fluctuations (RMSF) obtained from AA and CG simulations. The protein structures are color-coded according to their RMSF values. In the intracellular region, the kinase domainsforming contacts within the dimer interfaceremain notably stable, exhibiting RMSF values below 4 Å, in contrast to the more flexible, solvent-exposed portions of the protein. The extracellular (EC) domain displays higher flexibility, except for the segment bound to the SCF complex, which includes the D1–D2–D3 domains.

Additionally, the linker connecting the EC region to the transmembrane segment shows substantial mobility (RMSF ≈ 8–12 Å), influencing both the orientation and overall dynamics of the EC extension. To further investigate this effect, we analyzed the tilt angle (Figure ), calculated from both AA and CG simulations. The tilt angle is defined as the angle between the Z-axis and the principal axis of the EC domain (indicated by dotted lines in Figure A), representing the deviation of the EC region from a vertical orientation.

In the AA simulation, which runs for 200 ns, the tilt angle stays around 10°, implying that the EC domain is almost upright. In contrast, the CG simulation, which spans 4 μs, shows a tilt angle distribution peaking near 60°, indicating significant deviations from the vertical. This discrepancy highlights how the extended time scales of CG simulations enable the exploration of larger-scale, global protein motions. The notable flexibility and dynamic behavior of the receptor align with structural studies, which have so far made it challenging to determine a high-resolution structure of the entire receptor.

Dimerization of the WT Full-Length KIT

To investigate the dimerization mechanism of the wild-type KIT receptor, we performed CGMD simulations starting from a configuration in which two separate KIT monomers, each bound to SCF, were positioned approximately 4.5 nm apart (Figure A). The systems were embedded in a POPC lipid bilayer, and 20 independent simulations of 1 μs each were conducted to ensure adequate statistical sampling of the association process. In 10 out of 20 trajectories, spontaneous dimerization was observed within the simulated time window.

Simulation of the dimerization process of the WT KIT receptor. (A) Initial configuration used for the 20 replica simulations. Two KIT monomers bound to SCF are positioned 4.5 nm apart and embedded in a POPC membrane. Water molecules and ions are omitted for clarity. (B) Representative snapshot of the system at the end of the simulation, showing the newly formed KIT dimer with the EC domains strongly tilted toward the membrane. (C) Intermonomer contact maps averaged over 10 replicas at 0, 500, and 1000 ns, illustrating the progressive formation of the dimer interface. Extracellular (EC) region encompasses residues interval 0–490 and the kinase domains in the intracellular (IC) region residues 525–900. The dimers are stabilized by the interactions between the D4-D4 and D5-D5 (Residues 280–490). (D) Time evolution of the distance between the centers of mass of the two monomers over 1 μs for the ten trajectories in which dimerization occurs. (E) Time evolution of the tilt angle from molecular dynamics simulations, revealing a transition from approximately 10° to 70°, coinciding with the dimerization event.

The evolution of the center-of-mass (COM) distance between the two monomers (Figure B) shows that in ten trajectories, dimerization occurs within the first ∼600 ns. Monitoring this distance over time provides a quantitative view of the association kinetics and reveals distinct stages of the dimerization process. It is well established that KIT dimerization is triggered by ligand (SCF) binding, while stabilization of the dimer interface primarily involves homotypic interactions between the D4 and D5 domains of the EC region, which are dominated by hydrophobic contacts.

To characterize these interactions, we computed intermonomer contact maps averaged over the ten trajectories where dimerization occurred (Figure C), with individual maps provided in the Supporting Information (SI). At t = 0 ns, no contacts are present between the monomers. By 500 ns, transient contacts emerge in the peripheral extracellular regions, and by 1000 ns, stable interactions form between the D4–D5 domains, indicating the progressive assembly of the dimer interface. This sequential pattern of contact formation is consistent with the previously proposed “zipper-like” mechanism of KIT activation, in which inter-receptor interactions propagate cooperatively along the receptor axis.

Our simulations provide direct dynamic evidence supporting this model: the association initiates at the SCF-binding region and extends sequentially through D4, D5, and TM regions, eventually stabilizing a full-length dimeric structure. This process reflects a cooperative sequence of specific but individually weak homotypic interactions that together promote a tight association of the two receptor protomers. We performed a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the MD simulation of the KIT Wild type during the dimerization process. The results of the PCA are shown in Supporting Figure S4, where we highlight the projection along the first three principal components, which together account for 91% of the total variance. Visual inspection of the principal component (the one with the highest variance) reveals the approach of the EC domains during the dimerization reaction.

A representative snapshot of the system at the end of the dimerization trajectory (Figure D) shows the fully associated KIT dimer, with the EC domains strongly tilted toward the membrane, consistent with observations from the CG simulations of the preformed dimer. The tilt angle, monitored over time (Figure E), exhibits a sharp increase from approximately 10° to 70° around the point of dimer formation (∼600 ns). This conformational shift indicates that the establishment of intermonomer contacts is coupled to a large-scale reorientation of the EC domains. Tilt-angle trajectories for individual replicas are shown in the SI, confirming the reproducibility of this transition in most of the trajectories.

Ligand-Independent Dimerization of the T417I, Δ418–419 Oncogenic KIT Mutant

Oncogenic mutations in the extracellular domain, particularly in D5, lead to ligand-independent kinase activation and subsequent cell transformation. The T417I and Δ418–419 mutations in D5, identified in patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML), as well as duplications of the Ala502-Tyr503 sequence found in gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST), ,, enhance the hydrophobicity of the D5 interface. This increased hydrophobicity strengthens D5–D5 interactions, promoting spontaneous dimerization of KIT in the absence of SCF and leading to constitutive kinase activation.

In this section, we focus on the T417I, Δ418–419 mutant, which involves a threonine-to-isoleucine substitution at position 417, along with the deletion of tyrosine 418 and aspartate 419 (Figure A).

(B) Representative AA model obtained after backmapping and subsequent MD simulations, with the mutated region again highlighted in red. (C) Intermonomer contact map at the end of the simulation, highlighting prominent interactions between the D5–D5 domains (residues 280–490) and kinase domains. (D) RMSF values computed from the AA MD trajectory and visualized as a color gradient on the protein structure, illustrating the enhanced flexibility of the EC region. (E) Structural overlay between the MD-derived model (orange) and the crystal structure (yellow, PDB ID: 4PGZ), shown in front and top views. The D5–D5 interface is well reproduced, though slight discrepancies arise from the inherent flexibility of the EC branches.

Consistent with this, previous biochemical studies have reported that the dimerization constant of D4D5 fragments carrying the T417I, Δ418–419 mutation is approximately 100–250 times higher than that of the WT fragments. These mutations are thus classified as gain-of-function alterations. High-resolution structural characterization of the full-length KIT­(T417I, Δ418–419) mutant has proven challenging due to the high conformational flexibility of the receptor in the absence of ligand stabilization by SCF. Indeed, cryo-EM studies have revealed only a low-resolution map of the mutant, in which the D1–D5 domains can be resolved, displaying a distinct V-shaped conformation of the EC region. This structural arrangement is believed to arise from the direct D5–D5 contact that stabilizes the dimer in the absence of SCF.

Using the same simulation setup employed for the WT KIT receptor, we explored the spontaneous dimerization of the T417I, Δ418–419 mutant (Figure A). The simulations were initialized with two mutant KIT monomers positioned approximately 4.5 nm apart and embedded in a POPC membrane, this time without the SCF ligand. We conducted 20 replicate coarse-grained (CG) simulations, of which five showed spontaneous dimerization within the simulated time scale.

The resulting CG model (Figure B) clearly shows the V-shaped conformation of the EC domains, consistent with the cryo-EM reconstruction, and highlights the region containing the mutated residues (in red). The intermonomer contact map (Figure C) reveals that the D5–D5 interactions constitute the primary stabilizing interface of the mutant dimer, with additional, transient contacts observed between the intracellular kinase domains. This pattern supports the hypothesis that the enhanced hydrophobicity at the D5 interface drives spontaneous, ligand-independent dimerization.

To restore atomistic resolution, we applied a backmapping procedure to convert the CG structure into a CHARMM-based AA model, followed by 200 ns of AA MD simulations to refine the structural details (Figure D). To our knowledge, this represents the first full-length atomistic model of the KIT­(T417I, Δ418–419) mutant. We next assessed the flexibility of this mutant by computing the root-mean-square fluctuations (RMSF) along the MD trajectory (Figure E). The EC region exhibits markedly higher mobility, with RMSF values reaching up to ∼10 Å, consistent with its experimentally observed flexibility.

Finally, a structural overlay between our MD-derived model (orange) and the available crystal structure (yellow, PDB ID: 4PGZ) is shown in Figure F. The alignment demonstrates excellent agreement within the D5–D5 interface, confirming that our simulations capture the key interaction features of the mutant dimer. Minor deviations in the distal EC branches are observed, likely reflecting the intrinsic flexibility of the extracellular region in the absence of ligand stabilization.

Conclusions

In this study, we used multiscale molecular dynamics simulations to explore the dimerization mechanisms of the full-length KIT receptor in both its WT and oncogenic T417I, Δ418–419 mutant forms. Coarse-grained and all-atom simulations revealed how KIT dimerization proceeds cooperatively through successive domain–domain interactions, consistent with a “zipper-like” mechanism, and how oncogenic mutations in D5 can induce spontaneous, ligand-independent dimerization.

This work provides the first all-atom structural model of the full-length KIT­(T417I, Δ418–419) mutant, obtained through backmapping and atomistic refinement of a spontaneously formed dimer. Beyond elucidating activation pathways, our study offers a novel contribution by dynamically modeling KIT’s dimerization process in its full-length, membrane-bound state, an area not previously explored using these computational techniques. The ability to capture spontaneous dimerization and reconstruct atomistic models of the full-length receptor opens the way for structure-based therapeutic interventions.

Future models incorporating a more plasma membrane-like composition, including cholesterol, would enhance the realism of the membrane environment and provide deeper insights into how lipid composition, along with membrane components like cholesterol, modulates dimerization and receptor stability.

We have established a robust computational framework for studying receptor assembly and conformational dynamics, and the insights gained could inform the design of monoclonal antibodies or small molecules that target the D4–D5 interfaceregions crucial for stabilizing the active KIT dimer. This approach could be directly applied to simulate antibody binding and predict how specific antibodies may interfere with dimerization or alter the geometry of the extracellular domains.

Overall, these simulations provide a mechanistic and structural foundation for future studies aimed at designing next-generation KIT inhibitors that prevent aberrant activation by blocking or destabilizing receptor dimerization.

Methods

System Setup

Although several high-resolution structures are available for the individual extracellular and intracellular regions of the KIT receptor, a complete full-length model has not been reported. To construct the full-length KIT model, we integrated the crystallographic structure of its extracellular domain (PDB ID: 2E9W) with transmembrane and intracellular regions modeled using Dompé’s proprietary pipeline, part of the Comprehensive Therapeutic Target Database (CTTD). This resource enables the generation of 3D structural models informed by detailed structural and functional annotations, thereby enhancing the biological relevance and predictive accuracy of the resulting assemblies. The two portions were joined using Modeler, ensuring proper domain orientation and structural continuity. The resulting model was embedded in a 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) lipid bilayer via the CHARMM-GUI web interface. The system was solvated with explicit water molecules and neutralized with 150 mM NaCl.

All-Atom MD Simulations of Full-Length KIT Dimer

AA MD simulations were performed using GROMACS 2023. Proteins, lipids, ions, and water molecules were modeled with the CHARMM-CMAP, CHARMM36, and TIP3P force fields, respectively. Long-range electrostatics were treated using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method with a real-space cutoff of 12 Å. Lennard–Jones (LJ) interactions were truncated at 12 Å using a switching function beginning at 10 Å. Bond constraints involving hydrogen atoms were applied using the LINCS algorithm. Simulations were conducted at 303 K with a velocity-rescaling thermostat and a semi-isotropic C-rescale pressure coupling (1 atm, compressibility of 4.5 × 10–5 bar–1). Following energy minimization and equilibration, production runs were carried out for 200 ns with an integration time step of 2 fs.

Coarse-Grained MD Simulations of Full-Length KIT Dimer

To access longer time scales, CG MD simulations were performed using the Martini 3 force field. The CG structure of the KIT receptor was generated using martinize2, and the system was embedded in a POPC bilayer and solvated with approximately 54,000 water beads and 150 mM NaCl using the insane script. The simulation box contained roughly 67,000 particles (Lx = 140 Å, Ly = 140 Å, Lz = 400 Å). To maintain secondary structure integrity, an elastic network (EN) model was applied with a force constant of 500 kJ mol–1 nm–2, lower and upper bond cutoffs of 0.5 and 0.9 nm, respectively. The EN was also applied between the ligand and protein. Simulations were performed for 4 μs with a 20 fs time step. The temperature and pressure were maintained at 300 K and 1 bar using a velocity-rescaling thermostat (τ = 1.0 ps) and a semi-isotropic Parrinello–Rahman barostat (τ = 12 ps). Electrostatics were treated using the reaction-field approach (cutoff = 1.1 nm) with a shifted van der Waals potential and Verlet cutoff scheme.

CG Simulations of KIT Dimerization

For the dimerization simulations, two KIT monomers bound to SCF were initially positioned 4.5 nm apart. The simulation box dimensions were increased (Lx = 300 Å, Ly = 450 Å, Lz = 450 Å) to accommodate the association process, resulting in approximately 520,000 CG beads. The same simulation parameters described above were applied. A total of 20 independent replicas, each lasting 1 μs, were performed to obtain statistically robust sampling of the dimerization process. The same setup was also used for simulations of the T417I, Δ418–419 oncogenic mutant, this time in the absence of SCF.

Backmapping to AA Resolution

The final configurations from the Martini 3 simulations of the KIT mutant were converted back to AA resolution using the Backward script. The reconstructed systems were simulated using the CHARMM force field, following the same parameters described for the initial AA simulations. Prior to production, the systems underwent two cycles of 500-step energy minimization, followed by four 1 ns NVT equilibration runs with position restraints and progressively increasing time steps (0.2, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 fs). The 250 ns production trajectories were used for structural and dynamical analyses presented in this work.

Analysis

A custom-made MDAnalysis script was used to calculate the tilt angle along the MD trajectory. In our study, the tilt angle refers to the angle between the vector formed by the centers of mass (COM) of the initial and final regions of the extracellular domain (ED) of the protein, and the z-axis, which is aligned with the normal direction to the membrane bilayer. The z-axis is defined as the unit vector perpendicular to the membrane bilayer, pointing out of the membrane plane. The initial and final regions of the ED are defined using residues from both monomers, ensuring that the tilt angle calculation reflects the average orientation of the entire ED domain, accounting for both monomers simultaneously. MDAnalysis scripts were also employed to generate the contact maps and to perform the PCA analysis. The VMD software was utilized for visualizing and inspecting the MD trajectories.
