Effects of solution treatment, precipitation hardening, and shot peening on the cavitation erosion resistance of 17‑4PH steel produced by additive and conventional methods
Authors: Aleksander Świetlicki, Mariusz Walczak, Dariusz Chocyk, Mirosław Szala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract17-4PH stainless steel is widely used in hydraulic components exposed to cavitation, yet its cavitation erosion resistance (CER) depends strongly on both the manufacturing route and the applied post-processing. This study compares the CER of conventionally processed and DMLS-fabricated 17-4PH steel after solution treatment, precipitation hardening, and shot peening with glass, ceramic, and steel media. The novelty of the work lies in the systematic comparison of these post-processing routes across both manufacturing methods, supported by a structure–property analysis of hardness, surface morphology, phase composition, crystallite size, and microstrain-derived estimated stress magnitude. The DMLS material showed consistently higher CER than the conventional steel. The best performance was observed for the precipitation-hardened and ceramic-shot-peened DMLS specimen, which exhibited an erosion rate of 1.49 µm h−1, compared with 4.71 µm h−1 for the untreated conventional specimen, representing an improvement of approximately 216%. The superior cavitation performance of the DMLS material was associated with broader retention of the α/γ microstructure after post-processing, indicating that CER is governed not only by hardness, but also by phase constitution and the condition of the near-surface layer.Keywords: DMLS, Shot peening, Heat treatment, Precipitation hardening, Surface treatment, Cavitation erosionSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

17-4PH stainless steel is widely used in engineering components that require high strength, corrosion resistance, and good performance under erosive and tribological loading, including hydraulic machinery, turbine parts, and flow-exposed components1–3. Its favorable mechanical properties result primarily from precipitation hardening, which makes this alloy attractive for applications requiring both structural integrity and environmental resistance4–7. At the same time, the increasing use of additive manufacturing (AM) for complex metallic parts has expanded the technological relevance of 17-4PH steel. In particular, laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) methods such as direct metal laser sintering (DMLS) enable the fabrication of geometrically complex components with high density and good dimensional accuracy2,8,9. However, 17-4PH steel produced by additive manufacturing differs significantly from conventionally processed material because of rapid solidification, repeated thermal cycling, and process-induced microstructural heterogeneity10,11.

Cavitation erosion is a highly damaging phenomenon that significantly impacts the reliability and safety of machinery and equipment. Its mitigation is difficult and usually involves both appropriate material selection and surface engineering approaches. The cavitation erosion resistance (CER) of metallic materials is governed not only by hardness, but also by phase constitution, near-surface stress state, and the ability of the microstructure to accommodate repeated impact loading12–14. Thus, 17-4PH steel shows good fatigue resistance, which is beneficial for CER15,16. This study investigates the impact of heat treatment (HT) combined with shot peening (SP) on the cavitation erosion resistance (CER) of both conventionally and additively manufactured 17-4PH steel. Typically, surface hardening increases the CER of metallic surfaces. Most recent research focuses on the deposition of various hard coatings17,18, creation of nanoscale structures to mitigate their effects19,20, alloying, or treatment by laser techniques such as laser surface melting21,22. Nitriding of 17-4PH has also been discussed in some studies23. However, for practical engineering applications, more conventional and industrially accessible treatments such as heat treatment and shot peening remain highly relevant. In particular, precipitation hardening and shot peening can modify the hardness, stress state, phase stability, and surface morphology of the material, all of which may affect cavitation behavior. Despite this, systematic studies addressing the combined influence of conventional heat treatment and shot peening on the CER of both conventionally processed and additively manufactured 17-4PH steel remain limited22,24,25. This is important because both routes may produce different phase constitutions, crystallite-scale structures, stress states, and surface responses to peening, which can lead to different cavitation damage mechanisms.

Among the available post-processing routes, solution treatment, precipitation hardening, and shot peening are particularly relevant because they directly modify the structural state of the near-surface layer. Heat treatment affects phase balance, precipitation strengthening, and crystallite-scale structure, whereas shot peening introduces severe surface deformation and alters hardness, roughness, and stress state. The response of 17-4PH steel to these treatments is expected to depend on the manufacturing route. In conventionally processed steel, heat treatment typically promotes a predominantly martensitic structure, while in DMLS-fabricated material a mixed α/γ microstructure may persist over a wider range of post-processing conditions. Such differences may significantly affect cavitation damage evolution and, consequently, the final erosion resistance.

The aim of this study was therefore to evaluate the effect of solution treatment, precipitation hardening, and shot peening on the cavitation erosion resistance of 17-4PH steel produced by conventional processing and by DMLS. Particular attention was given to the relationship between cavitation behavior and the condition of the near-surface layer, including hardness, surface morphology, phase composition, crystallite size, and microstrain-derived estimated stress magnitude. The novelty of the work lies in the systematic comparison of these post-processing routes for both manufacturing methods, together with a structure–property interpretation of the observed cavitation response.

Materials and methods

Specimen preparation and treatment

Samples of 17-4PH steel, otherwise designated as AISI 630 or X5CrNiCuNb16-4 equivalents, were produced as follows. The total number of specimen types tested in this study equals twelve, see Fig. 1. Two reference samples were untreated, namely fabricated from a steel plate (RP) and as-built additively manufactured (RD). Next, the samples were heat-treated (HT) to achieve the supersaturated (STP and STD) and precipitation-hardened (PHP and PHD) states, and finally, the six precipitation-hardened samples were shot peened (SP) using different blasting media. To sum up, half of the listed above specimens were additively manufactured using the DMLS method using GP1 feedstock powder EOS GmbH (Krailling, Germany). This gas-atomized powder, according to the manufacturer, has an unimodal particle distribution, as follows: D50 = 36–44 μm, particles > 53 μm: max 6.0 wt%, Particles > 63 μm Max 1.0 wt% according to ASTM B822 and ASTM B214. Powder particles were characterized by a mainly spherical shape with some satellites, see26. An EOSINT M280 EOS GmbH (Krailling, Germany) machine was used to produce the steel samples. Manufacturing was performed using the optimal parameters recommended by the powder manufacturer (EOS GmbH, Germany). Laser power was set to 200 W, sintering thickness was 0.02 mm, and laser spot size was 0.1 mm. The laser was operated in continuous wave mode. Nitrogen was used as a shielding gas for fabrication. The DMLS process was conducted using laser scanning parallel to the base plate (XY), with the building direction perpendicular to this plane (as visualized in previous studies26). Cylinder specimens were subjected to stress-relieving annealing at 650 °C for 1 h in an argon atmosphere. The post-treatment temperature–time parameters recommended by the feedstock manufacturer for 17-4PH printouts are intended to maintain part accuracy. The flat-top surface of cylindrical samples is subjected to erosion testing.

Schematic diagram of twelve types of specimens utilized in the study, sample codes are written in capital letters.

Heat treatment (HT) was performed on both additively and conventionally fabricated samples an. Heat treatment consisted of supersaturating (solution treatment at 1040 °C/40 min + cooling in oil) followed by precipitation hardening (supersaturated + aging at 450 °C/240 min), named as PHP and PHD. HT conditions were selected based on previous studies27 and other authors recommendations28. Before and after heat treatment, the samples were sanded flat to level and clean the surface using a set of sandpapers ending at #1200 grit, which is important for further comparison of the effects of SP on 17-4 PH steel manufactured by the conventional and DMLS processes. Selected precipitation-hardened samples were subjected to shot peening (SP) using three media: glass (PHPG and PHDG), ceramics (PHPC and PHDC), and steel (PHPS and PHDS). SP uses a constant distance of approximately 20 mm between the nozzle and the material, which was maintained, and the shot medium was blasted onto the flat surface of cylindrical samples at a 90° angle to the material. The peening pressure was constant at 0.3 MPa, the surface treatment time was 120 s. Glass beads (Marbad) with a diameter of 200–300 μm, ceramic (Marbad, CERAM 350) (250–425 μm), and steel shots (Amacast 30) with a diameter of 150–500 μm were used as peening media. Shot morphology is shown in the author’s previous studies23,24,26. Top surfaces were treated with SP, with the nozzle perpendicular to the specimen’s top flat surface. After manufacturing, the test material was cleaned in an ultrasonic cleaner and then dried with compressed air. A schematic diagram of the sample processing and labeling is shown in Fig. 1.

Chemical composition and hardness

The chemical composition of the 17-4PH steel materials investigated was analyzed using a Magellan Q8 spark emission spectrometer from Bruker (Billerica, MA, USA). Five spark burns were performed on a powder, an additive fabricated via DMLS and conventionally processed samples. Then, the mean value and standard deviation (SD) of the estimated chemical composition were compared with the standard requirements. Hardness tests were carried out at a load of 1,961 N using a Future-Tech FM-800 microhardness tester and a dwell time of 15 s. On the top surface, at least ten indentations were made for each specimen to achieve statistical accuracy.

Surface morphology and roughness investigation

The surface characteristics before and after shot peening and after erosion testing were determined using a Phenom World ProX SEM. Analyses were conducted at × 2000 and × 5000 magnification in the central areas of the specimens.

Vertical Scanning Interferometry technique using the Countour GT-K1 (Veeco, USA) profilometer equipped with the 5 × magnification lens based on the VXI technique. The maximum scanning height in this technique is 10 mm. Surface microgeometry maps were examined in an area with a size of 1.26 mm × 0.946 mm before and after cavitation erosion testing. Roughness results were analyzed at the latest eight randomly selected locations before and after cavitation testing. Measurements were taken in accordance with the ISO 4287 standard, and the following parameters were estimated: Sa—arithmetical mean height of the surface, Sv—maximum pit height of the surface, and Sp—maximum peak height of the surface.

Cavitation erosion testing

Cavitation erosion resistance was assessed in accordance with ASTM G32-10 standard test method under the following conditions29 using the stationary specimen method. The erosion test rig, test setup, and an exemplary specimen after CER testing are shown in Fig. 2. The tip horn (Ø 15.9 mm), immersed in distilled water to a depth of 12 ± 4 mm, vibrates with an amplitude of 50 µm and a frequency of 20 kHz and was used in the test rig chamber at a temperature of 25 ± 2 °C. The distance from the sonotrode horn to the specimen top was set to 0.5 mm. Specimens’ mass loss was measured with a 0.01 mg accuracy. Erosion tests were conducted on the flat surfaces of Ø 30mm x 6 mm cylinders (Fig. 2c) fabricated from conventionally processed 17-4PH plate and on the top surfaces of DMLS samples (the top surface of printouts was parallel to the base plate (XY)26). Conventional samples were cut-out from steel plates while as-built cylinders were sanded flat to a level surface using a set of sandpapers ending at #1200 grit. The cavitation tests were performed on cylindrical samples in the as-received and sanded condition (RP and RD), after solution treatment (STP and STD), after precipitation hardening (PHP and PHD), and on the shot-peened surfaces of the precipitation-hardened samples. This made it possible to determine the effect of shot peening and shot type on CER. Based on mass loss, the erosion curves (MDE, MDER) were calculated using the following Eqs. (1–2)29. Cavitation erosion testing was repeated at least twice, and the mean values were reported. The eroded surfaces were examined using SEM and a profilometer.

Phase composition, crystallite size, and microstrain-derived stress estimation

The phase composition, crystallite size, and estimated stresses in the investigated materials were examined using a high-resolution X-ray diffractometer (Empyrean, Panalytical) equipped with a Cu Kα X-ray tube and a nickel filter. A proportional detector was employed for radiation detection. The measurements were performed in the θ-2θ geometry within the angular range of 20° to 100°. The profiles were collected with a step size of 0.01° and a counting time of 7 s per point. Anti-scatter slits of 1/2°, a 5 mm mask, and Soller slits were used. The positions of peaks, the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of peaks, and the crystalline phases present in the samples were identified using the HighScore Plus software with the Open Crystallographic Database. Phase analysis was conducted at the latest two randomly selected locations to quantify accuracy. The quantitative phase composition was determined using the Rietveld method. Williamson-Hall method was applied to calculate the average crystallite size (the size of the smallest crystalline domains ordered within a grain), and microstrain based on broadening of the diffraction peaks in the XRD patterns. This method assumes that total broadening of the diffraction peaks is a sum of broadening comes from grain refinement and microstrain. where, θ is the diffraction angle, λ is the wavelength of X-ray (λ = 1.5418 Å), K is the Scherer constant, which generally is taken as 0.9, D is the crystallite size and \varepsilon is the microstrain. The β is the diffraction peak’s full width at half maximum (FWHM) in radians corrected by instrument FWHM. The instrument FWHM was determined to use a standard Si reference sample. After measuring the β of some diffraction peaks, a curve of β cos(θ) vs. 4sin(θ) was plotted and fitted linearly by means of the least square method. The slope of the straight line is the microstrain, and from the intercept of the straight line on the ordinate axis, the average crystallite size was calculated. It should be noted that residual stresses were not measured directly using the sin2ψ method or a dedicated XRD residual stress analyser. Instead, the stress values reported in this work were estimated indirectly from the microstrain obtained by the Williamson–Hall analysis. Therefore, these values should be treated as microstrain-derived estimated stress magnitudes (4)31, useful mainly for comparative interpretation between processing conditions, rather than as absolute residual stress values. Since the Williamson–Hall method is based on peak broadening, it does not directly determine the sign of the residual stress state.

It should be emphasized that the grain size determined by the Williamson–Hall method refers to the size of domains undergoing coherent diffraction (crystallites), rather than to metallographic grains, such as primary austenite grains. Therefore, the observed reduction in grain size after supersaturation treatment should be interpreted as a decrease in crystallite size and/or an increase in microstresses and defect density, rather than as classical grain coarsening. This effect is of particular importance in the case of DMLS materials, where phase transformations and heterogeneous microstructures contribute to the broadening of XRD peaks. Detailed data of phase compositions, average crystallite sizes, and stresses of samples are summarized in Table 3.

In the conventionally fabricated specimens, the as-received material (RP) contained 93% α-martensite and 7% γ-austenite. After solution treatment (STP), the retained austenite disappeared and the structure became fully martensitic. Precipitation hardening (PHP) maintained this fully α-martensitic phase composition. Subsequent shot peening of the precipitation-hardened conventional specimens (PHPC, PHPS, and PHPG) did not alter the phase balance; all of these specimens remained fully α-martensitic. Therefore, in the conventionally processed steel, the effect of post-processing was expressed primarily through changes in crystallite size and stress state rather than through further phase transformation.

A different response was observed for the DMLS-fabricated material. The as-built specimen (RD) contained 85% α-martensite and 15% γ-austenite. After solution treatment (STD), the martensite fraction increased to 89%, while the austenite content decreased to 11%. Precipitation hardening (PHD) further increased the martensite fraction to 92%. However, unlike in the conventional material, a measurable amount of γ-austenite was still retained after heat treatment. The effect of shot peening on the DMLS specimens depended on the peening medium. Glass bead peening (PHDG) and steel shot peening (PHDS) led to moderate changes in phase balance, whereas ceramic shot peening (PHDC) resulted in a fully α-martensitic structure.

For the DMLS material, solution treatment reduced the average crystallite size from 46.4 nm in RD to 21.9 nm in STD, and decreased the microstrain-derived estimated stress magnitude from 0.58 to 0.13 GPa. Precipitation hardening increased the crystallite size to 33.7 nm and increased this stress-related parameter to 1.18 GPa. Shot peening is expected to introduce compressive residual stresses in the near-surface layer; however, in this study the stress-related values were estimated from Williamson–Hall peak broadening and therefore should be interpreted as microstrain-derived stress magnitudes rather than directly measured compressive residual stresses. Among the shot-peened DMLS specimens, the highest stress value was recorded for PHDC (1.42 GPa), which also exhibited complete disappearance of the γ phase. In the conventional material, RP showed an average crystallite size of 30.1 nm and a stress of 0.20 GPa. Solution treatment slightly reduced both values, whereas precipitation hardening increased them. Among the shot-peened conventional specimens, PHPG exhibited the largest crystallite size (90.0 nm) and the highest stress level (1.32 GPa). The stress values reported in Table 3 should be interpreted as estimated stress magnitudes derived from XRD peak broadening, not as direct residual stress measurements. Therefore, they reflect the average microstrain-related response of the diffracting near-surface volume. This explains why the estimated stress does not increase monotonically after all shot-peening treatments. In the DMLS specimens, PHDG and PHDS showed lower estimated stress values than PHD, although they were shot peened. This effect may result from stress redistribution during peening, partial relaxation of precipitation-hardening-induced microstrain, changes in retained austenite content, and differences in the deformation intensity imposed by the peening media. In contrast, ceramic shot peening produced a stronger near-surface deformation effect and a complete transformation to the α phase in PHDC, resulting in the highest estimated compressive stress among the DMLS specimens.

The opposite trends observed after ceramic shot peening in the DMLS and conventional specimens can be explained by their different initial phase states. The PHD specimen still contained retained γ-austenite, whereas PHP was already fully α-martensitic. In PHDC, ceramic shot peening caused strong near-surface deformation and complete disappearance of the γ phase, suggesting deformation-induced transformation and an increase in defect density and microstrain. Consequently, the microstrain-derived estimated stress magnitude increased from 1.18 GPa in PHD to 1.42 GPa in PHDC. In contrast, PHPC remained fully martensitic, and no additional γ → α transformation could occur. Therefore, the decrease from 0.61 GPa in PHP to 0.49 GPa in PHPC may be associated with redistribution or partial relaxation of heat-treatment-induced microstrain during ceramic shot peening. This confirms that the stress-related parameter obtained from Williamson–Hall analysis depends not only on peening intensity, but also on the initial phase constitution and deformation response of the material.

These results indicate that the manufacturing route strongly affects the phase stability and structural response of 17-4PH steel to heat treatment and shot peening. In conventionally processed steel, heat treatment readily eliminated retained austenite, yielding a fully martensitic structure that remained stable after shot peening. In contrast, the DMLS material retained a mixed α/γ microstructure over a broader range of post-processing conditions. This behavior is consistent with the known microstructural heterogeneity of additively manufactured 17-4PH steel, arising from rapid solidification, repeated thermal cycling, local compositional fluctuations, and differences in thermal history within the build volume.

Available studies emphasize that the 17-4 PH alloy produced using SLM contains martensite with a BCT structure and BCC ferrite phases33,34. In turn, Alnajjar et al.35 pointed out that, because of intense, repeated heating and cooling cycles occurring during the SLM process, this material may transform mainly into δ-ferrite rather than BCT martensite. According to their interpretation, very rapid cooling omits the austenite stability range, preventing the transformation of δ-ferrite into austenite and promoting the retention of δ-ferrite at room temperature. As shown by Lashgari et al.36, the microstructure of 17-4 PH alloy after the SLM process may experience reverse austenite formation during heating in the range of 880–910 °C. Our previous studies have demonstrated the presence of ferrite in the microstructure of DMLS immediately after printing. However, these were negligible amounts that subsequently disappeared because of further heat treatment. Lashgari et al.36 also stated that conventional heat treatment is not suitable for additive manufacturing, as it led to significant separation of nickel and copper, thereby compromising the chemical stability of austenite and shifting the martensite start temperature (Ms) below room temperature.

A similar composition to that obtained was also achieved by P. Monero-Garibaldi. et al.37. In the case of conventional material, three phases were detected after DMLS printing: α-ferrite, γ-austenite and martensite. In the case of 17-4 PH steel manufactured using a conventional process, α-ferrite and γ-austenite were detected. Due to the very low carbon content in the 17-4PH alloy (approx. 0.025%), the deformation of the crystal cell in the C-axis direction is very small, making it very difficult to distinguish between the ferritic (BCC) and BCT martensitic structures, because martensite hardly deforms. The structure does not become distinctly tetragonal. It remains almost cubic (BCC), and since martensite does not exhibit the typical strong tetragonal deformation, it resembles ferrite when examined by XRD33,34.

Based on previous studies, conclusions on phase composition can be drawn26,27. DMLS steel produced under the same conditions differs in phase composition in the as-built state. In raw DMLS steel, three phases are often identified: ferrite, austenite, and martensite. Depending on the gas used, the martensite-to-austenite ratio can vary38. When higher peening pressure in raw 17-4PH DMLS steel, martensite content increases and austenite decreases. Other studies also show this tendency39–41. At constant solution treatment temperature and time, with higher aging temperature at constant time, martensite content increases and austenite content decreases. Depending on the medium used during the SP process, the martensite-to-austenite ratio varies. In general, the highest martensite content can be achieved with the CrNi steel shot. Comparing additively manufactured 17-4PH steel to 17-4PH steel produced by conventional processes is difficult because various factors must be considered.

In the case of 17-4 PH steel manufactured using conventional process, the influence of SP on the average crystallite size is not visible, but the influence of heat treatment and shot peening on the stress level is visible. As a result of supersaturation, the residual austenite was removed, and the structure became completely martensitic. Therefore, the microstrain-derived estimated stress magnitude also decreased. However, heat treatment and shot peening had the strongest effect on increasing this stress-related parameter, with at least twofold higher values observed in selected conditions. The greatest increase in compressive stresses was observed for PHPG, which may have resulted, among other factors, from the location of powder particles on the surface, as reported in a previous publication42.

Specimens treated with steel shots exhibit spherical indentations of varying depths, similar to those in specimens subjected to SP with ceramic beads. However, compared with steel-shot-treated surfaces, the ceramic bead-treated surfaces exhibit scratches and lip formation comparable to those observed in glass-treated specimens. The glass and ceramic shots crack and fragment during peening, leading to cracks at the shot boundary and the formation of sharp particles that gouge and scratch. Finally, these shot particles are pressed and adhere to the SP surface, as reported in31 for glass beads peened from as-printed 17-4PH steel. Finally, roughness parameter analysis indicates an increasing roughening rate in the following order: steel, glass, and ceramic shot. In other words, the blasting medium used affects surface roughness. Moreover, the type of sample (conventionally or additively fabricated) has a negligible effect on roughness, whereas the type of blast medium is dominant, see Figs. 5, 6 and 7.

Following CER testing, eroded surfaces are compared in Figs. 8 and 9. The post-CER surface varies depending on the treatment used. The development of surface morphology agrees with the quantitative results given in Fig. 10. Generally, cavitation erosion in 17-4PH steel was governed by a fatigue-dominated mechanism involving initial plastic deformation at the phase boundary, brittle-plastic fracture, and material detachment, with the severity of these processes strongly dependent on manufacturing route and post-processing condition. Untreated samples RP and RD present serious roughening (Fig. 7) and high valleys and peaks (Sv and Sp) are noted on an overview of the eroded surfaces (Fig. 9) in comparison to other samples. Cavitation erosion produces a different surface erosion ratio depending on heat treatment. With successive treatments, the structure shows material detachment in progressively finer portions (Fig. 8). Consequently, the size of cavitation pits noted for the RP and RD exceeds that of solution-treated samples (STP and STD), and finer pits are present in precipitation-hardened samples (PHP and PHD), while all SP surfaces show relatively the finest surface pitting and cracking. The comparable level of surface damage across all precipitation-hardened surfaces is supported by similar surface roughness results measured after 10 h of cavitation testing (Fig. 7). This confirms the influence of heat treatment and shot peening on the cavitation erosion mechanism. The difference in erosion mechanisms is most evident in the untreated (PR and RD) and precipitation-hardened samples. As-received specimens, due to their as-received microstructure, show an erosion mechanism relying on the removal of material in massive chunks, which is related to relatively (Table 3) soft γ-austenitic structure discussed by Figure 3 and Fig. 4. In the martensite-dominated hardened microstructure of PH samples, erosion proceeds via the removal of fine particles, a characteristic of cavitation erosion damage in materials with high CER43.

Cavitation erosion resistance

Analysis of the cavitation erosion results shown in Fig. 10 supplements the erosion mechanism analysis and indicates that, in general, additively fabricated 17-4PH samples exhibit higher CER than conventionally fabricated steel samples. The erosion curves indicate that DMLS 17-4PH steel peened with ceramic PHDC has the lowest mean depth of erosion rate, MDER = 1.49 µm h-1, while the highest was noted for the RP sample, MDER = 4.71 µm h-1. The difference between them is therefore 216%, which is more than twice as high. Generally, the obtained CER for 17-4PH heat-treated and shot-peened samples is higher than those reported for untreated steel44 and exceeds those reported for other stainless steels AISI 316 steel29, nickel alloys45 and cermet coatings30, presents comparable erosion rates to other grades of different grades of martensitic overlays and steels46,47 and display higher material loss than reported for hard nickel-, cobalt-based48 alloys or high entropy alloys43. Moreover, the solution treatment also has a positive effect on CER in the case of both conventional (STP) and additively (STD) fabricated steels, showing the MDER = 4.51 µm h-1 and MDER = 2.11 µm h-1 respectively (Fig. 10). Solution treatment slightly hardened the as-built samples, and the hardness is an important factor for increasing the CER35, especially in the case of precipitation hardened samples, which is also proven by our study.

Synthesis of results from erosion curves and microscopic observations indicates that, in the initial stage of erosion, the peak erosion rate is observed for shot-peened samples, whereas the others exhibit an incubation stage (a characteristic of metallic alloys). This increase in erosion rate, so-called ‘cleaning effect’49, relies on the removal of all loosely attached particles from the surface. As demonstrated in a previous study, both ceramic and glass particles are present on the material surface, thereby altering the surface structure22, which in turn affects the erosion mechanism. These loosely bound particles, subjected to the “cleaning effect,” are material deformed by shot peening and shot residues that stuck to the peened surface. The highest initial material loss is observed for ceramic and glass beads (PHPG, PHDG), which are more prone to cracking than steel shot44. Therefore, the CER in the case of peened and unpeened surfaces should be done via analysis of (MDER) mean depth of erosion rate rather than MDE parameters. Taking this into account, the superior CER was noted for PHDC and was followed by PHDG samples, with low and leveling-off erosion rates of 1.49 µm h-1 and 1.56 µm h-1, respectively.

Overall, the DMLS-produced 17-4PH steel exhibited superior cavitation erosion resistance compared with conventionally manufactured steel, and both heat treatment and shot peening can improve hardness and, consequently, CER. This advantage is supported by both the lower erosion rates and the less severe surface damage, as shown in Figs. 5, 6, and 7, and discussed in the previous section.

The relationship between surface roughness (Fig. 5) and cavitation erosion resistance (Fig. 8) was not straightforward in the present study. In contrast, hardness showed a clearer positive effect on CER, since harder specimens generally exhibited lower erosion rates (Fig. 11). However, the comparison between conventionally and additively manufactured steel indicates that hardness alone does not fully explain the cavitation response. The superior performance of the DMLS specimens suggests that both phase composition and the condition of the near-surface layer play important roles in controlling erosion damage. Therefore, the cavitation erosion results should be interpreted not only in terms of hardness, but also in terms of phase analysis of microstructure development.

This is due to different mechanisms of cavitation erosion resistance. In general, 17-4PH DMLS steel is more resistant to cavitation erosion due to the fact that it contains both austenite and martensite, which is beneficial in this aspect, as shown by other studies50,51. During cavitation, γ austenite undergoes a transformation into α martensite and work hardening occurs. Therefore, the interpretation of the results should be divided into two groups, namely DMLS and 17-4 PH steel manufactured using conventional processes. The persistence of γ-austenite in the DMLS specimens is particularly important for cavitation erosion behaviour. Cavitation erosion in metallic materials is governed by repeated high-frequency impact loading, local plastic deformation, crack initiation, and progressive material removal. Under such conditions, a mixed martensitic–austenitic structure may be beneficial because retained austenite can contribute to energy absorption and may undergo strain- or impact-induced transformation, thereby locally enhancing work hardening. This provides a plausible explanation for the superior cavitation erosion resistance of the DMLS specimens compared with the conventionally produced steel, even when the hardness values alone do not fully account for the differences in erosion rate.

In contrast, the conventionally fabricated specimens became fully martensitic after solution treatment and remained so after precipitation hardening and shot peening. Although this microstructure provided high hardness and improved cavitation erosion resistance relative to the untreated state, it did not offer the same phase-transformation-assisted response as the DMLS material. Thus, the present results suggest that cavitation erosion resistance in 17-4PH steel is controlled not only by hardness, but also by the interplay between phase composition, crystallite-scale structural refinement, and stress state.

The role of shot peening should also be interpreted in this broader microstructural context. In both manufacturing routes, shot peening modified the near-surface structure and affected the microstrain-derived stress-related parameter. However, its effect on phase composition was limited in the conventional steel and more pronounced in the DMLS steel, especially for ceramic shot peening. Therefore, the beneficial effect of shot peening on cavitation erosion resistance cannot be attributed solely to surface hardening. Rather, it results from the combined influence of surface deformation, stress redistribution, and phase transformation effects, which depend on the initial microstructure created by the manufacturing route.

Overall, the XRD analysis supports the conclusion that the superior cavitation erosion resistance of DMLS-fabricated 17-4PH steel arises from a more favourable and more adaptable microstructural state, characterized by the coexistence of α and γ phases over a wider range of post-processing conditions. This mixed-phase structure, together with the stress state and defect structure introduced by heat treatment and shot peening, appears to be more effective in resisting cavitation-induced damage than the predominantly or fully martensitic structure of the conventionally processed steel.

Conclusions

In summary, the present study shows that the cavitation erosion resistance of 17-4PH stainless steel is controlled by the combined effect of manufacturing route, heat treatment, and shot peening. The results demonstrate that the superior performance of the DMLS-fabricated material arises not only from strengthening, but also from a more favourable evolution of phase composition and near-surface microstructure under post-processing.
