Postoperative Neutrophil-to-Albumin Ratio as a Prognostic Marker for Patients with Bladder Cancer Undergoing Radical Cystectomy: A Retrospective Observational Cohort Study
Authors: Wei Li, Chenhui Xiang, Guoping Xie, Yuanyuan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the prognostic value of the postoperative neutrophil-to-albumin ratio (NAR) in patients with bladder cancer undergoing radical cystectomy.Patients and MethodsA total of 368 patients with histologically confirmed bladder cancer who underwent radical cystectomy between January 2018 and December 2021 were retrospectively analyzed. Postoperative NAR was calculated using neutrophil count (%) and serum albumin concentration (g/L) measured approximately three months after surgery. Receiver operating characteristic analysis identified the optimal NAR cutoff value for predicting 5-year overall survival. Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression models were used to assess associations between NAR and overall survival or recurrence-free survival.ResultsThe optimal NAR cutoff value was 2.6 with an AUC value of 0.76. Elevated NAR was significantly correlated with bladder cancer of invasive pathological types (p = 0.005), advanced tumor stage (p < 0.0001), lymph-node metastasis (p = 0.002), and receipt of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (p = 0.014). Patients with elevated NAR had significantly shorter overall survival (HR=3.14; 95% CI, 1.27–6.24; p = 0.002) and recurrence-free survival (HR=3.27; 95% CI, 1.52–5.87; p = 0.001) compared with those with low NAR. In multivariate analysis, postoperative NAR (p = 0.001), tumor stage (p = 0.002), and pathological type (p = 0.013) were independent predictors of overall survival, while NAR (p = 0.002), tumor stage (p = 0.003), and lymph-node metastasis (p = 0.014) independently predicted recurrence-free survival.ConclusionAn increased postoperative NAR independently predicts poorer overall and recurrence-free survival of bladder cancer patients following radical cystectomy. As an inexpensive and readily available biomarker reflecting systemic inflammation and nutritional status, NAR may serve as a practical tool for postoperative risk stratification and individualized patient management.

Introduction

Bladder cancer ranks among the ten most common malignancies worldwide, with approximately 550,000 new cases and 200,000 deaths reported annually.1,2 About one-quarter of patients present with muscle-invasive bladder cancer at diagnosis, which carries a high risk of metastasis and poor prognosis.1,3 Radical cystectomy is the standard treatment for muscle-invasive bladder cancer and for high-risk non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer; however, outcomes remain unsatisfactory, with a high recurrence rate.4–6 Traditional prognostic models based on pathological stage and clinicopathological features are often insufficient to accurately predict individual outcomes. Therefore, there is an urgent need for simple, reliable, and cost-effective strategies that can detect patients at increased risk of recurrence or disease progression following surgery.

Accumulating evidence indicates that systemic inflammation and nutritional status are critical determinants of cancer development, progression, and prognosis.7–9 An enhanced systemic inflammatory response promotes tumor proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastasis, whereas poor nutritional status reflects cancer-associated cachexia and immune suppression.7,10,11 Consequently, a variety of inflammation-based indices have been investigated as prognostic markers in malignancy. Elevated platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and C-reactive protein–based scores have all been correlated with adverse outcomes across different cancer types.1,12–14 In bladder cancer specifically, increased preoperative inflammatory markers and decreased serum albumin levels have been linked to advanced stage and reduced survival.1,15,16 Moreover, composite indices of C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio integrating inflammatory and nutritional parameters have shown independent prognostic value in bladder cancer patients.1

Recently, the neutrophil-to-albumin ratio (NAR) has emerged as a novel biomarker reflecting both systemic inflammation and nutritional status.17,18 Derived from peripheral neutrophil counts and serum albumin concentrations, NAR integrates two key biological processes that play crucial roles in cancer progression. Neutrophils contribute to tumor growth, angiogenesis, and immune suppression through the release of inflammatory mediators, whereas serum albumin reflects both nutritional reserve and systemic inflammatory burden.7,19 Consequently, elevated NAR has been associated with adverse clinical outcomes in several malignancies, including gastrointestinal, breast, and oral cancers.7,20,21 Compared with commonly used inflammatory markers such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio or the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, NAR provides a composite measure that simultaneously reflects inflammatory activation and nutritional status.

Despite these promising findings, previous studies have evaluated preoperative NAR in other cancer types, and the prognostic significance of postoperative NAR in bladder cancer remains poorly defined. Radical cystectomy induces profound inflammatory and metabolic changes that may influence tumor recurrence and survival. Therefore, postoperative NAR may better reflect the host response to surgery, residual tumor burden, or occult micrometastatic disease. In this context, evaluating postoperative NAR may provide additional prognostic information and improve risk stratification for patients with bladder cancer following radical cystectomy. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the prognostic value of postoperative NAR in patients undergoing radical cystectomy and to determine whether it independently predicts survival outcomes.

Patients and Methods

Patients

This study analyzed a total of 368 individuals diagnosed with bladder cancer who underwent radical cystectomy at the First Affiliated Hospital of Chengdu Medical College between January 2018 and December 2021. All research activities follow the ethical standards established by the Declaration of Helsinki and received formal approval from the hospital’s Ethics Committee (No. 21023425). All participants gave their written informed consent prior to enrollment in the study.

Participants met the following inclusion criteria: (1) confirmed histopathological diagnosis of muscle-invasive bladder cancer or high-risk non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer; (2) underwent radical cystectomy; and (3) age above 18 years. Patients were excluded if they: (1) had other concurrent malignancies; (2) lacked complete clinical data or follow-up information; or (3) suffered from other medical conditions that could significantly impact overall survival.

Muscle-invasive bladder cancer was defined as tumors with pathological invasion of the detrusor muscle (T2–T4) according to the AJCC TNM staging system, whereas high-risk non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer included high-grade Ta or T1 tumors, carcinoma in situ, or tumors with adverse pathological features such as lymphovascular invasion, variant histology, or large/multifocal high-grade tumors.

Data Collection

Clinical, pathological, and demographic information were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical records. Blood samples were obtained at the first follow-up visit, approximately three months after surgery. This time point was selected to minimize the influence of immediate postoperative inflammatory responses. Patients with documented acute infections or severe postoperative complications requiring hospitalization at the time of blood sampling were not included in the analysis. Blood samples were tested within two hours after collection at our hospital’s Clinical Laboratory Center. Neutrophil count was analyzed using an automatic blood cell analyzer, and serum albumin was measured using an automatic biochemical analyzer. The NAR was then calculated by dividing the neutrophil count (%) by the serum albumin concentration (g/L) and multiplying the result by 100. Tumor depth of invasion, lymph node involvement, presence of metastasis, and pathological stage were assessed according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM classification.22

Follow-Up

The follow-up scheduled every 3 months during years 0–2, every 6 months in years 3–5, and once per year thereafter. Each follow-up visit included a physical examination, blood testing, and tumor marker testing if necessary, with imaging examinations added when indicated. Recurrence was identified by histopathologic confirmation from biopsy and/or the appearance of new lesions on imaging. Overall survival was defined as the interval from initial diagnosis to death from any cause or the last documented follow-up in June 2025. Recurrence-free survival was the time from radical cystectomy to the first evidence of disease on imaging or pathology. The median follow-up time was 56 months using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical data were summarized as counts and percentages, with group comparisons performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Overall survival and recurrence-free survival were estimated with Kaplan–Meier methods, and differences between curves were assessed by the Log rank test. Independent risk factors associated with overall and recurrence-free survival were examined using multivariable Cox regression model, applying forward stepwise selection among variables that showed p < 0.05 in univariable analyses. Potential multicollinearity among covariates was assessed before multivariable Cox regression analysis using variance inflation factors (VIFs) and tolerance statistics. Variables with VIF values <5 were considered to indicate no significant multicollinearity existed among the variables. Prior to fitting the Cox regression models, the proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using log-minus-log survival plots and Schoenfeld residuals. No significant violations of this assumption were detected.

The time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis based on 5-year overall survival status was performed, and the optimal cutoff value was determined using the maximum Youden index. The corresponding area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity were reported. A p value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. All statistical analyses were carried out in SPSS (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Determining the Optimal Cut-off Value for NAR to Predict 5-Year Overall Survival

Using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis with 5-year overall survival as the outcome, the optimal NAR cut-off value was identified as 2.6 (Figure 1). The NAR yielded an AUC of 0.76, with sensitivity of 88.72% and specificity of 86.67%. According to the cut-off value, patients were stratified into two cohorts: a high-NAR group for values more than 2.6 and a low-NAR group for values less than 2.6. Overall and Recurrence-Free Survival of Bladder Cancer Patients Stratified by Tumor Stage

Our results support tumor stage as an independent predictor of both overall survival and recurrence-free survival in bladder cancer patients undergoing radical cystectomy. To assess the stage-specific prognostic value of NAR, Kaplan–Meier analyses were conducted within subgroups stratified by tumor stage. Patients with elevated NAR showed significantly poorer overall survival in stage II tumors (HR=2.58; 95% CI, 1.22–4.38; p = 0.012), and stage III tumors (HR=3.64; 95% CI, 2.15–5.75; p = 0.006), but not in stage I tumors (HR=1.06; 95% CI, 1.01–1.27; p = 0.324), compared with those with lower NAR (Figure 4A–C). A similar trend was also displayed for recurrence-free survival, with stage I tumors (HR=0.94; 95% CI, 0.91–0.98; p = 0.415), and stage II tumors (HR=3.62; 95% CI, 1.61–5.52; p = 0.005), and stage III tumors (HR=4.38; 95% CI, 2.62–6.29; p = 0.002), respectively (Figure 4D–F). Discussion

This study demonstrates that elevated NAR was significantly correlated with bladder cancer of invasive pathological types, advanced tumor stage, lymph-node metastasis, and receipt of neoadjuvant chemotherapy. An elevated postoperative NAR independently predicts poor overall survival and recurrence-free survival in patients with bladder cancer following radical cystectomy. Patients with high postoperative NAR exhibited significantly shorter overall and recurrence-free survival compared with those with low NAR, and this association remained significant after adjustment for established prognostic factors. These findings underscore the prognostic significance of postoperative inflammatory and nutritional status in bladder cancer survival outcomes.

Our results align with previous studies emphasizing inflammation-based indices as powerful predictors of clinical outcomes. Kuroda et al reported that a high postoperative C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (≥ 0.27) was the independent risk factor for recurrence-free survival after cystectomy.1 Similarly, Kawahara et al found that bladder cancer patients with an elevated preoperative neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (≥ 2.38) had more than a fourfold increase in mortality.23 The present study extends these observations by identifying postoperative NAR, a composite marker integrating inflammation and nutritional status, as a simple and reproducible biomarker for postoperative prognostication in bladder cancer. Comparable associations between elevated NAR and poor survival have been reported in gastric, colorectal, and oral cancers, supporting the broader applicability of this index across malignancies.7,19,24

Several biological mechanisms may explain why elevated postoperative NAR predicts adverse outcomes. Neutrophils exert pro-tumorigenic effects by promoting angiogenesis, releasing growth factors, and suppressing anti-tumor immune responses.25–27 Therefore, increased neutrophil counts reflect a systemic inflammatory state conducive to residual tumor proliferation and metastatic spread. In contrast, serum albumin serves as a surrogate marker of nutritional and inflammatory status.28,29 Hypoalbuminemia often arises from cancer-related inflammation and malnutrition and has been linked to impaired immune function and reduced survival in numerous malignancies.30,31 Consequently, a high NAR—characterized by neutrophilia and hypoalbuminemia—reflects a pro-inflammatory, immunosuppressed, and nutritionally compromised state that is unfavorable for tumor control and recovery.

Moreover, postoperative NAR may indirectly capture the presence of minimal residual disease or occult metastases that perpetuate systemic inflammation after surgery. Kuroda et al proposed that elevated postoperative inflammatory markers in bladder cancer patients could signify undetected micrometastatic disease persisting after cystectomy.1 Additionally, cancer-associated malnutrition contributes to approximately 20% of cancer-related deaths, and inflammation-driven reductions in albumin levels mirror both nutritional decline and aggressive tumor biology.1,32 Thus, the prognostic significance of postoperative NAR likely arises from its integrative reflection of tumor burden (including residual and micrometastatic disease), host inflammatory response, malnutrition, and immune suppression.

From a clinical perspective, postoperative NAR represents a simple and cost-effective tool for postoperative risk stratification. Because it is derived from routine laboratory parameters, differential leukocyte count and serum albumin, and it can be readily incorporated into everyday clinical practice without additional cost and labor burden. Our findings reinforce the utility of NAR as an easily accessible biomarker to identify high-risk patients who may benefit from closer surveillance or adjuvant therapy. In our cohort, a high NAR effectively distinguished bladder cancer patients with markedly worse survival outcomes. Similarly, Kuroda et al suggested that bladder cancer patients with elevated postoperative inflammatory indices, such as the C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio, should undergo more intensive follow-up or receive additional treatment.1 The convenience and low cost of NAR measurement may allow clinicians to identify vulnerable patients earlier and implement individualized interventions, such as nutritional optimization, anti-inflammatory management, or timely initiation of adjuvant therapy, with the goal of improving long-term survival outcomes.

Furthermore, postoperative NAR may complement conventional prognostic variables, such as pathological stage and lymph node status, thereby enhancing risk stratification. Increasing evidence across malignancies including gastric, esophageal, and nasopharyngeal cancers have demonstrated that integrating inflammatory biomarkers with clinicopathological factors yields more accurate and individualized prognostic models.33,34 Our results support the development of a postoperative nomogram or risk-scoring system that incorporates NAR alongside established predictors such as tumor stage, lymph node involvement, and histological subtype. Such a comprehensive model could facilitate more precise prediction of recurrence and survival outcomes and assist in tailoring postoperative management to each patient’s risk profile.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, its single-center design may limit external validity and introduce potential selection bias. Larger, multicenter cohorts are required to validate the generalizability of our findings. Second, NAR was evaluated at a single postoperative time point; thus, longitudinal trends in NAR and their relationship with disease recurrence or progression were not assessed. Third, despite rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria, unmeasured confounders may still influence the results. Finally, although NAR remained an independent prognostic factor in multivariate analysis, its discriminative performance alone was moderate, indicating that it should be viewed as a complementary strategy rather than to replace established predictors.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrated that an elevated postoperative NAR independently predicted shorter overall and recurrence-free survival in patients with bladder cancer undergoing radical cystectomy. As a readily obtainable and inexpensive biomarker reflecting systemic inflammation and nutritional status, NAR provides valuable prognostic information beyond conventional clinicopathologic parameters. Incorporating postoperative NAR assessment into routine follow-up protocols could enable early identification of high-risk patients and guide individualized surveillance and therapeutic strategies. Future multicenter, prospective investigations are warranted to validate these findings and to explore the integration of NAR into comprehensive prognostic models for postoperative bladder cancer management.
