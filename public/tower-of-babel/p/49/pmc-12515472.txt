Sociodemographic differences in young adult actigraphic sleep among a diverse, national sample: the Future of Families Young Adult Sleep Study
Authors: Gina Marie Mathew, David A Reichenberger, Carrie L Shandra, Lindsay Master, Adwoa Dadzie, Orfeu M Buxton, Anne-Marie Chang, Lauren Hale
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesSleep health is essential for wellbeing, yet few studies examine sociodemographic differences among young adults using objective sleep measures. We examined sociodemographic differences in actigraphic sleep health among a national, diverse sample of several hundred young adults.MethodsData from the Young Adult Sleep Sub-Study were collected during year 22 (mean age = 22.1 ± 0.3 years; range 21.6–24.4) of the Future of Families and Child Wellbeing Study (n = 442), a national, diverse sample of US-based young adults. Participants wore wrist-actigraphs for ~2 weeks. Multivariable linear regression models assessed whether sex, race, Hispanic/Latino ethnicity, education level, and employment status (simultaneously adjusted) were associated with dimensions of actigraphic sleep health.ResultsIn adjusted models, women had longer total sleep time (TST), earlier sleep onset, and less weekend night catchup sleep than men. Black young adults had shorter TST, later sleep onset, more variability in nighttime TST, in sleep onset, and in sleep maintenance efficiency, and lower sleep regularity index (SRI) than White young adults. Young adults with a lower education level had greater variability in nighttime TST and in sleep timing and lower SRI than those with a higher education level. Young adults who were unemployed had later sleep timing, lower SRI, and less weekend night catchup sleep than those employed full-time.ConclusionsMale sex, Black race, lower education level, and unemployment are associated with poorer actigraphic sleep health among this sample of young adults. Strategies to improve young adult sleep health should specifically prioritize men, marginalized populations, and those of lower socioeconomic status. Statement of SignificanceThe present study examined sociodemographic differences in several dimensions of actigraphic sleep health, including total sleep time, sleep timing, sleep quality, and variability of these measures, among a large, national, diverse sample of young adults. Our findings indicate considerable disparities in young adult sleep health particularly affecting men, Black young adults, those with lower education levels, and those who are unemployed. Efforts should be made to address potential sources of these sleep disparities to improve young adult sleep health across the population. Keywords: actigraphy, total sleep time, sleep timing, sleep maintenance efficiency, sleep variability, sleep regularity index, sociodemographic differences, diversity, national sample, young adulthood

Introduction

Young adulthood, defined by the Society for Adolescent Health and Medicine as between 18 and 25 years of age [1], represents a period during which parental surveillance decreases, and autonomy over health behaviors such as sleep increases [2]. Sufficient, quality, regular sleep is vital for optimal wellbeing, including metabolism [3], mental health [4], and immunity [5]. Insufficient sleep is prevalent among younger adults, with 27 per cent of adults aged 18–44 years in the US sleeping fewer [6] than the recommended nightly minimum of 7 h [7]. Sleep patterns are not evenly distributed across sociodemographic groups and may contribute to health disparities among racial and ethnic minorities and those of lower socioeconomic status (SES) [8]. It is therefore important to identify sociodemographic differences in young adult sleep health, which may assist in the development of specialized interventions. Moreover, examining understudied sleep dimensions such as sleep variability is essential given their negative associations with various aspects of health and wellbeing [9].

Previous studies have demonstrated differences in young adult sleep health by sex, but few studies have examined the dimension of sleep variability. Female young adults self-report earlier sleep timing [10–12] and longer duration [11–14] but poorer quality [12, 15–20] compared to male young adults. One large nationally representative study among over 11,000 Americans ≥6 years of age measuring sleep with wrist actigraphy corroborated that female young adults had earlier sleep onset and longer sleep duration than male young adults. However, there were no sex differences in sleep efficiency, an objective measure of sleep quality [21]. Another study among 31 young adults measuring sleep with polysomnography confirmed that women had longer total sleep time (TST) but also found that women had better sleep efficiency than men [22]. Therefore, there is mixed evidence regarding differences in sleep efficiency between female and male young adults. Notably, neither of these studies examined sex differences in sleep variability, emphasizing the lack of research on this sleep dimension among young adults.

Regarding racial differences in sleep health, Black young adults self-report shorter sleep duration and lower sleep quality than White young adults [13, 14, 19], but there is a lack of studies on sleep variability specifically. A large, nationally representative study among over 11 thousand Americans using wrist actigraphy across the lifespan found that Black young adults had shorter sleep duration, later sleep onset, and lower sleep efficiency than White young adults, with the sleep efficiency difference minimal in young adulthood but increasing across the lifespan [21]. Other studies among large samples of adults aged 18 and older demonstrate greater sleep variability (represented by various measures including individual standard deviation and coefficient of variance) is greater among Black compared to White adults [23, 24], but there is a lack of research among young adults specifically. There is also mixed and limited evidence regarding differences in young adult sleep health by Hispanic/Latino ethnicity. In one national study of nearly 325,000 young adults, Hispanic young adults of any race reported poorer sleep quality than White young adults [19]. However, in another nationally representative sample of college students, there were no significant differences in sleep duration, insomnia symptoms, or non-restorative sleep between Black or Hispanic/Latino students of any race versus White college students after adjustment for other sociodemographic and health behavior characteristics [25]. Given that these studies examined Hispanic/Latino ethnicity as an umbrella category including any race, it is uncertain how sleep health differs by Hispanic/Latino ethnicity, beyond racial differences.

Unlike sex and race, few studies examine associations between SES and sleep health specifically among young adults. Studies among adults aged 18–65 demonstrate that lower income, lower education level [26, 27], and being unemployed [26] are associated with poorer self-reported sleep quality [26], and one national study demonstrated that young adults with lower education levels had shorter self-reported sleep duration [14]. However, most existing research does not include a national sample, reducing generalizability. Therefore, more research on sleep health disparities by SES among a national sample of young adults specifically is warranted, particularly examining sleep variability.

Sleep health is a multidimensional construct that includes sleep duration, timing, quality, and variability [28]. Notably, few studies examine sociodemographic differences in young adult sleep variability, which has been linked to negative wellbeing including poorer academic performance and higher body mass index [29] in this population. Investigating sociodemographic differences in sleep variability among young adults is therefore crucial. Moreover, most studies examine sleep using self-reported measures, which can deviate from objective measures such as actigraphy [30]. Therefore, there is a need to examine sociodemographic differences in sleep health among young adults across the United States using objective sleep measures. Moreover, few studies examine sociodemographic disparities in the same cohort over time. The present study examined sociodemographic differences in sleep health among a known cohort that has provided similar actigraphic sleep data during adolescence and will continue to be followed in the future, allowing the tracking of sleep health disparities across the lifespan within the same individuals.

The present study examined sociodemographic differences in several dimensions of actigraphic sleep, including TST, sleep timing, sleep quality, and sleep variability, among a large, national, diverse sample of young adults. We hypothesized that men, Black young adults, those with lower education levels, and those who were unemployed would demonstrate poorer actigraphic sleep health.

Materials and Methods

Participants

Data were from the Young Adult Sleep Sub-Study collected during the year 22 wave (data collected 2020–2024) of the Future of Families (previously named Fragile Families) and Child Wellbeing Study (FFCWS), a longitudinal cohort oversampled for nonmarital births, which resulted in a greater proportion of racial/ethnic minority mothers and those of lower SES compared to the US population. More details regarding the sample and design may be found elsewhere [31]. This study was conducted according to the guidelines established in the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975 (revised 1983), and all procedures involving human participants were approved by the Princeton University and Stony Brook University Institutional Review Boards (CORIHS B) (FWA #00000125). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

The FFCWS birth cohort consists of 4,898 children born from 1998 to 2000 in 20 large US cities recruited using a complex, multi-stage clustered sampling design [32]. In the first stage, a random sample of cities with populations ≥200,000, stratified by levels of welfare generosity, child support enforcement, and unemployment, was selected. Then, random samples of marital and non-marital births were recruited from local hospitals at the time of the child’s birth, and data were collected at subsequent waves, when focal participants were approximately ages 1, 3, 5, 9, 15, and 22 years old. During the year 15 wave, 3,444 young adults completed the larger FFCWS. A randomly selected subsample (n = 1,090) was invited to the FFCWS sub-study, and n = 1,049 (96.2 per cent) assented. Families were eligible for the year 22 follow-up wave if the young adult was alive and participated at year 15.

Of 603 young adults who consented to the year 22 sleep sub-study, n = 483 provided ≥1 night of actigraphy data; n = 39 were excluded due to not providing ≥3 nights of actigraphy data, and n = 2 were excluded due to not providing employment information, resulting in an analytical sample of n = 442. Female compared to male sex (OR = 1.39, p=.019) and being employed full-time compared to part-time (OR = 1.47, p<.001) predicted higher odds of inclusion in the analytical sample of n = 442 (vs. n = 607 excluded from the original 1,049 invited to the sleep sub-study). All sociodemographic factors were simultaneously adjusted in main analyses. Study method and results are reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement for cross-sectional studies [33] (Table S1).

Materials and measures

Wrist actigraphy

Sleep measures were collected with a wrist-worn accelerometer with off-wrist detection (Actiwatch Spectrum Plus; Philips-Respironics, Murrysville, PA), and study participants were asked to wear the watch on their non-dominant wrist for two weeks. Accelerometer devices measure movements, from which patterns of sleep and wake may be estimated [34]. Device data were downloaded with Philips Actiware software (Versions 6.0.9–6.1.1; Philips-Respironics, 2017). At least two independent scorers (blinded to each other) determined cut-point (i.e. start and end time that define a 24-h day), validity of days, and sleep intervals with a duration of ≥30 min using a validated procedure [35]. Differences between the scorers in the determination of the number of valid days, cut-point, number of sleep intervals, and any >15-min differences in duration or wake after sleep onset (WASO) for each recording were compared and adjudicated, with a third scorer adjudicating any remaining discrepancies. The scorers determined sleep intervals using a decrease in activity levels (medium-activity threshold of 40 activity counts/min) and the aid of light levels for sleep onset and offset [36], and a nighttime sleep interval was split into two intervals (main sleep and nap) if there was an awakening ≥1 h during this interval. No sleep intervals were set if the duration was <30 min. Sleep measures within the present study were based on data from the main nighttime sleep interval (the longest interval that overlapped any of the time between 22:00 and 8:00) and did not include naps, except 24-h TST and sleep regularity index (SRI) [37], which were based on 24-h sleep/wake patterns. A sleep actigraphy day was determined invalid and no sleep interval was set if there were ≥ 4 total hours of off-wrist time, with the exception of the first and last day (device should have been worn at least 2 h on the first day). Other invalidation criteria were constant false activity due to battery failure, data unable to be retrieved or recovered, or an off-wrist period of ≥60 min within 10 min of the scored beginning or end of the main sleep period for that day. Nights were excluded from present analyses if the young adult had an all-nighter (i.e. no sleep interval was set within that 24-h day); n = 2 nights or 0.04 per cent of all nights. Participants must have provided ≥3 valid actigraphy nights (any day type) to be included in the main analyses.

Actigraphic sleep measures

Average sleep measures

All average sleep measures were calculated as the person-average across all days of actigraphy monitoring. Nighttime sleep onset and offset were the start and end of the main nighttime sleep interval, respectively, calculated in time from midnight. Nighttime TST was calculated as the amount of time between sleep onset and offset, minus WASO. 24-h TST was calculated as the amount of time between sleep onset and offset for all sleep intervals (main nighttime interval plus any naps) during a 24-h interval, minus WASO. Nighttime sleep maintenance efficiency (SMEff) represents the percentage of sleep duration (sleep offset – sleep onset, which includes time in WASO) that the individual spent asleep during the nighttime sleep interval and was calculated as 1 − (WASO/sleep duration) and multiplied by 100 [38]. The term “sleep maintenance efficiency” is used instead of the more common “sleep efficiency” due to exclusion of sleep onset latency in the calculation, which is typically represented in sleep efficiency.

Variability sleep measures

Variability sleep measures were chosen based on previous literature [39]. Variability in nighttime TST, onset, offset, and SMEff across the monitoring period were each represented by the individual standard deviation (ISD) per young adult. Another measure was the SRI calculated per person based on 24-h sleep, ranging from 0 (low regularity) to 100 (high regularity) [37]. The score represents the percentage probability that an individual is in the same state (sleep/wake) at any two timepoints 24 h apart, averaged across all days. A “0” indicates the individual is sleeping and waking totally at random, whereas “100” indicates the individual is sleeping and waking at exactly the same times each day. Social jetlag (SJL), a misalignment of sleep midpoint between work and free days, was calculated in hours as | average sleep midpoint on Friday–Saturday nights − average sleep midpoint on Sunday–Thursday nights | [40]. Weekend night catchup sleep was calculated as average TST on Friday–Saturday nights − average TST on Sunday–Thursday nights and included negative values. Only participants with ≥1 weekday night and ≥1 weekend night (of their minimum three total nights of actigraphy) were included in the SJL and weekend night catchup sleep measures (n = 425).

Sociodemographic characteristics

Sex information was collected at birth, and age was calculated based on birth date. All other sociodemographic characteristics were self-reported on the year 22 young adult survey. Participants were asked, “Which of these categories best describes your race? (SELECT ALL THAT APPLY)” with the potential answers of “White,” “Black or African American,” “Asian or Pacific Islander,” “Native American or Alaskan Native,” and “Other.” Race was grouped into exclusive categories of White/Caucasian only, Black/African only, multi-racial, or a category with other (including “Asian or Pacific Islander,” “Native American or Alaskan Native,” and other race) and/or solely reporting a characteristic that was not race/ethnicity (e.g. nationality or skin color). Participants were next asked, “Are you of Hispanic or Latino origin or descent?” with potential answers of “Yes, Hispanic or Latino origin/descent” and “No, not Hispanic or Latino origin/descent.” Hispanic/Latino ethnicity was coded as a separate variable from race. Participants were asked, “What is the highest grade or educational degree you have ever completed as of today?” with potential separate options of 1st grade up until 12th grade (no diploma/GED [general educational development]), “High school diploma,” “GED,” “Associate/Junior College (AA),” “Bachelor’s Degree,” “Master's Degree (MA, MBA, MS, MSW),” “Doctoral Degree (PhD),” and “Professional Degree (MD, LLD, DDS).” Highest education level was grouped into exclusive categories of did not complete high school, completed high school (including GED), completed some college, or college graduate. Participants were also asked, “Last week, did you do any regular work for pay? Include any work you might have done in your own business (or military service) where you got a regular paycheck” with potential answers of “yes” or “no.” They were then asked, “How many hours do you usually work per week at this job? Include regular overtime hours. If you work more than one job at a time, answer about the one you usually work the most hours.” Employment status was grouped into unemployed (did not work last week for pay), part-time (usually works 1–34 h/week), or full-time (usually works ≥35 h/week). Currently attending school was queried as “Are you currently attending college or university, or graduate school, including medical and law school?” with potential answers of “yes” or “no.” Lives with primary caregiver (yes/no) was constructed based on the answer to the question, “Who lives with you? (SELECT ALL THAT APPLY)” with options including the youth's primary caregiver.

Statistical analyses

Analyses were conducted in SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina). Most variables met standards for normality (skew < |2| and kurtosis < |7|) [41]. The ISDs of sleep onset and offset were positively skewed (skew ≥ 2) and/or leptokurtotic (kurtosis ≥ 7) and were winsorized at the upper limit (i.e. values above the 99th percentile were replaced with the nearest value ≤99th percentile). After winsorization, all continuous variables in analyses and model residuals met criteria for normality.

Main analyses

We tested bivariate Pearson correlations among the sleep measures (Table S2). Linear regression models were used to test whether the sociodemographic characteristics of sex, race, Hispanic/Latino ethnicity, education level, and employment status predicted each sleep measure (average nighttime TST, 24-h TST, sleep onset, sleep offset, SMEff, ISD-variability in nighttime TST, in sleep onset, in sleep offset, and in SMEff, SRI, SJL, and weekend night catchup sleep). Analyses were first conducted with each sociodemographic characteristic separately predicting each sleep measure and then in multivariable analyses adjusted for all sociodemographic predictors simultaneously. All models additionally adjusted for age. Supplementary analyses examined sociodemographic differences in average sleep measures separately on weekdays (wake day Monday–Friday; Table S3) and weekends (wake day Saturday and Sunday; Table S4). Sensitivity analyses adjusting for whether the young adult was currently attending college, university, or graduate school, living with their primary caregiver, and for the year of data collection (due to potential for differences related to COVID-19), including only youth with ≥7 nights of actigraphy data with ≥1 weekend night (n = 388; Tables S5–S6), analyzing SRI among only those with ≥7 consecutive nights of actigraphy data (n = 293; Table S7), and analyzing ISD while adjusting for the mean (Table S8) yielded similar results as main analyses. Alpha <.05 (two-sided) was deemed statistically significant. Adjusted p-values were calculated within each of the four broader sleep dimensions (TST, sleep timing, sleep quality, and sleep variability) based on the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) [42] and presented in the Results section alongside raw p-values and in Table S9.

The mean number of valid actigraphy nights provided per participant was 11.5 ± 3.4 (range = 3–19). Other descriptive statistics are in Tables 1–2.

Sociodemographic differences in actigraphic sleep

Sex differences

In fully adjusted analyses, women had longer actigraphic nighttime (b = +27.4 min, 95%CI = 15.2, 39.6, β = .20; praw and pFDR < .001) and 24-h (b = +33.2 min, 95%CI = 21.7, 44.6, β = .27; praw and pFDR < .001) TST than men (see Fig. 1 and Table 3). Women had earlier sleep onset (b = −34.5 min, 95%CI = −54.3, −14.8, β = −.16; praw < .001; pFDR = .001) and less weekend night catchup sleep (b = −20.9 min, 95%CI = −37.3, −4.6, β = −.13; praw = .012; pFDR = .085) than men (see Figs 1 and 2 and Tables 3 and 4). There were no significant differences in sleep offset, SMEff, ISD-variability, SRI, or social jetlag by sex.

Race and ethnicity differences

In fully adjusted analyses, Black (b = −39.5 min, 95%CI = −55.0, −24.0, β = −.30; praw and pFDR < .001) and multi-racial (b = −28.1 min, 95%CI = −53.2, −3.0, β = −.11; praw = .028; pFDR = .057) young adults had shorter actigraphic nighttime TST than White young adults. Black young adults also had shorter 24-h TST than White young adults (b = −21.5 min, 95%CI = −36.0, −6.9, β = −.18; praw and pFDR = .004). Black young adults had later sleep onset (b = +51.1 min, 95%CI = 26.0, 76.1, β = .24; praw and pFDR < .001) and marginally lower SMEff (b = −0.8 per cent, 95%CI = −1.7, <0.1, β = −.12; praw and pFDR = .051) than White young adults.

Black young adults had greater variability (ISD) in nighttime TST (b = +9.6 ISD-minutes, 95%CI = 0.9, 18.3, β = .13; praw = .031; pFDR = .054), in sleep onset (b = +17.1 ISD-minutes, 95%CI = 3.0, 31.3, β = .15; praw = .018; pFDR = .054), and in SMEff (b = +0.4 ISD-%, 95%CI = <0.1, 0.7, β = .14; praw = .025; pFDR = .054) compared to White young adults. Black young adults also had lower SRI (b = −5.7 units, 95%CI = −8.8, −2.6, β = −.22; praw < .001; pFDR = .002). Multi-racial young adults had greater variability in sleep onset compared to White young adults (b = +28.3 ISD-minutes, 95%CI = 5.4, 51.2, β = .13; praw = .015; pFDR = .108). There were no significant differences in sleep offset, ISD-variability in sleep offset, social jetlag, or weekend night catchup sleep by race.

There were no significant differences between Hispanic/Latino and non-Hispanic/Latino young adults in actigraphic sleep.

Education level differences

There were no significant differences in average actigraphic sleep measures (nighttime or 24-h TST, sleep timing, or SMEff) by education level. Young adults whose highest education level was less than a college degree had greater variability in nighttime TST than young adults who graduated college (less than high school: b = +32.1 ISD-minutes, 95%CI = 17.5, 46.7, β = .28; praw and pFDR < .001; completed high school: b = +22.0 ISD-minutes, 95%CI = 10.2, 33.8, β = .30; praw < .001; pFDR = .002; some college/technical school: b = +19.6 ISD-minutes, 95%CI = 8.2, 31.1, β = .27; praw < .001; pFDR = .006; see Table 4). Only those whose highest education level was less than high school had greater variability in sleep onset (b = +39.9 ISD-minutes, 95%CI = 16.2, 63.6, β = .21; praw = .001; pFDR = .002) and in SMEff (b = +0.5 ISD-%, 95%CI = <0.1, 1.1, β = .13; praw = .040; pFDR = .056) than those who graduated college. Young adults whose highest education level was less than high school (b = +48.7 ISD-minutes, 95%CI = 20.2, 77.2, β = .22; praw < .001; pFDR = .002) or completed high school (b = +32.5 ISD-minutes, 95%CI = 9.5, 55.6, β = .23; praw = .006; pFDR = .013), but not those with some college/technical school, had greater variability in sleep offset than young adults who graduated college. Young adults with less than high school (b = −9.0 units, 95%CI = −14.3, −3.8, β = −.21; praw < .001; pFDR = .002) or completed high school (b = −6.0 units, 95%CI = −10.2, −1.8, β = −.22; praw = .006; pFDR = .013) as their highest level of education (vs. graduated college) had lower SRI compared to young adults who graduated college. There were no significant differences in social jetlag or weekend night catchup sleep by education level.

Employment status differences

There were no significant differences in actigraphic nighttime or 24-h TST or SMEff by employment status in fully adjusted analyses. Those who were unemployed or worked part-time had later sleep onset (unemployed: b = +46.9 min, 95%CI = 24.6, 69.2, β = .21; praw and pFDR < .001; part-time: b = +38.3 min, 95%CI = 13.9, 62.8, β = .16; praw = .002; pFDR = .004) and offset (unemployed: b = +47.9 min, 95%CI = 22.8, 73.0, β = .20; praw and pFDR < .001; part-time: b = +35.7 min, 95%CI = 8.1, 63.2, β = .14; praw and pFDR = .011) than those who worked full-time.

Those who were employed part-time, but not those who were unemployed, had less variability in SMEff (b = −0.3 ISD-%, 95%CI = −0.6, <0.1, β = −.12; praw = .030; pFDR = .071) and less SJL (b = −21.7 min, 95%CI = −36.9, −6.5, β = −.16; praw = .005; pFDR = .037) than those employed full-time. Those who were unemployed (b = −4.2 units, 95%CI = −6.9, −1.4, β = −.15; praw = .003; pFDR = .022) had lower SRI than those employed full-time. Those who were either unemployed (b = −25.8 min, 95%CI = −44.4, −7.2, β = −.15; praw = .007; pFDR = .024) or working part-time (b = −22.9 min, 95%CI = −43.2, −2.7, β = −.12; praw = .027; pFDR = .071) had less weekend night catchup sleep than those employed full-time. There were no significant differences in ISD-variability in nighttime TST, sleep onset, or sleep offset by employment status.

Discussion

The present study examined sociodemographic differences in multiple dimensions of actigraphic sleep health among a large, national, diverse sample of young adults, with standardized effect sizes for these differences ranging from small to medium. Our findings suggest considerable differences in young adult sleep health by sociodemographic characteristics including sex, race, education level, and employment status, offering priorities for future interventions to improve sleep health and overall wellbeing.

Female young adults demonstrated nearly 30-min longer nighttime TST than did men, driven by earlier sleep onset (more so on weekdays than during the weekend), corroborating findings at age 15 within this cohort [43] and previous literature among young adults [10–13, 22]. Biological differences such as hormone fluctuations, particularly during the late-luteal and early-follicular phases of the menstrual cycle, may drive higher sleep need in female adults [44]. Despite female young adults self-reporting poorer sleep quality than male young adults in previous studies [12, 15–20], the current study found no sex differences in SMEff, an objective measure of sleep quality. Although women had longer average TST than did men in the present study, with the difference larger on weekday nights, men had longer sleep on weekends compared to weekdays, possibly suggesting an accumulation of a sleep deficit across the work/school week that men attempt to “catch up” on weekend nights.

We found that Black young adults had shorter nighttime TST driven by later sleep onset primarily on weekday nights, more variability in nighttime TST, in timing, and in SMEff, and lower SRI than White young adults. The present findings regarding TST corroborate previous research both in this cohort at age 15 [43] and in other samples of young adults [13, 21]. Moreover, our findings expand upon previous literature by demonstrating that Black young adults experience later sleep onset and more day-to-day variability in their sleep than do White young adults. These findings remained even after adjustment for other sociodemographic characteristics related to SES, such as education level and employment status. Later sleep onset may be due to neighborhood factors such as crime or noise [45] or racial discrimination and/or microaggressions [46] that make it difficult to fall asleep. Differences across the week in these factors may promote more variable sleep. Future interventions to improve sleep health in Black young adults should consider prioritizing factors that interfere with sleep onset to not only increase TST but also potentially reduce sleep variability. We also found marginally lower sleep maintenance efficiency in Black compared to White young adults, with larger differences on the weekend. This disparity may grow progressively wider across the lifespan, as indicated by one large actigraphy study among several thousand Americans [21]. Additionally, most previous studies combined individuals of any race that had Hispanic/Latino ethnicity into one broad category, not accounting for racial differences among those identifying as Hispanic and/or Latino. The present study found no significant differences in sleep health when Hispanic/Latino ethnicity was treated as a separate characteristic than race, suggesting racial differences may be more relevant to young adult sleep disparities than Hispanic/Latino ethnicity.

The present study is among the first to examine disparities in young adult actigraphic sleep health by education level. Those with lower education levels, particularly no high school or equivalent diploma, had greater variability in nighttime TST, in sleep timing, and in SMEff, and lower SRI. These differences in sleep variability are reflected by shorter weekend but not weekday TST among those with lower education levels. These disparities by education level may also reflect SES, such that those from more affluent families may have more regular sleep timing in young adulthood due to set bedtimes during childhood and adolescence [47]. Additionally, those from less affluent families with less education may also experience more neighborhood disadvantage that interferes with sleep onset and quality [48], which could promote more variable sleep across the week. Widespread, systemic changes that help improve socioeconomic and neighborhood circumstances may be best suited to reduce sleep disparities by race and education level.

Those who were unemployed tended to have later sleep timing than those who were employed full-time, with larger sleep offset differences during the work week. Young adults who do not have to wake up early to work may prefer to go to bed and wake up later due to their natural circadian rhythm, which delays during adolescence and remains delayed in young adulthood relative to childhood and later life [49]. Those employed part-time also had less SJL than those employed full-time, whereas both those who were unemployed and those working part-time had less weekend night catchup sleep. Those who are unemployed may have similar SJL levels as those who are employed full-time for different reasons: those who are unemployed may have more time to engage in social interactions during the weekend that delay sleep relative to weekday nights, whereas those who work full-time may have insufficient sleep during the work week, attempting to “catch up” for lost sleep during the weekend.

The present findings indicate that inequities exist across multiple dimensions of sleep health among young adults. Sleep health was poorest among both men and marginalized populations, including Black young adults, those with lower education levels, and those who were unemployed. The present study is among the first to demonstrate sociodemographic differences in actigraphic sleep variability among young adults, which may have negative implications for health and wellbeing [29]. Given the many potential negative impacts of poor sleep health on wellbeing, including cardiometabolism [3], mental health [4], and immunity [5], these findings support the claim that sleep health is a public health priority that should be improved, particularly among marginalized communities, using a holistic, multidimensional approach [50]. Potentially modifiable factors such as higher education and employment are often subject to systemic and structural barriers that limit access to these opportunities. Policy-level factors should be targeted (e.g. improving high school graduation among marginalized communities) to reduce these barriers and increase college completion for young adults, which may help them obtain employment with regular schedules that are more conducive to sleep health.

The current study had notable strengths and some limitations. Strengths of the study include the moderately large, national, diverse sample of young adults, which increases generalizability, and the use of actigraphy, an objective measure of sleep. We also captured a number of sociodemographic characteristics including sex, race, ethnicity, education level, and employment status and measured several dimensions of sleep, including not only actigraphic TST but also timing, quality, and variability. A limitation is the cross-sectional nature of the present analyses, which precludes the ability to establish temporal precedence or causation. We also cannot elucidate the mechanisms underlying the observed sociodemographic differences in sleep health. Moreover, given our sample was limited to US-based young adults, the present findings may not be applicable to young adults residing in other countries. Lastly, missingness from the original 1049 who assented during the Year 15 wave was more likely among males and those who were employed part-time, potentially leading to selection bias in the results.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrated sociodemographic differences in several dimensions of actigraphic sleep health among a large, national, diverse sample of young adults. Efforts should be made to address potential sources of these sleep disparities, such as racial discrimination, lower education level, and unemployment, to improve young adult sleep health across the population.
