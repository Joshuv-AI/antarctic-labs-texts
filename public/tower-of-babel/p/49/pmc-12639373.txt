Differential reactivity of bacillithiol, mycothiol and glutathione with nitroxyl (HNO): Structural determinants and potential biological implications
Authors: Yang Zeng, Parker Owens, Zijun Xia, Patricia C. Dos Santos, S. Bruce King
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Low-molecular-weight (LMW) thiols play critical roles in maintaining redox buffer systems required for normal biological function. Glutathione (GSH) represents the most common LMW thiol found in Nature, but Gram-positive bacteria utilize bacillithiol (BSH) or mycothiol (MSH). Nitroxyl (HNO) can influence bacterial transcription through persulfide formation, a biological phenomenon that prompts the examination of the reactions of HNO with these LMW thiols. The development and application of colorimetric and enzymatic (Bacillus subtilis thioredoxin assay) methods combined with mass spectrometry of reaction products show the unique reactivity of BSH to favor sulfinamide adduct formation upon equimolar reaction with HNO. The reaction profile with GSH results in nearly equal distribution between sulfinamide:disulfide, whereas reaction with MSH only yields disulfide. These varied results led to the preparation of a group of BSH and MSH analogs, and their reactions with HNO reveal the requirement for a free amine group for sulfinamide formation. The thiol and amine group pKa's appear critical for sulfinamide generation, with the thiolate acting as a nucleophile to attack HNO and the ammonium donating a proton to facilitate water loss from the N-hydroxysulfenamide intermediate. Furthermore, the B. subtilis thioredoxin system efficiently reduces BSSB with a calculated KM_BSSB = 34 ± 3 μM and Vmax = 152 ± 3.4 nmol/min/nmol TrxR (kcat = 2.5 s−1), but does not reduce bacillithiol sulfinamide. Similarly, this thioredoxin reduces MSSM with a calculated KM_MSSM of 9 ± 2.1 μM and Vmax of 103 ± 7.1 nmol/min/nmol TrxR (kcat = 1.7 s−1). Bacillithiol possesses a unique structure that allows a rapid reaction with HNO to form a stable product that may provide a basis for antibiotic development or clues for further biological roles of nitroxyl.

Introduction

Glutathione (GSH) is the most common low-molecular weight (LMW) thiol in Nature, acting as a redox buffer and participating in many cellular activities [1]. Many Gram-positive bacteria produce structurally distinct LMW thiols, including bacillithiol (BSH) and mycothiol (MSH, Fig. 1) [2,3]. BSH and MSH are proposed to perform equivalent roles as GSH, but other distinct functions may exist. For example, BSH persulfide (BSSH), the formal oxidation product of BSH and H2S, influences overall bacterial sulfur metabolism through transcriptional control [4]. Treatment of Staphylococcus aureus with nitroxyl (HNO), a reactive nitrogen species (RNS) with robust thiol reactivity [5], produces a transcriptome like H2S exposure, and increases BSSH levels that influence transcription related to sulfur metabolism [6,7]. HNO treatment of Bacillus subtilis decreases the amount of free BSH, while maintaining the BSH/total thiol ratio when compared to control non-treated cells [8]. Mass spectrometric (MS) analysis provides evidence for the in vivo formation of bacillthiol sulfinamide (Fig. 1) after HNO challenge [8]. Furthermore, inactivation of BSH biosynthesis in B. subtilis leads to enhanced sensitivity to HNO, demonstrating BSH's protective capabilities towards HNO [8].Fig. 1Chemical structures of glutathione, mycothiol, bacillithiol and bacillithiol sulfinamide.Fig. 1

Chemical structures of glutathione, mycothiol, bacillithiol and bacillithiol sulfinamide.

Nitroxyl (HNO) shows distinct biological actions from nitric oxide (NO), but the requirement for HNO donors and difficulties with HNO detection limit HNO's study in biological chemistry [9,10]. Endogenous HNO formation in plants and HNO generation through S-nitrosothiol reduction in macrophages support current interest in HNO and clinical applications of HNO-releasing drugs [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. Chemically, HNO acts as a strong electrophile (much better than NO) and oxidizes thiols to disulfides and sulfinamides (Fig. 2) [5]. HNO reacts with thiols to give an N-hydroxysulfenamide adduct (like a thiol-formaldehyde adduct) that either rearranges to a sulfinamide or further condenses with thiol to yield a disulfide and hydroxylamine. This reactivity has been observed in both small-molecule thiols and thiol-containing proteins, but little work examining the factors that control product distribution exists (Fig. 2) [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. A single computational study suggests sulfinamides form upon protonation of the hydroxyl group of the N-hydroxysulfenamide intermediate [22].Fig. 2Generally accepted reaction of HNO and thiols leading to sulfinamides and disulfides.Fig. 2

Generally accepted reaction of HNO and thiols leading to sulfinamides and disulfides.

Recently, we reported that HNO reacts in vitro with BSH to generate BSH sulfinamide:BSSB in a 6:1 ratio as measured by MS [8]. The product ratio from the HNO reaction with GSH decreases greatly to <1, suggesting that the LMW thiol structure influences product distribution [8]. We tentatively proposed that the malic acid group of BSH provides a proton shuttled by the free amine group to facilitate sulfinamide formation [8]. The α-configuration of the malate group places the malate and cysteine moieties in a cis configuration and in proximity of the N-hydroxysulfenamide intermediate to allow proton transfer. While MS experiments indicate the formation of sulfinamides in reactions with HNO and BSH or GSH in vitro, the reliance on total ion count in the absence of the sulfinamide standards of these LMW thiols limits MS-based quantification of product ratios.

In this study, we characterize the distinct reactivities of GSH, BSH, and MSH using colorimetric and enzymatic methods to quantify the amount of reducible and non-reducible products of HNO reactions, providing complementary analytical approaches to MS detection. By taking advantage of the modular composition of BSH and MSH, we synthesized analogs to investigate the chemical factors dictating reaction paths, which reveal the amine/amide group of BSH/MSH to be important in product formation. Lastly, we provide kinetic evidence that the B. subtilis thioredoxin system is effective in reducing BSSB, a minor product of the HNO reaction, but not reactive towards sulfinamide adducts.

Materials and methods

Synthesis-Synthetic procedures and supporting mass spectrometry (MS) and 1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) data for the preparation of BSH, MSH, and their derivatives are described in the Supporting Information.

Colorimetric quantification of reduced and reducible thiol adducts-The original thiol concentration in freshly prepared 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazine ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) buffer (5 mM, pH = 7.4) was determined using the 5,5′-dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB, Ellman's) assay by monitoring the UV absorbance at 412 nm and the molar absorption coefficient of 14,150 M−1cm−1 [23].

Quantification of reaction products following treatment with Angeli's salt (AS) required a modified method. An AS stock solution (50 mM) was freshly prepared in NaOH (10 mM) and used immediately. Reactions of 500 μL included 1 mM thiol solutions in 5 mM HEPES buffer, pH = 7.4, were incubated with 1, 2.5, and 5 equivalents of AS at room temperature for 30 min. Reaction mixture aliquots (125 μL) were then subjected to a DTNB assay to determine the amount of remaining free thiol, and the remaining 375 μL was treated with 20 μL of 0.5 M tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP) for 30 min. The excess of TCEP was then quenched with 80 μL 3-azidopropanol [24,25]. Aliquots (125 μL) of this reaction mixture were used for a final DTNB assay to determine the amount of reducible product as described above.

Enzymatic quantification of reducible thiol and their adducts. The B. subtilis thioredoxin system (Trx), composed of thioredoxin reductase (TrxR) and thioredoxin A (TrxA), was used for the NADPH-dependent quantification of reducible oxidized thiols as previously described [26]. All Trx reactions were performed anaerobically in a glovebox and carried out with capped anaerobic quartz cuvettes (Starna). Solutions of NADPH, GSSG, BSSB, and MSSM were freshly prepared in degassed 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer pH 8.0 in the anaerobic chamber. Each reaction was initiated by adding the substrate GSSG, BSSB, or MSSM with a syringe. Reaction mixtures of BSH or GSH (1 mM) and AS (1 mM) in 5 mM HEPES buffer (pH = 7.4) were prepared in the anaerobic chamber and transferred to the cuvette with a syringe after a 30-min incubation at room temperature. Unless otherwise indicated, 1 mL reactions were performed with varying concentrations of each substrate in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH = 8.0) containing 147 nM TrxR, 20 μM TrxA, 0.4 mM NADPH, and 0.25 mM EDTA. NADPH oxidation was monitored by absorbance at 340 nm in a Cary 50 spectrophotometer in continuum mode, and the negative slopes of absorbance per minute were converted to nanomoles of NADPH oxidized per minute using the molar absorption coefficient of 6220 M−1 cm−1.

Results

Quantification of products of reactions between biothiols and HNO

Characterization of reactions of biothiols and HNO was performed in vitro and allowed for quantification of reaction products. BSH preparation followed the route we recently used that combines elements of the two reported BSH syntheses, and produces 200–225 mg of BSH [8,27,28]. MSH synthesis followed an earlier protocol with myo-inositol resolution being accomplished as described by Knapp [[29], [30], [31], [32]]. Synthetic details and spectra of BSH and MSH are presented in the Supporting Information.

Mass spectrometry and colorimetric quantification of the thiol or thiol adducts provide information on the products of the reactions of GSH, BSH, and MSH with varying amounts of HNO. MS analysis of reaction products shows formation of sulfinamides, including previously described cyclic sulfinamides [8], as well as disulfides, and the presence of unreacted thiol. The lack of pure sulfinamide standards of these natural LMW thiols limits MS-based assignment of accurate product ratios. A standard colorimetric assay measures the total free thiol concentration before and after the HNO reaction. Our adapted colorimetric assay, which utilizes TCEP reduction followed by quenching prior to DTNB thiol quantification, provides the combined concentration of free and thiol adducts that could be reduced by TCEP (reducible derivatives), while the standard assay gives the concentration of free thiol only. Thus, if [Total Thiol] = [free thiol and reducible derivatives] + [non-reducible derivatives], then a ratio [non-reducible derivative]/[reducible derivative] gives the ratio of sulfinamide/disulfide, as the predicted and MS-identified products of the HNO reaction. Fig. 3 outlines the basic premise of this colorimetric assay, distinguishing sulfinamides from disulfides. Control NMR (1H, 13C and 31P) experiments show that TCEP does not reduce a model, t-butylsulfinamide, after 1 h at room temperature. Control experiments also show that TCEP completely reduces GSSG in the absence of HNO to give the expected concentration of GSH in these reactions. Further control experiments, in the absence of HNO, show that 3-azidopropanol completely quenches TCEP evidenced by a lack of increased absorbance at 412 nm in DTNB reactions. While reaction of HNO resulted in formation of two main products, disulfides and sulfinamides, oxidative modification of biological thiols may yield numerous reducible (disulfides, sulfenic acids, persulfides) and non-reducible (sulfinic and sulfonic acids, thioethers, sulfinamides) derivatives, making this method potentially applicable to the measurement of varying oxidative stresses [33].Fig. 3Reduction of Ellman's reagent with thiols and a general protocol used to determine TCEP non-reducible:reducible products from the reaction of HNO with thiols. The reaction products of MSH and HNO have not been previously reported. Notably, exposure to 1 mM MSH and 1 mM AS yielded only disulfide (Table 1). Enzymatic assay of the BSH and HNO reaction product provides an independent measure of the presence and amount of BSSB formed. Thioredoxin systems (Trx), composed of thioredoxin and thioredoxin reductase, use electrons from NADPH to reduce disulfides to thiols, playing important roles in thiol homeostasis. Escherichia coli, Homo sapiens, and B. subtilis Trx systems reduce protein disulfide and persulfides, as well as low molecular weight disulfides such as GSSG and insulin [26,34].Here, we tested the reactivity of the B. subtilis Trx system against BSSB, which has not been previously evaluated [26], but could serve as a method for quantifying BSSB formed in reactions with HNO. In fact, Trx reactions performed under anoxic conditions and using BSSB as substrate give a stoichiometric oxidation of NADPH. Furthermore, products of BSH and AS were also reacted with the Trx system, in which quantification of NADPH oxidation matches with the calculated concentration of reducible thiol (e.g. BSSB) formed in these reactions, as reported in Table 1. Collectively, these results show that BSSB is a suitable substrate of the Trx system and that sulfinamides formed in reactions with AS are not reducible by these enzymatic reactions.

Equivalent experiments using the Trx system were then performed using MSSM as the substrate. These analyses showed that MSSM is also a suitable substrate for this enzymatic disulfide reductase system with calculated kinetic constants Vmax of 103 ± 7.1 nmol/min/nmol TrxR, and KM_MSSM of 9 ± 2.1 μM (Fig. 5). Synthesis of BSH and MSH analogs

The modular composition of both BSH and MSH provides a synthetic opportunity to dissect the structural and chemical features of these molecules that give widely different product outcomes in their reactions with HNO. Straightforward modification of the synthetic routes to MSH and BSH provides N-acetyl BSH and deacetylated MSH (Fig. 7). Ligation of advanced MSH intermediate (1) with S-trityl (Trt)-N-tert-butyoxycarbonyl (BOC) cysteine yields the MSH derivative (2, Fig. 7) in 45 % yield. Acidic removal of the Trt and BOC groups gives deacetylated MSH that oxidizes over time to the disulfide. Triphenyl phosphine reduction of 3 followed by coupling with N, S-diacetyl cysteine gives a fully protected N-acetyl BSH derivative (4, Fig. 7) [27]. Removal of the protecting groups gives N-acetyl BSH in 36 % yield (Fig. 7). Deacetylated MSH and N-acetyl BSH provide the reciprocal structural partners of the natural products (N-acetyl BSH mimics MSH and deacetyl MSH mimics BSH). Deacetyl MSH is also a known intermediate in MSH biosynthesis, acting as the substrate for MshD, which yields MSH [3]. The known dimethyl ester (5, Fig. 7) provides a starting point to the dimethyl ester of BSH via 6 [27]. Lewis acid-catalyzed coupling of protected glucosamine azide and diallyl malate, predominantly gives 3, the α-isomer used in the synthesis of BSH (Fig. 7). This process gives smaller amounts of the more polar β-isomer (7, Fig. 7) that was separated by column chromatography. Coupling constant analysis of the anomeric proton supports the assignment of the β-isomer (J = 8.04 Hz compared to J = 3.59 Hz for the α-isomer). Zinc reduction of 7 gave the free amine, followed by coupling with S-trityl N-BOC cysteine to yield fully protected β-bacillithiol (8, Fig. 7). Global deprotection gave β-bacillithiol, which quickly oxidizes in air to the disulfide. Profile of HNO reactions with BSH and MSH analogs

Reaction of HNO with N-acetyl BSH and deacetylated MSH reveals the importance of the α-amino group of the cysteine portion of these molecules in product distribution. Treatment of 1 mM N-acetyl BSH with 1 mM AS only yields disulfide with the ratios of sulfinamide:disulfide being 0.36:1 and 3.79:1 at higher AS concentrations as expected (Table 1). MS analysis further confirms the structure of these products and that no sulfinamide forms upon reaction of N-acetyl BSH with HNO at 1 mM AS. On the other hand, exposure of 1 mM deacetylated MSH to AS shows an opposite trend, where the deprotection of the α-amino group enables formation of the sulfinamide adduct.

The structure-reactivity relationship analysis of BSH was also explored through modification of the malate moiety. First, a β-BSH analog was synthesized that moves the carboxylic acids further from the thiol, but the rapid oxidation to its disulfide form prevented HNO reaction analysis. Second, a BSH analog containing dimethyl malate was reacted with HNO. The reactivity of the α-amino group of cysteine may be modulated by the carboxylic acids of the malate group. The reaction containing equimolar dimethyl malate ester of BSH with HNO showed a drastic reduction in the ratio of sulfinamide:disulfide (Table 1). This derivative possesses the α-amine group of the cysteine part of the molecule, but lacks free carboxylic acids.

HNO reactions were examined with cysteine-glucosamine and N-acetyl cysteine glucosamine. These truncated analogs are common fragments of both BSH and MSH, as they lack the malate and inositol moieties. The synthesis of these molecules followed the literature procedure [8,35], and they were used as further tools to examine the influence of the amine group on product identity. Treatment of these compounds with AS showed again that the presence of the free amino group modulates the reaction outcome towards sulfinamide rather than disulfide (Table 1). Collectively, the analysis of biothiols and their analogs show the unique reactivity of BSH with HNO, which is justified by the α-amino group of cysteine, which directs this HNO chemistry towards the non-reducible sulfinamide species (Table 1).

Discussion

Nitroxyl, a reactive nitrogen species (RNS) chemically related to NO by one-electron reduction and protonation, demonstrates both different chemistry and biology from NO [36,37]. Nitroxyl's reactivity requires the use of donor molecules and rapid detection methods, both of which limit HNO's study. Unlike NO, clear endogenous biological pathways for HNO formation have not been described, but nitric oxide synthase (NOS)-catalyzed reactions [38], oxidation of hydroxylamine derivatives, and the direct reaction of RSNO with nucleophiles have been proposed [17,39]. Direct reaction of NO and hydrogen sulfide (H2S) forms HNO, placing HNO at the intersection of RNS and RSS signaling [40].

Chemically, HNO is highly electrophilic (much more than NO) and represents the nitrogen analog of formaldehyde, and both molecules show similar self-condensation reactivity. HNO reacts with nucleophiles, including phosphines and amines, oxidizing them to aza-ylides and diazenes [41,42]. GSH, BSH, and MSH each react with formaldehyde to yield hydroxymethyl adducts, and GSH reacts with HNO to give a mixture of GSSG and GSH-sulfinamide (Fig. 2) [5,[43], [44], [45]]. Here we show a ∼1:1 ratio of these products upon GSH treatment with equal amounts of HNO. Given recent reports that HNO influences sulfur metabolism in Gram-positive bacteria (many of which contain a bacterial NOS) [6,7,46], we examined the reactions of HNO with bacillithiol and mycothiol (MSH), important LMW biothiols responsible for redox homeostasis. We found that BSH reacts to predominantly form the sulfinamide while MSH gives the disulfide product. Despite the lack of pure standards of these biologically relevant sulfinamides, the measured ratios of reaction products detected by MS matched with values determined through colorimetric and enzymatic assay analysis. These three LMW biothiols (GSH, BSH, and MSH) demonstrate vastly different product outcomes in their reactions with HNO.

A proposed mechanism of BSH reaction with HNO is proposed in Fig. 8A. The reaction scheme accounts for the protonation states of the thiol and amine groups of BSH/GSH/MSH and their derivatives, providing an explanation for the observed sulfinamide:disulfide ratios. For sulfinamide formation from the reaction with HNO, the most likely reactive species would be where the thiol exists as the thiolate (RS−) and the amine as the ammonium ion (R–NH3+). Considering the already determined pKas for BSH, at pH 7.7, a significant portion of BSH (21 %) exists in this charged state (Fig. 8A) [47]. In this proposed mechanism, the thiolate reacts as a nucleophile, adding to HNO to form the N-hydroxysulfenamide intermediate upon protonation. The low thiol pKa (7.97) of BSH better facilitates the initial addition to HNO as compared to Cys (8.38), MSH (8.76), and GSH (8.93). The adjacent ammonium ion provides electrostatic stabilization for the resulting thiolate ion [47,48]. The ammonium ion of the cysteinyl group acts as an acid to donate a proton to the N-hydroxysulfenamide to assist in water loss to yield the sulfenium ion, the product-determining step. Water loss to generate the sulfenium ion cleaves the N–O bond and thus effectively removes NO/HNO. From the sulfenium ion, addition of water followed by tautomerization gives the sulfinamide (Fig. 8A). Interestingly, the amino group of BSH is slightly more acidic (∼0.3 pKa units) than the thiol, making it a reasonable acid to donate the proton to facilitate N-hydroxysulfenamide dehydration and sulfinamide formation (Fig. 8A) [47]. Conversion of the free amine to the acetamide removes this proximal acid source, preventing water loss and yielding disulfide. In MSH, the amino group is acetylated, and the thiol is the only ionizable group (Fig. 8B). Thus, this structure prevents a reaction path leading to sulfinamide. Results from the reaction of N-acetyl BSH and deacetylated MSH with HNO directly support the involvement of this amine group in product determination (Fig. 8B). The malate group of BSH appears to play a role in controlling the pKa of the ammonium ion rather than directly acting as a proton source. The mixture of products observed with the dimethyl ester of BSH follows earlier work that shows replacement of the malate group of BSH with methanol (O-methyl glycoside) increases the ammonium group acidity by ∼0.5 pKa units, making this ammonium group even more likely to efficiently promote dehydration (Fig. 8B). BSH possesses a suite of functionally organized groups to maintain the equilibrium acid-base properties required for the HNO-mediated conversion of thiols to sulfinamides. This unique reactivity of BSH with HNO may be important, given the relatively few species that utilize BSH as their primary LMW thiol, and provides a clue for further biological roles of HNO. Further experiments at varying reaction pH levels could determine product distribution, and computational approaches predicting relevant pKa's would be valuable.

These reaction results likely have implications regarding the maintenance of in vivo redox homeostasis. Previously, we showed that the HNO challenge of B. subtilis led to the formation of BSH sulfinamide and to a decrease in total thiol content without changing the BSH/BSSB redox ratio. This study shows that the Trx system efficiently reduces BSSB, but it does not recycle BSH sulfinamide to BSH. Sulfinamides remain a poorly studied oxidative thiol modification group, even though these modifications have been identified by MS from the HNO reaction with thiol-containing proteins [20,21,49]. Sulfinamides have also been detected by MS in the reduction of S-nitrosoglutathione (GSNO) by NADPH and alcohol dehydrogenase III [[50], [51], [52]]. Reduction of the –SNO group yields the same N-hydroxysulfenamide intermediate as the addition of HNO to a thiol (Fig. 2, Fig. 8), thus bridging NO and HNO chemistry/biology. The GSH sulfinamide formed in these reactions was a stable product and did not act as a further substrate for alcohol dehydrogenase III [[50], [51], [52]]. The inability of the Trx system to reduce BSH sulfinamide to BSH is consistent with our chemical control experiments showing that TCEP does not reduce sulfinamides. DTT (a dithiol) slowly reduces sulfinamides to thiols under forcing conditions (50 mM DTT, 37 °C, 7 % conversion at 1 h, 30 % at 6 h and 69 % at 26 h). This inefficient reaction profile poses questions about the stability of the sulfinamide modification and its importance in redox biology [53].

Other redoxins capable of this reduction may exist, or BSH sulfinamide may be a substrate for the amidases BshB or Bca, which function in BSH biosynthesis and cellular detoxification [54]. Amide hydrolysis of BSH sulfinamide would form glucosamine-malate (Gln-Mal), the natural substrate for BshC (cysteine ligase), to be recycled to BSH and a sulfinamide of cysteine that would be processed through the mercapturic acid pathway. The TrxA reduction of BSSB functions to maintain the BSH:BSSB ratio, but the inability of Trx to reduce BSH sulfinamide accounts for the observed decrease in total levels of BSH [8].

Kinetic analysis of the BSSB reduction by TrxA indicates that this redoxin system is likely relevant in a biological context. BSSB levels fluctuate from 7 to 17 μM under standard growth conditions to 100 μM under induced stress [47,55], thus the KM_BSSB for Trx of 34 μM lies within this physiologically relevant range. BSH redox homeostasis is complex and likely involves more than one reductase to maintain the normal redox buffer ratio of thiol:disulfide [56,57]. BrxA and BrxB contribute by catalyzing the reduction of mixed BSH-thiol protein-based disulfides [56]. Brd (YpdA) is a TrxR ortholog and candidate to recycle BSSB pools to BSH. While initial investigation showed activity against BSSB [58], inactivation of it only modestly impacted the redox ratio of BSH:BSSB after ROS stress in S. aureus [43,59,60]. This work provides the first kinetic characterization of the Trx system as a suitable redoxin of BSSB, identifying another pathway for maintaining the thiol:disulfide redox equilibrium [56,57]. Establishing in vivo contributions of Trx as a dominant or supporting system in maintaining BSH thiol-redox homeostasis in vivo remains to be determined, but it is anticipated to be a challenging endeavor as both TrxA and TrxR are essential, performing multiple roles in thiol and sulfur metabolism that intersect with BSH.

The B. subtilis Trx system also efficiently reduced MSSM in reaction assays in vitro. The Mycobacterium tuberculosis TrxA is 54 % identical to Bsu TrxA and reduces MSSM to MSH with a KM_MSSM of 0.8 μM, a 10-fold decrease compared to our findings with Bsu TrxA (KM_MSSM of 8.8 ± 2.1 μM) [61]. In MSH-producing species, mycothiol disulfide reductase (MTR) also functions as an MSSM reductase [62]. Extensive genetic analysis, however, provides meaningful insight into genes critical for MSH synthesis and redox function. In M. tuberculosis, inactivation of the mshD gene, encoding the acetyltransferase mycothiol synthase, leads to phenotypic growth defects including smaller colonies, slower growth, decreased biofilm, and enhanced sensitivity to oxidation and other stress [63,64]. Results described in this study showed that deacetyl mycothiol displays reactivity with HNO more similar to BSH than MSH by forming a sulfinamide as the major product. The sensitivity of these knockouts may be related to their specific redox chemistry. Other structural work on MshD concludes that the acetylation of the primary amine is critical for the generation of a useful detoxification agent (MSH) [65]. Our in vitro results reveal distinct chemistries between HNO and deacetyl MSH and MSH that provide molecular evidence for the observed biological differences.

Conclusion

The distinct reactivity of these three natural LMW biothiols (GSH, BSH, and MSH) with HNO challenges a paradigm that they perform analogous functions. The specific structure and the balance of acid-base properties of BSH play a role in the reaction path and facilitate the formation of a sulfinamide, a little studied thiol modification. Chemical synthesis generated a series of analogs that reveal the importance of a free amine within the Cys moiety of BSH and MSH in sulfinamide formation. The B. subtilis Trx system does not reduce bacillithiol sulfinamide but efficiently reduces BSSB. This Trx system also accepts and reduces MSSM, and this work identifies Trx as a viable pathway for maintaining redox balance in these bacteria. The different chemistry of these biothiols with HNO can potentially be exploited in the generation of drugs specific to BSH-producing species.
