Comparison of active and passive case finding on diagnostic delay and treatment outcomes in older patients with pulmonary tuberculosis: a cohort study using real-world data
Authors: Qingxiu Yan, Wei Wang, Xing Zhang, Yu Gao, Ping Zhu, Min Wang, Hana Yu, Xiaogang Hao, Chunfu Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTuberculosis (TB) case detection in China predominantly relies on passive case finding (PCF). To evaluate the effectiveness of active case finding (ACF), a retrospective analysis of routine surveillance data from ACF programs implemented among individuals aged 65 years and older in Quzhou from 2020 to 2022 was conducted. However, Comparative studies evaluating these two strategies remain limited. This study analyzes the basic characteristics of ACF and PCF, evaluates the impact of ACF on diagnostic delay and treatment outcomes, and provides evidence-based support for optimizing TB control strategies.MethodsData on pulmonary tuberculosis cases and related variables among individuals aged 65 years and older in Quzhou City from 2020 to 2022 were collected from the Tuberculosis Management Information System. The primary outcomes were diagnostic delay and treatment outcome. Cox regression and multivariate logistic regression were used to assess the influence of different case-detection methods on these outcomes.ResultsDuring the study period, 2,383 pulmonary tuberculosis cases aged 65 years and older were registered in Quzhou City. Of these, 23.25% were identified through ACF. The median diagnostic delay (i.e., ≥ 28 days) in the ACF group was 17 days (Inter Quartile Range, [IQR]:7–36), which was significantly shorter than 24 days (IQR:10–55) observed in the PCF group. ACF was associated with a shorter time to diagnosis (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR]: 1.502, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.360–1.658). Both ACF (adjusted odds ratio, [AOR]: 1.658, 95%CI: 1.218–2.258) and absence of diagnostic delay (AOR: 1.57, 95%CI: 1.242–1.986) were associated with higher treatment success rates. Increasing age (AOR: 0.913, 95%CI: 0.898–0.928) was significantly associated with lower treatment success rate.ConclusionCompared with PCF, ACF is associated with shorter diagnosis times and a higher treatment success rate. To improve the effectiveness of tuberculosis prevention and treatment, screening strategies should be optimized to reach vulnerable populations and comprehensive management should extend from early detection to successful treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13673-0.

Introduction

Pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) is a chronic infectious disease transmitted through the respiratory tract caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB). According to the World Health Organization Global Tuberculosis Report 2025 [1], 696,000 new tuberculosis cases were reported in China, representing 6.5% of the global cases. China ranks fourth amongthe 30 countries with the highest tuberculosis burden.

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that fail to identify 3 million TB cases annually [1]. Emery et al. [2] reported that diagnostic delays increase patient morbidity and facilitate community transmission, with transmission rates reaching up to 70%. In China, pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) case detection is predominantly passive, depending on patients with tuberculosis-related symptoms to seek care at health facilities. Some medical institutions face challenges in diagnosis and management. For example, in some settings, symptoms such as fever and shortness of breath are frequently misdiagnosed as common respiratory infections, resulting in missed TB cases [3]. Delayed diagnosis results in irreversible lung damage, elevate mortality risk, and a raise the likelihood of developing multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) [4].

Current strategies for TB prevention and control encompass detection and treatment of active cases, prophylactic treatment for latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI), and vaccination [5]. The core approaches for the detection and treatment of active cases consist of PCF, ACF, and health examinations, with ACF playing a particularly critical role [6]. China’s guidelines mandate ACF for TB among key populations, including close contacts of pathogen-positive patients, individuals with HIV, the older, and those with diabetes [7]. Between 2015 and 2023, the average PTB registration rate among individuals aged 65 years or older in China was 96.9 per 100,000 population, which is approximately twice that of the general population [8]. In Quzhou City, which has a large older adults, the average annual incidence rate of PTB among individuals aged 65 years or older during the same period was 177.11 per 100,000 population, significantly exceeding the national average [9]. Owing to underlying diseases and reduced immunity, older individuals face a higher risk of developing and dying from tuberculosis [10]. Most previous studies have been conducted in varied settings and among general populations. However, evidence directly comparing diagnostic delay and treatment outcomes between ACF and PCF among adults aged 65 years and older in urban China, particularly under a comprehensive tuberculosis treatment reimbursement policy, remains scarce. Therefore, implementing ACF in the older populations is of considerable importance.

In 2020, Quzhou Cityinitiated a pilot program that provide free treatment for both drug-susceptible and drug-resistant tuberculosis. It was the first prefecture-level city in China to implement a policy offering full reimbursement for the diagnosis and treatment of rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (RR-TB) and drug-susceptible tuberculosis. From 2020 to 2022, Quzhou City conducted tuberculosis screening among rural older populations aged 65 and older, identifying multiple cases of PTB. The aim of this study was to compare the characteristics, diagnostic delay, and treatment outcomes of pulmonary tuberculosis cases identified through ACF and PCF among adults aged 65 years and older in Quzhou, China.

Methods

Study area

Quzhou City, situated in the western region of Zhejiang Province, China reported a gross domestic product of 2262 trillion RMB in 2024. The city a permanent resident population of 2.229 million, with approximately 467,000 individuals aged 65 and above, accounting for 20.3% of the total population, ranking the highest in population aging rate among all cities in Zhejiang Province [11]. This proportion of older residents is the highest in Zhejiang Province [12]. The healthcare system in Quzhou City has undergone digitization, enabling efficient referral, remote diagnosis, and continuous management [13]. These advancements support the implementation and evaluation of TB detection strategies among the older adults. The location of Quzhou City is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Data source

This retrospective cohort study utilized routine surveillance data. The resident health check-up platform provided free chest X-ray to individuals aged 65 years and older. The diagnostic process for PTB is illustrated in Fig. S1. All confirmed TB cases were recorded in the Tuberculosis Information Management System (TBIMS) in accordance with regulatory requirements. In the system, cases confirmed through physical examination and screening channels were categorized as ACF while those diagnosed after self-initiated visits to medical institutions to symptoms were classified as PCF. Diagnosis of PTB was established in strict accordance with national unified criteria. The initiation of diagnostic anti-tuberculosis treatment followed standardized clinical indications [14]. This diagnostic algorithm is uniformly applied in all institutions. The demographic characteristics, clinical diagnostic information, laboratory test results and treatment outcomes of each PTB case were recorded from TBIMS. The study population comprised new, relapse, and retreatment TB cases.

Definitions

This study included patients with laboratory-confirmed and clinically diagnosed PTB cases, and tuberculous pleurisy, in accordance with the the Chinese Tuberculosis Classification Standard (WS196-2017) [15]. Laboratory-confirmed PTB cases were defined as those with positive sputum smear, culture, or GeneXpert results, indicating infection with M. tuberculosis. Clinically diagnosed PTB cases were defined as those presenting with PTB-specific chest imaging and clinical manifestations such as cough, expectoration for at least two weeks, or hemoptysis, with no response to diagnostic anti-inflammatory therapy and negative or missing laboratory test results (excluding anti-TB drug use) [16]. Tuberculous pleurisy was defined as pleural inflammation caused by direct infection with M. tuberculosis or a delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction within the pleural cavity, typically accompanied by exudative pleural effusion [17, 18]. Microbiological confirmation was prioritized; in cases of inconsistent clinical, radiological, and microbiological findings, microbiological evidence was considered definitive. Patient delay was defined as the interval between the onset of PTB symptoms (persistent cough, fever, weakness, weight loss, or chest pain) and the first visit to a professional healthcare provider. Health system delay was defined as the time from the patient’s first presentation to a healthcare provider to the initiation of treatment. Total diagnostic delay (hereafter referred to as diagnostic delay) was defined as the sum of patient and health system delays. Symptom definitions were restricted to clear, typical TB-related manifestations and data were collected via standardized interviews conducted by trained staff using structured questionnaires to minimize recall bias and misclassification among older participants. In this study, 28 days cut-off was used to analyse total diagnostic delays [4]. The 14-day threshold for patient and health system delay was selected to identify early care-seeking and timely health system response. Sensitivity analyses using alternative cutoff values (60-day) for diagnostic delay were performed, and consistent results were observed across different threshold definitions (Table S1). ACF refers to cases identified through physical examination and screening pathways. PCF refers to case detection initiated by patients sought care at healthcare institutions due to symptoms. The high-risk groups screened in this study were primarily older individuals aged 65 years or older. Among patients with tuberculosis, a diagnosis of comorbid diabetes mellitus (DM) was established if any of the following World Health Organization (WHO) criteria were met: (1) fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, with fasting defined as at least 8 h without caloric intake; (2) glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%, reflecting average blood glucose over the previous 2–3 months; (3) 2-hour plasma glucose (2hPG) ≥ 11.1 mmol/L during an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) performed according to WHO methods, measured 2 h after consuming a 75-gram oral glucose solution; or (4) in the presence of typical hyperglycemia symptoms (polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, weight loss), a random plasma glucose level ≥ 11.1 mmol/L [19]. Other comorbidities were defined as TB complicated by any of the following: pneumoconiosis, mental illness, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection. Treatment outcomes were classified as follows: treatment success included (1) cure, completed treatment as recommended by the national policy with evidence of bacteriological response and no evidence of failure (2) Treatment completed, completed treatment as recommended by the national policy whose outcome does not meet the definition for cure or treatment failure. Other outcomes included (1) treatment failed, defined as a patient whose treatment regimen needed to be terminated or permanently changed to a new regimen or treatment strategy; (2) died, defined as a patient who died before starting treatment or during the course of treatment. (3) Lost to follow-up, defined as a patient whose treatment was interrupted for 2 consecutive months or more; (4) not evaluated, defined as a patient for whom no treatment outcome was assigned [20]. Although the definition of treatment success was identical across all diagnostic groups, the criteria for “Cured” were only applicable to bacteriologically confirmed PTB cases. Clinically diagnosed PTB and tuberculous pleurisy cases could only be classified as “Treatment completed” due to the absence of baseline bacteriological positivity for outcome monitoring. This inherent difference in outcome classification may introduce potential classification bias when comparing treatment success rates across distinct diagnostic subgroups.

General characteristics of pulmonary tuberculosis in the older adults over 65 years old

Data were collected on sex, age, occupation, type of diagnosis, comorbidities, date of onset of tuberculosis-related symptoms, date of first visit, date of diagnosis, tuberculosis detection method, and treatment outcome. Analyses encompassed all diagnostic categories, including Laboratory-confirmed PTB, clinically diagnosed PTB and tuberculous pleurisy, baseline drug resistance data were not clearly distinguished for all patients.

Variable selection and confounding adjustment

Candidate variables were selected using a priori clinical, epidemiological, and causal reasoning. The variables included sex, age, occupation, comorbidities, diagnostic type, diagnostic delay, case detection strategy (ACF/PCF), and treatment outcome. A causal framework informed by directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), was used to identify the minimally sufficient adjustment set for multivariable models, including age, sex, occupation, and comorbidities [21]. (Fig. S2). All identified confounders were retained in the final models.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized participants’ characteristics. Continuous data with non-normal distributions were reported as median and interquartile range (M [Q₁, Q₃]). while categorical data were presented as percentages (proportions, %). Kaplan-Meier survival curves and the Log-rank test were applied to compare the distributions of delay times between the two groups. A multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model assessed the impact of different finding methods on diagnostic delay, with adjusted Hazard Ratios (aHRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated. The proportional hazards assumption was examined using log-minus-log survival plots, which showed approximately parallel curves, suggesting that the assumption was satisfied (Fig. S3). A multivariate logistic regression model identified factors associated with treatment success, and adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% CIs were calculated. All variables were included in the multivariable model regardless of univariate results. Two-sided P values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant, with a significance level of α < 0.05. All variables in this study had no missing values, and all analyses were performed using the original complete dataset. Descriptive analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows (version 26.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) and Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Figures were generated using ArcGIS software (version 10.8.1; ESRI Inc., Redlands, CA, USA).

Result

Registration status of PTB cases aged 65 years and above

Between 2020 and 2022, a total of 2,383 PTB cases were registered among adults aged 65 years and older in Quzhou City, resulting in an average annual incidence rate of 190.02 per 100,000 population. Of these cases, 23.25% (554/2,383) were detected through ACF, while 76.75% (1,829/2,383) were identified through PCF. Male patients accounted for 74.74% (n = 1,781) of the cohort. Patients ages ranged from 65 to 96 years, with a median age of 73 years (interquartile range: 69 to 79 years). Diagnostic delay was observed in 52.41% (n = 1249) patients. The majority of patients, (84.39%, n = 2,011) were farmers. In terms of diagnostic results, 68.06% (n = 1,622) were laboratory-confirmed PTB, 22.58% (n = 538) were clinically diagnosed PTB, and 9.36% (n = 223) were diagnosed with tuberculous pleurisy. Comorbidities included diabetes mellitus (DM) in 4.78% (n = 114) of cases, other diseases in 7.68% (n = 183), and both DM and other diseases in 0.55% (n = 13). Successful treatment outcomes was achieved in 83.55% (n = 1,991) of the patients (Table 1).

Baseline characteristics of cases by different discovery method

Univariate analysis revealed statistically significant differences between ACF and PCF in terms of gender, age, occupation, diagnostic delay, type of disease diagnosis, presence of comorbidities, and treatment outcomes (P < 0.05). The ACF group had a higher proportion of men, farmers, patients without diagnostic delay and laboratory-confirmed PTB than the PCF group. Additionally, the ACF group exhibited a lower proportion of comorbidities, and a higher treatment success rate (Table 1).

Descriptive analysis of diagnostic delay

The ACF group demonstrated a significantly shorter patient delay than that compared to the PCF group [6.0 days (0, 25.0) vs. 18.0 days (6.0, 43.0)]. Similarly, medical system delay was significantly low in the ACF group than in the PCF group [2.0 days (1.0, 6.0) vs. 3.0 days (1.0, 10.0)]. The total diagnostic delay was also significantly shorter in the ACF group than in the PCF group [17.0 days (7.0, 36.0) vs. 24.0 days (10.0, 55.0)] Differences among the three groups were statistically significant (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Survival analysis of case finding methods and total diagnostic delay

All participants in this study were diagnosed with tuberculosis during the study period; therefore no censoring occurred in the survival analysis, which used “confirmed diagnosis” as the terminal event. Survival time was calculated as the number of days from the onset of tuberculosis-related symptoms to the date of confirmed diagnosis. The Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated a significant difference in diagnostic delay between the ACF and PCF (Log-rank test, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The 5th National Tuberculosis Epidemiological Sampling Survey conducted in China in 2010 reported that older adults accounted for 48.8% of PTB patients [22]. In Quzhou City, older patients accounted for 44.29% of PTB cases reported between 2011 and 2015 [23]. In May 2019, eight central ministries and commissions jointly issued the Action Plan for Tuberculosis Control (2019–2022), which recommended expanding the coverage of ACF for key populations, including individuals aged 65 years and older. Collectively, these data indicate that people aged 65 years and older represent a key population for tuberculosis prevention and control.

This study examined the effects of various case-finding methods on diagnostic delay and treatment outcomes among older TB patients. It also assessed the role of ACF in improving diagnostic timeliness and treatment outcomes, thereby providing a theoretical basis for optimizing tuberculosis prevention and control measures. Previous implementation studies have demonstrated that ACF increase tuberculosis cases identification, particularly among older individuals and those with a history of TB [24]. In this study, ACF resulted in a 20% increase in the detection of PTB cases. Yuen et al. [25] reported that a 25% increase in case detection could result in a 40–44% reduction in tuberculosis-associated mortality, a 22–27% reduction in incidence, and a 30–33% reduction in prevalence over a in 10 year period. Enhancing ACF among asymptomatic individuals is a critical for interrupting TB transmission.

In the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model, after adjustment for potential confounders such as gender, age, disease type and occupation, ACF remained a significant independent predictor of shorter time to diagnosis (aHR = 1.502). These finding are consistent with results from studies conducted in India [26], China [27],Cambodia [28], and Russia [3]. Descriptive analyses demonstrated that all delay indicators in the ACF group were significantly shorter than those in the PCF group. ACF was associated with a 7-day reduction in the median diagnostic delay among diagnosed patients compared to PCF (17.0 days versus 24.0 days). The interquartile range (IQR: 0–25 days) indicates that a proportion of patients were diagnosed on the same day as, or shortly after, symptom onset, which may contribute to reduced community transmission. Further, analysis revealed that the reduction in total diagnostic delay was primarily attributable to a shorter patient delay. Screening among key populations, a central strategy of ACF, facilitates the identification of suspected cases when symptoms are mild, thereby achieving “early detection” and reducing patient delay [29]. Additionally, ACF provides medical services directly to patients, mitigating barriers to caresuch as transportation, cost and time constraints, and facilitates the treatment of patients with mild symptoms [30].

Although the absolute difference in median health system delay between the two groups was modest (2.0 days versus 3.0 days), the delay times in the PCF group exhibited greater variability (IQR: 1.0–10.0 days). This findings suggests that healthcare system efficiency during the diagnostic process is less consistent among patients identified through PCF. Extended delays may be attributed to atypical symptoms, multiple referrals, or restricted access to medical resources [31]. In contrast, the delay distribution in the ACF group (IQR: 1.0–6.0 days) was more concentrated. This pattern can be explained by two main factors. First, suspected patients identified through ACF are often referred directly to designated tuberculosis institutions, reducing repeated visits and ineffective treatment at non-designated facilities [32]. Second, when patients seek care after abnormal results from community-based tuberculosis screening, clinicians typically prioritize chest imaging and tuberculosis etiological tests, thereby shortening the time required for differential diagnosis [33]. Consequently, optimizing routine healthcare-seeking pathways to establish a more standardized, predictable diagnostic process should be a central objective of health system improvement. In summary, ACF screening is correlated with earlier diagnosis, shifting the detection pattern from passive case identification after symptom onset to potential early case ascertainment, a strategy that is essential for reducing tuberculosis transmission at the population level.

Compared with PCF, the treatment success rate of ACF was 1.658 times higher during the same period. Additionally, patients without diagnostic delay exhibited a treatment success rate 1.571 times higher than for those experiencing such a delay. The increased treatment success rate among ACF identified cases is likely attributable to earlier detection, which is associated with milder infection, less severe pulmonary lesions, and better overall health status, thus improving treatment efficacy and adherence. Additionally, ACF involves active follow-up and health education for screened individuals, which may raise patient awareness of treatment adherence and lower the likelihood of treatment interruption, potentially correlating with better treatment success rates. These observational findings suggest that ACF may be linked not only to earlier case identification but also to favorable treatment profiles, possibly through timely intervention that optimizes individual clinical status, which could in turn contribute to reduced community MTB transmission [34]. After adjusting for confounding factors, the study demonstrated that each additional year of patient age was associated with an average 7.9% decrease in the odds of treatment success. This observation is consistent with the findings of Oshi et al. [35], further confirming that age is a consistent and universal risk predictor for tuberculosis treatment outcomes. We also noted that advancing age was associated with shorter diagnostic delay, which may be attributed to the fact that older adults with chronic diseases have more frequent medical visits, providing greater opportunities for incidental TB detection and thereby shortening diagnostic delay [36]. Consequently, tuberculosis prevention and control strategies should prioritize the older adults. Studies by Cioboata et al. have demonstrated that host factors and biomarkers are closely associated with tuberculosis severity, diagnostic efficiency, and clinical outcomes, providing complementary insights into the interpretation of our findings [37]. The study also identified that male patients and Laboratory-confirmed PTB exhibited poorer treatment outcomes, indicating the need for targeted interventions in these groups. Treatment success rates were lower among Laboratory-confirmed PTB cases, possibly because the criteria for “Cured” apply only to bacteriologically confirmed PTB cases. Clinically diagnosed PTB and tuberculous pleurisy cases could only be classified as “Treatment completed” because of the absence of baseline bacteriological positivity for outcome monitoring. The higher probability of favorable treatment outcomes among patients with clinically diagnosed PTB and tuberculous pleurisy should be interpreted with caution. This inherent difference in outcome classification may introduce potential classification bias when comparing treatment success rates across distinct diagnostic subgroups, rather than genuine differences in treatment efficacy.

The study was conducted from 2020 to 2022, coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic. We recognize that the pandemic likely influenced TB case detection movement restrictions and limited access to health care may have prevented some patients with TB symptoms from seeking medical care, potentially reducing the number of PCF-identified cases. However, our ACF program was systematically implemented as planned, with targeted screening of the older adults, which helped mitigate the negative effects of the pandemic on TB case detection.

This study demonstrates several strengths. Conducting the research in a region with a high incidence of TB, implementing screening three consecutive years facilitated the identification of interval cases and atypical presentations. This methodology improves the comprehensiveness of case detection and provides a more accurate representation of the actual TB burden. Additionally, analysis of three‑year trend data enabled an evaluation of the ACF strategy, evidence to support sustainable, data-driven decision. Nevertheless, certain limitations should be acknowledged. As a single-centre retrospective study, data completeness may be limited, and information bias may be affect data collection. Misclassification of exposure between ACF and PCF groups remains an inevitable potential bias in this observational study. Although adjustments for known confounding factors were performed using multivariable analysis, unmeasured confounders, such as patients’ health beliefs and proximity to medical facilities, may have influenced the results.The use of a composite outcome that with distinct clinical interpretations is acknowledged as a key limitation of this study. Furthermore, the effects of previous tuberculosis treatment, retreatment, and baseline drug resistance on diagnostic delay and treatment outcomes were not comprehensively assessed. The absence of a formal cost-effectiveness analysis restricts understanding of the the economic implications and long-term sustainability of ACF strategies. This limitation should be taken into account when interpreting the study findings. The study’s focus on a single city in Zhejiang Province also limits the generalizability of the findings. Future research should incorporate questionnaire surveys alongside routine surveillance to provide additional evidence for improving TB control in Quzhou City.

Conclusion

This study examined the relationship between case detection strategies, diagnostic delay, and treatment outcomes among elderly PTB patients. The findings suggest that active case finding is associated with shorter diagnostic delay and higher treatment success rates. These results highlight the potential role of early detection strategies in improving TB control among older populations. However, given the observational nature of the study, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Further studies are needed to confirm these associations and to evaluate the effectiveness and scalability of active case finding in different settings.
