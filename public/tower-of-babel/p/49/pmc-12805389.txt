Acute Myeloid Leukemia Drives Atrial Fibrillation Through TNFα Signaling Activation
Authors: Ninad Oak, Jose Alberto Navarro-Garcia, Minhua Li, Mara R Turkieltaub Paredes, Satadru K Lahiri, Bharat K Kantharia, Daisuke Nakada, Xander HT Wehrens, Mohit M Hulsurkar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical AbstractKey Words: acute myeloid leukemia, atrial fibrillation, cardiac comorbidities, cardio-oncology, tumor necrosis factor α

Cardiac comorbidities, particularly atrial fibrillation (AF), are prevalent and a leading cause of mortality in cancer patients and survivors.1 While the cardiotoxic effects of cancer therapies are well-studied, emerging evidence suggests that cancer itself may be an independent risk factor for AF. However, the molecular pathways underlying this association, termed onco-AF, remain poorly understood. There is an imperative need for targeted, preferably atrial-specific, interventions to prevent, treat, and manage onco-AF, which necessitates first a deeper mechanistic understanding.

To address this, we developed a mouse model of onco-AF by grafting acute myeloid leukemia (AML) cells into wild-type C57BL/6J mice (Figure 1A). All experiments were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Baylor College of Medicine. The AML cell-line was established by GFP-MLL-AF9 overexpression in the hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (Lin-Sca-1+c-kit+, LSK cells) via retroviral vectors.2 A total of 25,000 infected cells were transplanted into lethally irradiated C57BL/6 mice. Moribund mice were euthanized, and AML cells were harvested from the bone marrow. To generate the AML mice, 3,000 AML cells (or healthy bone marrow for control mice) were engrafted into 12-week-old mice following sublethal irradiation. Six weeks post-graft, we assessed AF susceptibility via surface electrocardiogram (ECG) and programmed electrical stimulation (PES).3 Baseline ECG parameters were measured using a temperature regulated ECG board under isoflurane-induced anesthesia. For PES, a 1.1-F octapolar catheter was inserted into the right atrium and ventricle via the right jugular vein, and electrograms were acquired by IOX2.4 software. A series of 2-second bursts were applied to the atrial tissue in 2-ms decrements, starting with a cycle length of 40 ms and decreasing to 20 ms. AF was determined as the absence of P waves with irregularly irregular R-R intervals lasting >1 second. Mice were classified as AF-positive if at least 2 of 3 stimulations induced AF. Our results revealed a 55.6% AF susceptibility in AML mice compared with 9.1% in control mice (P = 0.046) (Figure 1B). Additionally, AML mice exhibited longer AF episodes, with the longest AF episode averaging 13.7 ± 5.75 seconds (P = 0.034) (Figure 1C). Although there was no change in the atrioventricular effective refractory period (AVERP)100 (Figure 1D), the sinus node recovery time (SNRT)100 was shorter in the AML mice (142 ± 7.7 ms), compared with the control mice (170 ± 12.1 ms; P = 0.048) (Figure 1E).

To gain insights into the mechanisms driving this increased AF susceptibility, we performed RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) on atrial tissue from AML mice (Figure 1F), identifying 845 significant differentially expressed genes (Figure 1G). Gene set enrichment analysis of the RNA-seq data indicated that the tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα) signaling pathway via nuclear factor-kappa B1 (NFkB1) (Hallmark TNFA Signaling via NFKB1) was the most enriched pathway in AML mouse atria (Figure 1H). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) confirmed up-regulated expression of TNFα (2.2 ± 0.24-fold; P = 0.006) and the chemokine CCL2 (2.1 ± 0.17-fold; P = 0.004) in AML mouse atria (Figure 1I and 1J). Furthermore, Western blot analysis confirmed increased phosphorylation of the p65 subunit of NFkB in AML mice (Figure 1K and 1L), confirming the activation of the TNFα/NFkB1 signaling pathway in the atria of AML mice.

Furthermore, histological analysis demonstrated increased atrial fibrosis in AML mice (9.98 ± 0.954% vs 3.17 ± 0.38% in control mice; P = 0.004) (Figure 1M and 1N), suggesting that the activated TNFα/NFkB1 signaling pathway may be promoting AF susceptibility via increased fibrosis in the atria of AML mice. Interestingly, transthoracic echocardiography revealed no structural or functional changes in the ventricles of AML mice (left ventricular dimensions, ejection fraction, wall thickness; all P > 0.05), suggesting that atrial remodeling and AF may be the drivers of cardiac comorbidities in AML patients.

Collectively, our preclinical model indicates a potential causative relationship between AML and AF, representing a first validation of cancer as an independent risk factor in a controlled setting. Although TNFα signaling and fibrosis are associated with conventional AF, their involvement in onco-AF is novel. While systemic anti-inflammatory or immunosuppressive therapies could potentially reduce AF progression, their application in cancer patients is constrained by risk of infection and sepsis. The differentially expressed genes identified in our study establish a basis for forthcoming mechanistic studies aimed at developing targeted therapies. Although our model utilizes a combination of AML and intracardiac pacing, it might be modified for additional cancers, enabling more extensive investigations into onco-AF mechanisms. Collectively, this model also provides a robust platform to facilitate the development of future therapeutic strategies.
