Development and internal validation of a neonatal mortality risk prediction model for low birthweight neonates in public hospitals, North-West Ethiopia: a prospective follow-up study
Authors: Daniel Mulat Eshetu, Assefa Andargie Kassa, Wolde Melese Ayele, Mulatu Kassahun, Shashitu Tadele Zeleke, Betelhem Mekuriaw Anagaw, Mikias Getahun Molla, Kalaab Esubalew Sharew, Birhanu Kassie Abeje, Chalachew Abiyu Ayalew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to develop and internally validate a neonatal mortality risk prediction model for low birthweight (LBW) neonates in public hospitals of North-West Ethiopia.DesignAn institution-based prospective follow-up study.SettingThe study was conducted in seven hospitals, comprising three general hospitals and four primary hospitals, between 14 February and 31 October 2025. The general hospitals, Injibara, Finote Selam and Pawi, provide level II neonatal intensive care services, whereas the primary hospitals, Dangila, Chagni, Jawi and Gimja Bet, provide level I newborn care services. These hospitals are situated in the Awi, West Gojjam and Metekel zones of the Amhara and Benishangul Gumuz regions in north-western Ethiopia.Participants955 LBW neonates were enrolled in the study. Neonates born outside health facilities were excluded if their birth weight was unknown, particularly those delivered by traditional birth attendants, as were neonates whose first recorded weight was obtained more than 24 hours after birth. Participants were selected using consecutive sampling and data were collected electronically using the KoboCollect mobile application.Outcome measuresData were analysed using R software V.4.4.3. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator regression and multivariable logistic regression were used to identify predictors and develop a simplified risk score and nomogram. Model performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination and calibration, while internal validation was performed using bootstrapping. The clinical utility of the model was assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsThe final model included nine predictors: sepsis, perinatal asphyxia, respiratory distress syndrome, absence of exclusive breastfeeding, absence of Kangaroo Mother Care, gestational ages of 28 weeks to <32 weeks and 32 weeks to <37 weeks, and birth weights of <1000 g and 1000–1499 g. The original model demonstrated good discrimination, with an area under the curve of 84.2% (95% CI 81.5% to 86.9%). After internal validation, the simplified risk score and nomogram achieved areas under the curve of 83.85% and 83.5%, respectively. The model showed excellent calibration before and after validation. DCA indicated that the model provides meaningful clinical benefit.ConclusionsThe nomogram and simplified risk score demonstrated excellent calibration and good discriminatory performance. Their application in clinical settings may improve early intervention and potentially reduce neonatal mortality in Ethiopia by enabling rapid, individualised mortality-risk prediction among LBW neonates. However, external validation in different settings is required.Keywords: EPIDEMIOLOGIC STUDIES, Ethiopia, Mortality, NEONATOLOGY

Introduction

Low birth weight (LBW), defined as a birth weight of less than 2500 grams irrespective of gestational age, remains a major public health problem worldwide, particularly in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs).1 LBW neonates are at a substantially higher risk of morbidity and mortality because of immature organ systems, feeding difficulties, impaired thermoregulation, respiratory complications, and increased susceptibility to infections.2 3 Globally, LBW contributes considerably to neonatal deaths, especially during the first week of life.2 4 Despite improvements in neonatal care, mortality among LBW neonates remains disproportionately high in LMICs due to limited resources, shortages of trained healthcare professionals, and inadequate neonatal intensive care services.5 6

Ethiopia continues to experience a high burden of neonatal mortality, with neonatal deaths accounting for a substantial proportion of under-five mortality.7 LBW and prematurity are among the leading contributors to neonatal deaths in the country.3 8 Although national and global initiatives, including the Sustainable Development Goals and neonatal mortality reduction strategies, aim to improve neonatal survival, mortality among LBW neonates remains persistently high in many parts of Ethiopia.8 9 In North-West Ethiopia, studies have reported low essential newborn care service readiness, shortages of trained personnel, inadequate equipment and limited neonatal care resources, which further compromise the management of high-risk neonates.10

Several prediction models have been developed to estimate neonatal mortality among LBW neonates, with most demonstrating good discrimination and calibration performance. However, the generalisability and applicability of these models across different healthcare settings, particularly in low-resource countries, remain limited. A model developed by the Korean Neonatal Network (KNN) demonstrated good discrimination and calibration, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.86 and a Hosmer–Lemeshow test value of p=0.6838.11 Similarly, the Neonatal Mortality Risk (NMR 2000) Score developed using data sets from the UK and Gambia demonstrated strong predictive performance and external validity in India.12 13 The Neonatal del Cono Sur (NEOCOSUR) model developed in South America,14 the University of Alabama model,15 the United States Neonatal Research Network model,16 and models developed in Spain17 also demonstrated acceptable to excellent predictive performance. However, most of these models were developed in high-income or relatively better-resourced healthcare systems and often excluded predictors relevant to low-resource settings, such as postnatal complications and neonatal care practices. In addition, some models require detailed laboratory and clinical data that are not routinely available in resource-constrained settings.

Consequently, the applicability of existing neonatal mortality prediction models to Ethiopia remains uncertain because of differences in population characteristics, disease burden, healthcare infrastructure and availability of clinical resources. Furthermore, there is a lack of locally developed and validated prognostic models specifically designed for LBW neonates in Ethiopia. Early identification of high-risk neonates could support evidence-based clinical decision-making, improve resource allocation and facilitate timely interventions for vulnerable newborns. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and internally validate a prognostic model for predicting neonatal mortality among LBW neonates admitted to public hospitals in North-West Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design and setting

An institution-based prospective follow-up study was conducted from 14 February to 31 October 2025, among LBW neonates admitted to Injibara General Hospital, Finote Selam General Hospital, Pawi General Hospital, Dangila Primary Hospital, Chagni Primary Hospital, Jawi Primary Hospital and Gimja Bet Primary Hospital. These hospitals are located in the Awi, West Gojjam and Metekel zones of the Amhara and Benishangul Gumuz regions in North-West Ethiopia. Injibara, Finote Selam and Pawi General Hospitals provide level II neonatal intensive care services, including oxygen therapy, intravenous medications, phototherapy and Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC). Dangila, Chagni, Jawi and Gimja Bet primary hospitals offer level I newborn care, focusing on essential newborn care, management of common neonatal illnesses and referral of critical cases to higher-level facilities. All hospitals serve as referral centres for nearby health centres and play a vital role in managing LBW neonates within their catchment areas.

Population

The source population consisted of all neonates admitted to the neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) with a diagnosis of LBW. The study population included LBW neonates admitted to the NICUs of the selected hospitals between 14 February and 31 October 2025, who fulfilled the eligibility criteria.

Eligibility criteria

All neonates admitted to the NICU with a diagnosis of LBW were included in the study. Neonates born outside health facilities were excluded if birth weight was unknown, particularly those delivered by traditional birth attendants, as well as neonates whose admission weight was recorded more than 24 hours after birth.

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined based on the recommendation of at least 10 outcome events per predictor parameter for logistic regression models.18 Considering 25 candidate predictors and an anticipated neonatal mortality rate of 28.8% among LBW neonates from a previous study,19 the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 868 neonates. After accounting for a 10% potential missing data rate, the final sample size was 955 LBW neonates.

Sampling procedures

A consecutive sampling technique was applied. All eligible LBW neonates admitted during the study period were enrolled until the total sample size of 955 was reached. The sample was proportionally allocated based on patient flow from the preceding year to ensure representativeness across hospitals. Accordingly, 206 were from Injibara General Hospital, 187 from Finote Selam General Hospital, 169 from Pawi General Hospital, 112 from Dangila Primary Hospital, 103 from Chagni Primary Hospital, 94 from Jawi Primary Hospital and 84 from Gimja Bet Primary Hospital.

Data collection tools and procedures

All clinical variables were defined based on the neonate’s status at NICU admission. Data were collected prospectively using the Kobo Toolbox with a structured checklist developed from relevant literature, capturing sociodemographic, maternal, obstetric, neonatal and clinical care factors associated with mortality among LBW neonates. All clinical conditions and care practices, including sepsis, respiratory distress syndrome, birth asphyxia, exclusive breastfeeding, KMC, resuscitation at birth and oxygen support, were recorded only if present, initiated or documented at the time of NICU admission or during the initial clinical assessment. Predictors were therefore treated as baseline variables, and postadmission developments occurring after the initial assessment period were not included in the analysis.

Seven trained nurses served as data collectors and were assigned proportionally to each hospital based on patient flow (two each at Injibara, Finote Selam and Pawi general hospitals, and one each at Dangila, Chagni, Jawi and Gimja Bet primary hospitals). Three supervisors, each overseeing two to three hospitals, monitored data collection, conducted random quality checks and resolved discrepancies. Eligible neonates were enrolled consecutively at admission to the NICU, and data were recorded in real time using a structured checklist. LBW neonates were initially managed in the NICU, and those who became clinically stable and tolerated enteral feeding were subsequently transferred to mother-side care in the postnatal wards, with follow-up continuing until discharge.

Data quality control

Data collectors and supervisors received a 1 day training on the study protocol, eligibility criteria and proper use of the checklist. Supervisors monitored data collection, conducted random checks for completeness and consistency, and addressed discrepancies promptly. Daily reviews and digital entry through KoboCollect mobile applications with built-in validation minimised errors and ensured high-quality, reliable data.

Data processing and analysis

Software and descriptive analysis

Descriptive statistics, including mean, median, SD, IQR, frequencies, and percentages, were used to summarize the data. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test together with graphical methods, including histograms and Q–Q plots. A value of p≥0.05 in the Shapiro–Wilk test was considered to indicate a normal distribution. Accordingly, variables with a normal distribution were summarized using mean and SD, whereas non-normally distributed variables were summarized using median and IQR.

Missing data

Missing data were assessed using Little’s Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) test, which showed no evidence against the MCAR assumption (p=0.212). Given the low proportion of missingness and the MCAR test result, multiple imputation by chained equations was applied under the missing at random (MAR) assumption. Ten imputed data sets were generated and combined using Rubin’s rules for pooled estimates. Overall, missingness was low for most variables, including antenatal corticosteroid use (2.2%), maternal morbidities (3.35%) and obstetric complications (2.2%). APGAR Scores at 1 min and 5 min had 7.76% missing values due to out-of-facility deliveries where standardised assessment was not available and were therefore handled separately to avoid introducing bias.

Variable selection and model development

A structured and stepwise modelling strategy was used to ensure both predictive performance and clinical interpretability. First, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was applied to the full set of candidate predictors. This penalised regression method shrinks regression coefficients towards zero while selecting the most relevant predictors, thereby improving model stability, reducing overfitting and addressing potential multicollinearity among correlated variables.21

To assess potential clustering of outcomes within hospitals, an empty mixed-effects logistic regression model was fitted. The intraclass correlation coefficient was 0.004, indicating minimal variation in neonatal mortality attributable to differences between hospitals. Based on this negligible clustering effect, multilevel modelling was not performed, and a standard multivariable logistic regression model was used for the analysis.

Variables retained by LASSO were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. A backward stepwise elimination procedure was then applied to refine the model. This process was guided by a likelihood ratio test using a liberal retention threshold value of p<0.15, which is commonly recommended in prognostic modelling to avoid premature exclusion of potentially important predictors that may contribute to prediction accuracy.22

To ensure independence among predictors, multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). A value above 10 was considered indicative of significant multicollinearity; however, all variables in the final model demonstrated VIF values below 5, indicating acceptable independence among predictors.23

Model framework and predictive objective

The model was developed within a prognostic framework to estimate the probability of neonatal mortality among LBW neonates using predictors measured at the time of admission to the NICU. The model assumes a relationship between baseline predictors measured at time t₀ and the occurrence of mortality at a future time point tₙ. The primary objective of the model was risk prediction and stratification rather than causal inference, with the aim of supporting early clinical decision-making.

Model performance evaluation

Model performance was assessed in accordance with TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis) guidelines across three key domains: discrimination, calibration, and overall accuracy. Discrimination was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) curve, which measures the ability of the model to distinguish between neonates who survived and those who died. AUROC values were interpreted as follows: 0.50–0.59 no discrimination, 0.60–0.69 poor, 0.70–0.79 fair, 0.80–0.89 good and 0.90–1.00 excellent discrimination.24 Calibration was assessed using both calibration plots and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Calibration plots provided a visual assessment of agreement between predicted and observed probabilities across risk deciles, while the Hosmer–Lemeshow test provided a statistical evaluation of model fit. Overall model performance was assessed using the Brier Score, which measures the mean squared difference between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes. Lower Brier scores indicate better predictive accuracy and calibration.25

Clinical utility and risk stratification

To facilitate clinical interpretation, Youden’s Index was used to determine an optimal probability threshold for categorising neonates into low-risk and high-risk groups. This threshold was selected to maximise the combined sensitivity and specificity of the model for practical application in neonatal intensive care settings.26

Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) was conducted to evaluate the clinical usefulness of the model. Net benefit was calculated across a range of threshold probabilities and compared with treat-all and treat-none strategies, allowing assessment of the model’s potential clinical advantage in decision-making.27

Internal validation

Internal validation was performed using 1000 bootstrap resamples to assess the stability, robustness and optimism of the developed model. In each resample, the model was refitted and evaluated to obtain optimism-corrected estimates of discrimination, calibration and regression coefficients. Bootstrapping is widely recommended for prognostic prediction models because it reduces overfitting, improves internal validity and generally provides more efficient and less biased performance estimates than data-splitting and other resampling approaches.28 29

Risk score development and reporting standards

A simplified risk scoring system and nomogram were developed from the final multivariable logistic regression model to facilitate bedside application using routinely available clinical variables. To derive the risk score, the regression coefficient of each predictor in the final model was divided by the smallest coefficient in the model, and the resulting values were rounded to the nearest whole number to obtain integer scores. The individual predictor scores were then summed to generate an overall risk score for each neonate. Because some predictors, such as birth weight and gestational age categories, were mutually exclusive, not all score components could occur simultaneously in a single neonate. A nomogram was subsequently constructed based on the final model coefficients to provide individualised estimates of mortality risk. These tools were designed to translate the statistical model into a practical format suitable for use in resource-limited clinical settings. The study was reported in accordance with the TRIPOD statement to ensure transparency, reproducibility and methodological rigour in prediction model development and reporting.30

Results

Baseline characteristics and neonatal outcomes

A total of 955 LBW neonates were included in the study, of whom 693 (72.5%) survived and 262 (27.5%) died during the follow-up period. Of the neonates, 520 (54.5%) were male. The mean (±SD) maternal age was 27.51±6.23 years, and most mothers, 710 (74.35%), were aged 20–34 years. The mean (±SD) birth weight was 1772.8±430.5 g, and the mean (±SD) gestational age was 33.5±3.1 weeks. Regarding birth weight categories, 658 (68.9%) weighed 1500–2499 g, while 33 (3.46%) weighed <1000 g. For gestational age, 519 (54.4%) were born at 32 weeks to <37 weeks. Among neonatal clinical conditions, 393 (41.2%) had sepsis, 290 (30.4%) had respiratory distress syndrome and 89 (9.3%) had perinatal asphyxia. KMC was provided to 321 (33.6%) neonates and exclusive breastfeeding was practised in 623 (65.2%). Overall, 262 (27.5%) neonates died during the follow-up period (table 1).

Development of an NMR prediction model

Predictive modelling of neonatal mortality

The incidence of neonatal mortality among LBW neonates was 27.43% (95% CI 24.69% to 30.36%). LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation was used to select predictors, and 22 of 25 candidate variables were retained for multivariable analysis, while residence, sex, and place of delivery were excluded. The final model identified nine significant predictors of neonatal mortality: ELBW and VLBW, very preterm and moderate-to-late preterm birth, lack of exclusive breastfeeding, absence of KMC, neonatal sepsis, perinatal asphyxia, and respiratory distress syndrome (table 2). Multicollinearity diagnostics showed no evidence of concern, with all VIF values below 10 in both full and reduced models (highest: 3.45 in the full model and 3.23 in the reduced model for very preterm gestational age).

Discrimination and calibration performance of the risk prediction model

The final reduced model had an AUC of 84.2% (95% CI 81.5% to 86.9%) (figure 1) and a Brier Score of 0.1351. The prediction density plot shows good separation between positive and negative cases, with some overlap indicating limited uncertainty in a subset of predictions (online supplemental file 1). The model also demonstrated excellent calibration, with predicted probabilities closely matching observed outcomes. The calibration curve was non-significant (p=0.84) with narrow confidence bands around the 45 degree line (online supplemental file 1), and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test was also non-significant (p=0.456), indicating adequate model fit.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve illustrating the discrimination ability of the original prediction model, the bootstrap validated model and the derived risk score. AUC, area under the curve.

Determination of the optimal threshold for predicted NMR

Three probability cut-off methods were evaluated (table 3): Youden’s Index, sensitivity equals specificity (SpEqualSe) and a fixed threshold of 0.5. Youden’s Index identified 0.32 as the optimal cut-off, while SpEqualSe gave 0.27. At 0.5, the model showed high specificity (91.3%) but moderate sensitivity (51.9%), with an accuracy of 80.5% (95% CI 77.87% to 82.99%). At 0.32, sensitivity and specificity were better balanced (71.0% and 81.5%), with an accuracy of 78.6% (95% CI 75.9% to 81.2%), positive predictive value (PPV) of 59.2% and negative predictive value (NPV) of 88.1%. At 0.27, sensitivity and specificity were similar (75.6% and 75.5%), with an accuracy of 75.5% (95% CI 72.64% to 78.2%), PPV of 53.8% and NPV of 89.1%. Overall, the 0.32 threshold provided the best balance and was selected for risk classification.

Model validation

Development of a validated simplified risk scoring system

Based on the relative contributions of each predictor in the final multivariable model, a simplified risk scoring system was developed (table 2). Each coefficient was divided by the smallest coefficient (0.450) and rounded to the nearest integer to obtain the score. Since some predictors are mutually exclusive (eg, birth weight and gestational age categories), the maximum achievable score for an individual neonate at any given time is 17, while the total possible score across all predictors is 20 points.

To differentiate between neonates at low risk and high risk, a cut-off score of 6 was determined using the Youden Index (table 4). Low-risk newborns had scores between 0 and 5, and high-risk newborns had scores between 6 and 17. The observed mortality rate was 51.99% (209 out of 402) in the high-risk group and 9.58% (53 out of 553) in the low-risk group. The model had a sensitivity of 79.77% and a specificity of 72.15% at the best cut-off point of 6. The PPV was 52.0%, and NPV was 90.42%. The positive and negative likelihood ratio tests are 2.86 and 0.28, respectively. Overall, the model had an accuracy of 74.24%.

Development of a validated nomogram for predicting neonatal mortality

In addition to the risk score classification system, a nomogram was developed to estimate NMR (figure 3). After bootstrap validation, the model showed good discrimination (AUC=0.835, 95% CI 0.807 to 0.864) (figure 1). In the nomogram, each predictor is assigned points based on its category, which are summed to obtain a total score corresponding to the predicted probability of mortality. Using Youden’s Index, a cut-off probability of 0.322 was used to classify neonates into low-risk (<0.322) and high-risk (≥0.322) groups.

Validation of model discrimination and calibration performance

The model showed good discrimination after internal validation with 1000 bootstrap resampling (AUC=0.835, 95% CI 0.807 to 0.864) (figure 1), with minimal optimism (0.007). The Brier Score was 0.1387, and the corrected Somers' rank correlation coefficient (Dxy) was 0.67. The simplified risk score showed similar performance (AUC=83.8%, 95% CI 81.1% to 86.6%) (figure 1). Calibration was excellent, with the bias-corrected curve closely aligned with the 45 degree line and calibration slope and intercept of 0.956 and –0.033, respectively (figure 4).

Discussion

This study developed and internally validated a NMR prediction model for LBW neonates in northwestern Ethiopia. Among 955 neonates, the mortality incidence was 27.43% (95% CI 24.69% to 30.36%). The final model identified nine predictors: ELBW and VLBW, very preterm and moderate-to-late preterm, neonatal sepsis, perinatal asphyxia, respiratory distress syndrome, absence of exclusive breastfeeding, and lack of KMC.

ELBW and VLBW were strong predictors of neonatal mortality. These findings were consistent with previous studies that linked these categories to physiological immaturity and increased vulnerability.23 6 20 31,35 Very preterm and moderate-to-late preterm also have high prognostic value in this study, similar to existing studies that identify these gestational periods as critical due to underdeveloped organ systems.3 20 33 36 Neonatal sepsis was also a strong predictor in this study and has been widely recognised in the literature for its high case fatality rate, especially in low-resource settings.2 3 6 9

Perinatal asphyxia was another important predictor in the study, reinforcing earlier findings that associate birth asphyxia-caused hypoxic injury with poor outcomes in LBW neonates.6 20 31 Respiratory distress syndrome also had a strong predictive power. This might be due to a deficiency of surfactant factor in preterm LBW neonates that causes respiratory failure and death.2 3 20 35 The absence of exclusive breastfeeding was associated with higher mortality risk. This was in line with previous studies that associate the effect of breastfeeding with immune protection and pure nutrition.31 34 37 The absence of KMC was also a significant predictor, which is in line with previous studies highlighting its high benefits associated with thermoregulation and infection prevention.6 31 36 38

Globally, several NMR prediction models have been developed for LBW neonates, most using logistic regression. However, many were developed and validated in high-resource tertiary care settings, which may limit their transportability to low-resource environments due to differences in case mix, data availability and clinical care practices.

For example, the model developed by Ambalavanan and Carlo demonstrated good discrimination among extremely LBW neonates (AUC=0.87) using a limited set of early predictors, including birth weight, gestational age and 5 min APGAR Score.15 While this supports ease of application, the model is primarily based on baseline maturity indicators and does not account for postnatal clinical deterioration or care-related factors. From a TRIPOD and Prediction model Risk Of Bias ASsessment Tool perspective, this may introduce spectrum and predictor selection limitations, potentially reducing transportability to settings where postnatal complications strongly influence outcomes.

Similarly, the NMR 2000 Score, externally validated in the UK, The Gambia and India, showed consistently good discrimination (AUC 0.817 to 0.893) and reported good calibration (slope approximately 1, intercept approximately 0).12 13 Although this suggests robustness across diverse settings, the model is still based on a relatively small number of physiological and respiratory support variables measured early in life. Contemporary methodological guidance emphasises that such parsimonious models may not fully capture evolving clinical trajectories, particularly in low-resource settings where variability in postnatal care is substantial and may affect model calibration over time.

More complex models developed in Spanish NICUs reported very high discrimination (AUC 0.83 to 0.99).17 However, these models depend on extensive laboratory parameters, detailed maternal information and high-intensity monitoring data. According to TRIPOD recommendations, such reliance on resource-intensive predictors can limit external validity and restrict clinical applicability in low-income and middle-income settings, where data availability is often incomplete and inconsistent.

The NEOCOSUR model, externally validated across South America, achieved good discrimination (AUC=0.85) using variables such as birth weight, gestational age, APGAR Score, congenital malformations, antenatal steroid exposure and infant sex.14 While this model benefits from external validation across multiple settings, its predictor set remains largely restricted to perinatal factors measured at or immediately after birth. This limits its ability to incorporate postnatal complications, which are increasingly recognised as important determinants of neonatal mortality, particularly in resource-limited environments.

Similarly, the KNN model demonstrated good performance (C statistic=0.86) and included structured perinatal predictors such as antenatal steroids, gestational age, birth weight, APGAR Score and congenital anomalies, with external validation supporting its statistical performance.11 However, its development within a highly organised neonatal registry system may introduce concerns regarding data completeness and case mix differences when applied to less resourced settings, potentially affecting both calibration and transportability.

The United States Neonatal Research Network model, despite external validation across developed settings, showed slightly lower discrimination (AUC=0.82) and evidence of weaker calibration (p=0.05).16 This may reflect model optimism and limited recalibration when applied outside the derivation population, highlighting a known limitation in prediction model research when external transport is not accompanied by model updating.

Overall, although existing neonatal mortality prediction models show good discrimination and methodological rigour, many rely mainly on static prenatal and early postnatal variables with limited consideration of evolving clinical conditions and care-related practices. Contemporary prediction modelling guidance emphasises the importance of discrimination, calibration, external validation and model updating to ensure transportability.30 39 These limitations underscore the need for context-specific and methodologically robust models for resource-limited settings.

The implementation of this risk prediction tool in routine neonatal care has important clinical and public health value. Because predicted probabilities from regression models may be difficult to use in low-resource NICUs without electronic tools, a simplified risk score and nomogram were developed using routinely collected admission variables. These tools support rapid bedside risk estimation, clinical decision-making, triage and prioritisation of care. The nomogram provides a visual representation of the model, while the simplified score enables quick manual calculation, improving feasibility for front-line healthcare providers. Although not intended to replace predicted probabilities, these tools may enhance implementation, standardise risk assessment and support better allocation of neonatal care resources in resource-limited settings.

Beyond individual clinical use, the model also has important health system and policy applications. It may support estimation of the burden of high-risk LBW neonates; NICU planning; and allocation of limited resources such as staff, equipment, oxygen therapy and medications. In addition, the model may assist with workload forecasting, performance monitoring and identification of gaps in neonatal care services. It may also facilitate risk-adjusted comparisons across hospitals or NICUs for benchmarking and quality-of-care monitoring, particularly in settings with heterogeneous resources and clinical practices where unadjusted comparisons may be misleading.

Additionally, some predictors included in the model, such as KMC and exclusive breastfeeding, may reflect both underlying patient severity and quality of care because they are influenced by clinical care practices. This should be considered when interpreting model outputs, particularly for benchmarking purposes, as differences in outcomes may partly reflect variations in care delivery rather than baseline risk alone. More broadly, the model may help inform regional and national strategies aimed at improving neonatal survival and strengthening quality improvement initiatives in maternal and newborn health services.

Strengths

This study has several important strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first multicentre study in Ethiopia to develop and internally validate a NMR prediction model for LBW neonates using prospective follow-up data. The study included an adequate number of outcome events, supporting robust model development while minimising the risk of overfitting. In addition, a simple and practical risk score and nomogram were developed to facilitate individualised risk prediction in clinical settings. Multiple imputation was applied to handle missing data and reduce potential bias.

Limitations

The model was developed and internally validated using bootstrap resampling only, without external validation in an independent data set. While internal validation assesses model optimism and stability, it does not fully evaluate performance across different populations or clinical settings. Consequently, the generalisability and transportability of the model to other hospitals, regions or healthcare systems remain uncertain. Variations in case mix, clinical practice and resource availability may influence model performance, underscoring the need for external validation in independent cohorts before broader implementation.

The study was conducted in public hospitals in north-western Ethiopia, which may limit applicability to private health facilities and other regions with differing levels of neonatal care and clinical practices. Although multiple imputation was used to address missing data, it relies on the MAR assumption, which may not hold for all variables, particularly APGAR Scores with higher levels of missingness due to out-of-facility deliveries. In addition, some predictors may reflect both underlying neonatal illness severity and early clinical management decisions, introducing potential time-related bias.

Ethnicity and race data were not collected, as the study was conducted in a relatively homogeneous population and these variables were not included in the prespecified data collection tool or candidate predictors during model development. We acknowledge that sociocultural factors may be relevant in other settings and could be incorporated in future studies where such data are available. Finally, postdischarge mortality was not captured, which may have led to underestimation of overall neonatal mortality. Future work should focus on external validation in diverse settings and further refinement of the model to enhance its robustness and clinical applicability.

Conclusions

This study developed and internally validated a simple risk prediction model for identifying LBW neonates at high risk of mortality using readily available clinical variables. The model demonstrated good discrimination (AUC=0.842) and good calibration. A scoring system and nomogram were derived to facilitate individualised risk estimation. The findings suggest potential for clinical application in supporting risk stratification and decision-making in neonatal care; however, these results should be interpreted with caution. The model was only internally validated, and external validation in independent settings is required before any clinical use or broader application. In addition, possible bias related to some admission-related predictors should be considered when interpreting the findings. Future research should focus on external validation, assessment of clinical utility in different settings and further refinement of the model before implementation in routine care.
