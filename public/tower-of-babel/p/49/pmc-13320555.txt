Surveillance and response to emerging and re-emerging zoonotic diseases: advancing One Health from evidence to action
Authors: Xin-Yu Feng, Roger Frutos, Xing-Quan Zhu, Jordi Serra-Cobo, Xiao-Nong Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractZoonotic diseases pose significant threats to global health security, societal stability, and economic well-being by causing widespread illness, overwhelming healthcare systems, disrupting communities, and driving the majority of emerging infectious diseases. This editorial synthesizes key insights on surveillance and response to zoonotic diseases, arguing that current systems must evolve from fragmented, human-centric detection toward integrated, anticipatory platforms. The collected papers underscore that traditional surveillance fails to address the accelerating drivers of zoonotic emergence, such as climate change, agricultural intensification, and antimicrobial resistance. Instead, they advocate for a paradigm shift wherein genomics, artificial intelligence, and digital tools are leveraged to interpret risk signals across human, animal, and environmental domains—enabling early warning and source attribution. Critically, the issue emphasizes that technological advances alone are insufficient; effective response depends on embedding these tools within robust One Health governance, sustained cross-sectoral collaboration, and equity-focused investments. By framing surveillance as an action-oriented continuum, the contributions collectively assert that preventing future zoonotic crises requires transforming One Health from a conceptual ideal into an operational infrastructure capable of translating data into timely, context-specific interventions.Keywords: Zoonotic diseases, One Health, Surveillance, Genomic epidemiology, Artificial intelligence, Outbreak preparedness

Background

Zoonotic diseases have repeatedly demonstrated their capacity to disrupt societies, overwhelm health systems, and reshape global priorities. Despite decades of scientific progress, the drivers of zoonotic emergence, including climate change, ecological disruption, intensification of food systems, antimicrobial misuse, and unprecedented human mobility, persist and are accelerating [1,2]. The challenge, therefore, is no longer limited to identifying zoonotic pathogens, but to reconceptualizing surveillance and response systems capable of anticipating, contextualizing, and acting upon risk across the human–animal–environment interface.

This Special Issue of Science in One Health, “Surveillance and Response to Emerging and Re-emerging Zoonotic Diseases,” assembles 10 contributions that collectively advance the conceptual, methodological, and operational foundations of One Health surveillance and response. Rather than treating zoonoses as isolated events, the research articles and reviews published here illustrate a shift toward integrated intelligence, where molecular data, digital tools, ecological signals, and intervention research converge within a One Health framework. Together, these papers provide a timely perspective on how surveillance systems must evolve—from fragmented monitoring toward adaptive, action-oriented platforms.

From detection to anticipation: rethinking the purpose of surveillance

A recurring insight across this Special Issue is that traditional, human-centric surveillance remains insufficient for modern zoonotic threats. Effective zoonotic disease surveillance must extend beyond traditional human case detection to encompass animal reservoirs, vectors, food systems, and environmental sources. Several studies demonstrate how pathogens circulate silently across these interconnected domains before manifesting as human disease.

The molecular detection of potentially zoonotic Rickettsia species in dogs and their ticks in Malawi highlights the role of companion animals as epidemiological sentinels [3]. Yet realizing this potential requires surveillance systems that are proactive and upstream—reaching into animal reservoirs and vectors before spillover occurs, rather than waiting for human cases to signal a threat that is already spreading.

Leveraging genomics and molecular tools for early detection and risk assessment

Another unifying theme across the Special Issue is the growing role of genomics and molecular epidemiology in enhancing zoonotic disease surveillance. Several studies in this collection demonstrate how genomic and molecular tools now function as a shared analytical language across veterinary, environmental, and public health disciplines. Whole-genome sequencing and phylogenetic analysis are increasingly used not only to characterize pathogens, but to contextualize them within global transmission networks.

In vector-borne disease surveillance, sequence-based identification of Rickettsia conorii and R. massiliae expands the recognized geographical distribution of zoonotic pathogens in Southern Africa [3]. At the same time, molecular surveillance of endemic zoonoses, such as Nipah virus [4] and Mpox [5], illustrates how long-term genomic monitoring can elucidate evolutionary trends, clade replacement, and shifts in transmission patterns. Similarly, high-resolution multi-locus sequence typing of the zoonotic parasite Toxoplasma gondii across diverse hosts in China revealed substantial cryptic intra-clonal diversity within the dominant ToxoDB#9 lineage and documented the rare North American sylvatic ToxoDB#5 lineage in a captive caracal, extending its known geographic range and highlighting the power of molecular tools to detect hidden genetic diversity and potential transboundary introductions [6]. Such insights are crucial for refining risk assessments, updating diagnostic tools, and informing the development of vaccines or therapeutics.

Beyond individual case studies, broader reviews in this Special Issue emphasize that genomics has become foundational to modern zoonotic surveillance. Analyses of surveillance strategies and emerging technologies consistently identify molecular tools as essential for early detection, source attribution, and risk stratification. Importantly, these contributions also caution that genomic capacity must be coupled with data sharing, governance, and interpretation frameworks to achieve real-world impact.

Artificial intelligence and digital surveillance: from prediction to preparedness

A notable methodological advance highlighted in this Special Issue is the application of artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning, and digital technologies to zoonotic disease surveillance and response. The review by Guo et al. [7] focusing on AI-driven prediction models demonstrates how large-scale datasets, encompassing epidemiological records, climate variables, and social media signals, can be integrated to forecast outbreak risks and identify emerging hotspots.

Machine learning approaches applied to diseases such as Mpox demonstrate their potential for early detection, trend forecasting, and even public sentiment analysis, which can inform risk communication strategies [8]. Importantly, these tools offer opportunities to shift surveillance from reactive reporting toward anticipatory intelligence, enabling public health systems to act before outbreaks escalate.

These approaches represent a conceptual shift from retrospective analysis to anticipatory surveillance, where risk is modelled before it materializes as disease. However, the contributions also converge on a critical caveat: technological sophistication alone does not guarantee preparedness. Reviews of modern surveillance solutions emphasize persistent challenges related to data quality, interoperability, ethical governance, and inequitable access, particularly in low-resource settings [4,9]. Thus, this Special Issue positions AI not as a replacement for conventional surveillance but as an augmentation tool whose effectiveness depends on institutional readiness, cross-sector collaboration, and sustained investment.

From surveillance to action: evidence-informed response strategies

Beyond detection and prediction, the Special Issue places strong emphasis on response and intervention, reflecting the full surveillance-to-action continuum envisioned by the One Health concept. Several studies illustrate how integrated evidence can inform targeted, context-specific control strategies.

The field evaluation of smart collar-based dog deworming for echinococcosis control provides rare experimental evidence that technology-enabled interventions can enhance implementation fidelity and reduce zoonotic transmission risk at scale [10]. By embedding intervention delivery within a One Health framework, this study illustrates how surveillance insights can be operationalized into measurable public health gains.

Similarly, analyses of recurrent outbreaks, such as those caused by Nipah virus in Bangladesh, demonstrate how integrating climatic, ecological, and demographic data can identify persistent hotspots and seasonal risk patterns, thereby informing targeted prevention strategies [4]. In addition, evaluations of the evolving Mpox epidemic further underscore the importance of sustained surveillance, vaccination strategies, and global coordination in responding to re-emerging zoonotic diseases [5]. Collectively, these studies reinforce a key editorial message: One Health surveillance must be explicitly designed to support decision-making and intervention, rather than existing as a passive reporting system.

Building sustainable One Health surveillance systems

While the studies in this Special Issue highlight substantial methodological progress, they also expose enduring structural gaps. Reviews focusing on surveillance frameworks in Asia and globally note that many existing systems remain event-driven, pathogen-specific, and sectorally siloed. The absence of sustained funding mechanisms, integrated governance, and trained One Health workforces continues to limit the scalability and resilience of these efforts [11].

Importantly, several contributions argue that One Health surveillance should not be restricted to emerging threats alone, but must also address endemic zoonoses, antimicrobial resistance, and slow-moving ecological risks [12]. This broader framing positions One Health not as an emergency response model, but as a long-term public health infrastructure.

Conclusions

Taken together, the 10 papers in this Special Issue articulate a clear perspective: the future of zoonotic disease control depends on surveillance systems that are integrated, anticipatory, and action-oriented. Advances in genomics, artificial intelligence, and digital health have created unprecedented opportunities to detect and interpret risk signals across human, animal, and environmental domains. Yet these tools will only realize their potential if embedded within coherent One Health governance structures. As zoonotic threats continue to evolve under compounded global pressures, this Special Issue underscores that One Health is no longer a conceptual ideal but an operational necessity. The challenge ahead lies in translating the evidence and innovations presented here into routine practice, policy alignment, and sustained investment, ensuring that surveillance truly serves as the foundation for timely and effective response.
