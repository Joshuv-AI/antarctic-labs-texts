Educational strategies for integrating gender perspectives in health professions training: a systematic review to advance diversity, equity, and inclusion
Authors: Karina López, Ana María Cabanas, Fabian Pinazo, Nathalia Zambrano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreparing health professionals to deliver equitable, culturally competent care requires integrating gender perspectives throughout medical training. While essential for addressing health disparities affecting women, men, and gender-diverse populations, implementation approaches vary widely across institutions and regions. Evidence on effective strategies remains fragmented. This systematic review synthesizes educational interventions for gender-sensitive training, evaluates outcomes on student competencies, and examines global research patterns to identify equity gaps in the scholarship itself.MethodsFollowing PRISMA 2020 guidelines, we conducted a prospectively registered systematic review (PROSPERO CRD420251132158). We systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus for studies published between 2015 and 2025 that evaluated educational strategies for integrating gender perspectives in health professions training. Two independent reviewers performed screening, data extraction, and quality appraisal using a generic risk-of-bias tool. Thematic synthesis identified core educational strategy domains and their effectiveness. We complemented this with bibliometric mapping (co-citation, co-authorship, co-word analysis) to examine geographic distribution of research, international collaboration patterns, and conceptual evolution in the field.ResultsTwenty-four studies met inclusion criteria from 133 records. Six educational strategy domains emerged from thematic synthesis: curricular design, active learning methodologies, faculty development, assessment systems, digital technologies, and interdisciplinary collaboration. Interventions consistently improved clinical knowledge of gender-diverse health needs, recognition and reduction of implicit bias, ethical sensitivity, and gender-affirming communication skills. Bibliometric analysis revealed striking geographic concentration: 72.2% of research originates from European institutions (38% from Germany alone), with minimal international collaboration (12.5) and virtually no Global South representation. This pattern highlights persistent inequities in whose perspectives shape DEI frameworks in medical education.ConclusionsIntegrating gender perspectives into health professions education represents an evidence-based strategy for advancing DEI principles and preparing providers to deliver equitable, culturally competent care. Effective implementation requires dedicated institutional commitment through three key mechanisms: dedicated change agents who champion gender equity, formal structural integration rather than ad-hoc interventions, and systematic faculty development programs. However, the geographic concentration of research in high-income Western contexts raises critical questions about equity in DEI scholarship itself. Achieving gender equity in healthcare globally requires parallel commitments: integrating gender-sensitive curricula in all institutions while ensuring that diverse global voices, especially from the Global South, meaningfully shape DEI discourse and frameworks.Trial registrationPROSPERO CRD420251132158.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-08963-9.Keywords: Diversity, Equity, Inclusion, Gender-sensitive education, Health professions education, Implicit bias, Cultural competence, LGBTQ+ health, Systematic review, Bibliometric analysis

Introduction

Gender analysis in health recognizes differences in needs, behaviors, and experiences among women, men, and gender-diverse populations. Applied to clinical practice, this framework strengthens professionals’ capacity to identify and address stereotypes influencing diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up [1–4]. Building these competencies throughout health professions training creates more inclusive systems responsive to all patients [5–8].

Gender inequalities in health constitute structural social determinants that affect clinical outcomes, access to services, and quality of care across diverse populations [9–13]. The integration of gender perspectives in health professions training has evolved since the 1990 s, when the rise of gender studies and equity policies catalyzed structured curricular incorporations, particularly in North America and Europe [14–16]. However, adoption remains uneven and contingent on institutional policies, resources, and leadership commitment [5, 7, 8]. Recent educational frameworks have increasingly aligned gender perspective integration with broader Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (DEI) initiatives in health professions education. Framing gender as a key dimension of DEI underscores its role in advancing social accountability, cultural responsiveness, and equitable learning environments across medical training systems [17–19].

These disparities originate largely from social constructions of power, norms, and culturally assigned roles rather than biological factors alone [1, 20–22]. Table 1 provides concrete clinical examples illustrating how failure to integrate gender perspectives affects diagnostic accuracy, treatment effectiveness, and patient safety across diverse health conditions. As shown in the table, gender-related knowledge gaps contribute to missed or delayed diagnoses in cardiovascular disease, systematic under-recognition of pain, inappropriate pharmacological dosing, misinterpretation of mental health symptoms, barriers to gender-affirming care for LGBTQ+ populations, and reduced reproductive autonomy. These examples underscore that integrating gender perspectives in health professions education is not only an equity concern but a clinical quality and patient safety imperative.

Currently, incorporation varies widely across regions and programs. While some institutions in high-income settings have integrated dedicated sex- and gender-sensitive modules recognizing their relevance for reducing health inequities [6, 14, 34–36], training in many low- and middle-income settings remains limited or absent [37–39]. Educational disparities perpetuate clinical biases and unequal outcomes, with insufficient gender training affecting both providers’ decisions and patients’ ability to receive equitable care [3, 40–42]. Moreover, curricula frequently reproduce androcentric models that restrict gender content to sexual and reproductive health without incorporating critical perspectives on how inequalities operate in clinical practice [1, 39, 41, 43].

Despite growing recognition, a significant gap persists in systematized evidence about effective strategies for gender perspective integration. While promising approaches have been identified, including dedicated modules, blended learning, case-based learning, and digital platforms [3, 5, 14, 35, 44], these remain primarily isolated initiatives without integration into sustainable curricular structures or systematic evaluation [7, 42, 45, 46]. More critically, existing reviews have not examined equity dimensions within DEI scholarship itself, specifically, whose perspectives shape ’best practices’ and whether evidence production patterns reflect or contradict stated equity commitments. This fragmentation restricts the development of replicable training models adaptable to diverse institutional and cultural contexts [38, 47, 48].

Evidence demonstrates that professionals trained with a gender perspective more effectively identify and address inequities in care, improving patient outcomes while enhancing their capacity to navigate power dynamics in clinical environments and mitigate implicit biases affecting clinical decisions [2, 20, 21, 49–51]. These competencies support reflective practice and person-centered care essential for addressing complex health problems and advancing equitable healthcare delivery across diverse patient populations [4, 22].

Evidence synthesis in health professions education research faces unique methodological challenges when addressing complex, context-dependent interventions such as gender perspective integration. The conceptual heterogeneity of integration strategies, combined with diverse educational contexts and outcome measures, limits traditional quantitative meta-analysis applicability. While systematic reviews excel at integrating qualitative and mixed-methods evidence to identify patterns and mechanisms, they provide limited insight into collaboration networks, temporal trends, and geographic distribution of evidence production—dimensions particularly relevant for understanding equity in DEI scholarship itself [52]. Bibliometric analysis offers complementary meta-research insights revealing structural characteristics of knowledge domains, including research clusters, institutional collaboration patterns, and conceptual evolution [53, 54].

Two gaps characterize current literature: First, despite growing recognition of the importance of gender perspectives in health professions education, systematized evidence remains fragmented, with most reported interventions described as isolated initiatives lacking sustained curricular integration or systematic evaluation across contexts. Second, existing reviews have largely focused on identifying effective educational strategies without examining equity dimensions within DEI scholarship itself, specifically, whose perspectives shape prevailing frameworks, and whether patterns of knowledge production reflect or contradict stated equity commitments.

To address these gaps, this review adopts an innovative hybrid design that combines thematic synthesis with bibliometric analysis. Thematic synthesis enables in-depth examination of educational strategies, implementation mechanisms, and learner outcomes associated with gender perspective integration, clarifying how such interventions advance DEI principles in practice. Bibliometric analysis provides complementary meta-research insights into research production patterns, geographic distribution, and international collaboration networks, revealing structural inequities in knowledge generation and dissemination within DEI scholarship [17–19].

RQ1 and RQ2 are addressed through thematic synthesis following Braun and Clarke’s framework [55], enabling analysis of intervention content, pedagogical mechanisms, and reported outcomes. Addressing RQ3 requires methodological innovation: while thematic synthesis captures evidence-based recommendations, bibliometric analysis enables reflexive examination of equity within the scholarship itself by assessing who produces this knowledge, where it is produced, and how it circulates globally. In doing so, this review applies DEI principles not only to the educational interventions under study, but also to the meta-level examination of the evidence base that informs them.

By situating gender perspective integration within the broader DEI framework and critically examining both educational practice and knowledge production, this review contributes to advancing equitable, inclusive, and socially accountable health professions education.

Methods

Study design and registration

We conducted this systematic review following PRISMA 2020 guidelines and a prospectively registered protocol (PROSPERO CRD420251132158), ensuring transparency in identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and inclusion processes. This review examines gender perspective integration as a diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) strategy in health professions education. The completed PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Information sources and search strategy

We systematically searched three electronic databases: PubMed (MEDLINE), Web of Science Core Collection, and Scopus, covering publications from January 2015 through November 2025. This timeframe captures contemporary educational practices following major international gender equity policy frameworks while maintaining relevance to current health professions training contexts. The selected databases provide comprehensive coverage of biomedical, educational, and interdisciplinary health research, with complementary indexing strengths relevant to medical and health professions education.

The search strategy was guided by an adapted SPIDER framework (Sample, Phenomenon of Interest, Design, Evaluation, Research type), selected for its suitability for reviews of educational interventions, where traditional PICO-based frameworks are often less applicable. Three core conceptual domains were defined a priori: (1) health professions education (Sample), (2) integration of a gender perspective in health education (Phenomenon of Interest), and (3) educational strategies and curricular interventions (Design). These domains informed the grouping and combination of search terms using Boolean operators. No restrictions were applied by study design or research type in order to maximize sensitivity, given the exploratory and interdisciplinary nature of the field.

The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms across the three conceptual domains. Complete database-specific search strings, syntax adaptations, and retrieval results are presented in Table 2. Search terms were developed through an iterative, team-based process involving preliminary scoping searches, interdisciplinary consensus among the author team, consultation with a medical librarian, and pilot testing to ensure adequate sensitivity. Methodological rigor was subsequently ensured through eligibility screening rather than restrictive search filters.

Duplicate records were removed using Rayyan, a web-based systematic review management platform that provides automated duplicate detection. All automatically flagged duplicates were manually verified by reviewers to prevent erroneous exclusion of legitimately similar records. Rayyan was also used to support independent title and abstract screening. Data extraction and synthesis were conducted using structured spreadsheets with predefined fields to ensure consistency. Screening decisions, exclusion reasons, and extracted data were documented and reviewed during regular team meetings.

In addition to database searches, supplementary identification methods were employed. First, we conducted website screening of key organizations in health professions education and gender equity, including the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), identifying two relevant institutional publications (n = 2). Second, we performed citation searching by reviewing reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews identified during screening, yielding 11 additional records (n = 11). These methods were selected to enhance search completeness while maintaining methodological transparency, in accordance with PRISMA 2020 recommendations. Other sources (e.g., social media platforms or non-systematic web searches) were not used, as they were considered unlikely to yield peer-reviewed studies aligned with the review objectives. Records identified via other methods were assessed separately from database-derived records, as reflected in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). Full search strategies are provided in Supplementary File 2.

Eligibility criteria

We established inclusion and exclusion criteria during protocol development. Studies were included if they met four requirements: (1) peer-reviewed publications in English or Spanish from 2015–2025; (2) primary research using qualitative, quantitative, or mixed-methods designs, including systematized case studies; (3) explicit focus on gender perspective integration in health professions education through coursework, curricular activities, training experiences, or institutional policies; and (4) description, analysis, or reflection on integration processes, training effects, or implementation recommendations.

Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) duplicate publications; (2) conference abstracts, opinion pieces, editorials, or protocols lacking empirical data; (3) narrative or systematic reviews, retained solely as background literature; and (4) studies not explicitly addressing gender perspective in health professions education.

Study selection process

Study selection followed two sequential PRISMA stages. Three independent reviewers assessed titles and abstracts of all records, advancing studies deemed potentially relevant by at least one reviewer to full-text review. The same reviewers then independently evaluated full texts against eligibility criteria. We resolved disagreements through discussion to reach consensus, consulting a senior team member when necessary. Narrative or systematic reviews identified during full-text assessment were excluded from the synthesis per eligibility criteria but were retained for citation searching to identify additional primary studies, as described in Information sources and search strategy section. The complete selection process, including record numbers at each stage and exclusion reasons, appears in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Bibliometric analysis included all 24 studies meeting full-text inclusion criteria for thematic synthesis, with no additional exclusions applied for bibliometric purposes. This ensures alignment between intervention effectiveness findings and meta-research insights about research production patterns. No studies were excluded from bibliometric analysis due to missing metadata or other bibliometric-specific reasons. Details on bibliographic data export and processing are provided in subection 2.7.2.

Data collection and extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized form capturing study design, context, participants, curriculum or intervention characteristics, gender perspective integration, outcomes, and key findings, resolving discrepancies by consensus and contacting authors via email with up to two follow-up reminders over four weeks. The full data extraction table for all 24 included studies is provided in Supplementary File 4.

Quality assessment

Two reviewers independently assessed methodological quality of all studies, reaching consensus through discussion and consulting a third reviewer when needed. Given the heterogeneity of study designs, we employed the Generic Risk of Bias Tool suitable for reviews integrating diverse methodologies. We selected this tool over design-specific instruments because it provides standardized assessment across qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods studies while maintaining sensitivity to methodological rigor within each design—an essential feature for our corpus, which includes systematized case studies, surveys, mixed-methods investigations, and quasi-experimental designs that would otherwise require multiple incompatible assessment frameworks. Assessment covered eight domains: (1) clarity of objectives and gender perspective integration; (2) methodological rigor; (3) sample adequacy; (4) analytical depth and transparency; (5) consideration of biases and reflexivity; (6) findings’ relevance and transferability; (7) coherence between findings and conclusions; and (8) overall risk judgment. Each domain received classifications of low risk, some concerns, or high risk. We generated graphical synthesis using the robvis visualization tool [56]. A graphical summary and detailed, study-level justifications for all 24 included studies are presented in Supplementary File 3.

Data synthesis and analysis

Our dual analytical approach combined thematic synthesis and bibliometric analysis to provide depth and breadth in understanding the evidence landscape.

Thematic synthesis

Given the heterogeneity of designs, populations, and interventions, quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible. We conducted structured thematic synthesis following Braun and Clarke [57] and Thomas and Harden [54]. Our three-stage process involved: (1) line-by-line coding of Results and Discussion sections; (2) organizing codes into intervention-based categories; and (3) iterative refinement into analytical themes. Two reviewers independently coded all findings, resolving discrepancies through discussion. We employed a hybrid inductive-deductive approach to thematic synthesis. Initial coding was primarily inductive and data-driven, with reviewers independently identifying patterns emerging from the included studies. These initial codes were then iteratively grouped and refined through team discussion, informed by sensitizing concepts from educational theory and diversity, equity, and inclusion frameworks. Through this process, 47 initial codes were consolidated into 12 provisional categories and subsequently refined into six final themes based on conceptual coherence and distinctiveness.

We systematically consolidated codes into six main themes representing distinct integration strategies: (1) Curricular Design: systematic integration methods and organizational frameworks; (2) Faculty Development: educator training and capacity-building; (3) Assessment and Feedback: measurement approaches and evaluation tools; (4) Active and Blended Learning: interactive teaching combining online and face-to-face components; (5) Digital Technologies: ICT platforms and e-learning modules; and (6) Collaborative Strategies: multi-stakeholder engagement and inter-institutional partnerships.

We reviewed themes for internal coherence and relevance to the research questions. Descriptive themes captured educational strategies, outcomes (knowledge, attitudes, clinical skills, behaviors), implementation contexts, and barriers or facilitators. Analytical themes emerged through iterative discussion to interpret mechanisms of change, contextual moderators, and implications for sustainable implementation.

Paradigmatic positioning and reflexivity

Our thematic synthesis operates within a critical realist paradigm, acknowledging that educational interventions exist as real phenomena producing measurable effects, while recognizing that their interpretation is shaped by social context, power relations, and researcher positionality [55]. We approached the evidence with an explicit equity lens, examining not only intervention effectiveness but also whose voices, institutions, and perspectives shape the evidence base, a reflexive stance aligned with our research question about equity within DEI scholarship itself.

The research team comprises health professions educators and researchers from Latin America (Chile), bringing perspectives from the Global South that are markedly underrepresented in this evidence base (as our findings reveal). This positioning sensitized us to geographic imbalances, resource constraints, and cultural transferability concerns that might be less visible to researchers working exclusively in well-resourced Western contexts. Our pre-understanding included awareness of persistent androcentrism in medical education and recognition that gender perspective integration often faces institutional resistance—assumptions that influenced our attention to implementation barriers and sustainability mechanisms.

We actively questioned our interpretations through several mechanisms: (1) independent dual coding with consensus discussion, (2) examination of negative cases and contradictory findings, (3) explicit attention to context-dependency and transferability limitations in our synthesis, and (4) acknowledgment that ’best practices’ emerging predominantly from European contexts may not represent universal solutions. Our critical stance toward the evidence base itself—examining equity in knowledge production, reflects our commitment to applying DEI principles reflexively to DEI scholarship.

Bibliometric analysis

To complement thematic synthesis, we conducted bibliometric mapping of structural characteristics: geographic distribution, collaboration networks, and temporal evolution. This meta-research layer addresses equity in knowledge production—whose institutions and perspectives construct DEI frameworks, and whose voices remain absent from this discourse.

We extracted bibliographic metadata (authors, affiliations, countries, publication years, journals, keywords, abstracts, citations) from Web of Science and Scopus, processing records with R 4.3.2 and the bibliometrix package [58].

Our bibliometric examination covered four key areas: (1) descriptive indicators (publication trends, document types, author and citation data); (2) geographic distribution, highlighting institutional concentrations, collaboration patterns, and Global North–South disparities relevant to equity in DEI discourse; (3) temporal trends, assessing publication growth and field maturity; and (4) source analysis, identifying journal and disciplinary representation. These metrics offered insight into research production, geographic inequities, field evolution, and collaboration structures that influence the global shaping of gender-inclusive medical education.

Descriptive bibliometric indicators were calculated to summarize the corpus (publication trends, document types, authorship, and collaboration patterns) and are reported in the Results section.

Citation analysis serves a specific methodological purpose in addressing RQ3 regarding equity in DEI scholarship. We use citation metrics not as quality indicators or proxies for conceptual trustworthiness—as the reviewer correctly notes, citation counts do not necessarily reflect appropriate use, positive impact, or correct application of concepts [55]. Rather, citation patterns reveal knowledge diffusion dynamics, international visibility, and scholarly attention across geographic regions. This is particularly relevant for examining whether research from the Global South receives equitable recognition compared to Global North institutions, a core equity concern within DEI scholarship itself. We present normalized total citations (NTC) to account for publication year effects and report the top 10 papers to illustrate field-shaping contributions, not to validate quality.

Results

Study selection

Database searches identified 133 records. After removal of duplicates (n = 40), 93 records underwent title and abstract screening, of which 63 were excluded. Thirty-five reports were sought for retrieval, and five were not retrieved, resulting in 30 full-text reports assessed for eligibility. Ten reports were excluded due to insufficient methodological quality (n = 3), lack of educational integration of the gender approach (n = 3), or narrative or systematic review design (n = 4), yielding 20 studies included from database searches. Additionally, 13 records were identified via other methods, including website screening (n = 2) and citation searching (n = 11). All 13 reports were assessed for eligibility, and nine were excluded (insufficient methodological quality, n = 6; no educational integration of the gender approach, n = 2; narrative or systematic reviews, n = 1), resulting in four additional included studies. In total, 24 studies were included in the final analysis. The complete study selection process is presented in Fig. 1.

Educational strategies for incorporating gender perspective in health professions education

Curricular design and integration

Curricular design emerged as a primary strategy for sustainable gender perspective incorporation. Experiences include curricular reforms, elective and mandatory courses, and longitudinal models from early years through specialized clinical modules [14, 59, 61–63]. Thematic or modular organizations facilitate transversal integration of gender-differentiated biomedical and social content [14, 62, 63]. Approaches address gender-based violence, interpersonal relationships, professional stereotypes, and leadership-equity articulation [2, 49, 61, 64].

At the institutional level, curricular reviews include materials redesign, equity mapping, and incorporation of gender-based objectives [37, 38, 65], along with reproductive health modules oriented toward prevention and ethical approaches to discrimination [37, 63, 65]. Explicit learning objectives with gender focus have been incorporated into course plans [15], supported by tools like the Gender Lens Tool to evaluate and redesign programs [36, 50, 66]. Educational materials review mechanisms eliminate biases and promote inclusive resources [50, 66]. Biomedical content differentiated by sex appears in courses on physiology, pharmacology, and reproductive health [14, 67, 68], alongside activities promoting critical analysis of stereotypes and hierarchies [35, 60, 69].

Longitudinal curricular models were reported, exemplified by the Charité Medical School curriculum, which integrates gender-sensitive content progressively from foundational courses to clinical clerkships [14]. Targeted preclinical interventions were also described, such as the University of Ulm’s anatomy course incorporating a dedicated Gender Medicine module with clinical examples to address sex- and gender-related differences in care [59].

Active learning methodologies

Active learning methodologies emerged as recurring integration strategies to strengthen gender-perspective education by explicitly engaging learners in identifying gender bias, applying sex- and gender-based analysis, and practicing gender-sensitive clinical reasoning in authentic learning contexts.

Clinical simulation with standardized patients and sex-/gender-differentiated simulators enabled students to recognize biases, apply culturally competent communication, and strengthen clinical reasoning within gender-sensitive contexts [2, 62, 69]. Problem-based and case-based learning introduced gender issues in biomedical and psychosocial contexts, applying critical reasoning tools like the Gender Lens Tool [15, 36, 37, 62, 63]. For instance, a Gender Lens seminar at Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf engaged students in applying the tool to disease scenarios to examine gender-related differences in diagnosis, course, and treatment, facilitating the translation of gender concepts into applied clinical reasoning [36].

Thematic workshops integrated flipped-classroom approaches, critical self-reflection, and discussion spaces, while extracurricular activities promoted connection with clinical and academic environments [2, 38, 44, 49, 63]. A blended workshop at Western Sydney University School of Medicine combined online preparatory materials with face-to-face discussions to address gender-related content, allowing in-person sessions to focus on reflective analysis of bias and inequities in clinical practice [44]. Collaborative work and shared learning through interdisciplinary groups promoted peer dialogue and collective problem solving around gendered experiences in health care and education [35, 68].

Blended learning combined asynchronous resources with face-to-face sessions, incorporating multimedia on sexual diversity, gender identity, and clinical biases [35, 67, 68]. Critical discussion spaces in clinical courses fostered debates on diagnostic dilemmas, reflections on hegemonic norms, and analysis of discriminatory incidents, contributing to professionals’ ethical awareness regarding inequality [34, 60, 62, 63, 65, 70].

Faculty development

Faculty development emerged as key for gender perspective integration. Frequent initiatives included interdisciplinary workshops offering guidelines, case studies, and clinical content [37, 59, 61]. Training programs emphasized inclusive language, sex-gender identities, and elimination of institutional barriers [2, 62, 63, 67]. Digital resources like the Gender Lens Tool and eGender platform supported content planning and redesign [35, 36]. Training in active methodologies included clinical simulation, reflective workshops, and analysis of discriminatory incidents [49, 50, 63, 69].

Critical and emancipatory frameworks promoted appropriation of intersectional concepts and feminist perspectives [14, 34, 38, 62, 63, 66]. Instruments like the SGEM Toolkit provided guidance for addressing implicit biases and discriminatory situations [44, 51, 64]. Several programs advanced toward institutionalization through checklists, curricular audits, and evaluation mechanisms, ensuring sustainability [15, 63, 70].

A structured training program implemented at Bar-Ilan University in Israel included interdisciplinary workshops on physiology, pharmacology, and reproductive health, enabling instructors to revise biased materials, integrate gender-sensitive discussions, and strengthen pedagogical competence in equitable clinical education [67]. Similarly, a modular faculty development initiative conducted at Taipei Medical University in Taiwan incorporated guided reflection, gender-equity modules, and case-based dialogue to enhance instructors’ confidence, improve recognition of structural gender disparities, and support clinical teaching practices [62].

Assessment and feedback

Assessment and feedback emerged as central components for integration. Frequent strategies included gender-specific rubrics and questionnaires to measure competencies in knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions about structural inequalities [2, 59, 61–63]. Several programs institutionalized gender perspective in evaluation systems for written exams, practical tests, and oral examinations [14, 15, 34, 50, 66]. Structured evaluations in simulated and real clinical scenarios included observation of gender-sensitive performance [51, 62, 63, 67]. Critical formative feedback strengthened reflection on inequalities, implicit biases, and discriminatory clinical incidents [37, 38, 60, 62]. Qualitative analysis strategies like open-ended questionnaires enabled identifying patterns in students’ perceptions and adjusting curricular design [44, 70]. Digital platforms offered interactive rubrics, discussion forums, and online feedback, facilitating continuous learning and sensitivity to gender [35, 36, 68].

The Nijmegen Gender Awareness in Medicine Scale (N-GAMS), originally developed and validated in the Netherlands, proved useful for evaluating changes in gender sensitivity and gender-stereotypical attitudes among trainees. Its subsequent adaptations in Austria and later at the University of Lausanne confirmed its reliability across diverse medical education contexts [49, 65]. A curriculum auditing system was implemented at the Yale School of Medicine to evaluate and provide feedback on sex and gender content. This system involved trained medical students who used a standardized survey to audit lectures and small group sessions [34].

Information and communication technologies

ICTs emerged as effective strategies for integration. Asynchronous digital modules enabled comprehensive learning on complex issues and structural inequalities, favoring autonomous reflection [62, 63, 67]. Audiovisual resources like videos and recorded lectures promoted autonomous learning within flipped learning models [2, 44]. Learning management systems enabled interactive activities, forums, and case analysis that enriched participation and critical reflection [14, 63, 68]. Virtual reality created immersive simulations sensitizing students to inequalities and biases [67].

Several specialized platforms demonstrated effectiveness. The eGender provided a comprehensive blended learning environment combining self-paced modules, bibliographic resources, and discussion forums, supporting instructional planning and learner engagement while achieving high overall user satisfaction [35]. At the University of Bern in Switzerland, an e-learning module using interactive cases and video-based scenarios improved learners’ ability to identify gender-related differences in diagnostic reasoning [68]. In the United States, the Sex and Gender in Emergency Medicine (SGEM) toolkit integrated online modules with simulation cases across multiple residency programs, enhancing engagement and knowledge of gender-specific clinical topics [51].

Collaborative and interdisciplinary strategies

Studies reported diverse collaborative strategies for integration. Coordination between academic departments—particularly biomedical and social sciences—supported mainstream content integration [59, 61]. This facilitated addressing decision-making from intersectional perspectives and promoted multidisciplinary teams incorporating ethical, cultural, and gender dimensions [62–64]. Some programs developed joint modules between biomedical and social disciplines, strengthening analysis of social determinants and intersectionalities [14, 63].

In Germany, partnerships with LGBTQ+ community organizations enriched training through authentic patient narratives and co-designed materials that supported inclusive communication and reflective practice [50]. Interdepartmental collaborations within German-speaking universities highlighted coordinated efforts between faculty leadership, gender-medicine experts, medical sociology, and equal-opportunity offices to support unified curricular reforms (Cleaver et al., 2020). In Pakistan, an interdisciplinary team at Aga Khan University integrated gender and sexual diversity content into nursing education through cross-faculty collaboration, improving students’ knowledge and attitudes toward marginalized populations [63].

Educational and clinical benefits

Gender perspective integration reported benefits at educational and clinical levels. Strengthening of clinical competencies included sex-differentiated anamnesis, consideration of social determinants, prescription practice adaptation, and capacity to question biomedical biases [14, 36, 62, 67]. Situated learning enabled adapting interventions to sociocultural contexts and gender norms [37, 51, 63]. Sustained development of ethical sensitivity manifested in identification of structural inequalities, reflection on hidden curriculum, and recognition of power dynamics [2, 38, 50, 59, 60, 62–64].

Communication skills improved through empathetic dialogue, culturally competent communication, and linguistic sensitivity, directly impacting care for LGBTQ+ persons, women victims of violence, and vulnerable groups [2, 37, 67, 69]. Transformations in institutional culture included pedagogies focused on care humanization, teacher training, and greater student commitment to reporting discriminatory practices [38, 44, 61–63]. Implemented strategies contributed to improving person-centered care quality and preventing discrimination such as obstetric violence and invisibilization of gender-diverse symptoms [34, 37, 49, 51, 62].

Recommendations for integration

Evidence allowed identifying recommendations for gender perspective integration organized into three axes: professional training strengthening, clinical environment adaptation, and research methodologies attention.

Regarding professional training, longitudinal curricula that develop critical competencies from early stages are recommended [14, 15, 59, 63]. Training should address biological sex differences and sociocultural gender construction, integrating biomedical knowledge, ethical reflection, and intersectionality [37, 65, 69]. Institutional commitment to mainstream gender perspective is emphasized through strategic plans, explicit objectives, and sustained evaluation mechanisms [14, 15, 34, 62, 66]. Teacher training is fundamental for effective transversal implementation, as educators act as role models facilitating sustainable cultural transformation [38, 60–62]. Critical and emancipatory pedagogies should be strengthened, promoting situated learning, reflective practice, and institutional deconstruction of biases perpetuating inequalities [14, 38, 66].

Risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers independently assessed methodological quality using the Generic Risk of Bias Tool across seven domains, with discrepancies resolved through consensus. Figure 4 presents the domain-level assessment, with detailed justifications in Supplementary File 3.

Risk of bias assessment across seven domains for all 24 included studies. Detailed justifications provided in Supplementary File 3

All studies demonstrated explicit gender perspective integration (Domain 1: low risk). Methodological rigor (Domain 2) showed variable quality, with most studies rated “some concerns,” reflecting the exploratory nature of the research and limited procedural reporting. Sample adequacy (Domain 3) revealed mixed results: five studies achieved low risk through robust sampling [15, 34, 37, 44, 61], while one study showed high risk due to absent empirical data [50]. Analytical transparency (Domain 4) performed well overall, with ten studies achieving low risk, while one study was rated high risk due to significant sampling limitations [64]. Reflexivity (Domain 5) remained a consistent limitation, with only two studies explicitly addressing researcher positioning [2, 14]. Domains 6 and 7 (transferability and coherence) demonstrated strong performance overall.

Cumulative assessment rated all 24 studies as “some concerns,” with no studies classified as high risk overall.

Discussion

Gender-inclusive health professions education faces a paradox: interventions advance diversity, equity, and inclusion effectively, yet the field exhibits troubling inequities in implementation and scholarship. Evidence from 24 studies confirms that gender-sensitive training strengthens clinical competencies, communication with marginalized populations, and ethical sensitivity. However, successful implementations depend predominantly on isolated institutional initiatives rather than sustained curricular reforms.

Moreover, research production itself exhibits troubling inequities: 72.2% of evidence originates from European institutions, with minimal Global South representation or international collaboration. These findings position gender perspective integration as both a DEI strategy within medical education and a domain where DEI principles remain inadequately applied to scholarship itself [17–19]. Thematic analysis identified six intervention domains, curricular design, active methodologies, faculty development, assessment and feedback, digital technologies, and interdisciplinary collaboration, requiring structural transformations, institutional pedagogies, and mechanisms guaranteeing continuity and sustainability. International experiences such as German curricula demonstrated clinical reasoning improvement and ethical sensitivity [14, 59], while others in America and India consolidated person-centered care through critical and intersectional pedagogies [37, 38]. Tools such as the Gender Lens Tool and platforms like eGender were key for content analysis and institutional audits [35, 36, 50].

Addressing the research questions

This section systematically addresses our three research questions, synthesizing findings from both thematic analysis of intervention effectiveness and bibliometric examination of research production patterns.

Educational strategies integrating gender perspectives and advancing DEI principles (RQ1)

Our synthesis revealed six intervention domains representing distinct yet complementary approaches to advancing diversity, equity, and inclusion through gender-sensitive curricula (Table 5). Three key mechanisms emerged across successful implementations: (1) integration of dedicated change agents or institutional champions sustaining commitment beyond pilot phases [14, 15, 59]; (2) embedding within formal institutional structures through explicit learning objectives, assessment integration, and curricular audits rather than optional add-ons [36, 50, 66]; and (3) systematic faculty development ensuring educator capacity and cultural transformation [14, 38, 61].

Critical gaps include limited evidence on long-term sustainability beyond initial implementation phases, insufficient reporting of resource requirements and cost-effectiveness, and absence of comparative effectiveness studies across different intervention combinations. The predominance of single-institution studies limits understanding of transferability mechanisms across diverse educational and cultural contexts.

Impacts on learner competencies for equitable, culturally responsive care (RQ2)

Thematic synthesis revealed three interconnected benefit dimensions relevant to DEI competencies: technical competency enhancement (sex-differentiated clinical reasoning, consideration of social determinants), relational capacity development (culturally competent communication, empathetic dialogue with marginalized groups), and ethical-reflexive transformation (critical examination of power dynamics, hidden curriculum awareness, commitment to equity) [2, 38, 59, 60]. The consistency of these findings across heterogeneous methodologies and contexts strengthens confidence in their validity.

A significant insight is that benefits extend beyond individual learner outcomes to encompass institutional culture shifts, including increased reporting of discriminatory practices, pedagogical humanization, and systemic care quality improvements [38, 44, 61]. However, evidence gaps remain regarding mechanisms linking educational interventions to measurable patient outcomes and health system equity indicators. Most studies assessed immediate or short-term educational outcomes rather than sustained clinical behavior change or population-level health impacts.

Evidence-based recommendations and equity within DEI scholarship (RQ3)

Beyond specific intervention recommendations detailed in Results, two synthesis-level insights emerged. First, effective integration requires multi-level alignment: curricular content integration must be accompanied by parallel faculty development, assessment system modification, and institutional policy commitment. Isolated interventions without this systemic approach showed limited sustainability [14, 15, 61]. Second, critical pedagogies emphasizing power analysis, intersectionality, and structural inequality examination proved more effective than approaches limited to sex-based biological content, suggesting that transformative rather than additive integration yields deeper competency development [38, 60, 66]. Let us remark that these recommendations emerge primarily from European and North American contexts (86.1% of institutional affiliations) and must be adapted considering local resources, cultural contexts, and healthcare system structures.

The bibliometric analysis contextualizes these substantive findings within evidence production patterns revealing significant inequities in DEI scholarship itself. Geographic asymmetries in publication output (72.2% European, 13.9% North American affiliations) underscore inequities in research capacity and raise critical questions about whose perspectives shape DEI frameworks and evidence transferability to resource-constrained settings where gender inequalities often intersect with structural barriers to healthcare access [37, 39]. The minimal international collaboration (12.5%) represents missed opportunities for knowledge exchange, capacity strengthening, and development of contextualized implementation strategies across diverse settings. The modest growth rate (7.18% annually) and concentration of evidence in a narrow temporal window (2017–2020: 69.6% of publications) indicate an emerging rather than mature field, with publication volumes dependent on isolated institutional initiatives rather than systematic international coordination.

Our use of citation metrics requires important caveats. Citation counts indicate scholarly attention and knowledge diffusion patterns rather than quality, conceptual accuracy, or impact on practice. As [55] demonstrate in their thematic analysis guidance, highly cited works may be frequently misapplied. Nevertheless, citation analysis remains valuable for examining equity in knowledge production, specifically, whether research from diverse geographic regions achieves comparable international visibility. The predominant citation accumulation among European and North American publications (Table 3) reflects not necessarily superior quality but greater institutional resources, publication in high-impact journals, and English-language advantage—structural inequities that perpetuate knowledge hierarchies in DEI scholarship.

Implications for practice and research

Combining thematic synthesis with bibliometric analysis proved valuable for examining both intervention effectiveness and equity in knowledge production. Thematic synthesis identified intervention mechanisms and outcomes relevant to DEI implementation, while bibliometric mapping revealed structural inequities potentially limiting evidence applicability in underrepresented regions. This dual approach addresses an equity concern within DEI scholarship itself: whose perspectives shape frameworks, and whose voices remain absent.

For practice, findings support longitudinal curricular integration accompanied by systematic faculty development using critical pedagogies [14, 38, 62, 63, 66], institutional commitment through explicit policies and evaluation mechanisms [15, 61], and creation of inclusive learning environments supported by mentoring and accountability structures [63, 64]. These evidence-based strategies emerge predominantly from well-resourced Western contexts and require contextual adaptation when implemented in settings with different resource availability, cultural norms, and healthcare system structures.

For research, priorities include multicentric longitudinal studies examining sustained clinical practice impacts, mixed-methods investigations capturing organizational culture transformations, development of intersectional assessment instruments, and critically, investment in contextualized research within low- and middle-income settings addressing implementation under resource constraints [37, 39, 71].

Furthermore, the temporal distribution reveals interesting patterns beyond the 2020 peak (see Fig. 2. The modest but steady growth from 2015–2018 (7 studies total) accelerating to concentrated activity in 2019–2020 (10 studies) suggests field consolidation driven by specific catalysts. The apparent decline post-2020 (4 studies in 2021–2022, none in 2023) likely reflects multiple mechanisms: (1) citation accumulation lag for recent publications not yet indexed, (2) pandemic disruption of educational research activities during 2021–2022, and (3) potentially, as the reviewer insightfully suggests, shifting political climates in North America and parts of Europe where ’diversity’ initiatives face increasing political resistance and institutional retrenchment. The resurgence in 2024–2025 (4 studies) may indicate renewed momentum, though definitive interpretation requires additional years of evidence. The modest annual growth rate (7.18%) indicates gradual rather than exponential field expansion, suggesting that gender perspective integration remains dependent on isolated institutional champions rather than systematic international coordination or policy mandate.

Strengths and limitations

This review has several methodological strengths. These include prospective registration in PROSPERO, adherence to PRISMA 2020 reporting standards, independent dual screening and data extraction, and systematic assessment of methodological quality using a risk-of-bias tool appropriate for heterogeneous qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods study designs. Importantly, the hybrid synthesis framework combining thematic synthesis with bibliometric analysis represents a key strength, as it enables both in-depth examination of educational strategies and mechanisms of gender perspective integration, and meta-research insight into research production patterns, collaboration structures, and equity dimensions within DEI scholarship itself.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, restriction of the search to English and Spanish may have excluded relevant evidence published in other languages. Second, the exclusion of grey literature and institutional reports—while strengthening peer-reviewed rigor—may have limited capture of implementation experiences occurring outside academic publishing channels. Third, substantial heterogeneity in study designs, educational contexts, and outcome measures precluded quantitative meta-analysis and necessitated interpretive synthesis, increasing uncertainty regarding effect magnitude and comparability across interventions.

Risk of bias assessment highlights structural limitations of the evidence base rather than grounds for exclusion. Although all included studies demonstrated explicit integration of a gender perspective, most were rated as having “some concerns,” reflecting the exploratory nature of the field, limited procedural reporting, and insufficient reflexivity in many studies. These methodological constraints require cautious interpretation of individual findings but do not undermine the overall synthesis, as consistent patterns emerged across studies with varying risk profiles. Instead, they underscore the need for more robust evaluation designs, improved transparency, and greater reflexive practice in future research on gender perspective integration.

Finally, limitations related to the structure of scholarly infrastructure must be considered. The evidence base is geographically concentrated in high-income Western settings, and reliance on Web of Science, Scopus, and PubMed—although standard in systematic reviews—may have contributed to this concentration through database indexing bias. These databases predominantly index English-language journals published by major international publishers, favoring Global North scholarship while underrepresenting regional journals from Latin America, Africa, and parts of Asia, even when publishing rigorous research. Consequently, the geographic concentration observed in our bibliometric analysis likely reflects both unequal research production and unequal knowledge visibility. This limitation constrains transferability of synthesized recommendations and reinforces the equity concerns central to RQ3, highlighting that the evidence base informing DEI strategies in health professions education is itself shaped by structural inequities in global knowledge production systems.

Future directions

Research priorities include longitudinal multicentric evaluations examining sustained impact on clinical practice and patient outcomes [5, 71], mixed-methods studies capturing behavioral transformations in clinical contexts and organizational cultures [38, 61], and development of validated intersectional assessment instruments enabling cross-context comparisons [49, 65]. Critical investment is needed in research within low- and middle-income settings where gender inequalities intersect with structural healthcare barriers [37, 39]. Comparative implementation studies should identify facilitators and barriers across diverse educational systems, cultural contexts, and resource environments [15, 61].

Additional priorities include evaluating digital technologies and virtual reality for scalable gender-sensitive education [35, 67, 68], conducting cost-effectiveness analyses informing institutional decisions [14, 59], and implementing community-based participatory research incorporating voices of gender minorities, patients, and affected communities to ensure cultural appropriateness and social responsiveness [3, 50].

Conclusions

This systematic review reveals a paradox in gender-inclusive health professions education: while interventions demonstrate clear benefits for advancing diversity, equity, and inclusion, the field exhibits troubling inequities in both implementation and scholarship. Evidence from 24 studies confirms that gender-sensitive training strengthens clinical competencies for equitable care, yet successful programs remain isolated institutional initiatives rather than sustained systemic reforms. Research production concentrates in European institutions (72.2%), with minimal international collaboration (12.5%) and virtually no Global South representation—raising fundamental questions about whose perspectives define “best practices” in DEI education.

Three mechanisms distinguish successful implementations from fragmented pilots: dedicated institutional champions who sustain commitment beyond individual faculty enthusiasm, formal structural integration through mandatory curricula and assessment systems rather than optional modules, and systematic faculty development emphasizing critical pedagogies. Notably, interventions using intersectional frameworks and power analysis proved more transformative than those limited to biological sex differences, suggesting that depth of engagement with structural inequality, not just content coverage, determines educational impact.

The geographic concentration of evidence poses serious transferability concerns. Can frameworks developed primarily in well-resourced Western academic medical centers address implementation challenges in resource-constrained settings where gender inequalities intersect with structural healthcare barriers? Our findings suggest that advancing gender equity globally requires parallel transformation in two domains: universal adoption of evidence-based curricular strategies alongside fundamental democratization of knowledge production itself. This means not only implementing gender-sensitive education in all institutions but also investing in contextualized scholarship from low- and middle-income settings, ensuring that diverse voices, especially those historically marginalized, meaningfully shape frameworks, generate evidence, and define priorities.

Future research priorities include longitudinal multicentric evaluations examining sustained clinical practice impacts, validated intersectional assessment instruments enabling cross-context comparisons, cost-effectiveness analyses informing resource-constrained implementation, and critically, substantial investment in Global South-led scholarship. Without such parallel commitments to both curricular reform and research equity, health professions education cannot fulfill its stated commitment to preparing providers capable of delivering truly equitable, culturally responsive care across all populations and contexts. The DEI imperative applies not only to what we teach but also to who shapes the evidence base—a reflexive challenge this review both documents and exemplifies.
