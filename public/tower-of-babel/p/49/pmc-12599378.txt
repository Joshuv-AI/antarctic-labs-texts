Fourth ventricular ependymomas are rare posterior fossa neoplasms of neuroepithelial tissue, representing 1%–2% of intracranial lesions, and are more commonly seen in the pediatric population.1,2 These tumors often cause increased intracranial pressure and obstructive hydrocephalus, leading to presenting symptoms of headache, ataxia, and cranial nerve V–XII deficits.3 Surgical treatment typically involves a suboccipital craniotomy or craniectomy to access the fourth ventricle and achieve maximal safe resection while minimizing damage to critical surrounding nervous and vascular structures located along the ventricular walls. Postoperative care in the intensive care unit (ICU) is crucial, given the risk of complications such as cerebellar mutism syndrome, CSF leaks, or persistent hydrocephalus requiring external ventricular drainage.3 Multidisciplinary ICU care, emphasizing close neurological and hemodynamic monitoring, plays an essential role in optimizing outcomes following the challenging resection of these rare posterior fossa tumors, especially when unexpected complications arise.

Ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency (OTCD) is a rare X-linked recessive disorder and is characterized by a full or partial loss of the ornithine transcarbamylase enzyme.4 Age of presentation with OTCD is variable. Patients with little or no residual enzyme activity present in the neonatal period, while more residual enzyme activity correlates with an increased probability of later presentation. Adult presentation is uncommon but well documented. Variability of expression in women is strongly impacted by the random nature of X-inactivation, making all women who carry deleterious variants functionally mosaic in liver expression. In all later-onset presentations, a variety of nongenetic factors impact when, or if, the individual will present with symptomatic hyperammonemia.5 Notably, patients with OTCD are often on protein-restricted diets. Clinically, OTCD can present with neurological, psychiatric, or gastrointestinal symptoms.6 Acutely, hyperammonemia leads to encephalopathy and neuronal death, while chronic hyperammonemia leads to elevated GABAergic signaling followed by reduced neurocognitive functions.7

This case report highlights a unique presentation of late-onset OTCD triggered by posterior fossa craniectomy for the resection of a fourth ventricular ependymoma, making it, to our knowledge, the only case of its kind.

Illustrative Case

A 59-year-old right-handed woman presented with subacute nonspecific neurological symptoms of confusion, dizziness, and gait instability. Her past medical history was significant for bipolar disorder, which had been well controlled with lithium, and stage 2 chronic kidney disease; she reported being a lifelong vegetarian. Her past surgical history was significant for hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and sling urological surgery with mesh. Neurological examination was significant for mild confusion and without cranial nerve or focal deficit. Initial MRI showed a large 4.0 × 3.6 × 3.5–cm mixed solid-cystic mass centered along the inferior aspect of the fourth ventricle with extrusion through the foramina of Luschka and Magendie and into the foramen magnum. The mass was associated with effacement of the inferior fourth ventricle outflow tracts with resultant hydrocephalus (Fig. 1). She was admitted directly to the ICU and underwent placement of an external ventricular drain (EVD).

She underwent posterior fossa craniectomy and C1 laminectomy for intradural resection of the fourth ventricular tumor. The surgery achieved maximal safe resection of tumor without intraoperative complications. Pathology revealed the tumor to be a posterior fossa ependymoma, WHO grade 2. Postoperatively, she was extubated and transitioned to an oral airway and oxygen mask. However, overnight (POD 0), there was an increasing concern of her inability to protect her airway, and the decision to reintubate was made. Given her intubation and sedation status, an orogastric tube was placed and she started on enteral nutrition to improve caloric intake and achieve 60 g of protein per day.

On POD 3, she was noted to have hyperammonemia (234 μg) without signs of hepatic dysfunction, and lactulose was promptly started. On POD 4, postoperative MRI demonstrated interval resection without complication (Fig. 2). Unfortunately, she developed a new leftward gaze deviation with nystagmus and began to have abnormal movements of the left upper extremity, concerning for status epilepticus. Continuous electroencephalography (cEEG) found no seizure activity but diffuse slowing and near-continuous low-amplitude right temporal, parietal, and occipital lateralized periodic discharges (LPDs) at 1 Hz. The patient was noted to have clinical status epilepticus. Serial laboratory studies were significant for recurrent increased hyperammonemia (319 μg) despite continued lactulose treatment. LFTs continued to be within the normal range. Quantitative plasma amino acid analysis showed an elevated glutamine level of 1094 μmol/L (reference range 380–680 μmol/L), an elevated lysine level of 303 μmol/L (reference range 85–230 μmol/L), and a normal arginine level of 85 μmol/L (reference range 35–125 μmol/L), which significantly increased to 169 μmol/L within 5 days. Furthermore, she had an elevated orotic acid level (62.9 μg/mg), which was diagnostic for OTCD. The pediatric nephrology and hepatology teams were consulted to manage the suspected emerging inborn error of metabolism. Based on sex, age, history of bipolar disorder, and elevated orotic acid levels and unresolving hyperammonemia, a diagnosis of OTCD was made. Total parenteral nutrition (TPN) was initiated to better titrate levels of macronutrients in light of OTCD. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was initiated to manage hyperammonemia.

A:Preoperative axial (left) and sagittal (right) T1-weighted postcontrast MR images demonstrating a large midline fourth ventricular lesion. B: Postoperative axial (left) and sagittal (right) MR images demonstrating interval resection without complication and expected postsurgical changes.

Throughout the patient’s clinical course, ammonia levels trended down but remained elevated (range 83–284 μg), never reaching reference levels. Repeated CSF amino acid profiles revealed continued elevation of glutamine levels up to 1565 nmol/ml and elevation of ornithine to 15.9 nmol/ml (reference range 2–9 nmol/ml). Notably, blood citrulline levels trended down over time (from 45 to 8 nmol/ml, reference range 12–55 nmol/ml). The EVD was closely monitored for CSF egress and there was a maximum intracranial pressure of 16 mm Hg. Serial CT imaging and MRI demonstrated progressive hypoxic-ischemic injury and cortical edema, concerning for irreversible neurological injury (Fig. 3). She continued to transition in and out of status epilepticus based on cEEG localizing epileptic activity to the left posterior quadrant with severe diffuse background slowing, LPDs, and clinical correlation of recurrent facial and upper extremity myoclonic movements. Ultimately, the patient was transitioned to comfort care.

Discussion

This case illustrates a complex interplay of diagnosing an underlying late-onset metabolic disorder in the perioperative period. While the immediate focus was the surgical management of the tumor, postoperative complications, including cerebral edema, status epilepticus, and recurrent hyperammonemia, revealed an undiagnosed metabolic condition—OTCD. To better understand the variability and management of late-onset presentations of OTCD, a review of similar cases was performed in hopes of outlining common triggers, diagnostic challenges, and associated outcomes (Fig. 4).

Of the 185 articles found, only 28 were included in this review. Additionally, the cases that met criteria were further examined to determine the potential trigger associated with the presentation of disease. Of the 28 existing cases uncovered in the literature, the most common triggers associated with symptom provocation were steroid administration (n = 5),8–12 pregnancy and childbirth (n = 4),13–16 and recent high levels of protein intake (n = 4).17–20 Administration of antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) (n = 3),21–24 intense exercise (n = 1),25 and medical procedures (n = 1)26 were also indicated as potential triggers. Of the 28 cases reported in the literature, only 13 survived in the absence of significant cognitive deficit.9,14,16–18,20,21,25–30 As described above, OTCD is often revealed in periods of acute stress, but notably, of the 28 cases that passed exclusion, only 2 cases were observed immediately following surgery: one fatal case after coronary bypass surgery, while the other case was uncovered following bariatric surgery and survived.28,31 While these case reports have described late-onset OTCD revealed postoperatively, this is the only case, to our knowledge, that presented following posterior fossa craniectomy.

Observations

In female carriers of OTCD, metabolic decompensation can be triggered by peripartum states, surgery, extensive protein load, and stress. Additionally, drug administration, particularly with medications such as AEDs (namely valproic acid) and certain antineoplastic agents (e.g., asparaginase), can impair urea cycle function, exacerbate the deficiency, and lead to hyperammonemia.21,32 Since our patient case had undergone previous surgeries, had two spontaneous vaginal deliveries, and had no significant family history of metabolic deficiencies, OTCD was not suspected initially. The late diagnosis of her fourth ventricular ependymoma paired with the posterior fossa craniectomy caused observably more physiological stress than the other encounters.

OTCD diagnosis relies on biochemical and genetic testing, with key findings being elevated plasma glutamine, reduced plasma citrulline, and high orotic acid levels, all of which were present in this case.33 Management of OTCD focuses on dietary protein restriction.31 Following reintubation in the neuro ICU, the patient began eternal nutritional care through her placed orogastric tube, which has been found to be less likely to contribute hyperammonemia in patients with OTCD. However, in acute hyperammonemia crises, such as our case, TPN is preferred to carefully titrate the amount of protein and decrease oxidative stress from the urea cycle.34 There remains some discrepancy regarding starting dialysis as hyperammonemia treatment does not suggest dialysis unless ammonia levels do not drop after eight hours of treatment whereas a previous late-onset OTCD case report recommended immediate dialysis.31,33 In this case, CRRT was immediately started upon OTCD diagnosis, which did not have an impact on the overall outcome.

Early in the postoperative recovery, the patient was found to have a gaze deviation, and EEG confirmed clinical status epilepticus. Although epilepsy is not common in asymptomatic OTCD carriers, seizures are common in severe hyperammonemia. Bogdanovic et al. described a similar late-onset case of OTCD, which, after treatment with AEDs, was found to decline into partial clinical status epilepticus.24 In this case, postoperative status epilepticus was managed with Vimpat, Keppra, and Dilantin as well as Versed and ketamine, respectively. Only Dilantin and Depakote have been shown to increase urea cycle stress; these were not administered in our patient’s care. On POD 19, her MRI showed cortical ribboning, suggestive of neuronal damage due to ongoing status epilepticus in the setting of hyperammonemia (Fig. 3).

The persistent elevation in blood ammonia levels required a multidisciplinary care management approach, specifically including pediatric genetic and metabolic disorder expertise. Unfortunately, even with appropriate attempts at decreasing the ammonia levels (e.g., Ammonul, CRRT, transition from enteral nutrition to TPN, and avoidance of medications effecting the urea cycle), the patient’s hyperammonemia led to irreversible neurological damage and the family made the value-based decision to withdraw care.

Lessons

This case highlights the importance of multidisciplinary care and the need for early metabolic workups in the context of altered mental status. Once the clinical status decompensates to an altered level, ammonia levels should be tested for early diagnosis and intervention. Reacting to unexplained hyperammonemia in the postoperative setting may be futile, even with advanced and comprehensive ICU care. Future interventions should emphasize developing protocols for perioperative metabolic screening, such as serum ammonia levels, in patients with unexplained neurological decline; optimizing perioperative nutritional management in metabolic disorders; and leveraging emerging therapies for urea cycle disorders to effectively manage emerging symptoms and improve outcomes.
