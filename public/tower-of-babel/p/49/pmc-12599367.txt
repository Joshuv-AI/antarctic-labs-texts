Paratope plasticity determines anti-HER2 (4B5) antibody specificity
Authors: P Daniel Warren, Terry H Landowski, Mark S Dodson, Adnan Gulzar, John D Palting, Erika Walker, Penny Towne, Margaret H Smith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe PATHWAY anti-HER2/neu (4B5) Rabbit Monoclonal Primary Antibody immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay is used to identify patients eligible for anti-HER2 targeted therapy. Previous work has demonstrated that the 4B5 antibody can react with the related family member HER4, and the nuclear protein ZSCAN18, under experimental conditions. Here, we used surface plasmon resonance, automated capillary electrophoresis (ACE), and IHC to fully define the 4B5 epitope and binding characteristics. The key amino acids that contribute to antibody recognition were identified and characterized with the limits of detection determined for HER4 and ZSCAN18. Additionally, the protein target responsible for cytoplasmic and nuclear reactivity observed by 4B5 in non-breast tissue types, that is not attributable to either HER2, HER4, or ZSCAN18, was identified. By means of protein purification, mass spectrometry, ACE analysis and IHC, 4B5 was found to react with the cytosolic aldo-keto reductase family 1 members B1, B10 and B15 (AKR1B1, AKR1B10, AKR1B15). The binding characteristics and limits of detection for these off-target species were defined. In-silico docking analysis of computationally folded 4B5 paratope regions identified key differences in the 4B5 paratope interaction between a peptide containing the HER2 epitope versus that of a partially homologous peptide sequence common to the AKR1 family. Flexibility of the complementarity-determining region loops in 4B5 imparts paratope plasticity that enables interaction with AKR1B family proteins. This antibody off-target recognition can result in cytoplasmic and nuclear IHC staining.KEYWORDS: HER2 biomarker, epitope mapping, surface plasmon resonance, immunohistochemistry, molecular modeling

Introduction

Amplification or overexpression of the human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) occurs in approximately 20–30% of breast cancer patients and has long been a highly effective therapeutic target for patients with aggressive disease (reviewed in1). HER2 is a member of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) family that has no known ligand. Rather, ligand-independent HER2 homo-dimerization or hetero-dimerization with other members of the EGFR family drives tumor proliferation, invasion, and survival. Over 30 years ago, preclinical studies demonstrated a clear correlation between the level of HER2 overexpression and growth inhibitory activity of an anti-HER2 monoclonal antibody.2,3 This led to the development of trastuzumab (Herceptin), which was the first humanized monoclonal antibody approved to treat breast carcinoma.4,5 Trastuzumab was also the first therapeutic agent to implement a biomarker-enriched clinical trial design, in which a semi-quantitative immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay identified patients with HER2 overexpression as candidates for enrollment.6,7 This simultaneous approval of trastuzumab with a companion diagnostic HER2 IHC assay in 1998 established what is now standard practice for targeted therapies, the drug-diagnostic co-development model in which a predictive biomarker is developed in parallel to the drug for simultaneous approval as a selective companion diagnostic (CDx).8

To date the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved nine IHC companion diagnostic assays for HER2 protein expression that utilize four different antibody clones (https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/in-vitro-diagnostics/list-cleared-or-approved-companion-diagnostic-devices-in-vitro-and-imaging-tools). The original clinical trial assay (CTA) developed for trastuzumab studies utilized two different mouse monoclonal antibodies, the parent murine antibody to trastuzumab, clone 4D5, and another anti-HER2 clone, CB11 as well as a rabbit polyclonal antibody, A0485. This CTA was considered too complex for routine diagnostic laboratory use, driving the development of polyclonal A085, HercepTest™, as the initial companion diagnostic in 1998, followed by the approval of the optimized mouse monoclonal CB11 as the VENTANA PATHWAY HER2/neu™ in 2000. Advances in rabbit monoclonal antibody technology enabled the development of a new rabbit monoclonal antibody, clone 4B5, which replaced the CB11 clone in the VENTANA PATHWAY assay in 2007. Using fluorescence in situ hybridization as the reference standard, 4B5 demonstrated increased sensitivity, specificity, and inter-laboratory reproducibility for the detection of HER2 overexpression in breast cancer compared to CB11,9 and 4B5 remains among the most widely used and cited assays for HER2 in clinical use.

The original co-development of the HER2 companion diagnostic assay with trastuzumab over 20 years ago was designed to identify breast cancer patients with strong membrane expression of HER2 that would respond to the therapeutic antibody. More recently, therapeutic modalities have evolved to include antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) that are effective in patients with lower HER2 expression, and have expanded to indications beyond breast cancer.10 During the recent development of the CDx assay in additional tumor types, published work and unpublished observations have identified cytoplasmic and nuclear staining patterns that are incompatible with a transmembrane receptor.11,12 While the FDA-approved PATHWAY anti-HER2/new (4B5) Rabbit Monoclonal Primary Antibody Interpretation Guide states that only membranous staining should be considered for interpretation, off-target staining may become relevant to some new clinical applications. Figure 1 demonstrates a subset of representative images of cholangiocarcinoma, endometrial, urothelial, non-small cell lung (NSCLC), gastroesophageal, and gallbladder carcinomas stained with 4B5 that exhibit cytoplasmic and nuclear staining patterns. The cytoplasmic and nuclear signals appear to be independent of membrane HER2 expression and are often widely distributed across both tumor and normal tissues.

(a) Off-target staining by 4B5. HER2 (4B5) demonstrates cytoplasmic and nuclear staining by IHC in addition to membrane localized HER2 protein in various tissue types. All tissues were stained with 4B5 according to the pathway anti-HER2/neu (4B5) instructions for breast cancer.

Previous work has demonstrated that the 4B5 assay can cross react with another EGFR family member, HER4.13,14 More recently, others have reported that 4B5 can cross react with a nuclear protein, ZSCAN18.15 In the current study, we determined that cytoplasmic and nuclear staining by 4B5 in tissue samples resulted from binding to a family of proteins, aldo-keto reductase family 1 members B1, B10 and B15 (AKR1B1, AKR1B10, AKR1B15). Furthermore, we defined the binding characteristics and limits of detection for HER4, ZSCAN18, and the AKR1B family of proteins by the 4B5 assay.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

Cell lines used in the study are described in Supplemental Table S1. All cell lines were authenticated by the vendor, were maintained in a humidified incubator (37°C, 5% CO2 and 95% air) in growth media recommended by the vendor, tested for mycoplasma, and used at less than 10 passages upon receipt from the vendor. Criteria for inclusion of the cell line in the study was based on IHC staining results using the VENTANA HER2 rabbit (4B5) assay compared to HER2 rabbit (CAL27). HEK-293 cells engineered to express HER1, HER2, HER3 or HER4 were previously described.14

HER2, cat # H00002064-P01; HER4, cat # H00002066-P01, and ZSCAN18, cat # H00065982-P02 were from Abnova (Taipei City, Taiwan). AKR1B1, cat # ab275926 was from Abcam. Peptides

Peptides were synthesized by Anaspec, (Freemont, CA). The lyophilized peptides were dissolved in dH2O, adjusted to a concentration of 2 mM, and stored at −80°C until use. A table listing the peptides used in this study and their sequences is provided in Supplemental Table S2.

Immunohistochemistry

De-identified human formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue specimens were obtained from a commercial vendor with appropriate consent. High-density cell blocks from cells grown in tissue culture were prepared by immobilization and formalin fixation and paraffin embedding in LMP agarose. Four micron sections were cut from tissue and cell blocks and stained according to the package insert or manufacturer’s recommendations. IHC staining was done on the BenchMark ULTRA staining platform using either ultraView or OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit, Hematoxylin II, Bluing Reagent, ULTRA CC1, ULTRA LCS, EZ Prep, Reaction Buffer and antibody dilution solutions, all from Roche Tissue Diagnostics. Fully automated multiplex detection using yellow (dabsyl) and purple (TAMRA) chromogens was performed with the commercially available DISCOVERY RUO Yellow kit (Cat. No. 760–239), and DISCOVERY RUO Purple kit (Cat. No. 760–229), respectively. These kits also include the requisite enzyme-antibody conjugates. Serial tissue sections were stained with each marker and chromogen individually, and in a sequential multiplex assay as previously described.16,17 Details of the staining protocols are included in Supplemental Table S3.

Surface plasmon resonance epitope binding kinetics assays

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) epitope-binding analysis was done as previously described.16 Briefly, SPR kinetic analyses of peptide binding of both clones was performed on a Biacore T200 using Series S CM5 sensor chips (Cytiva). The rabbit antibody capture surface was prepared with 50 ng/mL goat anti-species IgG Fc in 10 mM Acetate pH 5. Surface conditioning was performed with 50 nM of the species specific whole-molecule IgG for 120 seconds at 75 µl/min for five cycles. Rabbit surface regeneration was accomplished using a four step process: 15 seconds of 10 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.1% Tween-20 at 20 µL/min, 20 seconds of glycine pH 1.5 at 30 µL/min, and two injections of 30 seconds each with glycine pH 1.7 at 30 µL/min. Kinetic measurements were performed at 25°C in a solution of 10 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.05% Tween. Antibody solution at 10 nM was captured for 120 seconds at 20 µl/min, and peptide interactions, visualized as binding curves, were measured for 150 seconds of association and 300 seconds of dissociation at 100 µL/min. Alanine substitution peptides were tested, at 10 and 20 nM. Motif matching peptide candidates were tested at 100 and 200 nM. Kinetic coefficients were calculated with Biacore T200 Evaluation software using the 1:1 Langmuir binding model with global fit parameters and the bulk refractive index value held constant at zero. Curve fitting was completed with least-squares regression using the Levenberg-Marquardt regression model.

Cell fractionation and Western blot analysis

Information regarding the cell lines used for protein isolation is provided in Supplemental Table S1. Cells were harvested using non-proteolytic detachment media. For whole-cell lysates, 1x107 cells were washed with cold Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) followed by 1x protease and phosphatase inhibitor solution in cold DPBS, lysed in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with DNase and a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. Debris was removed by centrifugation at 13500xg at 4°C for 5 minutes and the cleared lysate was stored at 4°C. Subcellular fractionation was done using the Qproteome Cell Compartment kit (Qiagen) scaled for 1-5x107 cells. Fractions were verified using control antibodies for each subcellular compartment (Cytoplasmic: GAPDH; Membrane: EGFR; Nuclear: Histone; Cytoskeletal: panCK). Western blot analysis was done using standard protocols as previously described.16 Briefly, bicinchoninic acid protein assay (BCA, ThermoFisher) was used to quantitate whole-cell lysates and subcellular fractions. Equal amounts of protein (per fraction) were diluted in the denaturing LDS sample, denatured at 60°C for 5 min, cooled and separated by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis on a 4–12% polyacrylamide gradient gel. Proteins were transferred to a 0.45 µm PVDF membrane, probed with the specified antibody and signal developed using the WesternBreeze chromogenic detection kit (ThermoFisher).

Automated capillary electrophoresis assays

Automated capillary electrophoresis (ACE) chemiluminescent and total protein assays were performed with the JESS anti-rabbit and anti-mouse Detection Modules, RePlex™ Module, 12–230 kDa Separation Module, and the JESS Simple Western™ instrument (ProteinSimple®, Bio-Techne, Minneapolis, MN, USA) as previously described.16 Assays comparing the relative levels of HER2, HER4, and ZSCAN18 protein expression in cell lines were performed with lysates diluted into the 0.1X sample buffer reagent and reconstituted 5X Fluorescent Mix (FM) to yield final lysate dilutions of 1:4. Sample loading was normalized using total protein Replex. For recombinant protein-binding titration assays, proteins were adjusted to their specified concentrations by serial dilution in 0.1X sample buffer reagent containing 1X FM. For the ACE analysis of each ion exchange (IEX) samples, individual column fractions were mixed with 0.1X sample buffer reagent and reconstituted 5X FM. For all assays, samples were heat denatured for 5 min followed by cooling on ice. Denatured samples and antibody solutions were loaded onto a separation module plate prefilled with separation matrix and buffers and processed using standard assay conditions: 25 min separation at 375 V followed by 30 min primary antibody and 30 min secondary antibody incubation. Images were generated with high dynamic range selection and peak areas were quantitated with the Compass for SW software Version 6.2.0 (ProteinSimple®). The EC50 values for recombinant protein binding to 4B5 were determined using a non-linear fit of the log concentration of protein versus the luminescent peak area at each protein concentration using the four parameter model of Prism GraphPad Prism with the bottom value constrained to zero.

Protein purification

Protein purification was done by immunoprecipitation or ion exchange (IEX) chromatography followed by acrylamide gel extraction. For immunoprecipitation, purified 4B5 antibody was coupled to Protein G Sepharose FastFlow beads (MilliporeSigma) (1 µg antibody per 20 µl of Protein G Sepharose). Samples prepared from the cytosolic or membrane lysate fractions of cell lines containing 200 ug of total protein were diluted in a TBE buffer with 0.1% Tween-20, pH 8.55 and heated to 95°C for 30 minutes, cooled on ice and combined with 4B5-Sepharose and rotated overnight at 4°C. Samples were washed 3x with IP Lysis buffer (Pierce), and following the final wash, beads were resuspended in 4x LDS sample buffer, heated to 95°C for 5 minutes and centrifuged 2 min at 10000xg. All the samples were separated on duplicate gels for (1) immunoblot with 4B5 and (2) Coomassie staining (GelCode Blue, ThermoFisher). Coomassie stained bands corresponding to the 4B5 precipitated proteins were cut from the gel and submitted for mass spectrometry (MS) sequence analysis.

IEX was completed on an Äkta Pure fast purification liquid chromatography (LC) system running the Unicorn software 7.3.0. Anion exchange utilized a Mono Q™ 5/50 GL column with starting buffer (buffer A) of 20 mM Tris (pH 8.5) and an elution buffer (buffer B) of 20 mM Tris and 1 M NaCl (pH8.5). For anion exchange, cellular cytosol fractions were concentrated with 30 kDa cutoff filters and buffer exchanged into the buffer A and loaded into a 1 mL sample loop. Using buffer A as the running medium, the samples were loaded onto a buffer A equilibrated column at 0.5 mL/min and eluted at 1 mL/min while being monitored at 280 nm with an in-line UV spectrometer. The elution conditions were created by mixing buffers A and B and initially started at 1% buffer B, before a step elution to 5% buffer B for four column volumes (CV), before a final gradient elution to 30% buffer B for 5 CVs. Ultimately, 0.50 mL fractions were collected into a multi-well plate, and assayed for the presence of a 4B5 reactive species using ACE. Positive fractions were then further purified by cation exchange with a Mono S™ 5/50 GL column with a starting buffer (buffer A) of 20 mM MES (pH 5.5) and an elution buffer (buffer B) of 20 mM MES and 1 M NaCl (pH5.5). For cation exchange, 4B5-positive fractions were concentrated with 30 kDa cutoff filters and buffer exchanged into the buffer A and loaded into a 1 mL sample loop. Using buffer A as the running medium, the samples were loaded onto a buffer A equilibrated column at 0.5 mL/min and eluted at 1 mL/min while being monitored at 280 nm with an in-line UV spectrometer. The elution conditions were created by mixing cation buffers A and B and initially started at 5% buffer B, before a step elution to 20% buffer B for three CVs, followed by a gradient elution to 30% buffer B for 2 CVs, before a final gradient elution to 60% buffer B for 2 CVs. Ultimately, 0.50 mL fractions were collected into a well plate and tested by ACE for the 4B5 reactive protein. Positive fractions were pooled, concentrated and separated by SDS-PAGE for parallel immunoblot and Coomassie stained gel extraction. Protein bands corresponding to the 4B5 reactive protein were cut from the Coomassie stained gel for MS sequencing.

Mass spectrometry

Protein bands of interest were excised from SDS-PAGE after identification with Coumassie staining. The gel slices were de-stained, dehydrated with acetonitrile, and then swollen in the presence of 100 ng of trypsin with 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate in water to digest the trapped protein. The reaction was quenched with formic acid and dried by a vacuum concentrator in preparation for MS analysis. Protein identification by LC-tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) analysis was carried out using a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer equipped with an Easy Spray nanoESI source. Solvent A consisted of dH2O and 0.1% formic acid while Solvent B consisted of 90% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. Peptides were eluted from an Acclaim PepMap 100 trap column (75 µm ID x 2 cm) onto an Acclaim PepMap RSLC analytical column (75 µm ID × 25 cm) using an increasing gradient of Solvent B into Solvent A. The flow solvent gradient increased Solvent B from 3% to 38% over 35 min, then 38–74% over 10 min and 74–100% over 5 min, followed by a hold of 100% for 10 min, and finally a return to 3% for 10 min. Flow rates were 300 nL/min using a Thermo Scientific™ EASY-nLC™ 1200 System. Data-dependent scanning was performed by the Xcalibur v4.0.27.19 software18 using a survey scan at 70,000 resolution, scanning mass/charge (m/z) 360–1600, automatic gain control (AGC) target of 1x105 and a maximum injection time (IT) of 65 msec, followed by higHER-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) MS/MS at 27 normalized collision energy (nce), of the 10 most intense ions at a 17,500 resolution, an isolation width of 1.5 m/z, an AGC of 1x105, and a maximum IT of 65 msec. Dynamic exclusion was set to place any selected m/z on an exclusion list for 20 secs after a single MS/MS. Ions of charge state +1, 7, 8, >8 and unassigned were excluded from MS/MS, as were isotopes. All MS/MS spectra were searched using Thermo Proteome Discoverer v 2.4 considering fully tryptic peptides with up to two missed cleavage sites. Variable modifications considered during the search included methionine oxidation (15.995 Da), and cysteine carbamidomethylation (57.021 Da). Proteins were identified at 99% confidence with XCorr score cutoffs19 as determined by a reversed database search. The protein and peptide identification results were also visualized with Scaffold Q+S v 5.0.1 (Proteome Software Inc.), a program that relies on various search engine results (e.g., Sequest, X!Tandem, MASCOT) and which uses Bayesian statistics to reliably identify more spectra.20 Protein identifications were accepted that passed a minimum of two peptides identified at 0.1% peptide False Discovery Rate and 90–99.9% protein confidence. Equipment, columns, and control software are from Thermo Fisher Scientific, unless otherwise noted.

Molecular modeling

To aid the interpretation of the experimental data, docking studies were performed and the binding free energies of the HER2 and AKR1B10 peptides to the 4B5 antibody were analyzed. The Fv regions of 4B5 were predicted using ImmuneBuilder.21 In the case of HER2, the complete structure was initially predicted using RosettaFold.22 The C-terminal peptide residues 1239–1255 were extracted for further investigation. All atom refinement of the starting HER2 peptide structure around the local conformational space was performed by the Relax module of Rosetta. In addition, the lowest energy conformations of the sidechains were sampled by using the Repack package of Rosetta. The peptide sequence spanning residues 216–232 of AKR1B10 was taken from the crystal structure, PDB ID: [1ZUA].23 The flexible docking tool, Snugdock,24 was then used to dock each peptide to the 4B5 antibody. A total of 5,000 docking samples were generated to obtain reliable docking poses. The most favorable docking structures were chosen based on optimal interface versus total energy scores. After identifying the best poses and energy minimizations, all-atom explicit-solvent molecular dynamics (MD) simulations spanning 500 ns lengths were performed using the GROMACS software.25 The final structures from MD simulations were then used to calculate the change in binding free energies (ΔΔG). Direct comparison with experimental results was achieved with SAAMBE-3D26 by computational alanine substitution into the HER2 and AKR1B10 peptides.

Results

Epitope mapping identifies the 4B5 critical binding motif

To gain insight into the sensitivity and specificity of the 4B5 antibody binding, fine epitope mapping was done using SPR analysis. A synthetic peptide encompassing the 4B5 target, corresponding to the 17 C-terminal amino acids of the HER2 protein (UniProt canonical sequence P04626-1, aa1239-1255), was used to establish a baseline 4B5-wild type peptide affinity of 3.3 nM (Figure 2a). To identify the specific epitope and relative contribution of each amino acid residue, systematic alanine scanning was done across the entire 17-mer peptide where each residue was replaced with alanine to generate 17 unique peptides (Supplemental Table S2). Binding kinetics of each alanine substituted peptide were determined at two selected peptide concentrations. Representative SPR response plots, shown in Figure 2, demonstrate the effects of three such alanine substitutions compared to the wild-type peptide, designated A5, which has a naturally occurring alanine at position A1243. Alanine substitution at P1241, designated A3, reduces the calculated affinity by 20-fold due to a reduced rate of association compared to the wild type (Figure 2b). This reduced on-rate additionally reduces the maximum signal (Response Units, RU) as seen by a lack of signal saturation during the binding event, indicating reduced formation of the 4B5-peptide complex. Similarly, alanine substitution at Y1248, shown in Figure 2c, results in a 10-fold reduction of the calculated affinity due to an increase in the rate of dissociation. The increased off-rate also negatively affects the maximum RU signal, suggesting insufficient stability of the antibody-peptide complex to reach signal saturation. Alanine substitution at P1246, peptide A8, results in no interaction, indicating the proline at P1246 is required for antibody binding (Figure 2d). The table in Figure 2 summarizes the contribution of the 17 residues that were identified as “critical” or “contributory.” The data show that the core epitope sequence required for 4B5 antibody recognition is 1244ENPEYL1249. This is a refinement of the epitope previously reported by Schrohl et al. using ELISA, which does not provide kinetic data.13 Amino acids E1244, N1245, P1246 and L1249 are critical for binding; therefore, the minimum critical-binding motif for the 4B5 clone binding to HER2 is defined as “E-N-P-X-X-L” where X is considered non-critical. Additionally, residues surrounding the core sequence P1241, T1242, Y1248, G1250, and D1252 affect the rate of association and stability of the complex. Complete kinetic constants determined for all alanine substitution peptides are provided in Supplemental Table S2.

Cross reactivity of 4B5 with the related protein HER4 requires high concentrations of protein

A sequence similarity search using the 4B5 motif to query the UniProt human protein database computationally identified potential interactions with family members HER4 at 1280ENPEYL1285, two sites in HER1 (EGFR) at 1134GNPEYL1139, and 1193ENAEYL1198, as well as a second site in HER2 at 1191VENPEYL1197. To determine the binding kinetics of 4B5 with similar HER family members, 17-mer peptides were synthesized corresponding to the sequence of each protein surrounding and including the minimum required 4B5 epitope (Supplemental Table S4). SPR response curves shown in Figure 3a demonstrate that the HER4 peptide epitope binds to the 4B5 antibody with an on-rate similar to HER2, but rapidly dissociates, resulting in a lower affinity compared to the 4B5 interaction with the HER2 peptide. No reactivity was seen with the HER1 derived peptides, while the alternate HER2 site showed a weak interaction. These data support the importance of the context of the epitope within the full protein, and suggest that not only are there required amino acids, but some residues may also be exclusionary due to structural disruption.

To determine the relative binding of 4B5 to HER2 vs. HER4, recombinant HER2 and HER4 proteins were titrated across a broad range of concentrations and probed with a constant concentration of 4B5 antibody. Binding curves were constructed using the chemiluminescence signal peak area derived from the ACE analysis. As shown in Figure 3b, ACE analysis demonstrated antibody binding reaches 50% saturation at 10.7 nM of HER2 compared to 32 nM of HER4, indicating that the 4B5 antibody limit of detection for HER4 is approximately 3-fold greater than HER2. These data are concordant with the SPR data, which shows a specific binding event, but a more rapid dissociation of the HER4 peptide compared to HER2. Although the SPR binding interaction does not identify limits of concentration, the increased off-rate of the 4B5-HER4 peptide indicates that the complex stability is lower than the 4B5-HER2 peptide complex. To relate the concentration of recombinant proteins to biological levels of expression, ACE analyses were used to test the 4B5 antibody against whole-cell lysate from the colorectal cancer cell-line NCI-H716 (Figure 3c). This cell line expresses relatively high levels of HER4 as shown by the HER4 antibody E200, but no 4B5 signal was detected in equivalently loaded capillaries.

Previously published work has shown that 4B5 can recognize HER4 in overexpression systems,13 although in breast cancer tissue specimens the limits of detection were found to be below levels that would interfere with clinical interpretation of the HER2 assay. In addition, the cytoplasmic localization of the HER4 is readily differentiated from the membrane localization of HER2.14 A panel of HEK-293 cells transfected with HER family expression constructs was used to establish specificity of 4B5 and an anti-HER2 comparator, CAL27 by IHC (Supplemental Figure S1). IHC staining of the HER family overexpression cell lines shows that 4B5 strongly reacts with HER2, and can detect HER4 in this overexpression system. CAL27 reacts only with the HER2 transfected cell line and does not recognize other HER family proteins even when expressed at high levels. Similarly, the anti-HER4 antibody, E200 recognizes only HER4 expressing cells while the anti-HER1/EGFR antibody 5B7 and the anti-HER3 antibody SP438 recognize endogenous as well as overexpressed HER1 and HER3, respectively. As shown in Figure 3d, 4B5 does not show any staining of endogenous HER4 expression in the cell line NCI-H716, which corroborates the ACE analysis demonstrating the level of HER4 is below the limits of detection by 4B5 in these cells. CAL27 shows no reactivity with HER4. Tissue from malignancies other than breast cancer were selected based on the presence of cytoplasmic or nuclear staining with 4B5, and serial sections were stained with 4B5, CAL27, and E200. In both the ovarian and gallbladder carcinomas, a distinct staining pattern of the strong cytoplasmic HER4 signal does not appear to co-localize with the off-target staining by 4B5. To determine if the overlapping localization of HER4 in the membrane interferes with HER2 identification, chromogenic multiplex IHC staining was used to delineate the HER2 signal in the context of HER4 expression. In Figure 3e, HER2, stained with 4B5 or CAL27 is shown in purple, and HER4 is shown in yellow. Because the chromogenic dyes are covalently deposited on the tissue, distinct structures display as the primary dye color, either purple or yellow, while co-localization of two antibody signals results in a unique color that is the blending of the two dyes.17 Multiplex staining of CAL27 and E200 appears as two distinct colors with the purple CAL27 staining the membrane HER2 and the E200 staining primarily cytoplasmic with some weak membrane. The multiplex image with 4B5 in purple and E200 also demonstrates distinct colors, supporting the conclusion that 4B5 is does not bind to endogenous HER4.

4B5 does not react with the nuclear protein ZSCAN18 at physiological levels of protein

Studies by other investigators have suggested that nuclear staining by 4B5 may be due to cross reactivity with the transcription factor ZSCAN18 (22). To examine the interaction of 4B5 with ZSCAN18, a 17-mer peptide including and surrounding a sequence of ZSCAN18 reported as a 4B5 reactive epitope was examined by SPR analysis (Figure 4a). This peptide, LKSFPEDPQHLGEWGHL, includes three of the four required amino acids from the 4B5 motif (EXPXXL). The interaction demonstrates a concentration dependent signal as demonstrated by the 10-fold increase in peptide concentration that was required to generate a signal equivalent to that obtained with the HER2 peptide. The rapid on- and off-rate of the interaction prevents the calculation of kinetic coefficients and more resembles a bulk shift in the signal. Degrading signal during the binding cycle of the SPR assay indicates that the 4B5-ZSCAN18 peptide complex stability is extremely low, and the complex is actively degraded during the experiment. To determine the relative sensitivity of 4B5 to HER2 vs ZSCAN18, recombinant HER2 and recombinant ZSCAN18 protein were titrated across a broad range and probed with a constant concentration of 4B5 antibody using ACE assay. Cytoplasmic and nuclear reactivity of 4B5 in a variety of carcinoma tissue

More recently, IHC studies using the 4B5 antibody on a broad range of carcinoma tissue has demonstrated off-target staining in the nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments of tumor cells that is not attributable to HER4 or ZSCAN18, nor is it recognized by the comparator antibody CAL27 (data not shown). Cytoplasmic and nuclear staining by 4B5 has been identified in a broad range of tissue types including NSCLC, biliary tract carcinoma, gastric esophageal junction, pancreatic, urothelial, ovarian, and endometrial carcinomas. To identify the off-target species, IHC was used to select a panel of cell lines representing a variety of tissue types with strong cytoplasmic or nuclear staining and low or no visible HER2 membrane staining (Supplemental Table S1). Cells were fractionated into cytoplasmic, membrane, nuclear and insoluble compartments and assayed by Western blot. As shown in Figure 5a, subcellular fractionation and Western blot analysis identified the 185 kDa HER2 and substantial fragmented protein products in the HER2-overexpressing gastric cancer cell line NCI-N87, including a product at approximately 42–45 kDa, all of which are recognized by both 4B5 and CAL27. In contrast, cell lines with cytoplasmic and nuclear staining with 4B5 express a prominent band at approximately 37 kDa that is not recognized by the comparator antibody CAL27. The NSCLC cell line A549 is representative of nine cell lines (described in Supplemental Table S1) that were fractionated and examined by Western blot, all of which display cytoplasmic or nuclear staining with 4B5 by IHC and express the cytoplasmic 37 kDa protein recognized by 4B5 (data not shown).

Cross reactivity of 4B5 with the AKR1B protein is concentration dependent

Alignment of the 4B5 epitope motif with the AKR1B family sequences identified a potential site of interaction, which was synthesized as a 17-mer synthetic peptide. This sequence, PWAKPEDPSLLEDPKIK, contains three of the required four critical amino acids, EXPXXL, and is identical in all three AKR1B family members, but is not conserved in the related AKR1C1 protein. SPR analysis demonstrated a dose-dependent interaction that was not related to solvent response or nonspecific binding (Figure 6a). The interaction demonstrates a concentration-dependent signal as demonstrated by the 10-fold increase in peptide concentration that were required to generate a signal equivalent to the HER2 peptide interaction. The graph indicates a strong dependency on concentration of peptide and a lack of stability of the peptide-antibody complex as the signal degrades during the binding phase. Kinetics cannot be accurately determined due to the speed of the on-rate. This result is identical to the ZSCAN18 interaction shown in Figure 4c. The on- and off-rate of the interaction prevents the calculation of kinetic coefficients and more resembles a bulk shift in the signal. Molecular modeling indicates the creation of two hydrophobic pockets

Molecular modeling and subsequent MD simulations were done to identify the main amino acids of the 4B5 antibody and respective ligands that are involved in contact formation. Following the computation of the 5000 docking samples (Supplemental Figure S3), the optimal structures were identified and are shown in Figure 8. The calculations show that the 1244ENPEYL1249 motif of the HER2 peptide is important for recognition by the 4B5 antibody (Figure 8, top). For example, E1244 and N1245 of the HER2 peptide participate in a salt bridge with R53 and forms a polar contact with K56 of 4B5 the complementarity-determining region (CDR) HL2 domain, respectively. P1246 of the HER2 peptide is buried within the structure, playing an important role in preserving the structural motif necessary for recognition by 4B5, and contributing to a hydrophobic core with I50, V100, and I94 of CDRHL2, CDRHL3, and CDRLL3, respectively. A second hydrophobic core is constituted by Y1248 and L1249 of HER2 and Y59 of 4B5, which is near the CDRHL2 domain. In the case of AKR1B10 (Figure 8, bottom), the 224EDPSLL229 motif, precisely the same hydrophobic core P-I-V-I core is observed, as seen in the case of the HER2-4B5 structure. Likewise, a second hydrophobic core, consisting of L228 and L229 of AKR1B10 with Y100 from CDRHL3, is present in the AKR1B10-4B5 complex. The AKR1B10-4B5 complex is further buttressed by polar contacts formed between E224 and K56 of CDRHL2, and D225 and S95 CDRLL2. In addition, residues D219/R220/W222 of AKR1B10 contribute significantly to the total energy of the complex interaction. It should be noted that the hydrophobic cores are conserved between the 4B5 complexes with the HER2 and AKR1B10 peptides, namely the first core, P-I-V-I, and the second hydrophobic core consisting of either Y-L-Y (HER2) or Y-L-L (AKR1B10). Both of these core motifs are important in 4B5 antibody recognition, and therefore relevant to the interaction mechanism of either HER2 or AKR1B proteins.

Discussion

The success of targeted cancer therapeutic agents is highly dependent on the availability of appropriate diagnostic tools. Retrospective analysis has suggested that the initial FDA approval of trastuzumab would have required more than 8000 enrollees to demonstrate significant benefit in an open trial without patient selection.28 The oncogenic activity of HER2 is driven by overexpression, which was the original mechanism targeted by trastuzumab and the basis for patient selection by IHC. With the addition of a cytotoxic payload and cleavable linker, the mechanism of action of the HER2-targeted ADCs is thought to be selective delivery of the cytotoxic agent, requiring only low levels of receptor expression for response to therapy. As HER2-targeted therapies have evolved and expanded to conditions beyond HER2-positive breast cancer, appropriate fit-for-purpose assay development should be considered.29,30

Antibody specificity can be defined as a relative trait that is context dependent.31,32 The functional specificity of an antibody, either as a therapeutic agent or in an IHC assay, is driven by preferential interaction with one target over other potential targets within a complex biological milieu. Although it is often presumed that high-affinity binding confers specificity, in a biological context the relevant interaction is driven by local protein concentration, as well as physiochemical characteristics. Recent reviews have classified antibody cross reactivity as two forms of polyspecificity.33 One form is defined as kinetic binding to a structurally similar epitope, or molecular mimicry. A second form is binding through CDR plasticity, where the antibody can expose buried surface area, and the binding interface can expose alternative paratopes. These traits are highly dependent on both the antigen and the physiochemical context.34,35 The results from this study show that in addition to high-affinity binding to its target protein, HER2, the HER4 motif is a match for four of the four critical residues and is considered a molecular mimic. Because of the extremely low affinity and dependence on local protein concentration, 4B5 recognition of ZSCAN18 and the AKR1B family is proposed to be due to CDR plasticity.

SPR kinetic analysis and alanine scanning demonstrate that 4B5 binds to the HER2 peptide immunogen with a calculated affinity (KD) in the low nanomolar range, which is generally considered to be a “good binder.” Identification of the critical peptide motif defines the interaction of the paratope with the HER2 epitope surface and is the interface of the binding complex. Molecular engineering studies have shown that epitopes including tyrosine are favorable for both affinity and specificity.36 In the HER2 epitope, Y1248 contributes to the complex stability with 4B5. Experiments including the full 17-mer immunogen provide evidence for the important contribution of the sequence surrounding the core motif in complex formation and stability. Molecular modeling suggests that the C-terminal of the HER2 protein is unstructured, but the identified critical proline and the downstream tyrosine provide some local structure that may not be conserved when the core motif is presented in the context of the HER4 protein. Proline (P1241), threonine (T1242), and glycine (D1252) residues outside of the core motif additionally contribute to 4B5 binding, and these residues are not present in the HER4 sequence. The 4B5 interaction with HER4 is considered molecular mimicry binding, as the SPR data demonstrates a kinetic binding event with the synthetic peptide that encompasses the core motif. Binding kinetics show that 4B5 has an altered-binding profile to the HER4 peptide sequence with a faster off-rate, indicating a less stable complex. ACE analysis using recombinant proteins identified the limits of detection and showed that, in a cell-free system, 4B5 binding to HER4 requires approximately three times more protein compared to HER2.

Expression of HER4 is highly variable across tissue types with respect to both level of expression and subcellular localization.37 Although the canonical HER4 protein is a transmembrane receptor, four isoforms have been described, two of which can be cleaved by limited intracellular proteolysis. Cleaved fragments containing the cytoplasmic epitope that would be recognized by 4B5 can remain in the membrane, aggregate in the cytoplasm or translocate to the nucleus. HER4 expression in the context of HER2-targeted therapy may be relevant, as HER4 has been identified as a dimerization partner with HER2. In breast cancer, the major HER4 isoforms are cytosolic, and increased expression has been associated with tumor differentiation and a more favorable prognosis.38 An earlier study by Jay et al.14 found no evidence of cross-reactivity by 4B5 with biologically relevant levels of HER4 in the clinical IHC analysis of breast carcinoma cases. The current study identifies strong HER4 expression in the cytoplasm of some tissues, including gallbladder, ovarian, and esophageal carcinomas, that did not appear to co-localize with the 4B5 signal. Some tissue types examined, particularly endometrial carcinomas, demonstrate moderate to strong HER4 staining localized to the basolateral membranes. Because 4B5 reactivity with HER4 requires more than three-fold greater concentrations of protein compared to HER2, interference with traditional HER2 classification is unlikely.

SPR analysis of peptides derived from the ZSCAN18 and AKR1B proteins show no kinetic-binding event despite reactivity of 4B5 with recombinant proteins in ACE assays. Peptide concentrations 10-fold higher than standard assays provide discernable, but uninterpretable signal. This would suggest that ZSCAN18 and AKR1B interactions are examples of CDR plasticity, where antibody binding is highly dependent on proximity of the antibody to a concentration that promotes interaction. Previous work by Qi et al. reported 4B5 reactivity with a nuclear protein, ZSCAN18, in a high-density protein microarray.15 ZSCAN18 (Zinc finger and SCAN containing protein 18) is a DNA binding transcription factor localized entirely to the nucleus. The authors identified a sequence in ZSCAN18 with three of the four critical residues in the 4B5 epitope. They further report 4B5 binding to the peptide sequence by ELISA, as well as massively overexpressed cell lysates in a Western blot assay. In the current study, semi-quantitative analysis using ACE assay demonstrates that 4B5 can react with recombinant ZSCAN18, but it requires more than 20-fold higher concentration of protein compared to HER2 reactivity. Similarly, both ACE and IHC data demonstrate 4B5 does not recognize ZSCAN18 at physiological levels of expression in biological samples and is unlikely to interfere with the 4B5 assay.

Expansion of HER2-targeted therapy and IHC assays to carcinomas of the digestive and urogenital systems has highlighted a greater prevalence of cytoplasmic and nuclear staining with 4B5 that is not attributable to HER2, nor is it HER4 or ZSCAN18.11,12 Ion-exchange chromatography, immunoprecipitation and MS sequencing identified AKR1B10 in the cytosolic fraction of a panel of cell lines with 4B5 off-target staining. BLAST search alignment with the HER2 core motif, composed of four critical residues, did not identify a potential interactive epitope until the ZSCAN18 motif, composed of three critical residues, was used as query. As in the ZSCAN18 analysis, the 17-mer peptide encompassing the putative epitope provided no SPR signal until the concentration was increased by 10-fold. Similarly, a semi-quantitative analysis using ACE demonstrated that 4B5 can react with AKR1B10, but it requires more than 60-fold higher concentration compared to HER2 reactivity.

AKR1B10 is a member of a large superfamily of NADP-dependent oxidoreductases that are divided into subfamilies based on sequence homology.27,39 Aldo-keto reductases play an important role in metabolizing xenobiotics, steroids, and carbohydrates and are widely expressed across normal and tumor tissues (reviewed in40). There are 3 members of the AKR1B subfamily expressed in humans. AKR1B1, AKR1B10, and AKR1B15 are 67% (AKR1B1 to AKR1B15), 71% (AKR1B1 to AKR1B10), and 91% (AKR1B10 to AKR1B15) identical, and 100% identical in the putative 4B5 epitope. AKR1B1, also known as aldose reductase, reduces glucose to sorbitol and is often associated with complications of diabetes. Meta analysis of the Oncomine database shows that AKR1B1 is frequently elevated in tumors of the bladder, liver, pancreas and kidney.41 AKR1B10 catalyzes a wide range of aldehydes and xenobiotics, including chemotherapeutic drugs and carcinogenic compounds. Expression is induced by tobacco, and it is frequently overexpressed in lung, pancreatic and hepatocellular carcinoma.42 AKR1B15 has more limited tissue distribution, with expression primarily in adipose tissue, testis, and placenta, where it plays a role in steroid hormone metabolism.43 Thus, the AKR1B family distribution and expression can be tissue dependent but additionally may be affected by patient-specific factors, such as prior therapies.

Recombinant proteins of all three AKR1B family members were recognized by 4B5 in Western blot, and cytosolic or nuclear staining was seen in all cell lines used for protein isolation and identification. Tissue samples that were selected for this study based on the presence of cytoplasmic or nuclear signal with 4B5 show strong expression of AKR1B1 and/or AKR1B10/15 that appears to correspond to the 4B5 signal. Chromogenic multiplex staining further supports the conclusion that 4B5 reacts with AKR1B family proteins, seen by the blending of purple and yellow chromogens. Recognition by 4B5 appears to be highly dependent on local concentration, as chromogen co-localization occurs primarily at sites with strong expression of one or both proteins. Analytical assays using recombinant proteins indicate 4B5 reactivity with AKR1B10 requires 60-fold more protein than is needed for binding to HER2. However, because 4B5 interacts with all three members of the AKR1 subfamily B, it is very possible that the 4B5 signal reflects the combined concentration of the proteins. The potential stacking effect makes it difficult to predict tissue types with the potential for interference with the 4B5 assay.

Molecular modeling was used to investigate the binding event between 4B5 and both the HER2 and AKR1B peptides. The proline, critical for 4B5-HER2 binding and present in both peptides, fits into a hydrophobic pocket formed by heavy chain CDR loops two and three, as well as light chain CDR loop three. However, the existence of the proline alone is insufficient for the interaction to occur. The modeling data, along with the alanine substitution data, identified how the previously mentioned loops further stabilize the interaction by incorporating other amino acids. However, SPR analysis of other proteins, with sequence homology to the HER2 core-binding motif, do not generate an interaction, as demonstrated by 4B5 and AKR1B (Supplemental Table S4). This result when compared with molecular modeling reveals additional insight. The docking of the 4B5-HER2 peptide target eventually converged upon a solution where the orientation of the peptide consistently resulted in an energetically favorable interaction. The docking of the 4B5-AKR1B peptide had more orientations where docking could be achieved, suggesting an interaction that is weaker and less specific. These results suggest that, in IHC, the HER2 target has a favorable target orientation, while the AKR1B protein needs to be flexible/unconstrained to achieve a specifically favorable orientation. Therefore, what is experimentally suggested to be a concentration-dependent interaction between 4B5 and AKR1B, is more specifically an interaction based on the concentration of an appropriately oriented target, further supporting the determination that the 4B5-AKR1B family interaction is due to CDR plasticity. Larger populations of AKR1B peptide or protein in IHC will tend to have a greater pool of proteins in the energetically favorable orientation. This concept is further supported by the immunoprecipitation of the AKR1B off targets, which required heat denaturation with high pH target retrieval (increased lability of targets) for capture. Modeling data could inform the design of a modified recombinant 4B5 antibody where the CDR LL2 could be stiffened, by amino acid substitution, to prevent an interaction with the aspartic acid of the AKR1B protein sequence. Because the CDR LL2 does not play a role in the 4B5-HER2 binding event, this modification could reduce the interaction of 4B5-AKR1B without affecting the desired function of 4B5.

Antibody and companion diagnostic assay development require both biophysical and functional characterization. Our study highlights some of the strengths and weaknesses of various methodologies, and demonstrates the utility of complementary approaches. For example, bioinformatics and sequence identity searches are tools that can reveal cross-reactivity potential, such as HER4, but can also provide a large number of homologs that require investigation by additional methods, seen with HER1 and additional sequences listed in Supplemental Table S4. In addition, sequence identity alone did not predict cross reactivity of 4B5 with the AKR1B family proteins, as only three critical residues aligned with the HER2 epitope. The complementary approach of SPR kinetic analysis and molecular modeling demonstrated that residues required for functional antibody binding can be outside of the core motif for the biomarker of interest. Similarly, singular reliance on overexpression systems without appropriate controls as in the ZSCAN18 study15 can lead to false positives and unduly burden assay development with characterization of irrelevant targets. It is not clear why the high-density peptide array utilized by Qi et al.15 did not identify AKR1B as a cross-reactive species. The current work also highlights the importance of functional characterization across disease conditions and tissue types to establish reliable diagnostic agents.

IHC with 4B5 remains an important diagnostic assay for the determination of HER2 status. A recent study of over 4500 patients with a wide range of solid tumors demonstrated that approximately 50% of solid tumors have some HER2 expression.44 As treatment opportunities expand to include other tumor types and increasingly lower biomarker thresholds, HER2 detection presents new challenges. Based on the analytical, IHC, and modeling studies reported here, 4B5 cross reactivity with HER4 and ZSCAN18 is unlikely to be detected in clinical IHC assays. Reactivity with the AKR1B family of proteins represents the identity of cytoplasmic and nuclear staining that has been reported in normal gastric mucosa.12 Although AKR1B expression has been reported in advanced breast cancer, well-established guidelines and the manufacturer’s instructions for use clearly state that HER2 evaluation should not consider cytoplasmic and nuclear staining and be directed to membranous staining only. IHC continues to be the gold standard for companion diagnostic assays, and continuous development and optimization is essential to ensure patient benefit from targeted therapies.
