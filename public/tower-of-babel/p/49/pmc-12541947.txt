Three-dimensional finite element analysis of two-implant-supported locator overdenture in different reinforced denture base designs under four loading conditions
Authors: Qian Chen, Xinyue Zhang, Tuanfeng Zhou, Kenan Ma, Zixuan Wang, Yong Wang, Yijiao Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study used three-dimensional (3D) finite element analysis to compare the effect of different reinforcement designs, including strengthening metals and fiberglass.in overdentures supported by two canine implants to restore edentulous mandibles.Material and methodsThe von Mises equivalent stress (VMES) and the maximum and minimum principal strains were used to evaluate the stress distribution in the implants and denture bases of eight 3D finite element models in different reinforced denture base designs under four different working conditions, including the vertical occlusal force of the anterior (F4) and posterior teeth (F1) and the lateral occlusal force (F2, F3) of the posterior teeth. ResultsUnder F1 loading, the stress concentration area and VMES in the MP (Internal strengthening metal plate) and MP-F (Internal strengthening metal plate with fiberglass in buccal base) models decreased significantly (from 0.53 MPa to 0.43 and 0.46 MPa). Under F2 and F3 loading, the addition of a metal framework resulted in a lingual central stress concentration area located away from the base edge transition, with a uniform overall distribution and a reduced lingual stress concentration area of the implant. In MP and MP-F models, the VMES and maximum stress were significantly reduced (1.27 MPa, 1.21 MPa and 1.40 MPa, 1.20 MPa). The labial fiber reinforcement bundle for MP-F strengthened the local structure, reduced the local maximum stress value, and promoted local stress distribution.Under F4 loading, the stress in all eight models was located at the alveolar crest of anterior teeth.ConclusionsIn overdentures supported by two canine implants to restore edentulous mandibles, the addition of a metal plate within the lingual base significantly reduced the denture and implant stress concentration areas, as well as the VMES of the denture and implant. It also reduced the maximum stress of the denture and implant under lateral occlusal, locally strengthened the fracture resistance of the denture base, and reduced the incidence of implant neck fracture, particularly with E-fiber strengthening of the labial base.Clinical relevanceThis study corroborates that the base of the implant overdenture with 2 implants in the lower jaw can be reinforced by designing lingual metal plates and labial fibers.

Introduction

The McGill Consensus Statement recommends an overdenture supported by two implants as the minimum standard for prosthetic rehabilitation of edentulous mandibles [1–4]. The Locator attachment has gained popularity because of its potential for use in limited inter-ridge spaces, dual retention, and higher retentive forces [5–7].

Microcracks and fractures are common in acrylic resin matrix polymer denture bases, mainly caused by flexural fatigue and stress concentrations[8, 9], resulting from continuous exposure to cyclic masticatory loads [10]. Eventually, these cracks and fissures lead to restoration failure [10]. Fractures are common in overdentures supported by two implants, with an incidence ranging from 9.3% to 21.4%[11, 12]. This is because the coping, which occupies a thin portion of the denture base prone to fracture and deformation, acts as a fulcrum of movement [13, 14].

To address this problem, acrylic resin bases containing rigid metal have been proposed [15, 16]. However, while metal reinforcement strengthens the base, poor adhesion to the acrylic resin weakens this effect[17], often causing breakage at the bonding interface.

The use of synthetic fibers, including ultrahigh-molecular-weight polyethylene (UHMWP), polyaramid, and E-glass, has been proposed because of their esthetics and excellent ability to chemically bond with resins [18]. Polyaramid fibers (Kevlar; Dupont) are mechanically stable but are unaesthetic, while UHMWP fibers bond poorly to the polymer matrix [19, 20]. In contrast, glass fibers can prevent crack propagation and offer good mechanical properties [21–24]. It has been demonstrated that bundled glass fiber designs are comparable to traditional cast metal designs in two-implant-supported overdenture models [25]. Fiber reinforcements positioned at the center of a composite denture base, with their long axes perpendicular to the applied force, have been shown to improve the mechanical properties of overdentures in ISO (Implant-supported overdenture) models [26, 27]. Current research is focused on reinforcing the denture base using a single material, either metal or fibers.

Three-dimensional (3D) finite element analysis (FEA) is widely used in dentistry to estimate the stress distribution in peri-implant bone, prostheses, and prosthetic components. Using a conventional unreinforced structure as a control, this study compared the effects of different reinforcement designs, including metals and fiberglass, on the stress distribution of two-implant-supported mandibular Locator overdentures through 3D FEA. The null hypothesis was that the reinforcement design would not affect the stress distribution in the two-implant-supported Locator overdenture model.

Materials and methods

The edentulous mandible model was scanned using an Imetric dental model scanner(Imetric 3D SA Inc, Switzerland) to obtain an STL file. The offset and accurate curved surface functions in Geomagic reverse engineering software༈Geomagic Wrap 2021, 3D Systems Inc, US༉were used to generate IGES data. Boolean operations were performed using SpaeeClaim 2022 R1 software (ANSYS Inc, US) for the construction of mucoperiosteum (thickness: 2 mm) [28], cortical bone (thickness: 2 mm) [29], and cancellous bone layers. Scan the standard model of the mandible for clinical teaching using the 3-Shape software (3shape Inc, Denmark), a two-implants Locator overdenture with seven base structure variations (A–G) was designed in STL format (Table 1). Eight grid elements and nodes for the models, follows: (C) conventional unreinforced structure; (MRO) reinforcing metal rod inside lingual base; (MRO-F) reinforcing metal rod inside lingual base with fiberglass inside buccal base; (MRI) internal strengthening metal ring; (MRI-F) internal strengthening metal ring with fiberglass inside buccal base; (MP) internal strengthening metal plate; (MP-F) internal strengthening metal plate with fiberglass inside buccal base; (F) only fiberglass inside buccal base. Additionally, implants with a 4.1-mm diameter and 10-mm length (Straumann Inc, Switzerland), as well as Locator attachments compatible with the implants (Zest Anchors Inc, US), were scanned to generate STL files. The digital assembly of the physical model was completed using Geomagic software, with one implant vertically placed in each mandibular canine area, and the corresponding Locator attachment components were seated.

Temporomandibular joint and the associated muscles, including masseter (M), temporalis (T), medial pterygoid (MP), lateral pterygoid (LP), and the anterior belly of digastric (D), provide support and fixation to the mandible. Therefore, rigid constraints were applied to each muscle attachment site in this study (Fig. 1).

This study simulated four working conditions based on masticatory forces (Fig. 1). In agreement with other overdenture FEA studies [33, 34], 150 N vertical force on occlusal load is applied in this study, which is very close to masticatory clinical. F1 involved a 150-N force applied to the central fossae of the mandibular bilateral posterior teeth, parallel to the long axes of the teeth, simulating the force exerted on the overdenture during central occlusion. F2 was a 150-N force applied at a 45° angle along the long axes of the posterior teeth, directed lingually on the left side and buccally on the right side, representing the force during right mandibular masticatory movement. The 45° angle was refer to the direction of the component of the lateral occlusion, and refer to the average cusp slope. F3 was a 150-N force applied at a 45° angle along the long axes of the posterior teeth, directed buccally on the left side and lingually on the right side, simulating the force during left mandibular masticatory movement. F4 involved a 150-N force applied vertically along the lower incisors, representing the force exerted while cutting the food using the anterior teeth of overdenture.

The von Mises equivalent stresses (VMESs) and the maximum and minimum principal strain were recorded, facilitating an evaluation of the stress situation in the implants and denture bases of the eight models under four different working conditions, along with the effects of different base designs on the biomechanical properties of the mandibular implant overdenture.

Results

The 3D finite element stress distributions of the eight implant overdenture models under four different directional forces are shown in Figs. 2 and 3. Under F1 loading, the stresses in all eight models were concentrated at the alveolar crest of the posterior teeth, as well as at the distal contact area between the base and implant abutment. There were no obvious denture base changes in the MRI, MRI-F, and MRO models, and the stress concentration area and position did not change significantly. In models MP and MP-F, the stress concentration area and VMES in the posterior tooth area decreased significantly, while fiber reinforcement had nearly no effect on the stress concentration area and VMES. The distal stress concentration area at the contact between the base and implant abutment also decreased significantly.

F2 and F3 loading. From the tissue surface of the denture base, the stress was concentrated around the implant abutment, particularly on the lingual side, and extended to the posterior molar pad. Metal stent enhancement decreased the stress concentration area on the lingual side of the implant, with the MP model exhibiting uniform stress distribution and an obviously decreased stress concentration area. Stress distribution was then observed from the denture polishing surface, revealing larger stress concentration areas in models C, F, and MRO, in the MP model, the lingual central stress concentration area was more evenly distributed, away from the marginal turning point of the denture base. When the posterior teeth were loaded laterally, the VMES of the denture base in models C, F, and (MRO, MRO-F) increased gradually, but the maximum stress decreased, while in models MP and MP-F, the VMES and maximum stress decreased significantly. For implants, the VMES values of the (C, F), (MRI, MRI-F), (MRO, MRO-F), and (MP, MP-F) models decreased gradually.

Under F4 loading, the eight models of stresses were present at the alveolar crest of the anterior teeth, and the stress concentration area decreased with enhancement through the metal rod, metal ring, or metal plate.

Under F1 loading, stress in all eight models was concentrated at the distal implant neck, with symmetrical distribution on the left and right sides. Under F3 loading, the stress in six models, with the exception of the MP model, was concentrated in the left implant neck, with the left implant exhibiting more pronounced stress. In models MP and MP-F, the stress concentration area of the implant decreased significantly, and the distribution tended to be uniform.

Discussion

The hypothesis of this study was rejected and the research showed that the results of the 3D finite element analysis of the base with different base reinforcement materials were different. The standard clinical restoration scheme for edentulous anterior region of the mandible involves an overdenture supported by two implants [35]. Locator implant overdenture is the optimal choice because of its additional advantages, including bite force dispersion and implant protection [36]. Previous finite element studies have demonstrated that, due to the implant retention support, the stress in locator button implant overdentures during normal physiological functions is mainly concentrated near the implant site and the midline area, which is highly consistent with clinical research [8–10]. Reinforcing the denture base through the addition of metals promotes a more uniform stress distribution in the functional state [25].

In vivo studies, standardization of all other factors and evaluation of a single factor is difficult. Finite element analysis (FEA) is a widely employed method in dentistry to estimate the stress/strain distribution in the peri-implant bone, prostheses, and prosthetic components in many diverse situations. In FEA, standard models can be obtained and analyses can be made by changing only intended factors and keeping all other factors constant. Therefore, FEA is an appropriate method for this study, which compared various occlusion types.

In this study, the 3D finite element stress distribution map also showed that the stress was concentrated at the denture midline (Table 4; Figs. 2 and 3). Metal bracket strengthening gradually reduced the range of stress distribution, particularly at the lingual base of the implant. The stress concentration gradually shifted to the occlusal surface, from the weak edge of the base to a thicker portion, thereby reducing the risk of base fracture. This finding was consistent with the previous studies [37]. It is possible to reduce the stress concentration area of the corresponding base using a combination of materials after a metal-reinforced bracket is added to the lingual side of the base, allowing stress concentration at an area with a greater Young’s modulus. Simultaneously, the stress on materials with smaller elastic modulus values can be transferred to adjacent materials with larger elastic modulus values [38, 39]. The addition of cobalt-chromium metal, having a higher Young’s modulus, in the MRO-F, MOI-F, and MP-F models blocked or diluted the stress transfer by the metal-reinforced lingual plate, thereby reducing the stress applied to the implant or denture base. The stress concentration area within the denture was reduced significantly after the addition of a metal reinforcement plate [38, 39]. Among the three most common metal designs used to enhance the denture base strength, the metal lingual plate had the greatest stress reduction effect, followed by the metal rod and the metal ring. Compared to the latter two, the metal-reinforced lingual plate exhibited the largest reinforcement area and the strongest structure, resulting in the most uniform stress transfer. The uniform or small stress of the denture base will prolong the clinical service life of the denture and greatly improve the clinical benefit of the patient. Previous studies have demonstrated that, as a strengthening material for denture bases, cobalt-chromium alloy can absorb and significantly reduce the stress on implant and bone surfaces [38, 39]. Although the fabrication of a metal-reinforced lingual plate is more complex compared to the remaining two metal stents, it is more conducive to stress dispersion and base fracture prevention in implant overdentures.

The addition of fiber-reinforced bundles, measuring 1.47 × 2.85 × 96.73 mm, on the labial side of the denture was based on previously reported dimensions of 1.5 × 3 × 50 mm in similar studies [25]. However, in contrast with the present study, where fiber structures were placed on the lingual side of the denture, labial structures could neither reduce the stress concentration area on the lingual side of the denture base nor improve the stress distribution. Consequently, it failed to reduce the VMES and maximum stress of the denture and implant, probably because the stress concentration area of the denture base was located lingually and not labially, which contrasted with previously reported findings [25]. When the posterior teeth were loaded vertically and laterally, labial addition of fiber-reinforced bundles had little effect on the stress distribution, VMES, and maximum stress of the denture base, while vertical loading in the anterior tooth area resulted in obvious dispersion of stress distribution and reduction of the maximum stress. Reinforcement of the anterior teeth with a labial fiber bundle altered the local physical properties of the labial base of the anterior teeth, with increased mechanical strength against anterior tooth loading and improved resistance strength of the denture base Fig. 4.

The stress concentration area of the implant varied under different designs and loading forms. F1 simulates the central occlusal force of the patient's daily chewing.The stress concentration area for the posterior tooth area had a greater influence on implant stress distribution compared to that of the anterior tooth area. F2 and F3 simulate the lateral movements in the patient's chewing cycle. Under lateral loading of the posterior teeth, the stress concentration area was located at the distal implant neck, exhibiting symmetry between the left and right sides. Additionally, the implant neck was subject to torsion and the stress concentration area was located labially in the implant neck. In the MP model, the stress concentration area of the implant decreased significantly and the distribution became uniform. Similar findings were observed in the MP-F and MP models, in agreement with previous studies demonstrating that metal-reinforced structures could reduce the stress on implants [40, 41]. During posterior tooth area loading, the fiber reinforcement bundle was thinner and was located mainly on the labial side of the anterior tooth area, at a considerable distance from the loading area, having no obvious effect on stress distribution. However, it still strengthened the local structure, reduced the local maximum stress value, and promoted local stress distribution (Table 4). Under vertical loading of the anterior tooth area, the stress and implant directions remained relatively consistent. While the VMES value was greater compared to the posterior area when the implant was loaded along the long axis, the stress concentration area decreased and distributed along the implant axis, with a more uniform stress distribution, thereby improving implant support. The results are similar to those of other literatures[42–44], which think that, without any friction, the interfaces between different materials and structures were continuous with a passive fit, and all materials were homogeneous. Material properties and structure geometries have a great influence on the stress/strain distribution, but in clinical reality, these assumptions are not always true, being a possible limitation Fig. 5.

Fiber-reinforced denture bases without any strengthening structure exhibited the highest maximum principal stress and stress concentration areas, regardless of the stress mode. However, addition of metal rods or rings to reinforce the support structure resulted in a significant decrease in the maximum principal stress and the stress concentration area of the denture, particularly at the base corresponding to the lingual side of the implant. Furthermore, the addition of a metal lingual plate for reinforcement significant reduced the maximum principal stress and the stress concentration area, regardless of the stress mode, particularly when lateral forces were applied to the posterior teeth. The stress distribution also tended to be more uniform.

Although literature suggests that the denture structure and material influence the stresses in implants and dentures under vertical and lateral occlusal forces, the clinical situation is often more complicated. Standardized and unified hypothetical factors are required for 3D FEA, while data obtained from clinics, including the patients’ chewing habits and bite force directions, are highly variable [34, 45]. However, the experimental results of this study can be used as a reference for clinical practice while taking its limitations into consideration. This study focuses on the ultimate load-bearing capacity under static loads, and the comparison of dynamic fatigue strength will continue in the future.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this FEA study, the following conclusions were drawn:

When overdentures supported by two canine implants were used to restore edentulous mandibles, the addition of a metal plate to the lingual base and an E-fiber strengthening structure was added to the labial base was more recommended than other methods of base strengthening in clinical practice. It is possible to prolong the clinical service life of patients by reducing the force of the dispersion base.
