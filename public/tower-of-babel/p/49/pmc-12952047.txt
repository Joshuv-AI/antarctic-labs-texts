Sodium bicarbonate for “metabolic acidosis”: an illustration of the Goodhart’s law
Authors: Sylvain Diop, Maxime Aparicio, Roman Mounier, Antoine Premachandra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCorrection of “metabolic acidosis” by sodium bicarbonate is a common practice in intensive care unit or operating room. The physiological rationale underlying its use relies on the potential harmful hemodynamics effects of prolonged low extracellular pH.Main textEvidence showed that the “artificial” and transient correction of blood pH has little if any effect on intracellular pH, the variable of interest, which drives enzymatic reactions and metabolic pathways. Conversely, infusion of sodium bicarbonate corresponds to administer a sodium load which may affect patient’s osmolarity thus cell functions. In this short comment, we recall the determinant of extra and intracellular pH, the lack of evidence regarding the clinical benefit of sodium bicarbonate infusion for metabolic acidosis, and the danger associated with acute change in blood osmolarity at the light of evolutionary biology.ConclusionThe physiological background to justify the use of SB for correcting metabolic acidosis in critically ill patients is thin if inexistent. This should be considered before initiating future trial.

Background

Administration of sodium bicarbonate (SB) to correct non-respiratory-induced low blood pH has been advocated to improve hemodynamics by restoration of smooth muscle cell reactivity and prevention of arrhythmia and cardiac dysfunction. The results of the BICAR-ICU 2 trial published on October 29, 2025 provide us an opportunity to revisit the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying SB infusion [1].

“Metabolic acidosis”

Acid–base definition in biological system

pH is an inverse logarithmic scale of hydrogen ions concentrations ([H+]), which is defined as a “dependent” variable in biochemical models [2]. Three independent factors determine [H+]: the strong ion difference (SID); the dissolved CO2 concentration (in an open CO2 system); and the total weak acids’ concentration ([Atot]). For a given pressure and temperature, those determinants set the ratio of dissociated over undissociated forms for all dependent species in the system (water included), thereby maintaining electroneutrality [2]. In biology, a water-based solution is “acid” when [H+] is higher to hydroxyl ion concentration ([OH−]), “neutral” when [H+] equals [OH−], and “basic/alkaline” when [H+] is lower than [OH−] [2, 3]. In standard temperature conditions, blood is almost always alkaline ([H+] < [OH−])—of note the neutral pH of blood at 37 °C is about 6.8 [2, 3]. In an alkaline biological solution (positive SID), the behavior of [H+] is completely different from a solution with a negative one. In the first case (SID positive), adding [H+] to the solution will result in a smaller increase in [H+] compared to the amount added ([H+] solution < [H+] added). In the second one (SID negative), adding [H+] to the solution will result in a linear increase in [H+] ([H+] solution = [H+] added). In brief, in a SID positive solution, adding [H+] to the solution will result in a solution more or less alkaline. Thus, talking about acidemia or alkalemia may be confusing [2, 3]. As proposed by Magder and colleagues, rather than “acidosis/acidemia” or “alkalosis/alkalemia,” “hyper,” or “hypo-hydrogenemia” would better characterize the deviation of [H+] from a standard value at a given temperature [2, 3]. The pH and composition of intracellular and extracellular spaces differ dramatically: On average, intracellular pH is lower than extracellular one (6.8–7.0 against 7.4), the respective SID is 130 and 40 mEq/L, and the concentration of Atot is tenfold higher inside the cells [2, 3]. Strong mineral ions—which are implicated in the SID—as well as proteins—implicated in Atot—do not cross cellular membranes freely [2, 3].

Hemodynamic effect of blood “metabolic acidosis”: between belief and evidence

“Metabolic acidosis” driven hemodynamic impairment is frequently put forward to justify SB administration [1]. However, experimental studies showed that a change in extracellular pH from non-respiratory origin did not affect intracellular pH, cells contractility, and vasoactive properties of catecholamines [4, 5]. In an experimental study on Chinese hamster ovary cells (CHO cells), the intracellular pH was relatively resistant to the change in extracellular pH from 6.8 to 7.8 [4]. Vidal et al. showed that decreasing extracellular pH from 7.4 to 6.8 did not alter significantly the vasoactive properties of epinephrine, norepinephrine and phenylephrine in human distal internal mammary [5]. They also point out that intracellular acidosis may have beneficial effects by potentializing the response to catecholamines, promoting cells’ preservation under hypoxic condition, and by positive conditioning effect during ischemia reperfusion phenomenon [5, 6].

How effective is SB?

Recurrent clinical evidence demonstrated the inefficiency of the symptomatic blood “metabolic acidosis” correction using SB, both on hemodynamics and clinical outcomes [10, 11]. No impact of SB administration was observed in patients with diabetic ketoacidosis [12]. Similar results were obtained in patients with hyperlactatemia-related metabolic acidosis and in patients with septic shock [10, 11, 13]. Also, the BICAR-ICU randomized trial failed to demonstrate an effect of 4.2% SB buffering for metabolic acidosis in terms of death or organ failure, except for the subgroup of patients with moderate-to-severe acute kidney injury [14]. Finally, the results of the BICAR-ICU 2 trial including patients with pH ≤ 7.20, bicarbonate ≤ 20 mmol/L, PaCO2 ≤ 45 mmHg, and moderate-to-severe acute kidney injury confirmed that SB administration did not reduce mortality within 90 days, with no evidence of group effect on the post hoc subgroup analysis [1].

What adverse effects to expect?

Physiological rationale and experimental evidences against SB infusion are not to be forgotten. A century ago, Minot et al. showed that several sodium salt administrations, including SB, during guanidine intoxication-related hyperlactatemia, decreased Ca2+ concentration in blood leading to symptoms worsening [15]. This was reversed by the concomitant infusion of calcium [15]. SB may also decrease O2 delivery by transiently left-shifting the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve [16]. SB may rapidly exacerbate intracellular acidosis by intracellular dehydration and local CO2 release (also this mechanism is debated during in vivo condition) [3, 17]. Intracellular acidosis induces myocytes/smooth cell dysfunction by decreasing intracellular Ca2+, high energy phosphate reservoir, and phosphocreatine content [4, 18]. Conversely, the alkalinizing effect is obtained through infusion of high sodium load—which widens the extracellular SID but has little if any effect on intracellular pH since cellular membranes are impermeable to Na+ [2, 3]. In various animal models of metabolic acidosis, rapid infusion of hypertonic SB solution has been shown to induce cardiac and smooth muscle cells dysfunction related to change in cell water content and decrease in Ca2+ availability [11, 19, 20].

The danger of hypertonicity at the light of evolutionary biology

Because water and ion—especially Na+ and Cl−—are scarcely available in terrestrial environments, humans being dryland vertebrates, we developed powerful mechanisms to limit water and salt loss. One of the most important roles of the kidneys is to retain sodium through the highly conserved renin angiotensin aldosterone system, and water via the antidiuretic hormone. Except few exceptions, the osmolarity of all terrestrial vertebrates is nearly identical [7, 9]. Plasma tonicity, mainly driven by the concentration of mineral ions (mainly Na+ and Cl−), is a tightly regulated variable, as it is essential for neuronal activities and overall cells functions [8, 9]. Osmoreceptor are mechanical-stretch receptors localized in the subfornical organ and the vascular organ of lamina terminalis. They sense any stretch or shrink of their membranes due to the change in tonicity in order to preserve cells volume. Accordingly, any increase in extracellular effective osmolarity leads to irrepressible thirst stimulation, water craving, decrease in angiotensin II, and increase in vasopressin circulating levels [9]. The intracellular volume of water appears to be critical to maintain optimal cells functions. Any decrease in water cells volume (e.g., by a hypertonic osmotic stress) leads to multiples harmful consequences from mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA alteration to cells apoptosis [8]. The quick response following a hypertonic stress involves various ionic channels that allow the re-equilibrium of the osmotic pressure across the cell membranes. This response is not sustainable, because the intracellular ingress of ionic molecules alters biochemical functions [8]. The delayed response is mediated by the transcription and synthesis of non-ionic osmotic molecules such as the polyols, neutral amino acids, or methylamine which do not interfere with normal cell functions [8]. These osmolytes are used by various vertebrates’ species to increase their osmolarity, particularly those who have the same osmolarity that sea water (sharks, coelacanth, hagfish, sea water frog). This mechanism of osmoregulation is highly conserved in the evolution [9]. Conclusion

There is a paucity of clinical evidence that the correction of “metabolic acidosis” by SB leads to better outcomes. The physiological rationale is thin if not inexistent. This should be considered for future trial. Treating laboratory values is not treating the disease. As theorized by Charles Goodhart, a measure that becomes a target, ceases to be a good measure [24].
