The landscape of tissue-resident microbiota across normal, polyp, and colorectal cancer tissues
Authors: Haoyi Xiang, Baining Shen, Weifeng Lao, Bingjun Bai, Min Chen, Leqi Zhou, Guanyu Yu, Wei Zhang, Huan Chen, Yuhao Wang, Hangjin Jiang, Zhangfa Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmerging evidence suggests that tissue-resident microbiota (TRM) is associated with tumor biology; however, their distribution and compositional characteristics across different colorectal tissue types remain incompletely defined. Here, we conducted a comprehensive cross-sectional analysis of TRM distribution and abundance across 1134 clinical specimens, including normal mucosa, precancerous polyps, and colorectal cancer (CRC) tissues. Our results reveal distinct microbial profiles among these diagnostic groups, with consistent differences in community composition between normal, polyps, and CRC samples. Integrative analyses further identified microbial signatures capable of distinguishing tissue categories and reflected differences in the local tumor microenvironment. In contrast, intratumoral microbiota composition showed subtle variation across established tumor stages and was not associated with clinical outcomes. These findings define diagnostic group-associated patterns of TRM in colorectal tissues and establish a foundation for future mechanistic investigations into the biological roles of TRM in colorectal cancer.Subject terms: Predictive markers, Surgical oncology, Tumour biomarkers

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a major global health burden, ranking as the third most diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths according to GLOBOCAN 2020 estimates1. Despite therapeutic improvements, CRC continues to carry a high mortality burden, emphasizing the pressing need to understand the biological mechanisms that initiate and sustain tumor development.

Beyond conventional risk factors, including genetic predisposition, epigenetic modifications, and stromal microenvironment alterations, the gut microbiota has emerged as a critical mediator of CRC susceptibility and progression. Mounting evidence suggests that gut microbiota influence colorectal tumorigenesis through multifaceted mechanisms, such as microbial metabolite production, intricate immune system modulation, and direct epithelial interactions2–7. In addition to gut microbiota, recent discoveries of tissue-resident microbiota (TRM), defined as bacterial communities that establish stable colonization within host tissues, have opened new avenues for understanding colorectal tumorigenesis. Unlike luminal microbiota, TRM establishes direct physical interactions with host cells, suggesting they may play more immediate roles in epithelial transformation8–10. While TRM profiles have been linked to cancer progression in other malignancies, their specific contributions to colorectal tumor initiation and progression remain poorly understood11–14.

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive multi-cohort analysis of the TRM landscape across 1134 clinical specimens, encompassing normal mucosa, precancerous adenomatous polyps, and invasive carcinomas. Using pre-amplified 16S rRNA sequencing coupled with advanced bioinformatic pipelines, we identified distinct differences in microbial community composition among these tissue categories. Notably, TRM differences were most pronounced between diagnostic groups, with minimal variation across later clinical stages or patient survival. These findings define tissue-associated patterns of TRM composition in colorectal tissues and provide a basis for future mechanistic investigations aimed at understanding the biological relevance of TRM in colorectal cancer.

Results

Colorectal cancer is associated with distinct taxonomic differences in TRM

To systematically profile the TRM in CRC, we conducted a cross-sectional study utilizing CRC and normal colorectal tissue specimens from the Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital of Zhejiang University Biobank. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the included participants are summarized in Tables S1 and S2. After quality control, 1,022 samples were retained, with 818 (182 normal, 68 polyps, 568 CRC) assigned to the discovery set and 204 (45 normal, 17 polyps, 142 CRC) to the validation set.

To characterize the TRM, we employed an optimized 16S rRNA gene sequencing protocol in which the V4 region was pre-amplified using biotin-labeled primers, followed by streptavidin bead-based enrichment to enhance specificity. High-throughput sequencing was subsequently performed with stringent contamination controls to ensure data reliability (Fig. 1A, B). Parallel culture-based assays confirmed viable bacteria in control, polyp, and CRC tissues (Fig. 1C). Quantitative analysis revealed a statistically significant increase in bacterial load in CRC tissues compared to both normal and polyp tissues, while no significant difference was observed between normal and polyp tissues (Fig. 1C).

Due to the relatively small number of polyp samples, we first focused on analyzing compositional differences in TRM between normal and CRC tissues. We conducted multivariate analyses using Bray-Curtis distances. Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) and Detrended Correspondence Analysis (DAC) revealed distinct clustering patterns separating normal intestinal tissues from CRC samples at the genus level (Fig. 1D, E). Taxonomic profiling demonstrated marked variations in bacterial abundance between groups (Fig. 1F). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) identified specific bacterial taxa differentially abundant between normal and CRC tissues (Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) score > 2.0, q-value < 0.05; Fig. 1G). Notably, normal tissues exhibited enrichment of Pseudomonadaceae, aligning with previous reports that highlight Pseudomonas aeruginosa’s tumor-suppressive capability15,16. In contrast, CRC tissues showed enrichment of several bacterial taxa with an established role in promoting CRC development. This included Fusobacterium specie17,18, Bacteroides species19, and Enterobacteroides species20,21. These findings collectively reinforce the established association between specific microbial communities and colorectal tumorigenesis while providing new insights into their tissue-specific distribution patterns.

To distinguish TRM from luminal microbiota, we compared tissue-derived microbial profiles with fecal microbiota from CRC patients aggregated across seven public datasets22–28. PCoA analysis revealed a clear separation between fecal and tissue microbiomes (PERMANOVA, p-value < 0.001; Figure S1A), accompanied by significant differences in alpha diversity (Fig. S1B) and dominant genera composition (Fig. S1C). Parallel differential abundance analyses identified partially overlapping but weakly correlated taxa (ρ = 0.106, p-value = 0.451; Fig. S1D), indicating that TRM represent distinct microbial communities not adequately captured by fecal profiling.

TRM shows a limited association with clinical features in CRC

While substantial differences exist between TRM in normal versus CRC tissues, their potential to discriminate clinical features of CRC remains unexplored. To address this, we conducted PCoA analysis of intratumoral microbiota stratified by multiple clinical parameters at the genus level. Contrary to our expectations, microbial communities showed no significant segregation when analyzed by age, gender, differentiation status, tumor node metastasis classification (TNM) stages, anatomical, or survival status (Fig. 2A–F). We observed the same pattern after controlling inter-individual confounding factors by analyzing on samples of matched tumor and adjacent normal tissue pairs. (Figures S2A–F). These findings indicate that while TRM composition differs fundamentally between normal and cancerous tissues, these microbial signatures do not appear to reflect the clinical heterogeneity of established CRC.

To investigate potential associations between TRM and clinical features, we performed a hierarchical clustering analysis of bacterial genera abundance across the aforementioned clinical characteristics. The resulting heatmap revealed diverse abundance patterns of specific bacterial genera across different clinical features, with certain taxa showing relative enrichment in particular subgroups (Fig. 2G). However, these associations failed to reach statistical significance, confirming no robust correlation between TRM and clinical features in CRC.

To further explore the relationship between TRM and clinical features in CRC, we developed random forest classification models for key clinical parameters and assessed their performance through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. In the training cohort, area under the curve (AUC) values ranged from 0.565 to 0.714 (Fig. 2H). The validation cohort showed comparable limitations, with AUC values between 0.541 and 0.762 (Fig. 2I). Analyses on matched tumor-normal tissue pairs from the same individuals yielded similar results (Fig. S2G). This suggests that the observed microbial patterns are independent of intra-individual confounders.

To evaluate potential clinical confounders, we performed multivariate analyses incorporating age, gender, tumor stage, location, differentiation grade, and chemotherapy status. Cox proportional hazards analysis, adjusting for clinical confounders, revealed that no individual microbial taxon marginally associated with survival outcome (Fig. S3A). To further investigate the joint impact of the microbiota on survival outcome, the Lasso-Cox model was used to select important taxa, and the selected features were further analyzed using multivariable Cox regression. The results revealed that their Hazard Ratios (HR) were near 1.0, indicating a negligible contribution to survival compared to clinical covariates (Fig. S3B). Analysis on 5-year survival outcome by using logistic regression gave similar results (Fig. S4A, B). Furthermore, community-level models (Random Forest) showed that adding microbial data did not improve the predictive performance beyond clinical variables alone (Fig. S4C).

Overall, these results demonstrate that no strong associations were observed between TRM composition and survival outcomes or other clinical features under the applied statistical frameworks in our cohort. Consistent suboptimal model performance (AUC < 0.7) indicates that TRM lacks sufficient discriminatory power to serve as a reliable biomarker for CRC tumor characteristics or clinical outcomes.

TRM exhibits distinct profiles across normal, polyp, and CRC tissues

Our observation of stable TRM composition across established tumor stages, contrasted with marked differences between normal and CRC tissues, indicates cross-sectional differences in TRM profiles between tissue types rather than stage-dependent variation within colorectal cancer. To investigate this hypothesis, we expanded our analysis to include precancerous polyps (68 in the discovery cohort, 17 in the validation cohort), encompassing samples from normal mucosa, adenomatous polyps, and invasive carcinoma.

Through PCoA and DCA analyses based on Bray-Curtis distances, we revealed three distinct microbial ecologies corresponding to each histological stage. The polyp-associated microbiota exhibited a distinct profile relative to both normal and colorectal cancer tissues, demonstrating clear microbial differences among normal, polyp, and CRC samples (Fig. 3A, B). Taxonomic profiling identified specific bacterial families with distinct abundance patterns across histological groups (Fig. 3C). Fusobacteriaceae, Streptococcaceae, Prevotellaceae, Leptotrichiaceae, and Burkholderiaceae showed the lowest relative abundance in normal tissue, intermediate abundance in polyp tissue, and the highest abundance in CRC tissue. Conversely, Coriobacteriaceae, Bifidobacteriaceae, and Tannerellaceae were most abundant in normal tissue. Genus-level analysis confirmed these patterns, with Fusobacterium, Streptococcus, Peptostreptococcus, Leptotrichia, Massilia, and Prevotella displaying the lowest abundance in normal tissue, intermediate levels in polyps, and the highest abundance in CRC, while Phascolarctobacterium, Collinsella, Bifidobacterium, Pseudomonas, and Akkermansia were enriched in normal tissue, reduced in polyps, and further depleted in CRC samples (Cochran-Armitage trend test, q-value < 0.05). Notably, several genera, including Bacteroides, Porphyromonas, and Campylobacter exhibited markedly higher abundance in CRC compared with both normal and polyp tissues (Fig. 3D, E).

These findings demonstrate distinct TRM profiles across normal, polyp, and colorectal cancer tissues. While each histological category is characterized by a distinct microbial signature, TRM composition appears relatively stable between polyps and established carcinoma, highlighting the relevance of TRM profiles in distinguishing diagnostic tissue categories, rather than across clinical strata within colorectal cancer.

TRM signatures distinguish colorectal tissue states

Building upon our observation of distinct TRM profiles in normal, polyp, and CRC tissues, alongside a relative stability of TRM composition across established tumor stages, we next sought to determine whether these microbial signatures could reliably differentiate tissue states. We initially developed a three-class random forest model (normal mucosa, polyps, CRC), which achieved acceptable performance (AUC > 0.8; Fig. S5). However, the limited number of polyp samples (n = 68) constrained classification precision. To address this and better capture microbial differences between tissue types, we employed pairwise models. Specifically, we compared polyps with Stage I CRC (n = 61). These groups were selected because of comparable sample sizes, allowing a balanced cross-sectional comparison of microbial profiles between polyp tissue and early-stage colorectal cancer tissue.

To identify core microbial features, we first ranked taxa by their importance scores derived from the random forest algorithm and selected the top 10 candidates for further evaluation. These taxa were then filtered based on prevalence, excluding those present in only a small subset of samples. Subsequent refinement incorporated pairwise correlation analyses to prioritize taxa with low intercorrelation, thereby minimizing redundancy. Finally, we excluded taxa with ambiguous taxonomic classification, retaining only those with clear biological relevance for modeling (highlighted in boldface; Fig. 4A, B).

To optimize feature parsimony, we monitored both training and validation performance (measured by mean of AUCs) using 5-fold cross-validation, and selected the number of variables that achieved the highest validation performance while controlling the risk of overfitting based on training performance (Fig. 4C; See Methods). The same selection protocol was applied to the normal-vs-polyp (Fig. S6) and normal-vs-CRC (Figure S7) models. Notably, we found that four key taxa: Pseudomonas, Pigmentiphaga, Parvimonas, and Fusobacterium were sufficient to achieve robust discrimination between Polyp and CRC_I tissues (highlighted in red; Fig. 4C). Similarly, six taxa: Pseudomonas, Parabacteroides, Bacteroides, Pigmentiphaga, Fusobacterium, and Collinsella provided effective discrimination between normal mucosa and polyps (highlighted in red; Fig. 4D).

The resulting models consistently classified tissue states with high performance within our cohort, achieving AUCs of 0.911 (training) and 0.954 (validation) for normal-versus-polyp, 0.986 and 0.996 for polyp-versus-CRC, and 0.944 and 0.917 for normal-versus-CRC comparisons (Fig. S8A, B). To ensure the robustness of these findings and reduce the risk of overfitting, we implemented a 5-fold cross-validation strategy across the discovery cohort for model evaluation. Additionally, up-sampling was applied during model training to address class imbalances, particularly the relatively small number of polyp samples, which produced more conservative and reliable estimates. Across all classification tasks, this rigorous validation yielded mean AUCs of 0.87-0.90, with standard deviations reflecting consistency across folds (Fig. 4E), demonstrating that the models capture reproducible differences in tissue-associated microbial composition. Notably, 55 patients in our dataset contributed both tumor and matched normal samples. To further assess model generalizability, we performed Leave-One-Patient-Out (LOPO) validation, which ensures that samples from the same patient are never included in both the training and validation sets. This analysis yielded results consistent with our original cross-validation findings (Fig. S8C).

Intersection analysis of discriminatory taxa across models (Fig. S9A) identified key overlapping features: Pseudomonas, Pigmentiphaga, and Fusobacterium were shared between normal-vs-polyp and polyp-vs-CRC_I models, while Pseudomonas and Fusobacterium appeared consistently across all three models. Differential abundance analyses across various clinical features showed that most microbial taxa did not differ significantly, indicating that these signatures are largely independent of common clinical variables (Fig. S9B–G). This stability across diverse patient characteristics highlights the robustness of TRM composition as a discriminator of colorectal tissue states.

To evaluate generalizability, we applied our models to an independent external cohort comprising 112 samples collected from a separate clinical center. The model demonstrated robust performance, achieving AUCs of 0.779 for Normal vs. Polyp, 0.796 for Polyp vs. CRC, and 0.767 for Normal vs. CRC (Fig. 4F). These AUCs provide a realistic estimate of model performance in an external clinical setting. Although modestly lower than the internal validation metrics, such reductions are expected in rigorous external testing and are consistent with patterns reported in prior large-scale microbiome studies27. These findings indicate that the microbial patterns identified in our study are reproducible across distinct populations and clinical contexts.

To further examine the influence of clinical heterogeneity, we stratified CRC samples by tumor location (right- vs. left-sided) and microsatellite instability (MSI) status. Due to the limited number of MSI-High samples (n = 28), analyses focused on the predominant MSS subgroup. Differentially abundant taxa were largely consistent across Right-sided, Left-sided, and MSS subgroups, and models rebuilt using stratum-specific microbial signatures maintained similar performance (Fig. S10). These findings indicate that TRM compositional differences are robust and largely independent of common clinical variables such as tumor location or MSI status, reinforcing the generalizability of the observed microbial patterns across diverse CRC subtypes.

Taken together, these findings show that TRM composition is distinct across the normal, polyp, and CRC status and remains stable across clinical subgroups and cohorts. The recurrent presence of taxa such as Pseudomonas, Pigmentiphaga, and Fusobacterium points to reproducible microbial patterns associated with colorectal tumorigenesis.

Validation of model-identified microbial signatures in clinical specimens

To experimentally validate the microbial signatures identified by our machine learning models, we performed fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) on clinical specimens representing normal mucosa, polyps, and CRC tissues. This approach enabled direct visualization and quantification of the signature bacterial taxa at their native tissue microenvironments.

For taxa shared between normal-vs-polyp and polyp-vs-CRC_I models, we observed distinct colonization patterns consistent with our taxonomic profiling results. Pseudomonas fluorescence intensity declined from normal tissue to polyps and CRC (Fig. 5A, B), whereas Pigmentiphaga was least abundant in polyp specimens (Fig. 5C, D). Notably, Fusobacterium intensity increased across tissue types, reaching its highest levels in CRC samples (Fig. 5E, F), in line with its established role in CRC development. FISH analysis of model-specific bacterial signatures further validated our machine learning models. In normal versus polyp comparisons, Parabacteroides, Collinsella, and Bacteroides all maintained significantly higher abundance in normal tissues, while Parvimonas was exclusively enriched in CRC specimens (Fig. S11).

To assess the consistency between FISH and 16S rRNA sequencing data, we conducted a group-level comparative trend analysis. For each diagnostic group (normal, polyp, and CRC), we compared mean relative abundance values from sequencing with corresponding FISH quantification results (Fig. S12A). Both methods revealed highly concordant patterns across tissue types. For example, Fusobacterium abundance increased from normal mucosa to tumor tissues in both datasets (Fig. S12B), demonstrating the robustness and reproducibility of this microbial trajectory across distinct analytical platforms.

Together, these experimental results demonstrate strong concordance between computational predictions and experimental observations at the single-microbe level. Our findings reinforce the biological validity of TRM-based microbial signatures and provide experimentally supported candidates for future mechanistic studies on microbiota-host interactions in colorectal cancer development.

Discussion

CRC remains one of the most prevalent and lethal malignancies worldwide, arising through a stepwise process from normal mucosa to adenomatous polyps and eventually to invasive carcinoma29. While host genetics and environmental factors are well-recognized contributors to this progression30, the role of TRM in shaping the tumor microenvironment remains incompletely understood. In this study, we systematically characterized TRM across a large cohort of clinical specimens to map their composition across normal tissue, polyps, and CRC. Our findings reveal distinct microbial profiles between tissue types, establishing a foundational framework for understanding the microbiota profiles associated with colorectal carcinogenesis and providing a basis for future mechanistic investigations.

Our cross-sectional analysis revealed a clear difference in TRM composition among normal mucosa, polyp, and CRC tissues. The increasing abundance of known pro-tumorigenic bacteria such as Fusobacterium31,32, Streptococcus33,34, and Peptostreptococcus35,36, coupled with depletion of potentially protective commensals including Collinsella37 and Akkermansia38,39, highlights the microbial changes that occur as tissue evolves from normal mucosa, precancerous polyps, and invasive carcinoma. These group-specific signatures, further validated through FISH, indicate consistent associations between TRM composition and histological category, and are compatible with proposed mechanisms involving immune modulation and epithelial barrier disruption40–42.

In contrast, TRM composition showed little variation across established tumor stages or clinical subgroups, including tumor location, differentiation status, and microsatellite instability. This pattern suggests that the variability in TRM profiles is more strongly associated with diagnostic tissue categories than with heterogeneity among clinically defined CRC subgroups. Consistent with this interpretation, we did not observe systematic shifts in microbial composition across later-stage disease strata or survival-related variables. Collectively, these findings support an association between TRM composition and tissue type, rather than stage-dependent variation within established colorectal cancer.

Although our machine learning models demonstrated strong classification performance within our cohort, the purpose of this analysis is not to propose TRM profiling as a diagnostic tool, as it requires tissue specimens and cannot be performed non-invasively. Rather, these results demonstrate the consistency and robustness of TRM patterns, which could serve as a useful adjunct for risk stratification in contexts where tissue specimens are already available, such as resected polyps or biopsy samples. In such settings, TRM characterization could potentially complement histopathological assessment, offering additional insights into the biological risk of CRC and informing follow-up or surveillance strategies. A key direction for future research is to translate these tissue-derived TRM signatures into clinically actionable tools. One possibility is to identify correlated, measurable microbial or microbial-derived signals in non-invasive samples such as stool or blood, enabling TRM-informed assessments without requiring tissue biopsies. Another important avenue is to integrate TRM signatures with established clinicopathological features, such as tumor differentiation grade, lymph node status, or molecular markers, to build composite models that extend beyond morphology alone. By linking TRM alterations to luminal or systemic indicators and embedding them within broader diagnostic frameworks, future work could bridge tissue-level microbial biology with clinically practical screening or risk-stratification strategies, thereby adding value to current diagnostic practice rather than replacing it.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and longitudinal validation is required to confirm temporal microbial shifts. Additionally, although our cohort was relatively large and included external validation, further replication across diverse populations and sequencing platforms will strengthen the generalizability of the findings. Finally, functional insights remain limited, as the current study primarily establishes descriptive and correlative relationships. Integrated multi-omics analyses, combining metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, and metabolomics, will be critical for uncovering the molecular mechanisms through which TRM influence early colorectal tumorigenesis.

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive cross-sectional delineation of TRM differences among normal mucosa, precancerous polyps, and colorectal carcinoma, establishing a foundation for future mechanistic investigations into the microbiota-host interface in CRC development. While not intended for diagnostic application, TRM analysis represents a promising adjunct approach for risk stratification in tissue-based assessments and a valuable entry point for developing non-invasive biomarkers and microbiome-targeted strategies for colorectal cancer prevention.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital (SRRSH) (Approval No. IRB-2022-0423). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to the collection and analysis of their samples.

Human samples collection

This retrospective cohort study utilized samples and data identified by systematically querying the SRRSH Biobank and clinical archives for patients who underwent colonoscopy or surgical resection between January 2017 and January 2018. Participants were categorized into three groups based on archived histopathological reports: CRC, polyp, and normal controls. The polyp group encompassed various pathological types, including adenomatous and non-adenomatous polyps.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The following criteria were applied to select the final study cohort:

Inclusion Criteria: Availability of a definitive histopathological diagnosis of CRC, polyp (any type), or normal mucosa; age between 18 and 90 years at the time of procedure; no documented history of anticancer therapy prior to sample acquisition; availability of high-quality, frozen tissue specimens in the Biobank; and completeness of essential clinical and pathological data.

Exclusion Criteria: Documented use of antibiotics, prebiotics, or probiotics within one month before the procedure; co-diagnosis of other gastrointestinal diseases (e.g., inflammatory bowel disease); prior history of colorectal resection; and insufficient tissue quality or missing critical clinical data.

Sample Collection, Quality Control and Processing

Normal, polyp, and CRC samples were collected from different individuals, meeting the above criteria. Among these samples, there are 55 paired normal and CRC tissues obtained from the same patients. Fresh human tissues were collected in the sterile surgery room from the medical centers of SRRSH. Fresh normal intestinal tissues (located at least 10 cm away from the colorectal cancer lesion), polyp tissues, and colorectal tumor tissues were immediately transferred to germ-free 15 ml centrifuge tubes containing sterile DMEM culture medium. The −80 °C frozen human tissue was obtained from the biobank of SRRSH. Each tissue sample was rinsed three times with PBS, each rinse lasting 5 minutes. Then, using autoclaved forceps and sterile blades, the surface tissue was dissected off, and the central core (about 200 mg) was retained for subsequent DNA extraction.

To minimize environmental contamination, the core regions of each tissue sample were carefully dissected prior to DNA extraction. Bacterial load across different tissue types was quantified by qPCR targeting the 16S rRNA gene. While all environmental control samples fell well below the threshold of 10³ CFU/g (as established by standard curve analysis) (Fig. S13), all examined tissue samples significantly exceeded this level. Consequently, samples below this threshold were excluded from subsequent analysis.

TRM sample preparation

There are three major challenges that affect bacterial DNA extraction: the presence of an enormous host genome, release of bacterial DNA, and contamination in reagents. To obtain high-quality bacterial DNA, we tested different DNA extraction kits, optimized the amount of tissue, and employed bead shearing techniques. However, contamination in reagents is difficult to eliminate completely. Therefore, we established stringent environmental controls that underwent the exact same procedure as the samples, allowing us to generate a comprehensive contamination landscape. To determine whether to eliminate the host cell genome, we tested two DNA extraction kits: the QIAamp DNA Microbiome kit (QIAGEN #51704), which can deplete host DNA, and the QIAamp PowerFecal (pro) DNA kit (QIAGEN #51804). All tested kits included a bead-beating step, which was performed in a TissueLyser II (QIAGEN, 85300) for 10 minutes at 30 Hz. The bacterial DNA quantity decreased without the bead-beating step. The QIAamp DNA Microbiome kit contains steps to lyse host cells with buffer AHL and Benzonase to digest host DNA. However, these additional steps consistently decreased the sample yield for tissues, resulting in lower sensitivity of qPCR quantification and frequent ambiguous results. Therefore, we decided to use the QIAamp PowerFecal (pro) DNA kit, which does not include a host genome elimination step, for subsequent bacterial DNA extraction.

For tissue processing, we determined that 150 mg of tissue should be used for DNA extraction, as larger amounts would decrease extraction efficiency due to the limited binding capacity of the spin column. Tumor tissue samples were ground in 1 ml sterile PBS. After centrifugation at full speed for 10 minutes, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was either extracted or frozen at −80 °C.

To set up controls, as contamination in reagents is difficult to eliminate completely, we established appropriate negative controls. We used PBS as a tissue surrogate to undergo the same processing steps as tissue samples. The resulting DNA product served as the environmental bacteria control (EC). Additionally, we used DEPC-treated water as the no-template control (NTC) when running the qPCR quantification assay.

qPCR quantification

YEASEN Hieff qPCR reaction mixture was used for bacterial quantification. The V9 region of the 16S ribosomal RNA was amplified using the following primers: 1369 Forward 5’-CGGTGAATACGTTCYCGG-3’, 1492 Reverse 5’-GGWTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3’. To calculate bacterial DNA concentration in the samples, we generated a standard curve using Escherichia coli DNA. No-template controls (NTCs) were included in all reactions. The qPCR program consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 10 minutes, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 15 seconds and 60 °C for 60 seconds. Raw threshold cycle (Ct) values were normalized against the E. coli DNA standard curve for accurate quantification.

16S library construction

To increase the recovery efficiency of PCR products in the first step during library preparation, the overhang of the amplification primer was modified by 5’ biotin. After the first-round biotin PCR, Dynabeads MyOneTM Streptavidin C1 Beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #5002) were used to pull down the biotin PCR products following the manufacturer’s instructions. The Streptavidin beads containing the first round PCR product were then directly subjected to the second round index PCR. Specifically, dual indices from TruePrep® Index Kit V3 for Illumina® (5ul for each index, Vazyme #TD203) were added to target amplicons in a second round of PCR using 23 Kapa HiFi Hotstart ReadyMix DNA polymerase. Then the Ampure XP beads (Beckman, A63881 AMPure XP) were added to clean up the final libraries, and the quality of these purified libraries was checked by Fragment Analyzer-12/96 (GENE-QC006). Finally, samples were sequenced on the Novaseq with 2×250 bp paired-end reads (Novogene, Tianjin).

For tumor-resident microbiota library construction, we used PBS that underwent all the sample preparation procedures as an environmental negative control and DEPC-treated water as no template control, as well as E. coli as the positive control. The V4 hypervariable region of 16S was amplified with biotinylated primer set 515 F (0.4uM, 5¢- TCGTCGGCAGCGTC AGATGTGTATAAGAGA CAGGTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA −3¢) and 805R (0.4uM, 5¢- GTCTCGTGGGCTCGG AGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAT-3¢), each with overhang adapter sequences (IDT), using 23 Kapa HiFi Hotstart ReadyMix DNA polymerase (Kapa Biosystems). Amplification was performed with an initial heating step of 95 °C for 3 minutes, 25 cycles of 30 seconds at 95 °C, 30 seconds at 55 °C, 30 seconds at 72 °C, and final extension of 72°C for 5 minutes. The PCR cycles were optimized to 30 cycles. PCR products were then purified and enriched by Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin C1 Beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #65002) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Biotin beads containing the first round PCR products were directly subjected to the second round index PCR. Dual indices from TruePrep® Index Kit V3 for Illumina® (5ul for each index, Vazyme #TD203) were added to target amplicons in a second PCR using 23 Kapa HiFi Hotstart ReadyMix DNA polymerase (25ul). PCR conditions were 3 minutes at 95 °C, with 8 cycles of 30 seconds at 95 °C, 30 seconds at 55 °C, 30 seconds at 72 °C, and final extension of 5 minutes at 72 °C. Libraries were purified by the AMPure XP (0.8X) bead before quantification by using the Qubit DNA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and qPCR, or quality check by Fragment Analyzer-12/96 (GENE-QC006). Negative controls, including no template controls and environmental controls, were included in all sequencing runs. Samples were sequenced on the Novaseq with 2 × 250 bp paired-end reads (Novogene, Tianjin).

Microbiome data analysis

Processing amplicon sequencing data

The sequencing data were split using de-multiplexing tools bcl2fastq2 (double check with the sequencing service company for the de-multiplexing method they used) to generate fastq format files for every sample using barcode sequences. All reads were analyzed using a standardized amplicon bioinformatics pipeline using vsearch v2.14.243 and usearch v1044. First, paired-end reads were merged using “vsearch-fastq_mergepairs” with default parameters. Then, adaptor trimming and low-quality read removal were carried out by “vsearch-fastx_filter” with a maximum expected error rate of 0.01. After quality control filtering, reads were processed. Amplicon templates were constructed using the quality control-passed reads. Reads were dereplicated using “vsearch-derep_fulllength” with singleton reads discarded. All remaining unique sequences were denoised by the unoise3 method to get candidate sequence features, which balances between resolution and specificit45. Lastly, chimeric features were removed using “vsearchuchime_ref” against the SILVA reference release123.

The abundance table was generated by mapping all quality control passing reads onto the template sequence features using “vsearch-usearch_global” with an identity threshold of 0.97. Sequence features were classified by “vsearch-sintax” with a cutoff set to 0.6. Non-specific sequence features from eukaryote, and mitochondria were filtered out. Only samples with enough prokaryote-specific reads were kept using the cutoff of 1000 for human samples, respectively. All kept samples were normalized into the same number of reads for a fair comparison (1000 reads for human) using the “rrarefy” function of R package vegan (v2.6-4)46.

Beta diversity analysis

Beta diversity analysis was performed to assess compositional differences between microbial communities at the genus level. PCoA and Non-Metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) were implemented using the vegan package (v2.6-4) in R (v4.3.1) to visualize multivariate patterns in community structure. Both methods utilized Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrices calculated from genus-level abundance profiles. While PCoA preserves original distance relationships through eigen decomposition47, NMDS optimizes the stress function to represent rank-order distances in reduced dimensions48, providing complementary perspectives on community dissimilarity.

Statistical significance of group differences was quantified using PERMANOVA through the adonis2 function (vegan package) with 999 permutations. This non-parametric multivariate analysis of variance 03 tests centroid dispersion and compositional differences between predefined groups while accounting for potential covariates49. Model construction followed sequential (Type I) sums of squares to control for confounding variables. Both visualization approaches and PERMANOVA outcomes were interpreted synergistically to characterize microbial community variation across experimental conditions.

Alpha diversity analysis

Alpha diversity analysis was conducted to evaluate within-sample microbial diversity across taxonomic hierarchies (family, genus, and species levels). The Shannon index (quantifying community richness and evenness) and Simpson index (emphasizing dominant taxa proportions) were computed using the diversity function in the vegan package (v2.6-4)50. Statistical comparisons between experimental groups were performed through pairwise Student’s t-tests with Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction for multiple comparisons, implemented via the pairwise.t.test function in the base R stats package51. This parametric approach was selected after confirming normality assumptions using Shapiro-Wilk tests and homogeneity of variance via Levene’s test. Results were visualized through boxplots annotated with significance levels (p < 0.05, p < 0.01, p < 0.001) to highlight inter-group diversity differences across taxonomic resolutions.

Discovery of microbial taxa differentially abundant among the three groups

Microbial biomarker discovery was performed using Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size LEfSe through the microeco package (v0.11.0) in R52. The analysis identified phylogenetically discriminative features between normal tissues and CRC tumor microbiomes using a three-stage approach: (1) Kruskal-Wallis tests53 detected genus-level abundance differences across groups (α = 0.25), (2) pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests54 confirmed subgroup consistency, and (3) LDA quantified effect sizes (log10 LDA score > 2.0). Low-abundance features were filtered (prevalence threshold: 5% across samples via filter_thres = 0.05) prior to analysis. The top 20 discriminative taxa with the highest LDA scores were selected for visualization, annotated with their relative abundance distributions (Fig. 1F, G). Multiple testing correction was applied using the BH procedure55 during feature selection.

We next sought to identify microbial genera that exhibit a significant trend in abundance across the normal, polyp, and tumor. To achieve this, we performed a Cochran-Armitage trend test56. First, for each genus, we converted its relative abundance in every sample into a binary variable (e.g., high vs. low) based on a quantile threshold calculated from its distribution across all samples. The Cochran-Armitage test was then applied to this binary data to assess the statistical significance of any monotonic trend across the three groups by the R package DescTools (v 0.99.60). The resulting p-values were corrected for multiple hypothesis testing using the BH method.

Composition and distribution of dominant bacterial communities

We constructed stacked bar plots to illustrate the relative mean abundance of microbial communities across different groups, including the environmental group, fecal group, normal tissue group, polyp tissue group, and CRC patient group (Fig. 3C, D). The analysis focused on the top 19 most abundant taxa at the family and genus levels, while the remaining taxa were collectively categorized as “others.” For each group, we calculated the proportion of the mean abundance contributed by these selected taxa. Similarly, we presented the proportional distribution of dominant genera in our TRM dataset and in fecal data from public databases at the genus level.

Microbial signature profiling was conducted to identify genus-level abundance patterns associated with clinical phenotypes. The top 30 most abundant genera across all samples were selected based on cumulative relative abundance. Group-specific mean values of log10(x + 1)-transformed abundances were calculated for comparative groups: 1) normal tissues vs. CRC tumors, and 2) CRC subgroups stratified by clinical characteristics (e.g., TNM stage, histological grade). Visualization was achieved using the pheatmap package (v1.0.12)57 with hierarchical clustering (Euclidean distance, complete linkage) applied to both rows (genera) and columns (clinical groups). Color gradients were scaled by Z-score normalization within genera to emphasize relative abundance variations.

Differential analysis between the TRM and the fecal microbiota dataset

A total of seven publicly available cohorts from the NCBI database were aggregated as our reference fecal microbiota dataset. The potential batch effects across diverse public datasets and our own cohort were reduced by the adjust_batch function implemented in the R package MMUPHin (v1.4.2)58. We performed a detailed analysis to investigate the relationship between the cancer-associated microbial signals in the fecal and tissue datasets. We conducted parallel differential abundance analyses using ANCOM (R package ANCOMBC, v2.4.0)59 on both datasets to identify taxa significantly associated with CRC in each environment. To formally test the relationship between these signals, we generated a scatter plot of the p-values from the tissue analysis versus the p-values from the fecal analysis for these taxa. The Spearman correlation test was used to confirm this observation.

Multivariate analysis of microbiota and patient survival

To control for potential clinical confounders that may influence patient survival outcomes, we conducted a multivariate analysis. This analysis was adjusted for the following covariates: patient age, gender, tumor stage (I–IV), tumor location (left or right), differentiation grade (MA, PD, MD, HD), and chemotherapy status (Yes or No). The patients’ ages were divided into Young(≤60) and Senior (>60).

To evaluate the association between microbial features and survival outcomes, we primarily employed Cox proportional hazards models for each individual bacterial on genus-level, adjusting for all listed clinical covariates. As a complementary analysis, we also evaluated the 5-year survival outcome using multivariate logistic regression. The resulting p-values were adjusted by the BH method.

We then proceeded to a community-level analysis. First, a Lasso-Cox proportional hazards model (R package glmnet, v4.1.10) was employed for feature selection to identify a combination of microbial and clinical variables most strongly associated with survival. These selected features were then used to build a Cox proportional hazards model. The p-values and Hazard Ratios with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals were shown in a forest plot.

We also built a multivariate logistic regression model based on the features selected by Lasso for the 5-year survival outcome. The regression coefficients, standard errors, p-values, and Odds Ratios (OR) with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals were shown in a forest plot. Next, a random forest model was employed for potential non-linear and high-dimensional effects. After partitioning the data into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets, we first built a baseline model using only demographic, clinical, and treatment variables. Another model was then constructed by adding the full microbial data to demonstrate its impact on the model.

This normalization strategy addresses compositional bias by scaling counts to the median cumulative sum of features up to the specified quantile60. Compared to rarefaction, CSS preserves quantitative relationships while mitigating sequencing depth artifacts, as demonstrated in sparse microbial datasets61. The final normalized matrix was used as input for downstream machine learning pipelines.

Random forest modeling based on the microbiome sequencing data at the family and genus levels was performed to address various classification tasks using default parameters. Initially, the cohorts were randomly divided into a discovery set (80%) and a validation set (20%). The discovery cohort comprised 182 normal, 68 polyp, and 568 CRC samples, whereas the validation cohort included 45 normal, 17 polyp, and 142 CRC samples. The discovery data were used for model training, the validation data for model validation, and an independent external cohort from another hospital served as the test set for final model evaluation.

For each classification problem, models were built using the caret62 and randomForest63 packages in R. The training set underwent three repetitions of 5-fold cross-validation to optimize the model performance, and the resultant models were subsequently validated on the test set. In the binary classification scenario, the AUC was computed from both the cross-validation results on the training set and from the independent testing set, with ROC curves being plotted for visual assessment. For the three-class classification problem involving normal tissue, polyp tissue, and tumor tissue, class-specific metrics—including accuracy, recall, and F1 score—were calculated on both training and testing sets using the MLmetrics package. Additionally, a confusion matrix detailing the model’s prediction outcomes on the test set was generated.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Asterisks in the figures indicate the level of statistical significance (*p-value < 0.05, **p-value < 0.01, ***p-value < 0.001, and ****p-value < 0.0001) as determined using either a two-tailed unpaired Student t test or a Mann-Whitney test as defined in figure captions. Tests were performed using GraphPad Prism software (Version 8, Graphpad Software, La Jolla, CA, United States). Data are expressed as mean ± SEM unless otherwise stated. P-values after FDR corrections are reported as q-values.

Statistics & reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. The sample size was determined by the availability of eligible samples in the Biobank that met the inclusion criteria. Data were excluded based on pre-established clinical and technical criteria as detailed in the “Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria” section above. No other data were excluded from the analyses arbitrarily. The experiments were not randomized. Participants were enrolled based on their diagnosis (CRC, polyp, or normal). The Investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment, as group information was necessary for the supervised comparative analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1).
