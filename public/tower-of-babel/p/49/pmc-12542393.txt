Cross-sectional analysis of tuberculosis burden and risk factors in Swabi district, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan
Authors: Iqbal Ahmad, Madiha Fatima, Ilyas Khan, Sulaiman Bahadar, Abdul Qadeer, Mohamad S. Hakim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Tuberculosis (TB) remains a serious public health concern in Pakistan, where it is considered endemic. Understanding the disease burden and its associated risk factors, particularly in an understudied population, is crucial for effective disease control. In this cross-sectional, hospital-based study, TB-suspected patients who visited the TB control centre of Swabi district, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan, during January 2023 to June 2024 were included. The aims and objectives of the study were to assess the frequency of TB and identify its associated risk factors and zoonotic TB within a population with limited access to healthcare services. After ethical approval, sociodemographic and clinical data were collected from individuals suspected of having TB, and statistical analyses were performed using the Chi-square test. A total of 1,164 individuals with suspected TB were included in our study. After microscopic and culture analysis, 232 were confirmed positive, comprising 118 males and 114 females. Of these, 218 individuals were infected by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and 14 were infected by M. bovis. Furthermore, 10 cases were identified as multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB), 13 were reinfections, and 12 were extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB) cases. The TB incidence was comparable across genders and different age groups; it was higher among people with comorbidities, low socioeconomic status, raising animals, and a smoking history. Notably, the detection of M. bovis in human cases and the association between animal presence and TB highlight the importance of incorporating the One Health approach strategies into TB control programs for effective control.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40794-025-00270-3.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global health issue. Pulmonary TB, the most common form, primarily affects the lungs and is caused by M. tuberculosis. It can also infect other vital organs such as bones, joints, lymph nodes, pleura, urogenital tract, and meninges, a form known as extra-pulmonary TB [1]. Besides M. tuberculosis, other mycobacterial species, such as M. bovis, M. africanum, and M. canetti, can cause TB [2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) report for 2024, there were an estimated 10.8 million new cases in 2023, representing a slight increase from 10.7 million in 2022. In 2023, TB caused 1.25 million deaths worldwide, and among them, 1.09 million were in HIV-negative people. Among the total TB cases, about 1.7 million cases are classified as extra-pulmonary TB [3]. In recent decades, extra-pulmonary TB has become increasingly common in high-income countries, including the United States of America [4], the European Union [5], and Brazil [6], accounting for a growing share of total TB cases. Most new cases were from the South-East Asian region (45%), followed by the African region (24%), the Western Pacific region (17%), the Eastern Mediterranean (8.6%), the Americas (3.2%), and Europe (2.1%). Overall, 87% of all TB cases globally were reported from 30 high-burden countries, including populous nations such as China (6.8%), India (26%), Pakistan (6.3%), Bangladesh (3.5%), Nigeria (4.6%), Indonesia (10%), and the Philippines (6.8%) [3].

In Pakistan, TB remains endemic nationwide and poses a serious public health challenge despite being a treatable and preventable disease. The disease causes an estimated 47,000 deaths in 2023, with roughly 0.5 million new infections each year, ranking Pakistan 5th among countries with the highest TB burden [3, 7]. Several factors contribute to the ongoing spread of TB in the country. These include a large population, widespread poverty, and overcrowded living conditions. Inadequate housing, poor ventilation, and high-stress environments, particularly in urban areas, also contribute to the issue. Additionally, limited access to healthcare, malnutrition, coexisting health conditions, and a weakened immune system increase susceptibility to TB. A general lack of education and awareness about TB transmission, prevention, and treatment leads to delays in diagnosis and medical intervention. Furthermore, reliance on traditional or home remedies and an under-resourced healthcare infrastructure significantly contribute to the disease’s occurrence [8–10].

Another critical issue is the rise of multidrug-resistant strains of TB (MDR-TB), caused by the lack of effective control measures. MDR-TB strains, which are resistant to both isoniazid and rifampicin, present a major challenge to TB management [11]. Contributing factors include delayed diagnosis, improper use of antibiotics, person-to-person transmission, inadequate follow-up care, and a lack of social support [12]. Pakistan’s healthcare system also faces additional issues such as a shortage of trained TB personnel, inadequate diagnostic facilities, unreliable drug supply chains, limited funding, and fragmented TB control programs [13]. Moreover, the separation between the public and private healthcare sectors and the lack of coordination between them complicate efforts to control MDR TB. The emergence of drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB) further heightens public health concerns, as it requires more specialized drugs, longer treatment durations, and increased financial resources, adding further pressure on an already overburdened healthcare system [14].

TB can also be transmitted zoonotically. Zoonotic tuberculosis (zTB), primarily caused by M. bovis, a member of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC), is considered an important zoonotic pathogen [15]. M. bovis is transmitted from animals, especially cattle, to humans. The WHO estimated that around 140,000 new zTB cases and 11,400 deaths occurred globally in 2019 due to zTB [16]. However, the actual numbers are likely much higher because of gaps in surveillance and underreporting. The absence of an organized and efficient surveillance system contributes to a limited understanding of zTB [17, 18]. Despite various control efforts, TB remains a persistent and critical public health challenge in Pakistan. Given its complex transmission dynamics involving human, animal, and environmental interfaces, a One Health approach is essential for comprehensive TB control. This integrated strategy will not only help in understanding the zoonotic potential of the disease but also in developing effective, multisectoral interventions to reduce its impact on both human and animal populations. In this study, we aim to identify the incidence and risk factors of rising TB cases, and to detect and highlight the occurrence of zTB. The study will provide a valuable basis for practical strategies to control and treat TB infections.

Materials and methods

Study design

The current cross-sectional hospital-based study aimed to determine the occurrence of TB in the Swabi district, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan, using data from the National TB Control Program Center. Data were collected from patients suspected of having TB who visited the National TB Control Center for medical check-ups between January 2023 and June 2024 using a designed questionnaire. Our study design offers a structured approach, including data collection, diagnosis, and identifying TB frequency and associated risk factors.

Study population

In the current study, patients from different age groups (above 10 years) and both genders, males and females, were included. A total of 1,164 participants (648 males and 516 females) were screened for TB. The inclusion criteria included patient willingness, providing samples, and the ability to answer questions. The exclusion criteria consisted of patients who were reluctant to participate, were under 10 years old, or were unable to provide a sample or answers.

Data and sample collection tools

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews with TB-suspected patients during check-ups and sampling. Socio-demographic information was gathered from patients according to the study design. The patient’s history was documented to confirm reinfection. The clinical diagnosis was based on etiology, chest X-ray, and clinical signs and symptoms (fever, night sweats, chills, weight loss, loss of appetite, and fatigue) [19]. From all patients, sputum samples for pulmonary TB and body fluids for extrapulmonary TB were collected in sterile containers (Joymed, China).

Microbiological, biochemical, and molecular identification

Slides were prepared from the collected samples, and the Ziehl-Neelsen stain (Baso, China) was used for detecting acid-fast bacilli (AFB) [20]. The positive samples for AFB staining were further processed. The sputum or body fluid samples were decontaminated with 2% NaOH and 0.5% N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NALC). The samples were then inoculated onto Lowenstein-Jensen (LJ) media with glycerol or pyruvate as previously reported by Silva et al. [21]. M. tuberculosis and M. bovis were differentiated based on colony morphology. This was further confirmed by biochemical profiling, including nitrate reduction and niacin accumulation tests [22].

Both species were further confirmed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of species-specific regions. DNA was extracted from the cultured samples using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, the Netherlands). The DNA concentration and purity were checked by NanoPhotometer (IMPLEN, Germany). The purified DNA was subjected to PCR using pncA forward and reverse primers (5’ATGCGGGCGTTGATCATCGTC3’) and (5’CGGTGTGCCGGAGAAGCGG3’), respectively, for M. tuberculosis. To amplify the M. bovis-specific region, primers JB 21 (5’TCGTCCGCTGATGCAAGTGC3’) and JB 22 (5’CGTCCGCTGACCTCAAGAAG3’) were used. These primers were previously reported by Rodriguez et al. [23]. The PCR reaction mixture contained 5 µL of 10x PCR buffer, 1 µL each of the primers, 5 µL of dNTPs (5 mM), 3.2 µL of MgCl2, 1 µL of Taq DNA polymerase, 5 µL of DNA, and 29.8 µL of water. The PCR reaction mixture was prepared using a thermal cycler (Mastercycler X40, Eppendorf, Germany). The PCR product was then electrophoresed in a 1.2% gel and visualized through a Gel Doc system (Multi-Gene Labnet International Inc., USA). The M. tuberculosis (H37Rv) and M. bovis BCG 35,734 strains were used as positive control [24].

Drug susceptibility testing

To determine antibiotic resistance, samples were rapidly analyzed using the GeneXpert system with the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra cartridge (Cepheid, USA). Positive samples were then tested for drug susceptibility using Middlebrook 7H10 medium. The procedure followed was previously described by Café Oliveira et al. [25]. The drug concentrations used were rifampicin (RMP) at 1 µg/ml, isoniazid at 0.2 µg/ml, ethambutol at 5.0 µg/ml, and streptomycin at 2.0 µg/ml, as per the WHO guidelines. Pyrazinamide (PZA) sensitivity testing at 25 µg/mL was performed using the BACTEC PZA test medium (Becton Dickinson, Sparks, MD, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. M. tuberculosis H37Rv and 636 strains served as sensitive and MDR control strains. Samples resistant to more than one antibiotic were classified as MDR cases.

Survey about TB awareness

To assess the level of awareness and knowledge about TB among confirmed TB-positive patients, a brief survey with four basic questions was conducted. The questions were included in the questionnaire. These questions aimed to gauge patients’ understanding of TB before their diagnosis, focusing on key aspects such as the causative agent, symptoms, modes of transmission, and preventive measures.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using RStudio version 4.5.0 (Posit, PCB, USA). Chi-square tests were performed to assess the association between various risk factors and TB. Statistical significance was determined using the P-value, with values ≤ 0.05 considered indicative of a significant association.

Results

Gender-wise distribution of TB

A total of 1,164 individuals suspected of having TB were screened during the study period, comprising both male and female participants. The distribution of suspected cases exhibits slightly higher numbers of cases in males compared to females. Out of the total screened individuals, 232 individuals (118 males and 114 females) tested positive for TB. When analyzing the gender-wise spread among the TB-positive patients, the frequency was found to be nearly equal between males (50.86%) and females (49.14%), indicating that the infection affected both genders with comparable frequency. There was no significant difference between males and females (p = 0.23). In our study, we found that most cases were positive for the M. tuberculosis species in both genders, 113 (51.83%) males and 105 (48.17%) females, respectively, with only minor differences in distribution. However, a notable finding arose regarding M. bovis infections. It also highlights the importance of including zoonotic TB surveillance in routine diagnostic protocols, particularly in regions where animal-human pathways are likely to occur. Age-wise distribution of TB-positive patients

The age-wise distribution of TB-positive patients was assessed by dividing the total 232 confirmed cases into four distinct age groups: 11–25 years, 62 (26.72%); 26–40 years, 69 (29.74%); 41–55 years, 52 (22.41%); and 56 years or above, 49 (21.12%). The analysis revealed that the distribution of TB cases was relatively uniform across all age groups, with no significant difference. However, the 26–40 years age group showed a slightly higher proportion of TB cases compared to the others. Conversely, the fewest TB cases were observed in individuals aged 56 years or older. The data suggest some patterns in age-related TB distribution; further analysis of lifestyle, comorbidities, and healthcare-seeking behaviour across these age groups would help better understand the observed trends. Occurrence of reinfection, MDR, and extra-pulmonary TB

Among the total number of TB-positive patients registered in this study, a subset was identified as having complicating factors that pose additional challenges in disease management and treatment response. All samples identified by GeneXpert were at least resistant to rifampicin and isoniazid. A small number of patients had MDR-TB pathogens 10 (4.31%), indicating the presence of strains resistant to at least the first-line anti-TB drugs. Moreover, 13 (5.6%) individuals experienced reinfection, suggesting either incomplete treatment of a previous infection or new exposure to the pathogen. This highlights the potential for ongoing community transmission and emphasizes the need for continued surveillance and follow-up care, even after successful treatment.

Extrapulmonary TB was also recorded in 12 (5.17%), emphasizing the disease’s ability to spread beyond the lungs and affect other organs such as lymph nodes, bones, or the central nervous system. These cases often present diagnostic challenges because of their atypical clinical features and may require more specialized diagnostic tools and treatment approaches. Sociodemographic factors associated with TB infection

Sociodemographic data of all suspected TB patients were recorded. After confirming TB and analysing the data, it was observed that TB infection was significantly higher (p = 0.000) among patients suffering from either diabetes or other illnesses. Furthermore, TB occurrence was higher among those living in muddy houses compared to cemented houses, although this difference was not statistically significant. Work type also showed an association with TB infection; particularly, most individuals involved in various kinds of work do not have specific or permanent jobs. Among farmers, TB rates were higher compared to healthcare workers, students, and teachers (p = 0.006). Additionally, TB cases were comparatively higher in those with lower socioeconomic status than in middle- and upper-class groups. Lifestyle factors such as smoking and the presence of animals in the house were significantly associated with TB (p = 0.000). Current and past smokers showed a notably higher incidance of TB, confirming the well-documented link between tobacco use and respiratory infections. The presence of animals underscores the One Health concept, where close human-animal interactions may facilitate the transmission of zoonotic TB strains, highlighting the need for surveillance and control measures in both human and animal populations. However, analysis revealed that marital status and living location were not significantly associated with TB infection (Table 4).

Survey about TB knowledge among positive patients

A brief survey was conducted to assess the awareness about TB in the infected population. The results reveal a concerning gap in public health awareness. Only a small percentage of patients correctly identified the causative agent (14.65%) of TB, recognized its symptoms (13.79%), or understood how the disease spreads (14.22%). Even more alarming was the lack of awareness regarding preventive practices, with only 5% of respondents reporting knowledge of measures to help prevent the disease’s spread. Most people (76%) were unaware of TB before contracting the disease (Table 5).

Discussion

Tuberculosis has afflicted people for thousands of years. However, it was not until 1882 that Robert Koch identified M. tuberculosis as its causal agent, marking a significant breakthrough in our understanding of the disease. Despite this milestone, progress in unravelling the complexities of TB and its transmission has been slow and incomplete, which is concerning given that it remains the leading cause of death from an infectious disease worldwide [26]. The current study offers valuable insights into the TB occurrence, risk factors, and awareness levels related to tuberculosis in Pakistan, underscoring the urgent need for coordinated, multisectoral strategies—especially within a One Health framework—to address the ongoing and multifaceted burden of this disease effectively.

In the study, we determined the occurrence of TB among 1,164 TB-suspected patients who visited the TB control center in Swabi district, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan. Among them, 648 were males, with 118 cases testing positive for TB, and 516 were females, with 114 TB-positive cases. The positivity of TB among males and females was approximately similar; however, the suspected cases were higher in males. Notably, Pakistan is among the countries where most females stay at home and males go out for work, which increases the risk of catching respiratory infections and other communicable diseases. Therefore, we speculate that the suspected patients were predominantly males. Similar results were previously reported from other parts of Pakistan, where the number of males (116) was higher than that of females (84) [27, 28]. Among the total positive patients (232), the occurance was nearly equal in males and females, at 50.86% and 49.13%, respectively. However, among the 648 male suspected patients, 118 (18.2%) tested positive, while among the 516 females, 114 (22.09%) tested positive. A study by Selvaraju et al. during 2019–2021 also reported approximately 50% occurance in both males and females [29]. Similarly, another study by Tahseen et al. in 2016 yielded consistent results [30]. Our study also aligned with a study conducted in Saudi Arabia and Pakistan [31–33].

Among 232 individuals, 218 cases tested positive for M. tuberculosis, while 14 cases were positive for M. bovis. M. tuberculosis infection was approximately similar across both genders, including males and females. However, it was observed that M. bovis was notably higher in females. This could be due to the presence of animals in the household, as shown in Table 4. In Pakistan, in areas like Swabi, women are responsible for household chores, including caring for animals. Consequently, they have greater exposure to zoonotic pathogens. This may explain the higher number of M. bovis cases in females compared to males. Similar results have been previously reported from another province of Pakistan [34].

In our study, we divided the TB-positive patients into four age groups. However, we did not observe any significant differences across these groups. Studies conducted in various Asian countries also reported similar findings [28, 30, 35, 36]. Furthermore, 13 (5.6%) patients experienced reinfection among the total positive cases. Our findings align with a previous study [34]. A total of 10 (4.31%) patients had MDR-TB, consistent with earlier research [37]. Extrapulmonary TB was found in 12 (5.17%) patients. These cases often present diagnostic challenges because of their atypical clinical features and may require more specialized diagnostic tools and treatment approaches. In our study, the occurrence of extrapulmonary TB was lower compared to previous studies [30].

After analysing sociodemographic factors, we found that a significantly high number of TB patients were either suffering from diabetes (39.65%) or other diseases (34.91%), while 25.43% of patients did not have any other illnesses. Our analysis shows that co-morbidity plays a crucial role in increasing the risk of contracting and developing TB. Co-morbidity might weaken the immune system, or stress conditions may be affecting the body, which can enhance the chances of TB development. Previously, it was reported that comorbidity increases the risk of pulmonary TB [38]. People living in muddy houses (61.20%) were more susceptible to TB than those in cemented houses (38.79%). This may be due to financial status, as most low-income individuals live in muddy houses. Low income can lead to malnutrition, poor hygiene, and inadequate health management. Additionally, dust could be another factor contributing to TB in muddy dwellings, as it may disturb the respiratory system and increase susceptibility. Our findings align with a previously reported study [39]. Similar findings were also reported by Pathak et al. from India [40].

After analysing the data, we also found that the type of work people do is a significant risk factor for TB. People working as farmers or those without a specific job or a permanent position were more vulnerable to TB. Additionally, the TB occurance was higher among individuals with low income (51.72%), followed by middle income (42.24%), and high income (6.03%). Similar findings were previously reported from China as well [41].

The analysis found that marital status and living location (rural and urban) were not associated with TB. Our results are consistent with those of a previous study reported from another part of Pakistan [28, 42]. Smoking was also significantly associated with TB positivity. In individuals who were either past or current smokers, the occurrence of TB was higher compared to nonsmokers. Previously, a study reported that smoking is a high-risk factor for TB in Pakistan [29, 43].

Furthermore, having animals in the house was found to be a significantly associated risk factor for TB. The incidence of TB was higher among people who kept animals at home. Similar findings were reported previously [44]. We also surveyed individuals to assess their knowledge of TB and discovered that 76.72% do not know about TB, which may contribute to the high incidence of TB in Pakistani society. A prior survey conducted in Pakistan also indicated a lack of knowledge about TB among the population, although its questions differed from ours [10]. A study conducted in China found that the population there showed better awareness about TB compared to our study population [45].

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of the current findings include a comprehensive analysis of TB occurrence and associated risk factors. Our data emphasizes the TB burden in remote populations of Pakistan, where healthcare facilities are limited. Additionally, we included a relatively large sample size comprising both male and female individuals from all age groups, socioeconomic statuses, and urban and rural areas, which enhances the generalizability of the results. Moreover, we reported M. bovis cases in the Swabi district for the first time. The inclusion of re-infection, drug resistance, and extrapulmonary TB further strengthens our data by highlighting key health challenges in Pakistan.

Despite its strengths, our study has some limitations. First, our study is hospital-based and conducted in an area with limited healthcare access, which might underestimate the overall TB incidence across the country or a broader region. Second, since our study is cross-sectional, it generally has limited ability to accurately determine the relationship between risk factors and the disease. Finally, including only a limited number of variables may have limited the in-depth analysis necessary to accurately assess the true burden of TB in the population. Based on our findings, future longitudinal studies should investigate risk factors, treatment outcomes, and the impact of TB across different populations. Improving diagnostic capabilities would help ensure more accurate and timely detection, leading to better treatment outcomes and a reduced disease burden. Additionally, awareness programs about TB should be implemented to increase public knowledge and support efforts to control and eliminate the disease.

Conclusions

In conclusion, TB is one of the most infectious diseases in Pakistan. Although much is known about TB epidemiology, no previous study has been reported from the current area. Additionally, very little information is available about TB awareness in the population and its risk factors. Our findings suggest that TB can affect both genders equally, and there is no significant link between TB and a person’s age. However, certain sociodemographic factors, especially low income and smoking habits, show a strong association with TB. Furthermore, zTB cases were also identified, posing serious threats not only to human and animal health but also to the economy at large. Importantly, cases of zTB are transmitted from animals. Our study revealed dangerously low awareness about TB among the population, emphasizing the need for targeted educational initiatives. Countries like Pakistan should adopt the One Health approach, which emphasizes the coordinated surveillance of M. bovis across humans, animals, and the environment to control its transmission effectively. To fight this life-threatening disease, collaboration between the veterinary and medical sectors is essential. Lastly, joint efforts, such as community awareness campaigns, educational seminars, and routine health screenings at the union and village council levels, should be organized to increase public awareness about zoonotic diseases.
