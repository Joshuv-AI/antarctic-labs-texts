Is splenectomy one of the contributory factors to pulmonary hypertension? An analysis of splenectomized hemolytic anemia and immune thrombocytopenia patients
Authors: Zeliha Birsin, Ayşe Salihoğlu, Kardelen Ohtaroğlu Tokdil, Betül Zehra Pirdal, Seçkin Bilgiç, Burçak Kılıçkıran Avcı, Deniz Özmen, Ahmet Emre Eşkazan, Muhlis Cem Ar, Zafer Başlar, Tuğrul Elverdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The development of pulmonary hypertension (PH) after splenectomy is one of the recently controversial issues. This study aims to investigate whether splenectomy itself is an independent risk factor for the development of PH or if the primary contributor to PH development is the underlying condition that necessitated splenectomy. This study was conducted prospectively. We included 21 patients with immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) and 22 with hemolytic anemia. The patients’ symptoms were assessed according to a questionnaire form. Blood tests, including N-terminal pro-B type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and D-dimer levels were done and the 6-minute walk test (6MWT) was performed. PH risk was evaluated using echocardiography (ECHO) and according to the study algorithm, Q-SPECT/CT (perfusion single-photon emission computed tomography/computed tomography) and right heart catheterization (RHC) were performed on selected patients for further assessment. Only one patient in the ITP group was diagnosed as group 2 PH based on ECHO findings and 3 patients with beta thalassemia in the hemolytic anemia group were diagnosed with group 4–5 PH by RHC. There was no statistically significant difference between ECHO-assessed risk for PH in splenectomized patients with hemolytic anemia and ITP (p > 0.05). ECHO risk for PH in the hemolytic anemia patients was found to be low in patients whose hemolysis decreased and transfusion needs disappeared after splenectomy. The results of the study suggest that the development of PH after splenectomy appears to be related to the underlying condition rather than the absence of the spleen.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-025-06583-9.

Introduction

Due to the anatomical and functional characteristics of the spleen, variable short- and long- term complications may occur after splenectomy. Vascular complications after splenectomy, including PH, have been reported [1–3].

PH is a physiopathologic disorder that involves multiple clinical conditions and further complicates most cardiopulmonary diseases. The diagnosis of PH is established when the resting mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) is > 20 mmHg, as determined by RHC [3]. In the 2015 guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and the European Respiratory Society (ERS) for diagnosing and treating PH, splenectomy was included in Group 5 PH and noted as a risk factor for Group 4 PH [4]. However, in the updated 2022 version of the guidelines, splenectomy is no longer listed among the causes within Group 5 [3]. PH developing in the course of chronic hemolytic anemia is classified within the group 5 PH [3, 4]. Furthermore, reports of chronic thromboembolic post-splenectomy PH (CTEPH, group 4) have also surfaced [3, 5, 6].

The pathogenesis of PH and other vascular events after splenectomy is not fully understood and is thought to be multifactorial. After splenectomy, the loss of the spleen’s filtration function can lead to an increase in hypercoagulability due to the accumulation of prothrombotic microparticles (MP) in the circulation which cannot be removed properly [7]. Additionally, in hemolytic anemias such as thalassemia and sickle cell disease (SCD), disruption of membrane asymmetry leads to the formation of abnormal erythrocytes. The absence of spleen function further increases their accumulation, contributing to hypercoagulability [8]. Thrombocytosis or platelet activation after splenectomy is also thought to be a possible cause of a hypercoagulable state, although there is no consensus [9]. These mechanisms may lead to various changes in the pulmonary vascular system that may cause PH through VTE or in situ thrombosis. In addition to the above-mentioned mechanisms, pulmonary vasculopathy that occurs as a result of processes such as anemia, hypoxia, decreased nitric oxide (NO) and increased endothelin-1 caused by hemolysis may also lead to the development of PH in individuals with splenectomy due to hemolytic diseases [10]. In patients without hemolysis, pulmonary vascular system changes occur with increased MP and platelet activation after splenectomy [2].

While the process in patients who underwent splenectomy due to hemolytic causes is better understood, the possible effects of splenectomy on PH in patients without hemolysis have not been clarified.

We evaluated two groups of patients who underwent splenectomy for hemolytic and non-hemolytic causes, with a focus on the development of PH.

Materials and methods

In the study we included 21 patients with ITP and 22 with hemolytic anemia who had been splenectomized and followed up since January 2000 in a single institution. Enrolled patients were planned to have a minimum of two years of follow-up and were analyzed prospectively. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. It was approved by the Istanbul University Cerrahpaşa Institutional Review Board (ID 83088843-604.01.01-74753).

We obtained the demographic characteristics and post-splenectomy clinical parameters of the patients from both their medical records and themselves. The symptoms were systematically assessed using a questionnaire form that was specifically designed for this analysis (see Supplementary File). Following completion of the questionnaire form, patients were evaluated with laboratory tests including peripheral blood smear (PBS), NT-proBNP and D-dimer, ECHO and 6MWT. A diagnostic algorithm was established and patients were directed to undergo Q-SPECT/CT followed by RHC if necessary according to this algorithm summarized in Fig. 1. This algorithm was developed based on the contents of the 2015 ESC/ERS Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Pulmonary Hypertension and the recommendations of the American Thoracic Society (ATS) Sickle Cell Disease Pulmonary Hypertension Committee [4, 11].Fig. 1Study algorithm

Study algorithm

To investigate CTEPH, planar ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) scintigraphy, V/Q SPECT, CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) and Q-SPECT/CT are utilized [3, 12, 13]. For both accurate diagnosis and treatment planning, RHC with pulmonary angiography (PAG) is essential [3, 14]. In the COVID-19 pandemic, ventilation scintigraphy was suspended due to the risk of aerosol contamination, so this method was replaced with Q-SPECT/CT in this study. To maintain study homogeneity, Q-SPECT/CT was used consistently throughout the study.

Echocardiographic evaluation was performed on a Philips ie33 device with an S5-1 probe. Measurements were evaluated according to the ASE Cardiac Chamber Quantification guidelines. Images and calculations were reviewed by two experienced cardiologists.

According to the study algorithm, left/right heart catheterization was performed in patients at high risk for PH. Two experienced cardiologists performed all catheterizations using a Philips Allura Xper FD10 device.

The Siemens Symbia T16 SPECT/CT hybrid imaging device was used for Q-SPECT/CT.'Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC) Q-SPECT/CT criteria’ were used to evaluate the patients [13]. Patient data were interpreted by a single nuclear medicine physician in the department of nuclear medicine who did not have clinical knowledge of the patients.

In the 6MWT, the patient was asked to walk as fast as possible without running and the total distance walked was measured at the end of 6 min. During the walking test, a mobile pulse oximetry device was placed on the patient and SpO2 changes during the test were recorded. In case of intolerance, the test was terminated.

In patients with transfusion-dependent hemolytic anemia, all tests were performed after blood transfusion.

Statistical analysis

SPSS v.21 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk, Q-Q plot and histogram graphs. Normally distributed continuous data were shown as mean (standard deviation) and non-normally distributed data were shown as median (minimum-maximum). Categorical data were reported as frequency and percentage. Independent samples-T test and Mann-Whitney U tests were used for two-group comparisons of continuous data and Kruskal-Wallis test was used for more than two group comparisons. The Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used for categorical data. All tests were two-way and statistical significance was accepted as p < 0.05.

After splenectomy, the need for transfusion disappeared in 6 patients diagnosed with HS, PKD and AIHA. Patients diagnosed with TM continued to receive regular blood transfusions (1–2 units every 3–4 weeks), while patients diagnosed with TI and SCD received intermittent blood transfusions. In the ITP group, only 5 patients needed treatment after splenectomy and eltrombopag was started. There was no statistically significant difference in current age and gender distribution between patients with hemolytic anemia and ITP (p > 0.05). Patients in the chronic hemolytic anemia group were younger at the time of diagnosis and splenectomy and had a longer follow-up period. The median follow-up time after splenectomy was 25 and 9 years in patients with hemolytic anemia and ITP, respectively, with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

There were no cases of perioperative thrombosis (Table S1). None of the patients with ITP had a history of arterial or venous thrombosis after splenectomy. Among patients with hemolytic anemia, 9.1% (n:2, SCD) had a history of arterial thrombosis and 9.1% (n:2, TM) had a history of venous thrombosis. There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in terms of thrombotic events (p:0.108) (Table 1). Only 11.6% (n:5, 4 with hemolytic anemia and 1 with ITP) of all patients in our study described symptoms. Patients reported experiencing fatigue and dyspnea. Dyspnea was classified according to New York Heart Association (NHA) criteria as NHYA I-II in 3 patients (TM, SCD, ITP) and NYHA II-III in 2 patients (TM, TI) (Table 1).

Relationship between ECHO findings, patient characteristics and laboratory parameters

The patients were stratified according to the ECHO findings into two groups: 38 (88.3%) patients had a low risk, while 5 had an intermediate-high risk (3 TM, 1 TI, 1 ITP) for PH. Among ITP patients, no patient was at high risk, only 1 patient was found to be at intermediate risk. There was no statistically significant difference between hemolytic anemia and ITP patients in terms of PH risk by ECHO findings (p:0.345) (Table 2). The analysis of the hemolytic anemia cohort revealed that PH risk in HS, PKD, SCD and AIHA was low according to ECHO. In the thalassemia group, the ECHO risk of three patients with TM and one patient with TI was classified as intermediate-high.

The median time elapsed after splenectomy was 18.5 and 38 years in patients with low and intermediate-high ECHO risk for PH, respectively (p:0.006) (Table 2, Fig. S1).

When the patients with a history of post-splenectomy thrombosis (n = 4) were evaluated, the ECHO risk of 2 patients with SCD with arterial thrombosis was low, while the ECHO risk of 2 patients with TM with a history of venous thrombosis was intermediate-high (Table S2).

The median Hgb value was 8.6 g/dl in patients with intermediate-high risk of PH by ECHO, while it was 12.6 g/dl in the low-risk group. The Hgb value showed a statistically significant difference between both groups (p:0.043) as the WBC, NT- proBNP, ferritin and D-dimer levels (p < 0.005). While none of the patients with TRJV < 2.5 m/s underwent RHC, only 1 patient diagnosed with TM with TRJV 2.5–2.9 m/s underwent RHC and normal pulmonary hemodynamic pressures were obtained. In four patients with TRJV ≥ 3 m/s, RHC was performed in 3 and results were consistent with Group 4–5 PH. RHC was not performed in 1 patient with ITP despite TRJV ≥ 3 m/s. RHC outcomes and additional diagnostic findings

Following the study algorithm, 4 (9.3%) of 43 patients underwent RHC. One patient had normal hemodynamic pressures and 3 patients were diagnosed with group 4–5 PH. None of the ITP patients had an indication for RHC. In the hemolytic anemia group, 3 patients (13.6%) with beta thalassemia were diagnosed with group 4–5 PH by RHC. All of these patients (1 TI, 2 TM) reported dyspnea and had a long interval since splenectomy (38–40 years). Thrombotic events developed following splenectomy in two patients (pulmonary embolism (PE) or deep vein thrombosis (DVT)) and two had persistent thrombocytosis. All were on aspirin therapy. PTE was detected by Q-SPECT/CT and confirmed by PAG in all cases with findings consistent with distal localization. Additionally RHC exhibited precapillary PH, with mPAP ranging from 25 to 39 mmHg and pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) < 15 mmHg. Coronary angiography revealed no significant abnormalities. The characteristics of the three patients are summarized in Table S3.

Two asymptomatic patients (TM) had enlarged right heart chambers with normal NT-proBNP, D-dimer, 6MWT, Q-SPECT/CT and low risk of PH based on ECHO and underwent TEE for further investigation which revealed patent foramen ovale (PFO).

Discussion

Discussions in the literature on splenectomy and PH have generally focused on whether splenectomy itself is a risk factor for PH or the underlying condition. Hemolytic anemia is a common indication for splenectomy and can cause vascular thrombosis and development of PH independent of splenectomy [15]. In splenectomized patients with hemolytic anemia, it is difficult to distinguish the real cause of PH. This issue has not yet been fully elucidated due to the coexistence of risk factors and the lack of prospective studies. On the other hand, several studies have shown an increased risk of PH and thrombosis in splenectomized patients without underlying hemolytic disease [16, 17]. This may imply that the spleen is important in maintaining coagulation homeostasis and its absence is itself a risk factor for thrombosis. However, this view is not yet conclusive. If the absence of the spleen is a risk factor for the development of PH and thrombotic complications, the development of such complications should also be expected in diseases not accompanied by hemolysis (trauma, ITP) or in which hemolysis is greatly reduced after splenectomy (HS, PKD).

PH after splenectomy was commonly defined according to ECHO findings and did not use RHC in the studies [18–20]. Additionally, short follow-up time was a frequent limitation in these studies for evaluating long-term complications such as PH that may occur after splenectomy. In our study we prospectively evaluated PH according to the algorithm we established and followed the patients for at least two years.

The development of CTEPH and idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension (IPAH) after splenectomy in the absence of hemolysis including ITP, trauma and conditions where hemolysis is significantly reduced after splenectomy, such as HS, was first reported by Hoeper et al. [17] in a study on IPAH and then by Jaïs et al. [16] in a study on CTEPH. These data suggest that splenectomy itself is a risk factor for vascular complications even in the absence of hemolysis [2]. However, apart from these studies, various studies have also found that splenectomy does not contribute to PH in the absence of hemolysis [19–21].

In contrast to the studies by Jaïs et al. [16] and Hoeper et al. [17], in our study, PH development was not observed in patients without hemolytic anemia (ITP) and in patients with reduced hemolysis following splenectomy (HS, PKD, AIHA). Although no significant difference could be demonstrated between patients with hemolytic anemia and ITP in terms of PH due to the small number of patients, all three patients diagnosed with PH had been diagnosed with hemolytic anemia and their hemolysis continued after splenectomy. This result may be consistent with the hypothesis that the underlying disease is the most important contributory factor for the development of PH after splenectomy, as suggested in various studies [19, 21]. In particular, Meera et al. reported that in cases where PH developed after splenectomy, PH may also develop without splenectomy [19]. This view is quite compatible with the results of our study. PH development has been reported in thalassemia patients with or without splenectomy [22–24]. Similar to our study, Singer et al. also found that PH was not detected via ECHO in patients who had undergone splenectomy for non-hemolytic causes (ITP and trauma) or in those with reduced hemolysis following splenectomy (HS and PKD). However, an increase in TRJV was observed in splenectomized patients with thalassemia [20].

In the literature, the time to develop PH after splenectomy has been defined in a wide range of 2 to 35 years [16, 17]. Most studies have reported that the development of PH after splenectomy occurs in the long term. In our study, consistent with the literature, the time elapsed after splenectomy was significantly higher in patients who developed PH compared to those patients without PH.

In the study conducted by Derchi and colleagues on patients with thalassemia (n:1306), the majority of the patients (94%) were considered to be at low risk for PH according to TRJV < 3.0 m/s by ECHO. RHC was performed in patients with TRJV > 3.2 m/s (n:33, 31 had PH) and it was found that 2.1% (n:27) of the cases had precapillary PH, while 0.3% (n:4) had postcapillary PH [25]. These data are consistent with the results of our study. In our study, none of the patients with TRJV < 2.5 m/s (n:16) underwent RHC, whereas only one (6.3%) of the patients with TRJV 2.5–2.9 m/s underwent RHC and had normal pulmonary hemodynamic results. In the group with TRJV ≥ 3 m/s (n:4), 3 of the patients underwent RHC and the results were consistent with Group 4–5 PH. As in the study by Derchi and colleagues [25], our patients diagnosed with PH by RHC were found to have precapillary PH. Although these three patients with high ferritin levels (median 2543 ng/ml) may have developed systolic or diastolic dysfunction due to possible myocardial iron accumulation and expected postcapillary PH, hemodynamic studies with RHC did not show evidence of postcapillary PH.

Among five patients presenting with symptoms, group 4–5 PH was identified in three and group 2 PH was detected in one. Therefore, symptom assessment was considered the most important aspect of clinical follow-up in this patient group.

In our study, thrombocytosis did not lead to significant differences in PH risk assessed by ECHO (p > 0.005). One of the patients diagnosed with PH by RHC did not have thrombocytosis at any time after splenectomy. In our study, there were patients who had significant thrombocytosis but had a low risk for PH. We concluded that persistent thrombocytosis after splenectomy may not be an absolute risk factor for the development of PH.

D-dimer, WBC, ferritin and NT-proBNP values were higher in patients with intermediate-high risk of PH by ECHO compared to patients with low risk (p < 0.05). These common laboratory tests can be used in the discrimination of splenectomized patients carrying a high risk for PH.

At this point, patients with thalassemia are already at risk for PH and vascular complications whether they are splenectomized or not. Previous studies have shown an increase in the incidence of PH and other vascular events in hemolytic diseases after splenectomy [20, 22, 26]. When we separately evaluate 12 thalassemia (11 TM, 1 TI) patients among the hemolytic anemia cohort three of them had PH. Splenectomy may have contributed to PH development in this patient group. Since all patients with thalassemia in our study had been splenectomized, there was no chance of comparison with a group without splenectomy.

In our study, two of the three patients with PTE identified with PAG had a history of venous thrombosis, while one patient had no history of previous vascular thrombosis. We thought that asymptomatic VTE or in situ thrombosis might be present in a patient with TI who had PTE with PAG. In the literature patients with PTE in the absence of venous thrombosis are described [16, 27].

Pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA), which is the gold standard in the treatment of CTEPH [3, 14], cannot be performed in cases with distal thrombosis. Previous studies have shown that patients with CTEPH with a history of splenectomy have an increased likelihood of distal thrombotic disease and are therefore not suitable for PEA [5, 6, 16]. In our study, all 3 patients with PTE also had distal thrombosis and were not considered suitable for PEA as reported in the literature. These patients had been treated with oral anticoagulants.

In the studies performed by Hoeper et al. and Jaïs et al. IPAH and CTEPH had been reported in ITP patients [16, 17]. Apart from these, in a retrospective study by Schwartz et al., it was noted that splenectomized ITP patients did not develop any cardiovascular disease or PH [28]. Data from Schwartz’s study were consistent with our results. In 21 ITP patients, the median follow-up period after splenectomy was 9 (2–29) years and no patient developed PH and vascular complications.

We also retrospectively reviewed the medical records of 137 patients diagnosed with ITP and 22 diagnosed with AIHA between January 2018 and December 2024. Among them, ECHO was performed in a subgroup of 29 non-splenectomized ITP and 4 non-splenectomized AIHA patients. This cohort (20 females, 13 males; median age: 47 years [range: 19–81]) consisted of 18 patients with TRJV < 2.5 m/s and 15 patients with TRJV between 2.5 and 2.9 m/s, all indicating a low probability of PH, similar to the splenectomized ITP patient group described in this study, in which only one patient was identified with group 2 PH. Given the small sample size and retrospectively obtained data, conclusions should be interpreted with caution. However, splenectomy does not appear to confer an increased risk of PH in this patient population with ITP. None of the AIHA patients (1 splenectomized and 4 non-splenectomized) had thrombosis or PH.

Two patients in the study were asymptomatic and all laboratory tests were within normal ranges. These two patients who were evaluated for PH had been finally diagnosed with PFO and were treated accordingly. This finding can highlight that different diagnoses can be established during workup for PH.

There are some inherent limitations in this study that should be noted. Due to COVID-19 restrictions, we were unable to expand the study’s patient cohort. Additionally, we could not include healthy individuals who had undergone splenectomy without an underlying pathological condition. The small sample sizes of the subgroups within the hemolytic anemia cohort limited our ability to make further interpretations.

Conclusion

Based on the results of this study, patients who have undergone splenectomy for hemolytic diseases may be evaluated with symptom questionnaire, serial NT-proBNP and D-dimer measurements and ECHO monitoring. In contrast, splenectomy for non-hemolytic diseases probably does not necessitate such an evaluation.

Whether splenectomy independently contributes to the development of PH remains unclear. Prospective studies with higher patient numbers are needed to elucidate the development of PH after splenectomy.
