Epidemiology, drug resistance, and molecular features of Klebsiella pneumoniae in Southwest Shandong, China
Authors: Liang Han, Chengfan Yang, Jian Shi, Lei Du, Jianming Sun, Weixia Li, Shuhua Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aims to delineate the antimicrobial resistance profiles and virulence gene carriage of Klebsiella pneumoniae (KP) in Southwest Shandong region of China, investigate the differences in clinical infection characteristics between classical Klebsiella pneumoniae (cKP) and hypermucoviscous Klebsiella pneumoniae (hmKP), and explore the correlation between hmKP and hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKP). This study collected Klebsiella pneumoniae-infected strains and clinical case data from the Affiliated Hospital of Jining Medical University in Shandong Province, performed isolation, culture, identification, antimicrobial susceptibility testing, serological typing, and virulence gene detection to analyze etiological characteristics, conducted a retrospective analysis of the epidemiological characteristics of infection cases, and analyzed their clinical features. No significant differences were observed between hvKP and cKP groups in age, gender, presence of underlying diseases, or infection categories. However, significant differences were identified in the types of underlying diseases and invasive procedures (P < 0.05). The hvKP group exhibited significantly lower resistance rates to 10 antibiotics (e.g., GEN, CSL) compared to the cKP group, while no differences were observed in resistance to 8 antimicrobial agents (e.g., AMK, IPM) (P < 0.05). In the hvKP group, the detection rates of K1 and K2 serotypes reached 25.00% (15/60) and 36.67% (22/60) respectively, significantly higher than those in the cKP group (12.00% [30/250] and 9.20% [23/250]) (P < 0.05). Additionally, the virulence gene carriage rate in the hvKP group (90.00%) was markedly higher than that in the cKP group (12.80%) (P < 0.05). In this region, KP infections predominantly affect elderly male patients. Patients comorbid with diabetes, hepatobiliary diseases, or those undergoing invasive procedures constitute a high-risk population for hmKP infections. The hmKP strains exhibited significantly lower resistance rates to most antimicrobial agents compared to cKP. hmKP isolates were predominantly of the K1 and K2 serotypes, with the hmKP group demonstrating widespread carriage of multiple virulence genes. An epidemiological correlation was observed between hmKP and hvKP. Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae (KP), a clinically significant Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen, commonly colonizes the human respiratory tract and gastrointestinal tract. It is a major cause of both community-acquired and nosocomial infections, manifesting as pneumonia, bloodstream infections, urinary tract infections, liver abscesses, meningitis, and other systemic infections, with particularly high mortality risk in immunocompromised patients1. In recent years, the indiscriminate use of broad-spectrum antibiotics and increased adoption of invasive medical procedures have driven a marked rise in KP infection rates. Notably, detection rates of hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKP) and carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) have substantially increased. The emergence of hypervirulent variants and multidrug-resistant strains has exacerbated challenges in clinical management, posing significant threats to therapeutic efficacy and public health security2. KP, as one of the ESKAPE (Enterococcus faecium, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter species) pathogens, has garnered significant attention due to its rising antimicrobial resistance. Asia remains a major hotspot for Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR), particularly concerning hvKP. Strains carrying multidrug-resistant (MDR) or hypervirulent determinants exhibit a relatively high overall prevalence in Asian countries, with India reporting the highest rate (27%), followed by Vietnam (16.6%) and Pakistan (15.8%)3.

A subset of KP strains exhibit hypermucoviscosity, allowing classification into cKP and hypermucoviscous Klebsiella pneumoniae (hmKP). While some studies equate hmKP with hvKP, recent advances in hvKP characterization reveal an inconsistent correlation between hypermucoviscosity and hypervirulence, as not all hmKP strains demonstrate elevated pathogenicity. Furthermore, standardized clinical criteria for identifying hvKP strains and associated infections remain lacking. The chromosomal genes encoding the aerobactin (iutA) receptor or iroE are present in many cKP strains. The presence of these genes alone cannot define an isolate as hvKP, nor can the presence of K1 or K2 capsule types. However, hvKP has acquired a large number of virulence genes through large virulence plasmids (e.g., pK2044 and pLVPK) and integrated chromosomal elements (ICE). Recent findings indicate that iroB, iucA, peg-344, rmpA, and rmpA2 are the most accurate molecular markers for defining hvKP, all of which are located on virulence plasmids. These markers represent the most precise and practical methods for identifying hvKP strains4.

We collected KP clinical isolates and conducted a retrospective analysis of their epidemiological distribution, antimicrobial susceptibility profiles, serotype classification, and virulence gene carriage patterns from January to December 2023. This investigation aims to provide evidence-based criteria for the preliminary clinical identification of hvKP.

Preliminary identification of Klebsiella pneumoniae

KP colonies cultured on Columbia blood agar plates for 24 h appeared as large, round, raised, gray-white colonies (Fig. 1), with hmKP exhibiting mucoid characteristics (Fig. 2). On enteric nutrient agar plates, KP formed large, round, raised blue colonies after 24 h of incubation (Fig. 3), while hmKP colonies displayed mucoid morphology (Fig. 4). The string test confirmed the hypermucoviscous phenotype of KP: when touched with an inoculation loop, colonies produced filaments > 5 mm in length, indicating a positive result (Fig. 5). Analysis of virulence gene results

The analysis of virulence gene results revealed significant differences between hmKP and cKP strains. Among 60 hmKP strains, 54 (90.00%) carried at least one of the five virulence genes (peg344, iroB, iucA, prmpA, prmpA2), with iucA being the most prevalent (88.33%, 53/60), followed by peg344 (81.67%, 49/60), prmpA (70.00%, 42/60), iroB (65.00%, 39/60), and prmpA2 (36.67%, 22/60). Notably, 36 strains (60.00%) co-harbored peg344, iroB, and iucA, while 22 strains (36.67%) carried all five genes. Only 6 strains (10.00%) lacked these virulence genes. In contrast, among 250 cKP strains, only 32 (12.80%) tested positive for any of the five genes, with markedly lower prevalence: iucA (7.60%, 19/250), peg344 (5.60%, 14/250), iroB (4.80%, 12/250), prmpA (1.20%, 3/250), and prmpA2 (0.80%, 2/250). Co-occurrence of peg344, iroB, and iucA was observed in 12 cKP strains (4.80%), and only 2 strains (0.80%) carried all five genes. Discussion

KP as a significant nosocomial and community-acquired pathogen, has shown a rising annual incidence of infections. It not only causes severe infections but may also lead to organ damage and life-threatening complications. KP has been widely recognized as a significant pathogen responsible for hospital-acquired infections and healthcare-associated infections. Patients can transmit KP to each other through contact with healthcare workers, medical equipment, or other indirect contact routes, particularly in intensive care units and long-term care facilities where infection risks are elevated. Data from the China Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance Network (CHINET) in 2023 revealed that KP accounted for 14.22% of clinically isolated bacterial strains nationwide, second only to Escherichia coli6.

KP is widely distributed globally, with notable regional and hospital-acquired transmission characteristics. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), hvKP has been detected in at least 16 countries and regions worldwide, including the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Japan, India, and Iran. In Europe, there are over 90,000 cases of KP infections annually, resulting in more than 7000 deaths7. In Asian countries such as Malaysia, Singapore, China, Japan, Thailand, and Vietnam, the colonization rates of KP in the feces of healthy adults are 87.7%, 61.1%, 75.0%, 18.8%, 52.9%, and 41.3%, respectively8.

Infections caused by KP are predominantly opportunistic, commonly affecting diabetic patients, cancer patients, and immunocompromised individuals. However, the emergence of hvKP has expanded susceptibility to populations regardless of immune status. The proportion of hypervirulent strains in community-acquired infections has been increasing annually, and their co-evolution with drug resistance genes could trigger a more severe public health crisis9. A multicenter study across 25 tertiary hospitals in 14 provinces in China revealed that among 230 KP isolates from 10 cities, 37.8% were hvKP, with the highest rate observed in Wuhan at 73.9%10.

In recent years, hvKP has garnered global attention due to its invasive phenotype and rapid transmission capability. Early identification of hvKP infection plays a crucial role in guiding antimicrobial therapy selection and improving patient prognosis.

KP possesses capsular antigens. Based on variations in the capsular antigens, KP is classified into multiple serotypes, with common types including K1, K2, K5, K20, K54, K57, among others. Among these, serotypes K1 and K2 account for the majority of clinical isolates. A study in Zhejiang, China, demonstrated that K1 and K2 serotype strains constituted 23.8% and 42.9%, respectively, of hvKP isolates causing invasive infections11. Research from South Korea indicated that hvKP isolated from urine samples of hospitalized patients is more frequently associated with K1 and K2 serotypes compared to cKP12.

No significant differences were observed in age, gender distribution, or overall prevalence of underlying diseases between hmKP and cKP infected populations, which aligns with the demographic characteristics of hvKP reported in a 2021 U.S. multicenter study13. However, statistically significant differences emerged in both the spectrum of underlying comorbidities and exposure to invasive procedures, suggesting that invasive interventions may serve as critical risk factors for hmKP acquisition. The distinct proportions of hepatobiliary diseases and diabetes mellitus between groups indicate heightened susceptibility to hmKP infection among patients with hepatobiliary disorders or metabolic dysregulation, consistent with findings by Liu et al.14. These trends mechanistically support the hypothesis proposed by Ye et al.15 that “metabolic disorders (e.g., diabetes) and hepatobiliary dysfunction may promote hvKP infection through microenvironmental alterations in the host.” Collectively, these findings suggest that susceptibility to hypervirulent strains appears more closely associated with host immune status, exposure to invasive risk factors, and specific comorbidity profiles rather than purely demographic characteristics.

This study’s antimicrobial susceptibility analysis revealed that hmKP exhibited significantly lower resistance rates to GEN, CSL, CZO, CXM, CRO, CAZ, FEP, ATM, CIP, and SXT compared to cKP (P < 0.05), while no statistically significant differences were observed in resistance to AMK, SAM, TZP, CTT, TOB, IPM, MEM, or LVX between the two groups. Based on these findings, cephalosporins (e.g., third-generation cephalosporins) should be prioritized as empirical treatment when KP infection is confirmed to be hypermucoviscous. The significantly lower resistance rates of hmKP to aminoglycosides, cephalosporins, and fluoroquinolones, coupled with its lower ESBLs positivity, suggest that hypermucoviscous strains may not exhibit widespread enhancement of broad-spectrum resistance phenotypes, which aligns with the perspective of Zhou Junchen et al. Notably, the detection rates of CRKP showed no significant difference between the two groups, indicating that carbapenem resistance mechanisms might be independent of virulence evolution pathways, though vigilance against the transmission risks of such resistant strains remains crucial. Despite the high costs, hospitals in developing countries often cannot access carbapenem antibiotics. However, the overuse of other beta-lactam antibiotics (such as cephalosporins) may lead bacteria to develop resistance mechanisms, such as producing ESBLs. These mechanisms can indirectly contribute to resistance against carbapenem antibiotics. Additionally, inadequate infection control measures in hospitals facilitate the spread of drug-resistant bacteria within healthcare settings. International comparative studies demonstrate that approximately 20% of CRKP strains in the United States are carbapenem-resistant hmKP16. European multicenter data further indicate that while hvKP exhibits slightly lower overall resistance rates, the transmission trends of multidrug-resistant strains remain concerning17. However, the antibiotic resistance rates of hvKP in some less-developed regions deserve attention. Studies in India have shown that isolated hvKP strains exhibited a carbapenem resistance rate of 78%, with multidrug-resistant hvKP strains accounting for 85.7%, which may pose a significant threat to public health18. The Asian region remains a primary hotspot for drug-resistant hvKP strains and requires adequate attention.

This study’s serotype analysis of 310 KP isolates revealed significant differences in capsular serotype distribution between the hypermucoviscous phenotype group (hmKP, n = 60) and the classical phenotype group (cKP, n = 250). The detection rates of K1 and K2 serotypes in the hmKP group reached 25.00% (15/60) and 36.67% (22/60) respectively, significantly higher than the 12.00% (30/250) and 9.20% (23/250) observed in the cKP group (P < 0.05), confirming the strong association of K1/K2 serotypes with hypervirulent phenotypes, potentially linked to coordinated expression of capsular polysaccharide synthesis gene clusters (e.g., rmpA, aerobactin). Notably, global surveillance over the past three decades indicates that K1/K2-type hvKP strains possess more abundant virulence islands and stronger biofilm formation capacity compared to other serotypes19. These characteristics highlight the critical importance of rapid identification and early warning reporting for K1/K2-type KP strains in clinical microbiology laboratories to guide targeted therapy and infection control measures.

This study systematically analyzed the virulence gene profiles of 310 KP isolates (including 60 hmKP and 250 cKP) using multiplex PCR. Results demonstrated that the virulence gene carriage rate in the hmKP group (90.00%) was significantly higher than in the cKP group (12.80%) (P < 0.05). Specifically, the detection rates of metabolic transport gene peg344 (81.67%), siderophore synthesis gene iucA (88.33%), and capsular regulation gene prmpA (70.00%) in the hmKP group markedly exceeded those in the cKP group (5.60%, 7.60%, and 1.20%, respectively). Notably, 60.00% of hmKP strains co-carried peg344, iroB, and iucA, while 36.67% exhibited co-expression of all five virulence genes (peg344, iroB, iucA, prmpA, prmpA2), compared to only 4.80% and 0.80% for these combinations in the cKP group. Odds ratio (OR) analysis quantified the association strength between hmKP/cKP and virulence gene carriage: all tested genes and combinations showed significantly higher positivity in hmKP (P < 0.0001), with OR values ranging from 29.75 to 193.33, indicating that hmKP carries 29- to 193-fold higher risks of harboring these genes compared to cKP. The high co-occurrence rate of multiple genes in hmKP (87.2% of cKP lacked target virulence genes) further highlights distinct virulence profiles between the two groups, strongly supporting that coordinated virulence gene expression constitutes a critical feature of hypermucoviscous phenotype strains.

This study confirmed that peg344, iucA, and prmpA can serve as molecular diagnostic markers for hmKP, though negative results cannot fully exclude hypervirulence risks. The hmKP group universally carried multiple virulence genes, demonstrating a certain correlation between hmKP and hvKP, yet not all hmKP strains qualify as hvKP. Clinically, detection of the hypermucoviscous phenotype in KP is straightforward via the string test, which preliminarily identifies hvKP given the high virulence gene detection rate in hmKP strains. This approach enables early clinical vigilance and guides timely antimicrobial selection.

The limitations of this study lie in the relatively small sample size of hmKP strains, the retrospective design, and reliance on single-center data, which may introduce selection bias and restrict the generalizability of its conclusions to other regions. Research on virulence factors may serve as potential targets for developing novel drugs and vaccines against hvKP, providing innovative approaches and theoretical foundations for prevention and control strategies. In-depth exploration of the mechanisms underlying virulence genes and drug resistance genes may emerge as a critical research focus for future development of new therapeutic agents and interventions to disrupt its transmission pathways.

Conclusions

In this region, KP infections predominantly affect elderly male patients, with cases. Patients comorbid with diabetes, hepatobiliary diseases, or those undergoing invasive procedures constitute a high-risk population for hmKP infections. The hmKP strains exhibited significantly lower resistance rates to most antimicrobial agents compared to cKP. hmKP isolates were predominantly of the K1 and K2 serotypes, with the hmKP group demonstrating widespread carriage of multiple virulence genes. An epidemiological correlation was observed between hmKP and hvKP. Clinically, the hypermucoviscosity phenotype may serve as a preliminary indicator for assessing bacterial virulence.

Methods

Strain sources

A total of 310 cases (strains) of KP infections and corresponding clinical isolates were collected from the Affiliated Hospital of Jining Medical College in Shandong Province between January 1, 2023, and December 31, 2023. All collected strains were stored in a −80 °C ultra-low temperature freezer for preservation. Exclusion criteria included: (1) Removal of duplicate strains from the same anatomical site in the same patient; (2) For specimens from different anatomical sites (e.g., cerebrospinal fluid, blood) collected from the same patient during the same period, only one strain was selected for study.The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Affiliated Hospital of Jining Medical University (2025-03-C011). All participants provided written informed consent before participation. The study was performed in accordance with approved guidelines including the Declaration of Helsinki.

Bacterial culture and staining

According to the 4th edition of National Clinical Laboratory Operating Procedures, the thawed bacterial strains were streaked onto Columbia Blood Agar Medium and Enteric Bacteria Agar Medium using a quadrant streaking method. The cultures were incubated at 35 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h. Bacterial growth was observed, and the hypermucoviscous phenotype was identified via the “string test”: A bacterial loop was used to lift a colony, and the formation of a viscous string > 5 mm in length confirmed the hypermucoviscous phenotype.

Bacterial identification

Bacterial identification was performed using the Vitek-2 Compact automated bacterial identification system (NH card) and confirmed with the automated rapid microbial mass spectrometry detection system (VITEK-MS).

Extended spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) detection

ESBLs detection was performed according to recommended screening and confirmatory methods: Ceftazidime (CAZ, 30 μg), Cefotaxime (CTX, 30 μg), Ceftazidime/Clavulanic Acid (CCV, 30 μg), and Cefotaxime/Clavulanic Acid (CTC, 30 μg) discs were placed on MH Agar Medium (Baibo Biotech, Jinan). After 16–18 h of incubation at 35 °C, inhibition zone diameters were measured. ESBL production was confirmed if the clavulanic acid combination discs (CCV/CTC) demonstrated ≥ 5 mm larger inhibition zones than their corresponding cephalosporin discs (CAZ/CTX) alone.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted according to the MIC method recommended by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines, supplemented by the Kirby–Bauer (K–B) disk diffusion method. Quality control strains included Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922, Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700,603, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29,213, and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25,923, all provided by the National Center for Clinical Laboratories (NCCL), National Health Commission.

Analysis of serological typing results

Molecular serotyping of KP was performed via multiplex PCR according to the method described by Compain et al.20, with the following key steps: DNA extraction was conducted using a bacterial DNA extraction kit following the manufacturer’s protocol, and DNA concentration and purity (A260/A280 ratio 1.8–2.2) were measured using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer, followed by storage at -20 °C; multiplex PCR amplification targeting serotype-specific genes K1 and K2 was performed in a 50 μL reaction system containing 25 μL 2 × Taq Master Mix, 1 μL each of K1/K2 forward and reverse primers, 2 μL DNA template, and ddH2O to 50 μL; agarose gel electrophoresis was carried out by loading 8 μL PCR products onto a 1.5% agarose gel stained with YeaRed Nucleic Acid Gel Stain under 180 V, 200 mA for 30 min, and gel images were captured using a Gel Image System to determine serotypes based on the presence of predicted band sizes.

Analysis of virulence gene results

Taking practicality and accuracy into account, the detection of five virulence genes (prmpA, prmpA2, peg344, iroB, and iucA)4 in 310 strains of Klebsiella pneumoniae was performed using PCR under the following conditions: a 50 μL reaction system containing 25 μL 2 × Taq Master Mix, 0.5 μL each of forward and reverse primers for prmpA, prmpA2, peg344, iroB, and iucA, 2 μL DNA template, and ddH2O to 50 μL; agarose gel electrophoresis was conducted at 180 V, 200 mA for 30 min, and PCR products were analyzed based on predicted band sizes using a 1.5% agarose gel stained with nucleic acid dye.

Clinical data

A retrospective analysis of Klebsiella pneumoniae infection cases and corresponding clinical isolates from January 1, 2023, to December 31, 2023, in our hospital was conducted to evaluate their epidemiological characteristics. Informed consent for participation in this study has been obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians.
