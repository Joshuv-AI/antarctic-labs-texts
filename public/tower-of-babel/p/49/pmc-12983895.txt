Psychological and emotional outcomes after bariatric surgery: a cross-sectional comparison of sleeve gastrectomy and gastric bypass patients
Authors: Arsalan Tariq, Muhammad Ammar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to assess depressive and anxiety symptoms after bariatric surgery and to identify clinical, socioeconomic and psychosocial factors associated with postoperative psychological outcomes.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 300 adults who had undergone bariatric surgery at least 12 months previously (172 sleeve gastrectomy and 128 gastric bypass). Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and anxiety symptoms using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7). Psychosocial factors were assessed using the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES), Body Shape Questionnaire (BSQ), Emotional Eating Scale (EES) and Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS). Socioeconomic status, psychotropic medication use and percentage of total weight loss (%TWL) were recorded. Group comparisons, correlation analyses and multivariable linear regression were performed.ResultsPatients who underwent gastric bypass reported significantly higher depressive and anxiety symptoms compared with sleeve gastrectomy patients (PHQ-9: 9.7±4.2 vs 7.3±3.7; GAD-7: 8.5±4.0 vs 6.5±3.9; both p<0.01). Greater %TWL was inversely associated with depressive (r = −0.29, p<0.001) and anxiety symptoms (r = −0.24, p<0.001). Participants with struggling socioeconomic status had higher PHQ-9 scores than those with comfortable status (10.4±4.5 vs 7.1±3.4; Cohen’s d=0.73), and psychotropic medication users reported greater symptom severity than non-users (PHQ-9: 10.8±4.3 vs 7.3±3.6; p<0.001). Depressive and anxiety symptoms were moderately correlated with lower self-esteem (RSES), greater body image dissatisfaction (BSQ), higher emotional eating (EES) and lower perceived social support (MSPSS) (all |r|=0.30–0.55). In multivariable regression, surgery type, %TWL, socioeconomic status and psychotropic medication use independently predicted depressive symptoms, with the model explaining approximately 33% of the variance.ConclusionsBariatric surgery affects physical and mental health. Gastric bypass patients report more depression and anxiety, sleeve gastrectomy boosts self-esteem, and weight loss improves well-being, though social and medication factors raise psychological risk.

Introduction

Obesity is a complex and multifactorial condition that significantly impacts both physical and mental health. The global prevalence of obesity has reached alarming levels, with the WHO estimating that “over 650 million adults worldwide are people with obesity”.1 This condition is associated with various comorbidities, including cardiovascular diseases, diabetes and certain cancers. Beyond the physical health implications, obesity is also linked to mental health disorders, particularly depression and anxiety.2

Bariatric surgery, encompassing procedures like sleeve gastrectomy (SG) and gastric bypass (GB), has become a standard treatment for severe obesity. These surgeries aim to induce significant weight loss and improve obesity-related comorbidities. However, the impact of these surgical interventions on mental health outcomes, particularly depressive and anxiety symptoms, remains a subject of ongoing research.

Previous studies have reported inconsistent effects of bariatric surgery on postoperative mental health, particularly depressive and anxiety symptoms. A systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that approximately 23% of patients undergoing bariatric surgery reported a current mood disorder, with depression being the most common. 3Additionally, research has shown that depressive symptoms can improve following bariatric surgery. For instance, a study found that bariatric surgery was associated with significant reductions in the prevalence and severity of depressive and anxiety symptoms among individuals with obesity.4

However, the trajectory of mental health improvements post-surgery is not uniform. Some studies have observed that depressive symptoms may improve more slowly than anxiety symptoms.5 Furthermore, certain research has reported that depressive and anxiety symptoms did not change significantly following surgery, despite marked weight reduction over a 2-year period.6 These findings underscore the complexity of the relationship between bariatric surgery and mental health outcomes.

While both SG and GB are effective for weight loss, they differ in their surgical approaches and potential impacts on mental health. SG involves the removal of a large portion of the stomach, resulting in a smaller, sleeve-shaped stomach. GB, on the other hand, involves creating a small pouch from the stomach and rerouting a portion of the small intestine. The differences in these procedures may influence postoperative outcomes, including mental health.

Comparative studies examining the effects of SG and GB on depressive and anxiety symptoms are limited. Some research suggests that both procedures can lead to improvements in mental health; however, the extent and duration of these improvements may vary between individuals and surgical types.7

Given the varying impacts of bariatric surgery on mental health and the limited comparative studies between sleeve gastrectomy and gastric bypass, this cross-sectional study aimed to assess and compare depressive and anxiety symptoms among patients undergoing these two surgical procedures, and to examine clinical, socioeconomic and psychosocial factors associated with postoperative psychological outcomes. By evaluating these outcomes, the study seeks to provide insights into the psychological effects of SG and GB, thereby informing clinical practice and enhancing postoperative care strategies for bariatric surgery patients.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a cross-sectional observational design to assess differences in depressive and anxiety symptoms among patients who had undergone SG and GB surgery. The study was conducted in Lahore, Pakistan, at two tertiary care hospitals, Fatima Memorial Hospital and Advanced Laparoscopic Surgical Associates (ALSA) Evercare Hospital Lahore Pakistan, both of which have specialised bariatric surgery units. Data were collected between January 2023 and June 2024. These centres were chosen because they maintained comprehensive postoperative databases and conducted regular follow-up assessments, enabling access to detailed demographic, clinical and psychosocial data.

The study population consisted of adults who had undergone primary bariatric surgery. Participants were included if they were aged 25 to 45 years at the time of participation, had undergone either sleeve gastrectomy or gastric bypass at least 12 months before recruitment, and were able to understand the study language and provide written informed consent. All gastric bypass procedures included in this study were Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB). This age range was deliberately chosen to ensure a homogeneous sample in terms of psychological, hormonal and social functioning. Individuals aged 25 to 45 years generally represent a period of psychosocial stability, with relatively fewer developmental or geriatric confounders affecting mood or cognition.8 Moreover, restricting the sample to this range reduced the confounding effects of puberty-related hormonal changes at younger ages and menopausal or age-related hormonal shifts that may influence mood and anxiety patterns.9 This age range captures the most common demographic undergoing bariatric surgery worldwide. Adults aged 25–45 represent the majority of patients with obesity who seek surgical treatment.10

Participants in this age group typically underwent bariatric surgery primarily for medical and psychosocial reasons. Medical reasons included severe obesity (Body Mass Index (BMI) ≥40 kg/m²).11 Psychosocial motivations included body image dissatisfaction, reduced quality of life, weight-related stigma, and impaired occupational or social functioning.3 12 These psychosocial motivations have been described in the existing literature and were not directly assessed among participants in the present study. These combined factors contributed to the decision for surgical intervention when conservative weight loss methods failed to achieve sustained results.

Exclusion criteria included patients who had undergone revisional or emergency bariatric procedures, individuals with severe psychiatric illnesses such as bipolar disorder, schizophrenia or active psychosis, and those with cognitive impairment that precluded reliable completion of self-reported questionnaires. Patients who were admitted to inpatient medical or psychiatric care were also excluded to avoid acute condition bias.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1 to detect a clinically meaningful difference in the prevalence of moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms between the SG and GB groups. A two-tailed test with a significance level of 0.05 and statistical power of 80% was used.

Using the exact test for two independent proportions (Fisher’s exact test) in G*Power, with p₁ = 0.21, p₂ = 0.35, α=0.05, power (1−β) = 0.80, and an allocation ratio (n₂/n₁) = 0.77 to reflect the expected larger SG group, the total required sample size was estimated to be 340 participants.

Accordingly, 192 patients were planned to be included in the SG group and 148 patients in the GB group. This sample size allows sufficient power to detect significant group differences in depression rates while accommodating a modest margin for incomplete data or non-response.

The sample size calculation was based on the prevalence of moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms to ensure adequate power for clinically meaningful group comparisons. However, depressive and anxiety symptoms were analysed primarily as continuous Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scores to enhance statistical sensitivity and maximise power, which is a standard approach in psychosocial and bariatric surgery research. The sample size required to detect differences in categorical outcomes is generally sufficient, and often conservative, for detecting differences in continuous outcomes using parametric analyses and multivariable regression.

Recruitment and sampling

Participants were identified through the hospitals’ bariatric surgery databases and outpatient follow-up clinics. A consecutive sampling strategy was adopted to minimise selection bias, ensuring that every eligible patient attending routine follow-up during the data collection period was invited to participate. Recruitment was stratified by surgery type to maintain comparable group sizes. Each participant received detailed information about the study, and written informed consent was obtained before enrolment.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected using a structured, standardised questionnaire administered either in person during routine clinical follow-up visits or online via a secure, hospital-approved platform. Clinical information such as type of surgery, preoperative and current weight, comorbidities, and medication use was verified through electronic health records to improve data accuracy.

Demographic variables included age, sex, marital status, educational level, employment status and socioeconomic status. Socioeconomic status (SES) was assessed both objectively, through monthly household income, and subjectively, through participants’ perception of their financial situation (categorised as ‘comfortable,’ ‘average,’ or ‘struggling’). Socioeconomic variables were included because lower SES has been associated with increased risk of depressive and anxiety symptoms following bariatric surgery, potentially mediated by reduced access to psychosocial resources and higher stress exposure.3

Clinical data encompassed type of surgery, time since operation, preoperative and current BMI, and percentage of total weight loss (%TWL) calculated as: (preoperative weight − current weight) / preoperative weight×100. Common comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension and dyslipidaemia were also recorded.

Medication history included both psychotropic medications (such as antidepressants, anxiolytics, antipsychotics and mood stabilisers) and non-psychiatric medications (eg, antihypertensives, insulin, thyroid medications and hormonal therapy). For psychotropic drugs, the type, dose, duration and adherence were documented. Including medication history allowed for assessment of whether current or prior use of psychotropic medication influenced postoperative mood or anxiety outcomes, as prior research indicated that antidepressant use may predict poorer postoperative psychological adjustment.13

Psychological and behavioural assessments

Depressive symptoms were measured using the PHQ-9, a validated tool for screening and measuring depression severity in medical populations.14 A PHQ-9 score of 10 or higher was considered indicative of clinically significant depressive symptoms. Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the GAD-7, a reliable self-report measure for generalised anxiety,15 with scores of 10 or higher representing moderate-to-severe anxiety.

These instruments are widely used in bariatric research and clinical settings, allowing a multidimensional understanding of post-surgical psychological well-being.

Depressive symptoms were measured using the PHQ-9, and anxiety symptoms were assessed with the GAD-7. Both instruments are widely used in clinical research and provide reliable continuous scores. The PHQ-9 has demonstrated adequate sensitivity and specificity for individuals with obesity undergoing bariatric surgery.19 The GAD-7 is well-validated in general populations, but evidence of its performance in bariatric surgery cohorts is limited. Therefore, while these tools are appropriate for screening, the limited bariatric-specific validation represents a study limitation.20

Data management and quality assurance

All data were entered into a secure, password-protected Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) database. Each participant was assigned a unique identification code to ensure anonymity. Identifiable information was stored separately from analytical data files. To ensure data quality, 10% of entries were double-checked by an independent researcher. In cases of missing data exceeding 5%, multiple imputation was performed under the assumption that data were missing at random.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using International Business Machines (IBM) Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 24.0, with significance set at p<0.05 (two-tailed). Descriptive statistics summarised sociodemographic, clinical and psychological variables as means±SD for continuous data and frequencies and percentages for categorical data. Group comparisons between SG and GB were assessed using independent-samples t-tests for continuous variables and χ² or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d for continuous outcomes and Cramer’s V for categorical variables, with thresholds of 0.2 (small), 0.5 (medium) and 0.8 (large) for d, and 0.1, 0.3 and 0.5 for V, respectively.

Associations between psychological outcomes (PHQ-9, GAD-7, RSES, BSQ, EES, MSPSS) and clinical variables such as current BMI, % total weight loss (%TWL) and time since surgery were examined using Pearson’s correlation coefficients (r), categorised as weak (0.10–0.29), moderate (0.30–0.49) or strong (≥0.50). A multiple linear regression analysis was then conducted to identify predictors of depressive symptoms (PHQ-9). Independent variables included surgery type, %TWL, psychotropic medication use and SES. Regression outputs were reported as unstandardised coefficients (β), SE, 95% CIs and model R² values to indicate the proportion of explained variance.

Normality testing

Preliminary analysis confirmed that most continuous variables (PHQ-9, GAD-7, RSES, BSQ, EES, MSPSS, BMI and %TWL) were approximately normally distributed. The Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of Q–Q plots indicated no significant deviations from normality for the majority of variables (p>0.05). Minor skewness was observed in some psychological scales, but these remained within acceptable limits for parametric testing. Therefore, parametric analyses were used for primary comparisons, supplemented by non-parametric tests for confirmation where appropriate.

Table 1 summarises the sociodemographic, clinical and lifestyle characteristics of the 300 participants according to surgical procedure (172 SG and 128 GB). The two groups were comparable in age (36.7±5.8 vs 37.1±5.7 years; p=0.42), sex distribution (77.9% vs 79.7% female; p=0.57), marital status (62.2% vs 64.8% married; p=0.63), education level, employment status, monthly income and socioeconomic status (all p>0.05). Overall, the groups were similar across most baseline characteristics, with significant differences observed primarily in weight loss outcomes and psychiatric history.

As shown in table 4, multiple linear regression analysis revealed that several variables significantly predicted depressive symptoms among bariatric surgery patients. Patients who underwent gastric bypass had higher PHQ-9 scores compared with those who underwent SG (β = +1.91, p=0.002). Given the higher prevalence of preoperative psychiatric diagnoses and psychotropic medication use in the GB group, these differences should be interpreted cautiously. A higher percentage of total weight loss (%TWL) was significantly linked to lower depressive symptom scores (β = −0.11, p=0.004). Patients using psychotropic medications (β = +2.27, p=0.003) and those with low SES (β = +1.94, p=0.001) exhibited greater depression severity. The model accounted for 33% of the variance in PHQ-9 scores (R² = 0.33, p=0.002), indicating that surgery type, weight loss, medication use and SES were key independent predictors of post-surgical depressive symptoms.

Online supplemental table S1 presents the results of a multiple linear regression analysis examining independent predictors of postoperative anxiety symptoms as measured by the GAD-7. After adjustment for surgical, clinical and socioeconomic factors, psychotropic medication use and low SES were significantly associated with higher anxiety scores. Specifically, participants using psychotropic medications exhibited greater GAD-7 scores (β = +1.89, p=0.004), and those with struggling SES also reported higher anxiety symptoms (β = +1.65, p=0.002).

In contrast, surgery type (gastric bypass vs sleeve gastrectomy) was not a statistically significant predictor of anxiety severity after adjustment (β = +0.84, p=0.08). Similarly, greater percentage of total weight loss (%TWL) showed a trend toward lower anxiety scores but did not reach statistical significance (β = −0.06, p=0.07). Overall, the model explained 24% of the variance in GAD-7 scores (R² = 0.24, p=0.006), indicating that postoperative anxiety symptoms were driven primarily by psychosocial and socioeconomic factors rather than surgical technique or weight loss alone.

Weight-related variables were also significantly associated with psychological outcomes. Higher current BMI correlated positively with depression (r = +0.28, p=0.005) and anxiety (r = +0.24, p=0.001), whereas greater percentage of total weight loss (%TWL) correlated inversely with both depressive (r = −0.30, p=0.003) and anxiety symptoms (r = −0.25, p=0.002). Time since surgery showed a small but significant positive association with depressive symptoms (r = +0.15, p=0.02), while its association with anxiety did not reach statistical significance.

Behavioural and social factors demonstrated additional significant relationships. Emotional eating was moderately and positively correlated with both depression (r = +0.34, p=0.002) and anxiety (r = +0.31, p=0.003), whereas perceived social support showed significant inverse correlations with depressive (r = −0.29, p=0.004) and anxiety symptoms (r = −0.26, p=0.006). In contrast, medical comorbidities, physical activity level and primary reason for surgery were not significantly associated with either PHQ-9 or GAD-7 scores, and a preoperative psychiatric diagnosis demonstrated only modest positive correlations with both outcomes.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study explored differences in depressive and anxiety symptoms among patients undergoing SG and GB, with particular attention to the roles of weight loss, socioeconomic status, medication use and psychosocial factors. Patients with obesity who underwent gastric bypass exhibited higher depressive and anxiety symptoms; however, baseline differences preclude causal conclusions. However, individuals in the GB group also exhibited higher rates of preoperative psychiatric diagnoses and psychotropic medication use, factors known to predict postoperative psychological distress. In the absence of standardised preoperative psychological severity measures, these baseline differences limit causal attribution of postoperative psychological outcomes to surgical procedure alone. Accordingly, the observed associations are best interpreted as reflecting underlying patient characteristics and pre-existing psychosocial vulnerability rather than procedure-specific effects. Poorer socioeconomic status, psychotropic medication use and greater body image dissatisfaction further emerged as important correlates of elevated psychological distress, underscoring the multifactorial nature of postoperative mental health outcomes.

The higher depression and anxiety levels observed among GB patients are consistent with studies reporting greater postoperative emotional variability in this population, which may reflect underlying differences in preoperative psychiatric burden and psychosocial vulnerability. Mitchell et al reported higher rates of mood disorders following GB compared with SG, potentially attributable to the greater metabolic and hormonal shifts associated with bypass procedures.21 Gastric bypass is known to involve more pronounced metabolic and hormonal changes, which have been hypothesised to interact with mood and anxiety regulation.22 In addition to these physiological mechanisms, GB patients frequently present with a higher preoperative prevalence of psychiatric conditions and psychotropic medication use, a pattern that aligns with our findings of increased baseline psychiatric treatment in this group.

In our cohort, depressive and anxiety symptom scores were inversely correlated with total weight loss (%TWL), highlighting the beneficial impact of postoperative weight reduction on psychological well-being. This finding aligns with Sarwer et al, who reported significant improvements in mood and quality of life within the first 2 years after bariatric surgery, particularly among individuals achieving greater weight loss.23 However, the persistence of depressive symptoms among a subset of patients, especially those undergoing gastric bypass, indicates that weight loss alone does not fully mitigate psychological distress. Consistent with this observation, the regression model explained approximately one-third of the variance in depressive symptoms, reflecting a weak-to-moderate fit that is typical of bariatric and psychosocial research, where postoperative mental health outcomes are recognised as multifactorial and not fully explained by clinical or surgical variables alone.24 Longitudinal studies and systematic reviews have similarly demonstrated modest explanatory power of regression models examining postoperative depression and anxiety,3 underscoring the influence of unmeasured factors such as personality traits, coping strategies, social support, expectations and life stressors.25 These factors contribute to substantial interindividual variability and are often not captured in clinical prediction models. Notably, despite favourable weight-related outcomes, patients undergoing gastric bypass exhibited worse overall psychological outcomes compared with sleeve gastrectomy patients. This apparent discrepancy reinforces evidence that psychological responses to bariatric surgery are highly variable and not solely dependent on weight loss magnitude,26 with group-level differences likely reflecting a complex interaction between pre-existing psychosocial vulnerability, higher rates of psychiatric diagnoses and psychotropic medication use, altered medication absorption following gastric bypass, and postoperative adaptation challenges.27 Accordingly, the unexplained variance in the present model likely reflects the complex biopsychosocial determinants of postoperative psychological health following bariatric surgery.28

SES emerged as a significant predictor of depression and anxiety. Patients reporting financial strain (‘struggling’ SES) had markedly higher PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores compared with those of comfortable SES. This aligns with findings from Dawes et al, who observed that lower SES is associated with worse psychological outcomes after bariatric surgery, likely due to chronic stress, reduced access to postoperative resources and limited engagement in follow-up care.3 The socioeconomic gradient in psychological health underscores the need for tailored interventions addressing both medical and social determinants of recovery.

Psychotropic medication use was associated with higher depressive and anxiety symptoms; however, this relationship should be interpreted with caution. Although previous studies have focused primarily on preoperative psychotropic medication use, the present study assessed current medication use following surgery. Bariatric procedures, particularly gastric bypass, alter gastrointestinal anatomy and may reduce the absorption and bioavailability of psychotropic medications, potentially diminishing their postoperative efficacy.29 30 Due to the cross-sectional design and absence of preoperative psychological baseline data, the direction of this association cannot be determined, and it is plausible that some patients initiated psychotropic medications after surgery in response to worsening psychological symptoms rather than medication use conferring increased risk.

Body image dissatisfaction and low self-esteem were strongly correlated with both depression and anxiety in our sample. Similar associations have been documented in prior literature, indicating that even after significant weight loss, residual body image concerns and excess skin can perpetuate psychological distress.31 Conversely, higher self-esteem was protective, consistent with research by Cash and Smolak, which emphasised the role of positive body perception in sustaining mental health improvements after bariatric surgery.32

Time since surgery has been identified in previous studies as a factor influencing postoperative depressive outcomes,33 and a positive correlation between time since surgery and PHQ-9 scores was observed in the present cohort. However, time since surgery was not included in the final multivariable regression models because the analysis focused on potentially modifiable psychosocial, socioeconomic and weight-related factors that were prioritised a priori. In addition, postoperative duration may reflect cumulative psychosocial experiences rather than an independent causal determinant. Consequently, the influence of temporal effects on depressive symptoms could not be fully evaluated and should be considered when interpreting these findings.

Overall, these findings highlight that postoperative psychological outcomes are shaped by multiple interconnected factors, including weight-related changes, pre-existing mental health conditions, socioeconomic status and body image adaptation. These results underscore the need for a comprehensive, multidisciplinary bariatric care approach that integrates psychological evaluation and long-term mental health support into routine follow-up to promote both emotional well-being and sustained weight management.

Limitations

This study was conducted in only two tertiary hospitals, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other populations or healthcare settings. In addition, baseline differences in preoperative psychiatric diagnoses and psychotropic medication use between the sleeve gastrectomy and gastric bypass groups limit the ability to attribute postoperative psychological differences to surgical procedure alone. The absence of standardised preoperative psychological baseline data further restricted direct assessment of changes in depression and anxiety following surgery. Although the final sample size was modestly lower than that estimated in the a priori power calculation, analyses using continuous PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores remained adequately powered; however, smaller effect sizes may not have been detected. Finally, unmeasured factors such as personality traits, quality of social support, coping styles and participation in postoperative psychological counselling may have influenced postoperative psychological outcomes.

Conclusion

Bariatric surgery impacts both physical and psychological health in patients with obesity. Those undergoing gastric bypass reported higher depression and anxiety, while sleeve gastrectomy patients showed greater self-esteem and body satisfaction. Greater weight loss improved emotional well-being, but low socioeconomic status and psychotropic medication use increased psychological vulnerability, highlighting the need for integrated mental health support in bariatric care.
