Deconstructing Chirality: Probing Local and Nonlocal Effects in Azobenzene Derivatives with X‑ray Circular Dichroism
Authors: Ajay Khanna, Victor M. Freixas, Lei Xu, Jérémy R. Rouxel, Niranjan Govind, Marco Garavelli, Shaul Mukamel, Sergei Tretiak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Resolving molecular chirality on the atomic scale remains a critical challenge in chemistry. Conventional Optical Circular Dichroism spectroscopy often overlooks subtle and localized structural features. Here, we computationally investigate site-specific X-ray circular dichroism (XCD) across a series of trans-azobenzene derivatives to deconstruct and interpret chiroptical signals at the atomic level. Our modeling reveals that XCD is capable of distinguishing dichroic contributions arising from both a local chiral center and a global molecular twist, revealing their intricate interplay and potential for constructive or destructive interference. We show that sterically induced global distortions can dominate the XCD signal in some cases, even suppressing the response from the chiral center itself. This insight suggests a new molecular design principle for tuning the chiroptical activity, which we extend by proposing strategies to achieve unidirectional photoisomerization through steric gearing. Altogether, this work establishes a quantitative framework for engineering chiroptical responses, laying the foundation for the design of functional chiral systems utilizing principles of unidirectional molecular motor-like conformational dynamics.

Molecular chirality, the geometric property of a molecule that prevents it from being superimposed on its mirror image, is a fundamental concept to many areas of chemistry, biology, and material science. Chiral molecules interact asymmetrically with polarized light, giving rise to distinctive optical activities and circular dichroism (CD) spectra. , Optical CD (OCD) spectroscopy, which measures the differential absorption of left- and right-circularly polarized ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) light, is a well-established and robust method for probing molecular chirality. Its sensitivity to electronic transitions involving valence electrons has made OCD particularly valuable for characterizing the secondary and tertiary structures of proteins, − protein–ligand interactions, − and the structural dynamics of complex biomolecules.

However, the interpretation of OCD spectra is often complicated by the convolution of signals from different regions of a molecule, making it difficult to resolve site-specific contributions. , This limitation has spurred the development of advanced techniques capable of probing chirality with higher spatial and temporal resolutions, driven in part by advancements in light sources like free-electron lasers. − One emerging technique, X-ray CD (XCD) spectroscopy, − an extension of OCD into the X-ray regime, has emerged as a powerful complementary technique. While applications to randomly oriented samples were historically sparse, recent progress in detection sensitivity now renders such XCD measurements increasingly possible. By utilizing element-specific core-level excitations that are spatially localized, XCD acts as a locally anchored probe capable of reporting on chirality with high elemental and spatial specificity. ,−

In this study, we employ quantum-chemical calculations to investigate the site-specific sensitivity of XCD and to deconstruct the chiroptical response of trans-azobenzene derivatives. Azobenzene, a well-known molecular photoswitch, is widely used in applications ranging from molecular electronics to photopharmacology. − The native symmetric cis/trans scaffold (Figure , top panel) lacks intrinsic chirality and thus produces no measurable CD signal. To introduce chirality, we functionalized one phenyl ring with a glycine unit, creating a phenylglycine moiety that serves as a localized chiral center. In our molecular design, we distinguish between two distinct sources of chirality: local chirality, which arises from a constitutionally defined stereocenter (the phenylglycine unit), and nonlocal chirality, which emerges from the collective structural distortion of the molecular backbone. The distortion caused by the trans-to-cis isomerization does not generally result in nonlocal chiral features since both foldings are equiprobable. However, the pump inducing the photoisomerization can push the molecule into one of the two chiral pathways, , resulting in the nonlocal chiral folding. To probe these effects, we introduce a bromine atom as a site-specific X-ray chromophore. , By positioning this probe at different locations within the molecule, we directly compare the sensitivities of OCD and XCD to both local and nonlocal sources of chirality. Our modeling shows that by strategically placing an X-ray chromophore, significant nonlocal chirality can be generated even in the absence of a traditional chiral center, driven purely by structural distortion. These insights may have implications for the design of novel chiroptical systems and the development of more precise XCD-based methodologies for probing and quantifying chirality in complex molecular architectures.

We then present results from our OCD and XCD simulations, highlighting differences in sensitivity and the contributions of local versus nonlocal chirality. Finally, we summarize the key conclusions and outline avenues for future research. The computational methodology is provided in Section I of the Supporting Information.

CD spectroscopy probes molecular chirality by measuring the differential absorption of the left and right circularly polarized electromagnetic radiation. Fundamentally, the CD signal arises due to interactions involving electric and magnetic transition dipole moments, rendering it sensitive to both structural and electronic asymmetries.

The differential absorption observed in a CD experiment, Δϵ­(ω), is formally defined as 1 Δϵ(ω)=AL(ω)−AR(ω) where A L(ω) and A R(ω) are the absorption intensities for left and right circularly polarized (LCP, RCP) photons with frequency ω, respectively. This CD signal is nonzero only for chiral molecules in isotropic media, exhibits opposite signs for enantiomers, and vanishes in racemic mixtures.

The physical origin of CD lies in the interaction between a chiral molecule and the electromagnetic field of an incident ray. The absorption signal intensity is directly related to the induced polarization P(ω) and magnetization M(ω) of the molecule: 2 A(ω)∝Im[E*(ω)·P(ω)+B*(ω)·M(ω)] where E*­(ω) and B*­(ω) are the Fourier components of the electric and magnetic fields, respectively. Expanding the induced polarization and magnetization in terms of molecular response functions and performing rotational averaging over an isotropic ensemble yields a practical form for the CD signal: 3 Δϵ(ω)∝2πω3c∑fRf0σ(ω−ωf0) Here, the quantity R f0 represents the rotatory strength ,, for the transition from the molecular ground state ⟨0| to the excited state |f⟩: 4 Rf0=Im(μf0·mf0) where μ0f = ⟨0|μ|f⟩ and m f0 = ⟨f|m|0⟩ are the electric and magnetic transition dipoles, respectively. These quantities can be routinely extracted from modern quantum-chemical simulations such as time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT). The line shape function σ­(ω – ωf0) commonly takes a Lorentzian or Gaussian form, describing the broadening of excited state transitions: 5 σ(ω−ωf0)=Γf0(ω−ωf0)2+Γf02 with Γf0 representing the line width parameter of state |f⟩.

The key difference between OCD and XCD lies in the nature of the electronic states involved. OCD operates in the UV–vis range, probing transitions of delocalized valence electrons. Consequently, OCD spectra are sensitive to the molecule’s entire three-dimensional structure, providing a signal that inherently convolutes contributions from all sources of asymmetry, including both local chirality (from a stereocenter) and nonlocal chirality (from structural distortion).

In contrast, XCD utilizes high-energy X-rays tuned to be resonant with core–electron transitions. Since core orbitals are tightly bound and localized on specific atoms, XCD acts as an element-specific and site-specific probe. This localization is the key to differentiating the origins of the chiroptical response. By placing the probe at or near the stereocenter, XCD can selectively detect the local chirality. Alternatively, by placing it on the molecular backbone, it can report on the geometric consequences of nonlocal chirality. This potentially offers the deconstruction of the chiroptical signal in a way that is inaccessible to the OCD.

With the theoretical framework in place, we now tested these expectations computationally. In this section we present results exploring the sensitivity of OCD and XCD spectroscopy to both local and nonlocal chirality. We examine how structural parameters influence the XCD intensity and identify conditions under which geometrical changes alone contribute significantly to the overall chiroptical response. Additionally, we suggest strategies to enhance the XCD signal and provide potential pathways for achieving unidirectional control of photoisomerization in trans-azobenzene to retain a net chiroptical signal during molecular switching.

Figure shows the trans-azobenzene scaffold and its three derivatives with a chiral phenylglycine and a bromine atom acting as an X-ray chromophore. There are ten hydrogens that can be replaced in each derivative leading to a total of 30 structures from all three derivatives. We calculate both OCD and XCD responses (see supplementary computational details section) as a function of bromine’s position as it moves across the molecular backbone. While core-level excitations from carbon atoms have sometimes been utilized for XCD analysis due to their ubiquitous presence, , their small core-level chemical shifts limit their effectiveness as local probes of chirality. Thus, we adopted Br due to its well-defined and distinguishable core-excitation transitions, specifically the Br L1-edge (2s transitions). For brevity, we refer to trans-azobenzene as azobenzene (Abz) and chiral center (R)-phenylglycine as glycine throughout this work. We first establish a baseline by examining planar azobenzene geometries with and without chiral substitution. As expected, CD signals vanish for planar symmetrical structures, as shown supplemental Figure S1, which compares the calculated OCD spectra of both planar azobenzene and 4-(1-bromo)­glycine-substituted azobenzene.

To systematically investigate local chirality effects in the globally achiral planar azobenzene scaffold, we introduced (R)-glycine as a chiral substituent. By replacing hydrogen atoms with bromine at various positions, we are able to probe site-specific XCD. While ten possible bromination sites exist on the azobenzene, we focus on six representative positions, as the remaining sites are symmetry-equivalent.

Figure presents the calculated OCD (top panel) and XCD (bottom panel) spectra for six bromination positions: position 4 (directly bonded to glycine), position 3 (adjacent to the chiral center), followed by position 2, and positions 2′, 3′ and 4′ (on the second azobenzene ring, further from the chiral center). For each bromine substitution, we use the same planar geometry without reoptimizing the geometries per substitution to preserve the achiral azobenzene scaffold. To assess the influence of relativistic effects − and spin–orbit coupling (SOC), which can be significant for bromine-contained systems, we compare computed XCD spectra with and without these contributions. As displayed in Supplemental Figures S2 and S3, both effects are found to be negligible for the systems studied.

As shown in Figure , the spectroscopies of the OCD and XCD exhibit notable sensitivity to the position of the bromine chromophore, with significant variations occurring when bromine is directly bonded to the glycine unit. Shifting bromine from the direct glycine attachment to an adjacent carbon results in a phase inversion in the OCD spectrum, while XCD signals show diminished intensity and fewer active electronic states. As expected, OCD spectra become increasingly similar and insensitive to bromine positions as they move further from the chiral center, eventually resembling spectra of unsubstituted phenyl glycine. Conversely, XCD spectra retain distinct site-specific signatures even at moderate distances, illustrating local sensitivity and spatial resolution.

While both techniques are driven by similar underlying phenomena, OCD and XCD spectroscopy exhibit significant differences in their sensitivity, spatial resolution, and electronic transitions probed. While the signals of OCD are often stronger than those of XCD, OCD spectra of the XCD have a tendency to be broad and poorly resolved due to significant spectral congestiona common challenge in UV–vis spectroscopy, where numerous closely spaced electronic transitions overlap within narrow energy ranges. This overlap makes it difficult to isolate contributions from individual excited states. For instance, in the 4-(1-bromo)­glycine derivative, the 1.0 eV window between 6.0 and 7.0 eV includes at least 16 closely spaced valence transitions, resulting in a convoluted spectrum. In contrast, the corresponding XCD spectrum involves transitions to a much sparser set of unoccupied states. Within a 1.0 eV window from 1760.0 to 1761.0 eV, only 9 discrete core-level transitions are observed. This reduced spectral density underscores one of the advantages of XCD. Although each XCD peak experiences broader intrinsic line widths due to short core-hole lifetimes, the larger energy spacing between transitions ensures that individual features remain well-resolved. As a result, XCD may provide a clearer spectral fingerprint of the unoccupied electronic states, allowing for a more direct correlation between spectral features and the local chiral environment. This resolution advantage can be helpful in experimental settings, where subtle variations in OCD spectra may be partially obscured by environmental broadening. − Furthermore, modern X-ray light sources provide access to ultrafast temporal resolution, ,− giving XCD the potential to probe dynamic electronic and structural changes in chiral molecules on femtosecond to attosecond time scales.

We next analyze the distance dependence of XCD signals using the integrated area under the absolute XCD intensity curves, as shown in Figure . The results show that the strongest XCD signals occur when the X-ray chromophore is directly bonded to the chiral center, with the signal intensity decreasing monotonically as the distance between the chromophore and chiral center increases. This distance-dependent behavior highlights XCD’s effectiveness in probing local chirality with high spatial resolution. We further explore strategies to enhance XCD signals, which are inherently weaker than their OCD counterparts. Specifically, we examine whether the incorporation of multiple chiral substituents results in constructive or destructive interference in the overall XCD intensity, thereby influencing the effectiveness of signal amplification in complex molecular designs.

Figure presents a comparative analysis of XCD signals from azobenzene derivatives containing single or dual chiral centers. The top panels illustrate cases of destructive interference in the XCD signal, while the bottom panels demonstrate constructive interference. For each optimized molecular geometry, the spectrum was computed, including 30 excited states, with the rotatory strength of each state shown as scaled stick plots beneath the corresponding spectra. The comparison reveals that spectral features vary significantly depending on the relative positioning of the chiral centers with respect to the bromine atom, emphasizing the importance of the substitution pattern in modulating the overall XCD response.

Enhancing XCD signals via constructive and destructive interference from multiple chiral centers. The integrated area under the absolute XCD curve is provided as a quantitative measure of signal strength. (Top panel) For 5-bromo azobenzene, adjacent 3,4-(S,R)-diglycine substituents interfere destructively, yielding an attenuated net signal in (c) compared to the monosubstituted derivative in (a). (Bottom panel) In contrast, for 4-bromo azobenzene, flanking 3,5-(S,R)-diglycine substituents interfere constructively, amplifying the net signal in (f) significantly beyond the individual contributions from (d) and (e).

For the 5-bromo azobenzene derivative (top panel), comparison of XCD signal intensities between the 4-(R)-glycine and 3-(S)-glycine-monosubstituted variants reveals significant differences. However, this variation arises primarily from the spatial separation between the (S)-glycine and bromine substituents rather than from the inherent R-S stereochemical configuration. When both R and S glycine moieties are incorporated simultaneously, as in 3,4-(S,R)-diglycine-5-bromo azobenzene, a net decrease in the XCD intensity is observed. This attenuation manifests as a diminished signal strength in the 1760 eV energy region, indicating destructive interference between the chiral contributions. In contrast, the 4-bromo azobenzene derivative (bottom panel) exhibits constructive XCD enhancement when the bromine atom is positioned between the 3-(S) and 5-(R)-glycine substituents. While the 3-(S) and 5-(R) monosubstituted derivatives display comparable individual XCD intensities, their combined effect results in a net amplification of the XCD signal, demonstrating constructive interference between the spatially arranged chiral centers.

These results illustrate that the spatial arrangement of chiral centers determines whether their contributions interfere constructively or destructively. The striking contrast between the 3,5- and 3,4-disubstituted systems underscores how subtle changes in chiral center positioning can profoundly impact the overall chiroptical response, suggesting the broad tunability of XCD signal amplification in molecular systems via structural modifications.

Overall, these observations confirm the unique sensitivity of XCD spectroscopy to subtle local variations in the electronic and geometric features. By providing detailed site-specific information, XCD complements traditional OCD analyses and potentially enhances analytical capabilities in molecular chirality studies across diverse molecular systems.

Having established the nature of the chiroptical signals from explicit chiral centers, we now turn to a second source of chirality: structural distortion. Although often drawn as planar, the azobenzene scaffold can undergo twisting due to dynamical processes such as photochemical isomerization, or steric interactions, particularly from substituents at the 2 or 2′ positions adjacent to the azo bridge. This backbone distortion can generate a powerful chiroptical response.

This phenomenon is clearly illustrated in Figure , which compares the XCD and OCD spectra of 2′-bromoazobenzene in both a constrained planar geometry and a sterically relaxed, nonplanar geometry. As expected, the planar structure produces negligible XCD and OCD (see inset) signals. The optimized nonplanar structure exhibits significant chiroptical activity arising purely from sterically induced distortion. Both spectral probes are sensitive to the nonlocal chirality. For the nonplanar structure the XCD spectrum is dominated by a single, intense bisignate (S-shaped) feature concentrated in a narrow energy window. In contrast, the OCD spectrum consists of multiple, less intense peaks distributed over a wider energy range. This again highlights the complementary nature of these spectroscopic techniques. Among bromine substitutions at positions 2 and 2′, steric repulsion between the bromine atom and the azo bridge (−NN−) is most pronounced at position 2′ (equivalent to position 6). This distortion becomes even more significant when a glycine group is introduced near these positions, as in the 2-glycine-2′-bromo azobenzene derivative, which exhibits the most substantial structural deviation in the series.

Comparison of XCD spectra for planar (unoptimized) and nonplanar (optimized) 2′-bromo-substituted azobenzene (lacking explicit chiral centers). The XCD/OCD spectrum for the optimized geometry corresponds to one of two energetically equivalent enantiomers that form upon spontaneous distortion from the planar geometry. This underscores the significant role of sterically induced structural distortions in generating chiroptical activity.

To quantify this relationship between the structure and signal, we next map the chiroptical response as a function of backbone twisting. By removing the chiral center and manually varying the ∠NNCC dihedral angle from −90° to +90° (Figure ), we simulate the XCD intensity across a range of conformations. The resulting signal vanishes at 0° corresponding to the achiral planar structure and reaches its maximum intensity at dihedral angles of approximately ±60°. This result identifies an optimal geometry for maximizing the chiroptical signal arising solely from sterically induced structural distortion.

The dihedral angle is systematically scanned from −90° to +90°, the resulting XCD spectra have equal magnitude and opposite sign. The plot illustrates how molecular conformation directly influences nonlocal chirality.

This sets the stage for the following section, where we aim to deconvolute the individual contributions from chiral center (local) and structural distortion (nonlocal) in order to understand their interplay in shaping the overall chiroptical response.

When bromine is placed at positions 2 (equivalent to 6′) and 2′ (equivalent to 6) on azobenzene, all three derivatives exhibit intriguing behavior. Here, we focus only on the 4-glycine azobenzene derivative (Supplemental Section S4, overviewing the remaining derivatives). Contrary to conventional expectations where the strongest XCD signals originate at the chiral center, this derivative shows unexpectedly intense signals at remote positions.

Structural analysis reveals that placing bromine or the chiral center near the azo bridge disrupts the molecular planarity. When both are positioned near the bridge, these distortions are amplified, causing significant structural perturbation around both the azo region and the chiral center. This cooperative distortion enhances the overall chiroptical response, producing high XCD intensities and a nonlinear relationship between signal strength and chromophore position.

Figure systematically dissects these two contributions. First, we consider the 4-glycine-3-bromo derivative as a reference system, where local chirality dominates. Here, the XCD signal (sky blue curve, Figure a) is generated by the bromine’s proximity to the chiral glycine; removing the glycine restores planarity and eliminates the chiroptical signal (Figure b). The behavior changes dramatically for the 4-glycine-2′-bromo derivative (dark purple curve), where the bromine occupies a sterically hindered site. Here, removal of the glycine substituent does not restore planarity. Instead, the inherent steric strain from the 2′-bromo group maintains a twisted, nonlocal chiral backbone.

Isolating (a) combined local and nonlocal chirality from (b) purely nonlocal chirality using XCD spectra. The negligible signal in 3-bromo azobenzene confirms the local chirality contribution from the chiral center in 4-glycine-3-bromo, while comparable intensities for 2′-bromo derivatives indicate additional structural chirality in 4-glycine-2′-bromo.

Most notably, we observe destructive interference between the two sources of chirality. When the glycine is added back to the 2′-bromo system, the net XCD intensity decreases (XCD strength drops from 0.083 without glycine to 0.076 with glycine). This counterintuitive result demonstrates that the chiral field from the substituent and the twist in the backbone can act in opposition, weakening the overall chiroptical signal, even though each component individually generates a strong XCD response (dark purple curve).

These observations emphasize the significant potential of structural modifications alone in inducing measurable chirality independent of explicit chiral substituents. Consequently, specific placement of an X-ray chromophore alone can effectively probe and quantify molecular chirality without explicitly introducing chiral centers. This finding suggests promising avenues for designing chiral-sensitive molecular probes and sensors where structural engineering alone can yield strong chiral responses.

Nonlocal chirality arising from structural distortion, as seen in 2′-bromo azobenzene, creates two energetically equivalent enantiomers. Without an additional internal or external chiral bias, these conformers will exist as a racemic mixture, yielding a net zero CD signal. This symmetry principle also governs the azobenzene’s photoisomerization dynamics. The clockwise and anticlockwise rotational pathways are equally probable, leading to a silent net signal in bulk measurement. Figure shows this symmetry in 2′-bromo azobenzene, where geometries with ±46.1° ∠NNCC–Br dihedral angles yield XCD spectra of equal magnitude but opposite sign. When summed, these spectra cancel each other, resulting in a vanishing net signal.

Net XCD signal (black) is obtained by combining the spectra of optimized 2′-bromoazobenzene structures with opposite NNCCBr dihedral angles (+ and – are shown in dark purple and sky blue, respectively). Equal probability of clockwise and anticlockwise rotation causes cancellation, resulting in negligible overall chirality.

To achieve unidirectional rotation, this energetic symmetry must be broken. We suggest two distinct strategies. The first approach builds on our findings from the source of non-local chirality presented above. By introduction of substituents (such as bromine or glycine) that enforce a stable, twisted ground-state geometry, the molecule becomes inherently chiral. This pre-existing structural chirality is expected to bias the potential energy surface of the excited state, favoring one rotational pathway over the other and thereby breaking the otherwise equal probability distribution.

A second, more subtle strategy, inspired by alkene-based molecular motors, − is to design steric gearing that preserves ground-state symmetry while biasing the rotational pathway. By introducing n-propyl groups at positions 5 and 3′ (Supplemental Figure S6), we maintain C 2 symmetry while creating differential steric interactions during rotation. To quantify this effect, we perform excited-state potential energy scans, which confirm this “steric ratchet” mechanism, revealing a −1.1 kcal/mol energetic preference for one rotational pathway over the other (see Supplemental Figure S7).

By employing either explicit ground-state chirality or subtle steric gearing, one can rationally bias the symmetric energy landscape of azobenzene can be rationally biased. This work provides a theoretical basis for exploring azobenzene derivatives with the potential to exhibit controlled, directional behavior at the molecular scale.

In conclusion, our computational study investigates the interplay between local and nonlocal dichroic responses using azobenzene derivatives as a representative example. Resolving molecular chirality at the local level is challenging with traditional OCD spectroscopy, as it is primarily sensitive to the molecule’s global chiral structure. We show that by employing a specifically designed set of trans-azobenzene derivatives, it is possible to systematically deconstruct the chiroptical signals measured by both OCD and the site-specific XCD technique.

Our findings emphasize the growing potential of XCD as a powerful probe for distinguishing between local chirality, induced by an explicit chiral center, and nonlocal chirality, arising from distortions of the molecular backbone. Notably, our results suggest that these two contributions are not simply additive. In some cases, chirality induced by steric distortion may have a significant influence on the overall chiroptical response and can potentially interfere with the signal from a conventional chiral center. Additionally, we observed that the XCD signal can be enhanced through constructive interplay between multiple chiral centers, hinting at a possible strategy for improving the signal strength. Taking an example of molecular motors, we further suggest strategies for unidirectional photoisomerization to enhance detectable chirality signals.

Overall, although further work is needed to generalize these insights for other molecular families, our study provides useful modeling guidelines for designing molecules with improved chiroptical sensitivity. These observations broaden the potential applicability of XCD and related chirality-sensitive spectroscopies in both fundamental studies and the development of functional chiral materials.
