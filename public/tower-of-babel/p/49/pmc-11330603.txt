Pitavastatin attenuates hypercholesterolemia-induced decline in serotonin transporter availability
Authors: Sy-Jou Chen, Rou-Ling Cho, Skye Hsin-Hsien Yeh, Min-Chien Tsai, Yi-Ping Chuang, Chih-Feng Lien, Chuang-Hsin Chiu, Yi-Wei Yeh, Chin-Sheng Lin, Kuo-Hsing Ma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHypercholesterolemia is associated with increased inflammation and impaired serotonin neurotransmission, potentially contributing to depressive symptoms. However, the role of statins, particularly pitavastatin, in modulating serotonin transporter (SERT) function within this context remains underexplored. This study aimed to investigate whether pitavastatin counteracts the neurobiological effects of hypercholesterolemia.MethodsLow-density lipoprotein receptor knockout (LDLR−/−) mice on a C57BL/6 background were assigned to three groups: a control group fed a standard chow diet, a group fed a high-fat diet (HFD), and a third group fed a high-fat diet supplemented with pitavastatin (HFD + Pita). We evaluated the effects of HFD with or without pitavastatin on lipid profiles, inflammatory markers, and SERT availability using small-animal positron emission tomography (PET) scans with the radioligand 4-[18F]-ADAM over a 20-week period.ResultsPitavastatin treatment in HFD-fed mice significantly reduced both total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol levels in HFD-fed mice compared to those on HFD alone. Elevated inflammatory markers such as IL-1α, MCP-1/CCL2, and TNF-α in HFD mice were notably decreased in the HFD + Pita group. PET scans showed reduced SERT availability in the brains of HFD mice; however, pitavastatin improved this in brain regions associated with mood regulation, suggesting enhanced serotonin neurotransmission. Additionally, the sucrose preference test showed a trend towards increased preference in the HFD + Pita group compared to the HFD group, indicating a potential reduction in depressive-like behavior.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that pitavastatin not only lowers cholesterol and reduces inflammation but also enhances SERT availability, suggesting a potential role in alleviating depressive symptoms associated with hypercholesterolemia. These results highlight the multifaceted benefits of pitavastatin, extending beyond its lipid-lowering effects to potentially improving mood regulation and neurotransmitter function.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-024-02236-4.

Introduction

Approximately 5% of the global adult population is affected by depression, representing a significant health challenge worldwide [1]. Hypercholesterolemia, a major risk factor for atherosclerosis, has been linked to depressive disorders in several epidemiological and clinical studies [2–5]. Evidence from animal models, including low-density lipoprotein receptor knockout (LDLR−/−) mice, C57BL/6 mice fed a high-fat diet (HFD), and Flinders Sensitive Line rats, suggests that hypercholesterolemia is associated with behavioral symptoms similar to anxiety and depression. This association may be mediated by immune system activation and subsequent alterations in neurotransmitters [6–8]. High-fat diets can alter the linoleic acid metabolism, adversely affecting the serotonin system and potentially contributing to depression and anxiety-like behavior in mice [8]. Furthermore, abnormal cholesterol levels might change the conformation of the serotonin transporter (SERT), impacting serotonin uptake dynamics and linking metabolic disorders to mood dysregulation [9, 10]. These studies indicate that abnormal cholesterol levels could influence mood disorders through various biochemical pathways, including inflammation, oxidative stress, and neurotransmitter system alterations [11–13].

Antidepressants target neurotransmitter systems, such as serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine, and play pivotal roles in treatment strategy. Emerging evidence from meta-analyses and randomized controlled trials suggests that adjunctive treatment with statins improves therapeutic outcomes by reducing symptom severity and increasing remission rates [14, 15]. Highly regarded for their anti-inflammatory properties and crucial roles in regulating lipid metabolism, statins also exhibit varied effects on depression and seizures across different classes of statins, an observation attributed to both their structural and anti-inflammatory properties [16–18]. It has been suggested that statins with greater blood–brain barrier permeability could have more pronounced antidepressant effects. Additionally, statins may offer neuroprotective benefits, preserving neuronal integrity and facilitating mood regulation, thus extending their impact beyond cardiovascular health through their diverse biological effects [19].

Pitavastatin, recognized as a moderate-intensity statin, is distinguished by its prolonged half-life and minimal risk of drug interactions. It offers additional benefits, including reduced markers of platelet activation, decreased lipoprotein oxidation, and enhanced endothelial function [20, 21]. Observational data suggest a lower incidence of new-onset diabetes in patients treated with pitavastatin compared to those treated with other statins [22]. In HIV-infected patients, its use has been linked to a reduced risk of major adverse cardiovascular events [23]. These findings highlight the diverse effects of pitavastatin. However, its potential neuroprotective effects against depression remain largely unexplored.

The SERT, crucial for modulating serotonin levels, is located in critical brain regions such as the dorsal raphe nuclei and limbic system [24]. Alterations in SERT function have been linked to depression and suicidal behaviors [25], although the serotonin theory of depression remains a subject of debate [26, 27]. Research employing 4-[18F]-ADAM positron emission tomography (PET) imaging has demonstrated decreased SERT availability in key brain areas among patients with depression, particularly those who have attempted suicide, indicating a potential neurobiological basis for depression and related behaviors [28]. Additionally, studies have identified a genetic component to depression, with reduced SERT availability observed in individuals with a familial predisposition to the disorder [29]. Conversely, an increase in SERT availability during depressive episodes has been linked to symptom improvement [30]. These findings underscore the complex association between SERT availability and depression within the serotonergic system.

This study explored the multifaceted relationships between hypercholesterolemia and SERT activity, as well as the efficacy of pitavastatin in ameliorating these effects. Using atherosclerosis-prone mouse models, we investigated the direct impact of hypercholesterolemia on SERT expression. We employed PET imaging with the radiotracer 4-[18F]-ADAM to examine the functional alterations in SERT attributable to hypercholesterolemia [31]. We hypothesized that elevated cholesterol levels reduce SERT availability, contributing to the pathogenesis of depression, and proposed that intervention with pitavastatin could counteract these detrimental effects.

Materials and methods

Animals and in vivo experiments

LDLR−/− mice on a C57BL/6 J background (RRID: MGI:3,772,339) were sourced from Taipei Veterans General Hospital in Taiwan. Male LDLR−/− mice were selectively bred and maintained in a controlled, pathogen-free environment, adhering to ethical guidelines approved by the Laboratory Animal Center (IACUC-18–171). These mice were randomly divided into three groups: Group 1 consisted of LDLR−/− mice fed a standard chow diet (CD); Group 2 consisted of LDLR−/− mice on a high-fat diet (HFD) with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) administration; and Group 3 consisted of LDLR−/− mice on a HFD receiving pitavastatin treatment (Kowa Co., Tokyo, Japan). The HFD began at 8 weeks of age and was sustained for 12 weeks. Starting at 12 weeks of age (i.e., 4 weeks into the high-fat diet), mice received either pitavastatin or DMSO via subcutaneous delivery through an ALZET® osmotic pump (Model 2006, Alza Co., Palo Alto, CA, USA), which was maintained for an additional 8 weeks. Surgical implantation of osmotic mini pumps was performed under aseptic conditions. Mice were anesthetized using intraperitoneal injections of Zoletil (40 mg/kg) and Rompun (2 mg/kg). A small incision was made in the dorsal region to insert the pump subcutaneously, and the wound was closed with sutures. The daily dosage of pitavastatin, dissolved in DMSO, was calculated as 3 mg/kg based on the weight at 12 weeks of age. At 20 weeks of age, following positron emission tomography (PET) scan procedures, the mice were anesthetized again, and blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture for lipid profiles and cytokine immunoassays (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Schematic of the study design. Measurements of lipid profiles

Plasma samples were collected from the mice using heparin at a concentration of 1000 units/mL as an anticoagulant to prevent blood clotting during the collection process. Blood was drawn via cardiac puncture using a heparin-rinsed syringe to ensure the effectiveness of the anticoagulant. The collected blood was then centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to separate the plasma. The assays for quantifying total cholesterol (TCHO), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), aspartate transaminase (GOT), and alanine transaminase (GPT) were conducted using FUJI Dri-Chem slides in conjunction with the Fuji Dri-Chem FDC NX500 analyzer (Fuji Photo Film, Tokyo, Japan) (Supplementary A).

Bead-based immunoassay

A MILLIPLEX® mouse cytokine/chemokine magnetic bead kit (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany, catalog #mcytomag-70 k) was utilized for the quantification of multiple analytes. Blood samples collected at week 20, prepared as described previously in the lipid profiles section, were combined with a premixed bead cocktail. This cocktail contained magnetic beads coated with antibodies specific to various cytokines and chemokines. After an incubation period allowing for the binding of cytokines and chemokines to the coated beads, the mixture underwent magnetic separation to remove unbound substances. Several washing steps followed, removing any residual unbound material. Subsequently, a detection antibody labeled with a fluorescent marker was added, and the samples were incubated again to facilitate the binding of the detection antibody to the captured cytokines or chemokines. Finally, the concentrations of cytokines or chemokines in the samples were quantified using a Luminex-based plate reader, based on the fluorescence of each bead set (Supplementary A).

Radiopharmaceutical synthesis of 4-[18F]-ADAM

The radiopharmaceutical compound 4-[18F]-ADAM was synthesized using an automated synthesizer, following a previously described method [31]. Briefly, the synthesis involved the nucleophilic fluorination of N,N-dimethyl-2-(2,4-dinitrophenylthio)benzylamine in DMSO, utilizing potassium [18F]fluoride and Kryptofix 2.2.2 at 120 °C. This was followed by reduction with Cu(OAc)2-NaBH4 in ethanol at 78 °C. The product was then purified high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), resulting in a final compound with a radiochemical yield of approximately 3% over a synthesis duration of 120 min. Both the chemical and radiochemical purities exceeded 95%, and the specific activity was greater than 3 Ci/μmol (111 GBq/μmol).

Positron emission tomography image data acquisition and analysis

Mice were anesthetized by passive inhalation of a mixture of isoflurane/oxygen (2.5% for induction, 1.5% for maintenance). Static images were reconstructed from three-dimensional list-mode data acquired 60–90 min after the injection of 4-[18F]-ADAM (0.36 ± 0.06 mCi) via the tail vein. The data collection lasted for 30 min and was conducted using an animal-PET R4 scanner (Concorde MicroSystems, Knoxville, TN). The energy window was set between 350 and 650 keV, with timing at 6 ns. Image reconstruction from the raw data involved Fourier rebinning and two-dimensional filtered back projection using a ramp filter with a cutoff at the Nyquist frequency (1.29/mm)[32].

Image analysis was facilitated by PMOD software (PMOD Technologies, Switzerland), focusing on the uptake value of radioactivity across various brain regions. Specific brain regions, including the amygdala, brainstem, cortex, hippocampus, hypothalamus, midbrain, striatum, thalamus, and cerebellum, were manually selected from the PET images, with reference to an MRI-based mouse brain atlas provided by PMOD. The concentration of radioactivity within each region of interest (ROI) was determined based on the mean pixel values. Initially, data were presented as uptake ratios, calculated by dividing the mean ROI value in the target region by that in the cerebellum, which served as the reference region due to its lowest SERT expression. The percentage change in the uptake ratio of radioactivity from week 12 to week 20 was calculated using the formula: Percentage Change = (Uptake ratio20w − Uptake ratio12w) /Uptake ratio12w) × 100%. The standardized uptake value (SUV) was defined as the radioactivity concentration in the ROI (Bq/ml) divided by the injected dose (Bq) per body weight (g). Finally, data were presented in terms of standardized uptake ratios (SUVr), which is the ratio of the SUV in the target region to the SUV in the reference region.

Sucrose preference test

Two bottles were prepared for the Sucrose Preference Test: one containing a 1% sucrose solution and the other containing plain water. The mice underwent an 18-h food deprivation phase, followed by a 2-h water deprivation interval. Subsequently, each mouse was individually placed in a test cage with access to both bottles. After a 2-h period, the volume of liquid consumed from each bottle was meticulously recorded. The affinity of the mice for sucrose was quantified as a percentage, calculated using the formula: Sucrose Preference (%) = (sucrose intake [g]/total fluid intake [g]) × 100%. Because the ability of mice to intake fluids varies and could significantly affect the outcomes of the sucrose preference test, we included comparisons of sucrose intake, total fluid intake, and the sucrose preference ratio for the experiment [33, 34].

Tail-suspension test

The Tail-Suspension Test was conducted on the same set of mice after a 7-day rest period. To minimize stress influences, the mice were acclimatized to the testing environment by being placed in the testing room for 30 min. A segment of non-irritating adhesive tape, approximately 1 inch (2.54 cm) in length, was affixed to the tail of each mouse and positioned about 1 cm from the tip of the tail. The mice were then suspended in an inverted position by securing the opposite end of the tape to a horizontal bar, with their heads approximately 15 cm from the floor. The observation period lasted 6 min, during which the total time each mouse remained immobile was meticulously recorded. Immobility was defined as the absence of any voluntary limb or body movements, except for those associated with respiration. The scoring sessions were video recorded and assessed by a rater from another laboratory who was blinded to the experimental conditions to ensure accurate and consistent data collection. The movement episodes were assessed based on the entire 6-min duration of each video.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8® software. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Data distribution was checked for normality by Shapiro–Wilk test and Q-Q plot before statistical analysis (Supplementary B). Group comparisons were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for parametric comparisons. For analyses involving multiple groups and repeated measurements, repeated measures ANOVA with uncorrected Fisher's LSD post hoc test was utilized. Test–retest reliability of PET results within control groups at two distinct time points was assessed using the Bland–Altman test. Non-parametric comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's multiple comparisons test for post hoc analysis. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Lipid profiles of atherogenic-prone mice

The lipid profiles of LDLR−/− mice were assessed at 20 weeks of age (Fig. 2, Supplementary C). Plasma samples were collected from mice fed either a CD, a HFD, or a HFD supplemented with pitavastatin (HFD + Pita) for analysis. The ANOVA and Tukey post-hoc test revealed that mice on a HFD exhibited significantly higher levels of TCHO [F(2, 12) = 60.54, p < 0.0001; CD vs. HFD, p < 0.0001], LDL-C [F(2, 12) = 47.76, p < 0.0001; CD vs. HFD, p < 0.0001], HDL-C [F(2, 12) = 23.87, p < 0.0001; CD vs. HFD, p < 0.0001], and TG [F(2, 12) = 19.20, p = 0.0002; CD vs. HFD, p = 0.0003] compared to those on a CD. However, the levels of the hepatic enzymes GOT [F(2, 12) = 0.1411, p = 0.8698] and GPT [F(2, 12) = 1.217, p = 0.3303] did not significantly increase. Mice on a HFD and treated with pitavastatin showed lower TCHO (HFD vs. HFD + Pita, p = 0.0044) and LDL-C (HFD vs. HFD + Pita, p = 0.0017) levels compared to those on a HFD alone. These findings suggest that a HFD significantly elevates lipid levels in LDLR−/− mice but does not affect hepatic enzyme levels. Pitavastatin reduces HFD-induced inflammatory cytokine and chemokine

At 20 weeks of age, plasma samples were collected and analyzed using multiplex assays to assess cytokine and chemokine levels among these groups. The ANOVA and Tukey post-hoc test revealed significant increases in the levels of interleukin (IL)-1α [F(2, 9) = 7.717, p = 0.0112; CD vs. HFD, p = 0.0268], monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1/CCL2) [F(2, 9) = 1.051, p = 0.0081; CD vs. HFD, p = 0.0076], macrophage inflammatory protein-1α (MIP-1α/CCL3) [F(2, 9) = 6.727, p = 0.0163; CD vs. HFD, p = 0.0135], Regulated on Activation Normal T-cell Expressed and Secreted (RANTES/CCL5) [F(2, 9) = 6.756, p = 0.0162; CD vs. HFD, p = 0.0260], keratinocyte chemoattractant (KC/CXCL1) [F(2, 9) = 7.045, p = 0.0144; CD vs. HFD, p = 0.0146], monokine induced by gamma interferon (MIG/CXCL9) [F(2, 9) = 9.865, p = 0.0054; CD vs. HFD, p = 0.0060], interferon gamma-induced protein 10 (IP-10/CXCL10) [F(2, 9) = 14.89, p = 0.0014; CD vs. HFD, p = 0.0406], and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) [F(2, 9) = 21.91, p = 0.0003; CD vs. HFD, p = 0.0004] in mice fed a HFD compared to those on a CD (Fig. 3, Supplementary D). However, pitavastatin administration notably reduced the levels of IL-1α (HFD vs. HFD + Pitva, p = 0.0148), MCP-1/CCL2 (HFD vs. HFD + Pitva, p = 0.0425), RANTES/CCL5 (HFD vs. HFD + Pitva, p = 0.0284), MIG/CXCL9 (HFD vs. HFD + Pitva, p = 0.0199), IP-10/CXCL10 (HFD vs. HFD + Pitva, p = 0.0011), and TNF-α (HFD vs. HFD + Pitva, p = 0.0014), highlighting its potential to mitigate the inflammatory response associated with hyperlipidemia. Additionally, levels of IL-5 [F(2, 9) = 12.08, p = 0.0028; HFD vs. HFD + Pita, p = 0.0054], eotaxin/CCL11 [F(2, 9) = 46.49, p < 0.001; HFD vs. HFD + Pita, p < 0.001], macrophage inflammatory protein-2 (MIP-2/CXCL2) [F(2, 9) = 4.274, p = 0.0496; HFD vs. HFD + Pita, p = 0.0407], and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) [F(2, 9) = 4.608, p = 0.0419; HFD vs. HFD + Pita, p = 0.0473] were decreased in the HFD + Pita group, although there was no significant increase in the HFD group compared to the CD group. Furthermore, the levels of IL-6 [F(2, 9) = 1.599, p = 0.2545] and IL-15 [F(2, 9) = 1.051, p = 0.3888] did not significantly change in either the HFD or HFD + Pita groups, suggesting that these cytokines were not notably influenced by either the HFD or pitavastatin administration. These results suggest that HFD consumption may contribute to elevated cytokine and chemokine levels, potentially leading to chronic inflammatory conditions. Pitavastatin may enhance SERT availability

The PET images were captured at weeks 12 and 20. Figure 4 presents representative images illustrating the uptake of 4-[18F]-ADAM in various brain regions of mice, superimposed on MRI images, which serve as an anatomical reference delineating brain regions including the cortex, striatum, thalamus, hypothalamus, hippocampus, amygdala, midbrain, and brainstem (far left). The 2-way repeated measures ANOVA with Fisher’s LSD multiple comparisons test revealed that there were no significant differences in the SUVr of 4-[18F]-ADAM among the groups across any brain region at week 12 (Fig. 5, Table 1, Supplementary E). Good test–retest reliability of PET results in control groups across two time points was confirmed using the Bland–Altman test (Supplementary F). By week 20, the reduction was significant in the brainstem (CD vs. HFD, p = 0.0266), cortex (CD vs. HFD, p = 0.0023), hippocampus (CD vs. HFD, p = 0.0142), midbrain (CD vs. HFD, p = 0.0374), striatum (CD vs. HFD, p = 0.0073), and thalamus (CD vs. HFD, p = 0.0183) in the HFD group compared with the CD group (Table 1). However, compared to the HFD group, the SUVr of 4-[18F]-ADAM in the HFD + Pita group was significantly higher in the amygdala (HFD vs. HFD + Pita, p = 0.0185) and midbrain (HFD vs. HFD + Pita, p = 0.0427) at week 20. Additionally, when comparing the changes of the SUVr of 4-[18F]-ADAM across various brain regions between weeks 12 and 20, the HFD + Pita group exhibited significant increases in several regions, including the amygdala (HFD vs. HFD + Pita, p = 0.0048), hypothalamus (HFD vs. HFD + Pita, p = 0.0064), midbrain (HFD vs. HFD + Pita, p = 0.0028), and thalamus (HFD vs. HFD + Pita, p = 0.0024) compared with the HFD group (Table 2). These results suggest that pitavastatin may enhance SERT availability in specific brain regions associated with mood regulation and cognitive functions in the context of a HFD. Pitavastatin may exert an antidepressant-like effect in the sucrose preference test

Comparisons of sucrose consumption (g), total fluid intake, and the sucrose preference ratio among CD, HFD, and HFD + Pita groups are presented in Fig. 6A(Supplementary G). The CD group exhibited an average sucrose consumption of approximately 1.4 ± 0.65 g. Discussion

Hypercholesterolemia, induced by HFD in LDLR−/− mice, precipitates significant inflammatory responses and disrupts neurotransmitter systems, notably impacting SERT availability. The administration of pitavastatin effectively reversed these changes, demonstrating dual effects on reducing cholesterol levels, lowering inflammatory markers, and normalizing SERT availability in critical brain regions implicated in mood regulation. These results support the hypothesis that elevated cholesterol levels diminish SERT availability, potentially contributing to depressive-like behaviors. Pitavastatin treatment mitigates these effects, highlighting its potential as both a lipid-lowering agent and a modulator of neurochemical and behavioral disturbances associated with hypercholesterolemia.

Genetically modified LDLR−/− mice, which mimic human lipid abnormalities and atherosclerotic processes more closely, carry mutations that lead to high circulating levels of LDL, similar to conditions such as familial hypercholesterolemia in humans. This makes them valuable for studying the mechanisms of hypercholesterolemia and testing cholesterol-lowering therapies [35]. Wild-type mice naturally have a lipid profile that is significantly different from humans and are resistant to atherosclerosis, even when fed a high-fat diet. Most of their cholesterol is carried by HDL, rather than LDL. Extremely high-fat diets or other severe interventions are required to induce any form of atherosclerosis in wild-type mice, limiting their insight into human hypercholesterolemia and therapeutic interventions. This limitation underscores the necessity of using LDL knockout mice for our study, as they provide a more relevant and robust model for investigating the pathophysiology of hypercholesterolemia and evaluating the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions.

Our study demonstrated that a HFD in LDLR−/− mice leads to elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, signaling an inflammatory and oxidative cycle in the brains of hypercholesterolemic animals. These findings align with previous research, underscoring the link between high-cholesterol diets, disrupted lipid metabolism, increased oxidized LDL-C, and heightened levels of proinflammatory cytokines, all of which collectively contribute to neuroinflammation [36, 37]. Numerous studies have reported elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α in individuals with depression [38], noting correlations between cytokine levels and the severity of depressive symptoms. These cytokines may directly affect neurotransmitter systems or act through neuroendocrine pathways [39, 40], potentially disrupting the metabolism of key neurotransmitters like serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine. For example, cytokines can activate indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), which degrades tryptophan, a serotonin precursor, thus potentially reducing serotonin levels and contributing to depressive symptoms [41]. Furthermore, chronic inflammation might alter brain structures and functions, affecting critical areas for mood regulation and cognitive functions, such as the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, and amygdala [42].

Pitavastatin exhibits a broad spectrum of anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting key proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1α and TNF-α, and reducing the recruitment and differentiation of various immune cells, including monocytes (MCP-1/CCL2), T cells (RANTES/CCL5, MIG/CXCL9, IP-10/CXCL10), eosinophils (IL-5, eotaxin/CCL11), and neutrophils (MIP-2/CXCL2, G-CSF). Its pharmacokinetic profile, characterized by superior bioavailability, an extended half-life, and elevated protein binding capacity, enhances its efficacy in reducing LDL-C levels, moderating immune responses, and alleviating arterial inflammation [20]. CC chemokines, such as MCP-1 (CCL2) and MIP-1α (CCL3) which involved in the modulation of immune cell trafficking to sites of inflammation, have been observed dysregulated in individuals with depression, suggesting a role in neuroinflammation, which is often linked to the progression of depressive symptoms [43, 44]. CXC chemokines play roles in attracting neutrophils to inflammatory sites and modulating neuron-glia interactions, which may influence neuroplasticity and impact depressive disorders [43, 45]. Certain inflammatory markers did not show significant differences between the HFD and CD groups, but they did exhibit differences between the HFD and HFD + Pita groups. This discrepancy suggests that pitavastatin may target specific signaling pathways or cells involved in the inflammatory response, leading to differential modulation of certain cytokines and chemokines. Moreover, pitavastatin demonstrates superior efficacy over atorvastatin and rosuvastatin in reducing the production of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-2, IL-6, IFN-γ, and TNF-α in human T cells, and in inhibiting inflammatory cytokines and chemokines release in lipopolysaccharide-induced blood‒brain barrier dysfunction models [46, 47]. It also stabilizes cytokine expression and reduces leukocyte aggregation in a model of N-methyl-D-aspartate-induced retinal ganglion cell death [48]. Notably, in HIV patients with dyslipidemia, pitavastatin effectively prevents adverse cardiovascular events more than pravastatin by reducing vascular inflammation, enhancing endothelial function, and increasing plaque stability [23, 49]. These multifaceted mechanisms of action, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antiapoptotic effects across various models of neurological injury and disease, indicating its broad therapeutic potential in neuroprotection.

Cholesterol also plays a critical role in modulating the SERT by binding at multiple sites, which are crucial for its activity and interaction with the cell membrane [50–52]. Research has identified a specific cholesterol-binding site on SERT, CHOL1, which influences the conformation and serotonin transport activity of SERT. Binding of cholesterol to CHOL1 shifts SERT to an outward-facing state with low substrate affinity, while its release shifts SERT to an inward-facing state with high substrate affinity, significantly impacting SERT function [10]. In the monkey nucleus accumbens, cholesterol depletion increased the binding of [3H]citalopram to SERT. Conversely, when membranes of the monkey caudate nucleus and putamen were supplemented with cholesterol, ligand binding to SERT decreased, indicating that cholesterol modulates SERT function by affecting ligand interactions [53]. Interestingly, the cholesterol-binding sites observed in SERT are preserved in dopamine transporters, suggesting that the interaction of cholesterol may extend to the entire family of monoamine transporters [54]. Additionally, individuals with different alleles of the SERT gene-linked promoter region (5-HTTLPR) exhibit varying LDL-C levels, indicating genetic interplay between LDL-C levels and the SERT polymorphism [55]. These findings suggest that cholesterol can modulate the conformation, pharmacology, and transport kinetics of SERT, thereby influencing ligand interactions with the transporter and the efficacy of SERT-targeted drugs.

Based on the PET imaging results, we demonstrated that hypercholesterolemia reduces SERT availability in specific brain regions of LDLR−/− mice, such as the midbrain, striatum, cortex, and, hippocampus, and tends to induce depressive-like behaviors. Meta-analysis of in vivo and post-mortem studies by Kambeitz et al. indicates that patients with major depressive disorders have reduced SERT availability in the striatum, amygdala, and brainstem [56]. The comparison of SERT availability in depressed humans and LDLR knockout mice reveals consistent findings of reduced SERT associated with depressive-like symptoms. The restoration of SERT availability by pitavastatin suggests its potential to directly modulate serotonergic neurotransmission, which is associated with depressive disorders. Statins have been shown to affect SERT activity and serotonin uptake, impacting serotonergic neurotransmission either directly or through changes in membrane properties and cholesterol levels. For example, a study involving 17 hypercholesterolemic patients treated with simvastatin reported an increase in SERT activity within the first month, which diminished after two months of treatment [57]. Additionally, statin treatment has been found to enhance the lateral diffusion and mobile fraction of SERT in primary rat-midbrain neurons by reducing the membrane cholesterol content, accompanied by increased phosphorylation of SERT at threonine 276 [58]. Moreover, simvastatin has been shown to increase serotonin uptake through a mechanism involving lipid signaling intermediates regulated by geranylgeranylation within the isoprenylation pathway, independent of cholesterol levels [59]. These findings underscore the complex effects of statins on serotonin systems. Our results indicate that pitavastatin effectively reduces inflammatory responses and restores SERT availability, suggesting that pitavastatin has a beneficial impact on serotonergic transmission.

This study has several limitations. First, it relies on animal models to investigate the effects of hypercholesterolemia and pitavastatin treatment, which might not fully replicate human physiology. This discrepancy limits the direct applicability of the findings to human conditions, particularly regarding depression and alteration in neurotransmitter levels. Second, the brain’s neurotransmitter systems are highly complex, involving various neurotransmitters and their interactions. By focusing predominantly on SERT, this study may not capture the intricate mechanisms underlying depression and associated neurological changes fully. It is important to consider that other neurotransmitters, such as dopamine and norepinephrine, along with several neuromodulators, also play significant roles in mood regulation and could be influenced by hypercholesterolemia and statin treatments. Third, the study did not thoroughly investigate the long-term impacts of pitavastatin, which is crucial for understanding its effects on chronic conditions such as hypercholesterolemia and depression. Fourth, the absence of an additional control group on a standard chow diet with the osmotic mini pump is notable. Such a control would help delineate the effects specifically attributable to the mini pump and the carrier solvent, which were not controlled for. Finally, while the study indicated that pitavastatin may counteract the effects of hypercholesterolemia, the exact mechanisms through which pitavastatin affects neurotransmitter systems and behavior have not been fully elucidated. Additional research is necessary to confirm and elucidate these mechanisms, providing deeper insight into the potential therapeutic benefits of pitavastatin in the context of hypercholesterolemia-induced depressive-like behaviors.

In conclusion, our study provides compelling evidence for the beneficial effects of pitavastatin on hypercholesterolemia-induced alterations in SERT availability and depressive-like behaviors. Elevated cholesterol levels and inflammatory cytokines can downregulate the expression of SERT in the brain. This reduction in SERT availability leads to impaired serotonin reuptake, contributing to depressive symptoms. Pitavastatin's anti-inflammatory properties may counteract this effect by suppressing cytokine-mediated downregulation of SERT expression, thereby restoring serotonin reuptake and alleviating depressive behaviors. By highlighting the neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory mechanisms of pitavastatin, our findings enhance the broader understanding of how lipid metabolism, neurochemistry, and mood regulation are interconnected. Further research is crucial to fully explore the therapeutic potential of pitavastatin in this context, aiming for holistic approaches that encompass both metabolic and mental health.
