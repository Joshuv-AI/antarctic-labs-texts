Relative contributions of meteorological factors and atmospheric pollutants to childhood adenoid hypertrophy: A time-series study in Chongqing, China
Authors: Linlin Nie, Bo Pan, Jia Liang, Di Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdenoid hypertrophy significantly impacts children’s health. While respiratory diseases are influenced by air pollution and meteorological factors, their effects on adenoid hypertrophy remain poorly understood.ObjectiveTo quantify the effects of meteorological factors and air pollutants on childhood adenoid hypertrophy hospital visits.MethodsWe analyzed 236,438 hospital visits for childhood adenoid hypertrophy (2014–2019, 2023–2024) in children < 18 years in Chongqing, China (2922 observation days) using distributed lag nonlinear models. Environmental exposures were assigned at the district level based on monitoring stations nearest to each patient’s residential district. We quantified associations using standardized regression coefficients and attributable disease burdens, examining non-linear exposure-response relationships and seasonal variability. Environmental exposures included six meteorological factors (temperature, atmospheric pressure, humidity, wind speed, sunshine duration, rainfall) and six air pollutants (PM2.5, PM10, NO₂, SO₂, CO, O₃).ResultsOzone demonstrated the largest model-based attributable burden, with an estimated 16,231 cases associated with IQR increases in exposure. Summer showed extraordinary disease concentration (79.5% of cases). These findings provide preliminary evidence for environmental associations with adenoid hypertrophy in subtropical basin settings, with ozone and summer season emerging as key factors.ConclusionThis time-series analysis identified strong environmental associations with childhood adenoid hypertrophy in a subtropical basin setting. Ozone demonstrated the largest model-based association with childhood adenoid hypertrophy hospital visits(16,231 attributable cases, 6.9% of burden), with extraordinary summer disease concentration (79.5% of cases). These findings provide important evidence linking environmental exposures to adenoid hypertrophy patterns, though causal confirmation through future studies would be needed before informing environmental health strategies in comparable subtropical basin regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00405-026-10337-2.

Introduction

Adenoid hypertrophy is one of the most common pediatric upper respiratory conditions worldwide, affecting 30–40% of children ages 2–10 years—making it a leading reason for childhood surgery [1]. The disease develops when nasopharyngeal lymphoid tissue enlarges, causing nasal obstruction, mouth breathing, and repeated infections and obstructive sleep apnea syndrome [2, 3]. Beyond these immediate problems, chronic adenoid enlargement leads to serious developmental issues: abnormal facial growth (the so-called “adenoid facies”), speech difficulties, poor growth, and cognitive problems from disrupted sleep. Importantly, adenoid hypertrophy represents the leading anatomical cause of pediatric obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), a major sleep disorder affecting sleep quality and neurocognitive development in millions of children worldwide [4]. Adenoidectomy is among the most frequently performed pediatric surgeries globally, with rates climbing in many countries [1, 5]. In China alone, it’s one of the most common pediatric surgical procedures, placing considerable financial strain on families and the healthcare system. Yet despite this substantial clinical burden, environmental factors driving adenoid hypertrophy remain poorly understood—limiting our understanding of disease etiology.

Growing evidence shows air pollution and climate factors affect children’s respiratory health. Studies link PM₂.₅, nitrogen dioxide, and ozone to asthma, respiratory infections, and spirometry deficits through oxidative damage, inflammation, and immune alteration [6–9]. Systematic reviews found pollutants produce 5–15% risk increase per 10 µg/m³ PM₂.₅ or NO₂ [10]. Climate effects occur via multiple mechanisms: temperature affects virus replication and immunity, humidity influences mucociliary clearance, pressure alters autonomic activity [11–13]. Non-linear temperature-health relationships show increased risk at extreme temperatures [14, 15].

But critical knowledge gaps remain. First, most environmental health research examines asthma, pneumonia, and acute respiratory infections—adenoid hypertrophy receives minimal attention despite being equally common [16]. Few published studies have examined environmental determinants of childhood adenoid hypertrophy; this represents a substantial evidence gap in pediatric environmental epidemiology. Second, adenoid tissue occupies a unique anatomical position where inhaled air first enters, making it the sentinel organ for children’s developing immune system [4, 17]. Unlike deeper respiratory structures protected by multiple barriers, adenoid tissue is continuously exposed to air pollution and weather conditions. Since adenoid hyperplasia is triggered by antigenic stimulation, and both air pollution and weather directly influence immune function, pathogen survival, and inflammation [6, 16, 18], the absence of adenoid-environment research is biomedically striking.

This presents a “hidden” conundrum. Adenoid hypertrophy has all necessary features for substantial environmental health research: extraordinary clinical burden (one of the commonest pediatric operations), compelling biological plausibility (adenoid tissue first contacts inhaled environmental agents), and substantial public health impact. Yet there is very limited published research on environmental determinants. The research gap is astounding: thousands of studies investigate environmental determinants of pediatric asthma (10%–20% prevalence) and allergic rhinitis (15%–25%), yet adenoid hypertrophy (30%–40%) has no studies despite its strategic anatomic location.

Chongqing, a subtropical basin city in southwestern China (population > 30 million), has great environmental variability: high humidity (mean 78.8%), low wind speeds (mean 1.6 m/s), and temperature variation from winter lows near 0 °C to summer extremes exceeding 35 °C. This contrasts with temperate conditions where most pediatric otolaryngology studies occur but represents children in similar subtropical basin environments [19]. The region’s geography creates tight coupling between weather variables and pollutants, increasing pollution accumulation and weather-pollutant interactions, creating opportunities to examine seasonal effect modification patterns relevant to comparable subtropical basin regions.

We undertook among the first systematic investigation of environmental determinants of childhood adenoid hypertrophy in 236,438 hospital visits over eight years in children using distributed lag non-linear models. We quantified the relative magnitude of meteorological factors (temperature, pressure, humidity, wind speed, sunshine, precipitation) versus air pollutants (PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, NO₂, SO₂, CO, O₃) attributable to pediatric adenoid hypertrophy. We described exposure-response relationships including thresholds and risk windows. We quantified environmental disease burden through standardized effect estimates and attributable fractions.

Methods

Study design and setting

Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University is a tertiary referral center and the only children’s hospital in Chongqing, serving urban, suburban, and rural populations. As a nationally top-tier pediatric specialty hospital consistently ranked third in China, it provides a robust clinical data foundation for this study. This study collected data from January 1, 2014, through December 31, 2019, and January 1, 2023 through December 31, 2024. Data from 2020 to 2022 were excluded due to COVID-19 pandemic disruptions affecting health-seeking behaviors and introducing systematic bias.

Data collection

Health data

The outcome was daily outpatient visits by patients under 18 years for adenoid hypertrophy, classified using ICD-10 code J35.2. Diagnosis was determined by otolaryngologists using clinical history (nasal obstruction, mouth breathing, snoring, sleep disturbances), physical examination, and radiologic evidence: nasopharyngoscopy and lateral neck radiographs identifying adenoid-nasopharyngeal ratio > 0.7 or adenoid tissue occupying > 70% of airway [20, 21]. It should be noted that hospital visits represent healthcare utilization and serve as a proxy for disease burden, rather than direct measures of disease incidence or prevalence. Factors influencing healthcare-seeking behavior (e.g., symptom severity, parental awareness, school schedules, accessibility) may introduce additional variability beyond true disease occurrence patterns.

Ethics statement

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (IRB No. 2025 − 530). The studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. As a retrospective analysis of de-identified administrative health data, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived.

Environmental data

Meteorological parameters included daily average temperature (°C), atmospheric pressure (hPa), relative humidity (%), wind speed (m/s), sunshine hours, and precipitation (mm). Air quality data from Chongqing Municipal Ecology and Environment Bureau included six pollutants: PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, NO₂, SO₂, CO (µg/m³ or mg/m³), and O₃ (µg/m³).

District-level exposure assignment Fig. 1: Each patient was assigned environmental exposure values based on the monitoring station nearest to their residential district (main urban districts or surrounding counties). This district -level exposure assignment approach accounts for spatial heterogeneity in environmental exposures across Chongqing’s complex mountainous basin topography.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described using mean, standard deviation, median, interquartile range, and range. Spearman’s correlation coefficients examined associations between environmental variables and daily visits.

Exposure-response relationship analysis

We assessed associations between environmental variables and daily adenoid hypertrophy visits using quasi-Poisson regression with distributed lag non-linear modeling (DLNM) [22, 23], characterizing non-linear dose-response patterns and lagged temporal effects.

Cross-basis matrices employed natural cubic splines with 4 degrees of freedom (df) for exposure-response and 3 df for lag dimension (maximum lag = 21 days), selected via AIC/BIC. The 21-day lag captures chronic inflammatory processes over 1–3 weeks. Models included cross-basis functions for all environmental variables, natural cubic splines for temporal trends (8 df/year), day-of-week effects, and holidays.

To address temperature-pressure collinearity (Spearman ρ = -0.857), we fit two specifications adjusted for either temperature or pressure, maintaining identical covariate structures. Covariates were selected based on biological plausibility and low multicollinearity (VIF < 5).

Environmental effects were reported using: [1] Standardized coefficients (coefficient × interquartile range) for cross-exposure comparisons, and [2] Attributable cases comparing observed to minimum-risk exposures, with confidence intervals from Monte Carlo simulation.

Sensitivity analysis

We assessed the robustness of our primary findings by varying the degrees of freedom for long-term trend adjustment from 4 to 12 per year, based on established practice in environmental time-series epidemiology [22, 24]. AIC/BIC indicated optimal fit at df = 8 per year. Exposure-response curves across specifications are in Supplementary Figure S1. Key environmental associations (NO₂, O₃, temperature, humidity) showed < 20% variation in standardized coefficients.

Season-stratified analysis

Seasonal exposure-response relations are in Supplementary Figure S2. Statistical computing used R (version 4.0.2). Two-tailed P < 0.05 were significant. Reporting follows STROBE statement [25].

Non-linear exposure-response relationships

Figure 2 shows distributed lag non-linear model exposure-response curves for six environmental variables. Temperature had a nonlinear association with minimum risk at approximately 8–9 °C, slightly elevated relative risk below the minimum (RR < 1.0 relative to the mean temperature of 18.6 °C), and mild elevation of risk at moderate-high temperature (27 °C) (however not statistically significant). Relative humidity revealed a U-shaped relation with minimum risk at 70–80% humidity. Nitrogen dioxide had a complex M-shaped pattern with two peaks. Ozone demonstrated a nonlinear positive association with risk increased across the range of concentration. Atmospheric pressure showed an inverse J-shaped relationship. Wind speed had positive associations in the low velocity range (RR ≈ 1.10, 95% CI: 1.03–1.18 at 2.0 m/s).

Multivariable regression analysis

Table 3 shows the standardized regression coefficients for the fitted models adjusted for trends over time, weekday patterns and public holidays, where nitrogen dioxide was the most strongly inversely associated pollutant (standardized coefficient = -0.491, 95% CI: -0.537 to -0.444, P < 0.05). This counterintuitive finding is explored in detail in the Discussion. Ozone was the only pollutant to show a positive association (standardized coefficient = 0.258, 95% CI: 0.184–0.333, P < 0.05). Sensitivity analyses to explore the effect of adjusting for trends (Supplementary Table S3) confirmed remarkable stability of key environmental associations. Stratifying analyses by season (Supplementary Table S4) indicates slight heterogeneity, but in general NO₂ is negatively associated in all seasons, most strongly so in spring (St_β = -0.616, P < 0.05) and summer (St_β = -0.543, P < 0.05)—when NO₂ levels are usually lowest due to dilution and less heating emissions.

Df sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table S5) indicated that attributable burden estimates for key environmental determinants were highly robust across different df specifications for temporal trend adjustment (df = 4–12 per year). Ozone burden ranged from 14,089 to 18,456 cases across df specifications, which means we retain high confidence in the primary estimate. The temperature burden ranged from 84,640 to 91,915 cases (< 10% variation). The humidity burden showed 6,734 to 9,145 cases. Together this demonstrates robustness supporting confidence in the findings.

Seasonal attribution analyses (Supplementary Table S6) revealed tremendous concentration of burden in the summer. Temperature and humidity together explained 79.5% of summer adenoid hypertrophy (temperature: 20,993 cases, 36.6%; humidity: 24,630 cases, 42.9%).

Discussion

This investigation represents among the first systematic quantitative assessment of environmental associations with childhood adenoid hypertrophy. Analyzing 236,438 hospital visits over 2,922 days using distributed lag non-linear modelling, we identified ozone as the primary factor associated with hospital visits in our analysis. Complex non-linear exposure-response relationships were observed: temperature exhibited biphasic associations; humidity showed U-shaped patterns; and nitrogen dioxide presented unexpected inverse associations. Seasonal analyses revealed extraordinary summer burden with heat and humidity associated with 79.5% of summer cases.

It is important to note that our findings represent population-level associations derived from aggregated time-series data, rather than individual-level risk estimates. The observed associations between environmental factors and hospital visits reflect temporal patterns at the population level and should not be directly interpreted as individual risk predictions. Our study also faces complex confounding pathways characteristic of environmental epidemiology: environmental factors (temperature, humidity, pollution) influence respiratory pathogen survival and transmission; pathogen infections trigger adenoid hyperplasia; allergen exposure varies seasonally and is modified by weather conditions; and healthcare-seeking behavior is influenced by symptom severity, school schedules, and seasonal access patterns. These interconnected pathways create challenges for isolating independent effects of individual environmental factors.

This study has several limitations. First, our district-level exposure assignment constitutes ecological exposure assessment and does not capture individual-level exposure variation. We lacked information on indoor air quality, individual mobility patterns, school locations, and time-activity patterns affecting actual exposure. This ecological exposure misclassification may bias effect estimates and limits causal inference. Second, respiratory pathogen circulation data were unavailable, potentially confounding environmental associations with seasonal infection patterns. Third, we examined only short-term associations over a 21-day lag period and cannot comment on effects of chronic long-term environmental exposures. Fourth, findings should be considered most applicable to subtropical basin environments similar to Chongqing, and extrapolation to other geographic contexts requires validation studies.

The observed summer concentration of adenoid hypertrophy cases (79.5%) may reflect multiple pathways beyond direct environmental causation. Healthcare utilization patterns may contribute substantially to this seasonal pattern: families may be more likely to seek medical care during summer holidays when children are not in school, symptoms may become more noticeable during vacation periods, and referral patterns may vary seasonally. Additionally, the summer peak may reflect complex interactions between environmental conditions, respiratory pathogen ecology, allergen exposure [18], and healthcare-seeking behavior, rather than solely representing true disease incidence patterns.

The inverse NO₂ associations contrast with positive associations reported in respiratory health literature [11, 12, 26]. Given the well-established oxidative and pro-inflammatory effects of NO₂ [18], a protective biological mechanism is implausible. The most parsimonious explanation is seasonal confounding: NO₂ concentrations peak in winter (heating emissions, atmospheric inversions) while adenoid hypertrophy visits peak in summer, creating inverse ecological associations. Seasonal stratification confirmed consistently negative associations strongest in spring/summer when NO₂ levels are lowest. Thus, NO₂ likely functions as a marker of unmeasured seasonal factors rather than a causal agent. This finding should be interpreted with appropriate caution and warrants investigation in other settings.

These observed associations may be explained by several biological mechanisms. Ozone demonstrated the largest model-based attributable burden in our analysis, consistent with established biological pathways [6, 11]. Ozone exposure may generate reactive oxygen species that could harm respiratory epithelium, disrupt epithelial barrier function through impaired tight junction proteins [27], and enhance inflammatory cytokine release. For adenoid tissue—nasopharyngeal lymphoid tissue serving immune surveillance functions—chronic inflammation is associated with lymphoid hyperplasia [28, 29]. However, these mechanistic interpretations should be considered alongside the methodological limitations discussed above.

Meteorological factors demonstrated complex non-linear associations. Temperature effects may operate through multiple pathways: lower temperatures are associated with reduced immune response and prolonged virus survival [30], while higher temperatures may favor different pathogen and allergen profiles [15]. Humidity demonstrated a U-shaped relationship consistent with respiratory physiology: optimal ranges (70–80%) may support mucociliary clearance [31], while extremes (low: <60%, high: >85%) are associated with increased disease burden [32]. These patterns may be relevant in similar subtropical basin environments, though local climate conditions and population characteristics may modify these relationships.

Wind speed effects in our study likely reflect Chongqing’s unique topographical constraints. In this basin environment, even moderate wind speeds may inadequately disperse pollutants, and higher winds could potentially resuspend particulates within the basin [33]. These findings may have limited applicability beyond similar basin cities and should not be generalized to other urban settings with different topographical characteristics [19, 34, 35].

Our findings from Chongqing’s subtropical basin environment may be relevant to similar urban settings with comparable climatic and topographical characteristics. However, generalizability to other geographic regions requires validation through multi-city studies across diverse climate zones [15]. Local climate conditions, population characteristics, and healthcare systems may modify environmental-disease relationships.

Our finding have potential implications for pediatric otolaryngology and environmental health policy. Ozone showed a notable association within our model with adenoid hypertrophy(16,231 attributable cases, 6.9% of burden), suggesting it may be an important environment factor in subtropical basin settings. Summer accounted for 79.5% of cases, revealing a striking seasonal concentration that could help target future research on environmental triggers. Given that adenoid hypertrophy is the leading anatomical cause of pediatric obstructive sleep apnea, these environmental associations may offer insights into sleep-disordered breathing prevention. If confirmed in future studies, our findings could inform environmental health strategies in comparable subtropical basin regions.

It is important to distinguish between standardized regression coefficients and attributable burden [22, 23]. While NO₂ demonstrated the strongest per-unit effect, ozone’s broader exposure range combined with its exposure-response pattern resulted in the largest attributable disease burden. This distinction is crucial for intervention prioritization: standardized effects guide mechanistic understanding, while attributable burden informs resource allocation and policy decisions.

This study has several strengths: it represents among the first systematic investigations of environmental determinants of childhood adenoid hypertrophy, includes a large pediatric cohort (236,438 visits), employs advanced DLNM modeling to capture non-linear exposure-response patterns, and provides district-level exposure assignment accounting for spatial heterogeneity. However, these findings require validation in other geographic and climatic settings.

Future research should prioritize prospective multicenter cohort studies with individual-level exposure assessment and detailed clinical phenotyping to enable stronger causal inference. Additionally, future studies should employ negative control analyses to assess potential unmeasured confounding and strengthen causal inference.

Conclusion

This investigation provides among the first quantitative evidence for environmental associations with childhood adenoid hypertrophy in a subtropical basin setting. Analyzing 236,438 hospital visits over eight years in a subtropical basin setting, we identified ozone as the leading factor associated with hospital visits in this study (16,231 attributable cases, 6.9%), with extraordinary summer disease concentration (79.5% of cases associated with temperature and humidity). Disease-specific environmental susceptibility patterns emerged through complex non-linear exposure-response relationships, with ozone, temperature, and humidity demonstrating the largest associations. These findings suggest potential targets for population-level interventions in similar settings, pending validation in other geographic settings. Focus on ozone reduction and summer-specific programs may help lower pediatric surgical burden in comparable subtropical basin regions.
