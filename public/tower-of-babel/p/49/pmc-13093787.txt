Non‐Specific Interstitial Pneumonia in Anti‐Jo1 Synthetase Syndrome With Probable Gougerot Syndrome: A Case Report
Authors: Ali Gohar, Ozaire Mohammad, Ruman Shahzad, Fatima Arshad, Abdul Rehman Shahid Khan, Hamad Qayyum, Joham Ajmal, Muhammad Husnain Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAnti‐synthetase syndrome (ASS) is a rare autoimmune disorder of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy spectrum, characterized by the presence of aminoacyl‐tRNA synthetase antibodies, most commonly anti‐Jo‐1. Clinically presents with interstitial lung disease (ILD), myositis, and arthritis, and may be confused with other autoimmune conditions such as polymyositis (PM), dermatomyositis (DM), and Sjögren's syndrome. We present a 41‐year‐old French‐Moroccan woman with progressive dyspnea, dry cough, weight loss, and diffuse myalgia. Extensive autoimmune and laboratory investigations revealed positive anti‐histidyl‐transfer RNA synthetase (anti‐Jo‐1), anti‐Ro52, and anti‐cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti‐CCP) antibodies, with elevated creatine phosphokinase (CPK) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). Electromyography (EMG) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) supported myositis, while chest computed tomography (CT) showed ground‐glass opacities and reticulations consistent with fibrosing interstitial lung disease (ILD). She was diagnosed with anti‐synthetase syndrome (ASS)‐associated ILD. High‐dose corticosteroids, cyclophosphamide, and later tacrolimus, alongside supportive care, were provided, which resulted in significant pulmonary function improvement (forced vital capacity [FVC] 56%–92%, diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide [DLCO] 32%–70%) with preserved exercise tolerance. Our case report illustrates the importance of considering ASS in patients with unexplained ILD and myositis. Prompt recognition and combined immunosuppressive therapy can achieve substantial functional recovery and prevent long‐term respiratory compromise.Keywords: anti‐synthetase syndrome, dermatomyositis, immunosuppressive therapy, interstitial lung disease, polymyositis, steroid therapy

Introduction

Anti‐synthetase syndrome (ASS) is an inflammatory syndrome of unknown etiology belonging to a subgroup of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIM) [1]. The characteristic finding of this novel syndrome is the presence of autoantibodies against aminoacyl‐transfer RNA synthetases (anti‐ARS Abs). There are 8 of these antibodies discovered till date, but Anti‐Jo1 (anti‐histidyl‐tRNA synthetase) antibody is the most prevalent of the autoantibodies in ASS, nearly found in approximately 70% of the patients [1], and is the strongest predictor of this syndrome [2]. Mostly affected patients are the ones with age more than 50 years, with female predominance [3].

The clinical manifestations usually involve a broad spectrum of symptoms ranging from fever, myositis, dermatological signs (exanthema on the hands) to systemic manifestations such as interstitial lung disease (ILD) and polyarthritis [4]. The prognosis of the patient usually depends on the degree of pulmonary insult/lung injury [5].

We present a rare case of a 41‐year‐old woman having anti‐synthetase syndrome with associated ILD and probable Sjögren's syndrome. The objective of this report is to highlight the importance of considering rare autoimmune disorders in the diagnostic checklists to diagnose and manage such conditions promptly and effectively.

Case History and Examination

A 41‐year‐old female, French Moroccan, and known diabetic began experiencing progressive symptoms including increasing dyspnea (NYHA class II), dry cough, fatigue, loss of appetite, and weight loss of over 18 kg in 6 months. She also developed diffuse muscle and joint pain, nasal crusting, dry throat and eyes, and minimal gastroesophageal reflux. She was a non‐smoker, non‐alcoholic, and used to travel annually to Morocco. Her family history was notable for a sister diagnosed with sarcoidosis involving the kidneys, liver, and lymph nodes.

Investigations and Management

Based on history and examination, differentials of ILD, sarcoidosis, obesity hypoventilation syndrome, malignancy, chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis, congestive cardiac failure, and polymyalgia rheumatica were made. An extensive autoimmune and inflammatory workup revealed a positive anti‐Jo1, anti‐Ro52, and anti‐CCP (112 U, N < 20), while anti‐DNA and ANCA were negative. Rheumatoid factor was mildly elevated at 31 (N < 14), and FAN was positive at 1/100. Aspergillus serology and QuantiFERON were negative. Basic labs showed persistent microcytic anemia with hemoglobin levels between 9 and11.8 g/dL, elevated creatine phosphokinase (CPK) (1050 IU/L), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) (515 IU/L), with mildly elevated gamma‐glutamyl transferase (GGT) at 1.7–2.5 times the normal limit. Renal and hepatic function were otherwise normal (Table 1). Electromyography showed moderate myogenic changes and spontaneous activity suggesting necrotizing myositis without motor deficit. Imaging including chest computed tomography (CT) showed ground‐glass opacities, reticulations, peribronchovascular thickening, and no honeycombing (Figure 1). Positron emission tomography (PET) scans excluded neoplasia but demonstrated moderate hypermetabolic interstitial abnormalities consistent with fibrosing interstitial lung disease (ILD) and myositis (Figure 2) (Table 2). Diaphragmatic ultrasound showed moderate right diaphragmatic paresis. Pulmonary function tests are shown in Table 3. The patient was diagnosed with anti‐synthetase syndrome, confirmed by the presence of anti‐histidyl‐transfer RNA synthetase (anti‐Jo1) antibodies, and associated with diffuse interstitial lung disease (ILD) of Nonspecific Interstitial Pneumonia (NSIP) type. She also exhibited features consistent with myositis, supported by muscle MRI and EMG findings, and a probable diagnosis of Gougerot–Sjögren syndrome, indicated by anti‐Ro52 positivity. Additionally, a moderate right diaphragmatic paresis was noted without full paralysis. The patient was enrolled in the CATR‐PAT clinical study, randomized to the cyclophosphamide (EDX) and azathioprine arm. She received 6 EDX cycles for 6 months. This was followed by three pulses of Solumedrol and initiation of oral corticosteroids at 100 mg/day, gradually tapered by 10 mg per month. She was switched to Imurel (azathioprine), which had to be discontinued due to hepatotoxicity. She was then started on Tacrolimus, which she continues to take at a dose of 4 mg twice daily. Corticosteroid therapy was tapered and maintained at 7.5–10 mg/day. Supportive treatments included Metformin for corticosteroid‐induced diabetes, atovaquone (after stopping trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole due to liver enzyme changes), calcium and vitamin D supplementation, lansoprazole, and paracetamol. Vaccination coverage was updated, including Prevenar 13, Pneumovax, COVID‐19 doses, flu vaccine, and Shingrix.

Outcome and Follow Up

Pulmonary function improved progressively. Her FVC improved from 56% to 92%, and DLCO from 32% to 70%, indicating significant recovery in lung capacity. The six‐minute walk test (TM6) showed preserved exercise capacity without desaturation. No hypoxia or hypercapnia was noted. Imaging studies remained stable with no signs of disease progression. Cardiac assessments including exercise tolerance test (ETT), troponin, and NT‐proBNP were normal. The patient continued to have some fatigue and mild musculoskeletal complaints but no serious complications. As of the latest evaluation, the patient is clinically and functionally stable under a regimen of Tacrolimus and low‐dose corticosteroids. Imaging and pulmonary function remain stable with no significant respiratory compromise.

Discussion

ASS is a chronic inflammatory autoimmune disorder, with a classic triad of ILD, arthritis, and mechanic's hands. The lung affectivity is seen in nearly 70% of the patients [6]. Most prevalent antibody is Anti‐Jo, followed by PL‐5 (2%–5%) and PL‐12 (2%–5%) antibodies [6]. This syndrome is closely related to polymyositis (PM) and dermatomyositis (DM).

The Anti‐Ro‐52 antibody is prevalent in nearly one third (37%) of the PM and DM cases and is strongly related to severe muscle, joint and lung involvement with a relatively worse prognosis due to the dampened response to steroids. La Corte et al. [7], compared clinical manifestations and antibody profile in 28 patients with novel ASS and 48 patients with typical PM and DM. Patients with later conditions had significant dermatological and muscular involvement while patients with ASS had ILD, mechanic's hands, and arthritis as unique features. Moreover, the study stated that ASS patients with positive anti‐Ro antibodies had worsening lung injury (ILD) as shown by CT scans, similar to our case (CT showed ground‐glass opacities, reticulations, and peribronchovascular thickening).

The myositis seen with PM and DM clinically presents the same as in ASS patients, but histopathologically there are few peculiar differences. In a study by Werner et al. [8] obtained muscle biopsies from 21 ASS patients. They stated that ASS‐associated myositis is characterized by unique myonuclear actin filament inclusions, including rod formation and a typical necrotizing perimysial myositis, which supported that ASS‐associated myositis is unique and should not be grouped among DM, PM, and other nonspecific myositis. The study provided evidence that the presence of myonuclear actin filament inclusions accurately identifies patients with ASS‐associated myositis with a sensitivity of 81% and specificity of 100% [8]. The weakening of the diaphragm (paresis) in our patient along with EMG changes (moderate myogenic changes and spontaneous activity) was attributed to the ASS‐associated myositis.

Moreover, the common dermatological manifestations of ASS include Raynaud's phenomenon and production of plaques on the hands and knuckles (mechanic hands) [9]. These two features distinguish ASS from DM, which usually present as heliotrope rash, V‐sign, violaceous rash, and shawl sign [9]. As far as the management protocol is concerned, steroids and immunosuppressive therapies are the treatment of choice for ASS [10] as per its autoimmune nature. Initially, steroids will be started at high doses (1 mg/kg/day) for 4–6 weeks, as depicted in our case (100 mg), then tapered over 9–12 months to the lowest effective dose (7.5‐10 mg) [11]. Immunosuppressive therapy, cyclophosphamide and tacrolimus arms, as in our case, was initiated as steroid‐sparing therapy [12]. Intravenous immunoglobulin therapy is recommended in patients who fail to respond to combined steroid and immunosuppressive therapy [13]. In our case, she responded well with steroids, cyclophosphamide, and tacrolimus. Symptomatic improvement (FVC improving from 56% to 92%, and DLCO from 32% to 70%) is a better marker of response to treatment. Comparison of our case with literature is shown in Table 4.

Limitations

The absence of histopathological confirmation for all suspected manifestations and the lack of long‐term follow‐up limit our study.

Lessons Learned

ASS can mimic other autoimmune and respiratory conditions (PM, DM, ILD), resulting in delayed or misdiagnosis. Anti‐Ro52 positivity may indicate a more severe ILD course and necessitate aggressive early intervention. Multimodal therapy, including corticosteroids and immunosuppressants, can achieve significant functional recovery. Long‐term monitoring is crucial to detect relapse or treatment‐related adverse effects.

Conclusions

To conclude, our case report points towards the importance of considering anti‐synthetase syndrome in patients with unexplained interstitial lung disease and myositis, particularly when anti‐Jo‐1 and anti‐Ro52 antibodies are present. Early recognition and initiation of combined immunosuppressive therapy can lead to substantial clinical and functional recovery, as demonstrated by the marked improvement of pulmonary function (FVC from 56% to 92% and DLCO from 32% to 70%) in our patient. A multidisciplinary approach remains essential to optimize outcomes and prevent long‐term respiratory compromise in this rare but potentially detrimental autoimmune condition.
