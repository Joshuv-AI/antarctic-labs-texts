Linking childhood adversity with borderline and depressive symptoms: the role of reward processing
Authors: Lia-Ecaterina Oltean, Radu Șoflău, Andrei C Miu, Aurora Szentágotai-Tătar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Childhood adversity (CA) predicts borderline personality disorder (BPD) and depression. Although underlying mechanisms are unclear, emerging data draw attention to reward processing (RP).Objective: Building on these, we investigated associations between variables and tested the mediating role of distinct RP dimensions in the associations between CA and both BPD and depressive symptoms using structural equation modeling.Method: Participants (N = 1048; m = 25.24; SD = 5.34) completed self-report measures on variables of interest.Results: We found significant associations between CA, BPD and depressive symptoms, and RP dimensions. Both reward responsiveness and learning mediated the association between CA and BPD symptoms, while only reward learning mediated the association between CA and depressive symptoms.Conclusions: These findings illustrate the key role of RP in the association between CA and both BPD and depressive symptoms and underscore the transdiagnostic nature of reward learning. Together, they suggest that this RP dimension may be an important treatment target in those with a history of CA experiencing BPD and depressive symptoms.KEYWORDS: Childhood adversity, borderline, depression, reward processing, structural equation modelling

Introduction

Borderline personality disorder (i.e. BPD) encompasses a pervasive pattern of instability in interpersonal relationships, self-image, and affect, and increased impulsivity (DSM-5-TR, American Psychiatric Association, 2022). With an early debut, chronic course (Bohus et al., 2021), and lifetime prevalence of around 5.9% (Grant et al., 2008), BPD is one of the most prevalent and impairing mental disorders, and its symptoms yield substantial costs, including high mortality (Leichsenring et al., 2024). These costs are often amplified by co-occurrence with other frequent disorders (Choate et al., 2021). Among these, depression stands out, with comorbidity rates between these two disorders raising as high as 83% (Lieb et al., 2004). In addition, BPD and depression share several similar affective (e.g. sadness, emptiness) and behavioural (e.g. suicidal behaviour) symptoms (e.g. APA, 2022), suggesting common factors may underlie them.

Among these factors, empirical data indicate the importance of childhood adversity (i.e. CA; e.g. Field et al., 2015). CA includes various experiences of abuse, neglect and household dysfunction (e.g. Cicchetti & Toth, 2005), and predicts both BPD (Porter et al., 2020) and depressive symptoms (Bronsard et al., 2016; Kessler et al., 2010). Moreover, CA predicts their earlier onset (Ibrahim et al., 2018; Nelson et al., 2017), lifelong impact (e.g. Cicchetti & Banny, 2014), and increased comorbidity (Field et al., 2015).

Still, major theoretical models maintain that CA does not produce these negative consequences by itself, but via underlying mechanisms (e.g. Beck & Haigh, 2014; Linehan, 1993). Albeit insufficiently understood, these mechanisms involve disruptions in affective, cognitive and behavioural developmental processes (e.g. Cicchetti & Banny, 2014). Reward processing (i.e. RP) is one of the most important candidates (e.g. Gerin et al., 2019), as it mobilises affective, cognitive and behavioural processes (Carcone & Ruocco, 2017; NIMH, 2016). RP serves the acquisition, consumption and learning based on rewards and has three corresponding dimensions: responsiveness, valuation and learning (NIMH, 2016).

Given that this system is particularly sensitive during development and reaches maturity only during adulthood (Geier, 2013), CA may recalibrate it (e.g. Gerin et al., 2019), altering responsiveness, valuation and learning of rewards (e.g. Pizzagalli, 2014; Ruge et al., 2024). However, RP recalibrations may contribute to significant dysfunction. Aligned with this view, data document RP impairments follow CA (for a detailed review, see Oltean et al., 2023), and these impairments are associated with psychopathology (e.g. see Paret et al., 2017 for BPD symptoms; for depressive symptoms Nusslock & Alloy, 2017).

Reward processing in borderline and depressive symptoms

According to Linehan, invalidating environments that punish, trivialise, and react inconsistently to emotional responses contribute to ineffective emotion regulation and may help to explain BPD symptoms (1993). RP impairments may be integrated into this theoretical model, in which invalidating environments prevent adequate responsiveness, valuation and effective learning based on rewards. Supporting this view, RP impairments in BPD emerge early and contribute to symptoms’ manifestation (e.g. Babinski et al., 2023; Barker et al., 2015; Berenson et al., 2021; Saunders et al., 2016).

While increased RP has been generally associated with BPD symptoms (e.g. Kimbrel et al., 2012), impairments tend to vary by dimension (e.g. Tull et al., 2010). There is support for elevated reward responsiveness (Vega et al., 2013) and valuation (Barker et al., 2015), and attenuated reward learning (e.g. Endrass et al., 2016), which may explain the high emotional reactivity, impulsivity and faulty decision making observed in those experiencing BPD symptoms (e.g. Paret et al., 2017). However, contrasting findings exist (Kobeleva et al., 2014), and while encouraging, the role of RP in BPD symptoms remains unclear in the absence of direct evidence. Moreover, isolating these impairments from those associated with other similar symptoms, especially depressive ones, is difficult (Koudys et al., 2019).

Still, theoretical models posit an alternative mechanism in which negative cognitive patterns formed as a result of CA contribute to depressive symptoms (Beck & Haigh, 2014; Linehan, 1993). Fewer consistent and reliable interactions with caregivers can contribute to learning that there are rare opportunities for positive experiences and little control over what causes them, which may blunt RP. Supporting this view, studies focusing on depressive symptoms report distinct RP patterns (e.g. Halahakoon et al., 2020; Kujawa & Burkhouse, 2017). Notably, most studies report attenuated RP across its dimensions (e.g. Admon & Pizzagalli, 2015; Keren et al., 2018; Vrieze et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2013).

However, existing literature delineating RP impairments in BPD and depressive symptoms is both limited and mixed (e.g. see Kasparek et al., 2023; see also Oltean et al., 2025; Potsch & Rief, 2023). Furthermore, whereas there is some evidence for RP’s implications for depressive symptoms following CA (e.g. Palacios-Barrios et al., 2024), direct evidence on BPD symptoms is lacking. Hence, it is unclear whether RP impairments that follow CA may have transdiagnostic implications or whether a symptom specific pattern may emerge (e.g. Soler et al., 2014; Williams & Uliaszek, 2019).

The current study

To address these gaps, the current study sought (1) to investigate associations between CA, RP, BPD and depressive symptoms, and (2) to investigate the mediating role of RP in the association between CA and both BPD and depressive symptoms. We sought to investigate these objectives by following current guidelines (NIMH, 2016), and built on work that argues parsing out RP dimensions has essential theoretical and practical implications (McLaughlin et al., 2019; Oltean et al., 2023). Therefore, we investigated reward responsiveness, learning, and valuation separately, and expected both symptom specific and common patterns of RP impairments would emerge.

Methods

Sample

Given that BPD symptoms tend to diminish in older populations (e.g. Stevenson et al., 2003), eligibility criteria was confined to being aged between 18 and 35. The a priori sample size estimation for a conservative minimum detectable effect size of 0.2, α = 0.05, power = 0.90 indicated a sample of 502 participants (Soper, 2022). By placing online announcements on social media (Facebook), we recruited a large sample (N = 1048; m = 25.24 years; SD = 5.34). Participants were mostly women (84.35%) and had at least some high-school education (98.42%). All participants provided online consent, filled in the questionnaires and were granted, upon request, a brief interpretation of their individual answers. All procedures were aligned with national and international ethical guidelines and adhered to internal regulations of the Babeş-Bolyai University Ethics Committee.

Measures

Sociodemographic variables

We collected sociodemographic data on age, gender and educational status.

Childhood adversity

We assessed CA with The Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ, Bernstein et al., 1997). CTQ is a well-known 28-item self-report scale that evaluates experiences of abuse (i.e. physical, sexual and emotional abuse) and neglect (i.e. physical and emotional neglect). Considering that these experiences often co-occur (Smith & Pollak, 2020), we computed a total CA score. CTQ has good validity and reliability (Bernstein et al., 1997). Likewise, in our study the scale exhibited excellent internal consistency (McDonald’s ω = .93).

BPD symptoms

We evaluated BPD symptoms with the McLean screening instrument for Borderline Personality Disorder (MSI-BPD; Zanarini et al., 2003). MSI-BPD is a 10-item self-report measure that yields dichotomous answers (Yes/No) and has both good sensitivity and sensibility (Zanarini et al., 2003). In our sample, the scale had acceptable internal consistency (McDonald’s ω = 0.77).

Depressive symptoms

We assessed depressive symptoms using a 10-item self-report screening measure: The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; Kroenke et al., 2001). This scale is easy to administer, and has good psychometric properties (Levis et al., 2019). Similarly, PHQ-9 displayed good internal consistency in our sample (McDonald’s ω = .88).

Reward processing

Aligned with existing guidelines (NIMH, 2016) and considering implications of isolating RP dimensions, we evaluated these dimensions separately. In doing so, we opted for brief self-report measures that have been previously used in similar studies (e.g. Johnson et al., 2016; Oltean & Șoflău, 2022). We assessed reward responsiveness using the Reward Responsiveness subscale of The Behavioural Inhibition System and Behavioural Activation System Scales (BIS/BAS; Carver & White, 1994). Its subscale consists of 5 items and exhibited acceptable internal consistency in this study (McDonald’s ω = .66). We evaluated reward learning using The Reward Expectancy subscale of The Generalized Reward and Punishment Expectancy Scales (GRAPES; Ball & Zuckerman, 1990). This 15-item subscale displayed good internal consistency in our sample (McDonald’s ω = .72). Finally, we evaluated reward valuation with the 4-item Drive Subscale of the BIS/BAS (Carver & White, 1994). The subscale exhibited acceptable internal consistency in this study (McDonald’s ω = .71).

Data analysis

We conducted all analyses in R (R Core Team, 2024). We computed descriptive statistics for all study variables, including socio-demographics. We estimated McDonald’s omega coefficients to assess internal consistency for all scales and tested multivariate normality using Mardia’s tests. We employed Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) with the lavaan R package (version 0.6.19; Rosseel, 2012) to examine the hypothesised model (see Figure 1), and test the mediating role of RP in the associations between CA and BPD and depressive symptoms. The model comprised six latent variables: (1) CA, measured with the five subscales of CTQ (Bernstein et al., 1997), (2) reward responsiveness, measured with the items of the Responsiveness subscale of BIS/BAS (Carver & White, 1994), (3) reward learning, measured with the items of The Reward Expectancy subscale of GRAPES (Ball & Zuckerman, 1990), (4) reward valuation, measured with the items of the Drive Subscale of BIS/BAS (Carver & White, 1994), (5) BPD symptoms, measured with the items of the MSI-BPD (Zanarini et al., 2003), and (6) depressive symptoms, measured with the PHQ-9 (Kroenke et al., 2001). Given the ordinal nature of our data, we chose the WLSMV estimator (Weighted Least Squares Means and Variance Adjusted), an estimator that is well-suited for ordered categorical variables and robust to non-normality (DiStefano & Morgan, 2014; Sass et al., 2014).

Model fit

Mardia’s tests for multivariate skewness (mSkewness = 647.24, p < .001) and kurtosis (mKurtosis = 5476.76, p < .001) indicated significant deviations from multivariate normality. The hypothesised model fitted the data well, χ²(1060) = 5840.86 (p < .001); CFI (Comparative Fit Index) = 0.932; TLI (Tucker-Lewis Index) = 0.928; RMSEA (Root Mean Square Error of Approximation) = 0.063 (95% CI = [0.061, 0.065]). These fit indices suggest that the model provided an acceptable fit according to recommended guidelines (e.g. CFI and TLI > .90, RMSEA < .08; Hu & Bentler, 1999; Xia & Yang, 2019). The analysis supported adequate relationships between latent variables and their observed measures (see Figure 2 for factor loadings).

Direct effects and covariances

Path coefficients indicated that CA was positively associated with BPD (β = 0.241, p < .001) and depressive symptoms (β = 0.185, p < .001; standardised and unstandardised coefficients are presented in Table 2). In addition, CA was also negatively associated with all RP dimensions (reward responsiveness, β = −0.214, p < .001; reward learning, β = −0.185, p < .001; and reward valuation, β = −0.180, p < .001). Significant associations emerged for reward responsiveness (β = 0.085, p = .005) and reward learning (β = −0.235, p < .001), and BPD symptoms. However, reward valuation was not associated with BPD symptoms (β = 0.028, p = .348). Moreover, a significant association for reward learning and depressive symptoms emerged (β = −0.322, p < .001). Still, reward responsiveness (β = 0.049, p = .067) and reward valuation (β = 0.008, p = .776) were not significantly associated with depressive symptoms.

Indirect effects

The indirect effect of CA through reward responsiveness was significant for BPD symptoms (β = −0.018, p = .010), but non-significant for depressive ones (β = −0.011, p = .082). Reward responsiveness explained around 6% of the total effect of CA on BPD symptoms. The indirect effects via reward learning were significant for both BPD (β = 0.043, p < .001) and depressive symptoms (β = 0.059, p < .001; see Table 3). Reward learning explained about 17.10% of the total effect of CA on BPD symptoms, and 25.80% of the total effect of CA on depressive symptoms. In contrast, we did not find support for the mediating role of reward valuation in the association between CA and neither BPD (β = −0.005, p = .361) nor depressive symptoms (β = −0.001, p = .777).

Discussion

Consistent with theoretical models (e.g. Beck & Haigh, 2014; Linehan, 1993) and empirical data (e.g. Field et al., 2015; Leichsenring et al., 2024), we found that CA was associated with both BPD and depressive symptoms. Moreover, replicating prior work that documents RP following CA (Oltean et al., 2023), we found associations between CA and reward responsiveness, learning, and valuation. Moreover, by parsing out these RP dimensions, we highlight nuanced associations with BPD and depressive symptoms.

Mirroring previous findings, including clinical and symptom-level research (e.g. Endrass et al., 2016; Kimbrel et al., 2012), we found associations between increased reward responsiveness and decreased reward learning and BPD symptoms. While our null finding on reward valuation apparently contrasts prior work (e.g. Barker et al., 2015), one possible explanation is that this RP dimension may characterise specific symptom profiles, such as those dominated by risky behaviours (Taylor et al., 2006). In contrast, impaired reward responsiveness and learning may reflect core BPD symptoms, rather than normative functioning, with increased reward responsiveness reflecting heightened emotional reactivity and decreased reward learning underlying impulsiveness and poor decision-making.

Adding to prior work (e.g. Oltean & Șoflău, 2022), we found decreased reward learning was associated with depressive symptoms, which could explain the enduring nature of negative cognitive patterns that accompany them. Partially diverging from literature (e.g. Admon & Pizzagalli, 2015; Keren et al., 2018), no associations for reward responsiveness or valuation emerged. Still, much of the existing literature relied on behavioural or neural measures of RP, which have modest associations with self-report ones (e.g. Zubovics et al., 2021), with our findings being mostly aligned with those of studies using comparable self-report measures (e.g. Li et al., 2015).

When we investigated the mediating role of RP, we found similar patterns. Both reward responsiveness and learning mediated the association between CA and BPD symptoms, whereas this latter RP dimension mediated the association between CA and depressive ones. However, reward valuation did not mediate any of these associations. One possible explanation for these findings stems from the type of measure used to evaluate reward valuation, which taps on persistence of reward pursuit. Extending previous work that focused on delayed gratification (e.g. Barker et al., 2015; Pulcu et al., 2014), this methodological choice may have led to capturing a distinct facet of reward valuation, underscoring the complexity of RP. While an alternative explanation is that impairments in reward valuation are specific to clinical populations (e.g. Barker et al., 2015), theoretical explanations illustrate why impairments in other RP dimensions may be more relevant.

On the one hand, models of BPD argue that invalidating environments characterised by CA contribute to emotional dysregulation and learned maladaptive responses (e.g. Linehan, 1993). Hence, reward responsiveness recalibrations following CA may lead to intense emotional reactions, and inconsistent reinforcement disrupting reward learning may contribute to maladaptive behaviours and suboptimal decision-making in those experiencing BPD symptoms. On the other hand, models of depression maintain CA bolsters maladaptive cognitions and negative distortions to cast shadow on subsequent positive experiences (e.g. Beck & Haigh, 2014). Consequently, reward learning impairments following CA may contribute to depressive symptoms via biased information processing.

Building on these theoretical explanations, we suggest that targeting BPD and depressive symptoms in treatments that capitalise on RP may prove effective (e.g. Craske et al., 2016). Those experiencing BPD symptoms may benefit from strategies aimed at regulating reward responsiveness (e.g. mindfulness) and learning (e.g. monitoring pleasant activities). Adding to work that advocates for treatments targeting reward learning (McLaughlin et al., 2019), our findings suggest that strategies aimed at enhancing it may prove useful for those experiencing depressive symptoms. Moreover, when BPD and depressive symptoms co-occur, treatments should prioritise this RP dimension. Given problematic treatment response rates for those with CA (e.g. Nanni et al., 2012), these clinical implications are particularly relevant.

Still, our findings must be interpreted considering study limitations. First, the cross-sectional design does not warrant direction nor causality between variables. Despite grounding our models in theoretical and empirical evidence (e.g. Babinski et al., 2023; Palacios-Barrios et al., 2024) and adopting a methodological approach (i.e. SEM) that accounts for simultaneous associations (Gunzler et al., 2013), alternative models are plausible. Although recent work suggests BPD symptoms mediate the association between CA and depressive symptoms (Zhong et al., 2024), and depressive symptoms predict RP (Potsch & Rief, 2023), we did not examine such models. Therefore, future research using longitudinal designs, accounting for alternative models, is needed.

Second, our study was conducted on a sample of mostly young, educated women. Though these characteristics correspond to eligibility criteria and are consistent with the incidence and prevalence of both BPD and depressive symptoms (APA, 2022), they may not reflect the spectrum of symptom manifestations across other demographic groups. Given that RP continues to develop into young adulthood (e.g. Geier, 2013) and may function differently across genders (e.g. Cardoso Melo et al., 2023; Pagliaccio et al., 2015), additional investigation on more diverse samples is warranted.

Third, the clinical status of participants could not be established. Although we assessed symptoms using consecrated self-report measures, these have faced criticism. For example, the utility of the PHQ-9 has been questioned, as it does not directly evaluate impairment or contextual factors that are crucial for clinical diagnosis (e.g. Carroll et al., 2020; Hlynsson et al., 2025). Hence, our findings are more applicable to dimensional presentations and require replication on clinical samples. Finally, we used self-report measures of RP. Whereas such measures are easy to administer and have clinical utility, they tap into subjective, explicit aspects of RP, and fail to capture its more objective, implicit features, which may be critical to the understanding of RP and its treatment implications. Therefore, additional research triangulating self-report measures with behavioural or neural ones is needed.

Despite its limitations, this study has merit, as it sought not only to illustrate intertwined associations between CA, BPD, and depressive symptoms but also to uncover potential mediators. To our knowledge, it is the first one to concomitantly probe the implications of distinct RP dimensions linking CA with both BPD and depressive symptoms. While our findings are suggestive of symptom-specific pathways, they also point out the transdiagnostic nature of reward learning. If replicated in future longitudinal studies, they may contribute to improving treatments in those with a history of CA that experience BPD and depressive symptoms.
