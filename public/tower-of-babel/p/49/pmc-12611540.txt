Residual Risks of Fetal Chromosome Aberrations When Cell‐Free DNA Prenatal Screening Is Normal: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Adriana I Iglesias, Diane Van Opstal, Florentine F Thurik, Mark Drost, Marjolein J A Weerts, Marieke Joosten, Karin E M Diderich, Vyne van der Schoot, Myrthe van den Born, Robert‐Jan H Galjaard, Stefanie van Veen, Eveline Goedegebuur‐Zwalua, Sabina de Weerd, Anneke Dijkman, Dimitri Papatsonis, Jérôme M J Cornette, Sander Galjaard, Maarten F C M Knapen, Krista Prinsen, Attie T J I Go, Kyra E Stuurman, Malgorzata I Srebniak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo estimate the residual risk of fetal chromosomal aberrations in pregnant women with normal cell‐free DNA (cfDNA) screening results to refine prenatal counseling.MethodsA retrospective single‐center study was conducted between April‐2017 and March‐2021. In total, 46,007 women received a normal cfDNA screening result. The cohort was subdivided into women receiving normal results for only chromosomes 13/18/21 (targeted cfDNA) and all autosomes (genome‐wide cfDNA with a test resolution ∼10–20 Mb). Cytogenomic follow‐up included prenatal or postnatal chromosomal microarray (CMA) data.ResultsOut of 46,007 women with normal cfDNA results, 806 (1.8%) were referred for CMA testing; the majority (511/806) were referred due to ultrasound anomalies. The residual risk for a pathogenic chromosomal aberration in the entire targeted cfDNA cohort was 1:641 (0.15%); in the genome‐wide cfDNA group, it was 1:699 (0.14%). For fetuses with ultrasound anomalies, the residual risk for a pathogenic chromosomal aberration in the targeted‐cfDNA group was 1:8 (13.3%), and 1:12 (8.1%) in the genome‐wide cfDNA group.ConclusionsThe residual risk of a pathogenic CNV after a normal cfDNA result is low in women who opt for screening. However, when ultrasound anomalies are detected, this risk is severely increased, justifying the use of invasive testing even with normal cfDNA results. These figures can be used for pre‐and‐post‐test counseling.Keywords: chromosomal microarray, chromosome abnormalities, prenatal cell‐free DNA screening, prenatal counseling, residual risk

Introduction

Prenatal cell‐free DNA (cfDNA) screening is increasingly used to detect fetal aneuploidies. Fourteen countries in Europe have adopted cfDNA screening as a national program/policy [1], and a variety of implementation models have been described [2]. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) strongly recommend the use of cfDNA screening over traditional screening with the combined test [3]. cfDNA screening has a high detection rate for the common trisomies: trisomy (T) 21 (Down syndrome), T18 (Edwards syndrome), and T13 (Patau syndrome) [4]. Its scope can be expanded to other chromosomal imbalances, such as sex chromosomal aneuploidies (SCA) [4], rare autosomal trisomies (RATs), structural chromosomal aberrations (SAs) [5, 6], and copy‐number variants (CNVs) [7, 8, 9]. As for any screening test, prenatal cfDNA screening will occasionally have discordant positive results (i.e., cfDNA results indicating a chromosomal abnormality while the fetus is chromosomally normal) as well as discordant negative results (i.e., normal cfDNA results when the fetus is chromosomally abnormal), the latter being the focus of this article (see also Section 2). It is worth noting that discordant negative cfDNA results are related to the biological and technical limitations of the test itself [10].

Although various studies have described the performance of cfDNA screening in large cohorts [11, 12, 13], only one study has specifically examined outcomes in a large cohort with normal cfDNA results and found 0.036% (4/10,975) discordant negative cases [14]. In addition, two studies calculated the theoretical residual risks for fetal chromosomal abnormalities based on chromosomal microarray (CMA) results, one in low risk pregnancies [15] and the other in pregnancies with ultrasound abnormalities [16]. In these two studies, theoretical estimates of residual risks for chromosomal aberrations were based on cfDNA screening‐non‐detectable aberrations. It is very difficult to investigate the residual risks in the general population due to obvious limitations of the follow‐up, as CMA was typically performed only in cases with ultrasound anomalies, intrauterine fetal demise (IUFD), or evident anomalies at birth. However, even underestimated figures may still provide useful guidance when counseling patients with normal results.

In this retrospective study (including cfDNA data from 2017 to 2021), we collected and evaluated available data from all pregnancies that received a normal cfDNA result and that were later referred to our department for CMA testing. Cases in which a chromosome aberration was detected later in pregnancy or after birth were defined as discordant negative cfDNA cases. These cases are described in detail and were used to calculate the residual risk for chromosomal abnormalities. These estimates can help refine patient counseling. In addition, we outlined the potentially underlying biological or technical causes leading to a discordant negative cfDNA result.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Population

We performed a retrospective single‐center study of a series of cases that underwent prenatal cfDNA screening between April 2017 and March 2021 at the Department of Clinical Genetics of the Erasmus MC University Medical Center, Rotterdam, The Netherlands. We included all pregnant women who (i) participated in the TRIDENT‐2 study, a nationwide implementation study of non‐invasive prenatal testing in the Dutch prenatal screening program for trisomies 21, 18, and 13 [11] and received a normal cfDNA result; AND (ii) that underwent follow‐up cytogenetic testing, primarily due to (a) abnormal findings discovered in an advanced fetal ultrasound scan (Supporting Information S1: Supplementary Table 1), (b) intrauterine fetal death, or (c) the birth of a child showing congenital anomalies; AND (iii) that received obstetric care in the referring region of the Erasmus MC. Further, postnatal clinical follow‐up, up to July 2024, was collected as well. Women participating in the TRIDENT‐2 study could choose between receiving cfDNA results for chromosomes 21, 18, and 13 (targeted cfDNA) or all autosomes (genome‐wide cfDNA). Due to national regulations, analysis of sex chromosomes was not performed within the TRIDENT‐2 study.

Laboratory Analysis and Bioinformatics of cfDNA Screening

Peripheral blood samples were collected at or after 11 + 0 weeks of gestation and sent to the Erasmus Medical Center in Rotterdam (one of the three clinical genetic laboratories performing cfDNA screening within the TRIDENT‐2 study). Included samples were processed as previously described [5] (see also detailed methods description in Supporting Information S1).

Follow‐Up Investigations

Chorion villus biopsies and/or amniotic fluid cells were investigated; blood of both parents was also sampled for trio analysis. When indicated, rapid aneuploidy detection for chromosomes 13, 18, 21, X, and Y with QF‐PCR (Quantitative fluorescence polymerase chain reaction) was performed. Normal QF‐PCR results were complemented with chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) with a resolution of 0.15 Mb (Illumina Infinium_CytoSNP_850Kv1.1 (cases from 2017), Illumina Infinium GSA + MD‐24 v1.0, v2.0 and v3.0 BeadChip (cases between 2018 and 2021)). Reported ultrasound anomalies were categorized as in Diderich et al., and Shaffer et al., (see also Supporting Information S1: Supplementary Table 1) [17, 18].

Definitions of Prenatal cfDNA Results and Classification of Chromosomal Aberrations

Discordant positive cfDNA result: An abnormal prenatal cfDNA screening result while the fetus is chromosomally normal as found after invasive diagnostic testing or neonatal examination.

Discordant negative cfDNA result: A normal prenatal cfDNA screening result, whereas the fetus is chromosomally abnormal as confirmed by invasive testing or after the child's birth. These chromosome aberrations involved submicroscopic abnormalities (microdeletions and microduplications < 10 Mb), (mosaic) RATs, (mosaic) SAs, uniparental disomy (UPD), triploidy, genome‐wide UPD and SCAs. Chromosomal abnormalities found by CMA were classified according to published recommendations [19]. (Likely) pathogenic variants and susceptibility CNVs for (mainly) neurodevelopmental disorders were first discussed within a multidisciplinary prenatal team and reported when considered relevant [20].

Descriptive Statistics and Residual Risk Calculations

The residual risk of a chromosome aberration after normal cfDNA results was calculated for the two groups. Group A represents the residual risk in the entire cohort (i.e., pregnant women that opt for prenatal cfDNA screening as first‐tier test, and received a normal cfDNA result). Group B represents the residual risk calculated in the group of pregnancies with fetal anomalies discovered in an advanced fetal ultrasound scan. The residual risks were calculated separately for the group that chose targeted cfDNA and the group that chose genome‐wide cfDNA and the resulting figures are given including and excluding susceptibility CNVs/risk alleles for neurodevelopmental disorders. The data were analyzed using R (http://www.R‐project.org). The difference in proportions was calculated using the function “prop.test.” A p value < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

Results

Between April 2017 and March 2021, 46,007 women received a normal cfDNA result from the genetic laboratory of the Erasmus MC: 9619/46,007 (21%) chose targeted cfDNA, while 36,388/46,007 (79%) preferred genome‐wide cfDNA testing. In total, 806/46,007 (1.8%) cases underwent follow‐up cytogenetic testing because of ultrasound anomalies detected by a fetal anomaly scan, intrauterine fetal death, or postnatally detected anomalies. In the group that chose targeted cfDNA, there were 22/9619 (0.23%) discordant negative cases (see Figure 1); in the group opting for genome‐wide cfDNA, there were 70/36,388 (0.19%) discordant negative cases (see Figure 2). There were no significant differences in cfDNA findings between the groups opting for targeted cfDNA and genome‐wide in the TRIDENT study (in terms of prevalence of T21), and in this cohort, we also found no difference in abnormal ultrasound findings between these groups (Figures 1 and 2).

General characteristics of women with follow‐up and discordant negative cfDNA results are shown in Supporting Information S1: Supplementary Table 2. The median maternal age was 31.3 years (range 20–42, mean 31.5), which is similar to the average age of pregnant women in the Netherlands (mean 31.4 years; source: Statistics Netherlands). The median BMI was 23.7, which is comparable with other publications in the Dutch population [5].

Table 1 summarizes the 22 cases where chromosomal abnormalities were found in the group that chose targeted cfDNA. It also includes the reasons for the discordant negative cfDNA results. Of these cases, seven (32%) could have been detected if the pregnant women had chosen genome‐wide cfDNA. These include one case of RAT (trisomy 9; case #6), four cases of SAs (trisomy 9p, trisomy 20p, a 6q16 deletion (20.6 Mb), and a 12q14 deletion (15 Mb); cases #7–10), and two cases of unbalanced familial translocations (cases #11 and 12). In both translocation cases, the familial nature of the chromosomal abnormality was unknown at the time of prenatal cfDNA screening. In 10 other cases (45%), submicroscopic chromosomal abnormalities (i.e., ≤ 10 Mb) ranging from 126 kb to 2.6 Mb in size were found. Two of these submicroscopic findings were microdeletions confirming the 22q11.2 deletion syndrome (OMIM: 611867). Finally, triploidy, undetectable with our cfDNA method, was identified in 5 of the 22 (23%) discordant cases. In the group that chose genome‐wide cfDNA, 70 discordant negative cfDNA cases were identified (Table 2). More than half of these cases (n = 41, 59%) involved submicroscopic chromosomal abnormalities with the 22q11.2 deletion syndrome found in five cases. The second largest group (n = 15, 21%) were cases in which the chromosomal aberrations were undetectable with “read‐counting‐based” cfDNA screening methods (i.e., uniparental disomy (UPD), triploidy, or genome‐wide UPD). In seven other cases (10%; cases #38–44), a discordant negative cfDNA result was often the consequence of a (combination) of biological and technical limitations of the test, including low FF, mosaicism and/or a borderline chromosomal aberration size (i.e., 12–15 Mb). This included cases #38 and 39, in which trisomy 18 was confirmed with CMA and that was likely missed due to low FF. In case #38, the SeqFF was 5.3%—VeriSeqFF unavailable. In case #39, SeqFF was 4.9 whereas VeriseqFF < 1%. Additionally, case #39 showed a discordant sex determination between cfDNA (based on the DEFRAG algorithm) and CMA results (see Supporting Information S1: Supplementary Table 4). In cases #40 and 41, SAs involving chromosome 18 were identified; Case #40 exhibited a 15.3 Mb deletion of 18p11.32p11.21, while case #41 had a 27.9 Mb deletion of 18q21.2q23, where fetal and placental mosaicism with a normal CTB was detected. In cases #42–44, mosaicism explained the normal cfDNA result. In case #42, a de novo unbalanced translocation between chromosomes 5 and 8 was identified, with an 8p gain present at only low levels in the CTB, while the 5p loss was not detected due to low FF (supported by a discordant sex determination between cfDNA test and CMA). In case #43, a mosaic 12.5 Mb deletion of the 3p26.3 region was found and in case #44 a de novo unbalanced between chromosomes 1 and 15 was identified postnatally; CMA revealed a 17 Mb 15q25.3q26 gain (unlike cases #42 and #43, mosaicism is suspected in case #44 as not placental examination was performed due to the postnatal nature of the investigation). Finally, seven of the 70 discordant negative cases (10%) involved SCA. Detailed information on these 70 cases can be found in Supporting Information S1: Supplementary Table 4, and Wisecondor profiles are presented in Supporting Information S1: Supplementary Figure 1.

The residual risk was calculated in the entire cohort (i.e., women that opt for prenatal cfDNA screening and received a normal results) and in a subgroup of pregnancies with fetal ultrasound anomalies. Table 3 summarizes the types of ultrasound anomalies found. The estimates presented below correspond to the residual risk of a chromosome aberration excluding susceptibility CNVs/risk alleles for neurodevelopmental disorders. In the entire cohort that opted for targeted cfDNA, the residual risk was 0.15% (1 in 641), whereas in the genome‐wide cfDNA group it was 0.14% (1 in 699). When focusing on pregnancies in which ultrasound abnormalities were observed after receiving a normal cfDNA result, the residual risk increased dramatically in both groups: from 0.15% to 13.3% in the targeted cfDNA group (p < 0.0001; 95% CI 6%–20%), and from 0.14% to 8.1% in the genome‐wide cfDNA group (p < 0.0001; 95% CI 5%–11%). Moreover, we observed that the residual risk was higher in the targeted cfDNA group (13.3%; 1 in 8) compared to the genome‐wide cfDNA group (8.1%; 1 in 12), with a p value of 0.15. Discussion

In this retrospective study, we present 92 cases with normal cfDNA results in which a chromosome aberration was identified during follow‐up cytogenetic testing. We show that for the population of pregnant women who opt for cfDNA screening and had a normal result, the residual risk for a (likely) pathogenic chromosome aberration is small (0.15% for targeted cfDNA and 0.14% for genome‐wide cfDNA). However, for the subgroup with fetal ultrasound anomalies, the residual risk for a chromosomal aberration in the fetus, despite a normal cfDNA, is significantly higher; this holds for both targeted cfDNA testing for trisomy 21, 18 and 13 (13.3%; 1 in 8) and genome‐wide cfDNA testing (for autosomal aberrations) (8.1%; 1 in 12). Although the difference between these groups (targeted vs. genome‐wide cfDNA) did not reach statistical significance, a trend towards significance was observed and may reach statistical significance in a larger sample size. This suggests that the choice of the cfDNA test can influence the residual risk. A higher residual risk following targeted cfDNA screening, particularly in the presence of fetal ultrasound anomalies, is not unexpected, given that this test only assesses chromosomes 13, 18, and 21.

Our data show that besides the choice of the cfDNA test, the residual risk is influenced by the inherent limitations of prenatal cfDNA screening in the Netherlands (cfDNA technology e.g., not able to detect triploidy and national decision not to test for sex chromosomal aberrations). These limitations are both technical and biological in nature. From a technical standpoint, the resolution was limited to 10–20 Mb (depending on the FF and read count [11]), and the “read‐counting‐based” algorithm used means that triploidy/UPD cannot be detected. Biological limitations include low fetal fraction (FF) and chromosomal mosaicism. Additionally, some chromosome aberrations were undetected as they are beyond the scope of the prenatal cfDNA screening in the Dutch context. This includes sex‐chromosomal aberrations (by law, not tested) and autosomal chromosome anomalies different from 13, 18 and 21 in the cfDNA targeted group. This might have led to a higher residual risk compared with genome‐wide NIPT analysis including analysis of sex‐chromosomal aneuploidies.

An essential parameter for interpreting cfDNA screening results is the fetal fraction [21]. FF is the amount of cfDNA, originating from apoptotic trophoblastic placental cells, relative to the amount of maternal cfDNA in the maternal circulation [22]. Therefore, any condition increasing the maternal cfDNA contribution and/or reducing the trophoblastic cfDNA contribution may lower the FF, which, in turn, will negatively impact the detection rate of prenatal cfDNA screening. Known factors influencing the FF can be divided into fetoplacental factors (e.g., gestational age, fetal aneuploidy, multiple pregnancy) and maternal factors (e.g., BMI, autoimmune disease, maternal age). In our cohort, two cases of trisomy 18 (cases #38 and 39) and a de novo unbalanced translocation (5; 8) (case #42) were not detected with cfDNA, most probably due to low FF. Increased maternal BMI [23] and fetuses with trisomy 18 cause lower FF [23, 24], leading to a decrease in detection rate. Maternal BMI > 25 was reported in both trisomy 18 cases, and a low FF (particularly for case #39) was measured. In case #42, placental studies revealed that the 5p loss (29 Mb) was 100% present in CTB; however, this was not identified with cfDNA screening due to low FF (supported by the sex discordance observed between cfDNA and CMA). It is worth mentioning that there is no gold‐standard method for FF calculation. Many approaches are described, each with its advantages and disadvantages [25]. Large variability in FF estimates has been reported by various studies, mostly based on differences in method, platform, and software used [26]. Therefore, it is important to be aware of these factors when interpreting cfDNA results.

Besides FF, the size of the CNV is another relevant factor influencing the residual risk of cfDNA screening. In this study, 51 out of 92 (55%) submicroscopic CNVs (< 10 Mb) were not detected with cfDNA; of these, 27 were (likely) pathogenic CNVs, and 24 were susceptibility CNVs. Although these undetected CNVs account for a very small percentage of the total cohort (0.06% for (likely) pathogenic CNVs and 0.05% for susceptibility CNVs, based on 46,007 cases), they represent a significant proportion—30% (27/92)—of all discordant negative cases. In an earlier work, we have shown in a systematic review that, in pregnancies where invasive testing was performed due to advanced maternal age or parental anxiety, 0.37% (1 in 270) carried a submicroscopic CNV associated with an early‐onset disorder [27]. Our current estimates are lower than earlier estimates [27]; this may be due to limited postnatal follow‐up (up to July 2024), the onset of some microdeletion syndromes (which may not yet have manifested itself), potential loss of follow‐up in patients who were referred to or studied at a different medical center, and the fact that some submicroscopic CNVs < 10 Mb were detected via prenatal cfDNA screening and thus excluded from this study. This aligns with findings from van Prooyen Schuurman et al. [5], where 16 of 80 fetal chromosome aberrations detected were smaller than 10 Mb. Lastly, the presence of mosaicism will also influence the performance of cfDNA, even if the FF is good enough and the size of the aberration is significant. Fetal chromosome abnormalities that are absent or only present at low levels in the CTB may not be detected with cfDNA screening [10]. The 8p gain in case #42 with only low‐level mosaicism in the CTB and the case of GMDD (#41, deletion 18q) are examples. These findings are consistent with the systematic review by Raymond et al. [28], who reported that genome‐wide cfDNA has a sensitivity of 77.4% for detecting structural chromosomal aberrations, even in cases with an optimal FF.

Our study shows that cfDNA screening detects a substantial number of fetal chromosomal aberrations; however, even after receiving a normal genome‐wide cfDNA result, the risk of chromosomal anomalies is still present. The published residual risk estimates in the literature are based on CMA results and are theoretical rather than actual risk figures [15, 16]. It is challenging to make a fair comparison between our study and others since these theoretical risk estimates do not consider biological factors that may impact the cfDNA result, such as low fetal fraction or chromosomal mosaicism. Moreover, the assumed cfDNA test from previous studies may differ from ours in terms of test resolution, scope of the test (i.e., common aneuploidies with or without sex chromosomes, or the definition of genome‐wide cfDNA) and indication for testing. In Maya et al. [15], the authors focused on pregnancies undergoing amniocentesis without fetal ultrasound anomalies and reported an overall theoretical residual risk of 1.07% when testing for common aneuploidies (excluding sex chromosomes) and 0.68% when testing genome‐wide cfDNA (which includes CNVs > 7 Mb and common microdeletions). In contrast, our study shows a lower residual risk in the entire cohort (0.15% for targeted cfDNA and 0.14% for genome‐wide cfDNA group). These differences may be related to variations in study design, inclusion criteria and assumed cfDNA screening method. For example, Maya et al. focused on pregnancies between 16 and 20 weeks, and assumed that mosaic abnormalities are not detectable by cfDNA screening, which in our experience is not always the case, as it depends on factors such as the level of mosaicism, the distribution of abnormal cells over the different compartments of placenta and fetus, but also on the FF. Additionally, incomplete follow‐up could explain the lower residual risks observed in our study. While we collected data from participants referred for follow‐up testing (either prenatal or postnatal microarray), we lacked access to data from the entire population with normal cfDNA results. Moreover, not all women with abnormal cfDNA results or fetal ultrasound anomalies opt for invasive testing and not all parents are willing to test their child. Submicroscopic aberrations may also manifest symptoms only in childhood rather than shortly after birth, making longer follow‐up necessary for their detection.

In a different study, Lu et al. [16] examined the theoretical residual risk of cfDNA in pregnancies with ultrasound anomalies, estimating an overall residual risk of 2%. In their study, pregnant women with ultrasound anomalies underwent CMA. In our study, we found a residual risk of 8.1% in cases with ultrasound anomalies and normal genome‐wide cfDNA results, which is higher than the 2% reported by Lu et al. This might be related to the scope of their theoretical test only targeting common aneuploidies, SCA and a limited set of CNVs (including 22q11.2 deletion and others) without specifying the size of cfDNA‐detectable CNVs or discussing RATs and their detection rates. Additionally, our study included cases of triploidy, which were not accounted for in Lu et al. Nonetheless, our findings—along with those of Lu et al. and others [29, 30]—reinforce the notion that cfDNA screening should not be used as a genetic test when fetal ultrasound anomalies are present. If ultrasound anomalies are detected during pregnancy and cfDNA results are normal, invasive diagnostic testing remains indicated.

This study has potential limitations; as mentioned previously, the lack of long‐term follow‐up is the major limitation of our study and probably causes an underestimation of the residual risks for a chromosome aberration when cfDNA test results are normal. Not all submicroscopic aberrations cause phenotypic abnormalities at birth, and some affected patients may not yet have been referred to a clinical geneticist. Moreover, data collection assumes that all patients from our region (South‐West Holland) who required cytogenetic testing were referred to our laboratory (either for chorionic villi biopsy or amniocentesis) when ultrasound anomalies were detected or after birth if the child presented an abnormal phenotype. This assumption is based on the fact that there are no other diagnostic laboratories in our region. However, not all patients with fetal ultrasound anomalies choose to undergo invasive testing, which further contributes to an underestimation of the calculated residual risks. Finally, an additional limitation of our study is the lack of data on sex chromosomal anomalies detected by NIPT, as these were not analyzed within the TRIDENT‐2 study.

In conclusion, based on our data, we can conclude that the residual risk for a chromosomal abnormality in the general obstetric population that received normal cfDNA result is about 1 in 641 (0.15%). However, in cases with ultrasound abnormalities, the residual risk is significant, at least 1 in 8 (13.3%) in the targeted cfDNA group, and 1 in 12 (8.1%) in the genome‐wide cfDNA group. These high risks justify offering invasive testing for the exclusion of chromosome aberrations in pregnancies with ultrasound anomalies despite normal cfDNA results.
