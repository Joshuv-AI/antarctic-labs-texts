A comment on ‘An MRI radiomics approach to discriminate hemorrhage prone intracranial tumors before stereotactic biopsy’
Authors: Xuewei Wu, Bin Zhang
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

We read the article entitled ‘An MRI radiomics approach to discriminate hemorrhage prone intracranial tumors before stereotactic biopsy’ with great interest1. The authors leveraged an MRI radiomics approach to discriminate hemorrhage-prone intracranial tumors prior to stereotactic biopsy. By extracting radiomic features from contrast-enhanced T1-weighted and T2-weighted images, the study constructed an SVM (Support Vector Machine) model with nine selected features that could predict future hemorrhage of intracranial tumors with favorable accuracy. Despite the strengths of this study, several issues need further discussion.

First, when compared to previous studies2,3, the hemorrhage rate (19.4%) reported between the initial MRI acquisition and the scheduled biopsy procedure appears incredibly high, which could be attributed to potential selection bias. Notably, the authors did not delineate the proportion of hemorrhagic occurrences in the training and validation sets as presented in Table 1. Moreover, the majority of lesions were either diffuse astrocytic tumors (n=44, 25.1%) or metastatic tumors (n=124, 70.9%). This ambiguity may confuse readers regarding the applicability of this model and its relevance within routine clinical practice for assessing the hemorrhage propensity of intracranial tumors. For instance, when diagnosing an intracranial tumor as either a metastatic carcinoma or a diffuse astrocytic tumor, it remains unclear whether this model can effectively predict its hemorrhagic potential. Additionally, a discrepancy in the original article’s discussion arises, as diffuse astrocytic tumors (n=44, 25.1%) are included in the study, yet limitations outlined in the text refer to ‘high-grade gliomas’.

Second, Zhang et al. employed the LASSO method for radiomic feature selection, followed by modeling with three machine learning classifiers [SVM, RF (Random Forest), and AdaBoost (Adaptive Boosting)]. They identified the SVM model with the best performance and further streamlined it using the ‘permutation importance’ method within the ‘DALEX’ package. This transparent and methodical approach resulted in the successful construction of a 9-feature model. We commend the authors for their exemplary methodology. Upon comparison between the SVM model with 20 features and the simplified SVM model with nine features, it was noted that the simplified version achieved comparable sensitivity, lower specificity, and decreased accuracy, but a higher AUC [area under the ROC (receiver operating characteristic) curve]. This indicates that the simplified SVM model outperformed in classifying hemorrhage-prone tumors, which shows an enhanced ability to differentiate between hemorrhage-prone and non-hemorrhagic tumors. However, in making this distinction, the simplified SVM model might erroneously classify certain samples as hemorrhage-prone tumors that should have been categorized as non-hemorrhagic tumors, resulting in reduced specificity and accuracy. To elucidate this further, a confusion matrix is essential for readers to grasp the disparities between the models. In the context of discerning hemorrhage-prone intracranial tumors, emphasis is typically placed on the ability to detect positives, with a focus on sensitivity and AUC. Hence, there is strong justification for selecting the simplified SVM model due to its superior performance in these aspects. We recommend that the authors incorporate additional evaluation metrics to provide a comprehensive assessment of the model’s efficacy, such as precision–recall curves, calibration, and clinical decision curve analysis.

Third, while the study extended the study period from 2005 to 2022 to include more cases, the limited sample size of the training and test dataset may have influenced these outcomes. The current favorable predictive performance may be attributed to an illusion caused by the small sample size, and there is a possibility of model drift occurring during validation at other centers, leading to a reduction in predictive accuracy. Therefore, the clinical application potential of the predictive model constructed in this study remains uncertain. More independent cohorts are required to train and validate the robustness of the feature selection process and model performance.

Despite these aforementioned points that need to be discussed, we sincerely appreciate the authors’ efforts in composing this article. Addressing these concerns will enhance the reliability of the study findings and their applicability in routine clinical practice.
