An approach to correlating the structural, electrical and optical properties of Mn2+-modified bismuth sodium titanate/BNT ceramics
Authors: Priyambada Nayak, Sasanka Sekhar Mishra, Subrata Karmakar, Hari Sankar Mohanty, Shakti P. Jena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In the current study, we investigated the structural, electrical, and optical characteristics of Mn-modified BNT ceramic systems with the nominal formula (Bi0.5Na0.5)1−xMnxTiO3, (x = 0.01–0.05), obtained by the solid-state reaction method. The structural analysis using Rietveld refinement suggested a rhombohedral phase with the R3c space group in all samples. Relaxor behavior was observed in all the modified samples, and Mn doping also promoted this behavior. At the same time, lower dielectric loss was noticed in the x = 0.05 ceramics. Detailed electrical characterization was performed through impedance spectroscopy studies. The Nyquist plot was fitted with the proposed RQC-RC circuit and found to be of the non-Debye type. Correspondingly, the grain and grain-boundary resistance values were determined. The AC conductivity data at different temperatures were fitted by the Jonscher's power law equation, σtot(ω) = A(T) × ωs + σdc(T); 0 < s < 1, supporting the correlated barrier hopping (CBH) model. The reduction in optical band gap energy (Eg) obtained from UV-visible spectroscopy and the decrease in the hopping barrier height (WM) suggest that Mn doping may induce defect states. The results are consistent with possible oxygen vacancy formation, which may contribute to localized hopping conduction in the modified ceramic system. Furthermore, the slim polarization loop confirmed the relaxor behavior observed in the modified samples. The combined results suggest that Mn doping is an appropriate approach for optimizing the functional properties of BNT ceramics, with x = 0.05 as the most favorable in terms of overall performance.

Introduction

In recent days, the rapid advancement of electronic information systems and increasing demand for sustainable energy have motivated researchers to develop novel materials that address environmental concerns and also fulfill the demands of modern electronic devices. Dielectric capacitors play a pivotal role in this context, and they have been used extensively in applications such as medical equipment, hybrid electric vehicles, inverters, and a range of other electronic systems owing to their high power density, fast charge–discharge characteristics, and suitable operational range.1,2 Among the various classes of dielectric materials, ferroelectric perovskites, with the ABO3 structure/perovskite crystal structure, have gained prominence. This is largely attributed to their ability to fulfill essential requirements related to electrical connectivity, multilayer integration, manufacturability, and packaging compatibility in several electronic devices.3–5 Consequently, these materials are widely utilized in our day-to-day life as actuators, sensors, ultrasonic motors, and ignitors. Mainly, the partially distorted BO6 octahedral structure enables the perovskite materials to support various technological applications. In this regard, capacitance, residual polarization (Pr), breakdown field strength (BDS), and maximum polarization strength (Pmax) are regarded as key factors when choosing a ferroelectric material for target applications.6,7 However, these parameters can be tuned by enhancing bulk resistivity, optimizing synthesis processes and other modifications. Particularly, the polarization mechanism is tailored by modifying the phase structure, regulating the dielectric relaxation, or defect engineering.8 Among the various ferroelectric candidates, lead-free Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3 (BNT) has attracted important attention in recent days due to its remarkable properties, such as high spontaneous polarization (Ps > 40 µC cm−2) and relatively elevated Curie temperature (TC ≈ 320 °C).9 This strong ferroelectric behavior is primarily attributed to the d0 electronic configuration and Bi-6p and O-2p orbital hybridization, which results in splitting of the Bi-6p and Bi-6s states, thereby polarizing the outer 6s electrons. Conversely, the large remnant polarization (Pr ≈ 38 µC cm−2) and high coercive field (Ec ≈ 73 kV cm−1) enhance the leakage currents, which limit the practical applications.10 In general, the high sintering temperature promotes Bi and Na evaporation during the synthesis process, leading to the formation of oxygen vacancies in the BNT sample. These oxygen vacancies significantly enhance the electrical conduction, and the system may undergo a transition from insulating to semiconducting and finally to the metallic state.11 Therefore, the use of BNT is restricted in ferroelectric and piezoelectric device applications. Extensive research has focused on modified BNT systems by making solid solutions with other perovskite materials (such as BaTiO3, K0⋅5Bi0⋅5TiO3, or K0⋅5Na0⋅5NbO3) or by adding rare-earth/transition-metal dopants on either site (A-site and B-site or both)12,13 to meet a particular requirement. Mainly, cation variations on the A-site, B-site, or even both sites are important to tune the structural and functional properties of BNT systems. Specifically, small modifications at the A-site in BNT not only ease the stoichiometric issues but also reduce the oxygen-vacancy concentration, which significantly improves the electrical properties and energy-storage performance. These enhancements are closely associated with phase transition behavior, structural modulation, and tilting of the oxygen octahedra. In previous studies, manganese (Mn) has been widely used as a dopant in various ABO3/A′A′′BO3 perovskite systems. For example, Mn doping in K0.5Na0.5NbO3 (a piezoelectric system) improves the ceramic density and enhances the electromechanical coupling factor. The presence of multiple valence states of Mn (Mn2+, Mn3+, and Mn4+) makes these materials particularly interesting. It has been observed that doping of Mn at the B-site of BaTiO3 ceramics acts as an effective sintering aid, reducing the sintering temperature and promoting grain growth. This leads to larger grain size, denser microstructures, and reduced dielectric loss with improved ferroelectric and piezoelectric properties.14 Zheng et al. reported a significant reduction of dielectric loss in Ba(Ti0.9625Zr0.0375)O3 ceramics with Mn doping at the B-site.15 The incorporation of Mn decreases dielectric loss primarily due to the formation of defect dipoles, represented as

To contextualize the novelty of this work, we focus on the previous result, which illustrates the research gap. For example, G. Ilamathi et al. determined the magnetic behavior of a BNT ceramic system modified with Mn on the B-site; however, details of the conduction mechanism were unclear.16 S. Madolappa et al. substituted Mn2+ at the A-site of BNT ceramics, and only explained the dielectric behavior using the brick-layer model.17 In our previous study, we also investigated the effect of Mn doping at the B-site on the phase structure and microstructure, as well as the dielectric and electrical conduction properties of non-stoichiometric BNT ceramics.18 In contrast, very little work has been done on the conductivity mechanism, frequency-dependent dielectric studies, ferroelectric studies, and optical properties of BNT ceramic systems with Mn modifications at the A-site. Building on the promising results from the previous study, the current research aims to investigate Mn modifications on the A-site of BNT ceramics with nominal formula (Bi0.5Na0.5)1−xMnxTiO3 (x = 0.01–0.05). Hence, for the following reasons, we have selected Mn doping on the BNT ceramics. Firstly, the effective ionic radii of Mn2+ (0.83 Å) and Mn3+ (0.645 Å) are comparable to those of the A-site cations Na+ (1.02 Å) and Bi3+ (1.03 Å), satisfying the tolerance factor requirements for stable perovskite formation.19 Secondly, Mn doping can suppress the formation of oxygen vacancies associated with Bi2O3 volatilization at high sintering temperatures, which are detrimental to energy-storage performance.20 In addition, Mn2+–oxygen vacancy defect dipoles may form under an applied electric field and act as an internal bias upon field removal, promoting reversible domain switching. This leads to slim hysteresis loops with high maximum polarization and low remnant polarization, thereby enhancing energy-storage efficiency.21,22 Furthermore, Mn incorporation may increase A-site disorder, disrupt long-range ferroelectric order, and enhance the relaxor behavior in BNT ceramics.23 Therefore, unlike the previous reports, the present work correlates the structural modification with the dielectric, impedance, optical, and ferroelectric properties of Mn-doped BNT ceramics. Both intrinsic (grain and grain-boundary) and extrinsic (electrode–sample interface and defect-related) contributions, along with the conduction and relaxation mechanisms, are systematically analyzed using complex impedance spectroscopy, and an appropriate equivalent circuit has been proposed.24,25 Additionally, the reduced optical band-gap energy (Eg) induced by Mn-doping is analyzed together with the conduction mechanism, which provides a comprehensive understanding of the relationship between defect chemistry and lattice distortion, highlighting their potential for optical and energy-storage device applications.

Experimental

Materials and methods

Highly pure (99.99%) raw materials of Bi2O3, Na2CO3, TiO2 and MnCO3 were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich for the initial synthesis. Solid-state reaction processes were used to prepare the samples. First, the precursors were mixed in a stoichiometric ratio, and different concentrations of manganese were also taken, as given by the following equations:

For pristine BNTBi2O3 + Na2CO3 + TiO2 → 4Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3 + 2CO2For the MnCO3-doped BNT samples(0.5 − x)Bi2O3 + 0.5Na2CO3 + MnCO3 + TiO2 → (Bi0.5Na0.5)1−xMnxTiO3 + 0.5CO2

The weighted powders were mixed for 24 h using zirconia balls in a planetary ball milling machine so that all the powders were completely homogenized. The powders were then dried and calcined at 900 °C for 3 h in a high-temperature programmable furnace. PVA (polyvinyl alcohol) was added, and pellets of 10 mm diameter and 1 mm thickness were formed with a hydraulic press machine and finally sintered at 1000 °C for 3 h.

Characterization

Structural analysis was performed by using X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Rigaku), with Cu-Ka radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å), at room temperature from 20° to 80°. The morphological analysis was carried out by SEM (JEOL\T-330) along with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). For electrical characterization, all the samples were painted with silver conductive paste, and the dielectric and impedance data were collected over a broad frequency range (10 Hz to 1 MHz) and temperature (RT-500 °C) using a Wayne Kerr impedance analyzer. For ferroelectric property measurements (polarization (P) versus electric field (E)), we use a standard ferroelectric testing machine (Radiant Technology). Finally, to measure the optical properties, a UV-Visible spectrophotometer (UV-Shimadzu) was used in the wavelength range of 220–1400 nm.

Result and discussion

Structural analysis and microstructural analysis

Fig. 1(a) shows the XRD patterns of Mn-modified (Bi0.5Na0.5)1−xMnxTiO3, x = 0.00–0.05 ceramic systems calcined at 900 °C. As can be seen, the peaks are exactly matched with the rhombohedral structure of the R3c space group, referring to JCPDS card no. 36-0340, and indexing was done accordingly.26 No secondary peak was detected in the XRD profiles, which indicates that Mn2+ ions were successfully integrated into the BNT ceramic up to x = 0.05. Besides this, there is a small shift of the XRD peak towards the left as the Mn-content increases, as shown in the expanded region of the XRD pattern (Fig. 1(b)). This phenomenon indicates that Mn2+ may be present in the Bi-site or Na-site of the BNT system and was explored in more detail, considering that the radius of Mn2+ (r = 0.83 Å) is smaller than the radius of Na+ (r(Na+) = 1.02 Å) and Bi2+ (r(Bi2+) = 1.03 Å).27 Therefore, substitution of Mn2+, with a smaller radius than that of Bi/Na, may contract the unit cell volume and shift the peak. To confirm this, Rietveld refinement was carried out with Full Prof software to determine the structural parameters of the rhombohedral phase of the R3c space group.

To better understand the link between the tolerance factor (t) and doping concentration, we plotted the graph between them, as presented in Fig. 4. From the graph, it is perceived that the tolerance factor decreases with increasing doping content. This decline can again be understood by considering the difference in the ionic radii. Further, the reduction in the tolerance factor may enhance octahedral tilting and ferroelectric stability, which may be promoted due to structural distortion. Recent investigations show that ‘t’ must be related to the material's properties. According to Reaney et al., the temperature coefficient of the dielectric constant (εr) is governed by the tolerance factor (t).30 Similarly, Suchomel et al. proposed a correlation between the morphotropic phase boundary (MPB) and the tolerance factor in lead-based piezoelectric ceramics.31 Since the tolerance factor is closely associated with structural distortion, which, in turn, influences the conduction mechanism, a detailed investigation of the conduction behavior is presented in the succeeding section.

Fig. 5 depicts SEM micrographs of the Mn-doped BNT ceramics. From the figure, it is seen that all the samples show homogeneous grains with clear grain boundaries, confirming the dense microstructure. The grain size statistics indicate a gradually decreasing grain size (GA) with increasing Mn doping. The decrease in GA can be attributed to the lattice strain energy (ΔGstrain). XRD analysis indicates that lattice strain gradually decreases with increased Mn doping, which hinders grain-boundary movement and reduces the grain-growth rate. This behavior is further associated with the mismatch in the ionic radii between the host and dopant ions. Additionally, chemical composition analysis was carried out using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), as presented in Fig. 6. Peaks corresponding to all the elements (Bi, Na, Mn, Ti, and O) confirmed the successful incorporation of Mn into the crystalline matrix.

Dielectric response

With the aim of determining the dielectric constant of the Mn-modified BNT ceramic, it is pertinent to examine the dielectric temperature spectrum at different frequencies, as shown in Fig. 7(a). From the figure, it is perceived that the pure BNT ceramic displays two pronounced dielectric anomalies at around 160 °C and 360 °C, confirming phase transformations from the rhombohedral to tetragonal phase (Td) and subsequently from the tetragonal to cubic phase (Tc), respectively. However, after introducing Mn into the BNT ceramic, only one dielectric peak emerges, and this dielectric peak transfers towards the higher temperature site with increasing frequency, which demonstrates relaxor ferroelectric characteristics. The relaxor behavior of the modified samples can be quantitatively analyzed by the modified Curie–Weiss law, as expressed below:324where εr is the dielectric constant measured at temperature T, εm is the maximum dielectric constant at Tm, and the parameters ‘C’ and ‘γ’ denote the Curie–Weiss constant and degree of diffuseness, respectively. For normal ferroelectric systems, γ = 1, whereas for ideal relaxor ferroelectric systems, γ = 2. Fig. 7(b) shows the plots of (ln(1/εr − 1/εm)) vs. (ln(T − Tm)) measured at 100 kHz, and the γ values were obtained from the slopes of the linear fits and found to be 1.53, 1.56, 1.62, 1.66, 1.69, and 1.72 for ceramics with x = 0.00, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, 0.04, and 0.05, respectively. The progressive increase in γ with Mn-content indicates an enhancement in the relaxor behavior. This behavior is attributed to Mn-induced structural distortion arising from the coexistence of Na, Bi, and Mn ions at the A-site of the BNT lattice. Similar relaxor characteristics have been reported in other ferroelectric ceramic systems, such as (1 − x) (Bi0.4Na0.2K0.2Ba0.2)TiO3-xSr(Mg1/3Nb2/3)O3 and (1 − x)[0.9BaTiO3-0.1Bi(Zn0.25Ta0.5)O3]-xBi0.5Na0.5TiO3 ceramics.33–35

Fig. 7(c), shows the dielectric loss (tan δ) with temperature for all the compositions at various frequencies. Similar to the dielectric constant, the tan δ peak shifts towards higher temperatures with increasing frequency, indicating relaxor-type behavior. Furthermore, at any particular temperature and frequency, the tan δ value is lower for Mn-rich samples, which is attributed to the decrease in DC conductivity with Mn modification. In addition to the relaxor behavior, the dielectric properties are strongly influenced by internal stress, domain structure, and grain boundaries.36 In the undoped sample (x = 0.00), the relatively large grain size minimizes the effect of internal stress. But, in Mn-doped samples, the reduction in grain size enhances the contribution of the domains to the dielectric constant. Because the dielectric constant at the grain boundaries is significantly lower than within the grains, a higher grain boundary fraction amplifies their negative effect on the overall dielectric response. Thus, the dielectric constant decreases with decreasing grain size. However, the observed increase in the dielectric constant suggests that a defect-related mechanism dominates. Suppression of Bi volatilization enhances the internal disorder, which enhances the space-charge polarization. Importantly, the ceramics maintain a high Curie temperature TC (>300 °C) at the optimal Mn-doping level (x = 0.05), which corresponds to the best electro-induced strain performance.

Impedance spectroscopy

For electrical characterization of the prepared composition, impedance measurements were carried out over a broad range of frequencies (10 Hz to 1 MHz) at selected temperatures. This also revealed the effect of grains, grain boundaries, and interfacial effects along with the relaxation behavior. Fig. 8 reveals the variation of the real part of impedance (Z′) with frequency for all Mn-modified compositions. The graph shows that Z′ is much steeper up to 3 kHz and shallower after that. Meanwhile, with a rise in frequency, the Z′ value decreases, and a similar behavior is also observed with increasing temperature. It is worth pointing out here that, at low frequencies, the grain-boundary contribution is dominant, which is associated with the Maxwell–Wagner effect. However, the merging of Z′ for all temperatures in the high-frequency window is due to the release of space charges.37 The slope of Z′, i.e., the change of Z′ with frequency, also decreases with temperature, implying a negative temperature coefficient of resistance (NTCR) for all samples. Again, the value of Z′ gradually decreases as the doping content increases.

Variation of the real part of impedance Z′ with frequency at selected temperatures for the (Bi0.5Na0.5)1−xMnxTiO3, x = 0.01–0.05 ceramics.

Fig. 9 demonstrates the variation in the imaginary part of the impedance (Z″) with frequency for all the modified compositions. The value of Z″ rises progressively and attains its maximum value, then starts to deteriorate with respect to frequency and finally reaches zero at the higher frequency window for all the samples. Although no peak was detected at ambient temperature for any of the modified compositions, the frequency at which the Z″ peak amplitude is maximum is known as the relaxation frequency (fmax). The widening of the peaks with increasing temperature indicates the existence of a temperature-dependent relaxation process in the material.38 This relaxation may originate from electrons or immobile species at lower temperatures, while defects dominate in the higher temperature region. The fmax peak falls in a lower frequency band for the base compound (x = 0.01), which reflects the ease of oxygen vacancy migration in that sample, whereas the peak shifts towards the right side with increased Mn doping. The gradually reduced Z″ values in the modified composition suggest that the electrical behavior of the ceramics is significantly affected by the doping content.

The respective contributions of grains/grain boundaries, and electrode–sample interfaces to the electrical properties can be resolved using Nyquist plots (Z′ vs. Z″). Due to the inhomogeneous grain size, the responses of the grains and the grain boundaries typically appear as a depressed semicircle in the complex impedance spectra. Fig. 10 depicts the Nyquist plots (Z′ vs. −Z″) recorded at different temperatures for all the Mn-modified samples. At lower temperatures, only arc-like features are observed, indicating the insulating nature of the sample. With a rise in temperature, these arcs progressively evolve into well-defined semicircular responses. We also note that only one depressed semicircle is observed in the x = 0.01 sample; thus, the sample's bulk response and the semicircle center lying below the real axis (Z′) indicate the predominance of non-Debye type relaxation processes.39 However, when the Mn-doping concentration is >0.03, the space-charge/grain-boundary effects would be slightly more prominent, as was clearly visible for the x = 0.05 concentration sample. A parallel combination of resistor and capacitor and a constant phase element (Q) was used to fit the Nyquist plots to obtain the respective resistance and capacitance values by using ZSIMPWIN software, as shown in Fig. 8(f). The series network of (RQC) (RC) for different contributions at different temperatures can be understood through the fitting of individual components. The extracted parameters, such as grain resistance (Rg) and capacitance (Cg), along with the grain boundary resistance (Rgb) and capacitance (Cgb), at selected temperatures, are summarized in Table 2. The impedance of the equivalent circuit incorporating a constant phase element (CPE) is expressed by the following relation:405Here, ‘ω’ denotes the angular frequency, and the exponent ‘n’ quantifies the degree of depression of the semicircle; its value lies between 0 and 1 (0 ≤ n ≤ 1). A value of n = 1 indicates ideal Debye relaxation, whereas n < 1 signifies a non-Debye behavior associated with a distribution of relaxation times of dipoles within the sample.41 The fitted curves show good agreement with the experimental data, confirming the presence of two distinct relaxation mechanisms in the modified system. The simulation results revealed that both grain resistance (Rg) and grain boundary resistance (Rgb) decrease with increasing temperature, indicating thermally activated conduction behavior. Again, both Rg and Rgb values decrease noticeably with increasing Mn-content. One plausible explanation for this behavior is the defect chemistry and enhanced charge-transport mechanism induced by Mn doping. As Mn2+ substitution occurs preferentially at the Bi site of the A-site, a charge imbalance may be created because of the lower valence state of Mn2+ compared to that of Bi3+. To maintain charge neutrality, oxygen vacancies may be generated in the lattice. These oxygen vacancies act as defect centers that facilitate charge carrier hopping and enhance electrical conductivity. As a result, both the grains and grain boundaries become more conductive. However, grain-boundary resistance was higher than grain resistance due to the higher concentration of defects, structural disorder, trapped charges, and oxygen vacancy accumulation at the grain boundary. During sintering and cooling, oxygen vacancies and impurity defects tend to segregate at the grain boundaries, forming potential barrier layers. These barrier layers obstruct the movement of charge carriers between adjacent grains, thereby increasing the resistance at the grain boundaries. Furthermore, the decrease in ‘n’ values with increasing temperature indicates an enhanced distribution of the dielectric relaxation processes. Therefore, the obtained impedance results suggest that the electrical conduction mechanism in Mn-doped BNT ceramics is predominantly governed by oxygen vacancy-assisted hopping conduction.

Conductivity analysis

Electrical conduction in the prepared ceramic system is governed by the motion of charges/ions in the presence of an applied electric field. To further extend the experimental investigation, the ac conductivity (σac) value was calculated from the equation σac = εo × εr × ω tan δ, where ε0 denotes the permittivity of free space, ω is the angular frequency, and tan δ represents the dielectric loss.42Fig. 11 illustrates the frequency-dependent σac(ω) measured at different temperatures for the Mn-modified BNT ceramics. All the modified compositions exhibit similar conductivity behavior over the investigated temperature range, signifying the good thermal stability of the samples. The frequency-dependent conductivity behavior is well explained by the jump relaxation model (JRM) proposed by Funke.43 The ac conductivity (σac) spectra over the investigated frequency range can be categorized into three distinct regions: (i) a low-frequency region (f < 103 Hz), (ii) an intermediate-frequency region (f ≈ 103–104 Hz), and (iii) a high-frequency region (f > 104 Hz). The low-frequency section exhibits plateau behavior corresponding to the dc conductivity contribution from grains and grain boundaries. The mid-frequency region shows the first dispersion, which is typically related to localized charge carriers within the grains and trapped charges at the grain boundaries. The high-frequency region is characterized by a second dispersion, indicating enhanced charge-carrier mobility. The crossover frequency separating the frequency-independent and frequency-dependent regions represents the transition of ionic hopping from long-range to short-range motion and is referred to as the hopping frequency. Furthermore, with increasing temperature, the conductivity relaxation shifts towards higher frequencies, and the ac conductivity behavior follows Jonscher's universal power law equation:446σtot(ω) = A(T) × ωs + σdc(T); 0 < s < 1Here, σdc signifies the frequency-independent dc conductivity, while the exponent ‘s’ (0 ≤ s ≤ 1) reflects the degree of interaction between the mobile charge carriers and the surrounding lattice. The angular frequency is defined as ω = 2πf, and A(T) is a temperature-dependent pre-exponential factor. The σac experimental data were well fitted using this relation by simultaneously varying the parameters A and s. The fitting result was assessed from the coefficient of determination (R2), with lower residual values indicating a good agreement between the experimental and fitted data. Furthermore, the temperature dependence of the exponent ‘s’ provides important information about the conduction mechanism in the modified system. Different transport models can be identified based on the behavior of ‘s’ with temperature. A decrease in s with increasing temperature corresponds to the correlated barrier hopping (CBH) model, in which charge carriers move by hopping over potential barriers between localized defect states. When ‘s’ remains constant with temperature, the conduction process is associated with the quantum mechanical tunneling (QMT) model. In contrast, an increase in ‘s’ with temperature indicates the non-overlapping small polaron tunneling (NSPT) mechanism, whereas a non-monotonous change of ‘s’ is associated with the overlapping large polaron tunneling (OLPT) model. To find out the exact transportation mechanism, we plotted the graph ‘s’ with temperature, as shown in Fig. 12. The decrease in ‘s’ value with increasing temperature supports the applicability of the correlated barrier hopping (CBH) model. These results suggest that Mn2+-modified BNT exhibits potential photoactive behavior.52 A slight reduction in band gap energy is observed with increasing dopant concentration, reaching a minimum value of approximately 3.045 eV for x = 0.05, which can be attributed to defect chemistry effects and lattice distortion induced within the BNT crystal structure. In general, the substitution behavior of Mn2+ ions in the BNT lattice, particularly at the A-site (Bi-site), is governed by factors such as ionic radius, valence state, and coordination number of the dopant relative to the host cations (Bi). Furthermore, the decrease in the band gap energy is closely correlated with the reduction in the hopping barrier height, WM, obtained from the AC conduction mechanism based on the correlated barrier hopping (CBH) model. The Mn-induced defect-related states and oxygen vacancies may increase the density of localized states near the Fermi level, thereby lowering the potential barrier for carrier hopping between adjacent defect centers. P–E loop

Fig. 16 presents the hysteresis loops (electrical polarization vs. electric field (P–E)) of Mn-modified samples measured at room temperature. The hysteresis behavior indicates that both the coercive field (Ec) and remnant polarization (Pr) decrease progressively with increasing Mn doping. These values are strongly affected by factors such as composition and compositional homogeneity, defect concentration, applied electric field, and domain orientation, all of which influence the overall material response.55 A uniformly oriented domain structure generally enhances the ferroelectric performance. In this study, the higher Pr value observed for the sample with x = 0.01 suggests a more uniform domain configuration. In contrast, the reduction in Pr at higher Mn2+ doping levels can be ascribed to the domain non-uniformity and the presence of oxygen vacancies and defect centers, as confirmed from the conductivity and impedance analyses. The accumulation of these defects at grain boundaries disturbs the domain alignment and promotes leakage conduction, which suppresses ferroelectric switching behavior. Unlike conventional ferroelectrics, which exhibit stable long-range ferroelectric order and relatively high remnant polarization, relaxor ferroelectrics are dominated by thermally unstable polar nanoregions (PNRs). The limited thermal stability of these PNRs inhibits the development of long-range polarization, resulting in reduced remnant polarization. Conclusion

In conclusion, Mn-modified lead-free BNT ceramics were prepared by the solid-state reaction method, and the effects of Mn incorporation on the structural, microstructural, dielectric, impedance, and optical properties were systematically investigated. Structural analysis by the Rietveld refinement of the XRD patterns indicates the existence of rhombohedral phases for all compositions, with Mn ions preferentially occupying the A-site of the BNT lattice. Temperature-dependent dielectric measurements reveal a diffuse phase transition, with the degree of diffuseness increasing with increasing Mn-content. The ac conductivity behavior was analyzed in detail and indicates that the conduction mechanism supports the CBH model. UV-visible spectroscopy studies confirm that the band gap energy gradually decreases with increasing doping content. Furthermore, the slim polarization loop revealed the relaxor behavior of the modified sample. Overall, the structural, electrical, and optical studies collectively suggest that Mn-doping in Bi0.5Na0.5TiO3 (BNT) induces lattice distortion, and the results are consistent with possible oxygen-vacancy formation, leading to narrowing band gap energy and enhanced defect-assisted hopping conduction. These increased defect concentrations further disrupt long-range ferroelectric order and promote relaxor behavior with slightly improved energy storage performance. Among all the investigated compositions, the sample with x = 0.05 exhibited the most favorable overall performance. In conclusion, the results suggest that Mn modification is a useful approach for tuning the functional properties of BNT ceramics.
