Advanced receiver design for AF-FD cooperative schemes
Authors: Mohammad Al-Hattab, Hala Mostafa, Mohamed Marey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work investigates an amplify-and-forward full-duplex (AF-FD) relaying system that employs an intentional delay at the relay to enhance communication reliability in the presence of residual self-interference errors. The artificial delay emulates a convolutional coding structure, thereby introducing a diversity gain. The interference resulting from simultaneous transmissions by the source and relay is constructively leveraged to design an optimal detection algorithm, which is implemented using a Viterbi algorithm. To mitigate the complexity and latency associated with buffering the entire received frame, a sub-optimal detector with reduced delay is also developed. In addition, a joint maximum-likelihood estimator is proposed for the simultaneous recovery of both the channel coefficients and the artificially introduced delay. Furthermore, analytical performance expressions are derived to characterize the error probability of the proposed detectors and to serve as tight lower bounds under ideal detection conditions. Simulation results confirm the effectiveness of the proposed detectors and estimators, demonstrating superior performance over conventional methods.

Introduction

Full-duplex (FD) relaying enables a relay node to transmit and receive simultaneously over the same frequency band, thereby significantly improving spectral efficiency compared to conventional half-duplex systems1. The combination of improved throughput and reduced latency makes FD relaying well suited for next-generation wireless systems. It has garnered considerable attention for its applicability in a wide range of use cases, including 5G and anticipated 6G systems, where ultra-reliable low-latency communications, massive machine-type communications, and enhanced mobile broadband are key requirements2. It also holds potential in Internet of Things (IoT) networks by enabling efficient device-to-device communication3,4 and in satellite communication systems where spectral efficiency is paramount5.

Most existing studies on FD transmission protocols primarily emphasize information-theoretic evaluations, such as rate optimization6 and power allocation7. Nonetheless, a key challenge in these protocols is the management of interference arising from the simultaneous transmissions of the source and relay, which significantly impairs both data detection accuracy and parameter estimation reliability. In8, the authors assess the outage performance of FD relaying systems, highlighting the influence of channel estimation errors on system reliability under diverse relay configurations. The work of9 develops a linear minimum mean square error estimator that tracks both the source-to-relay (SR) and relay-to-destination (RD) channels in rapidly time-varying environments. The work of10 proposes a channel estimation algorithm that computes the combined SR and RD channels utilizing a few training symbols. In11, the authors present an iterative estimation framework utilizing the space-alternating generalized expectation-maximization algorithm operating under high mobility. In12, the authors propose a blind channel estimation method that exploits signal statistics to estimate channel parameters without pilots, thereby improving spectral efficiency of FD relaying systems.

It is typically assumed in the literature that the direct link between the source and destination has a minor impact and can be ignored in favor of the dominant relay-destination path9–12. Since this extreme scenario may not hold in realistic deployments, the interference caused by simultaneous transmissions from the source and relay nodes can be mitigated by employing orthogonal frequency- or code-domain separation, at the expense of additional bandwidth consumption1. RSI modeling

While FD relaying increases spectral efficiency, it causes self-interference that cannot be fully eliminated. From a modeling perspective, two main approaches are commonly adopted to model the residual self-interference component (RSI). Deterministic modeling explicitly characterizes the RSI structure, while stochastic modeling treats it as a random process. In this work, we adopt the widely used stochastic approach, where the RSI is approximated as an additive Gaussian noise term13,14. This approximation is well justified by the aggregate effect of multiple independent distortion sources, which tend to exhibit Gaussian behavior according to the central limit theorem. It is important to emphasize that the focus of this work is on data detection and parameter estimation at the destination node. Therefore, while RSI affects the overall signal quality through the equivalent noise term, the proposed receiver design at the destination node remains independent of any precise distributional assumption about it.

Upon closer inspection, it becomes apparent that the transmission process mirrors a convolutional code with k memory elements. At each time step, the output is generated by a combination of the current input and the stored previous inputs, thereby enhancing the robustness of the transmission. The transmission process can be effectively described using a state diagram, which visually represents the memory states of S node and the possible transitions between them based on input data symbols. Parameter estimation procedure

The implementation of the proposed detectors assumes prior knowledge of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_{SD}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_{SRD}$$\end{document}, and k at the destination. For completeness, we recall that the effective cascaded channel is given by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_{SRD} = \beta h_{SR} h_{RD}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} denotes the relay amplification factor. These parameters may be unknown at the destination in practice. To overcome this challenge, an ML-based estimation procedure utilizing a small set of pilot symbols is proposed.

Step 1: ML formulation

We consider P pilot symbols p(n) defined for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0 \le n \le P-1$$\end{document}, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p(n)=0$$\end{document} for all indices outside this range. Referring to the transmission model in (5) and Fig. (2), the ML estimates can be written as follows,42\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Big [\widehat{h}_{SD},\widehat{h}_{SRD},\widehat{k}\Big ]&=\textrm{arg}\max _{\widetilde{h}_{SD},\widetilde{h}_{SRD},\widetilde{k}}\log \, \textrm{Pr}\left( \left\{ r(n)\right\} _{n=0}^{P+\widetilde{k}-1}\left| \left\{ p(n)\right\} _{n=0}^{P-1},\widetilde{h}_{SD},\widetilde{h}_{SRD},\widetilde{k}\right. \right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where r(n) represents the nth received sample. The superscript \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widetilde{\circ }$$\end{document} is used to represent the trial value of the associated parameter.

Step 2: Likelihood decomposition

Instead of treating samples individually, we process the received signal in a paired structure. For each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n = 0,1,\ldots ,P-1$$\end{document}, we define the observation pair \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{r}^{(\widetilde{k})}(n)=\left[ r(n),r(n+\widetilde{k})\right] ^{T}$$\end{document} with T the notation of the vector transpose action. This formulation is valid because both r(n) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r(n+\tilde{k})$$\end{document} include contributions from the same transmitted data symbol through the direct and relayed links, respectively. Consequently, each transmitted symbol is observed through a two-dimensional observation window. Then, we can write43\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{array}{l} \textrm{Pr}\left( \left\{ r(n)\right\} _{n=0}^{P+\widetilde{k}-1}\biggr |\left\{ p(n)\right\} _{n=0}^{P-1},\widetilde{h}_{SD},\widetilde{h}_{SRD},\widetilde{k}\right) =\log \displaystyle \prod _{n=0}^{P-1}\textrm{Pr}\left( \textbf{r}^{(\widetilde{k})}(n)\biggr |\textbf{p}^{(\widetilde{k})}(n),\mathbf {\widetilde{h}},\widetilde{k}\right) , \end{array} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf {\widetilde{h}}=\Big [\widetilde{h}_{SD},\widetilde{h}_{SRD}\Big ]^{T},$$\end{document} and\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{p}^{(\widetilde{k})}(n)=\left[ \begin{array}{cc} p(n) & p(n-\widetilde{k})\\ p(n+\widetilde{k}) & p(n) \end{array}\right] .$$\end{document}In cases where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n-\widetilde{k} < 0$$\end{document}, the transmitted pilot at that index is set to zero, as illustrated in Fig. (2). Physically, this corresponds to the fact that the relayed signal has not yet reached the destination due to the propagation delay. Consequently, no invalid pilot indices are involved, and indices exceeding the pilot duration are inherently avoided by construction. For cases where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n+\widetilde{k}> P-1$$\end{document}, the pilot symbol \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p(n+\widetilde{k})$$\end{document} is replaced by a data symbol d, as the shifted index exceeds the pilot interval and falls within the data portion of the transmitted frame. This treatment ensures a consistent formulation by accounting for the contribution of unknown data symbols beyond the pilot region. Since the data symbol d is unknown at the receiver, it is replaced in the pilot matrix by its statistical average. This substitution enables a tractable formulation while preserving the statistical contribution of the unknown data symbols.

Step 3: Gaussian likelihood model

Assuming complex Gaussian noise with variance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _n^2$$\end{document}, each term of (43) becomes44\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{Pr}\left( \textbf{r}^{(\widetilde{k})}(n)\biggr |\textbf{p}^{(\widetilde{k})}(n),\mathbf {\widetilde{h}},\widetilde{k}\right) =\frac{-1}{\pi \sigma _{n}^{2}}\exp \left( \frac{-1}{\sigma _{n}^{2}}\left\| \textbf{r}^{(\widetilde{k})}(n)-\textbf{p}^{(\widetilde{k})}(n)\mathbf {\widetilde{h}}\right\| ^{2}\right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf {\widetilde{h}} = \left[ \widetilde{h}_{SD},\; \widetilde{h}_{SRD}\right] ^T.$$\end{document} Thus, maximizing the likelihood is equivalent to minimizing the squared error.

Step 4: Equivalent optimization problem

After substituting (43) and (44) into (42) and removing irrelevant components, the ML problem reduces to45\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \left[ \widehat{\textbf{h}}, \widehat{k}\right] = \arg \min _{\widetilde{\textbf{h}}, \widetilde{k}} \sum _{n=0}^{P-1} \left\| \textbf{r}^{(\widetilde{k})}(n) - \textbf{p}^{(\widetilde{k})}(n)\mathbf {\widetilde{h}} \right\| ^2. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This corresponds to a least-squares estimation problem.

Step 5: Estimation of channel coefficients

For a fixed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widetilde{k}$$\end{document}, the optimal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf {\widetilde{h}}$$\end{document} is obtained by solving a standard least-squares problem, yielding46\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf {\widehat{h}}^{(\widetilde{k})} = \left( \sum _{n=0}^{P-1} \textbf{p}^{(\widetilde{k})H}(n)\textbf{p}^{(\widetilde{k})}(n) \right) ^{-1} \sum _{n=0}^{P-1} \textbf{p}^{(\widetilde{k})H}(n)\textbf{r}^{(\widetilde{k})}(n). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This step provides the channel estimates assuming a given delay value.

Step 6: Estimation of delay parameter

The optimal delay \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{k}$$\end{document} is then obtained by substituting (46) into (45) and selecting the value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widetilde{k}$$\end{document} that minimizes the residual error47\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \widehat{k} = \arg \min _{\widetilde{k}} \sum _{n=0}^{P-1} \left\| \textbf{r}^{(\widetilde{k})}(n) - \textbf{p}^{(\widetilde{k})}(n)\mathbf {\widehat{h}}^{(\widetilde{k})} \right\| ^2. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Finally, the channel estimate is obtained as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbf {\widehat{h}} = \mathbf {\widehat{h}}^{(\widehat{k})}$$\end{document}. It is worth noting that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widetilde{k}$$\end{document} lies within the range \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0 \le \widetilde{k} \le K-1$$\end{document}, where K denotes the maximum delay parameter defining the search space of the timing offset. As expected, increasing this range introduces a trade-off: while it enables the detection of larger delays, it also increases computational complexity and may degrade estimation accuracy due to the expanded search space. We emphasize that this trade-off is not specific to the proposed method but is a general characteristic of delay estimation algorithms. In practical systems, the value of K is not arbitrary but is typically determined by system-level considerations such as the cell size and propagation environment. For instance, larger cell radii or longer relay paths require a wider search range, whereas small-cell deployments allow for a much tighter bound on K.

Complexity analysis

The computational complexity of the considered algorithms is evaluated in terms of floating-point operations (FLOPs), which quantify the total number of basic arithmetic operations required for implementation. In this work, a FLOP is defined as a real-valued addition or multiplication, while complex operations are expressed in terms of their equivalent real operations (e.g., one complex multiplication corresponds to multiple real FLOPs). Under this metric, the considered algorithms exhibits fundamentally different scaling behaviors. The ML-based parameter estimation procedure involves low-dimensional least-squares operations and a one-dimensional search over the delay parameter, resulting in a total complexity that scales linearly with both the number of pilot symbols P and the search range \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_{max}$$\end{document}, i.e, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {O}\left( PK_{max}\right)$$\end{document}. In contrast, the optimal data detector, implemented via a Viterbi-based algorithm, requires the evaluation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M^{k+1}$$\end{document} branch metrics per time instant due to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M^{k}$$\end{document} trellis states and M outgoing branches per state, leading to an overall complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {O}\left( NM^{k+1}\right)$$\end{document}, which grows exponentially with the delay parameter k. To mitigate this complexity, the proposed sub-optimal detector adopts a symbol-by-symbol detection strategy with successive interference cancellation, reducing the search space to a two-dimensional constellation search. As a result, its complexity scales as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {O}\left( NM^{2}\right)$$\end{document}, independently of k. The detailed breakdown of FLOPs for each algorithm is summarized in Tables 1, 2, and 3, highlighting the significant complexity reduction achieved by the sub-optimal approach compared to the optimal detector.

Beyond computational complexity, the optimal detector requires the transmission of k termination (zero) symbols to ensure that the trellis terminates in the all-zero state required by the trace-back procedure. This introduces a bandwidth efficiency loss quantified by k/N which becomes more pronounced for short frame lengths. The sub-optimal detector, however, does not rely on trellis termination and therefore does not incur any bandwidth overhead.

Furthermore, the processing delay of the optimal detector is inherently high, as it requires buffering the entire frame of N received samples before the trace-back operation can be performed. In contrast, the sub-optimal detector operates in a sequential manner and requires buffering only k received samples, corresponding to the channel memory, enabling significantly lower latency and near real-time processing. Consequently, although the optimal detector achieves superior detection performance, it does so at the cost of high computational complexity, increased processing delay, and reduced bandwidth efficiency, whereas the sub-optimal detector offers a more practical trade-off between complexity, latency, and spectral efficiency. Simulation results

We perform a comprehensive performance analysis via Monte Carlo simulations to validate the proposed algorithms. The system operates under a packetized transmission scheme, where every packet includes 1000 data symbols. Both S and R nodes are assumed to transmit with unit power, using 16-QAM modulation. To reflect the influence of RSI, we assume \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu = 0.2$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda = 0.1$$\end{document}. Instead of explicitly modeling path loss, node geometry, and shadowing as separate components, we adopt a variance-based representation in which the channel variances implicitly account for their combined effects. This approach allows for a more flexible and general performance evaluation without restricting the analysis to a specific deployment scenario. For example, a shorter distance between nodes does not necessarily imply a stronger channel due to potential shadowing or blockage effects, which are inherently reflected in the channel variance. The channel coefficients are characterized as zero-mean complex Gaussian random variables with the following variances: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _{SD}^{2}=1$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _{SR}^{2}=1.8$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _{RD}^{2}=2$$\end{document}. All benchmark schemes are implemented under the same channel conditions, including identical fading statistics of the existing links. Conclusions

This study introduced an innovative AF-FD relaying technique that employed deliberate delay to improve the reliability of wireless transmissions. Simulated delays implemented at the relay were utilized to mimic convolutional codes, thereby attaining a diversity gain that enhanced detection efficiency. The interference from simultaneous transmissions by the source and relay was utilized as a valuable asset in the development of a Viterbi-based optimum detector. A sub-optimal detector was created for real-time deployment to mitigate the buffering and latency limitations of the ideal technique. A combined ML approach was devised to precisely estimate the unknown delay and channel characteristics. Additionally, analytical formulations are developed to assess the error probability of the proposed detectors and to yield tight lower bounds under ideal detection assumptions. The simulation results validated the efficacy of the estimate approach and showed enhanced BER performance for both detection schemes. The suggested architecture demonstrated significant enhancements compared to conventional AF-FD relaying systems.
