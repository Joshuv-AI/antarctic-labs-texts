Longitudinal Serum Metabolomic Signatures Related to Integrase Strand-Transfer Inhibitors-Associated Weight Gain in Women With HIV Enrolled in the MACS/WIHS Combined Cohort Study
Authors: Chin-An Yang, Young-Mi Go, Dean P. Jones, Brahmchetna Bedi, Qian Yang, C. Christina Mehta, Igho Ofotokun, Anandi N. Sheth, Mohammed K. Ali, Sami Teeny, Maria L. Alcaide, Frank J. ABSTRACT
Background.Integrase strand-transfer inhibitors (INSTIs) are associated with weight gain in people with HIV, particularly in women with HIV (WWH), but mechanisms remain unclear. We used longitudinal untargeted metabolomics to identify metabolic signatures of INSTI-associated weight gain in WWH.Methods.Serum samples from 192 Women’s Interagency HIV Study participants were analyzed: 38 virologically suppressed WWH who initiated INSTIs, 88 virologically suppressed WWH who remained on their original antiretroviral therapy, and 66 women without HIV. Samples were collected at baseline, 1–6 months post-initiation, and 1–2 years post-initiation, with matched visits for controls. High-resolution metabolomics was performed by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry. Within each group, linear mixed-effects models adjusted for baseline age and BMI compared longitudinal metabolite changes between weight gainers and maintainers, followed by Mummichog v2.0 pathway enrichment.Results.Mean age was 45.7 ± 8.9 years and mean BMI 32.4 ± 8.3 kg/m2. In the INSTI group, differentiating metabolites were enriched in 16 pathways, predominantly lipid-related, including linoleic acid and arachidonic acid metabolism. Comparator groups showed broader pathway patterns, including amino acid metabolism.Conclusions.INSTI-associated weight gain in WWH is characterized by a lipid- and PUFA-dominated metabolic profile.

Integrase strand-transfer inhibitors (INSTIs) have become a cornerstone of antiretroviral therapy (ART) for people living with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) due to their efficacy, tolerability, and high barrier to resistance [1]. However, accumulating evidence indicates that initiating or switching to INSTI-based regimens can be associated with significant weight gain, particularly in non-White women with HIV (WWH) [2]. INSTI-related weight gain can lead to obesity and related comorbidities, potentially compromising longterm health [3].

Despite the potential clinical significance of this phenomenon, underlying biological mechanisms of INSTI-related weight gain in WWH remain poorly understood, hindered by varying individual INSTI molecules, person-to-person variability, and the confounding influence of “return to health” weight gain seen in some ART-naïve individuals initiating first ever ART. Several hypotheses have been proposed, including effects on adipose tissue metabolism, alterations in insulin sensitivity, and changes in inflammatory pathways [3, 4]. However, comprehensive metabolic profiling to identify specific pathways involved in INSTI-related weight gain has been limited.

Based on our previous findings, WWH experience nearly twice the body weight gain compared to men after switching to INSTI-based regimens during 5-year follow-up [5]. In a single-center pilot study utilizing an untargeted, high-resolution metabolomics (HRM) approach to investigate INSTI-related weight gain, we found that neurotransmitter-related metabolism may predict who gains weight, and the mechanisms may involve acute changes in fatty acid-related metabolism [6].

In this study, we performed longitudinal, high-throughput, untargeted HRM to identify metabolic signatures associated with INSTI-related weight gain in virologically suppressed WWH from all sites in the Women’s Interagency HIV Study (WIHS), the largest cohort of WWH in the United States which in 2019 merged with the MACS/WIHS Combined Cohort Study (MWCCS). By comparing metabolic profiles across multiple visits among INSTI users, non-INSTI ART users, and demographically similar women without HIV (WWoH), we aimed to elucidate specific metabolic pathways and molecular mechanisms underlying INSTI-related weight gain. Throughout the analysis, all comparisons were stratified by study group, with weight gainers compared to weight maintainers within each group, allowing patterns observed in the INSTI group to be situated against parallel patterns in the non-INSTI ART and WWoH groups.

METHODS

Study Population

The study consisted of 192 participants from WIHS, now part of the MWCCS. The MWCCS follows persons with HIV and demographically similar at-risk persons without HIV across the United States [7]. Recruitment, enrollment, and study procedures for MWCCS have been described in detail previously [7, 8]. Participants attended study visits every 6 months during which clinical data were collected through standardized interviewer-administered surveys, physical examinations, and biological specimen collection. Data for these analyses were contributed by 11 sites: Atlanta, Georgia; Baltimore, Maryland; Birmingham, Alabama/Jackson, Mississippi (combined site); Bronx, New York; Brooklyn, New York; Chapel Hill, North Carolina; Chicago, Illinois; Los Angeles, California; Miami, Florida; San Francisco, California; and Washington, District of Columbia. The MWCCS is approved by the institutional review boards of all participating institutions, and all participants provided written informed consent.

The selection criteria for WWH in this study have been described previously [5]. In brief, for eligible WWH who initiated an INSTI during follow-up, inclusion criteria were (1) documented history of switching from a non-INSTI ART regimen to an INSTI-containing regimen or adding an INSTI to an existing ART regimen; (2) maintained viral suppression (HIV RNA < 200 copies/mL) for 1 year before and after regimen modification; and (3) continuous use of the same INSTI medication for at least 1 year following modification. Participants with reported pregnancy or within 2 years post-partum, tuberculosis diagnosis, or cancer treatment were excluded. For WWoH, visits with reported HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis use were excluded. The current multi-site analysis builds on a prior pilot metabolomics study restricted to women from the Atlanta WIHS site [6]. Thirty women with HIV included in the prior Atlanta pilot study were also included in the present analysis. The visits included in this analysis spanned from October 2009 to August 2019, with a median visit date of February 2015. Consistent with this study period, the INSTI regimens represented in the cohort were primarily elvitegravir (EVG), dolutegravir (DTG), and raltegravir (RAL), with minimal bictegravir (BIC) use because BIC became available later in the study period.

Study Design

Participants were categorized into 3 study groups: women who added or switched to an INSTI-based regimen (INSTI) (n = 38), women who remained on non-INSTI ART (non-INSTI) (n = 88), and women without HIV, WWoH) (n = 66). Henceforth, we refer to timing of INSTI initiation as the first documented visit where women reported either switching to an INSTI from a non-INSTI regimen or adding an INSTI to an existing regimen. For women in the INSTI-group, we used demographic data, clinical data, medical history data, and serum samples at 3 specific time points: (1) baseline (Visit 1): 6–12 months before initiating an INSTI; (2) short-term followup (Visit 2): 1–6 months post-INSTI initiation; and (3) longterm follow-up (Visit 3): 1–2 years post-INSTI initiation. For non-INSTI and WWoH groups, samples were collected to align with similar calendar time periods. Participant weight measurements at each time point were analyzed. We further classified participants based on weight change observed over the study period (Visit 3–Visit 1) using a definition of clinically significant weight gain based on prior literature [9]: weight gainers (≥5% increase from baseline) and weight maintainers (<5% change from baseline).

High-Resolution Metabolomics

Untargeted HRM was performed using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) with a dual-column chromatography approach to maximize metabolite feature detection: hydrophilic interaction chromatography (HILIC) in positive ionization mode and C18 in negative ionization mode. This complementary approach enabled comprehensive coverage of both polar and non-polar metabolites. Serum samples were analyzed using standardized HRM methods within the Emory Clinical Biomarkers Laboratory [10]. Samples were treated with acetonitrile and an internal standard mixture prior to analysis. A Thermo Q-Exactive HF high-resolution mass spectrometer operating at 120 000 resolution with a mass-to-charge (m/z) scan range of 85 to 1275 was used [11]. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. Quality control was maintained using pooled reference plasma from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (Standard Reference Material, SRM, 1950) and Equitech-Bio Inc (SHP45), placed at the beginning, middle, and end of each batch of 25 samples. Raw data processing utilized apLCMS [12] and xMSanalyzer [13] for feature extraction, alignment, and quantification. Metabolic features (metabolites) were annotated using xMSannotator [14], which integrates multiple databases (METLIN, HMDB, and KEGG), based on exact mass matching and MS/MS fragmentation patterns when available.

Data Processing

R (version 4.2.3) was used for data processing, including key steps such as metabolite feature filtering, missing value imputation, and normalization. Features detected in fewer than 20% of samples were excluded to ensure data robustness. To address missing values, a dual imputation strategy was utilized based on the underlying missing data mechanism. The pattern of missingness—classified as either missing not at random (MNAR) or missing at random (MAR)—was inferred using auxiliary features with a Pearson correlation coefficient greater than 0.5 [15]. For MNAR features, quantile regression imputation of left-censored data (QRILC) was applied, while random forest (RF) imputation was used for MAR features, following best practices established in a prior study that systematically evaluated the performance of various imputation algorithms [16]. After imputation, the data were log2-transformed and subjected to quantile normalization to minimize technical variability and enhance comparability across samples.

Statistical Analysis and Bioinformatics

All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.3). All analyses were stratified by study group. Continuous characteristics were summarized using mean and standard deviation while categorical characteristics were summarized using count and percentage. Group comparisons of demographic and clinical characteristics between the weight change groups (gainer vs maintainer) were assessed using Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and ANOVA for continuous variables. To evaluate the association between weight change and longitudinal levels of metabolites over time, linear mixed-effects models were performed. These models included fixed effects for time (Visits 1, 2, 3), weight change group, and their interaction (time × weight change group), with a random intercept for each participant to account for within-subject correlation. Baseline age and BMI were included as covariates in all mixed-effects models (Model 1).

Model 1: Linear Mixed-Effects Model for Longitudinal Analysis

Let Yij denote the normalized metabolite feature for participant i at visit j. The model is as follows: Yij=β0+β1(weightchangegroupi)+β2(timei2)+β3(timei3)+β4(weightchangegroupi∗timei2)+β5(weightchangegroupi∗timei3)+β6(Baselineage)+β7(BaselineBMI)+θi+εij where θi is the random intercept and εij is the residual. Baseline (Visit 1) is the reference time level.

In this model, the coefficient β1 reflects baseline differences in metabolite levels between weight gainers and maintainers; β4 reflects whether the change in metabolite levels from baseline to the short-term follow-up visit (Visit 2) differs between gainers and maintainers; and β5 reflects the corresponding difference in change from baseline to the long-term follow-up visit (Visit 3). A metabolite feature was considered associated with weight change if any of these 3 coefficients was significant at P < .05. Significant features were then analyzed using Mummichog v2.0 [17] to identify metabolic pathways associated with weight change within each ART group.

A subset analysis was conducted using metabolites included in an in-house reference library, consisting of compounds with confirmed identities based on ion dissociation mass spectrometry (MS/MS) spectra and co-elution with authentic chemical standards. These metabolites meet the Level 1 identification criteria defined by the Metabolomics Standards Initiative [18]. Reference standardization was performed by calibrating sample signals against pooled reference standards to generate quantitative estimates based on mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) and ion intensity [19]. The FDR was tested using the BH procedure.

RESULTS

Baseline Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 192 women aged 25–70 years were included in the study, comprising 38 INSTI ART users, 88 non-INSTI ART users, and 66 WWoH. Across the cohort the mean age was 45.7 ± 8.9 years and mean BMI was 32.4 ± 8.3 kg/m2, with no statistical differences between study groups. In the INSTI group only, the mean age was 46.2 years, and the mean baseline BMI was 32.8 kg/m2. A total of 18 (47.4%) in the INSTI group were receiving EVG, 15 (39.5%) DTG, 4 (10.5%) RAL, and 1 (2.6%) BIC. Additional baseline clinical and demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1, with comparable characteristics in the non-INSTI and WWoH groups (Table 1). At baseline, there were no significant differences in clinical or demographic characteristics between weight gainers and weight maintainers within the INSTI group. Similar findings were seen in the non-INSTI and WWoH groups (Table 1).

Longitudinal, Metabolome-Wide Associations With INSTI-Related Weight Change

Figure 1 shows the analytical workflow and results of the metabolome-wide association study within each group (INSTI, non-INSTI, WWoH). Pathway enrichment analysis with resulting significant metabolites across time points is shown in Figure 2. Within the INSTI group, 688 and 1187 metabolite features significantly differ by weight change group in the C18 and HILIC modes, respectively, at any of the 3 time points (P < .05). Enriched pathways with metabolites differing at baseline were arachidonic acid metabolism, linoleate metabolism, omega-3 fatty acid metabolism, vitamin K metabolism, and vitamin A (retinol) metabolism. Enriched pathways of metabolites significantly differing acutely (1–6 months) were linoleate metabolism, omega-3 fatty acid metabolism, de novo fatty acid biosynthesis, histidine metabolism, vitamin A (retinol) metabolism, ubiquinone biosynthesis, and phytanic acid peroxisomal oxidation. Enriched pathways of metabolites significantly differing over 1–2 years were bile acid biosynthesis, squalene and cholesterol biosynthesis, glycosphingolipid metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, fatty acid activation, the pentose phosphate pathway, and vitamin D3 (cho- lecalciferol) metabolism. A subset analysis restricted to Level 1 identified metabolites (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2) revealed several differentiating metabolites at P < .05 although they did not meet the FDR threshold. Accordingly, downstream interpretation focused primarily on pathwaylevel results.

Pathway-level patterns differed across study groups. While metabolites differentiating weight gainers from weight maintainers in the INSTI group were primarily enriched within lipid-related pathways (Figure 2), differentiating metabolites in the non-INSTI and WWoH control groups were enriched within a more diverse set of metabolic pathways, including lipid, amino acid, and energy metabolism pathways. Consistent with prior pilot study findings [6], pathway enrichment analysis revealed associations of omega-6 fatty acid pathways with weight change. DISCUSSION

This longitudinal, multi-site metabolomics study provides novel insights into the metabolic signatures related to INSTI-associated weight gain in WWH. Through comparisons with non-INSTI ART recipients and WWoH control groups, we identified a predominance of lipid metabolic pathways differentiating weight change groups among INSTI users, including PUFA-related pathways such as LA and AA. These findings highlight potential mechanisms linking INSTI use and weight gain [20]. Our results align with and extend a recent cross-sectional case–control metabolomics study in a Mexican cohort by Ascencio-Anastacio et al in PWH receiving second-generation INSTIs, which identified dysregulated lipid metabolism and mitochondrial dysfunction as key features of INSTI-associated weight gain [21]. Our longitudinal analysis across 3 visits additionally captures the temporal trajectory of lipid pathway perturbations both before and after INSTI exposure, allowing us to distinguish baseline susceptibility from on-treatment metabolic shifts.

In contrast to INSTI users, differentiating metabolites within the non-INSTI WWH were distributed within a broad range of metabolic pathways more similar to that of WWoH, encompassing amino acid, energy, and other metabolic pathways alongside lipid pathways. This broader pattern is consistent with prior population-based metabolomic studies of long-term body-weight change, which have shown that weight gain in the general population is accompanied by coordinated shifts across multiple, interconnected metabolic networks, including lipid, amino acid, insulin-related, and mitochondrial-function pathways [22, 23]. The preponderance of differentiating metabolites within lipid pathways in the INSTI group may reflect a more constrained metabolic phenotype dominated by impaired lipid handling. Such a pattern is consistent with metabolic inflexibility, a diminished capacity to switch fuel substrates in response to nutrient availability, which has been linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, ectopic lipid accumulation, and insulin resistance in cardiometabolic disease [24]. Notably, INSTIs have been directly implicated in adipose tissue mitochondrial dysfunction in mechanistic animal and cellular studies [25, 26]. Together, with the recent findings of Ascencio-Anastacio et al [21], these data suggest that the lipid pathway predominance we observed may reflect mitochondrial and bioenergetic perturbations specific to INSTI exposure, beyond the broader metabolic shifts that accompany weight gain in general populations.

Polyunsaturated fatty acids play important roles in human health and chronic disease. Through enzymatic and non-enzymatic oxidation, PUFAs are converted into oxylipins, which are bioactive lipid mediators that modulate inflammation, vascular function, and energy metabolism [27]. In this study, LA and its downstream oxylipin, 9,10-epoxyoctadecenoic acid (9,10-EpOME), increased from baseline to 1–6 months post-switch among weight gainers in the INSTI group (Figure 3). Elevated EpOMEs have been linked to cardiovascular disease and inflammation [28] and with obesity in a high-fat diet mouse model [29]. The change in LA differed from our pilot study [6]; however, this discordance may reflect the dynamic nature of LA utilization and diversion to downstream metabolites [30]. The observed increase in 9,10-EpOME may provide a more functionally relevant signal of downstream oxylipin production linked to inflammation and obesity-related metabolic stress. These results suggest that INSTI use may involve acute effects on weight gain through LA-related pathways, although kinetic-based studies will be required to delineate the underlying metabolic fluxes.

Within AA metabolism, several findings point to enzymatically driven shifts in oxylipin production among INSTI users that may reinforce a pro-inflammatory adipose tissue environment. First, weight gainers showed an acute decrease in AA at 1–6 months post-switch with a corresponding increase in 8,9-dihydroxyeicosatrienoic acid (8,9-DHET; Figure 3), consistent with upregulation of soluble epoxide hydroxylase activity, which has been increasingly recognized as a maladaptive feature of obesity and metabolic disease [31, 32]. Second, levels of leukotriene F4 and 15-hydroxy-11,12-epoxyeicosatrienoic acid (15H-11,12-EETA) were generally elevated among weight gainers relative to maintainers in the INSTI group. Their upstream biosynthetic enzymes, 5-LOX [33] and 15-LOX [34], respectively, are overexpressed in adipose tissue in obesity, and the 5-LOX/leukotriene axis has been shown to drive low-grade adipose inflammation and insulin resistance [35, 36]. Taken together, these patterns suggest that INSTI-associated weight gain may involve a coordinated reprogramming of AA metabolism favoring a pro-inflammatory adipose tissue state that promotes weight gain and related metabolic disturbances [37, 38].

Beyond PUFA-related pathways, we also observed enrichment of fat-soluble vitamin pathways with metabolites differentially associated with weight gain only in those who initiated INSTIs. Vitamin A and K metabolism differed between weight change groups at baseline, with vitamin A differences persisting by the second visit. Both vitamins have been linked to adiposity: vitamin A and its active metabolite retinoic acid regulate adipogenesis [39], and higher adiposity is associated with vitamin K sequestration in adipose tissue and lower circulating vitamin K status [40], with dietary intakes of both inversely associated with visceral adiposity [41]. As essential nutrients that must be obtained from the diet, these findings have implications for a role of diet quality in modulating the adipogenic effect of INSTIs. Together, alterations in fat-soluble micronutrient pathways highlight the broader impact of lipid-related dysregulation in INSTI users.

For several pathways and metabolites, differences between women who later gained weight and those who did not were already present at baseline, raising the possibility of a preexisting metabolic susceptibility to weight gain after INSTI initiation, rather than solely treatment-induced changes. At the same time, the divergent directions of change in metabolites between gainers and maintainers support the interpretation that these markers may capture both baseline vulnerability and early metabolic responses to INSTI exposure. Because our study was not designed to formally evaluate predictive performance, this possibility should be considered hypothesis-generating and warrants confirmation in future studies.

In this study, we utilized a large, well-established database to investigate the potential mechanisms of INSTI-associated weight gain among WWH. A strength of this study is the longitudinal study design, enabling us to track the changes of metabolites during follow-up. Furthermore, we compared findings to WWH on non-INSTI ARTs as well as WWoH, reinforcing differences in weight gain specially associated with use of INSTIs. Compared with our prior Atlanta-based pilot study, the present analysis included women recruited across multiple WIHS sites, improving sample size and broader cohort representation, although there was partial overlap between the 2 studies. This broader recruitment enhances generalizability but may also introduce heterogeneity in participant composition, care settings, and other site-level factors. Because site-specific sample sizes were modest, residual heterogeneity across centers may have influenced the observed metabolomic patterns. In addition, the visits included in this analysis spanned an earlier INSTI treatment era, which should be considered when interpreting the findings, as weight gain risk may differ across INSTI agents. While findings from our study were applicable to middle-aged women with well-controlled HIV, caution should be taken when extrapolating to men with HIV, younger or older populations, individuals with advanced HIV, those receiving an INSTI + tenofovir alafenamide or more recent BIC-based regimens, or those from different geographic and clinical settings. Additional limitations were the relatively modest sample size of the INSTI group. Dietary intake information was not available, which could have influenced PUFA levels and metabolism, as well as other metabolic pathways. Menopausal status may also influence both weight-related outcomes and metabolomic profiles through hormonal changes [42]. Although menopausal status defined by STRAW+10 criteria was included in the descriptive characteristics and was not significantly different across study groups or between gainers and maintainers in most comparisons, we did not include it as a covariate in the main models because of the modest sample size. Finally, while we controlled for age and baseline BMI, other potential confounders, such as concurrent medications and comorbidities, may have influenced the results. Additional targeted research is warranted to validate these findings.

In conclusion, in virally suppressed WWH who initiated INSTI-based ART, weight gain over time was associated with a distinct metabolic profile driven primarily by involvement of PUFA and other lipid-related pathways, as compared with WWH on non-INSTI regimens and WWoH whose weight change profiles were distributed within a broad range of metabolic pathways. Differences in PUFA metabolism and downstream oxylipins support a lipid-mediated, pro-inflammatory contribution to INSTI-associated weight gain in WWH. These findings provide potential insights into INSTI-related metabolic complications and may inform strategies to prevent or mitigate weight gain in this population.
