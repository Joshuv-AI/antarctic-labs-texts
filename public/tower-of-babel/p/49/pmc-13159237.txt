Negating neglect: social scientific contributions on Neglected Tropical Diseases and global health
Authors: Reya Farber, Rachel Rhee-Feitel, Amelia Lewis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal health actors, institutions, and communities are trying to respond to the unprecedented U.S. development aid cuts and the “America First” strategy to global health that focuses largely on HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, malaria, polio, and global health security. Amid this normalized neglect of people and health issues, we argue that a social scientific lens is increasingly necessary to understand and improve conditions associated with Neglected Tropical Diseases throughout the world. Neglected Tropical Diseases are an institutionalized group of 20 diverse parasitic, viral, bacterial, and fungal diseases that can cause death, disability, disfigurement, and pain for over a billion people in every low-income country. Based on a review of social scientific research on Neglected Tropical Diseases, as well as other primary and secondary sources, this paper explores macro, meso, and micro themes ripe for social scientific research, including: 1) the social construction of disease categories; 2) the politics of agenda-setting and governance in the global health field; 3) political, economic, and commercial determinants of health and disease; 4) tensions between global disease initiatives and community realities; and 5) neglected disease treatment access and illness experiences. The article lays the groundwork for more holistic social scientific and biomedical research on Neglected Tropical Diseases that considers the intricate interplay of biological processes and social factors. We call on researchers to resist neglecting health issues that do not rank as highly on the hierarchy of global health problems. Instead, social scientists and biomedical researchers can procure more nuanced representations of marginalized health issues impacting people across the world so they can be better addressed through research, policy, and programs.

It is the curse of humanity that it learns to tolerate even the most horrible situations by habituation, that it forgets the most shameful happenings in the daily shame of events.

—Rudolph Virchow, nineteenth century physician and founder of social medicine (as cited by Duggan and Bhutta [1])

Introduction

Beginning in the 1960s, the United States Agency for International Development (USAID) targeted issues such as maternal and child health, malaria, and tuberculosis, as well as water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH). WASH programs help mitigate Neglected Tropical Diseases, such as snail fever and hookworm, as well as prevent diarrheal diseases that can become fatal [2, 3]. As of July 2025, the United States has terminated 83% of USAID global health awards, including WASH initiatives [4–6]). Official development assistance for Neglected Tropical Diseases leads to a 54% reduction in mortality [4], and it is estimated that a one-year pause of USAID’s WASH programs will lead to 5850 deaths and 26,930 people will die due to a five-year pause [7]. Yet, the administration is now pursuing an “America First” strategy, which focuses on HIV, tuberculosis, malaria, polio, and global health security [8]. In this phase of stark scarcity and uncertainty, we argue that a social scientific lens is increasingly necessary to understand and improve conditions associated with Neglected Tropical Diseases, as well as other marginalized and neglected health issues, throughout the world.

This article explores the interventions that researchers can continue to make in our understandings of Neglected Tropical Diseases, a dynamic umbrella category currently comprised of 20 diverse parasitic, viral, bacterial, and fungal diseases that cause death, disability, disfigurement, and pain for over a billion people in every low-income country [9, 10]. Examples of Neglected Tropical Diseases include dengue fever, a mosquito-borne virus that can be transmitted through standing water, and schistosomiasis, a parasitic infection known as snail fever transmitted through water. Neglected Tropical Diseases impact the quality of life and livelihoods for the world’s poorest people, and can exacerbate chronic conditions and noncommunicable diseases, such as sudden cardiac death, renal disease, asthma, as well as infectious diseases like HIV/AIDS [11–14].

Neglected Tropical Diseases are deeply connected to core topics explored in social scientific research on health, including environments and ecosystems, political and economic inequality, health care policy and systems, health care work, patient-clinician relationships, illness experiences, sick roles, embodiment, and stigma [15–20]. Despite these rich connections, publications about Neglected Tropical Diseases do not currently appear in the flagship medical sociology journal, Journal of Health and Social Behavior. More broadly, over 95% of publications about four specific NTDs was biomedical, and the scarce social scientific work on the diseases was rarely driven by social scientists as lead investigators [21].

Such scholarly gaps are not individual faults or failures of researchers or editorial boards, but are instead indicative of broader structural arrangements, including professional rewards and funding mechanisms [22] see also [21]. These factors limit where in the world social scientific research has focused its attention and on which issues, as well as broader barriers to conducting interdisciplinary work across the biomedical and social sciences. Neglected Tropical Diseases thus offer a clear window into the co-production of science and society, demonstrating how science is a social activity and knowledge (or the lack thereof) is indeed a social product [23–28].

However, the relatively small body of social scientific research on Neglected Tropical Diseases has provided a rich and vibrant foundation for continued work in this area. This paper augments previous calls for greater social scientific engagement with Neglected Tropical Diseases by expanding upon relevant themes across macro, meso, and micro scales [29–32]. These themes include: 1) the social construction of disease categories; 2) the politics of agenda-setting and governance in the global health field; 3) political, economic, and commercial determinants of health and disease; 4) tensions between global disease initiatives and community realities; and 5) neglected disease treatment access and illness experiences.

To arrive at these themes, we read and categorized articles that substantively focused on Neglected Tropical Diseases in a variety of social scientific journals and books. We gathered these sources from academic databases – including PubMed, Google Scholar, Science Direct, Sociological Abstracts, and SocIndex – using search queries “neglected tropical disease” and “neglected tropical diseases” (see [32]). We created a list of categories inductively and iteratively based on the article content. To round out our interpretation of salient themes in published research, we also analyzed an array of primary materials, including NTD advocacy organization websites, scientific and advocacy conference proceedings, and technical guidance on NTDs from institutions such as the United Nations Development Programme and the World Health Organization (WHO).

We aim for social scientists to initiate more research on Neglected Tropical Diseases to better understand social, structural, and cultural factors embedded in the diseases. Social scientific work on Neglected Tropical Diseases contributes new insights about the “people-planet axis,” a core facet of the recently updated definition of global health that makes planetary health a visible priority, and emphasizes the interdependence of humans with animals and ecosystems [33]. This article aligns with the push for social scientists to bring global health issues more into our work, illuminating complex relationships between transnational networks and people’s lived, embodied experiences worldwide [15, 22, 31, 34–38]. A focus on NTDs extends a sociology of disease by demonstrating how social processes impact disease trajectories and how diseases impact social relationships [20].

At the same time, biomedical researchers can more thoroughly integrate these social scientific insights into their work, producing more fulsome understandings of how biological processes continually interact with social conditions. This ranges from studying links between immune function, economic recession, and neighborhood distress [39] to assessing how unequal shares of domestic labor impact girls’ and women’s NTD risk and health outcomes [40].

Together, these insights can ameliorate longstanding patterns of neglect in health scholarship, programs, and policies worldwide, especially amid seismic shifts occurring in development aid and the global health field.

We begin with a brief background on the Neglected Tropical Disease category itself, demonstrating how the diseases were first framed, funded, and institutionalized. Next, we expand on how global health governance and agenda-setting unfolds in NTD initiatives amid an evolving global health field. We then discuss macro-level determinants of Neglected Tropical Diseases, followed by how disease programs intersect with communities. Lastly, we discuss people’s illness experiences and disease management strategies. We conclude with a discussion of findings and implications for future research.

The social construction of Neglected Tropical diseases

When one first hears of the category “Neglected Tropical Diseases,” they might wonder how and why the diseases are neglected in the first place, or ask who is neglecting them. What undergirds the possibility and persistence of neglect? Through what conditions does neglect calcify or contort into care and attention? This section offers a brief background on how Neglected Tropical Diseases became an institutionalized category, highlighting the roles of scientific advocacy and technological solutions.

The first foundations of NTD policy emerged in the 1970s, with two initiatives by major global health institutions: the WHO’s Special Programme for Research and Training in Tropical Diseases (TDR, 1974-present) and the Rockefeller Foundation’s Great Neglected Diseases of Mankind (GND, 1978–1988) [41, 42]. Both the TDR and GND included different diseases under their purview, and these discrepancies about which diseases formally count as a Neglected Tropical Disease remain today across different organizations. The WHO’s TDR consisted of a partnership with the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) and the World Bank [42]. This external support increased its independence and laid the groundwork for private sector and philanthropic support for NTDs. However, the WHO’s role as a global health authority waned, as the global health field saw a dramatic rise in new actors and institutions, such as philanthropies and Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs) [43]. Likewise, the Rockefeller Foundation also faced increased competition and was eventually overtaken by the Gates Foundation ([41] see also [42]).

Even still, the TDR and GND established NTDs as their own disease category and policy initiative, and significant NTD milestones followed from 2004 to 2012 [41]. The milestones aligned with the broader “Golden Age of Global Health,” which saw the expansion of development aid for health, as various institutions addressed a range of global health issues [43]. In 2005, the WHO coined the category “Neglected Tropical Diseases” at an international workshop held in Berlin. What emerged from the meeting was a “new vision” and “paradigm shift” involving a practical strategy to control the diseases, one which was rooted in new preventative chemotherapy drugs administered to communities through Mass Drug Administration (MDA) [41]. MDA programs are implemented through public-private partnerships (PPPs), with a cost-effectiveness framework of “small costs, huge benefits” that emphasizes the inexpensiveness of treatments and the compounding results on health and poverty (Molyneux et al. [44] as cited by Smith et al. [45]). In short, Neglected Tropical Diseases were framed as an excellent return on investment involving targeted and efficient medical technologies [46].

Following the Berlin meeting, in 2005, scientists David Molyneux, Peter Hotez, and Alan Fenwick published the first article on Neglected Tropical Diseases in PLOS Medicine. The article argued that for 0.50 cents a person, a person could be provided with a “rapid impact package” of four drugs that prevent five different NTDs for a year [44]. In contrast to the difficulty and expense of controlling malaria, tuberculosis, and HIV/AIDS – deemed the “Big Three” due to their high death toll – the article argued that at least 7 of the then-13 Neglected Tropical Diseases were both cost-effective and seemingly straightforward to prevent. More knowledge production about Neglected Tropical Diseases then followed in 2007, with the establishment of a new journal, PLOS Neglected Tropical Diseases.

Other meetings took place and more reports were published on the diseases. In 2012, international politicians, pharmaceutical CEOs and heads of global health organizations, including Bill Gates and the WHO Director at the time, Margaret Chan, met in London to strategize about addressing NTDs [41]. The goals were to ramp up drug donations for ten of the “tool-ready” NTDs, such as leprosy, while also advancing research programs [41]. From this meeting, twelve pharmaceutical companies pledged 14 billion donated treatments to control or eliminate ten NTDs that comprise over 90% of the NTD burden [47]. Scientist-advocates played key roles forming many Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs) addressing NTDs, while the Gates Foundation contributed over 1 billion dollars aimed towards “developing new tools and methods of delivery” for the diseases [41].

The culminating “bookend” for NTDs becoming more visible on the global stage was the 2014 inclusion of NTDs in the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [41]. This was an expansion of the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs), which had previously ignored NTDs by not mentioning them directly [48]. The WHO has since put forth five strategies to address NTDs, including innovative and intensified disease management, preventive chemotherapy, vector control, veterinary public health, and the provision of safe water, sanitation and hygiene [9]. The WHO’s 2021–2030 road map for NTDs also involves the “One Health” approach, viewing the health of humans, animals, plants, environments, and ecosystems as interdependent and integrated [49]. The One Health approach seeks to address NTDs across multiple societal sectors, disciplines, and communities [50].

To summarize, through a combination of WHO guidance, NGO and scientific advocacy, philanthropic funding, public-private partnerships, drug donations, and bipartisan U.S. presidential and Congressional support, the category of Neglected Tropical Diseases was institutionalized and made visible. The history of Neglected Tropical Disease programs is largely centered on a technical, pharmaceutical focus targeting “tool-ready” diseases, versus diseases that are “tool-deficient.” While immediate technical interventions are of course needed, such programs largely do not address distal, or root, causes of NTDs through the more “integrated approach” advocated by the WHO [41]; see also [29, 30, 33]. The lack of an integrated approach is a core tension of global health programs, policy, and research – and a theme to which we will return in a later section.

We now discuss how global health governance and agenda-setting figure into NTD initiatives.

Global health agenda-setting, governance, and policy

NTDs offer important empirical and theoretical insights about the global health field, which includes various actors and institutions competing for resources and employing certain rules, norms, and standards [51, 52]. Global health activities involve national governments, regional associations, health systems, NGOs, corporations, universities/research centers, civil society, and multilateral organizations such as the WHO and Global Fund engaging with public health concerns, from epidemics to chronic health risks [36]. While NTDs were first addressed in a golden era of global health in the early 2000s, the current moment is marked by geopolitical fragmentation and decreased trust in science and public institutions, as global health is “rich in technical solutions yet increasingly poor in resilience” [53].

Longstanding power relations exist in the current order of global health, with many rooted in extractive and exploitative legacies of colonialism [54–58]. For instance, institutions from the Global North fund most of the health programs and initiatives throughout the world, while global health knowledge is typically produced by researchers based in the Global North [55]. Despite consistent calls and efforts to shift global health leadership and knowledge production towards the Global South [54, 55], it largely remains that health issues affecting people in high-income countries often become global priorities, whereas those issues disproportionately affecting people in low-income countries receive less recognition and funding [41].

The category of Neglected Tropical Diseases is a powerful window into agenda-setting in the global health field. Particularly, the diseases represent the phenomenon of “problem choice” [59]. This refers to how political and economic hierarchies determine who sets global health agendas, as powerful players determine the allotment of funding and resources for NTDs, rather than the communities who become the “recipients” of aid.

While NTDs have undoubtedly gained visibility in policy over the last few decades, and the explicit mentioning of NTDs in the Sustainable Development Goals is an important discursive and material milestone, global health research and programs are still largely dominated by a focus on the “Big Three” diseases of HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, and malaria. A concerted focus on these three diseases is undoubtedly warranted given that together they kill 2.4 million people a year [60], but this still leaves many other pressing health issues relegated to the margins. Thus, NTDs illuminate political, economic, and social drivers of prioritization in the global health field.

Neglected Tropical Diseases also shed light on shifting features of global health governance, defined as the “formal and informal institutions, rules, and processes by states, intergovernmental organizations, and non-state actors” working to address cross-border health issues [61]. Specifically, NTD initiatives demonstrate the hybridity of “globalist” and “statist” global health regimes, the former comprised of international institutions and an array of non-state actors, and the latter including protectionist, anti-multilateral, and state-driven elements [43, 51, 62]. The disease initiatives have incorporated both global stakeholders as well as local or state actors, such as community members and local government officials, to implement interventions in local settings [63, 64].

Amid the expansion of the global health field in the 1990s and 2000s, multinational firms have also partnered with international health agencies, NGOs, philanthropies, and other stakeholders to initiate Product Development Partnerships (PDPs), which produce or deliver healthcare technologies, such as drugs or vaccines [65]. For instance, the case of PDP initiatives for Chagas Disease, an NTD transmitted through “kissing bugs,” demonstrates the hybridity of global health collaborations, as well as varied unintended consequences of such arrangements, including an overemphasis on technological solutions [65].

NTD initiatives also unveil shifting trends in global health policy and funding, such as various setbacks since the COVID-19 pandemic that include reductions in Research and Development due to changing donor priorities, clinic closures, MDA delays, and overall increased poverty [66]. As mentioned earlier, the United States, once a leader in global health funding, is currently pursuing an “America First” strategy that pledges a U.S. response to diseases that largely threaten Americans through a security-focused and protectionist agenda [67]. The plan omits longstanding programs that have focused on issues like maternal and child health, cholera, measles, and Neglected Tropical Diseases [8]. As researchers are keen to point out: “NTDs are at risk of a quiet slide back into neglect after initial success” [53]. We discuss later in the Implications how social scientific work can attend to NTDs when examining such shifts in global health funding and governance [68].

In summary, NTDs offer an important lens through which to understand the evolving configurations of global health agenda-setting, funding, and governance – including interesting hybrid arrangements of public-private partnerships and PDPs [69] Ferpozzi 2023; [43, 53, 67].

We now discuss the political, economic, and commercial determinants that contribute to Neglected Tropical Diseases.

Political, economic, and commercial determinants of Neglected Tropical diseases

Social scientists and social epidemiologists have long pinpointed how political conditions, economic factors, and commercial entities impact health outcomes [38, 70–74]. Neglected Tropical Diseases largely affect the world’s poorest and most disenfranchised people: those who are “neglected by international systems” and who lack economic resources and political power, including poor people in high-income countries [75, 76]. Neglected Tropical Diseases are an example of embodied inequity in disenfranchised communities worldwide, demonstrating how large-scale societal factors like global capitalism and political conflict negatively affect environments, health, and disease outcomes.

On a macro institutional level, global capitalism is a multifaceted societal determinant of health that impacts Neglected Tropical Disease risk, prevalence, and initiatives [31, 32, 71, 77, 78]. Global capitalism broadly refers to a distinct phase of economic development involving neoliberal economic reforms that advance free trade, financial liberalization, deregulation, and privatization [71, 79]. Global capitalism is itself an extension of colonialism [80], rooted in extraction, exploitation, and racialized stereotypes and ideologies [80, 81]. Global capitalism is deterritorialized, meaning its functions are not contained within the boundaries of nation-states; rather, it involves flows of people, resources, goods, and finances across borders [71, 79]. In this context, multinational firms and philanthropies – which are territorially diffuse and less publicly accountable and regulated – have great effects on health agendas and outcomes worldwide [56, 57, 71].

Neoliberal structural adjustment policies imposed by international financial institutions such as the World Bank can have negative effects on health, health care systems, and specifically Neglected Tropical Diseases [82, 83]. Austerity measures that cut government health expenditures on public health care systems and universal health coverage have deleteriously affected health and health care, as well as NTD funding particularly [84, 85].

Given the specific impacts of transnational corporations on health, the Commercial Determinants of Health (CDoH) is a useful framework that spotlights how commercial entities, from tobacco companies to fast fashion, impact health outcomes [72, 73]. However, research on Neglected Tropical Diseases has not explicitly used this framework, which can prove useful in future work. For example, the global pharmaceutical industry is a multifaceted commercial player in the construction of Neglected Tropical Diseases. On the one hand, we previously discussed how pharmaceutical companies have, since the 2012 London Declaration on Neglected Tropical Diseases, diligently donated billions of lifesaving treatments to people affected by Neglected Tropical Diseases [9, 86]. But simultaneously, these for-profit entities also have a legal obligation to shareholders to produce a profit. As a result, pharmaceutical companies largely focus their drug research and development on higher-priced, small market “nichebuster” drugs, such as cancer treatments [77].

In this way, the corporatization of biomedicine prioritizes research and interventions for more profitable illnesses, while for those “economically neglected disease areas, patients are left to fill in the gaps” [87]. Through Neglected Tropical Diseases, we see both the benefits of private drug donations as well as the harms of a profit-driven multinational pharmaceutical industry that mostly prioritizes research and drug development for financial gains.

Global neoliberal capitalism also impacts health through its effects on environments, including extreme weather events and extreme temperatures [71]. In a somewhat straightforward example, global warming and increased rainfall have exacerbated the spread of the Aedes mosquito, the vector for dengue fever [88]. Additionally, transnational corporations can negatively affect environmental health, leading to issues like pollution and water contamination that then increase risks for Neglected Tropical Diseases [77]. Akin to the WHO’s One Health approach, social scientific work has parsed the webs of connections between transmission pathways of NTDs and socio-environmental variables, such as political, cultural, and economic processes [78]. The simultaneous analysis of social and ecological processes identifies synergistic interactions across local and global scales, as well as time [38], and further interdisciplinary research can help tailor context-specific interventions for Neglected Tropical Diseases.

Neoliberalism within the global health field is also a factor that structures Neglected Tropical Diseases and their interventions [32]. While the WHO once advocated for primary health care for all people, the rise of private philanthropies such as the Gates Foundation have put forth their own more selective global health agendas through “philanthrocapitalism” [89]. As discussed in the previous section, the philanthropic approach often has a narrower disease focus and prioritizes technical solutions, rather than addressing root causes of health or strengthening health systems overall [90].

On the political level, research has analyzed the links between Neglected Tropical Diseases, conflict, and political terror [91, 92]. Research has found that nations under conflict or with governments near collapse have the highest prevalence of ten NTDs ([88] citing 91]). In another study focusing on the incidence of visceral leishmaniasis – a neglected disease transmitted through sandfly vectors – it found that incidence was significantly greater in countries experiencing high conflict-terror [93]. This was facilitated by the migration of vulnerable populations into overcrowded conditions with increased human-vector interactions. Political terror is often accompanied by social instability and the breakdown of public health systems, becoming an even greater predictor for disease. Such findings demonstrate how instability, war, and conflict affect health and disease [33, 36, 53, 94].

To summarize, Neglected Tropical Diseases disproportionately impact politically and economically marginalized people due to global economic conditions, including transnational corporations, structural adjustment policies, and the vast and uneven negative effects of capitalism on environments. Such arrangements are rooted in colonial dynamics, as the ensuing “moral and economic inequalities shore up neglect” ([57]; see also [56]). In addition, global capitalism has negatively affected public health care systems and health care access, NTD programs and funding, as well as global clinical research and investment in interventions. On the political level, conflict, weak governments, and displacement exacerbate neglected diseases and human suffering.

In the next section, we focus on how Neglected Tropical Disease interventions unfold in different country settings and communities, illuminating core tensions and opportunities regarding how global health work is done and who gets to decide this is so.

Tensions in global health work and the role of community engagement

With colonialism still embedded in global health, programs can involve hierarchies and power, including “principal-agent” relationships, in which organizations often based in the Global North decide on budgets and protocols to be implemented by aid “recipients” in Global South countries [95]. This section demonstrates how such organizational hierarchies and patterns also emerge in Neglected Tropical Disease programs, illuminating rich insights about the disconnects between international disease program goals and national or district-level realities - and how such gaps can be overcome.

Global health program priorities and practices do not always align with the interests or realities of the communities they serve, despite the most carefully crafted evidence, measurable metrics, and standardized protocols [96]. This is true in the case of NTDs, where the contrast can be stark between the “[g]lossy global targets for elimination” and disease programs implemented in country contexts with no resources [45]. For instance, in poor and aid-dependent Uganda, where the Ministry of Health is weak, there is a lack of coordination and resource prioritization for NTDs at national and district levels. This involves “antagonistic relationship[s] between international actors and national and district leaders,” which means that “[p]artnerships … may act as gatekeepers, controlling resources by virtue of being the only entity that can easily access new forms of donor funding; sometimes by virtue of simply being an alternative to the state” [45]. A country’s reliance on foreign aid, combined with a lack of centralized coordination, prioritization, and resources for NTDs leads to fragmented goals and program implementation across local, national, and global scales.

In another example, in Laos PDR, most of the NTD information provided by an NGO included text-heavy informational flyers in a community that is highly illiterate [97]. In that community, two NGO-funded subsidy-based sanitation projects were implemented in a village where people were given supplies to build latrines for their homes, yet the programs did not offer information on how to set them up. As a result, during the time of the study, those who still had the latrines were using it for other purposes [97]. Instead, in future programs, the researchers emphasize involving community members in education programs. For example, creating community health groups would foster empowerment within the community and allow for more “culturally compelling” methods (Higginbotham et al. [98] as cited by Bardosh [30]. Overall, community participation would create more sustainable programs that last long after NGO support.

There are also disconnects between solutions proposed by domestic and international stakeholders, such as politicians, pharmaceutical company executives, and heads of global health organizations. As we previously discussed, global health programs, especially those targeting Neglected Tropical Diseases, typically adhere to a “vertical” model that focuses on addressing single diseases at a time with technoscientific pharmaceutical solutions. Yet this approach may not align with communities; for example, many Kenyan health workers, teachers, and government officials questioned the long-term usefulness of MDA for soil-transmitted helminths (STH), a type of parasitic worm [99]. With tablets given out regularly to control infection, they felt that this approach did not address root causes of the disease, such as poor sanitation and lack of hygiene education. One interviewee asked: “For how long are we going to take the tablets? I think we should look at measures of prevention” [99]. Instead of focusing only on giving medicine, the Kenyan stakeholders suggested investing in stronger health systems, cleaner environments, and education to help communities take charge of their own health. A community health worker explained: “If we health educate our community, they will be enlightened … rather than pumping drugs in hospital” [99]. This highlights how sustainable disease control is about more than providing medicine or tools, but rather involves long-term planning, better cooperation across government departments, and strong community involvement.

Relatedly, in the Southern Ghanaian context, addressing root causes of, and involving local stakeholders in, the control of urinary schistosomiasis, a parasitic disease caused by contact with freshwater snails, makes for more successful efforts rather than drug treatments alone [100]. Human behavior and limited water infrastructure in southern Ghana affect the spread of this disease. Based on a survey of over 300 schoolchildren, and observations of their interactions with surface water – such as swimming, fishing, and fetching water – the study found that water contact is very common and often unavoidable, especially for children. MDA with praziquantel was the main control strategy used in the region, but the study found that while it lowered infection risk, it wasn’t enough to stop the disease.

In this case, MDA control strategies do not “sustainably break the transmission cycle,” because they “do not tackle the underlying causal factors” [100]. Causal factors included open defecation near water sources, poor sanitation, and lack of access to safe drinking water. For example, a third of the children reported practicing open defecation, and almost half said it occurred in or near the river. Despite children's high awareness (88%) of risk factors, behavior change was limited due to lack of alternatives. Thus, effective control of schistosomiasis requires more than just medication. As the authors argue: “The transmission cycle cannot be broken by exposure reduction, but rather, requires the development of effective primary barriers” (Ntajal et al. 2020, p. 10). Community-level programs should therefore focus on improving sanitation, reducing reliance on untreated river water, and integrating health education with infrastructure investment.

In contrast, when programs are thoroughly integrated at the village level, communities become empowered and trust the programs. For example, in Tanzania, researchers from the London School of Economics & Political Science, the Tanzania National Institute for Medical Research, and the University of Notre Dame implemented an Enhanced Development Governance (EDG) program, with the goal of creating “sustainable village governance” over Neglected Tropical Disease management [64]. The EDG program was successful in different villages, and over time the communities began to organize monthly community meetings, reinforcing sanitation practices and implementing village clean-ups [64]. Thus, investing in community capacity building is an effective, long-term strategy to control NTDs by integrating community knowledge, values, and resources. This in turn empowers and routinizes community members as active agents of change in disease prevention.

Additionally, the narrow goals of HIV/AIDS prevention and treatment programs can preclude the incorporation of Neglected Tropical Disease initiatives [101]. The outsized HIV/AIDS focus is what some public health stakeholders described as the “AIDS industry” and a “big business” [101]. The logic of single disease-driven programs, like those focusing on HIV/AIDS, makes it difficult or impossible to incorporate other interlocking health issues and diseases, including NTDs. Even though interventions for neglected diseases epitomize cost-effectiveness, “[f]ragmented funding and reporting processes … do not impel project managers to work towards achieving collectively elaborated cross-sectional goals by considering the broader outcomes of their activity …” [101]. Integrating NTD programs into existent and well-funded initiatives, such as those addressing HIV/AIDS, would be both sustainable and efficient [88, 102]).

As this section has shown, NTD programs reflect and impart longstanding disconnects between international goals and national and local realities, offering lessons for, and powerful examples of, better outcomes. Based on surveys, interviews, and observations, this line of research illuminates the donor-funded resource constraints of technical disease initiatives and the importance of community involvement in NTD programs. Taken together, these studies also show how community involvement strengthens the effectiveness of global health interventions carried out by different organizations, while unique community dynamics and local leadership continually play a vital role in the success or failure of disease interventions. More holistic approaches to global health programs, which broaden the focus beyond single diseases, would better encapsulate the WHO’s integrated “One Health” approach.

We now turn to people’s lived experiences with Neglected Tropical Diseases, highlighting decisions to seek, and barriers to accessing, treatment; their beliefs and knowledge about conditions; as well as stigma and mental health.

Disease management, socioeconomic barriers, and illness experiences

We have so far elucidated the construction of the Neglected Tropical Disease category amid a dynamic global health field, some of the macro political and economic factors that undergird Neglected Tropical Diseases, as well as meso-level interactions between global health organizations, in-country stakeholders, and community members. Now, our attention shifts to more micro-level experiences with treatment decision-making, health care access, and stigma. We also discuss specific themes about lay versus biomedical knowledge, traditional medicine, and the intersections of socioeconomic status and geographic location.

Rich evidence has shown how disease management is negotiated at the individual and community levels, highlighting how people’s lived experiences and structural conditions are central to understanding illness experiences and real-world disease control outcomes. For instance, in an ethnographic study about how Bolivian migrant women living in Catalonia experience and evaluate Chagas disease treatments, particularly parasiticidal drugs like Benznidazole and Nifurtimox, the conflicts between biomedical and lay knowledge are clear, with the latter involving “practices shaped by sociocultural contexts” [103]. Treatment efficacy is not simply determined by clinical trials, but rather shaped through everyday life, cultural beliefs, precarious working conditions, and patient-doctor relationships. Many women in the study expressed deep skepticism of pharmaceuticals, while others experienced side effects so severe that they stopped treatment altogether. While biomedical systems see treatment efficacy in technical terms, patients judged treatment efficacy based on whether it improved their daily life and helped them continue working. For the women in this study, treatment side effects could jeopardize income and stability, especially in informal or unprotected jobs like domestic care work. In this case, women often blended biomedical care with traditional practices, such as using onion juice to cleanse one’s blood along with pharmaceuticals. The use of traditional medicine, by itself or in tandem with pharmaceutical drugs, is an important theme ripe for future research.

Social scientific work has also delved into important questions about treatment access and the intersections with place and socioeconomic status [104, 105]. Barriers to NTD prevention and treatment in rural communities include the cost of treatment, geographic location, and local understandings [104]. Patients from rural and impoverished communities are less likely to seek any form of treatment because of the cost alone [105]. For example, while the Departmental Chagas Program in Bolivia offered free medication for Chagas disease, a parasitic illness carried by insects, private and semi-public providers still charged for medication. Patients in these settings often stopped taking medication and seeking check-ins with providers because they simply could not afford treatment [104]. Thus, cost of medication is a clear obstacle to receiving treatment for NTDs.

Similarly, in countries like Ethiopia and Ghana, even if treatment for the disease itself is free, there are other costs patients are still responsible for, including laboratory fees, medications for complications or side effects, and other medical supplies [105]. A patient’s geographic location also intersects with the cost of treatment for NTDs because patients must not only travel to receive treatment but also take time off work to travel [104]. As a result, travel expenses can deter people from receiving any treatment, and the costs motivated people to seek other forms of treatment through traditional healers [105]. In the Bolivian case mentioned above, some patients opted to seek “ethnomedical treatment,” which involves homeopathic treatments based on local understandings of a condition [104]. For example, patients in Brazil consumed noni fruit for its analgesic properties if infected with Chagas disease, largely because it is affordable for them [104]. Similarly, in Ethiopia, patients often preferred treatment from traditional healers because they are often local and conduct home visits, which alleviates the burden of travel expenses [105].

Even after a patient accesses medical treatment, they can have delayed diagnosis because of limited provider knowledge of NTDs or local (mis) understandings of NTDs [106]. For example, in Colombia, local providers and specialists have limited knowledge of NTDs and often misdiagnose Chagas disease. This is because many NTDs, including Chagas, are not part of formal medical education and training, even for providers in endemic regions [106]. In Bolivia, some rural communities believe that Chagas disease is incurable, comparing the disease to cancer and diabetes [104]. This misinformation can be reinforced by doctors and other healthcare professionals because of poor understandings of, and lack of training about, the disease. An initial misdiagnosis delays treatment and healing.

Stigma and discrimination, topics of great importance to social scientists studying health and illness, are also associated with certain Neglected Tropical Diseases [106, 107]. Of the 20 Neglected Tropical Diseases, more than half present with skin manifestations, which are often associated with long-term disability, stigmatization, and mental health problems [108, 109]). NTD stigma can also be exacerbated by misinformation and a lack of understanding of certain NTDs. For example, male community leaders from the Kpandai district in Ghana believed that Lymphatic Filariasis (LF) is caused by personal contact, even though this is not true [110]. As a result, people with LF are stigmatized in society, as people avoid close contact with them [105]. On the other hand, in communities where NTDs are locally well understood, patients do not experience such stigma. In Suriname, South America, patients infected with the skin disease cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) did not experience stigma because community members largely know that CL is curable and not contagious [111].

By delving into people’s lived experiences with illness and barriers to treatment, social scientific research has highlighted the intersections between socioeconomic status, knowledge, and place. It has also demonstrated important themes about knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs about diseases, illuminating the importance of traditional medicine for different communities. These rich examples illustrate provider-patient interactions and negotiations about treatments, as well as people’s decision-making about treatment cost and time off work, particularly in different health care system contexts. This work has also explored the psychological, social, and cultural dimensions of Neglected Tropical Diseases, including stigma and discrimination, which are ripe for further social scientific analysis.

We now summarize the social scientific insights discussed thus far.

Conclusion

This article has detailed the uniquely important contributions social scientists can continue to make in our understandings of Neglected Tropical Diseases across macro, meso, and micro scales. The study of Neglected Tropical Diseasesshows how material and human worlds are continually interconnected [26, 27], expanding a burgeoning sociology of ignorance by illuminating how neglect is constructed, experienced, and resisted globally [112, 113]. The neglected disease category reveals the social conditions through which knowledge and non-knowledge, or that which is not studied, are produced across various fields, disciplines, and institutional settings [22, 41, 113–115]. The diseases thus expose the false binary between “the social” and “the scientific/biomedical,” as knowledge and its lack are connected to global health policies, research, programs, and inequities worldwide [27].

Neglected Tropical Diseases illuminate key insights about the global health field, including the construction of disease categories, global health governance, agenda-setting, and how issues are framed – as well as the dominance of technical solutions to poverty, health, and disease. Amid a changing funding and policy environment, the global governance of NTDs involves hybrid public-private configurations of actors, companies, and organizations, with clear tensions between statist and globalist regimes.

Social scientists have also unveiled how Neglected Tropical Diseases are connected to political, economic, and commercial conditions – including changes to the environment, neoliberal structural adjustment programs and financialization, colonial legacies, as well as mainstream media and the pharmaceutical industry.

Social scientific work offers important insights about health interventions and programs, demonstrating opportunities and areas for improvement. Neglected disease programs illuminate tensions between global organizations and communities, biomedical and lay knowledge, and the lived realities that are sometimes obscured by global metrics of success. In Ghana, for example, the ability to avoid water contact is constrained by lived realities, not knowledge gaps (Ntajal et al. 2020). In school-based hand hygiene programs, efficacy isn’t just teaching children to wash their hands; it’s creating routines and environments that support the habit (Ntajal et al. 2020). For donors and funders, success may be measured in coverage rates or drug distribution. But for patients like the Bolivian women managing Chagas disease, treatment efficacy means being able to work and feel safe from side effects [103].

Disease programs, social scientists have shown, can be improved through sustained community involvement. Several studies critiqued the sole reliance on pharmaceutical interventions, such as MDA or antiparasitic treatment, and instead advocated that programs also address the root causes of diseases. Future research in this area can continue to explore these dynamics, especially as communities mobilize in important ways after the U.S. has halted most global health funding. Amid longstanding and ongoing calls to reimagine development aid for health, research on Neglected Tropical Diseases demonstrates the need to rethink leadership and decision-making, as well as metrics of efficacy and success, in global health work [52, 116, 117].

Lastly, our article delved into people’s micro-level illness experiences, socioeconomic barriers to treatment, knowledge and beliefs about diseases and treatment, as well as stigma and discrimination. These studies reinforce a biosocial approach, which “stresses the linkages between biological processes and social conditions” [118]; see also[119, 120]. Across the social scientific research we reviewed, a recurrent theme is that people’s health is not just about biological processes or the technical tools of medicine; it’s also about politics, power, and social structures and dynamics [28]. Biomedical research on NTDs can better attend to “both biography and biology,” nuancing how biological processes, biomarkers, and disease conditions are always connected to social contexts ([121]; see also [39]).

We now wrap up with suggestions for future research.

Implications for future research

Simply put, the progressive disintegration of global health governance is “no longer theoretical” [53]. Official development assistance for Neglected Tropical Diseases is lifesaving [4], but the "America First" approach to global health and USAID cuts undermine health for people in the U.S. and worldwide: clinic doors close, essential treatments remain locked away or undelivered, and the root causes of diseases and health inequities remain unaddressed [1, 122, 123]. Now more than ever, we call on social scientists to turn our attention toward diseases that are becoming even more marginalized in the global health field, particularly by focusing on Neglected Tropical Diseases. Biomedical researchers can also integrate social scientific insights to better attend to how social contexts impact biological processes as well as health and disease outcomes.

Beyond the themes addressed in this article, there are many other generative areas for research on Neglected Tropical Diseases.

Neglected Tropical Diseases offer a fertile window to understand human-environment relations, and the operationalization of the WHO’s One Health approach. It will be important to assess the impacts of funding shifts on NTD research across the biomedical and social sciences, as well as how rearrangements in global health governance, such as the changing roles of China and the United States, will impact NTD initiatives and people’s health outcomes. Perhaps there will also be new manifestations of global health diplomacy through NTD initiatives [124, 125].

Another area of study is if and how NTD prevention and treatment become incorporated into health policy and insurance coverage schemes worldwide, and which social groups might still be left behind [17, 109, 126]. Neglected Tropical Diseases encapsulate issues with large-scale health surveillance and data collection, as they are frequently unreported or underreported and thus underestimated in global disease burden [32, 53]. Social scientific research can interrupt the invisibilization of people and underestimation of disease burden through a concerted focus on the diseases and diverse groups who are impacted by them.

And with neglect partially constructed by limited clinical health research , future research can explore how this large-scale neglect is countered by scientist activists and nonprofit health research organizations [87]. Such work would add important insights about alternative scientific networks and health social movements [127, 128]. Mass Drug Administration also largely relies on volunteers, and the labor dynamics of disease programs is another important aspect for social scientific research to understand, including how gender factors into this work (129).

Social scientists have demonstrated how an intersectional approach is crucial to understanding Neglected Tropical Diseases, and more work is needed on how the diseases impact people across a range of identities, including sex, gender, race, socioeconomic status, citizenship status, and place [130, 131]. Future NTD research would benefit from more explicitly attending to the Commercial Determinants of Health [72, 73]. Migration scholars can continue to offer important insights on how population movement and displacement are associated with disease prevalence [132], while war and conflict contribute to the collapse of public health systems and the resurgence of diseases [53].

For scholars interested in health technologies, there is a range of tools wielded in neglected disease programs, including mobile phones and Artificial Intelligence (AI), whose benefits and tensions are worthy of exploring [133, 134]. Social scientists can delve into essential questions about access and ethics relating to AI in NTD research and drug development, further contributing to a Sociology of AI [135]. Product Development Partnerships are another important trend to study (Ferpozzi 2023) - particularly in the AI era, as international organizations partner with private AI companies to develop treatments and diagnostics for NTDs [136].

Researching Neglected Tropical Diseases allows health researchers to “decenter the West” [137] by focusing on issues largely affecting people in non-Western countries, and who are politically and/or economically disenfranchised [22]. Researchers can raise awareness of these diseases in and beyond academic communities [33], creating evidence that can augment or complicate biomedical research and inform health policies and programs. This might alleviate unnecessary suffering and debilitation from NTDs throughout the world. Across fields, the biosocial approach would furnish more nuanced and holistic understandings of health issues impacting people across the world, which could help improve funding, policy, and programs.

The institutional barriers and cultural challenges of conducting global health research cannot be understated. This is true particularly at a time of unprecedented uncertainty regarding not just global health funding and programs, but also of academic research and even globalization itself.

Still, we argue there is no better time for social scientists to study aspects of Neglected Tropical Diseases, and for biomedical researchers to incorporate these social scientific insights. As neglect is fervently normalized by the U.S. government on the world stage, we call on researchers to resist neglecting health issues that do not rank as highly on the hierarchy of global health problems. A social scientific focus on Neglected Tropical Diseases would ensure that health issues affecting the most marginalized and disenfranchised people – issues which are deeply systemic, political, and economic – do not become even more ignored amid ongoing resource constraints.
