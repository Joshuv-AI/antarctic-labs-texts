Delays in diagnosis and intervention for paediatric hearing loss: the role of parental psychological inflexibility in a multicentre Iranian study
Authors: Sadegh Jafarzadeh, Majid Hadadi Aval, Jamshid Jamali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo characterise the Early Hearing Detection and Intervention (EHDI) timeline and assess its relationship with psychological inflexibility among parents of children with hearing loss in Khorasan Razavi Province, Iran.DesignCross-sectional multicentre study.SettingMultiple hearing rehabilitation centres in Mashhad, Iran.PatientsA total of 193 parents (71 fathers and 122 mothers) of children with documented congenital hearing loss.Intervention: not applicable (observational study).Main outcome measuresThe primary outcomes were the child’s age at diagnosis, age at intervention and the diagnosis-to-intervention interval. Parental psychological inflexibility was measured using the validated Persian version of the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire–Managing Child Hearing Loss (AAQ-MCHL).ResultsThe mean age at diagnosis was 16.93±14.63 months and the mean age at intervention was 23.22±15.35 months, resulting in a mean diagnosis-to-intervention interval of 6.43±9.25 months. A weak but significant negative correlation was found between parental age and AAQ-MCHL score (r=−0.171, p=0.014). No significant correlations were found between AAQ-MCHL scores and any EHDI timeline variables (age at diagnosis, age at intervention or diagnosis-to-intervention interval; all p>0.05).ConclusionsDespite universal newborn hearing screening, substantial delays in diagnosis and intervention persist (16.9 and 23.2 months, respectively). Parental psychological inflexibility showed no meaningful association with service timing, suggesting that systemic, social and cultural barriers likely play the dominant role in EHDI delays.

Introduction

Congenital hearing loss stands as one of the most prevalent sensory disorders, with significant implications for a child’s linguistic, cognitive and social-emotional development.1 The establishment of Early Hearing Detection and Intervention (EHDI) programmes represents a cornerstone of public health strategy to mitigate these adverse effects. Internationally, the EHDI framework is guided by the 1–2-3 benchmarks: screening by 1 month, diagnostic evaluation by 2 months and enrolment in early intervention services by 3 months of age.2 Adherence to this timeline is critical during the first years of life, a period of maximal neuroplasticity, to ensure the development of age-appropriate auditory, speech and language skills.3

The global success of EHDI programmes is well-documented.47 In the northeastern region of Iran, including Khorasan Razavi Province, remarkable progress has been made, with hearing screening coverage increasing from less than 1% in 2005 to over 95% of live births in recent years.1 However, the ultimate effectiveness of EHDI depends not merely on identification but on the seamless transition from screening to diagnosis and the timely initiation of intervention. Despite high screening coverage, studies consistently reveal that significant delays often occur between the confirmation of hearing loss and the start of intervention services, creating a critical bottleneck that undermines the programme’s potential benefits.1 3 8 In many settings, including various African nations and low-resource environments, the subsequent steps of diagnosis and intervention are fragmented, with parental vigilance often being the primary driver for seeking care.8 This highlights that the completion of the EHDI process is vulnerable to factors beyond the healthcare system’s control.

Among these factors, the psychological profile of parents is increasingly recognised as a pivotal element. A recent study demonstrated that the psychological inflexibility and psychological distress of parents mediate the impact of a child’s hearing loss on the parents’ work and social adjustment.9 Psychological inflexibility, a construct rooted in Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), describes a pattern of avoiding distressing thoughts and feelings, which can hinder value-based actions such as pursuing consistent intervention for a child’s hearing loss. Parents with higher psychological inflexibility have been found to experience higher rates of depression and anxiety symptoms and greater caregiver burden in previous research9 10; these factors may in turn be associated with intervention timing, though this relationship was not directly examined in the present study. This study is necessary to move beyond screening coverage and investigate the systemic and psychosocial barriers that prevent children from receiving timely intervention. By examining the early detection and intervention processes alongside the psychological inflexibility of parents of children with hearing loss in Khorasan Razavi Province, this research will provide vital insights for developing integrated, family-centred support systems that address both audiological and psychological needs, thereby ensuring that children not only are identified early but also have the opportunity to achieve their full communicative potential.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted in 2025 at three major hearing rehabilitation centres, which received referrals from 20 diagnostic clinics in Mashhad, Iran. The centres are overseen by the Social Welfare Organization of Khorasan Razavi Province, and two-thirds of them are private clinics. This mix of public and private facilities ensures representation of the diverse service delivery contexts available to families of children with hearing loss in Mashhad. All centres provide comprehensive audiological and speech-therapy services to children with hearing loss.

Participants and sampling

The study participants were parents of children with confirmed congenital hearing loss who were receiving services at the participating centres. The inclusion criteria for the children were: (1) a documented diagnosis of congenital sensorineural hearing loss, confirmed by a battery of tests including behavioural audiometry, tympanometry, otoacoustic emissions, auditory brainstem response and auditory steady-state response; and (2) an active referral to the rehabilitation centre. Parents were excluded if they declined to participate or submitted incomplete questionnaires. Children with confirmed or suspected conductive hearing loss (eg, due to otitis media with effusion, chronic suppurative otitis media or other middle-ear pathologies) were excluded from the study to ensure homogeneity of the sample and to focus on congenital sensorineural hearing loss requiring long-term audiological rehabilitation.

A simple random sampling method was employed to select the participant pool from the eligible families. Of the 205 parents initially approached, 12 (5.8%) were excluded from the final analysis. The reasons for exclusion included incomplete questionnaires (n=7, 3.4%) and declining to participate due to time constraints or personal reasons (n=5, 2.4%). No significant demographic differences were observed between included and excluded participants based on available data (parental age, child’s age or child’s sex; all p>0.05).

Ethical considerations

The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUMS.REC.1401.387) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to participation, all parents provided written informed consent after being fully informed of the study’s aims and procedures. The confidentiality of all personal information was assured.

Data collection

Data were collected using two instruments:

A demographic and clinical information form completed by the researcher, which captured data on parental age and sex, the child’s sex, the age at diagnosis, the age at intervention, the degree of hearing loss and laterality (unilateral/bilateral).

A demographic and clinical information form completed by the researcher, which captured data on parental age and sex, the child’s sex, the age at diagnosis, the age at intervention, the degree of hearing loss and laterality (unilateral/bilateral).

The Persian version of the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire–Managing Child Hearing Loss (AAQ-MCHL). This validated instrument measures psychological inflexibility in the context of managing a child’s hearing loss, where higher scores indicate greater inflexibility and experiential avoidance.11 12

The Persian version of the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire–Managing Child Hearing Loss (AAQ-MCHL). This validated instrument measures psychological inflexibility in the context of managing a child’s hearing loss, where higher scores indicate greater inflexibility and experiential avoidance.11 12

The questionnaires were administered in a quiet room at the rehabilitation centres. A researcher was present to provide guidance and parents completed the paper-based forms within 15–20 min.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS software (V.25; IBM). Descriptive statistics are presented as means±SD for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. The normality of the distribution for all continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Bivariate analyses were conducted to examine the relationships between parental psychological inflexibility (AAQ-MCHL score) and other variables (eg, parental age, age at diagnosis, age at intervention and the diagnosis-to-intervention interval). Independent samples t-tests (or Mann-Whitney U tests for non-parametric data) and one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) (or Kruskal-Wallis tests) were used for categorical variables with two or more groups, respectively. Associations between AAQ-MCHL scores and continuous variables were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (or Spearman’s rank correlation where appropriate). A multiple linear regression was performed to identify factors independently associated with parental psychological inflexibility. The regression model was checked for critical assumptions, including the normality of residuals, homoscedasticity, absence of multicollinearity (assessed via Variance Inflation Factor, VIF) and independence of observations. A two-tailed p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 193 parent–child dyads participated in this study. The parent sample consisted of 71 fathers (36.8%) and 122 mothers (63.2%). The children had a mean age of 5.09±2.02 years (median: 5.00 years, IQR: 4.00–6.00), with 82 boys (42.5%) and 111 girls (57.5%).

Regarding hearing loss characteristics, the vast majority of children exhibited bilateral sensorineural hearing loss (n=188, 97.4%), with only five children (2.6%) having unilateral loss. The distribution of hearing loss severity was as follows: mild in 5 children (2.6%), moderate in 44 (22.8%), moderately severe in 29 (15.0%), severe in 33 (17.1%) and profound in 82 (42.5%).

Parental education levels were heterogeneous. Six parents (3.1%) were illiterate, 70 (36.3%) had less than a high school diploma, 84 (43.5%) held a high school diploma, 28 (14.5%) possessed an associate’s or bachelor’s degree and 5 (2.6%) had a master’s degree or PhD.

The mean parental age was 32.78±6.48 years (median: 33.00 years, IQR: 28.00–37.00). The mean AAQ-MCHL score, measuring parental psychological inflexibility, was 18.32±8.82 (median: 18.00, IQR: 12.00–25.00).

Timelines of diagnosis and intervention

The data revealed a substantial delay in the EHDI process. The mean age at diagnosis was 16.93±14.63 months (median: 13.00 months, IQR: 4.00–27.25), and the mean age at intervention was 23.22±15.34 months (median: 20.00 months, IQR: 12.00–25.00), resulting in a mean diagnosis-to-intervention interval of 6.43±9.24 months (median: 3.00 months, IQR: 0.00–7.00). The distribution of these timelines was positively skewed, with the median values substantially lower than the means, indicating that a subset of children experienced exceptionally late diagnoses or intervention starts that pulled the means upward while most children received services earlier.

Factors associated with parental psychological inflexibility (AAQ-MCHL Scores)

The mean score on the AAQ-MCHL, measuring parental psychological inflexibility, was 18.32±8.82. Bivariate analyses revealed limited associations between AAQ-MCHL scores and most demographic and clinical variables. There was no significant difference in scores based on parental role (father vs mother), child’s sex, hearing loss laterality or parental education level (all p>0.05, table 1 and table 2).

Multivariate predictors of psychological inflexibility

A multiple linear regression was performed to identify independent predictors of parental psychological inflexibility (AAQ-MCHL score). The model (table 4) revealed that after controlling for other variables, higher parental age (β=−0.291, p=0.001) and higher parental education (β=−0.153, p=0.049) were significantly associated with lower psychological inflexibility. Older child age showed a trend towards association with higher parental psychological inflexibility (B=0.677, 95% CI −0.052 to 1.406, β=0.157, p=0.069). Although the CI for this effect included zero, the standardised coefficient (β=0.157) was comparable in magnitude to the significant predictor of parental education (β=−0.153), suggesting a potentially meaningful association that warrants examination in larger studies. Later age at intervention showed a trend towards association with lower psychological inflexibility (B=−0.136, 95% CI −0.282 to 0.010, β=−0.247, p=0.067). The CI for this effect narrowly included zero, and the standardised coefficient (β=−0.247) was the second largest in the model, suggesting this relationship merits further investigation with adequate statistical power.

Discussion

This study demonstrates concerning delays in EHDI diagnosis and intervention in Khorasan Razavi Province, despite successful newborn hearing screening. The mean age at diagnosis (16.93±14.63 months) and age at intervention (23.22±15.34 months) substantially exceeds both international benchmarks (1–2-3 months) and adjusted guidelines for resource-limited settings.2 This diagnosis-to-intervention interval of approximately 6.43 months represents a critical missed opportunity for language development during the sensitive period for auditory brain development.13 These findings suggest that while Iran has made significant strides in the initial screening phase of EHDI, substantial systemic bottlenecks impede progress through subsequent stages.

The wide variation in delays (as evidenced by large SD) suggests that certain subpopulations may experience more pronounced barriers, although these differences did not reach statistical significance in our sample. Notably, while children with milder or unilateral hearing losses showed descriptively later ages at diagnosis and intervention (table 2), the diagnosis-to-intervention interval did not differ significantly across either hearing loss degree (p=0.718) or laterality (p=0.928). This indicates that once diagnosed, the time to intervention is relatively consistent across all types and degrees of hearing loss in our sample.

However, the absolute ages at diagnosis and intervention for children with mild or unilateral loss are clinically concerning. For example, children with mild hearing loss were diagnosed at a mean age of 10.60 months and began intervention at 17.80 months—both substantially later than the recommended 1–2-3 benchmarks.2 Similarly, the small sample of children with unilateral loss (n=5) showed a mean age at diagnosis of 26.20 months and intervention at 33.80 months, suggesting potential delays that warrant investigation in larger studies.

These descriptive patterns align with emerging literature, indicating that children with minimal/mild hearing loss face unique barriers to service access despite being at significant risk for language and academic difficulties.6 While our study did not find statistically significant differences in the interval between diagnosis and intervention, the delayed entry into the diagnostic and intervention process for these children represents a critical clinical concern. Future research with larger samples of children with mild and unilateral hearing loss is needed to confirm these descriptive patterns and identify the specific barriers these families face.

The psychological profile of parents in our study revealed predominantly low to moderate levels of psychological inflexibility, with a mean AAQ-MCHL score of 18.32±8.82. The mean of 16.80±7.10 in a US sample,12 and a subsequent US study of parents of deaf or hard-of-hearing children reported 18.40±7.80,14 cross-cultural comparisons must be interpreted cautiously due to differences in sample characteristics, healthcare systems and the timing of data collection relative to diagnosis and intervention. Our scores are similar to those observed in some Western samples, suggesting that Iranian parents’ psychological inflexibility is broadly comparable, while the larger SD in our sample (8.82) indicates greater heterogeneity that may reflect the diverse educational backgrounds, socioeconomic statuses and access to resources within our population.

A statistically significant but weak negative correlation was observed between parental age and psychological inflexibility (r=−0.181, p=0.014). While this finding suggests that older parents may tend to report slightly lower psychological inflexibility, the practical significance of this association is limited. The correlation coefficient indicates that parental age accounts for only 3.3% (r²=0.033) of the variance in AAQ-MCHL scores, meaning that the vast majority of individual differences in psychological inflexibility are explained by factors other than parental age.

This weak association, while statistically detectable in our sample of 193 participants, may have minimal clinical utility. For example, knowing a parent’s age would allow only a very imprecise prediction of their psychological inflexibility, and interventions designed to support parents should not target based on age alone. The finding is better interpreted as a population-level trend rather than a clinically meaningful predictor for individual families.15

The direction of the association—with older parents showing slightly lower inflexibility—aligns with theoretical expectations that emotional regulation capacities may develop across the lifespan.16 However, the weakness of the association suggests that other factors, such as social support, coping styles, prior experience with disability or access to resources, likely play more substantial roles in determining parental psychological inflexibility.14 Future research should explore these potentially more powerful predictors.

It is also noteworthy that in the multivariate regression model, parental age emerged as a significant predictor even after controlling for other variables, with a standardised coefficient (β=−0.291) that was the largest in the model. This suggests that while the bivariate correlation is weak, parental age may have a more meaningful role when considered alongside other factors. However, even in the full model, the explained variance remained modest (adjusted R²=0.144), underscoring that psychological inflexibility is a complex construct determined by multiple interacting factors.17

The lack of significant correlation between psychological inflexibility and clinical variables such as degree of hearing loss or intervention timelines is particularly noteworthy. This suggests that parents’ psychological responses may not be directly tied to objective measures of hearing loss severity but rather to their subjective interpretation of the condition and its implications. This pattern has been observed in other contexts, where parental psychological functioning was more closely associated with internal processes than external clinical factors.14

The delays observed in our study reflect a broader global challenge, particularly in low and middle-income countries (LMICs). Recent systematic reviews of EHDI programmes in non-Asian LMICs document similar challenges, with many countries struggling to implement uniform protocols and achieve target timelines.6 In South Africa, for instance, researchers have reported comparable delays, with screening often occurring at 11 months rather than the recommended first month of life.18 Our findings in Iran show some improvement over historical data from Tehran (2007), which reported diagnosis at 15.2 months and intervention at 22.3 months, but the progress remains insufficient to meet internationally recognised benchmarks.

The persistent structural barriers identified in our study mirror those documented in other settings. A SWOT analysis of EHDI programmes in South Africa similarly identified lack of resources, insufficient follow-up systems and limited awareness among healthcare professionals as critical barriers.18 This consistency across different geopolitical contexts suggests that systemic challenges in EHDI implementation may share common features across LMICs, requiring coordinated policy responses and resource allocation.

Our findings regarding psychological inflexibility align with emerging research on parental factors in childhood chronic conditions. Recent evidence indicates that psychological inflexibility is a transdiagnostic process that significantly impacts caregiver well-being and adaptive functioning.19 20 The identification of parental age as a correlating factor finds support in studies suggesting that emotional regulation capacities may develop across the lifespan, potentially enhancing coping abilities in older parents.

The mediating role of psychological inflexibility between mental health symptoms and parenting efficacy demonstrated in recent literature19 helps contextualise our finding that parents with higher inflexibility were less likely to pursue interventions. This pattern suggests that psychological flexibility may be a relevant construct to consider in supporting families, consistent with research advocating for ACT approaches in audiological rehabilitation.21

This study possesses several notable methodological strengths that enhance the validity and generalisability of its findings. The multicentre design encompassing various hearing rehabilitation centres in Mashhad increases the representativeness of the sample and reduces selection bias that might affect single-centre studies. The relatively large sample size (193 parent–child dyads) provides adequate statistical power to detect meaningful associations and strengthens the reliability of our conclusions.

Furthermore, the use of a validated instrument (AAQ-MCHL) to assess psychological inflexibility represents a methodological advancement over previous studies in this region that relied solely on demographic or clinical variables. This approach aligns with recent trends in audiological research emphasising the importance of psychosocial factors in rehabilitation outcomes.21 22

Several important limitations warrant consideration when interpreting our findings. The cross-sectional design precludes any causal inferences about the relationships between variables; while we examined associations between psychological inflexibility and EHDI timelines, we cannot determine directionality or rule out reverse causation. A major limitation is the potential for unmeasured confounding by variables not included in our study, including socioeconomic status and financial challenges, which may affect both parental psychological functioning and access to timely services; parental psychological health such as anxiety or depression, which often co-occur with psychological inflexibility; social support from family, friends or community resources that may buffer against psychological inflexibility and facilitate service navigation; health literacy and system navigation skills that independently affect both psychological responses and service timing; and prior experience with hearing loss or disability, which may influence both psychological responses and access pathways. The absence of these variables means that the associations we observed—or failed to observe—may be confounded by these unmeasured factors. Additionally, we assessed psychological inflexibility via self-report but did not directly measure parental behaviours related to service engagement; the absence of significant correlations between AAQ-MCHL scores and EHDI timelines may reflect this measurement gap, as psychological inflexibility could influence the quality of engagement rather than the timing of service initiation. Recall bias is another significant concern, as the retrospective reporting of diagnosis and intervention timelines may be subjected to inaccuracies, particularly for parents of older children (mean child age=5.09 years). Parents may misremember exact dates of diagnosis or intervention initiation, and this recall error could differentially affect subgroups (eg, parents with higher psychological inflexibility might recall timelines differently than those with lower scores). Future studies should employ prospective data collection with real-time tracking of EHDI milestones, such as using electronic health records or parent-held logs, to minimise recall bias and improve the accuracy of timeline estimates. Regarding the clinical characteristics of the sample, while we excluded children with confirmed conductive or mixed hearing loss, the possibility remains that some participants may have had undetected middle-ear pathology superimposed on their sensorineural loss, which could have influenced diagnostic assessments or intervention timelines; future studies should incorporate systematic otoscopic examination and tympanometry at the time of data collection to definitively rule out middle-ear involvement. Furthermore, the sample was drawn from urban rehabilitation centres in Mashhad and may not represent families in rural areas or those not connected to rehabilitation services, limiting the generalisability of our findings to other regions of Iran or countries with different healthcare systems. Finally, the small sample sizes in certain subgroups, particularly children with mild hearing loss (n=5) and unilateral hearing loss (n=5), resulted in wide CIs and limited statistical power to detect differences involving these clinically important populations, underscoring the need for larger, targeted studies in future research.

This study identifies several productive avenues for future research. First, longitudinal studies are needed to examine the developmental trajectories of children with hearing loss in relation to both EHDI timelines and parental psychological factors, addressing the causal limitations of our cross-sectional design. Second, intervention trials should test the efficacy of ACT-based approaches for reducing psychological inflexibility and improving EHDI completion rates, building on evidence supporting ACT in audiological rehabilitation.21 Third, qualitative investigations are warranted to explore the specific emotional and logistical barriers faced by parents at different stages of the EHDI process, particularly for understudied populations such as families of children with mild or unilateral hearing loss. Finally, implementation research should focus on strategies for building sustainable EHDI systems in resource-limited contexts, identifying effective models for integrated tracking and family support.

Conclusion and implications

This study reveals that despite successful universal newborn hearing screening in Khorasan Razavi Province, substantial delays persist in diagnosis and intervention, with mean ages of 16.9 and 23.2 months, respectively—far exceeding recommended benchmarks. Parental psychological inflexibility showed no meaningful association with any EHDI timeline variable, suggesting that this was not the right question to ask. The consistent diagnosis-to-intervention interval across all subgroups indicates that once children enter the system, delays are comparable regardless of hearing loss degree or laterality. These findings suggest that systemic, social and cultural barriers—such as referral pathways, follow-up systems, insurance coverage and health literacy—likely play the dominant role in EHDI delays. Future research should prioritise investigation of these systemic factors rather than parental psychological characteristics.
