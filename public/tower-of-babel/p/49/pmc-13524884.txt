Cluster of Herbaspirillum spp. bacteremia among patients with solid tumors in a tertiary cancer center: clinical characteristics and infection control implications
Authors: Bassem Awada, Wala Samara, Entesar AlYafai, Mahipal Nimmala, Sabitha Pinto, Ramesh Bora, Manyando Mulipi, Sulaiman AlMaqbali, Patrizia Andreoni, Nelly Kanberg Al-Dury, Karimeldin Khalifa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Herbaspirillum aquaticum is an emerging pathogen in immunocompromised patients.•Bloodstream infection is the most common clinical presentation.•Healthcare-associated outbreaks should prompt thorough investigation.•Four of five cases recovered after central venous catheter removal and intravenous therapy.Keywords: Bacteremia, Catheter-related infections, Herbaspirillum, Cancer outbreak

Introduction

Herbaspirillum spp. are aerobic, motile, nonfermenting Gram-negative bacilli belonging to the class Betaproteobacteria, which also includes Burkholderia spp., Ralstonia spp., and other plant-associated genera [1,2]. These organisms are typically catalase-, oxidase-, and urease-positive. Because Herbaspirillum spp. share important phenotypic characteristics with members of the Burkholderia cepacia complex (BCC), they have historically been misidentified as BCC organisms [3,4]. Herbaspirillum spp. are primarily environmental bacteria and have been isolated from plants, groundwater, and drinking water distribution systems [1,3]. In their natural habitats, they are known to promote plant growth through nitrogen fixation and the production of phytohormones [2].

Recognition of Herbaspirillum spp. as potential human pathogens has increased in recent years, largely because of advances in diagnostic microbiology [5]. In particular, the widespread use of matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) and sequencing-based methods has improved species-level identification and helped clarify previous misclassifications of phenotypically similar nonfermenting Gram-negative bacilli, especially isolates formerly attributed to the BCC [5].

Although human infections with Herbaspirillum spp. remain uncommon, these organisms have been reported in a range of clinical settings, including respiratory specimens from patients with cystic fibrosis, as well as cases of bloodstream infection (BSI), pneumonia, and cellulitis [3,[5], [6], [7], [8]]. To date, only two outbreaks of Herbaspirillum BSI have been described in the literature [9,10].

Here we report a cluster of five cases of Herbaspirillum aquaticum BSI identified at the Sultan Qaboos Comprehensive Cancer Care and Research Center–University Medical City (SQCCCRC-UMC), a tertiary cancer center in Muscat, Oman. We aimed to describe the clinical and epidemiological features of the five cases and to review the relevant literature.

Methods

Setting

SQCCCRC-UMC is a tertiary cancer center specializing in the care of adult patients with solid malignancies. The hospital has 120 inpatient beds that include medical and surgical wards, a critical care department, a day care unit, and outpatient clinics.

Sample collection and microbiological identification

At SQCCCRC-UMC, blood culture specimens were processed using the BACT/ALERT system (bioMérieux). Isolates recovered from positive blood cultures were identified to the species level using MALDI-TOF MS (Bruker Daltonics) with database version 4.2.90. Because no established Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute breakpoints are available for Herbaspirillum spp., antimicrobial susceptibility testing was not performed. To assess genetic relatedness, molecular typing of available isolates was performed at the national reference laboratory using pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE).

Outbreak detection and case definition

On July 10, 2025, a cluster of three health care–associated BSIs caused by H. aquaticum was identified at SQCCCRC-UMC. A case was defined as any patient from whom H. aquaticum was isolated from a clinical specimen.

Central line–related bloodstream infection (CRBSI) was defined according to the Infectious Diseases Society of America diagnostic criteria [11]. Sepsis was defined in accordance with the Sepsis-3 consensus definitions. Septic shock was defined as sepsis requiring vasopressor support despite adequate fluid resuscitation [12].

Outbreak investigation

Following identification of the cluster, the infection prevention and control (IPC) team conducted a systematic assessment of infection prevention practices. This included evaluation of hand hygiene compliance, use of personal protective equipment, adherence to central venous catheter care bundles, preparation of injectable medications in inpatient care areas and in the central pharmacy, and environmental cleaning and disinfection procedures.

An environmental investigation was subsequently undertaken to identify potential reservoirs within the hospital environment. Between July 21 and August 18, 2025, environmental surveillance sampling was performed in selected high-risk clinical areas, including operating rooms, the critical care department, operating room scrub areas, and supply storage areas. Sampling targeted high-touch environmental surfaces, patient-care equipment, medication preparation areas, intravenous fluids and devices, and water sources.

A total of 97 samples were collected, including 78 environmental surface and equipment samples and 19 water samples obtained from handwashing basins and equipment-cleaning sinks. Environmental sampling included anesthesia and operating tables, fluoroscopy equipment, intravenous sets and fluids, central venous catheter sets, blood transfusion sets, manometer lines, bedside rails, ventilator tubing, oxygen humidifiers, suction canisters, infusion pumps, medication preparation benches, stethoscopes, and storage surfaces. Water samples were collected from handwashing basins located in operating room scrub areas, patient rooms, medication rooms, dirty utility rooms, hand scrubbing sinks, and patient bathrooms.

In addition, medications commonly administered to all five affected patients were reviewed. Based on this assessment, furosemide, octreotide, and total parenteral nutrition were identified as shared exposures and were therefore included in the investigation.

Results

Five cases of H. aquaticum BSI were identified at SQCCCRC-UMC between May and August 2025 (Table 1). The median age was 53 years (range, 40-62 years), and three patients were male. All patients had an underlying malignancy, including gastrointestinal cancers (n = 3), ovarian cancer (n = 1), and head and neck cancer (n = 1). Three patients (60%) had received chemotherapy prior to admission.

All patients were admitted for surgical or procedural interventions, including Whipple’s procedure (n = 2), cytoreductive surgery (n = 1), gastrectomy with hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (n = 1), and percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy tube insertion with peripherally inserted central catheter placement (n = 1). The median time from surgery to the first positive blood culture was 9 days (range, 6-28 days), and the median time from hospital admission to bacteremia was 10 days (range, 7-50 days).

The index case was a patient with pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor undergoing Whipple’s procedure who developed fever and chills on day four of admission. Blood cultures obtained from both central and peripheral sites grew H. aquaticum, with a differential time to positivity exceeding 2 hours, consistent with CRBSI. The central venous catheter was removed, and intravenous antimicrobial therapy was initiated. The postoperative course was complicated by intra-abdominal bleeding requiring blood transfusion, interventional radiology embolization, and drainage of the hematoma. The patient was discharged after completing a 10-day course of intravenous therapy.

All subsequent cases had central venous catheters in place for more than 2 days at the time of bacteremia. Three of the four cases met diagnostic criteria for CRBSI based on differential time to positivity. Two patients developed septic shock, while the remaining patients exhibited clinical features of sepsis, including fever and chills. Management in all cases included catheter removal and initiation of intravenous antimicrobial therapy.

All patients experienced complicated postoperative courses, with a median length of hospital stay of 38 days (IQR, 24-72.5 days). Apart from the index case, two patients (cases C and D) developed intra-abdominal collections requiring drainage; cultures from these collections were negative for H. aquaticum. Case B had a prolonged hospitalization complicated by bladder leak with early intra-abdominal sepsis requiring drainage and surgical correction, followed by recurrent and persistent H. aquaticum and Achromobacter spp. bacteremia, as well as superficial and deep upper-limb venous thrombosis. Positron emission tomography/computed tomography imaging and transthoracic echocardiography excluded deep-seated infection and infective endocarditis. The patient had a complicated clinical course characterized by recurrent and persistent BSIs caused by H. aquaticum and Achromobacter spp. Management required a combined approach, including removal of central venous catheters and prolonged antimicrobial therapy. Several sequential intravenous regimens were used, including piperacillin-tazobactam, ceftazidime-avibactam, and meropenem. During the course of infection, the patient was also found to have upper-limb deep venous thrombosis with surrounding cellulitis. Given the persistent and recurrent bacteremia, the temporal association with thrombosis, and the absence of another infectious focus on positron emission tomography/computed tomography, the thrombosis was managed as septic thrombosis. Treatment therefore included prolonged intravenous meropenem together with therapeutic anticoagulation. After microbiological clearance, the patient was transitioned to oral ciprofloxacin and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and ultimately recovered.

The final patient developed gastric perforation and septic shock following percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy tube insertion, with secondary Serratia marcescens bacteremia requiring surgical intervention and intensive care unit (ICU) admission for multiorgan failure. Approximately 1 month later, recurrent sepsis occurred because of H. aquaticum, followed 4 days later by polymicrobial BSI with H. aquaticum, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Candida tropicalis. Despite catheter removal and antimicrobial therapy, the patient developed refractory septic shock and died following cardiac arrest.

Overall, four patients recovered clinically and were discharged without recurrence of H. aquaticum infection, while one patient died because of polymicrobial septic shock.

All patients had undergone surgical procedures prior to bacteremia, and their postoperative courses were complicated by intra-abdominal sepsis or bleeding. H. aquaticum was not isolated from other clinical specimens, including endotracheal aspirates, intra-abdominal fluid, or urine cultures. During hospitalization, all patients underwent CT imaging. One patient required continuous renal replacement therapy prior to the onset of bacteremia. Review of administered medications identified shared exposure to furosemide, octreotide, and total parenteral nutrition.

Infection control interventions

Infection control interventions were implemented promptly and included multidisciplinary meetings, reinforcement of standard and contact precautions, and qualitative monitoring of hand hygiene compliance. Following identification of the third case, the IPC team initiated an environmental investigation targeting the high-risk patient-care areas, including the critical care department, operating rooms, and pharmacy medication preparation areas. All environmental cultures were negative.

However, during routine daily IPC rounds in the critical care department, a breach in infection prevention practices was identified, involving the sharing of a normal saline solution during medication preparation, representing a potential source of cross-contamination. Immediate corrective measures were implemented, and the practice was discontinued.

Discussion

This report describes a cluster of five health care–associated H. aquaticum BSIs occurring over a short period. Molecular typing by PFGE demonstrated indistinguishable profiles among four of the five available isolates, suggesting a common source (Figure 3). Four of the five cases fulfilled diagnostic criteria for central line–related BSI and were managed with central venous catheter removal and intravenous antimicrobial therapy, with clinical recovery observed in four patients.

Herbaspirillum species are environmental organisms with a well-established association with water and soil [1,2]. Although they were historically regarded as nonpathogenic, they are increasingly recognized as opportunistic human pathogens, particularly in immunocompromised hosts. Reported clinical syndromes include primary bacteremia [3,5,6,[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]], pneumonia [7,[19], [20], [21], [22]], cellulitis [8], and infective endocarditis [20,23]. Identified risk factors are predominantly related to immunosuppression, especially underlying malignancy, but also include liver cirrhosis, advanced age, hemodialysis, and chronic lung disease [3,[6], [7], [8], [9],17,19,22]. In our series, all affected patients developed bloodstream infection, consistent with the spectrum of disease described in the literature.

To date, only two outbreaks of Herbaspirillum species infections have been reported. Chemaly et al. [9] reported eight cases, predominantly bacteremia, with molecular relatedness demonstrated in four isolates. Most infections were health care–associated, and although no definitive source was identified, an infusion-related mechanism was proposed given the predominance of bloodstream infections. In contrast, Dalgic et al. [10] identified a clear environmental source during an outbreak of H. huttiense bloodstream infection involving 12 pediatric patients, in which contaminated saline solutions and medical plasters were implicated and molecularly linked to patient isolates.

In the present cluster, the predominance of bloodstream infections and the high proportion of cases meeting criteria for central line–related infection similarly support health care–associated acquisition, with infusate contamination representing a plausible hypothesis. Despite extensive environmental sampling, including intravenous fluids and commonly used medications, no microbiological source was identified. However, after recognition and correction of a breach in medication preparation practices involving shared normal saline use among ICU patients, no additional Herbaspirillum spp. cases were detected. Cultures obtained from the suspected shared fluid were negative.

Accurate microbiological identification is essential for recognizing such clusters. Herbaspirillum species may be misidentified as Burkholderia species when conventional phenotypic methods are used, as previously reported in both respiratory and bloodstream specimens [3,4]. The increasing availability of advanced diagnostic techniques, including 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing and MALDI-TOF MS, has substantially improved species-level identification [5]. In our center, identification was achieved using MALDI-TOF MS, enabling timely recognition of this uncommon pathogen and prompting further outbreak investigation.

Management of Herbaspirillum spp. bacteremia has generally relied on antimicrobial therapy combined with source control, particularly removal of central venous catheters [3,6,9,19]. However, antimicrobial management remains challenging because neither the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute nor the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing provides species-specific susceptibility breakpoints for Herbaspirillum spp. In previous reports, minimum inhibitory concentrations have therefore been interpreted using breakpoints established for nonfermenting Gram-negative bacilli. Reported active agents have included ceftazidime, piperacillin-tazobactam, carbapenems, fluoroquinolones, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and minocycline, although no definitive consensus exists regarding the optimal antimicrobial regimen [5]. Given the uncertainty surrounding minimum inhibitory concentration interpretation, antimicrobial susceptibility testing was not performed in the present series. In our cases, all central venous catheters were removed, and treatment consisted mainly of carbapenem-based therapy. With regard to outcomes, recurrence of Herbaspirillum bacteremia has rarely been reported, with only one recurrent case and two fatalities described previously [6,9,22]. Notably, no deaths occurred in the outbreaks reported by Chemaly et al. [9] or Dalgic et al. [10]. In contrast, one patient in the present series experienced recurrence and one patient died.

This report has several limitations. First, its retrospective design, single-center setting, and small number of cases limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, antimicrobial susceptibility testing was not performed, reflecting the lack of species-specific interpretive breakpoints for Herbaspirillum spp. Third, one isolate was unavailable for PFGE analysis, which limited molecular comparison across all cases. Finally, although the epidemiological investigation suggested a possible outbreak source, this source could not be microbiologically confirmed.

Conclusion

Herbaspirillum spp. are uncommon but important opportunistic environmental pathogens capable of causing health care–associated bloodstream infections in immunocompromised hosts, including those with cancer. In this series, management consisted of central venous catheter removal and antimicrobial therapy. Although a definitive source and route of acquisition could not be established, previous reports suggest that contaminated intravenous infusions may represent a possible mode of transmission. Given the organism’s environmental reservoir and its potential for misidentification as other nonfermenting Gram-negative bacilli, heightened clinical awareness and access to advanced diagnostic methods are important for timely identification, appropriate management, and prompt implementation of IPC measures.
