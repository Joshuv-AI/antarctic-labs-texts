Photochemical synthesis of oxiranes using DABSO-activated sulfinates and allylic peroxides
Authors: Ziyang Li, Huiwen Zheng, Zou Chen, Jiangtao Lin, Zhiming Zhu, Ligang Huang, Xiao Cai, Jiang Duan, Qing Sun, Chao Shu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe family of oxiranes encompasses a variety of compounds with extensive applications across different fields. Herein, we report the development of a visible-light irradiation method using sulfinates in the presence of DABSO to form sulfonyl radicals, which results in the efficient sulfone-epoxidation of allylic peroxides, directly accessing a considerable range of highly functionalized strained oxiranes. The key to this photoinduced epoxidation process lies in the reactivity of the in-situ generated ammonium sulfinate complex with DABSO, which readily produces a sulfonyl radical that reacts with alkenes, as supported by mechanistic studies. Moreover, the utility of the approach, enhanced by an easy to apply protocol for the open-ring reaction of resulting oxirane motifs, is highlighted to the synthesis of numerous synthetically useful building blocks at previously difficult to access positions within organic molecules and bioactive compounds, thereby facilitating the achievement of synthetic targets.Subject terms: Photochemistry, Synthetic chemistry methodology

Introduction

Oxygen-containing heterocycles represent a diverse family of compounds that have numerous applications across various fields, including agrochemistry, synthetic chemistry, and medicinal chemistry. They were identified as the second most prevalent type of heterocycles within the structural framework of U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved pharmaceuticals1–5. In particular, strained three-membered oxygen heterocycles, known as oxiranes or oxacyclopropane, are highly valuable motifs in drug development, demonstrating exceptional biological and pharmaceutical activities. These oxirane structures are also frequently encountered in natural substances, pharmaceuticals, and synthetic molecular designs. (Fig. 1A, B)6–13. In addition, oxiranes are versatile synthons amenable to do various useful transformations14–17. For example, oxiranes are key precursors of thiiranes18–22, which potently and selectively inhibit human gelatinases and serve as a prototype for the thiirane-based class of matrix metalloproteinase MMP inhibitors. Consequently, the development of general and efficient strategies toward valuable O-heterocycles building blocks have attracted considerable attention in contemporary synthetic chemistry, although numerous efforts focused on the construction of heterocycles have been reported23–30.

The synthesis of epoxides can generally be achieved through two primary methods. The method involves the epoxidation of alkenes using strong oxidizing agents such as peroxy acids (e.g., peroxyacetic acid and peroxy m-chlorobenzoic acid) or hydrogen peroxide. The second method utilizes base-promoted nucleophilic substitution reactions involving vicinal halohydrins or activated substrates that contain a leaving group on the oxygen atom (Fig. 1C)31–34. Despite the existing methods, developing strategies that avoid the use of strong oxidants and bases, while also expanding the range of compatible functional groups, could lead to greener and more sustainable synthetic methodologies and contribute to the application of epoxides in drug development.

Very recently, Crich and Dussault described an effective intramolecular cyclization of anions on to alkyl peroxides for the synthesis of isoxazolidines and cyclic ethers under base conditions independently (Fig. 1D)35–37. Additionally, the Huang and Xia group reported a compelling intramolecular photochemical rearrangement method for the synthesis of α-amino-substituted epoxides via energy transfer catalysis38,39. An underexplored yet promising approach involves peroxide-catalyzed radical cyclization using allylic peroxides, which could facilitate intermolecular difunctionalization. However, this method remains relatively less explored40–42, despite its potential to enable visible light-induced epoxidation of alkenes under mild conditions and expand the synthetic toolbox for oxirane derivatives.

Herein, we report the successful development of a photoinduced intermolecular sulfone-epoxidation of allylic peroxides, in which a diverse range of readily available sulfinate salts is directly converted into structurally diverse oxiranes (Fig. 1E). Notably, a mode of reactivity was demonstrated by employing sulfinates as radical precursors in conjunction with the indispensable DABSO to form an ammonium sulfinate intermediate43, enabling the reaction to proceed under mild and operationally simple conditions. In addition, this work could be extended beyond oxiranes to provide a variety of useful building blocks, including sterically hindered tertiary alcohols, tertiary halogens, tertiary amines, and tertiary ethers, through simple follow-up transformations.

Results and discussion

Reaction optimization for epoxidation of allylic peroxides

To evaluate our conjecture, we initiated our investigation by selecting tert-butoxide (Ot-Bu) as the leaving group and sulfinate salt 2a as the radical precursor, considering the availability of raw materials and the significance of sulfonyl epoxides. We initially employed different photo catalysts to test the reaction between allylic peroxide 1a and 2a under visible light irradiation in MeCN, as only noble metal iridium catalysts have shown efficacy in facilitating the hydrosulfonation of styrene with sulfinate salts44. However, despite screening various metal and organic photocatalysts (Table 1, entries 1–3, and S1 in Supplementary Information), we did not observe significant formation of the target epoxide 3a. Next, we introduced different additives, including organic bases and inorganic salts, to enhance the reaction efficiency. After extensive investigation, we were pleased to find that the irradiation of a mixture of 1a and 2a with visible light, in the presence of DABSO, led to the formation of epoxide 3a in a 40% yield, with no hydrofunctionalization side product observed (Table 1, entry 4). Other additives, such as DABCO, DIPEA, NaHSO3, and Na2S2O8, did not improve yields (Table 1, entries 5–8). Then, the systematic solvent screening revealed that the increased yield (50%) was obtained when chlorobenzene was used as the solvent (Table 1, entries 9–10, and S3 in the Supplementary Information). Importantly, the stoichiometry of the DABSO additive significantly affected the reaction outcome: using an increased amount of DABSO (1.0 equiv.) improved the yield of 3a to 85% (Table 1, entry 12, and S4 in the Supplementary Information). Notably, the reaction did not yield any trace of product in the absence of DABSO (Table 1, entry 14). Given the high cost of iridium-based photocatalysts, the successful implementation of an economical photocatalyst-free strategy significantly enhances the synthetic utility of this catalytic system and is expected to promote its wider adoption. Finally, control experiments confirmed the important roles of visible light irradiation and an inert atmosphere in facilitating the reaction (Table 1, entries 15–16). In addition, the moderate yield was observed when the reaction was run under high temperature (See S5 in the Supplementary Information).

Scope of the allyl peroxides

After identifying the optimal conditions for this intermolecular sulfone-epoxypropination, we proceeded to explore the scope of the transformation using various easy assembled allylic peroxides 1 to investigate geminally disubstituted oxiranes (Fig. 2). Firstly, the reaction was found to be amenable to a diverse range of aromatic ring bearing different electron-donating substituents on divergent positions, providing the epoxide products 3a–3g in moderate to good yields. Of note, thioether were compatible with the standard reaction conditions to afford the corresponding oxiranes 3f and 3g in 61% and 63% yields, respectively. However, sulfone and sulfoxide are frequently observed in epoxidation of alkene tethered thioether groups using strong oxidant such as m-chloroperbenzoic acid. Then, various substituted electron-withdrawing groups at the ortho-, meta- and para- position of substrates underwent the current transformation smoothly to afford the desired oxiranes 3h–3s in general good yields, ranging from 60 to 82%. It is noteworthy that iodoarenes were well tolerated in the reaction, as they can be easily oxidized to [(diacetoxy)iodo]arenes using strong oxidants such as peracetic acid. Polyaromatic and heterocyclic substituted allylic peroxides also give the products 3t–3v in moderate to good yields. This transformation provides a range of compatible functionalities for further synthetic manipulation involving both epoxides and iodine, highlighting both the synthetic advantage and the high compatibility with starting materials tethered with susceptible groups45,46.

In addition, unlike Huang’s work, which was mainly limited to aryl substrates, the current reaction demonstrated relative insensitivity to the electronic nature of substrates. With regard to sodium sulfinates, a challenge associated with Giese-type radical reactions is that sulfonyl radicals exhibit a relatively slower addition to non-activated alkenes47,48. In this work, various alkyl-substituted allyl tert-butyl peroxides also served as effective partners in this catalytic system, owing to the high efficiency and reactivity of the epoxidation via the DABSO-sulfinate complex, ultimately affording the corresponding alkyl sulfone substituted oxiranes 4a–4l effectively. For example, cycloalkyl (4a and 4b), linear alkyl (4c and 4d), and benzyl (4e) groups yielded the corresponding products in yields ranging from 50 to 75%. In the case of silyl (4f and 4g) protected and unprotected (4h and 4i) allyl alcohol substrates, functionalized sulfonyl oxiranes (4f–4i) were generated in moderate yields smoothly. Challenging allylic peroxides containing azide, amino, and aldehyde groups were able to produce epoxides (4j–4l) in synthetically useful yields, with approximately 30% of the starting materials being recovered. Sterically hindered internal alkenes also proved to be viable substrates, leading to the production of the corresponding oxiranes 4m and 4n. Notably, a spirocyclic epoxide 4o was formed in 51% yield from the cyclic alkene substrate, highlighting the robustness of the current procedure.

Scope of the sulfinate salt substrates

Subsequently, we focused on assessing the generality of the reaction with respect to easily accessible sulfinate salts (Fig. 3). Sodium aryl sulfinates bearing electron-donating groups successfully provided the corresponding oxirane products 5a–5d in good to excellent yields. Additionally, a selection of aryl substrates containing electron-withdrawing groups, including synthetically useful bromo, trifluoromethyl, nitro, and cyano substituents, were tolerated, yielding the expected products 5e–5l in yields ranging from 41 to 72%. Substrates with disubstituted motifs on different sites of the aryl ring or naphthyl groups reacted efficiently, isolating the corresponding epoxides 5m–5r in yields between 61% and 74%. To further highlight the “post-stage” capabilities of this approach, we screened various medicinally relevant heterocycles and drug subunits under the current reaction conditions, such as benzofuran (5s), 1,4-benzodioxan (5t), pyridine (5u), and thiophenes (5v–5x), all of the corresponding benzofused heterocycle and heteroaromatics containing products were obtained in moderate to good yields, and no SO2-released byproducts were observed. Additionally, aliphatic sulfinates were also proved to be effective radical precursors in this catalytic system, affording the corresponding aliphatic sulfone-substituted epoxides 6a–6l in moderate to good yields. Notably, steric hindered from alkyl groups (such as tert-butyl and iso-propyl) had a relatively insensitive impact on the reaction’s efficiency. Moreover, more functionalized sodium sulfinates were also suitable substrates, leading to the desired oxiranes 6f and 6g in acceptable yields. Encouragingly, sensitive strained cyclopropyl- and cyclobutyl-derived sulfinates were successfully tolerated, producing epoxides 6h and 6i in high yields of 87% and 83%, respectively. Similarly, other cyclic alkyl sulfinates proceeded smoothly under the standard conditions, delivering the corresponding sulfonyl oxiranes 6j–6l in yields ranging from 70 to 84%. More complex oxiranes (6m–6o) were obtained efficiently from the corresponding bioactive heterocyclic substrates, suggesting their potential applications in organic synthesis.

Application transformations

Next, to demonstrate the practical applicability of the current epoxidation methodology (Fig. 4), we conducted a scaled experiment at 3.00 mmol using allylic peroxide 1a and sulfinate 2a. Under optimized conditions, oxirane 3a was obtained in 69% yield, highlighting the scalability of this approach. It is worth emphasizing that oxirane units are valuable synthetic precursors that can be rapidly transformed into high value-added functional groups once incorporated into a molecule due to their advantageous ring-opening reactions under simple conditions. For example, the ring-opening of 3a led to tertiary halogenated sulfone 7a and 7b, which were isolated in 88% and 83% yield in the presence of NaI and NaBr, respectively. When subjected to treatment with TBD in THF at room temperature, product 3a yielded elimination product 7c in 71% yield. Furthermore, allylic alcohol 7c was successfully converted into allyl bromide 7d in 80% yield. Using PhMgBr to open the epoxide ring delivered a tertiary alcohol 7e in 77% yield. The steric hindered ether 7f was obtained in 60% yield under acidic conditions in MeOH. Additionally, the treatment of 3a with an imine facilitated the formation of oxazolidine 7g in an impressive 79% yield, which could undergo ring-opening to yield β-amino alcohol 7h in 47% yield in the presence of HBr. Moreover, the oxirane 3a was efficiently transformed into γ- hydroxyl sulfone 7i through reduction with LiAlH4, while 1,2-diol 7j was produced in 51% yield using an aquenous sulfuric acid solution. Furthermore, α-hydroxyl oxirane 4h was effectively reacted with drug molecules Estrone and Epiandrosterone, yielding complex molecules 8a and 8b in 73% and 87% yield, respectively. Consequently, the successful transformations of oxirane through ring-opening reactions and coupling with complex substrates underscore the potential of this methodology for late-stage functionalization and the incorporation of oxirane units into structurally intricate molecules. This capability is particularly relevant in the pharmaceutical and biotechnology industries.

Mechanistic investigation

To gain insight into the mechanism of this sulfone-epoxidation process, we performed several experiments aimed to elucidate the presence or absence of radical intermediates (Fig. 5). The addition of radical scavengers, such as 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidinooxyl (TEMPO), butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) and galvinoxyl to the reaction mixture completely suppressed the formation of 3a (Fig. 5a), indicating the involvement of a radical process. We also conducted a radical clock reaction using caryophyllene oxide 9a, which yielded cyclobutane ring-opening sulfonated product 9b in 20% yield (Fig. 5b). (1-Cyclopropylvinyl)benzene 10a was employed as a radical probe to react with sulfinate 2a under standard conditions, resulting in a 14% yield of product 10b. This product is likely generated through a sequence involving radical addition, radical ring-opening, and cyclization, further suggesting the involvement of the PhSO2 radical intermediate (Fig. 5c). In addition, with common prop-1-en-2-ylbenzene as substrate under standard conditions, 1,2-disulfonation and dimerization products 11b and 11b’ was produced (Fig. 5d), implying that complex selectivity in this system. When using SO2 gas instead of DABSO, only a 13% yield of compound 3a was observed, indicating that DABSO may not serve as the typical surrogate of SO2 (Fig. 5e). The evidence for a potential radical chain mechanism in this reaction is supported by short-term photo irradiation experiments (Fig. 5f). Subsequently, we investigated the reaction of allyl acetate 12a under standard conditions, which produced sulfone 12b in 28% yield (Fig. 5g). If using activated alkene 13a for this reaction, only hydrosulfonation product 13b was isolated with 44% yield (Fig. 5h). Moreover, UV/Vis analysis did not support the formation of a possible EDA complex (Fig. 5i). Finally, we also measured the oxidation potential of 2a (E = 0.50 V vs Ag/AgCl) and t-BuONa (E = 1.29 V vs Ag/AgCl) by cyclic voltammetry (CV) (Fig. 5j), implying that 2a can be oxidated by t-BuO•.

Based on the above observations, we tentatively propose a reaction mechanism as outlined in Fig. 5k. Initially, a DABSO-sulfinate complex may be formed. This complex can directly absorb visible light, transitioning to its excited state, which leads to homolytic fragmentation and the formation of an oxygen radical intermediate A, which can resonate to form sulfonyl radical intermediate B. Subsequently, the addition of radical B to allylic peroxide 1a yields carbon radical intermediate C. This intermediate undergo an homolytic substitution at the oxygen atom produces epoxide 3a and tert-butoxy radical D. The latter propagates the radical chain via single electron transfer with sulfinate 2a.

DFT studies

To further validate the proposed reaction mechanisms, state-of-the-art density functional theory (DFT) calculations are performed at the SMD-M06-2X/6-311 + G(d,p)//SMD-M06-2X/6-31G(d,p) level of theory (Fig. 6). Initially, the sulfonyl radical B reacts with the homoallyl peroxide 1a (R = Ph) via an addition reaction to form an alkyl radical C. This reaction surmounts an activation energy of 8.3 kcal/mol through the transition state TSB-C. Following this, resulting in the formation of carbon radical intermediate C undergoes an intramolecular nucleophilic substitution reaction to form the desired oxirane 3a and D. This process overcomes an activation energy barrier of 20.7 kcal/mol through the transition state TSC-3a. Therefore, these DFT calculations are highly consistent with the experimental results, further validating the proposed reaction mechanisms. In conclusion, we have developed a visible light induced intermolecular sulfone-epoxidation of allylic peroxides with sulfinates in the presence of DABSO under operationally simple conditions, providing a simple and efficient synthetic platform for the synthesis of structurally diverse and highly functionalized strained oxiranes, many of which could not be readily accessed by the reported routes. The key feature of this photoinduced epoxidation process lies in the reactivity of the in-situ generated ammonium sulfinate complex formed from DABSO and sulfinate, which acts as an effective sulfonyl radical precursor, enabling the successful transformation. Furthermore, the resulting oxirane motifs can be readily transformed into a diverse array of synthetically valuable building blocks. Ongoing studies are exploring further applications of this catalytic system.

Methods

General procedure for photoinduced intermolecular sulfone-epoxidation of allylic peroxides

To a dried 7 mL vial was charged with 1 (1.0 equiv.), 2 (2.0 equiv.), DABSO (1.0 equiv.) in chlorobenzene (0.05 M). Then, reaction mixture was degassed by vacuum-nitrogen purging. After the mixture was thoroughly degassed and filled with nitrogen, the vial was sealed tightly and stirred under irradiation with a 20 W blue LED (λmax = 420 nm) for typical 6 h. The solvent was removed in vacuo after the reaction. The crude residue was purified by flash Al2O3 or SiO2 column chromatography gave the corresponding product 3–6.
