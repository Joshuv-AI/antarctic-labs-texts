Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a form of precapillary pulmonary hypertension (PH), defined by right heart catheterisation as a mean pulmonary arterial pressure greater than 20 mmHg, pulmonary vascular resistance greater than two Wood units, and pulmonary arterial wedge pressure less than 15 mmHg [1, 2]. Beyond idiopathic and heritable forms, PAH can be associated with various conditions – mainly connective tissue disorders, HIV infection, or portal hypertension – but also with exposure to drugs and toxins [1, 2]. The history of drug- and toxin-induced PAH is linked to several health scandals, including those involving aminorex and fenfluramine derivatives [3, 4].

In the early 1980s, another health crisis emerged in Spain, where nearly 20 000 people developed a novel syndrome after ingesting denatured rapeseed oil – so-called toxic oil syndrome (TOS) [5, 6]. The outbreak primarily affected the central and northwestern regions of the country. In its acute phase, TOS presented as a clinical picture resembling acute infectious pneumonitis, with nonproductive cough, dyspnoea, pleuritic chest pain, headache, fever, and bilateral pulmonary infiltrates. Among survivors, chronic conditions subsequently developed, including neuromuscular and skin diseases, scleroderma, and PAH with features similar to other known forms of the disease [7, 8].

In this issue of ERJ Open Research, Cruz-Utrilla et al. [9] report the long-term outcomes of patients who developed PAH following toxic rapeseed oil exposure. These patients were included in the Spanish registry of PAH, which has documented adult patients with group 1 or group 4 PH in Spain since 1998 [10]. The authors identified 59 cases of TOS-associated PAH, including 13 prevalent cases at the time the registry began. Adding these to the previously identified 40 cases yields a total of 99. Given that around 20 000 individuals were affected by TOS, this suggests a PAH prevalence of approximately 0.5%, similar to those linked to dasatinib or mitomycin C [11, 12].

A central unresolved question is whether TOS-PAH should be classified as Group 1 toxin-associated PAH or as a pulmonary manifestation of a broader toxic systemic vasculopathy. Although patients met haemodynamic criteria and histology showed classical PAH features (including plexiform lesions), a high proportion (88%) presented with pulmonary infiltrates – characteristic of TOS – although computed tomography scans were normal in most cases. Features of pulmonary veno-occlusive disease (PVOD) were found in four patients, suggesting a pathophysiological spectrum ranging from arteriolar PAH to PVOD like disease. Moreover, more than half of the study population had extracardiac and/or extrapulmonary manifestations, including eosinophilia, myalgias, neuropathy, Sjögren syndrome, generalised skin rash, and sclerodermiform skin changes – findings consistent with TOS.

These patients developed PAH after a median delay of approximately 22 years (270.8 months), which is significantly longer than delays observed in patients who developed PAH after exposure to proteasome inhibitors (median 6 months), mitomycin C (median 8.5 months), or dasatinib (median 42 months) 11–13]. This delay is even longer than in patients who developed PH after exposure to appetite suppressants [14–17]. The remarkably long latency raises fundamental questions: is such a delay mechanistically plausible, or do these late cases reflect coincidence or a “second-hit” phenomenon? The idea that a single toxic insult could cause latent vasculopathy that progresses to PAH decades later is not yet supported by mechanistic data. Latent vascular damage might instead be a risk factor for later PAH development after a second hit (e.g., comorbidities, ageing, or environmental exposures).

Based on previous descriptions of drug- and toxin-associated PAH, several patterns can occur after exposure: most patients will not develop PAH;some will develop PAH within months while still exposed to the drug, with potential reversibility, as seen with proteasome inhibitors, bosutinib, ponatinib, or mitomycin C;some will develop PAH several years after exposure, as with anorexigens or methamphetamines;in TOS, some patients develop PAH acutely, while others develop it many years later, with rare cases of partial reversibility (figure 1).

Various evolutions after exposure to a drug potentially inducing pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). In the majority of cases patients will not develop PAH. Shortly after the exposure some patients will develop PAH or pulmonary veno-occlusive disease (PVOD), which can sometimes be reversible after withdrawal of the drug, as seen after exposure to dasatinib, proteasome inhibitors, indigo naturalis, or Mitomycin C. To date, no cases of late PAH development have been described in those cases. After a few years some patients will develop PAH, as seen in patients treated with anorexigens. Eventually, as in the case of toxic oil syndrome, patients will develop PAH or PVOD a few months after the exposure, and may develop PAH years later, as seen in the study by Cruz-Urtilla et al. [9].

In the study by Cruz-Utrilla et al. [9], patients had precapillary PH with a median mean pulmonary arterial pressure of 57 mmHg (interquartile range (IQR) 44–74), median pulmonary vascular resistance of 11.7 Wood units (IQR 8.3–17.8), and median pulmonary arterial wedge pressure of 10 mmHg (IQR 8–13). Data from 1980 to 2020 showed that patients who developed disease earlier had more severe haemodynamic profiles at presentation compared to those diagnosed later. However, including patients over a 40-year period inevitably led to data loss, and prognosis is difficult to assess given the considerable evolution of PAH therapies since the 1980s. The seemingly milder presentation in some cases may also reflect selection bias, as patients with early undiagnosed PAH may have died before inclusion.

Overall, these findings suggest a heterogeneous pathophysiology: some patients, predisposed or heavily intoxicated, develop early severe disease, while others require a second unidentified event to trigger PAH years after initial exposure. The mechanisms underlying drug- and toxin-associated PAH remain largely unknown, and animal or preclinical models have failed to reproduce TOS or TOS-associated PAH [3, 8].

The TOS case illustrates that even well-characterised toxic events can have consequences decades later, and that long-term registries are essential to detect late complications. It also serves as a reminder that large-scale toxic exposures could still occur, particularly in today's globalised market for food and dietary supplements, with inadequate regulation and increasing use of unvalidated herbal or synthetic agents.

This study underscores the importance of lifelong follow-up for individuals exposed to substances known to be associated with PAH and of monitoring the long-term evolution of acute toxic syndromes. It also highlights the difficulty of defining disease in survivors of systemic toxic injury. Future research should aim to clarify the pathophysiology of drug- and toxin-associated PAH, identify risk factors for late-onset disease, and model the long-term sequelae of toxic exposures.
