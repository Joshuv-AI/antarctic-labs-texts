Evaluation of Macula and Optic Disc of Adult Amblyopia Patients with Diverse Etiologies using Optical Coherence Tomography Angiography
Authors: Aylin Tosun, Pınar B. Kızıltunç, Huban Atilla
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
PURPOSE:To compare macula and optic-disc optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) metrics of adult amblyopic eyes with healthy controls and investigate whether these differ according to amblyopia etiology.METHODS:This cross-sectional study included 35 unilateral amblyopic adults (n = 35 eyes) and 35 age-matched healthy controls (n = 35 eyes). Amblyopia subgroups consisted of anisometropic (n = 12 eyes), strabismic (n = 12 eyes), and combined-mechanism amblyopia (n = 11 eyes). Foveal avascular zone area (FAZA), vessel density (VD), and retinal thickness were obtained using OCTA. Retinal thicknesses were measured at the fovea, parafovea, perifovea, and retinal nerve fiber layer. Foveal, parafoveal, perifoveal, whole-macula, intradisc, peripapillary, and whole-disc VD were recorded. Groups were compared regarding OCTA metrics after adjusting for axial length, spherical equivalent, age, gender, and signal quality index using a one-way analysis of covariance test.RESULTS:Amblyopic eyes had significantly lower foveal, parafoveal, perifoveal, whole-macula VD in superficial capillary plexus (SCP), and deep capillary plexus (DCP) than control eyes after adjusting for confounders (P < 0.05 for all). All amblyopia subgroups had significantly lower parafoveal, perifoveal, and whole-macula VD in SCP and DCP than control eyes after adjusting for confounders (P < 0.05 for all). There was no significant difference in retinal thickness, FAZA, intradisc VD, peripapillary VD, and whole-disc VD between amblyopic and control eyes, as well as amblyopia subgroups and control eyes.CONCLUSION:These results suggest that effect of amblyopia on macular microvasculature should be considered when interpreting OCTA metrics of adults with concomitant diseases. It also supports that adult amblyopic eyes of different etiology have similar microvascular features.

Introduction

Amblyopia is characterized by the persistent cortical processing deficit of visual stimuli. Postmortem studies have demonstrated that the number of neurons responding to the amblyopic eye decreased in the primary visual cortex and lateral geniculate nucleus, while those responding to the nonamblyopic eye increased.[12] While extensive research has investigated amblyopia from a neural perspective, advances in imaging technology have provided the opportunity to evaluate the retina in detail. However, no consensus was reached in optical coherence tomography (OCT) studies investigating amblyopia.[3] On the other hand, OCT angiography (OCTA) has enabled in vivo and noninvasive assessment of retinal microvasculature and found widespread clinical use in follow-up of retinal and optic nerve diseases. Initial studies investigating the microvasculature of amblyopic children reported that OCTA metrics of amblyopic eyes differ from healthy controls.[45] Moreover, a recent meta-analysis demonstrated that pediatric amblyopic eyes have lower parafoveal vessel density (VD) than healthy controls.[6] Therefore, it is crucial to evaluate whether microvascular alterations seen in amblyopic children persist into adulthood, considering its importance in interpreting OCTA results of patients with concomitant diseases. Although few studies have investigated macular[78910] and optic-disc[7] microvasculature of amblyopic adults, none excluded the impact of axial length (AL) and spherical equivalent (SE) on measurements. However, it has been demonstrated that SE, AL, age, and gender affect OCTA parameters.[11] As high refractive error often accompanies amblyopic eyes, it is crucial to exclude the effect of SE and AL on results when comparing amblyopic eyes and healthy controls. Besides, recent studies have shown that amblyopia treatment affects OCTA metrics, whereas previous studies evaluating amblyopic eyes included a heterogeneous group in this respect. Finally, there is a void in the literature to account for whether OCTA metrics differ according to etiology in amblyopic adults. Our study thus aims to address these issues by including a homogeneous group of treatment-resistant amblyopic adults and comparing OCTA metrics of various amblyopia etiologies after adjustment for confounders.

The primary aim of our study is to compare adults’ amblyopic eyes with healthy controls in terms of the macula and optic-disc OCTA metrics after adjustment for confounders. The secondary aim of this study is to examine whether amblyopia subgroups with diverse etiology differ in this respect.

Methods

This cross-sectional observational study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki tenets and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Ankara University, Ankara, Turkey (IRB Number: I1-68-20). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant. The study population was recruited from patients admitted to Ankara University, Department of Ophthalmology between August 2018 and December 2019. Inclusion criteria for the amblyopia group: treatment-resistant unilateral amblyopia due to strabismus and/or anisometropia between ages 18 and 45. Exclusion criteria: systemic disease, medication usage, prematurity history, ocular disease (corneal disease, cataract, vitreous opacity, glaucoma, retinal or optic nerve disease), intraocular surgery or ocular trauma history, deprivation amblyopia, bilateral amblyopia, inadequate image quality (images with decentration or segmentation error or signal quality index (SQI) ˂7/10), and fixation instability. Unilateral amblyopia was defined as an interocular difference of ≥2 lines in the logarithm of the Minimum Angle of Resolution (logMAR) of best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) without ocular disease and anisometropia was defined as an interocular difference in sphere or cylinder >1.50 diopter (D), as specified by the American Academy of Ophthalmology’s Preferred Practice Pattern guidelines. Amblyopic eyes were subdivided according to etiology: anisometropic amblyopia (AA), strabismic amblyopia (SA), and combined (strabismus + anisometropic) amblyopia (CA). The AA group consisted of amblyopic eyes with anisometropia without heterotropia and/or previous strabismus surgery. Amblyopic eyes with heterotropia and/or previous strabismus surgery were included in the CA group if accompanied by anisometropia or in the SA group if not. The control group was recruited from the right eyes of healthy individuals presenting for routine ophthalmic examination by simple random sampling.

Data acquisition

Each participant’s age, gender, BCVA (logMAR), SE, AL measurement (IOLMaster version 7.1, Carl Zeiss, Meditec AG, Jena, Germany), and OCTA measurements of macula and optic-disc (AngioVue version 2017.1.0.155; Optovue, Inc.) were obtained. The SE was calculated by adding sphere power to half of the cylinder power. All OCTA images were obtained using Angiovue software of RTVue XR Avanti SD-OCT using split-spectrum amplitude decorrelation algorithm demonstrated erythrocyte motion-dependent angiography with 70.000 A-scans/second (wavelength = 840 nm, bandwidth = 45 nm) and 400 × 400 A-scans/B-scan. Microvasculature of macula and optic disc of amblyopic and control eyes is accesed Figure 1. For macular and optic-disc OCTA scans, a 6 mm × 6 mm cube is centered on the fovea and a 4.5 mm × 4.5 mm cube on the optic-disc, respectively. Macular OCTA scans are segmented automatically as superficial capillary plexus (SCP) from the inner limiting membrane to 9 μm above the inner plexiform layer (IPL) and as deep capillary plexus (DCP) from 9 μm above IPL to 9 μm below the outer plexiform layer. The software identifies 3 regions by centering 3 concentric rings of 1 mm, 3 mm, and 6 mm in diameter on the fovea: fovea, parafovea, and perifovea, respectively. The automated Angioretina and Angio Disc QuickVue modules provide numerical data for foveal thickness (µm), parafoveal thickness, perifoveal thickness, retinal nerve fiber layer thickness, foveal avascular zone area (FAZA) (mm2), foveal VD (%), parafoveal VD, perifoveal VD, whole-macula VD, intradisc VD, peripapillary VD, and whole-disc VD. A recent Angiovue software version was used, which subtracts large vessels from the image to reduce the effect of vessel masking on DCP. All scans were evaluated by 2 independent and blinded ophthalmologists (AT and PBK) and scans with decentration or segmentation error or SQI ˂7/10 were excluded.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics V.20.0 software (IBM, New York, USA). The normality was checked by Shapiro–Wilk test. Data were presented as means ± standard deviations or median (minimum–maximum) and percentage (%) for nominal variables. Baseline characteristics of amblyopia and control groups were compared using independent samples t-test if normally distributed or Mann–Whitney-U test if not. Baseline characteristics of amblyopia subgroups and control group were compared using one-way analysis of variance test if normally distributed or Kruskal–Wallis test if not. One-way analysis of covariance test was used to assess differences in FAZA, VD, and retinal thickness between groups after adjusting for AL, SE, age, gender, and SQI. A sequential Bonferroni post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 70 eyes of 70 individuals, including 35 adults with unilateral amblyopia (35 amblyopic eyes) and 35 healthy individuals (35 control eyes), were included. Of the amblyopic eyes, 34% were AA, 34% were SA, and 32% were CA. Tables 1 and 2 summarize the demographic and ophthalmologic characteristics of groups. There was no significant difference in age, gender, and SQI between the amblyopia and control groups, nor between the amblyopia subgroups and the control group. The amblyopia group had poorer LogMAR BCVA, higher SE, and shorter AL than the control group (P < 0.001 for all). In subgroup analysis, AA and CA had significantly higher SE and shorter AL than SA and the control group, showing a statistically significant difference (P < 0.05 for all). The retinal thickness, FAZA, and VD were compared between groups after adjusting for SE, AL, age, gender, and SQI. Tables 3 and 4 summarize the comparative results of the groups’ OCTA metrics. Amblyopic eyes had significantly lower foveal, parafoveal, perifoveal, whole-macular VD in SCP and DCP than control eyes after adjusting for confounders (P < 0.05 for all). All amblyopia subgroups had significantly lower parafoveal, perifoveal, whole-macular VD in SCP and DCP than in control eyes after adjusting for confounders (P < 0.05 for all). There was no significant difference in retinal thickness, FAZA, intradisc VD, peripapillary VD, and whole-disc VD between the amblyopia and control groups, nor between the amblyopia subgroups and control group.

Discussion

Our results demonstrated a significantly lower macular VD in SCP and DCP in adult amblyopic eyes than in healthy controls after adjusting for SE, AL, age, gender, and SQI. Besides, after adjusting for confounders, each amblyopia etiology subgroup had lower macular VD than the control group.

In recent years, there has been growing interest in the microvasculature of amblyopia, yet most studies have focused only on children. Few studies have examined the microvasculature of amblyopic adults and reported inconsistent results.[78910] In adult amblyopic eyes, while Dereli Can reported a lower VD in SCP and DCP, Demirayak et al. detected a lower VD in the DCP and Hamurcu et al. a lower VD in SCP compared to the control group.[789] Whereas, Bayraktar et al. found no difference between the groups.[10] Yet, there are some critical issues when interpreting previous research on the microvasculature of amblyopic adults. Although these studies included hypermetropic amblyopic eyes with shorter AL than the control group, none considered the effect of AL difference between amblyopia and control groups on OCTA metrics.[78910] However, it has been proven that AL, SE, age, gender, and SQI affect the retinal thickness, FAZA, and VD measurements and should be adjusted when comparing groups.[11] Therefore, the effect of the AL difference between the amblyopia and control groups on measurements cannot be excluded. In our study, we thus adjusted retinal thickness, VD, and FAZA measurements for age, gender, SE, AL, and SQI to exclude their impact on results and detected that VD in SCP and DCP was lower in amblyopic eyes of adults than in healthy controls. Moreover, after adjustment, each amblyopia subgroup had lower macular VD than the control group. Therefore, our results suggest that the microvasculature of adults’ amblyopic eyes does not differ by etiology. Only one study by Chen et al. investigated the effect of etiology on microvasculature but involved amblyopic children.[12] They reported that, unlike our results in amblyopic adults, children with AA had more macular VD loss than those with SA.[12] This difference might be due to discrepancies in participants’ ages and treatment stages, as shown in recent studies.[131415]

There is a considerable amount of literature investigating the microvasculature of amblyopic children. However, initial studies did not consider the effect of AL on results even though they included patients with a wide refraction range.[45161718] However, some recent studies have evaluated the microvasculature of amblyopic children after adjusting for AL and SE.[121920] In agreement with our study in adults, Yılmaz et al. and Doğuizi et al. reported that macular VD in SCP and DCP in amblyopic eyes of children is lower than healthy controls after adjustment for AL.[1819] These results support that microvascular features are similar in amblyopic adults and children. On the other hand, Chen et al. reported a lower VD in SCP in amblyopic eyes of children compared to the control group, while Wong et al. found no difference between the groups.[1220] Considering that we included amblyopic adults who completed the critical period in our study, the younger age of the patients included in these studies might be a possible reason for the discrepancy in results.[1220] Although our results differ to some extent from studies involving younger children, studies involving older children are in line with our results in amblyopic adults.[1819]

Another reason for the discrepancy in the results of previous studies of amblyopic children might be the inclusion of heterogeneous patient profiles in terms of treatment response. Recent studies evaluating whether the microvasculature of amblyopic eyes differs according to treatment have also reported supportive results.[131415] A longitudinal study examining the effect of treatment on the microvasculature of amblyopic children by Gunzenhauser et al. demonstrated that macular VD in DCP and foveal VD in the SCP increased significantly in those amblyopic eyes that experienced improvements in visual acuity following patching therapy.[14] Moreover, Zhang et al. detected that although newly diagnosed amblyopic children had lower macular VD in SCP and DCP than healthy controls after adjusting for AL, there was no difference in VD between recovered amblyopic children and healthy controls.[13] Besides, Salerni et al. demonstrated that VD in SCP was significantly lower in treatment-resistant amblyopic eyes than in recovered amblyopic eyes after adjustment for AL, but they did not examine DCP measurements.[15] Therefore, when interpreting the results of previous research, it is crucial to consider the heterogeneity of patient profiles in terms of treatment stage and response. Our study included a homogenous group of treatment-resistant amblyopic adults to exclude the effect of treatment response on results and agrees with these studies evaluating the influence of treatment on the microvasculature of amblyopic children.[131415]

Besides, several studies have compared the amblyopic eyes of unilaterally amblyopic children with fellow eyes.[2122] Araki et al. found no difference between amblyopic and fellow eyes regarding macular VD and Nishikawa et al. detected a lower macular VD in SCP and DCP in amblyopic eyes than fellow eyes after adjusting for AL.[2122] However, it is inapplicable to compare our results, as these studies compared amblyopic eyes with fellow eyes, not with healthy controls.[2122] Previous research has demonstrated that fellow eyes differ from healthy eyes, as unilateral amblyopia also affects the fellow eye.[23] Therefore, we included healthy eyes in the control group considering the possible effect of amblyopia on the fellow eyes. A recent meta-analysis further increased the power to provide a more convincing assessment of OCTA metrics in amblyopic children.[6] This meta-analysis demonstrated that amblyopic eyes had lower VD in parafoveal SCP and DCP than healthy controls, but there was no significant difference between amblyopic and fellow eyes. It also detected no significant difference between amblyopic, fellow, and control eyes regarding FAZA and foveal thickness.[6] Our results in amblyopic adults support this meta-analysis evaluating amblyopic children.

In addition to the macula, we investigated optic-disc microvasculature in adults with amblyopia and found no difference between amblyopia and control groups. Only two studies evaluated optic disc microvasculature in amblyopic eyes.[57] Sobral et al. reported a lower optic-disc VD in amblyopic eyes of children than in control eyes.[5] Dereli Can found a lower intradisc VD in amblyopic eyes of adults than in control eyes, but no difference in the peripapillary and whole-disc VD.[7] However, the effect of the AL difference between groups on the results cannot be excluded, as AL measurements are not considered in these studies.[57]

Although our study design cannot evaluate a causal relationship regarding microvascular alterations in amblyopia, decreased metabolic demand due to suppression might be a possible reason, as suggested in previous studies. It has been shown that vascular endothelial growth factors secreted by the retina during maturation affect the vascular formation of SCP and DCP.[24] Besides, the lack of difference in optic-disc microvasculature between groups might be related to the fact that peripapillary vessels are less affected by autoregulatory mechanisms than the macula.[25] However, the etiopathogenesis of these changes is unknown.

Regarding FAZA in amblyopic adults, previous studies agree that there is no significant difference between amblyopic and control eyes,[81017] except for one reporting a larger FAZA than healthy controls.[7] However, these studies in adults did not adjust FAZA measurement for AL.[78910] Furthermore, our results regarding the FAZA are consistent with the previous research evaluating amblyopic children after adjusting for AL.[12181920]

Regarding retinal thickness, we found no difference between amblyopic and control eyes. Although there is no consensus on whether retinal thickness differs between amblyopic and control eyes, the fact that previous histological studies evaluating the retina demonstrated no difference between amblyopic and control eyes supports our results.[123]

The limitations and strengths of this study should also be noted. The strengths of our study are as follows: the inclusion of a homogeneous group of treatment-resistant amblyopic adults, comparison of diverse amblyopia etiologies, examination of optic-disc and macula together, adjustment for confounders, the inclusion of a healthy control group, the use of a recent OCTA software which subtracts large vessels from deep images and allows a more thorough assessment of DCP, and the inclusion of high SQI images. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate macular and optic-disc OCTA metrics of amblyopic adults after adjustment for confounders and to compare various etiologies of amblyopia in this regard.

The main limitation of our study is that our study design was not applicable for evaluating longitudinal changes in amblyopia. This study thus could not assess whether the microvascular changes were the cause or consequence of amblyopia and could not indicate a causal relationship. Studies investigating longitudinal changes in the microvasculature during treatment in different amblyopia etiologies will provide a better understanding of this issue. Further studies are needed to evaluate the etiopathogenesis of microvascular alterations in amblyopia.

Conclusion

These results suggest that the effect of amblyopia on macular VD should be considered in the follow-up of amblyopic adults with concomitant retinal and optic nerve diseases.
