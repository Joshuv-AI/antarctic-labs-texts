The influence of the ratio between Li2O and B2O3 on optical, luminescence, and thermal properties of samarium-doped lithium borate glass
Authors: I Kashif, A Ratep
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe advantageous characteristics of photoluminescence in lithium borate glass indicate that alterations in the microstructural unit greatly influence these properties. This work used FTIR spectroscopy in conjunction with peak fitting and density analysis to study the x Li2O·(100-x) B2O3-1 Sm2O3, where x = 20, 33.33, 50, 66.66, and 75 mol% glass. Using quantitative analysis of the FTIR spectrum, it was possible to determine how the trend of each structural unit and the tetracoordinated boron content changed as the Li2O concentration increased. The fundamental structural components (BØ3, BØ2O, and BØ4) of the lithium borate glass were found to correlate with its attributes (density, and molar volume). The compositional changes revealed a shift in the BO4 and BO3 groups within the glass network. Furthermore, an improvement in the tightness of the glass network was noted, which enhanced the LiBSm: Sm glasses’ thermal stability and glass-forming ability and demonstrated their applicability as lasing materials. In addition to the reduction of dangling bonds, the optical characteristics reveal the ionic nature surrounding Sm3+ ions. Optically, all the distinctive optical absorption bands of the Sm3+ ions appeared in the spectral region of 200–2500 nm at 2014 nm, 1582 nm, 1514 nm, 1462 nm, 1396 nm, 1216 nm, 1070 nm, 936 nm, 470 nm, 460 nm, 400 nm, 372 nm, and 358 nm. Additionally, a significant impact of the BO4/BO3 ratio on the optical properties was observed. The trend Ω4 > Ω6 > Ω2 for observed Sm3+ absorption transitions is followed by the estimated oscillator strengths and Judd–Ofelt parameters (Ω2, Ω4, Ω6) for reported Sm3+ absorption transitions. When the host glass was excited with violet wavelengths of 393 and 403 nm, three wavelengths were produced: one in the green area at 564 nm, one in the orange region at 600 nm, and one in the red region at 646 nm. As the excitation wavelength increased, a notable improvement in radiation intensity was noted. It was also discovered that the modification of the borate groups in the lattice structure affected the color emission and purity. This set of samples is a good fit for optoelectronic applications.Keywords: Lithium borate glass, FTIR, DTA, Absorption, Excitation, Emission, CIESubject terms: Materials science, Optics and photonics

Introduction

Glass has many properties that make it more common in use in terms of low cost, stability of thermal properties in a wide range of temperatures and transparency that make it contribute to versatility in optical properties. The ability of glass to dope a certain amount of rare earth makes it suitable for various applications including filters, color emission devices, solid-state batteries, fuel cells, solar cells, and superionic conductors1.

Rare earth element-doped glass is a superior alternative for LED applications compared to traditional LEDs that use phosphors encapsulated in plastic packaging2. The choice of B2O3 as a glass former is significant due to its high optical transparency, excellent thermal stability, good solubility for rare earth ions, and overall robustness3.

Furthermore, it is shown that the boron oxide structure changes from BO3 to BO4 when lithium oxide (Li2O) is added to borate glasses doped with Sm3+ ions, changing the physical characteristics. This modification leads to higher orange-red emission intensity, excellent quantum efficiency, and a slight reduction in non-radiative decay in the glass samples, making them suitable for laser applications4,5.

The emission resulting from the 4G5/2 level in the visible region exhibits strong luminescence intensity, a large, stimulated emission cross-section, high quantum efficiency, and a low probability of non-radiative decay. These properties make it an attractive area for future research in laser production.

The wide emission band from metastable state (4G5/2) to the lower level 6F11/2 record a high energy gap about 7000 cm−1 exceeds the phonon energy formed by borate group that in range 1400 cm−1, making it the multi phonon relaxation is neglected6–10.

Many researchers studied the impact of the replacement of various concentrations of modifier or former glass by Sm3+ ions on glass samples’ physical and optical properties. Thomas and colleagues11 investigated the spectroscopic and dielectric properties of Sm3+ ions in glasses made of lithium zinc borate (25 Li2CO3 + (65 − x) H3BO3 + 10 ZnF2 + x Sm2O3, where x = 0.0, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0). They discovered that the glass with 0.5 mol% Sm had the highest emission properties, and that concentration quenching occurred as the concentration of Sm3+ ions formed. In their investigation of the optical, structural, and physical characteristics of Sm3+-doped lithium zinc aluminum borate glasses, Prabhu et al.6 found that samples with 0.3 mol% Sm2O3 exhibited the maximum luminescence in comparison to the other glasses under study. Using magnesium borosilicate glasses, Kumar et al.10 investigated the conversion of cool-to-warm orange-reddish light-emitting diodes by adding Sm2O3. They discovered that the sample with the composition of 1.5 mol% Sm2O3 has the highest intensity when compared to other samples. This happens because of bridging oxygens (BOs) being produced and the glass volume decreasing. The structural and luminescence characteristics of samarium-doped lead alumino borate glasses were investigated by Mohan et al.4. They discovered that the intensity of all emission peaks’ emission spectra increases as the concentration of Sm2O3 rises from 0.1 mol% to 1.0 mol% but decreases at 2.0 mol%. In their physical and spectroscopic analysis of Sm3+ ion-doped aluminum tungsten borate glasses for photonics, Murty et al.12 observed that emission peak intensities rise from 0.5 to 1.0 mol% as the concentration of Sm3+ ions increase and then progressively fall from 1 mol% to 2.5 mol%. In their physical, structural, thermal, and spectroscopic analysis of Sm2O3 -doped LAB glasses for orange LEDs, Pawar et al.2 discovered that the intensity of all the peaks rises as Sm2O3 increases up to 0.5 mol% before falling because of concentration quenching. When comparing the physical, structural, and optical characteristics of Sm3+-doped lithium zinc aluminum borate glasses, Prabhu et al.6 discovered from the plot that Sm 0.3 glass exhibited the maximum luminescence.

Many researchers have studied lithium borate glass doped with samarium (Sm3+) ion and analyzed how the addition of Sm3+ affects the emission properties of the glass. Prabhu et al.6 examined the physical, structural, and optical properties of Sm3+-doped lithium zinc aluminoborate glasses and found that substituting Sm with Al2O3 resulted in a quenching of luminescence intensity due to collisions of Sm ions in the excited state. Similarly, Thomas et al.11 investigated the spectroscopic and dielectric properties of Sm3+ ions in lithium zinc borate glasses and noted that increasing the concentration of Sm3+ in B2O3 enhanced certain properties.

The researchers discovered that as the concentration of Sm2O3 increased, the emission intensity decreased due to concentration quenching, which occurred through dipole-quadrupole relaxation. They determined that the prepared glass is suitable for applications in fiberglass, laser technology, and photonics within the visible region. El khoshkhany et al.13 studied the spectroscopic properties of simple cost-effective glasses doped with Sm2O3 and containing alkaline oxides for display laser emissions. Their findings indicated a dependence of the high gain coefficient on the Sm2O3 concentration. Additionally, they observed a strong emission in the reddish-orange color resulting from the transition 4G5/2 to 6H7/2. Consequently, the results suggest that the glass composition (50B2O3–14SiO2–20Li2O–15Na2O–1Al2O3–500 ppm Sm2O3 is a promising candidate for white light-emitting applications when excited by 402 nm light.

This study investigates the internal structure of lithium borate glass samples, focusing specifically on the borate BO3 and BO4 groups that vary according to the lithium-to-boron ratio. It also aims to examine how alterations in borate groups impact thermal and optical properties, as well as how these changes affect the color of light emitted by the excitation of this group of samples.

A series of glass samples were prepared with the following composition: (100-x) mol % B2O3 –x mol % Li2O − 1 mol % Sm2O3, where x takes the values of 20, 33.33, 50, 66.66, and 75 mol %. The samples were created using the melt-quenching technique. The samples were melted in a porcelain crucible at 1100 °C for one hour in an electric furnace (LENTON) and then rapidly cooled using a pair of copper blocks.

Structure properties

When examining the impact of one substance on another’s properties, the following methods can be employed: substitution or addition. For oxide glass, a certain percentage of the oxide under study replaces an equivalent percentage of the original elements (substitution). In addition, a percentage of the oxide is added to the original glass composition without altering it, resulting in a total that exceeds 100% (addition). This methodology is documented in numerous scholarly papers19,30–32.

The study of the internal structure of materials involves density measurements, revealing that density depends on two key factors: changes in mass due to the addition of compounds to the glass and changes in volume. Volume variations are influenced by the coordination number, crosslink density, and the dimensionality of interstitial spaces3.

Structural analysis (FT-IR analysis)

The identification of chemical bands and vibrational properties is achieved through FTIR measurements. FTIR provides information for classifying substances as either crystalline or amorphous based on the characteristics of the bands.

In Fig. 2, the width of the peaks indicates disorder in the material, suggesting it is amorphous. Borate glass begins with a base structure of B3O6, which consists of boroxol rings33. The addition of a modifier disrupts the covalent bonds, resulting in various stretching and bending vibrations. This modification leads to the formation of four-coordinated boron structures, along with penta-borate, tetra-borate, tri-borate, and di-borate groups4. The effect of the modifier depends on its type, the number of oxygen atoms, and the presence of cations in the interstitial spaces. FTIR can be divided into four regions: 400–1500 cm−1, 1500–2000 cm−1, 2000–2500 cm−1, and 2500–4000 cm−12. The first region, 400–1500 cm−1, serves as a fingerprint for each compound, as it contains different absorption bands.

FTIR spectra of prepared glass samples.

The vibration modes of the borate group can be categorized into different regions based on their characteristics. The stretching vibrations of BO3 units are found in the range of 1200–1600 cm−1, while the vibrations of BO4 units appear between 800 and 1200 cm−1. Additionally, the bending vibrations occur in the 600–800 cm−1 range. The broadness of these bands indicates the presence of various borate group formations.

Specifically, the band between 800 and 1200 cm−1 splits into two distinct bands: one at 885 cm−1, which represents the vibration modes of different BO4 units, and another at 1040 cm−1. These bands correspond to different shapes of borate groups, such as triborate (B3O7-), tetra-borate (B8O162-), and pentaborate (B5O10-)34.

Furthermore, the band around 1200–1600 cm−1 can be divided, with one peak at 1230 cm−1 representing symmetric stretching vibrations and B-O bridging between B3O6 and BO3 triangular units within meta and orthoborate chains33. The band at 1380 cm−1 reflects the stretching elongation of the B–O bonds in trigonal (BO3)3- units, while higher frequency bands are attributed to anti-symmetric elongations of B–O–B groups34,35.

In the range of 400–600 cm−1, bands related to cation vibrations of Li+ and Sm3+, as well as bending vibrations of O–B–O, are observed3,35. Notably, the broadness of these bands increases with higher concentrations of Li ions. Additionally, a band around 1640 cm−1 signifies O–H group bending vibrations22. The smoothing and eventual disappearance of the band around 806 cm−1 indicates a complete transition from the boroxol ring to borate groups36. All the peaks were assigned and given in Table 1. The results obtained are in good agreement with the reported literature22.

Physical parameters

From Fig. 7 presented, it was observed that density increased to 66.66 mol% Li2O, after which a decrease was noted at 75 mol% Li2O. This indicates that density is influenced more by the change in size rather than by the added mass relative to the volume. The mass change has a lesser effect compared to how the percentage of lithium varies at the expense of the primary component, boron. Previous studies have shown that an increase in lithium concentration facilitates the conversion of BO3 to BO4, which results in stronger bonding3.

Density of prepared glass samples.

The molar volume is influenced by bond length or interatomic distance4. It was found that the bond length of lithium is 0.201 nm, while that of boron ranges from approximately 1.28 to 1.34 nm. As a result, the volume is reduced. This was confirmed through calculations of the molar volume based on the established relationship.

Based on the diagram, the volume decreases, which increases density, making the glass more compact.

According to the infrared results, the fundamental structure of the glass samples under study consists of tetrahedral BO4 groups, triangular BO3 groups, and triangular BO3 groups with non-bridging oxygen (NBO). When Li2O is added to the glass network, a transition occurs from tetrahedral BO4 groups to triangular BO3 groups with non-bridging oxygen (NBO). At 20 mol%, the BO3 groups are nearly equal to BO4 groups, as shown in Fig. 6. And at Li2O content of more than 20 mol%, both groups show the same trend, but the BO4 groups are larger than the BO3 groups. The density results of the glass samples under study show the same trend as N4 or N4/N3, indicating a dependence of density on basic structural groups, molecular weight of lithium oxide, and boron oxide. When the Li2O/B2O3 ratio equals 0.66, N4 reaches its maximum point; nevertheless, the tricoordinate boron remains the main coordination type. Density measurements indicate that an increase in N4 results in a rise in density while simultaneously causing a reduction in the molar volume of the glass as shown in Table 2. Optical properties

Optical absorption

Figure 8 displays the optical absorption characteristics of the prepared glass samples. The sharp peaks observed indicate the solubility of Sm3+ ions within the glass. At low wavelengths, a flattened absorption edge suggests the amorphous nature of the material. The transitions associated with Sm3+ ions can be grouped into two categories: a low-energy group, which shows high intensity at wavelengths longer than 700 nm, and a high-energy group, which exhibits lower intensity in the 400–700 nm range. The lower intensity in the latter range is due to the overlapping of various (2s+1) LJ levels4.

The optical absorption of prepared glass.

The transition of Sm3+ from the ground state 6H5/2 to various excited states occurs at the following wavelengths: 6H13/2, 6F1/2, 6H15/2, 6F3/2, 6F5/2, 6F7/2, 6F9/2, 6F11/2, (4I11/2, 5M15/2), 4I13/2, 6P3/2, 4K13/2, and 4D5/2 at 2014 nm, 1582 nm, 1514 nm, 1462 nm, 1396 nm, 1216 nm, 1070 nm, 936 nm, 470 nm, 460 nm, 400 nm, 372 nm, and 358 nm, respectively. Additionally, two indistinct peaks at 526 nm and 562 nm are attributed to the transitions 7F0 → 5D3 and 7F0 → 5D2 for Sm2+2.

The observed high intensity of the 6H5/2 state transitions to the 6F, 6H, and 6P terms indicates strong absorptions. In contrast, the transitions from 6H5/2 to 4I, 4M, 4G, 4L, and 4D states are spin-forbidden, resulting in weaker intensity peaks4. Samarium ions (Sm3+) doped in glass feature electric dipole transitions that adhere to the selection rule of ΔJ ≤ 6, while magnetic dipole transitions follow the rule of ΔJ = 0, ± 14. A high-intensity peak appears at 400 nm, corresponding to the transition from 5H5/2 to 6P3/2, which is characterized as a hypersensitive transition that complies with the selection rules: |ΔS| = 0, |ΔL| ≤ 2, and |ΔJ| ≤ 239.

Emission properties

Photoluminescence occurs when a sample doped with rare earth elements (RE) is excited by a specific wavelength of light, producing an emission. To determine the appropriate excitation wavelength, we monitor the emission from the glass sample using an excitation curve at an emission wavelength of = 589 nm.

The excitation curve depicted in Fig. 11 shows excitation peaks in the visible region at wavelengths 346, 362, 374, 394, 404, 416, 462, 472, and 529 nm. These peaks represent the transitions of Sm3+ from the ground state 6H5/2 to various excited states, including 4H9/2, 4P7/2, 6P3/2, 6P5/2, and 4F3/222. Additionally, some references indicate the presence of a low-intensity peak at 529 nm, which represents the transition from 7F0 to 5D3, linked to the presence of Sm2+2. This peak appears only at low concentrations of lithium (Li) and disappears at higher concentrations of Li+.

The excitation spectra of prepared glass at λ emission = 589 nm.

Samarium oxide (Sm2O3) is added to lithium borate glass. (Samarium (Sm) typically has a 4f 6 s electron configuration). Samarium is incorporated into oxide compounds as Sm3+ (4f5) ions, which exhibit transitions within the unfilled 4f electron shell. The spectral properties of Sm3+ ions depend on the energy levels and transitions involved in the absorption and/or emission processes. The most intense transitions in the Sm3+ luminescence correspond to the 4G5/2 → 6HJ (J = 5/2, 7/2, 9/2) electronic transitions, which lie in the yellow, orange, and red spectral regions, respectively. These transitions are very intense; therefore, when Li2O is introduced into the glass lattice, a transition from tetrahedral BO4 groups to trigonal BO3 groups with non-bridging oxygen (NBO) occurs. This modification results in a change in the intensity of the reddish-orange emission.

From the Fig. 11, it can be observed that there is a high intensity at 393 nm and 403 nm with a 20 mol% concentration of Li. The two peaks show equal intensity, but as the concentration of Li increases, the peak at 393 nm becomes more prominent than that at 403 nm. Therefore, it is essential to investigate the effects of various parameters, such as different concentrations of modifiers, varying intensities of excitation wavelengths, and different wavelength values, to study the formation of these two high-intensity peaks.

The glass samples doped with Samarium (Sm3+) ions were exciting using intense wavelengths of 393 nm and 403 nm. During this process, the emitted light results from the excitation of the sample, where an electron is raised to a higher energy level, specifically above the 4G5/2 level. Subsequently, the electron experiences non-radiative decay to reach the closest upper energy level39. The energy difference between the 4G5/2 level and the lower level is approximately 7600 cm−1, which surpasses the phonon energy. This high energy difference helps to prevent multi-phonon relaxation6,8.

Under the excitations of 393 nm and 402 nm, as shown in Figs. 12 and 13, three peaks are observed that represent the de-excitation of the electron from the metastable state 4G5/2 to different ground states: 6H5/2, 6H7/2, and 6H9/2 at wavelengths of 562 nm (green), 600 nm (orange), and 646 nm (red), respectively. Also, the emission intensities vary with the BO4/BO3 concentration.

In addition, the presence of a peak at 710 nm corresponds to the transition from 4G5/2 to 6H11/2 under excitation at 403 nm or may be due to the emission produced by Sm2+, associated with the transition 5D0 → 7F2, according to a previous study by Power2. The emissions from Sm3+ ion can produce different colors52, including green, yellow, orange, reddish, and red, corresponding to the various emission peaks that will be discussed later. The unchanged peak position further suggests a homogeneous distribution of Sm3+ ions throughout the glass material.

As observed in Fig. 7, the peak positions remain unchanged across different modifier concentrations, confirming that the 4f orbitals of Sm3+ are shielded by the outer 5 s and 5p orbitals, rendering them less sensitive to local structural variations26.

Additionally, Fig. 9 illustrates a shift in the high-intensity peak, which appears to split due to the Stark effect.

Stark is produced when the host electric field interacts with RE. In Sm3+, this splits the ground state 6H5/2 into two levels, and the excited state 6F1/2 into two sublevels. The split allows transition between them, causing the output peak to widen8,53,54.

The analysis of the transition reveals that the (4G5/2→6H5/2) transition is forbidden and has a magnetic dipole (MD) nature, adhering to the selection rule ΔJ = 0. In contrast, the (4G5/2→6H7/2) transition is partly electric and magnetic, following the selection rules ΔJ = ± 1. The sensitivity of the two transitions, (4G5/2→6H9/2) and (4G5/2→6H11/2), to the crystal field makes them purely electric dipole (ED) transitions3,4,55. The symmetry inversion of the ligand surrounding the Sm3+ ion can be determined by the ratio of magnetic dipole to electric dipole (as shown Table 8). It has been observed that the intensity of the magnetic dipole is greater than that of the electric dipole, indicating that there is symmetry around the Sm3+ ion with no deviation from the inversion center2,3,5,56–59. Furthermore, it is shown that the boron oxide structure changes from BO3 to BO4 when lithium oxide (Li2O) is added to borate glasses doped with Sm3+ ions, changing the physical characteristics. This alteration makes the glass samples appropriate for laser applications by increasing their orange-red emission intensity, improving their quantum efficiency, and somewhat reducing their non-radiative decay.

Conclusion

This study investigated the impact of varying the lithium-to-boron ratio on the luminescence properties of Sm3+-doped lithium borate glass under optical excitation. The effects of increasing the amount of lithium oxide at the expense of boron oxide on the structural groups in glass samples were examined using infrared measurements. A disorganized change was observed in groups BO4 and BO3. The glass transition temperature (Tg), thermal stability, glass forming ability and optical bandgap of oxide glasses is found to depend on the Internal structural groups BO3 and BO4, as well as non-bridging oxygen.

The research demonstrates that this effect is the main factor influencing all properties. Transitions of the Sm3+ observed in the UV–vis– NIR optical absorption and luminescence (excitation and emission) spectra have been identified. The optical spectra of the glass samples are almost identical and quite similar to the optical spectra of the Sm3+ in other borate glasses. Optical characterizations, based on absorption and emission band measurements, showed notable results in the visible and near-infrared spectrums for the Sm3+ ion-doped glass. Our findings suggest that these glasses have significant potential and could play a promising role in the advancement of luminous optical systems.
