Characteristics of multilineage‐differentiating stress‐enduring cell clusters in different culture conditions
Authors: Jing Ning, Yan‐Yun Cao, Ru‐Zhi Zhang, Yue Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo observe the morphological characteristics of clusters of Muse cells from normal human dermal fibroblasts (NHDFs) under different culture conditions.MethodsMuse cells were sorted by magnetic activated cell sorting (MACS) from NHDFs, and were evaluated by flow cytometry. Muse cells were cultured in suspension and in adherent conditions to obtain Muse cell clusters (M‐clusters), which were further characterized by alkaline phosphatase (AP) staining, immunofluorescence (IF) staining and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The M‐clusters were further cultured on Lando artificial dermal regeneration matrix (LADRM) for analysis by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and IF staining of frozen sections.ResultsThe proportion of SSEA3 and CD105 double‐positive cells obtained by MACS was 87.4%. The sorted cells rapidly formed M‐clusters after suspension culture, and showed internal characteristics of stem cells under TEM. After adherent culture, M‐clusters stained positively for AP, SSEA‐3 and OCT‐4. Each M‐cluster on the surface of the LADRM displayed an outer membrane of amorphous materials under SEM. Frozen sections and fluorescence staining of LADRM loaded with M‐clusters showed an uneven fluorescence intensity of SSEA‐3 within the clusters.ConclusionsMuse cells sorted by MACS from NHDFs could generate M‐clusters, which included cells of different stemness and are wrapped in membrane‐like structures.Keywords: dermal fibroblasts, magnetic activated cell sorting, muse cell clusters, pluripotency, stem cells

INTRODUCTION

Combinations of stem cells and/or progenitor cells have a strong proliferation ability and are suitable biological materials to repair damaged tissues, which is a major goal of regenerative medicine. 1 Embryonic stem (ES) cells and induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells are limited in future clinical applications due to ethical issues, potential teratoma formation and possible immune rejection after transplantation. 2 Therefore, finding a suitable source of stem cells that can overcome these shortcomings has become an urgent task. Multilineage‐differentiating stress enduring (Muse) cells were accidentally isolated from bone marrow (BM) aspirates by Dezawa et al. in 2010. 3 Since then, Muse cells have shown an excellent differentiation ability and excellent safety and have received attention and praise from researchers and clinicians.

According to the characteristics of Muse cells that can tolerate harsh living conditions, some researchers use a combination of long term trypsinization (LTT) and low adhesion suspension culture to obtain Muse cells. 4 However, that method requires large numbers of starting cells and is a complicated culture process, by which only low‐purity target cells can be obtained. Since Muse cells specifically express SSEA‐3, using fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS) to sort Muse cells from fibroblasts is an accurate and specific method, but it requires expensive equipment. 5 Magnetic activated cell sorting (MACS) is a simple and convenient method of cell sorting that doesn't require special equipment and facilities, and only needs suitable magnetic microbeads with various specific antibodies. 6 The goal of this study was to sort Muse cells using MACS and to evaluate the purity of target cells using FACS.

Muse cells are pluripotent and in suspension culture, individual Muse cells produce cell spheres termed “M‐clusters”. In the literature, most studies have focused on the differentiation of Muse cells into functional target cells, and there have been few morphological studies on M‐clusters. This study characterized M‐clusters under different culture conditions, including suspension culture, adherent culture and culture on biological materials. With the help of scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and immunofluorescence (IF) staining, we explored the biological characteristics of M‐clusters and provide scientific data for subsequent basic research and clinical applications.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cultivation of normal human dermal fibroblasts (NHDFs)

The prepuce tissues were obtained from the outpatient's circumcision after informed consent from nine male patients(age 21.6 ± 8.3 years). After obtaining informed consents from patients and signing informed consent forms. This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Suzhou University (Changzhou, China; protocol number 2016‐03‐02). According to a method reported in the literature, the dermis was separated from the epidermis by treatment with 0.25% neutral protease II (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.) at 37°C for 2 h, and dermal cells were then obtained by sequential digestion with 0.25% type IV collagenase (Sigma‐Aldrich; Merck KGaA) at 37°C for 2 h and 0.25% trypsin‐EDTA (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) for 15 min. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (HyClone; Cytiva) in an incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. The medium was changed every 2–3 days and when the cells reached 80% confluence, they were subcultured. In order to confirm that the cultured cells were fibroblasts, a primary antibody against vimentin (1:500; mouse monoclonal; cat. no. ab8978; Abcam) and a secondary antibody goat anti‐mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488) (1:200; cat. no. ab150113; Abcam) labeled with fluorescence were used to stain those cells. Their nuclei were stained with DAPI (1:1000; cat. no. MA0127; Dalian Meilun biotechnology co., Ltd.).

Sorting SSEA‐3 positive cells from NHDFs by MACS

NHDFs in the fourth generation were used for sorting SSEA‐3 positive cells by MACS. It is necessary to remove dead cells and cell fragments by low‐speed centrifugation several times before sorting in order to reduce non‐specific binding. Each magnetic sorter (MS) column (cat. no. 130042201; Miltenyi Biotec; Bergisch Gladbach) can sort up to 2 × 108 cells. Added the SSEA‐3 antibody (1:20; rat monoclonal; cat. no. ab16286; Abcam) and then the anti‐rat kappa microbeads (cat. no. 130047401; Miltenyi Biotec; Bergisch Gladbach). During the purification, the incubation time and temperature should be well controlled, and no visible bubbles should be produced to avoid clogging of the sorting column. The cells can be sorted again to improve cell purity if necessary. Finally, SSEA‐3 positive cells were collected in separate tubes for the next step of culture.

Detecting the proportion of SSEA‐3 positive cells by flow cytometry

The SSEA‐3 positive cells sorted by MACS were transferred into a flow tube and were rinsed with PBS containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Antibodies against SSEA‐3 (1:100; cat. no. MA1‐020‐PE; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.), CD105 (1:100; cat. no. 17‐1057‐42; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.), an SSEA3 isotype antibody (1:100; cat. no. 12‐4341‐81; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) and a CD105 isotype antibody (1:100; cat. no. 17‐4714‐82; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) were used according to the recommended dosage. Those antibodies in flow tubes containing 5% BSA in PBS were incubated 30 min in a refrigerator at 4°C in the dark. Before flow cytometry, the cells were washed three times with PBS containing 5% BSA.

Observation of M‐clusters in suspension culture

The sorted cells were inoculated in 6‐well plates (Corning Inc.) pre‐coated with poly‐HEMA (2‐hydroxyethyl methacrylate; Sigma‐Aldrich; Merck KGaA), and methylcellulose (MC) medium (cat. no. 4100; STEMCELL Technologies Inc.) and an equal volume of DMEM supplemented with 20% FBS was used as the original medium. The above medium was added every 2 days at 1/4 of the original volume. On the 7th day of culture, cell clusters were harvested. A small amount of each suspension containing cell clusters was dropped onto a coverslip. After natural air drying at room temperature, the cell clusters were fixed with a 4% paraformaldehyde solution (cat. no. MA0192; Dalian Meilun biotechnology co., Ltd.) and were observed using a light microscope (CKX41; Olympus optical co., Ltd.). In order to study the potential application of M‐clusters in injury models in the future, CellTracker CM‐DiI, which is a fluorescent dye well suited for monitoring cell movement or location, was used to label those clusters. In brief, the M‐clusters were incubated with CM‐DiI (1:200; V22888; Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) at a concentration of 5 μg/mL in DMEM without FBS at 37°C for 5 min and then at 4°C for 15 min, after which they were observed and imaged using a fluorescence microscope (IX73; Olympus optical co., Ltd.).

Another part of the collected M‐clusters was fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.) for 4 h at 4°C. After conventional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) processing steps, the specimen was cut into sections at a thickness of 70–90 nm, which were placed on 200‐mesh copper grids and stained with uranium acetate and lead hydroxide. Cell ultrastructure was observed using TEM.

Characteristics of M‐clusters in adherent culture

The M‐clusters obtained were inoculated in 6‐well culture plates pre‐coated with 0.2 % gelatin (Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.), and then were cultivated in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C and 5% CO2. 24 h after inoculation of the M‐clusters, AP staining (cat. no. 86R; Sigma‐Aldrich; Merck KGaA) and IF staining of pluripotent cells was carried out. All operative steps of staining were strictly performed in accordance with the manufacturer's requirements. All procedures were gentle during the process so as not to destroy the actual state of the M‐clusters. Primary antibodies against SSEA‐3 (1:400; rat monoclonal; cat. no. ab16286; Abcam) and Oct‐4 (1:400; rabbit monoclonal; cat. no. ab19857; Abcam), and fluorescent labeled secondary antibodies of goat anti‐rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 594) (1:200; cat. no. ab150080; Abcam) and goat anti‐rat IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488) (1:200; cat. no. ab150157; Abcam), were used in this study. The nuclei were stained for 10 min with DAPI (1:1000).

M‐clusters on (inside) biological materials

The combination of stem cells and biomaterials will have great benefits for future clinical applications. The M‐clusters obtained from the above suspension cultures were seeded on a Lando artificial dermal regeneration matrix (LADRM; Shenzhen Lando biomaterial Co., Ltd), also called bilayer artificial skin, which is made of bovine Achilles tendon type I collagen and chondroitin sulfate cross‐linking, that has been used for clinical wound repair. After culture for 3 days, the specimen was divided into two parts. One part was observed by SEM to determine the sizes and contours of M‐clusters on the surface of the material, and the other part was freeze sectioned and then fluorescence stained with an antibody against SSEA‐3.

For observation by SEM, the biomaterials with cell spheres were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution for 4 h and were then rinsed three times with PBS for 20 min. The specimens were immersed in 1% osmium tetroxide solution at 4°C for about 1 h, subsequently dehydrated in a series of increasing concentrations of ethanol, dried in a CO2 critical point dryer, gold‐plated by vacuum sputtering, and finally observed using SEM. The M‐clusters that had grown on the LADRM for 3 days were embedded in OCT and freeze sectioned, and then stained with an antibody against SSEA‐3.

RESULTS

Sorting SSEA‐3 positive cells from NHDFs

The primary NHDFs were spindle‐shaped and had a strong proliferation ability. They reached 85%–90% confluence and were subcultured after 1 week of primary culture. After the second generation, the cells became more slender and flat, and maintained their rapid proliferation. IF staining with an antibody against vimentin confirmed that those cells were all fibroblasts, which were vimentin‐positive cells (Figure 1A).

NHDFs and the purity of Muse cells sorted by MACS. (A) NHDFs staining positive for vimentin antigen (200×). (B) Flow cytometry showing that 87.4% of cells were SSEA‐3/CD105 double‐positive. NHDFs, normal human dermal fibroblasts.

NHDFs in the fourth passage were used to sort SSEA‐3 positive cells using MACS. Those sorted cells were stained with antibodies to SSEA3 and CD105 for flow cytometry. The results showed that 87.4% of those cells were SSEA‐3/CD105 double‐positive cells (Figure 1B), indicating that a high purity of Muse cells could be sorted using MACS.

Morphological observation of M‐clusters from suspension culture

The SSEA‐3 positive cells sorted by MACS were transferred to MC medium for suspension culture. Single cells proliferated rapidly and formed embryoid body‐like cell clusters, known as Muse cell‐derived cell colonies (M‐clusters). Most of the M‐clusters were quasi‐round, with clear boundaries and were up to approximately 230 μm in diameter (Figure 2A). After fixing these cell clusters with 4% paraformaldehyde, their boundaries and the membrane‐like structures around the M‐clusters were more obvious (Figure 2B).

Observation of characteristics of M‐clusters in suspension culture by multiple methods. (A) Most of M‐clusters in suspension culture were quasi‐round observe by microscopy (100×). (B) Characteristics of M‐clusters in suspension culture fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde observed by microscopy (100×). (C) M‐clusters labeled by CM‐DiI as orange fluorescence (200×). (D) A representative M‐cluster placed on a glass slide were quasi‐round with clear boundaries observed by microscopy. scale bars = 100 μm. (E, F) Cells in M‐clusters have a high nucleus‐to‐cytoplasm ratio, few organelles and many vacuoles in the cytoplasm observed by TEM. Scale bars = 2 μm. TEM, transmission electron microscopy.

CM‐DiI is a kind of stem cell tracer dye, which was added to the suspension culture system in order to label stem cells. Three days later, orange‐red cell spheres were observed using a fluorescence microscope (Figure 2C). A representative cell cluster in normal suspension culture was taken out and placed on a glass slide for closer observation (Figure 2D). Because cells in the M‐clusters were tightly arranged and were wrapped in an outer membrane, it was impossible to count their number. Under TEM, the cells displayed a high nucleus‐to‐cytoplasm ratio, had more protruding nuclei and a larger nucleolar volume, with few organelles in their cytoplasm, suggesting structural immature characteristics of Muse cells (Figure 2E,F).

Characteristics of M‐clusters in adherent culture

The M‐clusters formed in suspension culture were transferred to gelatin‐coated petri dishes containing DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Within 24 h, the M‐clusters had adhered to the bottom of the culture plates and fibroblast‐like cells grew radially around the clusters (Figure 3A). AP staining showed that the M‐clusters were positive (purple‐red) but the fibroblasts around the clusters were not stained, as a negative control (Figure 3B). IF staining was performed on M‐clusters that adhered to the wall for 24 h, using antibodies against the cell stemness‐related antigens SSEA‐3 and Oct‐4, and the results were positive (Figure 3C,D). The cells in the centers of the M‐clusters were small and densely arranged, while cells around the M‐clusters were sparse (Figure 3E,F).

Observation of characteristics of M‐clusters in adherent culture by multiple methods. (A) Cells in the center of M‐clusters were small and quasi‐round, while the surrounding expanded cells were large, multi‐protuberant, spindle‐shaped or polygonal observed by microscopy. scale bars = 100 μm. (B) AP staining was positive for cells in the center of M‐clusters. Scale bars = 100 μm. (C–F) M‐clusters were positive for Oct‐4 (red) and SSEA‐3 (green) staining by IF staining. Scale bars = 100 μm. IF, immunofluorescence.

M‐clusters on (inside) biological materials

Under SEM, several different morphologies of M‐clusters were observed. The most characteristic morphology was M‐clusters of different sizes ranging from 40 to 80 μm in diameter. They displayed spherical or quasi‐spherical shapes with obvious membranes that were presumed to be composed of amorphous materials (Figure 4A,B). Some scattered cells were attached to the membranes of those M‐clusters. The diameter of individual cells ranged from 2 to 7 μm, and most of them were about 5 μm. According to the diameter of the empty nests formed by the shedding of single cells, we estimated that there were about 200 single cells in each M‐cluster. In addition to the M clusters, there were many scattered or aggregated cells on the biological material. There were also amorphous substances between those aggregated cells, which were not wrapped by membrane‐like structures.

Observation of M‐clusters on the biomaterial. (A, B) A large cluster of M‐cells is surrounded by a membrane, and there are many scattered cells near the cluster observed by SEM. Scale bars = 10 μm. (C, D) Immunofluorescence staining of SSEA‐3 (green) and DAPI (blue) staining of M‐clusters inoculated on the biomaterial on the 3rd day. Scale bars = 100 μm.

The artificial skin containing M‐clusters was analyzed by frozen sections and fluorescent staining. The results showed that the M‐clusters retained their spherical shape after 3 days of culture. These M‐clusters were fluorescently stained with an antibody against SSEA‐3 located on the surface and inside of the LADRM (Figure 4C,D). Different from the fluorescent staining of M‐clusters, staining of frozen sections further showed the expression of SSEA‐3 by cells in the M‐clusters. The fluorescence intensity in the M‐clusters was not uniform, indicating that the stemness of different cells was different.

DISCUSSION

Muse cells are widely distributed throughout the body and are not restricted to specific organs or tissues. Muse cells are mainly derived from bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BM‐MSCs), adipocytes and fibroblasts according to the literature. 7 Fibroblasts are easily obtained from the human dermis of discarded prepuce tissues, and their number can be rapidly expanded by in vitro culture. Therefore, dermal fibroblasts were selected as source cells in this study and their identify was confirmed by immunofluorescence staining for vimentin. 8 At present, stem cells are mainly sorted by adherent screening and mechanical separation, by density gradient centrifugation, by FACS and by MACS. 9 , 10 In order to avoid using expensive equipment such as FACS and to overcome the shortcomings of the low purity obtained by LTT sorting, MACS was used in this study to sort Muse cells. 11 The results showed that the sorted target cells had a purity of 87.4% and appeared to have excellent viability.

Muse cells are a special kind of pluripotent stem cells and have a high self‐renewal ability. 12 The Muse cells sorted by MACS quickly produced cell clusters in low‐adhesion suspension cultures. Under suspension culture, these cells quickly grow into spheres (termed M‐clusters) of various sizes. 13 However, probably due to the existence of non‐Muse cells in the outer layer or to intracellular signal transduction caused by suspension, the proliferation of M clusters in suspension will gradually slow down and stop at about 14 days. 14 In our study, each M‐cluster contained tens to hundreds of cells and was surrounded by a membrane‐like structure, suggesting that they were formed by the proliferation of a single (or 2–3 cells) instead of scattered multiple cells that clustered together. The M‐clusters were labeled with orange fluorescence by CM‐DiI, which is a lipophilic fluorescent dye that has been widely used as a tracer of multiple types of stem cells. 15

TEM observations showed that the cells inside these M‐clusters had fewer organelles and a high nuclear‐cytoplasm ratio, suggesting the structural basis for the immaturity and rapid proliferation of those cells. When these M‐clusters were cultured adherently, fibroblast‐like cells migrated out of the M‐clusters and grew radially. AP staining and IF staining with antibodies against SSEA‐3 and Oct‐4 was positive, indicating that the M‐clusters had pluripotent characteristics and that the surrounding cells had differentiated. 16 SSEA‐3 is usually expressed in the early stage of embryonic development, and is also positive in undifferentiated pluripotent stem cells. The expression of SSEA‐3 will decrease with the differentiation of stem cells. 17 Oct4 is a stem gene and a member of the transcription factor family that plays an important role in maintaining the pluripotency of stem cells and regulating their self‐renewal. 18

In view of the attractive application prospects of Muse cells, this study explored the compatibility of Muse cells and biological materials. We seeded the M‐clusters on LADRM, which has been widely used to promote the recovery of skin damage in China and found that they had excellent biocompatibility. 19 Using SEM, the diameter and surface structure of M‐clusters on the LADRM surface was determined. It is worth mentioning that a membrane‐like structure wrapped the M‐clusters. Previous literature reported that the outermost membrane is a monolayer of non‐Muse cells similar to slender fibroblasts. 20 However, our optical microscope images and SEM photographs have not observed that the outermost membrane has a typical cellular structure. We are inclined to think that the membrane is an extracellular matrix secreted by cells within the M‐clusters. Subsequently, frozen sections and anti‐SSEA‐3 fluorescent staining were performed on the material seeded with M‐clusters, and the results revealed that each cell within an M‐cluster expresses different levels of SSEA‐3. Due to the gradual production of non‐Muse cells during the proliferation process, the proportion of Muse cells in culture will gradually decrease and will finally account for only a few percent of the total cells.

Muse cells can home and migrate to a variety of injured tissues, and can then differentiate into target cells and integrate into the tissue to play a repair role. 21 M‐clusters have a smaller exposure area, which can better accommodate and protect internal cells, and can also interact with external substances. 22 This study focused on M‐clusters and provides a useful basis for clinical applications and scientific research.

CONCLUSIONS

MACS is a simple, effective and applicable method to sort Muse cells from NHDFs. The sorted cells proliferated rapidly and formed M‐clusters in suspension culture. The M‐clusters can be labeled by CM‐DiI, a dye that traces stem cells. Observation by TEM showed that Muse cells have the internal structural characteristics of stem cells. The adherent M‐clusters were positive for AP staining and for the expression of pluripotent markers. Muse cells have a good biocompatibility with LADRM. Using biomaterials as a carrier, combined with SEM and cryosection techniques, we carefully observed the external characteristics of M‐clusters and the fluorescence intensity within M‐clusters, showing the outer membrane structure of M‐clusters and the unevenness of stem cells within the M‐clusters.
