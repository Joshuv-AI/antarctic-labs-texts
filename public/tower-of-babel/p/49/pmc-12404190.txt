Speed of life: tuning the ticktock of the segmentation clock
Authors: Sally Lowell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this Outlook, Lowell discusses a study in this issue of Genes & Development by Meijer et al. that reports that the stability of the Notch1 intracellular domain (NICD) and its interaction with E3 ligase FBXW7 are essential to tightly control the segmentation clock and somitogenesis. Lowell highlights the study's use of “somitoid” models, derived from human pluripotent cells, as a tractable method to mechanistically evaluate clock oscillation and the morphological consequences of its disruption.

It is remarkable that our tiny cells seem able to count time (Ebisuya and Briscoe 2018). Cellular “clocks” are particularly important for somitogenesis, the process by which paired somites progressively emerge from anterior to posterior as the vertebrate axis elongates (Hubaud and Pourquié 2014; Matsuda et al. 2024). This process is governed in part by cell-intrinsic oscillations of a number of regulators, including the transcription factor HES7. The regular “ticktock” of these oscillations, termed the “segmentation clock,” dictates the pace of somite formation. This pace differs between species and so must be under tunable molecular control (Hubaud and Pourquié 2014).

Notch signaling is an important regulator of the segmentation clock (Jiang et al. 2000). The Notch signaling pathway is simple and direct: Upon activation, the transmembrane Notch receptor releases its intracellular domain (termed “NICD”), which travels to the nucleus to control gene expression. The strength and duration of Notch activity depend on how quickly NICD is subsequently degraded. It is well established that phosphorylation of NICD influences its stability (Borggrefe et al. 2016), but it is not entirely clear how this level of regulation influences the pace of the segmentation clock, particularly in humans. It is this question that Meijer et al. (2025) set out to address.

The segmentation clock operates within the presomitic mesoderm, the tissue from which somites emerge. The investigators first asked how NICD stability is regulated in human presomitic mesoderm. They took advantage of protocols to generate large numbers of presomitic mesoderm cells from human pluripotent cells (Diaz-Cuadros et al. 2020). This provides a system amenable to biochemical analysis, including assays for cell–cell interactions and measurements of protein half-lives.

The investigators focused on the E3 ligase FBXW7, because this is known to regulate NICD stability in other contexts. They engineered tunable manipulation of endogenous FBXW7 levels within human pluripotent cells and then differentiated them into presomitic mesoderm cells. The investigators then measured NICD stability and were able to confirm that FBXW7 is indeed required for rapid degradation of NICD in the human presomitic mesoderm.

How does FBXW7 interact with NICD to regulate its stability? In previous work, the investigators of this study have shown that the serine 2513 residue of NICD is critical for its interaction with FBXW7 in various cell types (Carrieri et al. 2019). They mutated this serine residue to alanine (S2513A mutation) in pluripotent cells and then used these cells to generate a S2513A mutant presomitic mesoderm. Using a range of biochemical assays, they discovered that this single point mutation abolished the interaction of FBXW7 with NICD and stabilized NICD in the context of the human presomitic mesoderm.

Overall, these cell culture experiments identified a molecular mechanism controlling the stability of NICD in the human presomitic mesoderm and identified a S2513A point mutation that could abolish this mechanism and therefore stabilize NICD. This opened up the opportunity to ask how NICD stability influences the pace of the human somite clock.

The first challenge was to find a way to model human somitogenesis. In recent years, there has been remarkable progress in steering the self-organization abilities of human pluripotent cells to model various aspects of human development. For example, “somitoid” models are cellular aggregates that mimic aspects of human axis formation, including elongation along the anterior–posterior axis, and formation of paired somites (Sanaki-Matsumiya et al. 2022; Miao et al. 2023; Yamanaka et al. 2023). This provides a tractable system for studying the effects of the S2513A mutation on human somitogenesis. Somitoids are derived from pluripotent cells, so Meijer et al. (2025) were able to take advantage of the S2513A mutant cells that they had generated for the earlier part of their study (described above). They generated S2513A mutant somitoids and discovered that they had morphological abnormalities, failing to properly polarize and elongate.

The morphological defects of S2513A mutant somitoids were consistent with misregulation of the segmentation clock, so the investigators sought a way to explore this further by directly measuring clock oscillations. To achieve this, they made use of an HES7-ACHILLES reporter that had previously been shown to provide a readout for periodic clock oscillations in human cells (Diaz-Cuadros et al. 2020); ACHILLES is a modified version of yellow fluorescent protein, and HES7 is a core component of the somitogenesis clock. Meijer et al. (2025) used these reporters to generate beautiful, quantitative, live-imaging data that confirmed regular oscillations of Hes7-Achilles in somitoids generated from wild-type pluripotent cells. In contrast, somitoids harboring the S2513A mutation (the point mutation that stabilizes NICD) exhibited accelerated periodicity in clock oscillations, which then became rapidly dampened.

In summary, this work reveals that the stability of Notch is important for controlling the characteristic timing of the somite clock in the human presomitic mesoderm. This system provides one regulatory node that allows this periodicity to be retuned. It would be interesting in future work to explore whether differences in the regulation of NICD stability might explain differences in the pace of the segmentation clock between different vertebrate species.

This study provides a beautiful example of how models built from pluripotent cells can be used for precise dissection of molecular mechanisms and quantitative analysis of dynamic phenotypes during human development. It also exemplifies how these systems may give insights into mechanisms driving human disease. For example, the S2513A mutation is associated with a particular type of leukemia (Bonn et al. 2013), which is likely driven by pathological stabilization of NICD. Understanding how cells keep tight control over NICD stability is therefore likely to be important far beyond the remarkable process of somitogenesis.
