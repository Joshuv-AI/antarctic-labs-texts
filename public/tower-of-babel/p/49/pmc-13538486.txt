Ionic liquid-grafted PVDF membrane incorporated with g-C3N4-Fe3O4 for the removal of cationic dye and improved anti-bacterial activity
Authors: Ameneh Lari, Abdolmajid Bayandori Moghaddam, Mohammad-Hossein Sarrafzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of multifunctional membranes with tunable surface charge, high permeability, reactive pollutant removal, and bacterial resistance remains a critical challenge in wastewater treatment. Here, a poly(vinylimidazolium)-functionalized PVDF membrane incorporating g-C3N4-Fe3O4 was fabricated through surface activation, composite deposition, free-radical graft polymerization, and subsequent hydroxide anion exchange to tailor surface physicochemical properties. The engineered membrane exhibited significantly enhanced hydrophilicity and high water permeability (109 L m− 2 h− 1 bar − 1), corresponding to a 25-fold increase over pristine PVDF. It showed strong removal of methylene blue (97%) but lower removal of methyl orange (30%) at 20 ppm, indicating charge-dependent reactive removal behavior. Methylene blue displayed a strong affinity for the modified surface, enabling effective interfacial adsorption and subsequent photocatalytic degradation under visible light, whereas methyl orange interacted much less effectively. Although most dye-removal experiments were conducted under static conditions to evaluate surface activity, pressure-driven filtration still achieved up to 83% methylene blue removal within the microfiltration pore-size range, confirming that pollutant removal was governed by reactive surface interactions rather than conventional size-exclusion mechanisms. Adsorption kinetics followed pseudo-second-order model and fit the Freundlich isotherm, suggesting heterogeneous interactions between dye molecules and the membrane surface. Interfacial adsorption governed pollutant localization in both dark and illuminated conditions, with photocatalysis providing an additional contribution under light. The membrane also exhibited excellent antibacterial activity, with near-complete flux recovery over eight filtration cycles. Overall, integrating polymeric ionic liquids with carbon nitride-based nanocomposites offers a promising route to low-pressure reactive membranes with enhanced pollutant removal and antibacterial properties for wastewater treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-59899-5.Keywords: Ionic liquid functionalization, VImBr/g-C3N4–Fe3O4 composite, Reactive dye removal, Photocatalytic degradation, Antibacterial membraneSubject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) is widely used in the fabrication of membranes for water purification due to its high mechanical strength, thermal stability, and chemical resistance1,2. However, PVDF membranes show a strong nonspecific adsorption of pollutants and are highly susceptible to fouling and biofouling due to their inherent hydrophobicity, which limits practical application and increases operational costs3–5. Therefore, hydrophilic surface modification is essential for improving membrane performance6,7.

Recently graphitic carbon nitrite (g-C3N4) has been widely employed as a promising photocatalyst due to its visible-light response (photocatalysis), metal-free nature, stable chemical properties, and non-toxicity8,9, which is especially important in terms of the membrane modification. Photocatalysis is considered a sustainable environmental technology that is essential for primary applications, particularly in the oxidation of harmful pollutants in water, owing to the production of highly reactive oxygen generated10–12. The incorporation of g-C3N4 into PVDF membranes has been reported to improve both photocatalytic activity and membrane hydrophilicity. Under visible-light irradiation, immobilized g-C3N4 can facilitate the degradation of organic pollutants such as cationic dyes while simultaneously introducing nitrogen-containing surface functionalities that enhance water permeability and antifouling behavior. Previous studies have also shown that g-C3N4 modified PVDF membranes exhibit improved self-cleaning performance and enhanced flux recovery during biological filtration processes13,14.

Coupling other materials with g-C3N4 can create a heterojunction through changes in band gap energy, significantly enhancing the separation efficiency of electron/hole (e-/h+) pairs in g-C3N4 and improving the performance of the PVDF membrane15,16.

Imidazolium-ionic liquids (ILs), comprising organic asymmetric cations and organic or inorganic anions, have a variety of applications including alternative catalysts17, solvents18, gas and liquid separation19,20, batteries and fuel cells21,22, adsorbents for water pollutant removal23, and biocidal agents24. Due to their tunable polarity, high thermal stability, and chemical robustness, ionic liquids have emerged as promising membrane modifiers for water treatment applications. In microfiltration membranes based on 1-vinyl-3-butylimidazolium chloride grafted PVDF, water flux dramatically increased compared to pristine PVDF membrane, attributing to increasing surface pore sizes and the enhanced hydrophilicity25. The membranes exhibited antibacterial properties against both S. aureus and E. coli with increasing butylimidazolium chloride loading, attributing the cationic nature of imidazole ring which electrostatically interact with the microbial cell membranes and the alkyl chains could disturb the lipid layer structure of the cell membranes. Furthermore, ILs-based PVDF membranes were further enhanced by a simple anion exchange process to develop membranes with the hydrophilic surface and the optimized charge to adsorb cationic and anionic dyes and fouling resistance, due to the controllability of the ionic liquid structure26,27.

Although PVDF membranes modified with either carbon nitride or imidazolium-based ionic liquids have been investigated previously, studies combining these functionalities within a single membrane platform remain relatively limited. In particular, the integration of photocatalytic g-C3N4-Fe3O4 nanostructures with polymeric ionic-liquid functionalities offers the possibility of simultaneously tailoring surface charge characteristics, hydrophilicity, adsorption behavior, and antibiofouling properties28. Motivated by this perspective, the present work investigates a multifunctional membrane design based on the combined incorporation of g-C3N4-Fe3O4 and poly(vinylimidazolium)-based functionalities onto a PVDF substrate. In this study, we develop a PVDF membrane functionalized with a g-C3N4/imidazolium polymer composite via a free-radical polymerization strategy. The modification approach includes controlled surface activation followed by grafting of PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 onto the membrane substrate to introduce tailored interactions, tunable surface charge, and improved hydrophilicity. This work aims to elucidate how the simultaneous incorporation of ionic-liquid and carbon-nitride functionalities can be leveraged to design membranes with enhanced separation, antibacterial, and bio-interaction properties. The study provides useful insight into the integration of multifunctional nanomaterials and polymeric ionic liquids for the design of advanced membrane systems.

Materials and methods

Materials and characterization

Hydrophobic PVDF membranes with a pore size of 0.45 μm were purchased from a membrane solution company. Azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN), sodium hydroxide, methylene blue, methyl orange, 1-vinylimidazole, potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7), polydopamine (PDA), and 1,4-benzoquinone (BQ) were supplied from Sigma Aldrich Inc. Urea, Tetrahydrofuran (THF), Isopropanol (IPA), and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt (EDTA-2Na) were obtained from Merck. Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) were prepared from the Iranian Collection for Type Culture. All the chemicals and solvents were used without purification unless otherwise noted.

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy measurement was conducted with a Perkin-Elmer Frontier FT-IR spectrometer. The water contact angle was calculated using a CAG-20 equipped with a side camera. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) was performed using a FEI Inspect F. UV-Vis. absorption spectra of the samples were obtained using a DRS S-4100 SCINCO. Zeta-potential and DLS measurements were collected using a Malvern ZN series. X-ray photo electron spectroscopy (XPS) was analyzed using a Versa Probe by XPS Thermo K-Alpha with an angular-resolved electron analyzer. The surface morphology of the modified membranes was imaged using a field-emission scanning electron microscopy equipped with an energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) (FE-SEM; TESCAN MIRA 3). The surface topography was characterized by Atomic Force Microscope (AFM, Solver model).

Membrane modification method

The hydrophobic PVDF membranes, with an area of 20 cm², were soaked in a 1 M NaOH aqueous solution and stirred at 60 °C for 1 h to generate a significant number of hydroxyl groups on their surface, as depicted in Fig. 132. Afterward, the hydroxylated PVDF was dried at room temperature for 24 h. Subsequently, the dried hydroxylated PVDF was fully immersed in a suspension of VImBr/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 (0.02 g in ethanol) and modified physically through vacuum-assisted filtration of the suspension. For the chemical grafting of the PVImBr/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 layer, the coated membrane was treated in an AIBN solution (0.5 g in THF) under argon conditions for 24 h at 80 °C to facilitate polymer formation. The resulting membrane (PVImBr/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF) was then washed with water and ethanol. Finally, the crosslinked PVDF was shaken in a 1 M NaOH solution for 24 h at room temperature and thoroughly rinsed with deionized water, resulting in a brown film (PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF). The g-C3N4-Fe3O4 composite was selected due to the complementary combination of visible-light-responsive photocatalytic properties and improved charge-transfer characteristics. g-C3N4 provides photocatalytic activity and surface functionality, while Fe3O4 may facilitate electron transport, reduce recombination effects, and improve interfacial characteristics. Although the individual contributions of each component were not independently isolated in this study, the observed membrane-performance enhancements suggest cooperative effects arising from the integrated composite structure[28, 33, 34].

Poly(vinylimidazolium hydroxide) was selected as the ionic liquid component due to its ability to simultaneously enhance membrane hydrophilicity, introduce tunable surface charge, and improve interfacial interactions with charged species26. The hydroxide counterion contributes to increased wettability and the development of a negatively charged surface after ion exchange, which favors the adsorption of cationic pollutants and improves anti-bacterial activity. The combined effects of these properties make PVImOH a multifunctional modifier for membrane performance enhancement.

To further evaluation of the individual components, we investigated Fe3O4, g-C3N4-Fe3O4, and PVIm/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 coated onto PVDF, as illustrated in the Supplementary section.

Antibacterial experiments

A mixed bacterial culture of E. coli and S. aureus was prepared in LB broth and incubated with shaking at 110 rpm for 24 h at 37 °C. The sterilized membranes, including pristine PVDF, PVImBr/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF, PVImOH1/g-C3N4–Fe3O4/PVDF (modified with 0.01 g of VImBr/g-C3N4-Fe3O4), and PVImOH2/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF (modified with 0.02 g of VImBr/g-C3N4-Fe3O4), were then immersed in the bacterial suspension and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h under both visible-light and dark conditions. To visualize and distinguish live and dead bacteria, 1 µL of the solution in contact with each membrane was transferred onto a petri dish and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.

Filtration experiments

Anti-bacterial activity of the pristine membrane and PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF were assessed using a membrane module setup with an effective membrane area of 45 cm236. Before measuring the flux, the membranes were prewetted by deionized water for 24 h, and the pure water flux was recorded as J0 (L m−2 h−1) at 1 bar for 30 min. Subsequently, a feed solution containing E. coli and S. aureus at a concentration of 107 CFU mL− 1 was pumped over the membranes, and the water flux was recorded as J (L m−2 h−1) over a period of time of 90 min. Following this, the membranes were regenerated using pure water and irradiation for 30 min. For methyl orange and methylene blue solutions at a concentration of 5 ppm, the water flux and dye separation performance were evaluated using a dead-end filtration device with an effective membrane area of 45 cm² under controlled pressure and flow conditions.

Results and discussions

Hydrophobic PVDF membranes are prone to fouling formation and require modification with photocatalytic and antimicrobial materials . As shown in Fig. 1, alkaline treatment acted as a surface-activation step that improved membrane wettability and created anchoring sites for subsequent surface grafting reactions. The introduction of a VImBr/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 into the membrane led to free radical polymerization in the presence of AIBN. Furthermore, the hydrophilicity of the membrane was improved by exchanging bromide with hydroxide anion, resulting in the formation of PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF.

Characterization of membranes

The optical properties of the modified membrane were investigated in Fig. 2. Unlike the pristine PVDF, the modified membrane, similar to the PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 and VImBr/g-C3N4-Fe3O4, exhibited an increase in the absorption edge. The light absorption capacity was further improved, likely due to the interactions between the PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 molecules and the PVDF matrix as a result of the presence of g-C3N439. Furthermore, when each component of the composite (PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4), including Fe3O4, g-C3N4-Fe3O4, PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 was coated onto PVDF, the results showed that the last composite exhibited the superior photocatalytic properties (Fig. S1). This enhancement is attributed to the imidazolium ionic liquid, which improves the photophysical properties of g-C3N440,41.

The pristine PVDF exhibits two distinct characteristic peaks at 1170 cm−1 and 1400 cm−1, which correspond to the stretching vibrations of C-F and C-H of the primary functional groups in the hydrophobic membrane, respectively42. However, the peak strength was weak due to the chemical grafting of the PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 on the membrane. Peaks located at 803 cm−1, 1200–1700 cm−1 and 670 cm−1 are ascribed to the triazine units, C–N heterocycles, and Fe3O4, respectively43–45. In addition, the broad band at 2700–3700 cm−1 can be attributed to the N-H and OH groups of the aromatic rings, which become stronger in the modified PVDF due to the presence of hydroxyl anions46,47. It can be seen that the most significant change between VImBr/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 and modified PVDF is the fading of the peaks at 1640 (vinyl groups), and 1170 cm−1, due to chemical reaction of the PVDF surface with the imidazolium ring and PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 formation, respectively. The peak strength of imidazolium IL decreased more than that of g-C3N4 compared to the polyvinylimidazolium, indicating more bonding between ILs and the PVDF surface. Specific interactions between the IL ions and the PVDF chains primarily drive these modifications. Wang et al. indicated that PVDF disturbs the local structures of the imidazolium cations, particularly the C-H groups, suggesting significant cation-polymer interactions49. Additionally, Shamsuri et al. identified that the main mechanism is based on ion-dipole interactions, in which the positively charged imidazolium cation interacts with the electron- withdrawing CF2 groups of the PVDF backbone50. However, the characteristic F signal of pristine PVDF was weakly detected by EDS (Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy). So that the weight% of F atoms has decreased from 56.92% by weight to 8.41% (Table S1), which could be attributed to the surface coverage of the PVDF substrate by the PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 layer51. In addition, the Br 3 d peak was not observed after modification, which may suggest successful anion exchange with NaOH solution, consistent with the XPS, EDS, and elemental mapping results (Tables S1 and S2, Figures S3a, S4 and S5). As shown in Fig. 3c, the high-resolution C 1 s spectrum could be deconvoluted into peaks centered at 284.6, 285.7, 286.5, 287.7, and 287.9 eV, corresponding to C–C/C = C, C–OH, C–NH2 groups located at the edges of g-C3N4 units, C = O, and (C)3–N species within the tri-s- triazine structure, respectively52. In pristine g-C3N4 without NaOH treatment, the peaks corresponding to oxygen-containing groups such as C = O and C–OH were not observed53. However, when poly vinyl imidazolium bromide was treated with hydroxide, no C = O and C-OH peaks were reported, indicating the replacement of Br with OH‾. In agreement with this observation, the EDS and XPS analyses showed a reduced Br signal after modification compared with the pristine membrane. In addition, the decreased intensity of the C = C related peak in the modified membrane may be associated with partial crosslinking of vinyl groups or vinyl polymerization on the PVDF surface54. The N 1 s spectrum of PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF (Fig. 3d) could be fitted into three peaks centered at 398.4, 399.5, and 401 eV, which may be assigned to C–N = C, N–H, and N+–C bonds, respectively, and are comparable to those observed for PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O452. Similarly, the O 1 s spectrum (Fig. 3e) exhibited deconvoluted peaks at 529.1 and 531.9 eV, which may correspond to Fe–O and C–OH species, respectively, and are generally similar to those observed for PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O428.

AFM images of both the pristine PVDF membrane and the modified PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF membrane are shown in Fig. S7. The roughness parameters, including average roughness (Sa) and root mean square roughness (Sq), were analyzed as well. The modified membrane demonstrated slightly lower Sa and Sq values compared to the pristine PVDF membrane. This decrease in roughness can be attributed to the successful and relatively uniform deposition of PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 particles on the membrane surface. Importantly, the decreased roughness is related to the presence of the functional coating layer rather than any structural damage. Furthermore, the AFM images showed no signs of crack formation or surface defects, indicating that the integrity of the membrane was preserved following the modification.

In the characterization of polymeric membranes, the molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) analysis is an important parameter for membrane classification, as it indicates the pore size of a membrane and its potential applications. Ultra-filtration (UF) membranes typically have an MWCO ranging from 10 to 15 kDa, which is significantly larger compared to nanofiltration (NF) membranes that exhibit MWCOs between 200 and 2000 Da. Generally, the MWCOs of microfiltration membranes are larger than UF membranes56,57. Traditionally, the MWCO of a membrane has been determined by exposing it to various solutes with known sizes, such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) and dextran. The concentration of the solute in the permeate is then measured, and when 90% of the solute is retained by the membrane, the MWCO is established. To further investigate the separation characteristics of the modified membrane, rejection experiments were conducted using PEG (MW = 50,000 Da) and polyethylene oxide (PEO) (MW = 100,000 Da). The rejection values were approximately 5% and 10%, respectively. These low rejection rates indicate that even large polymer molecules can readily permeate through the membrane, confirming that the surface modification did not create a dense selective layer. As a result, the membrane maintained its inherent microfiltration characteristics after modification. Consequently, the enhanced removal of methylene blue cannot be attributed to molecular sieving; instead, it mainly results from adsorption-assisted and photo-assisted interactions occurring on the membrane surface.

Permeability and hydrophilicity of membranes

According to Fig. 6a, the unmodified PVDF exhibited significant hydrophobicity with an initial contact angle of 104.5° ± 5°, which dropped to 97.5° ± 2° for hydroxylated PVDF, whereas PVImBr/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF demonstrated a progressive reduction in contact angle, reaching 60.5° ± 2°. The introduction of hydroxyl anions in PVImOH2/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF further lowered the contact angle to 41.6° ± 2°, enhancing hydrophilicity with 0.02 g of VImBr/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 layer grafted onto the membrane, compared to 0.01 g in PVImOH1/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF. This result is consistent with those of Liu et al., who reported increased grafting of imidazolium moieties onto the PVDF surface due to improvements in pore size and porosity58. The water contact angle of PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF was comparable to that of other hydrophilic membranes modified with different materials listed in Table 2. Additionally, the water permeability of PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF highlighted the impact of the polymer layer on the pristine membrane (Fig. 6b). The hydrophobic PVDF showed low permeability (4.3 L m−2 h−1 due to its structural hydrophobicity. However, this permeability rose to 24 L m−2 h−1 for hydroxylated PVDF. When a grafted polymer layer was introduced into PVDF, the water permeability increased significantly to 66 L m−2 h−1, with even higher values of 75 and 109 L m−2 h−1 for PVImOH1/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF and PVImOH2/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF, respectively. This enhancement suggests that the polymer brushes improve the free energy of the membrane surface and strengthen the interaction between the surface and water molecules. Additionally, the modified membranes with carbon nitride, imidazolium moieties, and hydroxyl anions further increase the content of oxygen and nitrogen elements on the surface, which contributes to the membranes hydrophilicity59. In contrast, introducing more than 0.02 g of VImBr/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 inhibited the connection of the PVImBr/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 to the PVDF, which lacked a uniform surface.

Additional control-membrane experiments were performed to evaluate the feasibility of single-component modifications (Fig. S8a,b). While Fe₃O₄, g-C3N4-Fe3O4 and PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 nanoparticles could be successfully immobilized on PDA-coated PVDF membranes, g-C₃N₄ particles exhibited poor adhesion and significant particle loss during filtration and washing processes. Therefore, the improved membrane performance observed in this study should be attributed to the synergistic action of the composite modification layer rather than to any individual component alone.

Adsorptive and photoreactive removal of organic dyes

Part (a) of Fig. 7 depicts the removal of methylene blue and methyl orange dyes by the PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF membrane over time. The PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF membrane showed fast and efficient removal of the cationic dye methylene blue compared to the anionic dye methyl orange. It achieved a removal efficiency of over 97% for methylene blue at initial concentrations of 5, 10, 15, and 20 ppm after 3 h, while it could only remove methyl orange dye at a maximum of 31% under the same conditions. The higher negative surface charge (zeta potential: −11 mV (inserted in Fig. 7a) of PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4, which includes hydroxyl anions, enhanced its attraction to cationic dyes60. This implies that the membrane surface gains a higher negative charge, significantly influencing the electrostatic adsorption between cationic dye molecules and the membrane surface, resulting in catalytic/photocatalytic degradation62. The observed decrease in dye concentration in this study is attributed to a combination of interfacial adsorption, membrane-assisted retention, and catalytic/photocatalytic degradation processes occurring on the surface of the reactive membrane. In contrast to conventional pressure-driven membranes, the PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF membrane was designed as a low-pressure reactive membrane system. In this system, pollutant removal is primarily driven by surface interactions and subsequent catalytic transformations, rather than by molecular sieving (Fig. 7b). Filtration experiments demonstrated that the membrane could remove up to 83% of methylene blue despite operating under microfiltration conditions, confirming that pollutant removal is not governed by size exclusion but rather by coupled reactive interfacial processes. In contrast, methyl orange exhibited minimal removal due to weaker interaction with the negatively charged membrane surface. Compared with a previous study in which PVDF grafted with 1-allyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate (average pore size of 70 nm) achieved 80% removal of methylene blue, the PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF membrane demonstrates notably improved performance27. It should be noted that the PVImOH/g-C₃N₄-Fe₃O₄/PVDF membrane was designed as a low-pressure reactive membrane rather than a conventional molecular sieving membrane. Therefore, pollutant removal in this system is primarily governed by interfacial adsorption and catalytic/photocatalytic surface reactions rather than size-exclusion-based removal mechanisms. The membrane surface acts as an active reactive interface where pollutant molecules interact with functionalized catalytic sites prior to degradation.

To further evaluate the performance of the membrane in static conditions, experimental results (Fig. S10) showed significant methylene blue removal (87%) even under dark conditions, indicating strong interactions between the dye molecules and the functionalized membrane surface. However, this behavior should not be interpreted as purely physical adsorption because the ionic liquid functionalities may also participate in catalytic surface-mediated reactions in the absence of light. Under visible-light irradiation, an additional increase in removal efficiency was observed, indicating the contribution of photocatalytic pathways promoted by the g-C3N4-Fe3O4 active sites. To investigate the potential reactive species involved in the photocatalytic process, we conducted scavenger experiments using benzoquinone (BQ), isopropanol (IPA), potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7), and sodium ethylenediaminetetraacetate (EDTA-2Na) as scavengers for superoxide radicals (•O2-), hydroxyl radicals (•OH), electrons, and holes, respectively. As illustrated in Fig. 8a, the addition of BQ and IPA led to a significantly greater decrease in methylene blue removal efficiency compared to the effects observed with K2Cr2O7 and EDTA-2Na.

Tauc plots obtained from UV-Vis. DRS analysis (Fig. 8b) reveal that the apparent band-gap energy decreased from about 3.0 eV to approximately 2.3–2.4 eV after incorporating Fe3O4 and poly(vinylimidazolium) into the g-C3N4-based structure. Previous studies have reported that the contribution of Fe3O4 to band gap narrowing and improved charge separation is more pronounced than that of other materials when coupled with g-C3N439,63,64. Moreover, the well-ordered microporous structure of the g-C3N4/Fe3O4/PVDF composite facilitates light penetration and enhances visible-light absorption28. This structure introduces intermediate energy levels within the PVDF framework, further reducing the band gap of the composite film. The observed band gap reduction suggests more efficient visible-light utilization and increased generation of charge carries during photocatalysis. This behavior suggests enhanced visible-light absorption capability of the modified system. Nevertheless, direct investigation of charge-transfer pathways and band alignment was beyond the scope of the present study, and therefore no definitive conclusions are drawn regarding the detailed electronic mechanism.

For further evaluation of the modified membrane performance under different environmental conditions, the dye removal efficiency of PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF was investigated at various pH values ranging from 5 to 9 (Fig. 9a). As the pH values increased, the removal efficiency of methyl orange increased, whereas the removal efficiency of methylene blue decreased. This behavior is attributed to the tendency of imidazolium moieties to capture anionic dyes by exchanging their counter anions, which enhances their capture capacity for anionic materials at higher pH65.

To investigate how coexisting ions affect the removal efficiency of methylene blue and methyl orange, experiments were conducted in the presence of various cations, including Na⁺ and Mg²⁺, as well as the anion Cl⁻. Part (b) of Fig. 9 shows that the removal efficiency for methylene blue remains relatively stable in the presence of both cations and the anion, with no significant decrease observed. However, the presence of the Cl⁻ anion notably reduced the removal efficiency of the cationic dye more than the tested cations. This reduction may be attributed to the exchange of hydroxyl groups with chloride ions, as hydroxyl species play an important role in the interaction with methylene blue. In contrast, for methyl orange, the Cl⁻ anion had an opposite effect. The competing cations tend to adsorb onto the positively charged sites of the membrane, which results in a decrease in removal efficiency.

Adsorption kinetics

Rapid adsorption of dyes by the membranes is highly desired for the effective treatment of wastewater. Consequently, the performance of PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF prepared in this study was evaluated through time-resolved adsorption experiments. Four different concentrations of methylene blue and methyl orange were tested: a low initial concentration of 5 mg L− 1 a higher C0 of 20 mg L− 1. Samples were collected every 30 min, as illustrated in Figs. 10a,b. The methylene blue concentration reached equilibrium after 180 min, resulting in complete decolorization. However, for the methyl orange dye, an adsorption-desorption equilibrium was established after just 30 min, although the level of adsorption remained limited. Consequently, the investigation of the adsorption mechanism of methylene blue was focused on the modified membrane. Adsorption and interfacial interaction mechanism

The interaction of methylene blue with the modified membrane is likely governed by several interconnected interfacial phenomena. The negatively charged surface of the membrane promotes electrostatic interactions with cationic dye molecules, which is consistent with the zeta potential measurements. Additionally, the conjugated structures found in both the membrane (including imidazolium rings and triazine units) and the dye molecules may enhance π–π interactions. Cation–π interactions may also contribute to the localization of pollutants near the active surface (Fig. S12)67,68[,69. Supporting ATR analysis (Fig. 12) demonstrated changes in peak intensities after dye exposure, indicating that membrane surface functional groups, such as N-H and OH groups at 2700–3700 cm− 1, as well as aromatic C = C/–C = N/–C–N bonds at 1000–1500 cm− 1, are involved in the interaction process. The partial recovery of these spectral features after membrane regeneration further suggests the reversible nature of the interaction and the regenerative capability of the reactive membrane surface. Importantly, findings from other researchers indicate that active electrons on imidazolium ionic liquids can facilitate the reduction of methylene blue, suggesting a catalytic reaction occurs between the cationic dye and the ionic liquids. This phenomenon may explain the stability of the modified membrane after regeneration70[,71. The spectra of the regenerated modified membrane showed no significant differences compared to the PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF membrane before adsorption, confirming near-complete desorption72. Unlike conventional adsorption membranes, the system described here operates as a reactive membrane where adsorption serves as an interfacial enrichment step prior to the catalytic and photocatalytic transformation of pollutants. As a result, membrane regeneration involves not only desorption processes but also partial photodegradation of adsorbed species under visible light irradiation. However, these interaction pathways are inferred from indirect experimental observations and structural considerations, and the individual quantitative contributions of each pathway cannot be conclusively distinguished within the scope of this study.

Anti-bacterial properties of membranes

The antibacterial properties of PVDF membranes, along with those modified with PVImBr/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 and PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4, were tested against suspensions of E. coli and S. aureus. This evaluation was conducted under both dark and visible-light conditions using a plate counting method (see Fig. 13a–d). The modified membranes demonstrated significantly enhanced antibacterial activity compared to the pristine PVDF. This improvement is partly due to the presence of imidazolium groups, which can interact electrostatically with negatively charged bacterial cell membranes, disrupting their integrity73[,74. The incorporation of imidazolium polymers introduced a cationic functional layer that can capture negative bacteria and form stable chemical bonds with the nanocomposite75. Under visible light irradiation, the antibacterial performance was further enhanced by the photocatalytically generated reactive oxygen species (ROS)76. Scavenger experiments indicated that superoxide radicals are the dominant reactive species involved in this process. The combined effects of surface functionality, improved hydrophilicity, and ROS generation contribute to the bactericidal behavior of the modified membrane. To assess the antibacterial effectiveness of the modified PVDF, tests were conducted using a highly concentrated feed solution containing both E. coli and S. aureus. Part (a) of Fig. 14 illustrates the normalized water flux (J/J0) of the PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF membrane compared to the pristine hydrophobic PVDF membrane over a period of 90 min. Incorporating the g-C3N4 and imidazolium moieties with notable antibacterial properties achieved the highest flux by minimizing the bacterial adhesion or attachment to membranes. It could be attributed to the transfer of photo-induced charges by g-C3N4 and electrostatic repulsion, and adsorption between the membrane imidazolium moiety and its anions and bacteria77,78[,79. However, first, a drastic flux decline was observed for the modified membrane within the initial 10 min. A dense bacterial layer is likely to form on the membrane surface due to the high loading of bacteria, leading to a sharp decrease in water flux by 88% and then maitaining the water flux between 88 and 92% of the initial pure water flux. PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF exhibited almost complete water flux recovery after the light treatment, retaining > 88% of flux for eight repeated cycles, demonstrating the excellent inhibition of biofilm formation on the modified membrane (Fig. 14b). High water recovery of the PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4/PVDF over the cycles proves the high stability of PVImOH/g-C3N4-Fe3O4 on the PVDF membrane for the long term. However, photocatalyst activity has the effective role in the antibacterial activity of the modified membrane. According to Fig. S13, approximately after 30 min, the permeability decreased to 70% and remain constant under dark condition.

Conclusion

In this study, a multifunctional PVDF membrane was successfully engineered by integrating a g-C₃N₄-Fe₃O₄ nanocomposite with a poly(vinylimidazolium)-based ionic liquid using surface activation and graft polymerization techniques. This modification strategy significantly improved the membrane’s hydrophilicity and water permeability compared to the pristine PVDF, demonstrating the effectiveness of introducing ionic liquid functionalities and nitrogen-rich nanostructures to the membrane surface. The experimental results indicated that the modified membrane exhibits strong removal performance for cationic dyes, particularly methylene blue, while showing comparatively lower affinity for anionic dyes. This behavior aligns with the measured surface charge characteristics and suggests that electrostatic interactions are crucial in governing the interactions between the dye and the membrane. Kinetic and isotherm analyses further indicate that the removal process is primarily controlled by adsorption on heterogeneous surface sites, consistent with previous reports on ionic liquid-functionalized systems. Comparing dark and illuminated conditions revealed that dye removal occurs even in the absence of light, which indicates that adsorption is the dominant mechanism under the tested conditions. While the photocatalytic activity associated with g-C₃N₄-Fe₃O₄ contributes to additional removal under visible light, its role appears secondary to that of adsorption. Thus, the overall performance of the membrane can be better characterized as adsorption-assisted removal with a supplementary photocatalytic contribution rather than a purely photocatalytic process. In addition to dye removal, the modified membrane demonstrated improved resistance to biofilm layer and high flux recovery over repeated cycles. This behavior can be attributed to the combined effects of photodegradation and the presence of antimicrobial imidazolium groups, which collectively reduce bacterial adhesion and enhance membrane cleaning efficiency.

Overall, this work highlights that incorporating polymeric ionic liquids into g-C₃N₄-based membrane systems is an effective way to enhance surface properties and improve removal performance, without depending entirely on photocatalytic activity. Additionally, the findings indicate that it is crucial to distinguish clearly between adsorption, photocatalytic degradation, and membrane filtration mechanisms in order to accurately interpret membrane performance.
