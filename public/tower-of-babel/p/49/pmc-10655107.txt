Exploring Sulfur Sites in Proteins via Triple-Resonance 1H-Detected 77Se NMR
Authors: Janusz Koscielniak, Jess Li, Deepak Sail, Rolf Swenson, Clemens Anklin, Sharon Rozovsky, R Andrew Byrd
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractNMR spectroscopy has been applied to virtually all sites within proteins and biomolecules; however, the observation of sulfur sites remains very challenging. Recent studies have examined 77Se as a replacement for sulfur and applied 77Se NMR in both the solution and solid states. As a spin-1/2 nuclide, 77Se is attractive as a probe of sulfur sites, and it has a very large chemical shift range (due to a large chemical shift anisotropy), which makes it potentially very sensitive to structural and/or binding interactions as well as dynamics. Despite being a spin-1/2 nuclide, there have been rather limited studies of 77Se, and the ability to use 1H-indirect detection has been sparse. Some examples exist, but in the absence of a directly bonded, nonexchangeable 1H, these have been largely limited to smaller molecules. We develop and illustrate approaches using double-labeling of 13C and 77Se in proteins that enable more sensitive triple-resonance schemes via multistep coherence transfers and 1H-detection. These methods require specialized hardware and decoupling schemes, which we developed and will be discussed.

Introduction

The application of NMR to proteins has a robust history over the past approximately 60 years and has enabled a vast array of studies that reveal structure, dynamics, ligand binding, and functional mechanisms. Key to these studies was the introduction of isotopic labeling and, particularly, the use of uniform 13C and 15N labeling, which led to the development of multidimensional triple-resonance 1H/13C/15N experiments,1 conducted with high-sensitivity 1H-detection. These efforts have led to monitoring virtually every atomic site within proteins (e.g., 1H, 13C, 15N, and 2H), leaving only oxygen and sulfur as “invisible”. The roles of oxygen and sulfur nuclei are quite important, and oxygen, as 17O [I = 5/2], has been studied by NMR;2 however, as a quadrupolar nuclide, there are significant challenges, particularly for proteins in solution. Nevertheless, recent developments in 1H-detected, 17O solid-state NMR are very encouraging.3 Sulfur also has a quadrupolar isotope, 33S [I = 3/2], which could potentially be monitored by NMR, but this approach has equal or greater difficulties as 17O applications. Instead, we examine the use of 77Se (7.6% natural abundance) as a substitute for the natural abundance S in proteins. Early work4−6 illustrated the sensitivity of the 77Se chemical shift to the environment and even illustrated the potential of seeing signals from proteins.4,5 Recent work has described the potential utilization of 77Se direct detection NMR as a means of exploring S sites in soluble proteins and revealed the sensitivity of 77Se to environment and dynamics.7 Additionally, interest in selenoglycosides8 has led to some innovative 1H-detected NMR experiments on relatively small molecular systems. These latter experiments utilized the moderate two-bond 1H–77Se J-couplings (∼10–14 Hz) to enable coherence transfers.9 Furthermore, Rozovsky and co-workers have established protocols for biosynthetic incorporation of isotopically enriched 77Se (available at up to 99%),10 which significantly increases the sensitivity and feasibility of biomolecular applications of 77Se NMR.

The use of 1H-detection is both commonplace and critical to NMR applications to proteins. These methods rely on coherence transfer from 1H to a heteronucleus (13C or 15N), and often further transfer to and back from additional heteronuclei (usually 13C and/or 15N), followed by transfer back to 1H for detection. For the case of detecting or monitoring 77Se sites, there are no directly bonded, nonexchangeable protons to facilitate such double-or triple-resonance methodologies. Examples of 1H-detected multiple bond detection have been reported, which have been collected with an HMBC-type experiment7 and a clever extension of HSQC spectroscopy referred to as HSQCMB,9,11 both of which rely on two-bond 2J1H–77Se. Each method requires a long time (∼1/2J = 35–50 ms) for both the forward and reverse coherence transfers; hence, the applications are limited to rather small molecular systems with long T2 relaxation times. In order to develop more sensitive and general NMR methods, we considered the construction of multispin systems that could provide large one-bond (1J) couplings for coherence transfer and efficient 1H-detection.

Experimental Section

Consideration was given to both methionine and cysteine with the anticipation/expectation that the amino acids could be biosynthetically incorporated into recombinantly expressed proteins. For the case of methionine, we synthesized a dual, specifically labeled variant as shown in Figure 1A (synthesis described in the Supporting Information). This amino acid provides large 1J1H13C (125–140 Hz) and 1J13C77Se (60–70 Hz) coupling constants, which enables the design of 1H–13C–77Se triple resonance correlation NMR experiments, as shown in Figure 1B. The pulse sequence (Supporting Information) is conceptually based on the well-known HNCO pulse sequence, and coherence is transferred as follows: 1Hmethyl → 13Cmethyl → 77Se → 13Cmethyl → 1Hmethyl(detect). The sequence uses a constant-time evolution of the initial 13Cmethyl coherence to allow development of antiphase 13C–77Se coherence, under the influence of the 1J13C77Se coupling. The experiment allows for a variety of variants that can acquire (i) a two-dimensional (2D) spectrum correlating 1H and 13C, filtered through 77Se; (ii) a 2D spectrum correlating 1H and 77Se, filtered through 13C; (iii) a full three-dimensional (3D) 1H–13C–77Se correlation spectrum; and (iv) the option to insert a relaxation module to sample 77Se relaxation times (illustrated in the Supporting Information). It is significant that the values of 1J1H13C and 1J13C77Se are large and differ by approximately 2-fold. This property enables the sequence to utilize different values of constant-time (2T) during the 13C chemical shift evolution that correspond to n/(2*1J13C77Se), where n = 3 or 7. The selection of n is made depending on whether the final spectrum is designed to filter based on 13C (n = 3), where sensitivity is optimized, or to detect 13C chemical shifts (n = 7), wherein resolution can be increased by sampling through a longer constant-time period, albeit with a decrease in signal-to-noise. Additionally, it is possible to utilize nonuniform sampling (NUS)12 in any of the experiments, particularly the full 3D version.

Implementation of the H–C–Se triple resonance experiments requires a high-resolution NMR probe with three channels individually tuned to the frequency for 1H, 13C, and 77Se, including pulsed field gradients (pfg). This equipment falls outside of normal commercial probes, and we have re-engineered an existing room-temperature 1H, 13C, and 15N triple resonance pfg probe, originally manufactured by Nalorac, for use with a Bruker NMR system at a 1H frequency of 600.08 MHz. As described in the Supporting Information, we were able to retune the trap elements of the double-tuned circuitry associated with the original 13C and 15N channels to provide efficient operation at 13C (150.9 MHz) and 77Se (114.5 MHz). Double-tuning this type of probe circuit becomes more difficult as the ratio of the two frequencies becomes smaller (e.g., 1.32 for 13C/77Se versus 2.48 for 13C/15N). The resulting probe was optimized for 77Se pulse performance (90° pulse length = 19 μs at 220 W RF power), while maintaining adequate 13C performance (90° pulse length = 34 μs at 280 W RF power), recognizing that the 13C spectral width would be limited to a modest range (10–30 ppm) based on the labeling scheme. Additionally, it is necessary to provide simultaneous decoupling of 13C and 77Se during the acquisition of 1H signals. This was accomplished using COMB decoupling,13 which provided efficient decoupling at low power in the double-tuned circuit [see the Supporting Information: 13C with 3.2 W (1760 μs BIP 180deg pulse14) and 77Se with 1.99 W (400 μs 180° rectangular pulse)]. These developments yielded a high-sensitivity room-temperature probe capable of H–C–Se triple resonance experiments, complete with sample temperature control and pfg.

Results and Discussion

Performance and sensitivity enhancements for H–C–Se triple resonance detection of 77Se sites are provided by examination of one-dimensional (1D) direct observe 77Se (with 1H decoupling) compared to 2D 1H–13C HMQC, 2D 1H–77Se HMBC, 2D 1H–13C HCSe (77Se-filtered), and 2D 1H–77Se HCSe (13C-filtered) spectra. It is important to recognize that the 1D 77Se direct detection spectra were measured with a liquid nitrogen temperature cryoprobe at 500 MHz for 1H, and the 2D HC and HCSe spectra were measured with the room-temperature 600 MHz probe described in this report. In Figure 2, spectra for the same sample of 13CH3–77Se–methionine (13CH3–77Se–Met, see the Supporting Information for synthesis) are compared, and the computed signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) is provided in Table 1. The clear, dramatic gain in sensitivity when using 1H-indirect detection, as has been well-known in biomolecular NMR spectroscopy, is shown for 13CH3–77Se–Met. Detection of the methyl resonance by 1H–13C HMQC represents an approximately 67-fold increase in S/N compared to the 1D 77Se detection, which benefits from both 1H-detection and the presence of three protons for each 13C atom. The H–C–Se experiments provide an apparent 40-fold improvement in S/N for the HCSe experiment compared to that for the 1D 77Se detection. Recognizing that the 77Se direct detection benefited from advanced cryoprobe technology, providing a 2–2.5-fold improvement, the actual gain of the HCSe experiment over direct detection is roughly 80–100-fold for equivalent probe technology. The gain in S/N indicates a potential reduction in measurement time of ∼2000-fold to achieve the equivalent S/N as direct detection, assuming equivalent line widths. Of course, the gain in sensitivity is more readily applied to observing systems at lower concentrations and to larger molecules, e.g., proteins. The need for this gain is illustrated in previous direct detection 77Se applied to proteins.5,7

The 13CH3–77Se–Met (Figure 1A) was used to prepare two proteins [CUE (6 kDa) and trPH (13 kDa), see the Supporting Information], in which the methionine residues were substituted to >95% with the double-labeled 13CH3–77Se–Met (Supporting Information). Each protein has two Met residues and reveals two resonances, where the 13C chemical shifts are nearly degenerate, and the 77Se chemical shifts are separated by 5–10 ppm. The spectra shown in Figure 3 illustrate the resolution and sensitivity of these proteins at a concentration of 300 μM. For each protein (Figure 3A–C,D–F), the total acquisition time for the individual 2D experiments was the same: CUE spectra were each acquired in 1 h 50 min, and trPH spectra were acquired in 2 h 45 min total. The 1H–13C H–C–Se experiments have approximately 2-fold higher sensitivity compared to the 1H–77Se H–C–Se experiments; however, the excellent resolution afforded by the 1H–77Se H–C–Se experiment illustrates both the power and the potential of the triple resonance 77Se-resolved experiments. The sensitivity afforded by the 1H-indirect detection triple resonance experiments and an optimized probe compares to directly detected 77Se spectra on proteins at 5–10× higher concentrations (∼1.5–3 mM) and reduced or comparable acquisition times.7

It is well established that the chemical shift of 77Se is very sensitive to the local environment, temperature, and dynamics.7,9 Having established the high sensitivity and resolution afforded by H–C–Se experiments, investigations of protein/protein interactions and ligand binding were pursued with both the CUE and trPH proteins. CUE is known to bind the small protein ubiquitin with a 12–15 μM affinity.15 Substitution of 13CH3–77Se–Met with Met does not alter the binding affinity. In a titration of CUE with ubiquitin (Figure 4A), the binding on the NMR time scale shifts from fast exchange (measured previously via 1H–15N spectroscopy)15 to slow, based on a chemical shift of approximately 19 ppm at the 77Se frequency. The shifted resonance corresponds to residue M467, which is in the interface of CUE/Ub.15 The titration suggests that the exchange has shifted toward intermediate to fast exchange on the NMR time scale, which is appropriate given the Kd and the observed CSP. A second example is provided in a titration of ASAP1 trPH with the lipid analogue diC4PIP2 (Figure 4B). Monitoring the binding of diC4PIP2 to trPH by 31P NMR shows that the 5-phosphate exhibits the larger CSP.16 For the two Met residues (M436 and M338), M338 is at the flexible N-terminus and shows no effects of titration, and M436 is very distant (∼27 Å) from the binding site16 yet shows clear 77Se chemical shift perturbations in the HCSe spectra. As reported previously,17 M436 is in an area of proposed conformational shifts when PH binds to PIP2 in a membrane, and the extreme sensitivity of 77Se chemical shifts can reveal this remote structural perturbation. Together, these examples demonstrate the sensitivity and utility of 77Se reporters in protein NMR, when enabled by the triple resonance methods reported herein. The exquisite sensitivity to the environment, facilitated by the large chemical shift range for 77Se, suggests numerous powerful applications. For example, in addition to the remote sensing seen in the ASAP1 trPH, extension of the HCSe experiments to include CEST would take advantage of the shifted NMR time scale (generally bringing a faster exchange process into the realm of detection by CEST)18 and provide new approaches for exploring low-population states of proteins and complexes. Another intriguing property that may be explored is the occurrence of chalcogen bonds in seleno-methionine.19 These interactions are estimated to occur at approximately a 6–7% frequency, and it would be anticipated that the 77Se shift and line width should be sensitive reporters. It appears that none of the proteins examined in this study exhibit chalcogen bonds to the selenium atom, as the 77Se chemical shifts and line widths suggest only subtle changes in the environment and not the large shift or broad lines that would be expected for the reduced mobility and direct interaction to the 77Se atom that defines a chalcogen bond.

Assignment of the seleno-methionine resonances may be accomplished by three approaches: (1) mutation of a residue to another residue type, resulting in loss and identification of the resonance; (2) assignment of the methyl 13C and 1H resonances by NOESY —since the use of HCSe methods is most likely to be used following a structure determination of the protein that would facilitate assignment of NOESY contacts—and then direct correlation to the 77Se resonances via the three-dimensional HCSe experiment; and (3) for the case of a small number of seleno-methionine residues within the known structure, the mobility, dynamics, or a ligand interaction may be sufficient for assignment.

Additionally, the measurement of dynamics and relaxation times are important properties that should be accessible by 77Se NMR, as Rozovsky and co-workers7 have demonstrated. It is valuable to explore the measurement of 77Se relaxation times via 1H-indirect detection of triple resonance in a manner similar to previous studies of 15N relaxation.20 The pulse sequence shown in Figure 1C illustrates an approach to measuring 77Se T1 via the establishment of in-phase 77Se magnetization, followed by a relaxation period, and then conversion back into antiphase magnetization and return to 1H via stepwise transfer to 13C and 1H. An example of monitoring 77Se T1 relaxation via this scheme is shown in Figure 5. The data indicate that measurement of 77Se relaxation times becomes accessible via the 1H-indirect detection scheme, and it would be readily applicable to multiple sites in a protein via the acquired 2D spectra acquired.

Lastly, we have also explored the replacement of natural sulfur-containing cysteine in proteins with 77Se–β-13CH2-labeled cysteine, which creates a similar 1H–13C–77Se three-spin system as shown above for 13CH3–77Se–methionine. Chemical synthesis provided the dimer 77Se–β-13CH2–cystine (see the Supporting Information). Two-dimensional triple resonance HCSe spectra of this compound are shown in Figure S4, which validate the creation of the three-spin system and the effectiveness of selectable evolution of either 13C or 77Se chemical shift (Figure 1A). Direct detection 77Se 1D spectra illustrate the potential value of such species, as the chemical shift difference between the cysteine and cystine species is approximately 350 ppm (Figure S5). We have been unable to obtain biosynthetic incorporation of 77Se–β-13CH2-labeled cysteine into proteins due to the difficulties of generating free, reduced 77Se–β-13CH2-labeled cysteine in vivo and its increased reactivity to redimerize compared to the sulfo-species in bacterial cell cultures. The incorporation of such isotopically labeled species requires further development.

In summary, we have demonstrated that, through creation of a directly bonded 1H–13C–77Se spin system exhibiting large one-bond scalar J-couplings, triple resonance NMR methodology may be applied to seleno-methionine and seleno-cysteine residues to enable dramatically improved sensitivity for monitoring sulfur sites in proteins. The improved sensitivity (on the order of 500×) suggests that a wide range of applications can be envisioned. We have demonstrated both facile ligand/protein and protein/protein binding interactions and the ability to measure 77Se relaxation times. The HCSe experiments have worked well for proteins up to ∼20 kDa in molecular weight. The large chemical shift anisotropy (CSA) provides sensitivity to the environment, but it may also limit applications due to CSA relaxation and line broadening. It is not yet clear what the size limit may be, as it will depend on local side chain mobility, temperature, and concentration. The current work establishes excellent sensitivity at 300 micromolar concentrations; however, as line widths increase, compensation may be made by increasing concentration and temperature. Additionally, if a suitable probe were available, the CSA relaxation could be reduced by acquisition at a lower magnetic field. It is anticipated that further development of the HCSe experiments will ensue, similar to what was realized following the early development of the 1H–13C–15N triple resonance spectroscopy methods.
