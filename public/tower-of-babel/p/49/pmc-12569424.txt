Analysis of Gut Microbiota and Their Metabolites in CADASIL Patients
Authors: Akiko Watanabe-Hosomi, Ryo Inoue, Ikuko Mizuta, Jun Matsuura, Hiraku Matsuura, Daiki Fukunaga, Tomoyuki Ohara, Yuji Naito, Toshiki Mizuno
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and PurposeCerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) is a major hereditary small-vessel disease caused by mutations in NOTCH3. We hypothesized that the gut microbiota may serve as an environmental factor influencing disease progression or phenotype diversity among patients. Our previous study identified taxonomic differences between CADASIL patients and controls. In this study, we aimed to replicate our initial findings and further analyze gut microbial metabolites.MethodsWe performed 16S rRNA sequencing of gut microbiota in the 24 patients and 17 controls. Of these, 15 patients and 13 controls underwent analyses of fecal metabolites.ResultsAmong the 24 CADASIL patients and 17 controls recruited, 16S rRNA sequencing of fecal samples revealed a significant difference in b-diversity (p=0.015), while no significant difference was observed in a-diversity. Taxonomic analysis identified significant differences in specific bacterial genera, particularly Bacteroides dorei. These findings confirm our previous results, demonstrating that gut microbiota composition differs between CADASIL patients and controls. Additionally, metabolite analysis of fecal samples from 15 CADASIL patients and 13 controls showed a significant elevation of the indole concentration in the patients. Moreover, indole levels were positively correlated with the abundance of Streptococcus mutans and Streptococcus parasanguinis.ConclusionsBoth indole and Streptococcus species have been reported to be associated with atherosclerosis. Our results suggest that alterations in gut microbiota and their metabolites may be associated with the pathophysiology of CADASIL by promoting atherosclerosis.

INTRODUCTION

Cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) is a major hereditary small-vessel disease caused by mutations in NOTCH3.12 It is well-known that CADASIL patients exhibit variability in symptom progression even with the same genotype; age at onset of the first stroke ranges from 20–70 years old, and some patients remain asymptomatic even after 70 years old.3 We hypothesized that the gut microbiota may be one of the environmental factors influencing disease phenotype variability. Recently, it was shown that the gut microbiota is closely related to many neurological disorders, especially neurodegenerative diseases,4 stroke,5 and neuroimmune diseases.6 In the presence of stroke, it has been reported that the microbiota is closely associated with activity of the immune system and the subsequent modulation of neuroinflammation and stroke outcomes.5 Moreover, a causal relationship between multiple sclerosis and gut microbiota has been well-established and an association with fecal metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids, has also been suggested.6 To our knowledge, there are very limited reports on the role of gut microbiota in CADASIL patients. We previously reported analysis of gut microbiota in such patients.7 In taxonomic microbial analysis, we found that several genera of bacteria had either increased or decreased significantly. Liu et al.8 also showed several differences in microbiota between CADASIL patients and controls, and discussed their association with inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)-1b and IL-6. Both reports described differences in microbiota between patients and controls; however, the bacteria identified in these studies were not the same. Additional studies are necessary to clarify the contribution of gut microbiota to CADASIL. In the present study, to advance our previous findings, we replicated taxonomic analysis of gut microbiota and analyzed fecal metabolites in CADASIL patients and controls.

METHODS

Participants

We recruited 24 Japanese CADASIL patients who visited our outpatient clinic at University Hospital, Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine. Seventeen of their family members were recruited as controls. We performed 16S rRNA sequencing of gut microbiota in the 24 patients and 17 controls. Of these, 15 patients and 13 controls underwent analyses of fecal metabolites. We collected completed questionnaires and fecal samples from November 2020 to December 2021. The Ethics Committee of Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine approval the research protocol (permission No. EBR-C-725-1), and all participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Nutrition survey

A nutrition survey was performed using the Brief-type Self-administered Diet History Questionnaire (BDHQ)9 to evaluate food and nutrient uptakes. Answers to BDHQ were available from 23 patients and 13 controls.

Meta 16S analysis of gut microbiota

Meta 16S analysis of gut microbiota was performed according to Takagi et al.10 In brief, fecal sampling was performed using a sample kit (Feces Collection kit, Techno Suruga Lab). Meta 16S analysis of fecal DNA was performed at the Biochemical Center, Takara Bio, and PreMedica. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for DNA extracted from fecal samples was comprised of two steps. In the first step, PCR was performed to amplify with primers of 341F (5’-TCGTCGGC AGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGCCTACGGGN GGCWGCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GTCTCGTGGGCTC GGAGATGTGTATA AGAGACAGGGACTACHVGGGT WTCTAAT-3’), corresponding to the V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene. In the second PCR step, index sequences for Illumina sequencer were added. Then, the libraries were subjected to the sequencing of paired-end 300 bases on MiSeq (Illumina). Processing of sequence data, amplicon sequence variant (ASV) definition, and taxonomy assignment were conducted using QIIME2 (ver. 2023.2; https://qiime2.org/). Singletons were removed in the present study. Taxonomy assignment of the resulting ASV was carried out using the sklearn classifier with SLIVA 138 database (https://docs.qiime2.org/2023.2/data-resources/). Microbiota diversity within a sample (a-diversity) was evaluated as the Chao1 index (ASV richness estimation) and Shannon index (ASV evenness estimation) calculated by QIIME2. Differences in diversity between samples (b-diversity) were based on the UniFrac metric by quantitative (weighted) or qualitative (unweighted) analysis, and the distances between samples were visualized by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) plotting, using the R “phyloseq” package (https://joey711.github.io/phyloseq/).

Statistical analysis

Data were compared in two ways: between CADASIL patients and controls, and between CADASIL patients with and without a history of stroke. Background, food/nutrition intake profiles, the relative abundance of bacterial genera, a-diversity, and fecal metabolites were compared with Fisher’s exact test, the Wilcoxon rank sum test, or Welch’s t-test using JMP14.0.0 (https://www.jmp.com/ja/home). Clustering on the PCoA plot (b-diversity) was statistically analyzed using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). Values of p<0.05 were considered significant. Differences in relative abundance of each species of bacteria in microbiota between the groups were analyzed by linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe), a method for biomarker discovery.11 A heatmap of Spearman’s correlation coefficients between metabolites and bacterial species was created using ggplot (https://ggplot2.tidyverse.org/).

RESULTS

Background and nutrition survey

There was no significant difference in age, sex, or vascular risk factors between CADASIL patients (n=24) and controls (n=17) (Table 1).

Based on answers to BDHQ, there were no significant differences in food/nutrient intakes between the patients and controls, except for a lower intake of fatty fish (p=0.0472) and higher intake of pickled leafy vegetables (p=0.0200) in patients (Supplementary Table 1 in the online-only Data Supplement). When comparing CADASIL patients with and without a history of symptomatic stroke, intakes of some nutrients and foods, including coffee, raw fish, vitamin B12, and fatty acids, were significantly higher in those with a stroke history (Supplementary Table 2 in the online-only Data Supplement).

Analysis of gut microbiota diversity

No significant difference in a-diversity was observed between the patients and controls (Fig. 1). Regarding b-diversity, PCoA plots showed a significant difference in clusters between patients and controls only in UniFrac distance (weighted) (Fig. 2). On comparing CADASIL patients with and without a history of stroke, no significant differences were observed in either a- or b-diversity (data not shown).

Taxonomic microbial analysis

LEfSe analysis between CADASIL patients and controls identified bacterial species which were relatively more abundant in the former or latter (Fig. 3A). A cladogram of the identified species showed that at the species level, Bacteroides stercoris and Bacteroides dorei were more prevalent in CADASIL patients (Fig. 3B, red circles), whereas Bacteroides caccae was more prevalent in controls. At the genus level, Butyricicoccus and Collinsella were more prevalent in controls (Fig. 3B, blue circles).

When comparing CADASIL patients with or without a history of stroke, at the genus level, a higher prevalence of Phascolarctobacterium was observed in the group with a stroke history, and Dialister and Akkermansia were more prevalent in controls (Fig. 4). At the species level, Bacteroides eggerthii was more frequently observed in CADASIL patients with a stroke history (Fig. 4).

Metabolite analysis

Microbial metabolites in fecal samples were compared between 15 CADASIL patients and 13 controls (Table 2). Of the nine short-chain fatty acids quantified, lactic acid levels showed significant differences between patients and controls; however, they were only detectable in samples from 5 patients and undetectable in one control sample. Formic acid was not detectable in any samples. Regarding the other metabolites and pH, a significant difference was noted in indole alone, being higher in patients than controls (means: 305.40 and 149.76, respectively, p=0.0271). No significant differences in fecal metabolites were observed between CADASIL patients with and without a history of stroke (data not shown).

Heatmap of correlation coefficients between metabolites and bacterial species

A heatmap showing correlation coefficients between fecal metabolites and gut microbiota across combined samples (15 CADASIL patients and 13 controls) is presented in Fig. 5. From the above findings (Table 2), we focused on indole. Streptococcus mutans and Streptococcus parasanguinis were significantly and positively correlated with levels of indole (Fig. 5).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we replicated our previous results whereby the composition of gut microbiota differed between CADASIL patients and controls, although the bacteria identified were not the same. In LEfSe analysis, the cladogram of taxa identified showed that Bacteroides dorei was significantly increased in CADASIL patients. The cause and significance of this elevation in CADASIL patients remain unknown; however, it is noteworthy that Bacteroides dorei was reported to have effects on vascular diseases, including an inhibitory effect on atherosclerosis12 and being a causative pathogen in infected aortic aneurysms.13 Dai et al.14 reported that Bacteroides dorei increases apolipoprotein B levels, which are associated with heart failure. We thus consider that Bacteroides dorei exhibits pathogenic traits. We demonstrated a difference in Bacteroides dorei, but further investigation with a larger number of cases is necessary to examine the potential involvement of Bacteroides dorei in the pathogenesis of CADASIL. Conversely, while the association between Bacteroides stercoris and stroke or neurodegenerative diseases remains unclear, there has been a report suggesting an association with retinal artery occlusion.15 When comparing the microbiota between CADASIL patients with and without a history of symptomatic stroke, higher levels of Phascolarctobacterium and Bacteroides eggerthii were observed in CADASIL patients with such a history. This conflicts with our previous finding of higher levels of Phascolarctobacterium in patients without a stroke history. Phascolarctobacterium has been reported to be associated with psychiatric disorders, but no association with stroke has been identified.16 Bacteroides eggerthii has been reported to be more abundant in patients with carotid atherosclerosis compared with healthy controls.17 However, in the cladogram, the imbalance of the microbiota between patients with and without a history of stroke was less clearly observed. We also examined metabolic products of the gut microbiota, and found that indole was significantly elevated in the CADASIL group. Indole and its derivatives, as products of the gut microbiota metabolism of tryptophan, are associated with various diseases.18 Indoxyl sulfate, a metabolite of indole, causes vascular inflammation and promotes atherosclerosis.19 Nemet et al.20 conducted a 3-year longitudinal cohort involving 4,833 participants, and demonstrated that elevated levels of indoxyl sulfate and other compounds in gut microbiota-generated metabolites derived from aromatic amino acids in plasma are associated with an increased hazard ratio for cardiovascular events. We consider that tryptophan-derived metabolites including indole contribute to arteriosclerosis and small vessel disease independently of NOTCH3 pathology. We also found that Streptococcus mutans and Streptococcus parasanguinis were significantly and positively correlated with the concentration of indole. It is of note that both Streptococcus mutans and Streptococcus parasanguinis are oral bacteria. In recent years, there has been increasing attention on the detection of oral bacteria in feces.21 The presence of oral bacteria in feces has been associated with inflammation and intestinal diseases.22 Furthermore, Streptococcus mutans23 and Streptococcus parasanguinis24 are associated with atherosclerosis. In CADASIL, along with mutations in NOTCH3, vascular risk factors significantly contribute to the occurrence of stroke.25 Taken together, we suggest that vascular inflammation caused by indole and also these oral bacteria might contribute to the progression of cerebral small-vessel disease in CADASIL patients. In the taxonomic microbial analysis, some differences were observed between CADASIL groups with and without a history of stroke, but we did not find a significant difference in gut metabolites between patients with and without such a history, suggesting that gut microbiota may not modify the onset of stroke in CADASIL patients. Because it was difficult to reproduce the gut microbiota results from our previous work,7 we considered the associated metabolic pathways to be more important. There were several limitations of this study. Firstly, the sample size was small, mainly due to the low prevalence of the disease. Further validation in longitudinal studies with larger cohorts and analyses stratified by genotype will be necessary. Additionally, comparisons with general control groups beyond family members are important. Recently, it was reported that the location of NOTCH3 mutations in CADASIL is an important determinant of disease severity.26 We conducted additional investigations, excluding two cases with cysteine-sparing mutation, p.Arg75Pro: 22 patients were investigated by dividing them into those with NOTCH3 mutation in EGF repeats 1 to 6 (16 cases) and 7–34 (6 cases). No significant differences were observed in fecal metabolites such as indole (data not shown). However, we noted differences in the gut microbiota on taxonomic analysis (Supplementary Table 3 in the online-only Data Supplement). Therefore, future studies that consider the location of NOTCH3 mutations in a larger number of patients will be important. Secondly, there was no consideration of medication. In this study, to prevent adverse effects of antithrombotic medication, five patients received proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), and one patient took an H2 blocker. We identified Streptococcus mutans and Streptococcus parasanguinis as candidates of CADASIL-associated bacteria. However, it is known that the use of PPIs can alter the composition of the gut microbiota,10 but the abundance of these bacteria was not significantly different between patients medicated with and without PPIs (data not shown). Thus, we consider that the present study’s findings may not have been influenced by PPI medication.

In conclusion, we demonstrated differences in the gut microbiota between CADASIL patients and a control group. It was considered that the investigation of environmental factors, including gut microbiota, is essential to more comprehensively understand the pathology of CADASIL.
