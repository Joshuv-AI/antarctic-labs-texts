Omadacycline for the treatment of acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Wen-qiang Kong, Yun-feng Shu, Juan Tang, Shi-qin Li, Hong Zhong, Xuan-yi Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFew studies have compared the efficacy and safety of omadacycline with other treatments for acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections (ABSSSI). Therefore, updated meta-analyses on this topic is necessary.MethodsWe conducted a network meta-analysis (NMA) of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to compare omadacycline with other anti-MRSA antibiotics. A systematic search of seven databases was undertaken to identify eligible RCTs enrolling adults with ABSSSI. Two reviewers independently assessed the quality of the included studies and three reviewers independently extracted data from all manuscripts. All meta-analyses were performed using R software.ResultsOur NMA included 39 RCTs with a total of 20,862 patients with ABSSSI. Eighteen anti-MRSA antibiotics were analyzed in the NMA. In terms of early clinical response, omadacycline was comparable to oxazolidinones like linezolid, tedizolid, and contezolid. Across various populations, including the intention-to-treat (ITT), modified ITT, and clinically evaluable populations, omadacycline showed higher or comparable clinical success rates, although these results were of borderline statistical significance. The results showed that omadacycline ranked first in efficacy among 18 antibiotics, based on surface under the cumulative ranking curve values. Additionally, omadacycline had significantly fewer serious adverse events than telavancin and demonstrated a safety profile comparable to other antibiotics.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that omadacycline is a promising treatment option for ABSSSI, demonstrating efficacy that is comparable to or potentially superior to many other anti-MRSA agents, along with a favorable safety profile. However, these results should be interpreted with caution due to the study’s inherent limitations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11892-5.Keywords: Acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections, Omadacycline, Network meta-analysis, Efficacy, Safety

Introduction

Acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections (ABSSSIs) include cellulitis, erysipelas, wound infections, and major cutaneous abscesses with lesion of at least 75 cm2 [1]. ABSSSIs are among the most common bacterial infections in patients presenting to the emergency department, placing a heavy economic burden on both the healthcare system and society [2, 3]. ABSSSIs represent a more severe subset of skin and soft tissue infections, typically requiring hospitalization [4]. Staphylococcus aureus, particularly methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), is a major cause of ABSSSIs, especially in patients with co-morbidities such as diabetes [5, 6]. Epidemiological studies show that the prevalence of MRSA in China has declined over a 17-year period, but remains above 28% [7]. MRSA remains endemic in some regions globally, including the United States [8]. Patients with MRSA infections facing a higher risk of hospitalization or death than those with non-MRSA infections [5]. Vancomycin has long been the standard treatment for ABSSSIs when MRSA infection is suspected or confirmed [9, 10]. However, the overuse, suboptimal concentrations, or inappropriate use of vancomycin has led to the emergence of MRSA infections that have reduced susceptibility to vancomycin [11]. In recent years, the prevalence of heterogeneous vancomycin-intermediate S. aureus (hVISA) has been gradually increasing, albeit slowly [11]. The prevalence of hVISA increased from 4% before 2010 to 5.3% between 2010 and 2019 [11]. hVISA or MRSA strains with high vancomycin minimum inhibitory concentrations are likely associated with poorer patient outcomes including increased mortality [12–14]. Recently, MRSA strains resistant to linezolid or are non-susceptible to daptomycin have been isolated [15, 16]. Although various anti-microbial agents can treat MRSA infections, challenges like safety, adherence concerns, and drug costs underscore the need for new antibiotics to treat ABSSSIs [17].

Omadacycline is a novel semi-synthetic aminomethylcycline with broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against a diverse range of gram-positive, gram-negative, atypical, and anaerobic pathogens. It was approved by the Food and Drug Administration in 2018 for treating community-acquired bacterial pneumonia (CABP) and ABSSSIs in adults [18–20]. Omadacycline is available in both oral and intravenous (IV) formulations. Pharmacokinetic studies have demonstrated several advantages of omadacycline for the treatment of ABSSSIs including (i) no need for dosage adjustment in patients with renal dysfunction or hepatic impairment, (ii) once-daily dosing, and (iii) low risk of drug-drug interactions and high systemic exposure in the plasma, epithelial lining fluid, and alveolar cells [18, 21]. Furthermore, phase III trials have shown that omadacycline is non-inferior and has an acceptable safety profile compared to linezolid and moxifloxacin in treating CABP and ABSSSIs [18, 22]. Additionally, IV-to-oral and oral-only administration of omadacycline has been shown to be cost-effective, and to improve quality of life compared to other treatments (e.g., vancomycin) by reducing hospital stays and avoiding hospitalization for the treatment of ABSSSIs [23, 24].

A well-conducted NMA, which simultaneously compares multiple interventions by statistically combining both direct and indirect evidence, serves as a highly practical tool for decision-making. NMA are particularly valuable for assessing the comparative efficacy and safety of novel drugs, especially when head-to-head trial data are limited [25]. Thus, we conducted a network meta-analysis (NMA) to compare the effectiveness and safety of omadacycline with other antibiotics and to explore potentially optimal therapies for treating ABSSSIs.

Materia and methods

This NMA was carried out according to the specifications of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) extension statement for the reporting of systematic reviews incorporating NMAs of healthcare interventions [26] (Table S1).

Search strategy and study selection

Five electronic databases including PubMed, EMBASE, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Web of Science and Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), were systematically searched from inception to March 2024 to identify eligible randomized controlled trials (RCTs). ClinicalTrials.gov (via https://www.clinicaltrials.gov/) and International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (via https://trialsearch.who.int/) were also searched to identify completed but unpublished RCTs. The references list of relevant meta-analyses, reviews, pooled analyses, and included trials were manually checked to identify additional studies. Combinations of MeSH terms and text words, which included “soft tissue infections”, “named comparators”, and “randomized controlled trials” were used (Table S2).

Two reviewers (WQK, YFS) independently selected the studies in accordance with pre-specified inclusion criteria, and any disagreements were settled via discussion. After removing duplicate studies, the reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining records, read the full text of the remaining reports and identified eligible studies.

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility criteria for studies included in this meta-analysis were as follows: (1) RCTs of any size and duration; (2) included adults (≥ 18 years) with ABSSSIs or complicated skin and soft-structure infections (cSSSIs); (3) evaluated omadacycline or MRSA-active antibiotics; (4) reported at least one efficacy-related outcome (e.g., early clinical response [ECR]); (5) constituted patients with ABSSSIs as a subgroup of the study population, and provided separate data for the group of patients with ABSSSIs; and (6) were published in English language or Chinese language.

The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) RCTs including patients with chronic infections (e.g., decubitus ulcer infections) or compromised immune function (e.g., cancer chemotherapy); (2) pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic studies; (3) non-RCTs, conference abstracts, and unpublished studies; (4) RCTs evaluating the same antibiotics at different doses; (5) studies evaluating topical anti-bacterial agents; and (6) infection diagnostic criteria were not available. When duplicate studies were identified, only those that presented the most comprehensive and informative data were included.

Data extraction

Three reviewers (WQK, HZ, SQL) independently extracted data from studies that met the eligibility criteria, and managed the data using Microsoft Excel spreadsheets. One reviewer (WQK) then performed a thorough additional check to ensure that the data were accurate. The data included: (1) study characteristics (first author, ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier [NCT], publication year); (2) patient characteristics (age, body mass index [BMI], sex ratio, infection type); (3) interventions (drugs, dose); (4) concomitant antibiotics; and (5) outcomes of interest. The primary outcome was ECR in line with the draft guidance issued by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2013 or 2010 [1, 27]. The secondary outcomes were (1) investigator-assessed clinical success at the test-of-cure (TOC) or similar end point, as defined by the European Medicines Agency [28], (2) investigator-assessed clinical success in the population with MRSA isolated at baseline, (3) investigator-assessed clinical success in clinically evaluable (CE) population, (4) all-cause mortality, and (5) discontinuation due to adverse events (AEs) and serious adverse events (SAEs). Since this study focused on the clinical efficacy of omadacycline, bacteriological efficacy was not selected. Due to the lack of a standard definition of SAE, we used the criteria defined in each study. The intention-to-treat (ITT) population or modified intention-to-treat population (mITT) were extracted for ECR or clinical success at TOC. The safety outcomes were evaluated in the safety populations. Full details of definition for outcomes or population were presented in Table S3. Discrepancies were resolved via discussion.

Risk of bias assessment

The quality of the included trials was assessed by two reviewers (WQK, YFS) in accordance with the Cochrane Collaboration’s risk of bias tool, as described in the Cochrane Collaboration Handbook [29]. Subsequently, the quality of the studies was separated into three levels from low risk to high risk based on the method described in Chap. 8 of the Cochrane Collaboration Handbook [30]. Discrepancies were resolved via discussion.

Network meta-analysis

First, we performed pairwise meta-analyses. Then, a random-effects model was used to derive pooled estimates across studies to account for between-study differences. Between-study heterogeneity was quantitatively assessed using the I2 statistic, with I2 >50% indicating high heterogeneity and I2 < 50% indicating low heterogeneity [31]. NMAs were then performed using STATA software. A frequentist framework was applied to combine evidence from direct and indirect comparisons with a random effects model [32]. In the networks of drug comparisons, each drug is drawn by a node and randomized comparisons between drugs are shown by links between the nodes. Since the validity of the NMA relies on the fulfilment of fundamental assumptions of transitivity, global and potential local inconsistencies were explored [32, 33]. The outcomes were dichotomous data, and the results are presented as pooled risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) was calculated to rank the treatments for each outcome [34]. Comparison-adjusted funnel plots were generated to assess the presence of study-small effects in the intervention network [35]. The robustness of our findings for all outcomes were validated by performing sensitivity NMAs based on the following conditions: (1) restricted to studies adopting the definition of ECR issued by the FDA in 2013 [1], (2) exclusion of high risk of bias studies, and (3) restricted to studies following the FDA draft guidance.

Network meta-analysis results

Selected studies and study characteristics

Our electronic searches yielded 1,530 citations, leading to a full-text of 52 publications (Fig. 1). Of these, 15 were excluded based on the inclusion criteria (Table S4). The final analysis included 37 studies that met the inclusion criteria, plus two unpublished trials from ClinicalTrials.gov [36, 37]. This resulted in a total of 39 English-language RCTs published between 2002 and 2022 being included in the NMA. Three RCTs compared omadacycline with linezolid in patients with ABSSSIs were eligible. Basic information from each of the 39 RCTs is shown in Table S5. Four trials included multiple diseases, from which ABSSSIs-specific data were extracted. In total, 18 anti-MRSA antibiotics were investigated. Among the 20,862 patients analyzed, 62.3% (12,997) were male. The mean age ranged from 37 to 68 years, and the mean BMI ranged from 25 to 33. Aztreonam and metronidazole were used for polymicrobial infections. All RCTs reported financial support from the pharmaceutical industry. Due to the network’s star-like configuration, inconsistency for ECR couldn’t be statistically tested (Fig S1). Other evidence networks with one or two closed loops were tested for loop inconsistency (Fig S2-S7). Networks with closed loops were tested, revealing inconsistencies in the all-cause mortality network (Table S6). No global inconsistencies were found (all P values >0.05).

Risk of bias within studies

Detailed random sequence generation was not reported in 15 of the included studies, and 19 studies had inadequate allocation concealment. Four trials had an open-label design, 16 studies did not describe blinding of participants and personnel, and 19 studies were rated as having unclear risk of outcome assessment blinding. Five trials were rated as having high risk of bias due to incomplete outcome data, and six studies had a high risk of bias due to selective reporting. The majority of studies were deemed to have a low risk of other biases. Overall, four trials were rated as low risk, while 17 studies were rated as high risk. The results of study quality assessment are shown in Table S7.

Early clinical response

In the network, omadacycline was not directly compared with vancomycin, dalbavancin, ceftobiprole, iciprim, delafloxacin, oritavancin or TD-1792. Omadacycline was comparable to its comparators in terms of ECR in both the network and pairwise meta-analyses (Table 1 and Table S8). Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings (Table S9).

Clinical success in different populations

The NMA revealed that in the ITT/mITT, CE, and MRSA populations, omadacycline was more effective than a range of comparators, including linezolid, tedizolid, telavancin, delafloxacin, oritavancin, ceftobiprole, ceftaroline, contezolid, vancomycin, tigecycline, iclaprim, fusidic acid and GSK 1,322,322, though the differences were of borderline statistical significances (Table 1). Pairwise meta-analyses revealed borderline significant trends towards higher clinical success rates when omadacycline was compared with linezolid in mITT/ITT and CE populations (Table S8). Sensitivity analyses showed that the clinical success of omadacycline was generally comparable to the other drugs in mITT/ITT and MRSA populations (Table S9). In sensitivity analyses that excluded high-risk bias studies and restricted the analysis to studies following FDA guidelines, omadacycline continued to show higher clinical success rates in the CE population compared to linezolid (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.95–1.00), tedizolid (RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.92–0.98), and ceftaroline (RR 0.91, 95% CI 0.83–0.99) (Table S9).

Safety

The NMA found no significant differences in drug discontinuation due to AEs between omadacycline and other antibiotics (Table 1). Sensitivity meta-analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings (Table S9). Regarding SAEs, telavancin was associated with a significantly higher incidence compared to omadacycline (RR 4.52, 95%CI 1.27–16.14) (Table 1). Sensitivity analyses did not reveal any significant differences between omadacycline and other antibiotics in this regard (Table S9). Additionally, the incidence of all-cause mortality was similar between omadacycline and other drugs, with sensitivity analyses generally supporting these results (Table S9).

Surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA)analysis

The SUCRA curve analyses that omadacycline had the highest probability of being the best regimen among all assessed regimens. It ranked ninth for drug discontinuation due to AEs, seventh for SAEs, and third for all-cause mortality (Figs 2 and 3).

Publication bias

Comparison-adjusted plots across all included studies were generally symmetrical, indicating no significant publication bias for any outcomes (Fig S8-S14).

Discussion

The study provides substantial indirect evidence on the comparative effectiveness and safety of omadacycline versus several other treatments for ABSSSI. This network meta-analysis indicates that omadacycline is both effective and safe option for treating ABSSSI, with efficacy comparable to, and in some cases exceeding, that of other anti-MRSA agents. The results are particularly compelling in the mITT/ITT, CE, and MRSA populations, where omadacycline outperformed the majority of other anti-MRSA agents. Subgroup population analyses suggest that omadacycline may offer enhanced benefits in the CE population, where its efficacy was notably higher than that of linezolid, tedizolid, and ceftaroline. This indicates potential advantages in patients who align more closely with those typically encountered by clinicians, compared to the broader mITT or ITT populations. Sensitivity analyses further reinforced the robustness of these findings, showing consistent results even after adjusting for study quality and trial guidelines. Our findings align with four other NMAs that explored the efficacy and safety of antimicrobial agents for treating ABSSSIs, including omadacycline [38–41]. Although there were differences in the study aims and the studies included, these NMAs also found that omadacycline was associated with higher clinical success rates compared to other anti-MRSA agents or was comparable to delafloxacin in ECR response.

In 2013, the FDA issued draft guidance for the conduct of clinical trials for ABSSSIs, adopting the ECR as the primary endpoint [1, 42]. ECR is more objective, quantifiable, and reproducible than traditional efficacy assessments [42], and subsequent studies have validated it as a reliable indicator of late treatment success [42, 43]. Consistent with these recommendations, we used ECR as our primary endpoint. Our sensitivity analyses confirmed that omadacycline is comparable to oxazolidinones (linezolid, tedizolid and contezolid). Additionally, we evaluated omadacycline’s efficacy for targeted therapy against MRSA, finding it potentially superior to both established drugs like vancomycin and newer agents like tedizolid in three different populations, which is consistent with previous meta-analyses [38]. However, sensitivity analyses revealed that these results were not robust. In terms of safety, omadacycline showed no significant differences compared to other antibiotics, aligning with previous integrated safety summaries [22]. Despite this favorable safety profile, gastrointestinal AEs, particularly nausea and vomiting, were the most common in clinical trials [22]. The package label also includes warnings about the side-effects such as tooth discoloration, enamel hypoplasia and inhibition of bone growth when administered to children under 8 years of age, during late pregnancy, or in infancy [22].

Several limitations of our study should be considered in the interpretation of our data. First, like other NMAs, heterogeneity was an inherent limitation [44]. Some of the RCTs included in this NMA were conducted before the FDA draft guidance on ABSSSI drug development [1], leading to variability in population definitions, therapy durations and outcomes. We also observed statistical inconsistencies in the network for all-cause mortality. Second, the methodological quality of some studies was low. 17 out of 47 studies were assessed as having a high risk of bias, and four trials evaluating linezolid were open-label studies. Moreover, the NMA data for the clinical success outcomes were not stable when sensitivity analysis was performed. Thus, the high risk of bias could diminish the reliability and robustness of our findings. Third, each direct comparison involved only a few trials, rendering the results inconclusive and necessitating further data to verify the findings. Fourth, all trials were sponsored by the pharmaceutical manufacturers of the antibiotics, which could introduce reporting bias. Fifth, these findings approached borderline statistical significance, suggesting trends that could become more pronounced with further research or larger sample sizes. Finally, differences between dosing regimens were ignored to facilitate the NMA, which may have affected the outcomes.

Conclusions

In summary, omadacycline appears to be a promising option for ABSSSI with efficacy comparable to or better than many other anti-MRSA agents. However, the study’s methodological limitations and potential biases necessitate cautious interpretation of the results and a need for further research to confirm its clinical utility.
