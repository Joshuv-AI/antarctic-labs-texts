Decoding single-crystal lithium growth through solid electrolyte interphase omics
Authors: Gongxun Lu, Zhiyuan Han, Lei Shi, Zhilong Wang, Mengtian Zhang, Xinru Wu, Zhihong Piao, Xiao Xiao, Shengyu Tao, Jianwei Nai, Zhijin Ju, Xuan Zhang, Yanqiang Han, Karl Luigi Loza Vidaurre, Hongyan Fu, Jinjin Li, Xinyong Tao, Guangmin Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurately understanding the impact of solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) on lithium deposition is critical for high-energy lithium metal batteries. Yet traditional strategies, focusing solely on isolated components, fail to capture multi-constituent synergies and underlying mechanisms. To address this challenge, we introduce the concept of SEI omics and establish a dataset of cryogenic transmission electron microscopy images combined with co-localized component information. By integrating interpretable machine learning and physics-based feature selection, we decoupled the roles of SEI constituents, revealing that higher N/S/P/F content and reduced O in the SEI improve lithium deposition. Combined density functional theory and electrochemical phase-field modeling uncovered multi-scale effects of SEI components on Li growth. Results confirm that designing an inner SEI layer with high surface energy and migration ability significantly refines deposition morphology. Guided by machine learning-optimized composition, a highly disordered SEI was engineered, achieving high average Coulombic efficiency of 99.35% over 800 cycles for Li||Cu cell at 1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2. This work establishes a universal framework for understanding SEI-coupled effects on lithium growth, offering transformative strategies for electrolyte and interface design.

Introduction

Lithium metal batteries (LMBs) are recognized for their potential to deliver high energy density but are plagued by uncontrolled dendritic lithium (Li) deposition, leading to reduced cycling efficiency, shortened lifespan, and significant safety risks1–4. The nucleation and growth of Li are strongly influenced by the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), which is closely associated with the composition of the electrolyte5–8. Numerous past studies have aimed to alter the composition and structure of the SEI via electrolyte design, to enhance its lithium-ion (Li+) transport ability or mechanical strength9–11. Notably, recent advancements in electrolyte engineering, including high-concentration electrolytes12–14, localized high-concentration electrolytes (LHCEs)15–17, and weakly solvating strategies that enhance anion decomposition18–20, have been developed to create SEIs with the ideal components (such as LiF, Li2O, Li3N, LiNxOy crystallites, or organic components) uniformly distributed with desired physicochemical properties to obtain uniform lithium deposition morphology (LDM) (Fig. 1a). Recent research has suggested that applying ultra-high current densities can decouple Li deposition from SEI growth, resulting in a well-defined rhombic dodecahedron structure across various electrolyte types21. However, the universal relationship between SEI composition and LDM remains elusive, posing a fundamental challenge of accurately understanding and predicting deposition behaviors. This challenge originates from several factors: (1) The difficulty in obtaining non-destructive information of LDM and its composition, and the nonlinear coupling between the highly complex SEI components and deposition behavior, compounded by limitations in analyzing large, heterogeneous, high-dimensional datasets (Fig. 1b). (2) Comprehensive analytical tools to evaluate the synergistic mechanisms of Li conduction and dendrite suppression in multi-component SEIs are lacking (Fig. 1c). (3) The absence of standardized definitions for LDM makes it challenging to offer quantitative guidance for controlling deposition patterns (Fig. 1d).

Here, we introduce the concept of SEI-omics to decouple the correlations between complete SEI composition and lithium single-crystal structures. Using datasets and co-localized compositional information from cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM) experiments, a wide range of lithium deposition morphologies and corresponding SEI compositions are analyzed across various mainstream electrolyte systems. By employing the geometric structure factor λ to quantitatively describe the fine structure of lithium metal nanocrystals, this approach surpasses the limitations of traditional qualitative descriptions of lithium deposition morphologies (such as dendritic, spherical, mossy, or needle-like). Combined with data-driven models, the method systematically decouples the cross-scale quantitative relationships between SEI components and the fine structure of lithium metal nanocrystals. By integrating SEI component mechanisms into a collaborative model and utilizing multiscale simulations, the influence of SEI layer properties on the λ value is investigated. The positive impact of elements such as N, S, P, and F introduced into the SEI on LDM is identified, and it is confirmed that reducing the O content in the SEI is crucial for achieving a highly uniform deposition behavior. These insights provide theoretical guidance for designing an optimal SEI composition, resulting in a uniform LDM with ultra-low λ values and high average Coulombic efficiency (CE) with cycling over 800 cycles. Notably, the configured electrolyte enables stable cycling in pouch cell under low electrolyte-to-electrode (E/C) and negative-to-positive electrode (N/P) ratios.

Results and discussion

Decoupling the effects of SEI components on lithium growth using machine learning

The first step of the three-step workflow (Supplementary Fig 1) of the SEI-omics study is data collection and feature extraction. A substantial dataset from previous cryo-TEM experiments conducted under consistent electrodeposition parameters across various electrolyte conditions was utilized to explore the relationship between SEI composition and LDM (Supplementary Fig 1). The morphology of deposited areas was correlated with corresponding elemental contents, including C, N, O, F, P, and S, obtained through energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) (Fig. 1e). Elemental ratios were also incorporated as features (Supplementary Table 1) to gain a more nuanced understanding of how SEI components influence LDM. Through a data-driven approach, three distinct predictive models were established using ensemble learning methodologies and cross-validation, aiming to analyze different morphologies (Fig. 1f). These models underwent rigorous assessment for both intrinsic and post-hoc explainability, with features ranked based on their significance in determining the outcomes. This analysis of feature ranking and significant values not only facilitates the understanding of lithium deposition behavior but also clarifies the interfacial mechanisms affecting LDM (Fig. 1g).

To improve the understanding of deposition morphologies, we introduce a unified indicator of single-crystal Li growth size, λ, which represents the ratio of Li’s longitudinal growth length (L) to its horizontal growth width (W) (Fig. 1h). The utility of λ is validated through recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV), where it is identified as the most significant among ten morphology indicators (Supplementary Note 1), showing the strongest correlation with the morphology of deposited Li metal (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). This unified morphology indicator, λ, effectively offers a reliable metric for regulating Li deposition morphology by controlling its value. The magnitude of λ is categorized into two distinct classes: dendritic Li (λ > 1) and spherical Li (λ ≤ 1), simplifying the interpretation of deposition patterns (Fig. 2a). To quantify these morphologies, measurements of L and W for multiple Li single crystals in Scanning TEM (STEM) images are averaged, yielding two key metrics: the generalized width of dendritic Li (GWD) and the generalized diameter of spherical Li (GDS). Due to the extensive array of input features, all models are systematically evaluated via a three-part cross-validation process (Supplementary Table S4). An advanced classification model, referred to as the morphology classification (MC) model, is meticulously crafted with the primary goal of predicting LDM, distinguishing between dendritic and spherical configurations, under specified elemental compositions of the SEI (Supplementary Fig 2a, b). The MC model’s performance is convincingly attested by its receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, boasting a notable area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.92 (Supplementary Fig 2c). Additionally, post-hoc explainability analysis of the MC model reveals the top ten features most critical to the LDM differentiation. Supporting this, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) results offer a robust, quantitative chemical viewpoint, outlining the positive or negative impacts of the eight principal features on the model’s discriminative power between the two morphological scenarios (Supplementary Fig 2d, e). Especially noteworthy is the observation regarding NOr and N values. For higher values, which are indicated by points closer to a deep red hue, the corresponding SHAP values significantly increase (SHAP values > 0). This trend underscores a direct association: a rise in NOr and N values is directly proportional to an increased likelihood of the morphology being classified as spherical over dendritic.

To further substantiate the accuracy and universality of the model, empirical evidence from electrolyte design studies was juxtaposed against its outputs, concentrating on correlations between Li deposition patterns and electrolyte formulations. Given the scarcity of direct cryo-EM observations, a morphological gradient was constructed using SEM data, with λ-values serving as the horizontal axis, reflecting deposition uniformity, and N content in the electrolyte dictating the vertical axis (Fig. 1i). Given that the components in the electrolyte usually show a positive correlation with the SEI components, we explore how N content in the electrolyte influence LDM through the mediating effect of the SEI. It shows that electrolytes featuring LiPF6 tend to yield higher λ-values, suggestive of irregular Li deposition22,23 (Supplementary Table 5). Conversely, substitution with N-bearing salts, such as LiFSI and LiTFSI24,25, or the incorporation of NO3− anion additives into the electrolytes26,27, dramatically reduced λ-values, thereby transitioning Li growth from dendritic to a more desirable morphology. In electrolytes fortified with FSI− and NO3−, the decomposition of these anions fosters a chemically diverse SEI, which thereby promoting a stable, uniform Li deposition landscape, typified by λ-values persistently below 1. The congruence between these empirical findings and the machine learning-derived feature importance rankings attests to the MC model’s precision and reinforces its utility in elucidating Li deposition dynamics within the intricate electrolyte chemistry framework.

Delving into the SEI’s role in dendrite suppression, a GWD prediction model was developed to elucidate how SEI attributes influence dendritic width (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Note 2). Statistical analyses underscore that dendritic λ-values cluster around 8–20, coinciding with GWDs ranging from 0.1 to 1 μm, as visually corroborated by STEM imagery (Fig. 2b, c). Acknowledging the limitations of visualizing full dendrite lengths, a GWD-centric model, leveraging the strong correlation between W and λ, was devised. Its performance metrics, notably the AUC, affirm high accuracies averaging 0.91 across three key metrics (Supplementary Fig 3a–c and Table 6). The top six influencers on GWD ranked in descending order of importance, include C, O, OCr, NOr, FNS/Or and P. SHAP analysis elucidates these features’ collective and individual impacts, highlighting that while O and OCr impair longitudinal dendrite growth by enhancing the inorganic fraction of the SEI and compromising its flexibility, increments in other constituents (C, NOr, FNS/Or and P) generally favor horizontal growth (Supplementary Fig 3d, e).

Transitioning our attention to spherical Li deposition, we note a striking level of uniformity and control in growth patterns. Even within the λ-values less than 1, indicating spherical morphology, variations in GDS values are discernible (Fig. 2d), highlighting the subtleties introduced by different electrolyte formulations, such as those incorporating LiFSI, LiTFSI, LiNO3, and a variety of additives. STEM images illustrate that even within a narrow λ-range of approximately 0.8 to 0.98 (Fig. 2e), there are marked differences in GDS, driven by the diversity in SEI compositions. The significance of GDS, as an indicator of deposition uniformity, is pivotal for maximizing CE in battery cycling. To this end, a model was developed that parses the intricate compositional nuances associated with spherical Li deposits, achieving high accuracies averaging 0.84 across three key performance measures (Supplementary Fig 4a–c and Table 7). At the same time, elements like N, F, P, and S positively contribute to larger GDS, fostering uniform deposition (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig 4d). Although certain electrolyte systems, such as the Li2O suspension electrolyte, can achieve high oxygen content in the SEI, resulting in high SHAP values and a positive impact on LDM, it is important to recognize that this is just one aspect of a complex interplay of factors28. Further analysis of O-related features revealed that high NOr, FP/Or, and FOr positively influence the formation of spherical lithium (larger SHAP values) and favor achieving larger GDS sizes, which supports that high oxygen content is detrimental to the growth of spherical lithium.

Using fitting methods, we explore the relationship between the GWD/GDS model and λ (Supplementary Fig 5e–g, Tables 8–10 and Note 3–4), and identify the ideal values of critical features that govern the changes in morphology (Supplementary Table 11). A polynomial fitting method was selected to achieve an optimal balance between the number of fitting features and the order of polynomial fitting, as detailed in Supplementary Note 3. Consistency between GWD or GDS and λ was demonstrated from two perspectives: Firstly, the best fitting features for λ align well with the results from our model, indicating that they share equally important features. Secondly, changes in important features obtained from the GWD and GDS models similarly impact the changing trend of λ. These findings suggest that the single morphology indicator λ has the potential to unify these models and simplify the analysis of Li deposition morphology in the SEI.

To achieve a depositional morphology with high W and low λ properties, constructing superior SEIs rich in N, NOr, NSr, FCr, FP/Or and low in O content is necessary (Fig. 2g). Quantitative analysis provides a guideline for selecting specific elemental contents to form the target morphology, which indicate the influence of the most important features on the target morphology. For example, when the NOr exceeds 0.02 or the N is greater than 1.3, the formation of spherical Li is more likely. If the N > 2.6 or the NOr > 0.03, particularly with an O below 81%, a larger GDS of deposited Li will be produced (Supplementary Table 11). Conversely, conditions favoring the undesired formation of dendritic Li in SEI design include a C above 8, OCr below 11, and O below 85%. Additionally, SHAP results reflect that higher levels of S (above 2.8) and F (above 5.9) are beneficial for the formation of spherical Li.

Further analysis of the impact of important features on the λ was conducted. The top seven fitting features of λ: N, NOr, O, C, OCr, F, and FOr were chosen as variables, and their relationship with λ was observed (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Table 12). From the perspective of reducing λ, ranges of N > 3.2 and NOr > 0.036 are encouraged to achieve λ values less than 2.8 (under these conditions, most λ values are less than 1). In LiPF6-containing carbonate electrolyte systems, the SEI component is usually characterized by C > 10, O > 93, and OCr > 48, leading to large λ-values (up to 50) for the morphology. Advanced electrolytes such as LHCE-based (LiFSI-DME/TTE) can achieve lower λ-values (up to 13) due to the elevated F-content (F > 14, FOr > 0.15). The proper ranges of elemental features mentioned above correspond well with the ideal values in Supplementary Table 12. This consistency between the important features used to distinguish large or small GDS or GWD and the unified indicator λ strongly supports the validity of using λ to predict the depositional morphology of Li. Consequently, it becomes possible to use these top features to quantitatively predict λ and clearly differentiate between large/small dendritic or spherical Li.

Multi-scale theoretical insights into the lithium growth mechanism

Theoretical calculations based on density functional theory (DFT) have been employed to elucidate the significant impact of the inorganic component on the growth behavior of Li. This study focuses on six inorganic components typically found in SEI layers and evaluates their effectiveness in regulating LDM by examining their theoretical modulus, interfacial energy, bulk conductivity, interfacial migration energy barrier, and band gap29,30 (Supplementary Fig 6a). Given the complexity and the challenge in defining the structures of the various elements and their corresponding components, we opt to focus on six representative and widely acknowledged inorganic components as typical constituents of the SEI, including LiF, Li2O, Li2S, Li2CO3, LiCl, and Li3N (Fig. 3a). All six inorganic components in the SEI layer have high theoretical modulus, which is advantageous for inhibiting Li dendrite growth. High-modulus components can withstand the mechanical stresses associated with Li plating and stripping, maintaining the integrity of the SEI layer and preventing the formation of dendrites. The interfacial energy between these inorganic components and lithium is determined by their wettability and ability to inhibit Li dendrite growth. Among the components, LiF exhibited the highest interfacial energy of 73.28 meV Å−2 (Fig. 3b), indicating the strongest ability to inhibit Li dendrite growth. High bulk conductivity components can improve the overall ionic conductivity of the SEI, thereby facilitating faster Li+ transport. Moreover, the interfacial migration barrier of Li+ is also a key parameter in determining the transport rate of ions in the inner layer of the SEI. Li3N and Li3P were found to exhibit high bulk conductivity, while LiF and Li3N also have low migration energy, making them ideal components for the SEI layer (Fig. 3b). Improved bulk conductivity and reduced migration barrier can enhance the overall performance of the battery by lowering the internal resistance and improving the rate capability. In addition, the band gap of the inorganic components in the SEI layer determines their electronic conductivity and, consequently, influences the Li+ transport mechanism and LDM. Typically, the SEI should be an ion conductor and an electron insulator. Theoretical analysis results indicate that LiF has the highest band gap, while Li3P and Li3N have lower band gaps. Lower band gap values, indicative of possible electronic properties, limit the amount of relevant components in SEIs. Therefore, it is crucial to balance the proportions of the different components in the SEI to achieve optimal characteristics.

To investigate the role of SEI properties at the nano and micro scales on Li anode nucleation and growth morphology, we used finite element analysis (FEA). Using λ as a validated descriptor for evaluating LDM, we analyzed SEI properties from two aspects: transport ability (SEI bulk conductivity and interface migration rate) and Li-SEI interfacial properties (surface energy and migration energy) (Fig. 3c). The analysis shows that increasing SEI Young’s modulusup to 5 GPa help suppress dendrite formation, but further increases have minimal positive impact on deposition morphology, indicating modulus is not a dominant factor (Fig. 3d). At the microscale, enhancing the Li+ conductivity and reducing the migration energy can effectively increase the Li+ transport capacity, reducing concentration polarization and mitigating the electric field tip effect, leading to a more uniform LDM (Supplementary Fig 7a–d). At the nanoscale for Li nucleation and growth, a low SEI bulk conductivity can lead to the emergence of multiple growth points at branching sites during the nucleation process, culminating in a mossy or dendritic morphology.

Although increasing transport ability suppresses branching growth to some extent, leading to denser deposition, it cannot fully prevent morphological branching during nucleation (Fig. 3e). In contrast, Li-SEI interfacial properties significantly influence deposition morphology. Enhancing interfacial contact and Li atom migration promotes a transition from dendritic to dense, spherical growth (Fig. 3f). Quantitative analysis shows that the SEI bulk conductivity, Li-SEI surface energy, and mobility of Li atoms collectively determine λ during the nucleation and growth of individual crystals at the nanoscale, the properties of the inner SEI and the Li metal anode interface exerting a greater influence on λ (Fig. 3g). This is because, during the Li nucleation process before reaching the critical growth radius, the energy required for Li growth to form new surfaces is greater than the bulk free energy, making the crystals less inclined to branch and more likely to maintain a spherical morphology. Enhancing the surface energy between Li and SEI can effectively enlarge the critical radius and reduce the probability of forming other crystal facets and branching. As for the mobility of Li atoms at the Li-SEI interface, higher mobility ensures that Li atoms, after being deposited and reduced from Li ions, can rapidly migrate along the interface to morphological defects, thereby maintaining a complete spherical morphology. Thus, SEI conductivity affects microscale polarization and current distribution, while inner SEI and Li interface properties regulate nanoscale nucleation behavior. Optimizing these SEI characteristics can improve lithium deposition behavior and enhanced Li metal anode performance.

ML-guided SEI design and performance validation

To verify the effect of SEI nature on deposition behavior, we designed an electrolyte additive to improve the SEI’s structure and composition based on ML prediction guidance. According to ML results, optimal SEI components for achieving low λ-values should exhibit higher N, NOr, FCr, and FP/Or characteristics. To modulate the SEI composition, we introduced the easily decomposable additive HFTP, which is free of O and composed of F, N, and P elements, into LiTFSI-DOL/DME and LiFSI-DME/TTE electrolyte systems (detailed selection criteria in Supplementary Note 5). AIMD simulations demonstrated that HFTP molecules decompose preferentially on the Li metal surface, forming Li−F, Li−P, Li−N, and other related products (Fig. 4a). In the different ether-based electrolyte systems without HFTP, Li deposition at a capacity of 0.5 mAh cm−2 results in a mixture of spherical and dendritic structures, with smaller spherical Li (characterized by high λ-value and low W) (Supplementary Fig 8a–c). On a Cu mesh, deposition shows a similar morphology with irregular Li and some dendrites. Moreover, even in the LiNO3-free electrolyte, the addition of HFTP significantly altered the LDM, reducing the λ-value and increasing the width of the lithium sphere from 3 µm to ~5 µm (Supplementary Fig 8d). When LiNO3 and HFTP were used as electrolyte additives simultaneously, the lithium spheres could further grow to 7–10 µm, and the lithium deposition on the Cu grid also formed larger bulk lithium, confirming the significant effect of HFTP on the deposition behavior (Supplementary Fig. 8e, f). As shown in Fig. 4b, the deposited Li has an ultralow λ-value of about 0.65, much lower than other dataset results. EDS mapping of the corresponding area confirmed that the SEI layer on the Li surface primarily consists of evenly distributed elements such as C, N, O, F, P, and S. Additionally, analysis of ten different areas confirmed that the SEI formed by HFTP has a low O content (average ~65%) and high NPF content (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig 8g). This SEI aligns perfectly with the ideal range of values for key features predicted by ML, including high NOr, NSr, and FP/Or. The combined effect of these components achieves a low λ-value transition during Li deposition, preventing dendrite formation and promoting an ideal morphology. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) was used to characterize the nanostructure of the SEI. In conventional electrolyte systems, the SEI exhibits a classical mosaic structure consisting of an amorphous phase embedded with large Li2O nanocrystals (~5–10 nm)6,31,32 (Supplementary Fig 8h, i). In contrast, the HFTP-induced SEI layer displays highly disordered structural features with only a small amount of Li2O lattice present (Fig. 4d). This unique SEI formation is primarily due to the presence of various positive characteristic elements (P, F, N, S) within the SEI.

DFT results confirmed that doping the Li2O surface with five key elements significantly reduces the spin density of surface atoms (Fig. 4e). This reduction in spin density indicates fewer unpaired electrons, making the atoms less likely to interact with Li and thereby promoting Li+ diffusion on the Li2O surface. Moreover, the introduction of these characteristic elements can also affect the stability of the Li2O crystal structure itself, which is a key factor in promoting the formation of a highly disordered SEI layer. FEA simulations were used to examine the differences in Li+ transport paths and conduction capabilities under different SEIs (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig 9h). The conventional mosaic structure results in uneven Li+ transport and significant local current density differences, leading to uneven Li deposition behavior and a tendency towards high λ values. In contrast, the highly disordered SEI layer achieves uniform Li+ flow and local current density dispersion, promoting uniform Li deposition.

The electrochemical benefits of the HFTP-induced SEI layer were further validated. Compared to the LiTFSI electrolyte system without additives, the introduction of HFTP enhances the CE of the cell to 99.64% and exhibits lower voltage polarization (Supplementary Fig 9a). Additionally, the HFTP-modified SEI reduces the initial nucleation potential and continuously affects Li deposition/dissolution behavior, promoting stable cycling of the battery for more than 350 cycles (Supplementary Fig 9b). This performance enhancement is primarily attributed to the improved physical and chemical properties of the SEI layer. Detailed analyses using time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) and XPS confirmed that the preferential decomposition of HFTP contributes to constructing the SEI inner layer, forming superior inorganic components such as Li3P, Li3N, and LiF (Supplementary Fig 9c, d). The presence of these multiple inorganic components facilitates Li+ diffusion at the interface, induces uniform nucleation, and promotes low λ-value growth of Li. AFM results of the LDM also confirm that HFTP introduction encourages the lateral growth of Li metal, resulting in Li deposits with low λ and high W values. Compared to the composite components of conventional mosaic-structured SEI layers, the inorganic-rich nature of the HFTP-derived SEI inner layer provides stronger mechanical strength (Supplementary Fig 9e, f). Additionally, the presence of numerous characteristic components renders the overall SEI layer more elastic, enabling it to adapt to volume expansion during continuous Li deposition and maintain SEI layer stability33,34.

Furthermore, HFTP was applied to the LHCE electrolyte system to compare the effects of the additive on SEI, deposition behavior, and electrochemical performance. In the conventional LHCE electrolyte system, Li deposition is more disordered and predominantly consists of dendrites (Supplementary Fig 10a). The SEI in this system is mainly composed of oxygen (high O and OCr), with low contents of several important characteristic components (N, F, NOr, FNS/Or, etc.), resulting in high λ-values for the deposited morphology (averaging about 6.3) (Supplementary Fig 10b). In contrast, the introduction of HFTP in the LHCE electrolyte promotes the formation of wider dendritic Li with a low λ (~3.4 on average) (Supplementary Fig 10c). The corresponding SEI fractions exhibit better properties, such as reduced O and OCr content and significantly higher levels of positive characteristic components (Supplementary Fig 10d). This result validates the accuracy of the ML predictions and confirms the beneficial effect of HFTP on SEI composition and deposition behavior. In Li | |Cu battery tests, the HFTP-added electrolyte demonstrates a stable cycle life of over 800 cycles at 1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2 with an average CE of 99.35% (Supplementary Fig 9g). The performance of CE measurement surpasses many advanced LHCE-based electrolytes, highlighting the significant improvement in electrochemical performance attributed to the addition of HFTP (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Table 13). In the Ah-level Li | |NCM811 pouch cell, HFTP-involved electrolyte shows stable cycling performance, achieving approximately 220 cycles at a cut-off voltage of 4.5 V and a current density of 0.5 C, with a capacity retention rate of 81.9% (Fig. 4h). The performance of Ah-level pouch cell is superior to many advanced electrolyte systems (Fig. 4i and Supplementary Table 14). These results confirm that the SEI with a specialized composition based on ML predictions not only optimizes LDM but also enables efficient and stable cycling under extreme practical conditions.

In summary, this work presents a concept of SEI-omics to decouple the cross-scale quantitative relationships between SEI components and the fine structure of lithium metal nanocrystals by employing a data-driven approach using cryo-TEM datasets. We introduce a universal descriptor to quantify deposition behavior and use machine learning models to reveal the quantitative correlation between SEI composition and λ values, identifying the optimal ranges for ideal SEI components. Moreover, this model can quantify the synergistic or antagonistic effects on Li-metal morphology under specific SEI compositions, moving us beyond single-factor analyses to understand complex multi-element interactions. Combined with multiscale simulations, the high surface energy and migration rate of the inner-layer SEI components were identified as the most critical factors contributing to the low λ-value deposition behavior. Building on these insights, we design a special electrolyte additive that optimizes the SEI composition to achieve a highly disordered SEI layer, resulting in a LDM with λ value as low as 0.65. This optimized SEI layer facilitates stable cycling for 800 cycles in Li | |Cu cells, with a high average CE of up to 99.35%, and ensures cycling for over 220 cycles in pouch cells. Our SEI omics framework can reveal the hidden physical processes and mechanisms among the complex components of the SEI, and can be easily applied to new electrolyte systems to accelerate the development of advanced SEI.

Methods

Experimental section

Materials

For the data collection process utilized in machine learning, the electrolyte used is based on LiPF6/LiFSI/LiTFSI as Li salt, EC/DEC/FEC/DOL/DME/TTE, and other solvents and additives in different ratios. All the above electrolytes/salts/solvents were obtained by DoDo Chem. For the experiment, a conventional ether-based electrolyte LiTFSI-DOL/DME+LiNO3 (DoDo chem) was used as received. All chemicals were used as received unless otherwise specified. HFTP (98%) was sourced from Rhawn. DME (DoDo chem) and TTE (1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropylether, Macklin) were purified by passage through molecular sieves (Aladdin) pre-treated at 300 °C. Baseline electrolytes comprised 1 M LiTFSI in DOL/DME (1:1 v/v) with or without LiNO3 additive, and 1.5 M LiFSI in DME/TTE (3:7 v/v). The experimental electrolyte was formulated by adding 5 wt% HFTP to the 1.5 M LiFSI DME/TTE baseline. All electrolyte preparation was conducted in an argon-filled glovebox (O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm). Electrode materials and cell components, including Li foil (500 μm), Cu foil, Cu mesh, Al foil, 2032-type coin cell cases, and dry 1 Ah NCM811 | |Li pouch cells, were supplied by Guangdong Canrd New Energy Technology Co. Ltd.

Cell assembly and electrochemical measurements

All coin cell (2032-type) assembly was performed within the argon-filled glovebox. Each cell incorporated one layer of Celgard 2500 separator and 40 μL of electrolyte. Electrochemical performance was evaluated using a Neware multichannel battery test system (CT-4008) within a constant temperature chamber maintained at 30 °C. For Li | |Cu cell CE cycling tests, cells underwent five pre-cycles (0.01 - 1.5 V) to stabilize the Li/Cu electrode interface. Subsequent cycling involved Li deposition onto Cu followed by stripping to 1.5 V at varying current densities. For the Aurbach’s CE test, a standard protocol was employed: Coulombic efficiency = (nQc + QR)/ (nQc + QT). QT denoted an initial total Li plating capacity of 5 mAh cm−2, Qc the fixed cycling capacity of 1 mAh cm−2, and QR the capacity retention measured during the final stripping step. For Li | |NCM811 pouch cells, two initial cycles at 0.1 C for activation were conducted, and then cycled at 0.5 C for long-term tests (1 C = 200 mA g−1). The electrolyte filling of the pouch cell was conducted in an argon-filled inert atmosphere before sealing. An external pressure of 100 kPa was applied to the pouch cell. Detailed pouch cell parameters are shown in Supplementary Table 15.

Material characterizations

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed on a PHI 5000 VersaProbe II or Escalab Xi+ system. Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS, PHI Nano ToF II) with an Ar⁺ sputter rate calibrated at 0.3 nm s⁻1 (for SiO2) at 3 keV and 100 nA over a 50 × 50 μm2 area. Morphology and microstructure of samples were conducted by Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, HITACHI SU8010) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Talos-S operated at 200 kV). Surface Topography and mechanical properties of deposited Li and SEI were investigated by atomic force microscopy (AFM, MFP-3DSA).

Cryo-TEM data collection and experimental details

All cryo-TEM characterizations were performed using a transmission electron microscope (TEM, FEI Talos-S) operating at 200 kV, equipped with a Gatan 698 cryo-transfer holder. For the data collection process utilized in machine learning, the electrolytes comprised conventional ether-based electrolytes, ester-based electrolytes, or partially advanced electrolytes. The data collection process focused solely on the STEM (scanning transmission electron microscopy) images of the collected samples, along with their corresponding EDS (energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy) results, extracting the relevant features. All test conditions were standardized with an areal capacity of 0.5 mAh cm−2 at a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2. After deposition, the Cu meshes were washed using DEC (diethyl carbonate) or DME (1,2-dimethoxyethane) and then dried. The Cu meshes were subsequently transferred into the sealed container of the cryo-TEM holder within an argon-filled glovebox. The cryo-TEM holder was rapidly inserted into the TEM, and the samples were stabilized by liquid nitrogen cooling at a low temperature of −180 °C. All TEM images were acquired at cryogenic temperatures ( − 180 °C).

DFT calculation

Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP)44 was performed using the plane wave basis set with an energy cutoff of 500 eV, the projector augmented wave (PAW) potentials45, and the GGA-PBE exchange-correlation functional46. For the calculation of absorption energy between oxygen radicals and CEI inorganic components (LiF, Li2S, Li2O, Li2CO3, Li3N, Li3P), the surface of LiF (001), Li2S (111), Li2O (111), Li2CO3 (001), Li3N (110), Li3P (103) was modeled with a 15 Å in the vertical, which were selected based on previously reported lattice faces with the smallest Gibbs free energy. The model structure with the fixed supercell was optimized using thresholds for the total energy of 10-5 eV and force of 0.05 eV/Å. As for the calculation of Li+ diffusion barrier, the climbing-image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method was applied for computing decomposition barriers47, which can obtain the minimum energy path between the given initial and final position. In the spin density calculations, the y-axis represents the distribution of net spin charge on the atoms. MD simulation

All the ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) calculations are carried out using Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package48. The electron exchange and correlation energies are described by the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional within the generalized gradient approximation49. The projector augmented wave45,46 is applied to represent the electron-ion interactions. The energy cut-off of the plane-wave basis expansion is set to 400 eV. To simulate the Li metal anode, we construct 9-layer (3 × 3) supercell slab from Li (100) surface, where the three layers in the middle of the slab are kept fixed, the rest allowed to relax fully. Moreover, the thickness of the vacuum region filled with 6 DOLs, 4 DMEs and 1 HFTP is set as 15 Å to represent the electrolyte system. This ratio of electrolyte components is estimated based on the density of the electrolyte (1.13 g cm−3 for DOL/DME (1:1, v/v)). The AIMD simulations are finished using VASP within the NVT ensemble at 330 K for 10 ps. A Nose-mass parameter of 0.5 as Nose thermostat is utilized to control the temperature oscillations in all simulations. A 1×1×1 Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh is utilized for Brillouin zone integration50. Furthermore, in order to keep time step of 1 fs, the hydrogen mass is replaced with the tritium mass. The IVDW is set to 12 to describe the intermolecular forces. The convergence tolerance for energy and force is set at 10−5 eV and 10−2 eV Å−1, respectively.

Finite Element Method and Phase Field Simulation

Based on the Comsol Multiphysics 6.2 platform, we employed the Li-Ion Battery interface and the Partial Differential Equation interface to respectively conduct finite element method and phase field simulations. The effects of various SEI properties—including Young’s modulus, transport ability, and the interfacial characteristics—on lithium growth behavior were detailed simulated, in which the governing equation51 were described in the Supplementary Information. The simulation geometry and model of the computational details for Fig. 4f are depicted in Supplementary Fig 9h and Supplementary information. Key parameters include: anodic and cathodic charge transfer coefficients (0.5), exchange current density (10 mA cm⁻2), Li⁺ concentration in electrolyte (1 M), temperature (298 K), and applied current density (0.1 mA cm⁻2).
