Modeling network evolution by multi-agent reinforcement learning
Authors: Dong Li, Tianwei Lin, Zhaoyang Bao, Bingqiao Gu, Yatao Zhang, Fei Yang, Yanhao Sun, Zhanwei Du, Petter Holme
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Modeling network evolution is foundational for understanding and regulating networks. Existing models simplify away the fact that network evolution is often a group decision process, leading to two primary limitations: that nodes lack the ability to learn policy and that there is no coordination among node policies. To address these shortcomings and consider the effectiveness of multi-agent reinforcement learning in solving group decision tasks, this paper proposes a complex Network Evolution model based on Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (NEMARL). In our model, swarm intelligence emerging through collaborative interactions among autonomous nodes drives the evolution of the network structure. Our extensive experiments demonstrate that the NEMARL model accurately reproduces classical network characteristics and fits real network data well. Furthermore, we demonstrate its effectiveness through scenario testing.

Introduction

Early research on complex network evolution models relied on the random network model1, which posits that any two nodes in the network can be connected with a specific probability. With the advancement of data processing capabilities, researchers noticed that many real-world networks are far from random. To address the shortcomings of the random network model, small-world models2–4 represented by the Watts-Strogatz model and scale-free models5–7 represented by the Barabási-Albert model were developed. They elucidate the principles behind the generation of topological characteristics (e.g., small average path length, large clustering coefficient, and a fat-tailed degree distribution) through mechanisms such as randomized rewiring, randomized edge addition, preferential attachment, copy, and redirection. Subsequently, as shown in Fig. 1a, building on these foundational models, researchers have sought to integrate evolutionary mechanisms or rules—including weights8–12, local world13–15, popularity and similarity16–18, triangle closure19–21 and geometric distance22–24—into the construction of network models, in order to achieve the purpose of describing the dynamic evolution process of network topology more accurately.Fig. 1Motivation diagram of leveraging multi-agent reinforcement learning to develop the network evolution model.a Evolutionary mechanisms in existing network models and their associated limitations. b Reinforcement learning, whose policy self-learning mechanism helps address the first limitation of existing models. c Multi-agent systems, whose policy negotiation mechanism helps address the second limitation. d The multi-agent reinforcement learning architecture that we have developed for building the network model combines the advantages of both (b) and (c).

Although the construction of network-evolution models is a mature topic, there are gaps: First, nodes lack the capabilities of policy self-learning. With few exceptions25, existing models, all nodes passively execute fixed evolutionary rules, which do not adjust over time or in response to environmental changes. However, nodes in real networks are essentially autonomous intelligent individuals capable of learning and optimizing behavior policies based on their own surrounding environments. Second, the behavior policies of the nodes are isolated from each other. Under the domination of fixed rules in existing models, each node independently executes its own behavior without considering the policies of other nodes. However, nodes in real networks are not completely isolated, and their behavior policies affect each other.

Distinct from mechanisms proposed in existing models, this paper offers an alternative explanatory perspective: bottom-up swarm intelligence, emerging through collaborative interactions of autonomous nodes, may constitute a candidate plausible explanation for complex network evolution. Modeling network evolution mechanisms from this perspective is the key to overcoming the limitations of existing models. Multi-agent reinforcement learning26–33 utilizes reinforcement learning algorithms to control multi-agent systems, thereby providing critical theories and methods for solving group decision-making tasks and realizing swarm intelligence. In this paper, we attempt to leverage multi-agent reinforcement learning to construct the complex network evolution model. The theoretical foundations of our model are as follows.

Reinforcement learning34,35 is well-suited for modeling the autonomous learning process of node behavior policies. Here, we take the co-author network as an example to explain the reason. The evolution of the co-author network results from the cooperative behaviors of researchers. At a certain time, researchers are guided by their cooperative experiences in deciding with whom to collaborate, and these experiences are continuously updated based on the academic influence derived from such behaviors. Reinforcement learning is particularly effective in the above-mentioned scenario where decision-making is influenced by prior experiences and the goal is to optimize utilities36–43. Specifically, as shown in Fig. 1b, agent i (i.e., researcher) performs an action a based on its policy (i.e., cooperative experience) in its environmental state S. The environment feeds a reward r (i.e., academic influence) back to the agent, which is then used to learn the policy with the aim of maximizing the cumulative reward. It is evident that the reward-driven mechanism and policy self-learning method are applicable to the modeling task of the complex network evolution process, which helps to address the first limitation of existing network models. Further details regarding the rationale for modeling node behaviors via reinforcement learning can be found in Supplementary Section 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1.

The dynamic evolution process of complex networks can be effectively characterized using multi-agent systems44–49. The multi-agent system adopts a bottom-up modeling approach: the swarm intelligence of the system at the macro-level emerges from individual behavioral interactions at the micro-level. As shown in Fig. 1c, the system task of the network model is to evolve various group properties, such as small-world, scale-free, community, and non-linear growth, which can be achieved through the aggregation of subtasks of individual nodes. In specific subtasks, each node, regarded as an intelligent agent, ultimately makes behavioral decisions that benefit itself through local information interaction and collaboration. The preceding analysis demonstrates that complex networks can be viewed as multi-agent systems composed of multiple autonomous and interacting nodes within the same environment. Collaborative control technology is a crucial research direction of the multi-agent systems field and has achieved significant progress, which is helpful in addressing the second limitation of existing network models.

Multi-agent reinforcement learning is an interdisciplinary technology combining reinforcement learning and multi-agent systems, which possesses the advantages of both technologies in modeling complex network evolution mechanisms. Based on the above analysis, we develop the complex Network Evolution model based on Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (NEMARL), which introduces a distributed collaborative communication architecture, as shown in Fig. 1d. Unlike traditional fully centralized learning50,51 and centralized training-distributed execution architectures52–54, the developed architecture takes full advantage of the network topology as a communication channel and offers enhanced scalability. Specifically, in the NEMARL model, each node i can observe local information oi from the network environment, and obtain communication collaborative information hi from its surrounding neighbors through the network structure. It then makes the strategic decision ai by integrating these two aspects of information. The reward ri, fed back from the environment, is used to optimize the policy of node i.

A large number of experiments demonstrate that our proposed NEMARL model has the following advantages: (1) The NEMARL model successfully reproduces various typical network features, such as power-law or log-normal degree distribution, degree assortativity or disassortativity, small-world effect, formation and evolution of community structures, and role transformation process of nodes between peripheral and core individuals. (2) The proposed model effectively fits real network data from multiple perspectives: degree distributions, clustering coefficients, and average path lengths on static networks, degree distributions, largest connected component and Latora efficiency on dynamics networks, as well as the distributions of the number of links established or deleted over time on dynamic networks. (3) The NEMARL model achieves logical results in several scenario tests: similar degree, low closeness centrality and contradictory reward. (4) The architecture of our model has universality, allowing specific calculation components (e.g., network representation algorithm, node influence estimation method) to be updated with more advanced technologies in the future.

The aforementioned experimental results and analytical findings substantiate the efficacy of the proposed interpretable generative model in characterizing and reconstructing the dynamic processes of network evolution. This paper breaks through the traditional network model construction framework and introduces a new modeling paradigm that employs a multi-agent system with self-learning ability from the perspective of node swarm intelligence. The proposed NEMARL model has the ability to reproduce various topological and evolutionary properties of real networks and provides an effective theoretical tool for understanding real-world systems, predicting network behaviors and improving network designs.

(d) Model training module based on experience replay and target network. This module presents how to leverage feedback rewards from the environment to train neural networks used for estimating expected utilities of node actions. The network evolution process driven by the NEMARL model generates a sequence of state-action transitions, which are archived in a prioritized experience replay buffer. Mini-batches sampled from this historical dataset are used to optimize the neural network parameters via stochastic gradient descent. Furthermore, the stability of the learning process is enhanced through the utilization of the target network technique.

Overall, the learning architecture of the NEMARL model belongs to the communication-based distributed type. The comparisons of different kinds of popular learning architectures are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Section 2. For the convenience of understanding, the key symbols in the proposed model and their explanations are provided in Supplementary Table 1. In summary, each node in the NEMARL model first incorporates two neural networks to estimate the utilities of the actions of establishing and deleting links, respectively. Subsequently, nodes decide which new links to establish and which existing links to delete, based on the estimated utilities of actions and their policy negotiations. During the network update process, the nodes’ behavior trajectories and obtained rewards are archived into the experience pool for facilitating the training of the corresponding neural networks.

Reproducing typical network features

Here, we attempt to leverage the NEMARL model to reproduce typical network features, such as degree distribution, degree correlation, clustering coefficient, average path length, community structure and node role transformation. How to measure these network metrics is illustrated in Supplementary Section 3.

Degree distribution

The degree of a node represents the number of its connections with other nodes, and the degree distribution describes the probability distribution of the degrees across all nodes in the network. Identifying the characteristics of degree distributions, along with interpreting and reproducing these typical distributions, are significant research challenges in the field of complex networks. In 1999, Barabási and Albert5 proposed the concept of scale-free networks whose degree distributions follow the power-law patterns. This concept immediately attracted widespread attention and sparked considerable research interest. However, in recent years, the skepticism55,56 regarding the power-law degree distributions has gradually increased. Representatively, Broido and Clauset56 found that strongly scale-free networks are empirically rare via testing on nearly 1000 technological, information, transportation and social networks. They noted that log-normal distributions often fit the real networks as well or better than power laws.

In light of the divergence in network degree distributions discussed above, we attempt to employ our NEMARL model to generate network structures exhibiting various degree distributions. In each figure, the x-axis and y-axis represent the degree k and the probability p(k) of randomly selecting a node with degree k, respectively. The colors (blue, red, and green) of three curves correspond to different network sizes (100, 300, and 500 nodes). Degree correlation

Degree-degree correlation is a fundamental property of networks that reveals the underlying patterns of connectivity between nodes with varying degrees. Understanding degree correlation is important for analyzing the structure and dynamics of complex networks. Based on degree correlation, complex networks can be divided into two categories57: assortative networks and disassortative networks. In the assortative networks represented by social networks, nodes tend to connect with other nodes that have similar degrees. In disassortative networks, typically seen among biological and technological networks, nodes prefer to establish connections with others of a vastly different degree. This property is typically quantified using three approaches: the joint degree distribution (JDD), the Pearson degree correlation (PDC), and the k-nearest neighbors (KNN). The details of these three methods can be found in Supplementary Section 3.5.

As shown in Fig. 3c–f, we adopt JDD and PDC to characterize the degree of correlation of the networks generated by the NEMARL model. In these subfigures, whenever a pair of connected nodes has degrees (k1, k2), the (k1, k2) entry in the joint degree distribution matrix is incremented by one. This process is repeated for all edges of the network, resulting in a matrix showing the distribution of connections between nodes of different degrees. In addition, each subfigure is annotated with its corresponding Pearson degree correlation coefficient, positioned above the subfigure. It can be seen from Fig. 3c–f that our model can generate both assortative and disassortative networks.

Clustering coefficient and APL

Clustering coefficient measures the degree of network collectivization, and average path length (APL) represents the average distance of the shortest path between all pairs of nodes in the network. These metrics are crucial indicators for assessing the small-world characteristics of a network. For a network, a high clustering coefficient indicates that it resembles a regular network, while a small average path length suggests it is akin to a random network. A network with both of the above properties is known as a small-world network. In this context, we evaluate whether our model can generate small-world networks.

In order to more clearly demonstrate the performance of our proposed NEMARL model, we adopt networks with small clustering coefficients and large average path lengths as the initial networks. Figure 3g–h presents the changes in network clustering coefficient and average path length over time steps. The blue, red and green curves correspond to three networks containing 100, 300 and 500 nodes, respectively. It can be observed that, driven by the NEMARL model, the clustering coefficient evolves from 0.01–0.03 to 0.60–0.62, and the average path length evolves from 5.5–5.8 to 1.5–1.7. Therefore, we can conclude that the NEMARL model is capable of generating networks with the small-world effect.

Community structure

In complex networks, similar nodes typically cluster into local structures with dense connections, which are referred to as “communities.” A community not only possesses topological properties, but also owns specific functions or attributes. Consequently, understanding how communities emerge and change is crucial for uncovering the mechanisms behind network evolution.

As modularity is an important metric of measuring community properties of complex networks, we first present the change of modularity with time steps for various network sizes, as depicted in Fig. 3i. Here, the initial utilized networks are random networks without community structures. It can be seen from Fig. 3i that the modularity of the networks increases from low to high values, and eventually stabilizes without significant fluctuations. More intuitively, Fig. 3j–q displays the network topology structures with 300 nodes at eight specific time steps, which clearly illustrate the evolution process from a random network to a network with 5 communities, driven by the NEMARL model. In summary, all our above results demonstrate the effectiveness of our model in terms of uncovering the network evolution mechanism from the perspective of the community.

Node role transformation

In real networks, nodes within a community assume different roles, such as core nodes and periphery nodes58–60, which can constantly change over time. For example, in a co-author network, when a PhD student joins a research group, s/he only has a very small influence and is positioned at the periphery of the entire group. Initially, s/he collaborates with his/her influential advisor to publish academic papers. Later, through participating in academic conferences and other activities, s/he establishes cooperative relationships with more influential researchers. His/her influence also becomes greater and greater, gradually shifting from a periphery node to a core node within a community. Conversely, if the number of published papers and collaborative behaviors of a core node decreases over time, s/he will also become a periphery node again.

Here, we leverage our NEMARL model to reproduce the node role-changing process described above. Specifically, as shown in Fig. 4, the node with red color starts as a periphery node with a small influence at time 1, whose size is decided by its influence (e.g., PageRank value). During the subsequent network evolution process, it continuously establishes connections with nodes with greater influence, which causes its own influence to continue to increase. At time 10, the red node has become a core node within its community. However, from time 20 to 30, the research activity of this node decreases, causing it to revert to a periphery node. Fitting real networks

In addition to reproducing the common characteristics of complex networks, we further employ the NEMARL model to fit real networks from the following four key aspects: degree distribution on static networks, clustering coefficient and APL on static networks, degree distribution on dynamic networks, and number distribution of established or deleted links on dynamic networks. Specifically, we develop a hybrid algorithm that combines simulated annealing with heuristics to fit the NEMARL model to real-world networks. The detailed procedure of this fitting algorithm is described in Supplementary Section 4. In the implementation, we choose a regular network as the initial network and use PageRank to calculate the node rewards. It should be noted that other importance calculation methods could also be used for real data fitting, as long as the same parameter analysis and parameter fitting algorithms are followed.

We collect 50 real-world static networks sourced from Network Repository, Koblenz Network Collection and Vlado, which span across social, chemical, biological and engineering domains. Descriptions about these networks are available in Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Section 4.1.

Largest connected component (LCC) and Latora efficiency fitting on dynamic networks

We extend our evaluation of the NEMARL model to two additional global structural metrics: largest connected component (LCC) and Latora efficiency. The LCC quantifies the size of the largest connected subgraph, which directly reflects the macroscopic connectivity of a network. In contrast, Latora efficiency measures the global communication efficiency by accounting for the inverse of shortest path lengths between all node pairs. These two metrics capture different but complementary aspects of network robustness; their combined use provides a more comprehensive assessment of network vulnerability.

Given that the dynamic network datasets used in our previous evaluation exhibit minimal fluctuations in LCC values over time, we adopted three additional datasets (Contact_primary_school, Tij_inVS and Ht09_contact_list) to more rigorously assess the model’s performance. For each dataset, we extract network snapshots at four distinct time steps, and then employ the NEMARL model to generate corresponding synthetic dynamic networks. We then compute the LCC and Latora efficiency at each time step for both the real and generated networks.

Supplementary Fig. 8 presents the comparative results: (a)–(c) display the temporal evolution of the LCC across the three datasets, while (d)–(f) illustrate the corresponding Latora Efficiency dynamics. Red curves denote empirical observations derived from real-world networks, whereas blue/green curves represent NEMARL-generated results. Across all datasets, the synthetic networks closely match both the LCC and Latora efficiency of the empirical networks over time. These results demonstrate that NEMARL effectively captures the co-evolution of both connectivity structure and transport efficiency in dynamic networks. By simultaneously fitting both the LCC and Latora efficiency across diverse networks over multiple time steps, our model exhibits strong capability in reproducing the macroscopic characteristics of real-world dynamic systems.

Discussion

The existing studies represented by small-world models, scale-free models, and related extended models exhibit two primary shortcomings: First, all nodes in these models are limited to passively following the same fixed rules and lack the ability to autonomously learn behavior policies. Second, the behavior of each node is isolated, disregarding the collaborative interactions that exist among node behavior policies. Policy self-learning methods in reinforcement learning and collaborative control methods in multi-agent systems have the potential to address the above shortcomings. Multi-agent reinforcement learning, a hybrid technology merging reinforcement learning and multi-agent systems, combines the advantages of both in the task of modeling complex network evolution mechanisms. In this paper, we built the complex Network Evolution model based on Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning, referred to as the NEMARL model. In this model, each node, regarded as an intelligent agent, first employs two neural networks to estimate the expected utilities of its link establishment and deletion actions, then negotiates policies with candidate nodes through communication information and executes the negotiated behaviors. The rewards taken by these behaviors are used to train the node’s own neural networks.

Extensive experiments demonstrate that the NEMARL model achieves excellent performance in terms of reproducing classic network features (i.e., scale-free, small-world, assortativity, disassortativity, community structure formation, node role change), fitting the characteristics of real network data (i.e., degree distributions, clustering coefficients, and average path lengths on static networks, degree distributions, largest connected component, Latora efficiency and link update quantity distributions on dynamics networks), and scenario testing (i.e., similar degree, low closeness centrality, pagerank/degree).

These results demonstrate that local, reward-driven decision processes are sufficient to induce the emergence of global topological features observed in empirical networks. Grounded in the principle of “Generative Sufficiency”61, our model offers a plausible alternative explanation for network dynamics: bottom-up swarm intelligence arising from collaborative interactions among autonomous nodes may constitute a key driving mechanism for network evolution. Consequently, this work not only bridges the gap between micro-level incentives and macro-level structural outcomes but also provides potential analytical tools for exploring natural phenomena and understanding social dynamics.

Calculation components in the model

In this paper, we develop a modeling framework for complex network evolution mechanisms based on multi-agent reinforcement learning. This framework includes some key calculation components, such as a network representation learning algorithm used to obtain node vector representations, and a node importance calculation method used to measure nodes’ energies. A pertinent question arises: Does our model depend on specific calculation components, or does the selection of these calculation components significantly impact the model performance?

To answer the above question, we use some alternative network representation learning algorithms (Supplementary Section 8) and node importance calculation algorithms (Supplementary Section 9) for further testing our NEMARL model. Specifically, we first replace GraphWave with Struc2vec62 and VGAE63, respectively, while keeping other components of the model unchanged. Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15 present the performance of the NEMARL model with two alternative algorithms in terms of reproducing typical network characteristics, such as degree distribution, degree correlation, clustering coefficient, APL and community structure. Similarly, we also replace PageRank with HITS and degree centrality, respectively. Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17 illustrate the performance of the NEMARL model with new chosen algorithms. It is evident from Supplementary Figs. 14–17 that, under the aforementioned settings, the NEMARL model is capable of consistently generating networks with scale-free, small-world, assortativity, disassortativity and community properties. Thus, the alternative calculation components do not influence the model performance. These results conclusively demonstrate that our proposed NEMARL model is not dependent on specific calculation components, and the architecture of our model possesses universality and rationality.

Utility-maximizing vs. ε-greedy models

After the policy negotiation process between nodes is completed, nodes have obtained the actions taking maximum utilities. Then, the NEMARL model employs the decayed-ε greedy strategy64,65 to dynamically balance the proportion between utility-maximizing actions and random selection actions. This strategy ensures that the agent initially explores the environment more aggressively but progressively shifts toward exploiting known optimal actions during the training process. Specifically, at the beginning of training, ε is set to a high value (e.g., 0.9) to encourage exploration of the environment. As training progresses, ε is gradually reduced according to a decay schedule (e.g., linear or exponential), enabling the agent to increasingly exploit the learned policy while still maintaining occasional exploration to avoid suboptimal convergence. Here, we experimentally verify the necessity and effectiveness of using the decayed-ε greedy strategy.

Supplementary Figure 18a presents the variation of the mean reward for scenarios with utility-maximizing and ε-greedy assumptions. From this figure, it is evident that, during the initial training stage, the reward obtained by ε-greedy strategy is lower than that of the utility-maximizing strategy. However, as the number of episodes increases, the ε-greedy consistently achieves more stable and higher rewards than utility-maximizing. Furthermore, we compare the utility-maximizing strategy with the ε-greedy approach in terms of fitting degree distributions on real networks. As demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. 18b–d, ε-greedy performs better than utility-maximizing. These results fully confirm that incorporating moderate random actions can enable nodes to broaden their global perspective, which proves beneficial for achieving higher rewards and fitting real-world networks better. Therefore, employing the ε-greedy strategy in the NEMARL model is necessary and effective.

Reward design analysis

The design of the reward function is one of the core components of the NEMARL model, which directly affects the effectiveness of the policies learned by nodes. Here, we first experimentally examine the impact of errors in the reward function on the fitting performance of the model. In scenario testing, we propose a reward function based on low closeness centrality, whose role is to verify the rationality of the NEMARL model via generating the expected network structure. However, in real-world networks, low closeness centrality is typically not the objective pursued by the nodes. We employ the NEMARL model with this low closeness centrality-based reward to fit the degree distributions, clustering coefficients, and average path lengths of real-world networks. The poor results, shown in the Supplementary Fig. 22, indicate that the inaccurate or misspecified reward function significantly impairs the network model’s ability of fitting real-world networks.

Below, we turn to explain the rationale of adopting node centrality metrics (e.g., degree, PageRank) to measure the rewards associated with nodes’ link updating behaviors from three aspects:Challenge in unified reward quantification. Directly quantifying the “benefit” or “reward” taken by the node behavior is often intractable due to its highly domain-specific nature. The heterogeneity of rewards, ranging from economic profit and valuable information to social influence, makes it difficult to establish a uniform measurement framework across different network types.Network flow as the core abstraction of node objectives. Although the specific goals pursued by nodes appear phenomenologically distinct across domains, they can be abstracted into a unified principle from the perspective of network science: the competitive acquisition of network flow. As illustrated in Supplementary Table 8, banks in financial networks pursue profit maximization, which essentially translates to the competition of capital flow; hubs in transportation networks strive to optimize connectivity, which amounts to attracting passenger flow; users in social media networks seek to amplify their influence, the core of which lies in capturing and controlling information flow. As demonstrated above, the abstraction of diverse goals as network flow competition effectively transcends domain boundaries.Centrality as topological proxy for flow control. Network science establishes a well-grounded theoretical framework linking centrality measures to a node’s capacity to attract, mediate, or control network flows. As noted by Borgatti66, structural importance is fundamentally a function of flow characteristics. This theoretical perspective is instantiated in several widely used centrality measures. For instance, PageRank models the stationary probability of a random walker visiting a node. Thus, optimizing PageRank is equivalent to enhancing a node’s visibility and flow attraction. HITS simulates the interaction between content seekers and providers. Consequently, HITS scores represent a node’s capacity to distribute or attract network flow. Betweenness quantifies the fraction of shortest paths passing through a node, thus nodes with high betweenness scores serve as critical bridges controlling flow between other nodes.

As illustrated in Supplementary Table 8, banks in financial networks pursue profit maximization, which essentially translates to the competition of capital flow; hubs in transportation networks strive to optimize connectivity, which amounts to attracting passenger flow; users in social media networks seek to amplify their influence, the core of which lies in capturing and controlling information flow. As demonstrated above, the abstraction of diverse goals as network flow competition effectively transcends domain boundaries.

Centrality as topological proxy for flow control. Network science establishes a well-grounded theoretical framework linking centrality measures to a node’s capacity to attract, mediate, or control network flows. As noted by Borgatti66, structural importance is fundamentally a function of flow characteristics. This theoretical perspective is instantiated in several widely used centrality measures. For instance, PageRank models the stationary probability of a random walker visiting a node. Thus, optimizing PageRank is equivalent to enhancing a node’s visibility and flow attraction. HITS simulates the interaction between content seekers and providers. Consequently, HITS scores represent a node’s capacity to distribute or attract network flow. Betweenness quantifies the fraction of shortest paths passing through a node, thus nodes with high betweenness scores serve as critical bridges controlling flow between other nodes.

Overall, the devise behavioral drivers of real-world nodes can be abstracted as the competitive acquisition of network flows. Since node centrality metrics quantify a node’s structural ability to access or mediate such flows, they thus constitute a principled and universal proxy for measuring node rewards.

In addition to the theoretical analysis above, we further empirically validate why node centrality serves as an appropriate measure of node rewards. Specifically, we design an experiment to compare the evolutionary dynamics of random networks and real-world networks. Four real-world datasets used in this experiment are collected from the network repository platform, which respectively correspond to four distinct categories of networks. Specifically, for each dynamic network, we select the first snapshot as the initial network, and construct a corresponding random network with an identical number of nodes and edges via the ER model. Subsequently, a specific number of actual established links are added to the real network, while an identical number of randomly generated links are added to the random network. Then we evaluate the average PageRank increase for the nodes directly incident to these added links.

Supplementary Figure 23 shows the increased proportion of PageRank value in two distinct networks as links are incrementally added. It can be seen that all real networks spanning different domains exhibit consistently stronger PageRank growth compared to random networks. The above results suggest that link formations in the real networks are not random processes, and enhancing self-importance should be a fundamental driving force for a node to update its links. This experimentally demonstrates the rationality of using the increase in node importance as the reward of node behaviors.

To demonstrate that the observed outcomes reflect genuine structural emergence rather than trivial optimization consequences, we conduct systematic negative control experiments using an alternative reward signal. Specifically, we retrain our model using the assortativity coefficient as the reward signal, which quantifies the tendency of nodes to connect with others with similar degree and serves as a plausible local structural objective.

As shown in Supplementary Fig. 24a–d, the NEMARL model with the alternative reward captures community structures (high modularity) but exhibits limitations in reproducing power-law degree distributions and small-world effect. Furthermore, Supplementary Fig. 24e–h demonstrates that the assortativity-based reward cannot match the performance of centrality-based rewards in fitting real-world networks. These limitations indicate that the suitability of a network metric as a reward signal is not arbitrary. We attribute the performance gap to the following principle: within our framework, nodes restructure their local links with the objective of improving their ability to control and access flow, which implies that only network metrics capable of capturing flow characteristics are appropriate for formulating rewards. Centrality satisfies this condition because it is grounded in implicit assumptions about how flow propagates through a network66. In contrast, assortativity characterizes degree-degree correlations among neighboring nodes and has no intrinsic connection to flow dynamics.

In summary, the rewards involved in our study can be categorized into two categories based on their objectives: rewards for specific scenarios and rewards for real-world networks. The first category defines reward inputs based on prescribed structural features, which are used to verify the framework’s ability to generate expected network structures. The second category aims to reproduce typical network features and fit real-world networks, which are designed to maximize the local utility of individual nodes rather than explicitly targeting global structural properties. For instance, when simulating scientific collaboration networks, agents are incentivized to pursue personal academic influence rather than to construct a scale-free topology. Therefore, the observed macroscopic network features arise as emergent phenomena resulting from the local-incentive-to-global-structure mechanism inherent in the NEMARL model.

The above interpretation is empirically supported by the diversity of properties exhibited by the generated networks. If the reward function is designed to explicitly encode a specific global metric (e.g., generating a power-law distribution), the model would be constrained to reproduce only that specific feature. In contrast, our model yields multiple distinct global network characteristics (e.g., scale-free, small-world effect) derived from a unified and local reward signal. This evidence confirms that the observed patterns genuinely emerge from individual utility maximization rather than from predefined structural objectives.

Centrality selection via network flow typology

Within the proposed NEMARL model, we employ node influence (centrality) as the “energy” to quantify individual node rewards. However, centrality can be quantified through various metrics, each of which captures distinct aspects of network topology. This raises a critical challenge: how to select the most appropriate centrality measure for a given domain-specific network to accurately reproduce and fit its structural properties.

To address this challenge, we propose a principled framework for selecting centrality metrics, grounded in established network theory66. According to trajectory properties, network flows can be categorized into four fundamental types: geodesics (shortest paths), paths (no repeated nodes), trails (no repeated edges), and walks (unrestricted). Crucially, these flow types not only correspond to specific real-world network domains but also determine the appropriate centrality metrics for quantifying node rewards.

In particular, Supplementary Tables 9–12 provide a comprehensive description of our centrality metric selection framework. Each table is dedicated to a distinct flow type, including: (i) definition of this network flow type; (ii) corresponding real-world network domains and their underlying rationale; (iii) recommended centrality metric; (iv) theoretical justifications for the recommended metric; and (v) relevant supporting literature.

To validate the proposed framework of centrality metric selection, we design a cross-evaluation experiment to test whether the principled centrality metric yields superior performance compared to mismatched alternatives, in terms of fitting degree distribution, modularity, clustering coefficient and APL. Specifically, guided by this framework, we select one representative network along with its corresponding principled centrality metric for each flow category: geodesics (power grid; betweenness centrality), paths (epidemic contact network; degree centrality), trails (information network; closeness centrality), and walks (collaboration network; PageRank). For each network, we train the NEMARL model using four reward functions based on the selected centrality metrics, and subsequently fit the model to various structural properties of the target network. This yields a 4 × 4 experimental matrix (4 × 4 centrality choices) on each fitting task.

Supplementary Figure 25 summarizes the results as a set of 4 × 4 heatmaps, where each row corresponds to an evaluated network, and each column represents the centrality metric used for reward calculation. Color intensity indicates the goodness of fit, with darker shades representing better performance. Across all four tasks, the results reveal a clear tendency toward diagonal dominance: in most test cases, the centrality metric matched to the underlying flow type achieves optimal or near-optimal performance within its corresponding network. Specifically, even when these diagonal entries do not universally attain the absolute maximum across all configurations, they consistently rank among the top-performing choices (typically first or second).

Notably, PageRank demonstrates robust performance across diverse network types. We analyze that this may be because walks can be interpreted as a generalized relaxation of constrained flow dynamics. Specifically, geodesics, paths, and trails can be viewed as special cases of walks with imposed node and edge restrictions. This hierarchical relationship suggests that PageRank captures the fundamental connectivity structure underlying all flow categories, which may explain its effectiveness as a robust centrality proxy across diverse network systems. These experimental results provide empirical validation for the proposed flow-based centrality selection framework. Consequently, this framework offers a principled and generalizable approach for selecting appropriate centrality measures in a wide range of networked systems.

In the above experimental evaluation of the node centrality selection framework, the framework-guided centrality metrics do not always yield optimal results. We consider that this result may be caused by the heterogeneity of network flow dynamics. In some real-world networks, flows of different categories may coexist simultaneously. For instance, in logistics networks, packages are routed along shortest paths (geodesics) for delivery, while avoiding repeated visits to the same transit hub to maximize efficiency (paths). Similarly, social networks may simultaneously host information cascades (trails) and social capital propagation (walks). In such heterogeneous scenarios, a single centrality metric may be insufficient to capture the full spectrum of node influence. Beyond this, our framework requires prior knowledge of the underlying flow type, which may not always be available in novel network domains. A more rigorous alternative should be inferring flow types directly from network structure, coupled with observed dynamical processes.

Based on the above discussions, we further investigate how to infer flow types directly from empirical behavioral data, thereby replacing the reliance on experience or intuition. Specifically, we classify diffusion trajectories by mathematically formalizing their routing characteristics. For a given trajectory, we calculate the ratio of the total node activations (A) to the trajectory length (L), alongside the shortest path distance (D) between the initial and terminal nodes. The underlying flow type of each trajectory is systematically determined as follows: (i) Geodesic flow if A/L ≈ 1 and L = D, representing strictly optimal routing; (ii) Path flow if A/L ≈ 1 but L > D, indicating non-optimal but non-overlapping routing; (iii) Trail flow if A/L > 1 with evidence of alternative path exploration; and (iv) Walk flow if A/L > 1, characterized by prominent revisiting behaviors, loops, and cyclic patterns. By aggregating and normalizing the frequencies of these categorized trajectories, we derive the proportion of each flow type. The measure dictated by our centrality selection framework for the dominant flow type is then used to simulate network evolution.

To validate this empirical extraction strategy, we compare the performance of different flow-based centrality metrics on the degree distribution fitting task using the Wikipedia clickstream dataset. Following the computational strategy described above, we obtain the proportions of geodesic, path, trail, and walk flows in the Wikipedia dataset as 0.0188, 0.0404, 0.0330, and 0.9078, respectively, indicating that the walk flow is the dominant routing mechanism within this network. As shown in the Supplementary Fig. 26, among the four centrality metrics, PageRank achieves the best fit to the real-world data. Notably, PageRank is precisely the centrality metric corresponding to the walk flow in our flow-based centrality selection framework. These results provide empirical support for the validity of our automated flow inference strategy.

Reward function optimization

The effectiveness of the proposed centrality-based reward function has been validated in terms of reproducing typical network features and fitting real networks. However, this design is not necessarily the only approach to calculating node rewards. In addition, we also develop an alternative reward function based on inverse reinforcement learning (IRL)67,68, which directly trains a neural network using real-world network data to serve as the reward function.

Supplementary Fig. 27 illustrates the framework of how to learn the reward function from real-world networks via IRL. As depicted in Supplementary Fig. 27a, the initial step is to collect expert trajectories from dynamic networks. For a dynamic network consisting of n snapshots, we first employ network representation learning to obtain a low-dimensional representation ei,t for each node i at t-th snapshot, which encodes the node’s structural and relational features. An expert trajectory for a single node is then constructed by concatenating its sequence of representations across all n snapshots. By gathering expert trajectories from different nodes across diverse networks, we construct a training dataset for the reward function.

Subsequently, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 27b, the reward function is implemented as a trainable neural network, which is optimized via an adversarial process based on the maximum-margin principle. Specifically, during the training process, we iteratively generate a series of non-expert policies through deep reinforcement learning with the learned reward function. Then, the reward function is further optimized with the objective of maximizing the margin between the expected returns of the expert policies and those of the generated non-expert policies. Through this adversarial optimization process, the learned neural network gradually captures the intrinsic behavioral patterns underlying real-world network dynamics, thereby providing a data-driven reward mechanism that governs decision-making and interactions among nodes.

In the NEMARL model, we replace the original centrality-based reward function with the IRL-based reward function learned from real-world data, and thereafter evaluate the performance of the updated model. In terms of reproducing network features, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 28, the NEMARL model with IRL-based reward successfully replicates typical network characteristics, including scale-free, small-world, assortativity, disassortativity and community structure. Regarding the tasks of fitting real-world networks, Supplementary Figs. 29–31 demonstrate that the IRL-based reward function achieves competitive results in fitting real features of both static networks (i.e., degree distribution, clustering coefficient, and average path length) and dynamic networks (i.e., degree distribution, link update quantity distribution). Compared with the centrality-based reward function, the IRL-based reward function more accurately reflects the true motivations or objectives of real-world nodes. However, the reward learning process also requires more computational resources and execution time.

To rigorously demonstrate that our framework does not rely on global network observability, we employ a localized PageRank variant to calculate node rewards. Unlike standard PageRank, which requires access to the complete network structure and performs iterative computations over the entire adjacency matrix, this local PageRank operates strictly within a node’s k-hop neighborhood (with k = 1, in our implementation). Specifically, each node estimates its centrality score by aggregating information solely from its direct neighbors through local message passing. This formulation completely eliminates the need for access to the global network topology, ensuring that the reward mechanism operates under the realistic constraint of strictly bounded local visibility.

As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 32, the NEMARL model trained using the local PageRank reward achieves performance comparable to that of the model trained with the global PageRank reward, both in reproducing macroscopic network topologies and fitting real-world data. These results demonstrate that strictly local information is sufficient to capture realistic network structures, thereby validating the core premise of our NEMARL model: the emergence of global structures from local incentives.

Topological feature reproduction. We first evaluate each model’s ability to reproduce three core topological properties: scale-free degree heterogeneity, small-world effect, and community organization. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 33, none of the alternative models is capable of reproducing all three features simultaneously. The BA model reproduces power-law degree distributions but fails to capture high clustering coefficients and generate communities. Conversely, the WS model captures high clustering coefficients and short average path lengths, and the SBM generates clear community structures; however, neither is able to reproduce power-law degree distributions. The SARL ablation reproduces small-world properties but fails to generate power-law degree distributions, while the NFU ablation produces power-law degree distributions but cannot recover small-world properties. Neither ablation variant successfully reproduces community structures. Only the full NEMARL model successfully reproduces all three topological features, which clearly demonstrates that the policy self-learning mechanism and the policy negotiation mechanism play mutually complementary roles in shaping network topology.

Real-world data fitting. We next evaluate the goodness of fit to real-world networks using three key structural metrics: degree distributions, clustering coefficients, and average shortest path lengths. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 34, all classical baseline models struggle to accurately reproduce these empirical network features. This limitation primarily stems from their reliance on fixed structural rules, which lack the adaptive behavioral capacity required to generate real-world complex topological features. The two ablation variants yield partial improvements but remain limited in scope: the SARL model captures individual adaptation but lacks inter-node coordination and negotiation, whereas the NFU model captures coordination among nodes but lacks sufficient adaptive capacity to optimize the overall network topology. In contrast, the full NEMARL model achieves the closest match to the observed network metrics, suggesting that neither policy self-learning nor policy negotiation alone is sufficient to reconstruct the complex structural properties of real-world networks.

Out-of-sample temporal prediction. To move beyond in-sample structural reconstruction, we conduct out-of-sample temporal prediction evaluations using two process-level metrics: the attachment kernel and the triadic closure rate. Specifically, the attachment kernel characterizes how the likelihood of forming new edges depends on existing local structures, while the triadic closure rate quantifies the tendency of nodes with common neighbors to establish new links over time. As fundamental microscopic indicators of network evolution, these diagnostics rigorously assess whether a model captures the generative dynamics driving future link formation.

Specifically, we optimize model parameters using sequential network snapshots up to time t − 1 and then employ the calibrated models to forecast the corresponding metric values at time t. Predictive performance is quantified via a similarity score defined as S = 1/(1 + ∣yreal − ypre∣), where yreal and ypre denote the empirical and predicted values, respectively. This score is bounded within (0, 1], with a higher value indicating greater predictive accuracy.

As shown in Supplementary Fig. 35, both classical baselines and ablation variants exhibit limited prediction capabilities. Classical models rely on fixed, predefined generative rules that break down when estimating unseen and non-stationary future states. Meanwhile, the ablation models capture only partial evolutionary mechanisms and thus fail to achieve accurate predictions at the microscopic level. By contrast, the full NEMARL model consistently outperforms all competing models on both evaluation metrics, which confirms that accurate temporal prediction requires the integration of both policy self-learning and policy negotiation.

In summary, while classical models can replicate certain network features, none can simultaneously achieve accurate topological reproduction, empirical data fitting, and out-of-sample temporal prediction. Furthermore, the ablation analysis reveals that individual self-learning and inter-node policy negotiation serve distinct yet complementary functions in driving network evolution. Consequently, we position the NEMARL model not as representing a definitive causal mechanism, but rather as providing a comprehensive and empirically grounded candidate explanation for the observed network dynamics.

NEMARL model vs. agent-based models

Agent technology is a powerful methodological tool for understanding the emergent global phenomena via simulating the individual behaviors with specific objects, which has been used to model the process of network evolution. In agent-based network models, each node’s behavior is governed by a common set of rules that dictate its interaction with other nodes. Representatively, in ref. 71, nodes form links with each other based on a logistic selection function depending on their similarity and physical distance. In ref. 72, nodes built and resolve friendships based on a utility function which incorporates the similarity between nodes and the spatial closeness of their residential locations and workplaces. In ref. 69, nodes decide whether to establish links based on six different network indicators. Game theory has been combined with agent technology to construct network models70,73,74, which optimize the strategic coordination among agents, but also bring the challenges related to limited scalability.

Compared with traditional network models, agent-based models take into account the interaction of behavioral strategies among nodes. However, they still share a common limitation with traditional models: agents lack autonomous learning capabilities. In existing agent-based network models, each agent executes an identical set of rules that remain static over time. In contrast, in our proposed NEMARL model, each agent can continuously update the Q-function used to estimate its expected utility, which improves its decision-making strategy. As a result, every agent is able to autonomously learn the optimal strategy for itself. These strategies differ across agents and evolve over time instead of remaining uniform and static.

Furthermore, we experimentally verify the learning capabilities of nodes in the NEMARL model. In this model, when a node intends to establish or delete a link, it must negotiate with the target node, and the link will be updated only after successful negotiation. Supplementary Fig. 36a illustrates the variation in the average negotiation success probability of nodes over time under four different parameter configurations during the training process. It can be seen that the negotiation success probability is relatively low at the initial time, but gradually increases until it reaches a relatively stable value over time. This phenomenon occurs because nodes continuously optimize their policies through interactions with the environment, enabling them to more accurately estimate their expected utilities and thereby increasing the probability of successful negotiation. The rise in negotiation success further leads to an increase in the mean reward obtained by nodes shown in Supplementary Fig. 36b. These results clearly demonstrate the significant learning abilities of nodes within the NEMARL model.

Communication information. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 37d, node 1 receives valuable information (e.g., states, actions) from other nodes and also transmits its own information to them. This approach effectively addresses the issues of partial observability and instability in the multi-agent environment. In the NEMARL model, the communication between nodes involves policy negotiation information.

Node utility calculation based on DQN

The data dimensionality of a complex network depends on its number of nodes, which is typically substantial. Table-based reinforcement learning methods, such as Q-learning, struggle to handle high-dimensional network data. The Deep Q-Network (DQN) integrates Q-learning with deep learning, which utilizes a deep neural network to approximate the optimal Q-function and effectively addresses the high-dimensional state space challenge. Consequently, this study employs the DQN algorithm to estimate the utility values of node actions, with specific details illustrated in Fig. 2a.

Since high-dimensional network topology data is not well-suited for direct input into the neural network, as shown in Fig. 2a, the initial step involves using network representation learning77,78 to derive the low-dimensional vector representation ui for each node i. Network representation learning aims to embed a network from its original topological space into a low-dimensional vector space while preserving a variety of structural and semantic features. Through the transformation afforded by network representation learning, network data can be applied more flexibly and efficiently to various network computation and mining tasks, such as node classification79–81, anomaly detection82–84, trajectory prediction85–87. This paper adopts the classic GraphWave algorithm88 to obtain the vector representations of nodes. However, it should be noted that our model architecture is universal and is not confined to a specific network representation learning algorithm.

Here, we take node 1 presented in Fig. 2a as an example to elucidate the utility calculation process. When node 1 intends to establish a link at time t, the inputs of its corresponding neural network \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Q}_{1}^{add}$$\end{document}Q1add are the sum of low-dimensional vectors of neighboring nodes within the observation range of node 1 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{1}^{t}=\sum {u}_{i}| i\in {o}_{1}^{t}$$\end{document}σ1t=∑ui∣i∈o1t), and the low-dimensional vector representation of a node not directly connected to node 1 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${u}_{i}| i\in V\setminus {N}_{1}^{t}(1)$$\end{document}ui∣i∈V\N1t(1)). Candidate links in these lists are ranked in descending order according to their Q-values. These lists will be used to facilitate the negotiation of behavioral policies among nodes, aiding in implementing the coordination of node actions that drive the network structure evolution.

Network structure update based on policy negotiation

This paper primarily examines the evolution process of undirected networks, where establishing or deleting a link is a bidirectional behavior requiring mutual consent from both involved nodes. From this perspective, the network evolution process can be viewed as a Markov game, where each node is a player striving to maximize its discounted reward. Game theory studies how individuals make strategic choices to achieve maximum benefits and has found widespread application in multi-agent reinforcement learning89,90. Researchers typically substitute the optimal solution with an equilibrium solution to achieve a relatively effective policy. In such equilibrium-based methods, each agent needs to interact with all other relevant agents, which significantly increases computational complexity. Therefore, these methods are unsuitable for the large-scale network environment.

Differently, in this paper, we leverage communication information to facilitate negotiation among node policies, ensuring that the behavior of the initial node can achieve the expected utility, and preventing the situation where the obtained utility is reduced to zero due to the disagreement of the target node. Compared to equilibrium-based methods, our proposed communication negotiation-based method offers enhanced scalability for the following reasons: (1) In real networks, for a node i, the policies of nodes with which i intends to establish or delete links have the most significant impact on its utility. Based on this fact, in our proposed approach, a node only needs to negotiate with a small number of target nodes. (2) The negotiation process between nodes can be executed in a parallel and distributed manner, significantly accelerating the calculation speed of the NEMARL model.

In the communication-based policy negotiation process, we define two crucial parameters for the actions of establishing links: (1) Negotiation threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha }_{i}^{add}$$\end{document}αiadd. Node i initiates a negotiation with another node j only if its utility from this action (i.e., creating a new link with node j) exceeds the threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha }_{i}^{add}$$\end{document}αiadd. This utility combines the expected Q-value and the target node’s intrinsic quality. (2) Upper limit of the number of candidate nodes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta }_{i}^{add}$$\end{document}βiadd. In real networks, there is usually an upper limit to the number of new links a node i establishes at a given time, corresponding to parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta }_{i}^{add}$$\end{document}βiadd. Specifically, when node i seeks to establish new links, it first filters out nodes with expected utilities below the threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha }_{i}^{add}$$\end{document}αiadd, and then selects top \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta }_{i}^{add}$$\end{document}βiadd nodes from the remainder for separate negotiations. During each negotiation process, node i picks a node j from the filtered candidates and sends negotiation information about establishing a new link (i, j) to j. After receiving the negotiation information, node j checks whether node i is also in its filtered top \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta }_{j}^{add}$$\end{document}βjadd node list. If so, this indicates that establishing the link (i, j) would bring satisfactory utilities to both nodes, and then the confirmation information that the link (i, j) has been successfully established will be fed back from node j to i. Otherwise, the negotiation fails, and the link (i, j) will not be established.

Moreover, we also define two parameters for the actions of deleting links: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\alpha }_{i}^{delete}$$\end{document}αidelete and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\beta }_{i}^{delete}$$\end{document}βidelete. As can be seen from above, the communication between nodes comprises only the request and feedback information for establishing or deleting links, and excludes any details about node states and Q-functions, thereby enhancing the security of our NEMARL model.

Clearly, the mechanism in our NEMARL model aligns more closely with the evolutionary characteristics of real networks.
