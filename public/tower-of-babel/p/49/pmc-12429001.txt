Strain-optimized copper dual-atom sites for selective electroreduction of carbon dioxide to ethylene
Authors: Xuewei Huang, Xinwei Li, Shuhao Yan, Dawei Wang, Chang Long, Yue Ying, Pengfei An, Zhiyu Guo, Qun Li, Caoyu Yang, Sheng Chen, Jianyu Han, Lin Chang, Siyu Lu, Zhiyong Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Electrocatalytic carbon dioxide (CO2) reduction holds the great potential to convert excess emissions of carbon footprint into high value-added chemicals, but its activity, selectivity, stability, and reproducibility are still far away from satisfactory. The molecular catalysts with precise structures are unique platform to decipher the electrocatalytic mechanism, but they usually suffer from low performance. Herein, we report a strain-optimized dual copper complex immobilized in mesoporous carbon, which exhibits remarkable ethylene (C2H4) Faradaic efficiency (FE) up to 49.9% along with a multicarbon (C2+) product’s FE up to 65.2% at −1.19 volts versus reversible hydrogen electrode. Concurrently, the catalyst displays considerable stability for 15 hours at a full cell potential of −3.1 volts. The density functional theory calculation reveals that the strain effect imposed by mesoporous carbon regulates the neighboring dual copper sites in the electrocatalyst to decrease the energy barrier of rate-determining step (*COCO → *COCOH), thus significantly promoting ethylene production.

INTRODUCTION

Powered by the sustainable and clean electricity obtained from solar light and wind power, electrocatalytic carbon dioxide (CO2) reduction has emerged as a promising carbon-neutral approach to alleviate excess emissions of the greenhouse gas (1, 2). Great efforts have been made to invent efficient electrocatalysts for promoting electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR) to suppress the competitive hydrogen evolution reduction, and different C1 products like carbon monoxide (CO) (3), methanol (CH3OH) (4), and formic acid (HCOOH) (5) as well C2+ products including C2H4, ethanol (C2H5OH), and propanol (C3H7OH) have been acquired (6, 7). Among those, to selectively synthesize C2+ products is more intriguing but challenging due to the high value and additional C–C coupling reaction (8).

To date, Cu-based materials have been widely recognized to be the most active electrocatalysts for the conversion of CO2 to C2+ products thanks to their suitable adsorption and desorption energy with the *CO intermediate (9). Generally, there are two types of Cu catalysts used in electrocatalytic CO2RR: bulk metal and molecular compound. As for bulk Cu catalysts, many parameters such as morphology (10–12) and crystal facet (13–15), grain boundary (16–18), lattice strain (19, 20), and oxidation state (21) have been demonstrated to affect the activity and selectivity of electrocatalytic CO2RR. Yet, the genuine active centers of bulk Cu for electrocatalytic CO2RR remain particularly controversial. For instance, some studies reported that oxygen plasma-activated oxide-derived Cu catalysts exhibited a high C2H4 selectivity of 60% at −0.9 V versus reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) and the presence of Cu+ species inside was the active sites (22), whereas others showed that Cu-based electrode prepared via either heat (containing Cu, Cu2O, and CuO) or electrochemical treatment [containing Cu and Cu(OH)2] was fully reduced to Cu0 that activated and converted CO2 to C2+ products (23). Even more intricately, the synergy of Cu0-Cu+ in Cu/CuSiO3 was found to simultaneously facilitate the *CO absorption and the electron transfer to boost C–C coupling (24), while Cu+ species and rich grain boundary in copper-tetracyanoquinodimethane–derived Cu electrocatalysts worked together to enhance C2H4 selectivity (18). Consequently, the interplay of theses multiple influence factors not only makes the elucidation of structure-performance correlation difficult but also reduces the reproducibility of using bulk Cu in electrocatalytic CO2RR.

Alternatively, Cu-based molecular electrocatalysts usually have the dual Cu site structure for promoting C–C coupling to attain C2+ products. Although their explicit structure endows us the opportunity to understand the activity origin in CO2RR (4, 25, 26), the Cu-based molecular electrocatalysts face substantial problems including low Faradaic efficiency (FE) and poor stability. For instance, many influence factors reported in bulk Cu do not exist in simple molecules, markedly limiting optimization of the electrocatalytic activity; besides, most Cu-based compounds prefer aggregating and transforming into Cu particles during CO2RR, resulting in the reduced stability (27, 28). However, mesoporous carbon and carbon nanotubes (CNTs) have large specific surface areas and tunable pore sizes, rendering them both excellent support materials for heterogeneous catalysis. With their expansive specific surface areas, mesoporous carbon and CNTs can efficiently disperse nanoparticles and prevent agglomeration, while their high electronic conductivity is beneficial for electrochemical applications (29, 30).

Herein, we suggest immobilizing a dual Cu complex, copper(I) 2-hydroxy-1,10-phenanthroline [Cu2(ophen)2], inside mesoporous carbon for electrocatalytic CO2RR based on below considerations. First, mesoporous carbon support not only anchors the dual Cu complex via strong π-π interactions but also offers the narrow channel to confine the key *CO intermediates (31). Second, the good conductivity of mesoporous carbon guarantees obtaining the large current density at a low working voltage, which is crucial for industrial application of electrocatalysis. Third, Cu2(ophen)2 characteristic of strong cuprophilic interactions (with Cu–Cu distance between 2.4 and 3.0 Å) is beneficial for stabilizing Cu(I) ions and improving the electronic conductivity (32, 33). Last but not least, the curved surface of mesoporous carbon enables applying additional strain field to regulate the configuration of Cu2(ophen)2. Previous studies have indicated that the surface strain could adjust the d-band center of bulk metal catalysts, thus altering the adsorption and desorption energy of the reaction intermediates at the metal active sites to improve the catalytic activity (34, 35). Nevertheless, there is still notable challenge to achieve multicarbon products in CO2RR on molecular electrocatalysts with such strain effect (29, 30).

RESULTS

Preparation, characterization, and CO2RR performance of mesoporous carbon support

The mesoporous carbon support with controlled channel diameter was prepared via emulsion assembly and subsequent thermal treatment by using poly(ethylene oxide)–poly(propylene oxide)–poly(ethylene oxide) triblock copolymers as a template, 3-aminophenol and hexamethylenetetramine (HMTA) as the carbon source, and 1,3,5-trimethylbenzene (TMB) as channel size mediator (see the details in the Supplementary Materials) (36, 37). The prepared mesoporous carbon is named as C-X-Y, where X represents the thermal treatment temperature (X = 900°, 1000°, 1100°, or 1200°C) and Y stands for the channel diameter.

Taking C-1100-4 as a typical sample, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image discloses the ordered channel arrangement with the mesopore diameter of 3.9 nm, and mesoporous carbon C-1100-4 exhibits a high degree of periodicity viewed along [100] and [110] directions, respectively (Fig. 1, A and B). Accordingly, the small-angle x-ray scattering (SAXS) patterns of C-900-4, C-1000-4, and C-1100-4 display three sharp peaks, assigned to the (110), (200), and (211) reflections of the body-centered cubic Im3¯m symmetry (Fig. 1C). Noteworthily, the very weak signals in the SAXS pattern of C-1200-4 (blue curve in Fig. 1C) suggest that the too high treatment temperature leads to the partial collapse of ordered mesoporous structure (figs. S1 and S2 and table S1). With the increased treatment temperature, the surface area estimated from the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) plot is 799.69, 832.60, 964.10, and 699.57 m2/g for the C-900-4, C-1000-4, C-1100-4, and C-1200-4, respectively (table S1), while all the C-X-4 samples maintain the narrow channel size distribution centered at ~4 nm measured by the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) method (fig. S1). Therefore, C-1100-3, C-1100-4, and C-1100-5 have uniform circular pores (figs. S2 to S4).

Apart from the mesoporous characteristics, the microstructures are investigated by powder x-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns and Raman spectra. Two broad diffraction peaks centered at 24.2° and 43.5° in the XRD pattern of C-X-4 samples are attributed to the (002) and (101) planes of graphitic carbon, respectively (fig. S5). Except for one peak at 1585 cm−1 ascribed to graphitic carbon (G band), the other broad peak at 1345 cm−1 associated with defect carbon (D band) is observed in Raman spectra (fig. S6A). Obviously, defect carbon is abundant in all the C-X-4 samples, and the gradually increased ID/IG ratio (where ID and IG are the D-band and G-band Raman intensity) manifests the incremental defect carbon with temperature increasing (38). We also note that both carbon and nitrogen are likely derived from 3-aminophenol and HMTA precursors. Nevertheless, the nitrogen content of all the C-X-4 samples is measured to be less than 1% (fig. S6B and table S2).

To determine the ideal catalytic support, we evaluated the electrolytic CO2RR performance of C-X-4 samples in the H-type three-electrode cell using a CO2- or argon (Ar)–saturated 0.1 M potassium hydrogen carbonate (KHCO3) electrolyte by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV), and possible gas and liquid products were detected by gas chromatography (GC) and proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR), respectively. As for all the C-X-4 samples, the current density at all the applied potential noticeably increases in CO2-saturated electrolyte (fig. S7), and C-1100-4 shows an exceptionally high FECO above 90% at −0.79 V versus RHE (red curve in Fig. 1D). Meanwhile, 1H NMR spectroscopy suggests no detectable amount of C2 products in the catholyte (fig. S8). Hence, C-1100-4 is chosen as the carrier of Cu2(ophen)2.

Preparation and characterization of Cu2-C-1100-4 electrocatalyst

Following the previously reported recipe (39), the deep dark Cu2(ophen)2 crystals were synthesized by solvothermal treatment of cupric nitrate trihydrate [Cu(NO3)2·3H2O], 1,10-phenanthroline, and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) mixture at 160°C. The purity of as-synthesized Cu2(ophen)2 products was proved by XRD patterns (Fig. 2A) and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time of flight mass spectrometry (fig. S9). Afterward, the Cu2(ophen)2 catalysts were immobilized into the mesoporous C-1100-4 support via a vacuum negative pressure method (40), denoted as Cu2-C-1100-4.

Structure characterization of Cu2-C-1100-4.

(A) Powder x-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of Cu2(ophen)2 and Cu2-C-1100-4. (B) SAXS patterns of C-1100-4 (black curve) and Cu2-C-1100-4 (red curve). (C) TEM and energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping images of Cu2-C-1100-4.

The XRD pattern of Cu2-C-1100-4 shows only broad peak of graphitic carbon in absence of the peaks associated with metal complex (Fig. 2A), while the SAXS analysis presents the sharp (110) peak with decreased peak intensity after infiltration of Cu2(ophen)2 (Fig. 2B). Similarly, the BET surface area of Cu2-C-1100-4 is considerably reduced from 964.10 to 379.66 m2/g after Cu2(ophen)2 adsorption. The TEM image and corresponding energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping manifest homogeneous distribution of Cu atoms (Fig. 2C) (4). Besides, we conducted etching on the catalyst Cu2-C-1100-4 by Ar+ ions, with an etching depth of 5 nm. The Cu2p content of Cu2-C-1100-4 before and after etching is identical to be 1.45 and 1.61 at %, respectively (table S3). All these results disclose that the molecular catalyst Cu2(ophen)2 is uniformly immobilized in the mesoporous carbon through π-π interactions.

To elucidate the correlation between curvature and strain, we further synthesized the mesoporous carbon of varied channel diameter. The channel size of the mesoporous carbon was controlled to be ~5 nm, denoted as C-1100-5, by adjusting the adding amount of TMB in the reaction system (see the Supplementary Materials for details). As-synthesized C-1100-5 has the uniform channel diameter of 4.9 nm and the BET surface area of 1119.87 m2/g (fig. S1E). Evidently, the curvature of the channels inside mesoporous carbon would be reduced with the channel diameter increasing from 3.9 to 4.9 nm (fig. S10).

To quantitatively compare the relative strain in the case of different curvature, the mesoporous carbon is simplified to a single-walled CNT (SWCNT) model with the specific diameter. Based on the geometric analysis, the in-plane uniaxial strain (ε) in the mesoporous carbon is deduced by equation ε=τ2ρ=τ2R+τ2=τ2R+τ , where τ is the thickness of carbon monolayer, R is the inner radius of CNT, and ρ is the radius of curvature (fig. S11) (41, 42). Assuming τ = 0.34 nm, the ε in C-1100-4 of ρ = 2.12 nm and C-1100-5 of ρ = 2.62 nm is calculated to be 8.02 and 6.49%, respectively.

The electrocatalytic CO2RR performance of C-1100-5 is examined under the same condition, which displays a peak FE of 79.8% for CO product at −0.89 V versus RHE (fig. S12). Note that the CO selectivity on C-1100-5 is slightly lower than that on C-1100-4 at the same potential. Analogously, Cu2-C-1100-3 and Cu2-C-1100-5 catalysts were prepared using the same method as Cu2-C-1100-4 (fig. S13).

Evaluation of electrocatalytic CO2RR performance

To explore the strain effect of Cu2-C-1100-3 and Cu2-C-1100-4 and Cu2-C-1100-5, the electrocatalytic activity of Cu2(ophen)2, Cu2-C-1100-3, Cu2-C-1100-4, and Cu2-C-1100-5 was compared by LSV curve in a CO2-saturated 0.1 M KHCO3 electrolyte using gas-tight H-cell, while the gas and liquid products were quantified by GC and 1H NMR spectroscopy, respectively (fig. S14).

Among the three samples, Cu2-C-1100-4 shows the largest current response (Fig. 3A). To be specific, at a low reduction potential of −0.79 V versus RHE, a FECO of 20.5 ± 0.7% is obtained on Cu2(ophen)2 (Fig. 3B), much lower than 51.9 ± 0.9% on Cu2-C-1100-4 (Fig. 3C). The dimerization of *CO intermediate has been recognized as the rate-determining step (RDS) in the formation of multicarbon products, so the high local CO concentration should benefit C2H4 production (43). As the reduction potential increases from −0.79 to −1.19 V versus RHE, compared to Cu2(ophen)2, the FEC2H4 on Cu2-C-1100-4 markedly increases to 49.9% and the corresponding partial current density reaches 7.4 mA/cm2. Meanwhile, the FE of total C2+ products is up to 65.2% at −1.19 V versus RHE. It deserves pointing out that both FE-based selectivity and turnover frequency–based activity of Cu2-C-1100-4 toward C2+ products are the top values among the reported molecular catalysts under the neutral conditions (tables S4 and S5). When the potential further moves to −1.29 V versus RHE, the FE of C2H4 product instead decreases.

The strain effect is disclosed by comparing the electrocatalytic CO2RR performance of Cu2(ophen)2, Cu2-C-1100-3, Cu2-C-1100-4, and Cu2-C-1100-5 at −1.19 V versus RHE. As the channel size of mesoporous carbon decreases from 4.9 to 3.9 nm, which corresponds to increase of strain effect from ε = 6.49 to 8.02% (fig. S11), the FEH2 decreases concomitantly from 41.6 to 21.5% at −1.19 V versus RHE (Fig. 3D), while the FEC2H4 increases substantially from 36.1 to 49.9%. As the channel size of mesoporous carbon decreases from 3.9 to 3.3 nm, which corresponds to increase of strain effect from ε = 8.02 to 9.34%, the FEC2H4 decreases from 49.9 to 34.5% (Fig. 3E and fig. S15). Notably, FEC2H4 on Cu2-C-1100-4 is 1.45 and 1.38 times greater than that on Cu2-C-1100-3 and Cu2-C-1100-5, respectively, highlighting that the optimized strain effect significantly contributes to generating C2H4 via tunable pore size (Fig. 3E).

To gain the insight into the reaction kinetics, the Tafel analysis and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy were conducted. Tafel analysis of C2H4 product displays 157.8, 97.6, and 109.8 mV/dec for Cu2, Cu2-C-1100-4, and Cu2-C-1100-5, respectively (Fig. 3F). The smallest Tafel slope of Cu2-C-1100-4 confirms that the stronger strain effect endows faster electron transfer between the molecular catalyst and the mesoporous carbon support, thus promoting the electrocatalytic CO2RR. This observation is consistent with the result obtained from Nyquist plots based on impedance spectroscopy (Fig. 3G, fig. S16, and table S6). Also, as indicated by the Koutecký-Levich plot for the Cu2-C-1100-3, Cu2-C-1100-4, and Cu2-C-1100-5 catalyst (fig. S17), diffusion mass transport does not play a substantial role. We deduce that the enhanced performance of Cu2−C-1100-4 during the CO2RR is likely mainly attributed to the strain effect.

To testify the adaptability of Cu2-C-1100-4 in the membrane electrode assembly electrolyzer, we conducted electrocatalytic CO2RR on gas-diffusion electrodes in 0.1 M KHCO3 electrolyte (fig. S18). In this configuration, the anion exchange membrane was used to separate the cathode electrode (work electrode) and the anode electrode (NiFeOx deposited Ni foam). The liquid products were collected by using a cold trap at the cathode gas outlet. The selectivity of C2H4 products increases with cell voltage, and Cu2-C-1100-4 offers a high FEC2H4 of 50.9% at a full cell voltage of −3.1 V (Fig. 3H). We further investigated the stability of Cu2-C-1100-4 catalyst for long-term CO2RR by recording the current density at the constant potential (Fig. 3I). Impressively, FEC2+ on Cu2-C-1100-4 reaches 63.4% with a total current density of around 180 mA/cm2 at −3.1 V, which shows slight decrease within 15 hours (figs. S19 to S21). This performance also represents one of the best values among the reported molecular catalysts (table S7).

In situ characterizations of activity origin of Cu2-C-1100-4 electrocatalyst

In situ attenuated total reflection–surface-enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR-SEIRAS) of Cu2-C-1100-4 catalyst was carried out to study as-reduced intermediates at the Cu2-C-1100-4 electrode. As shown in Fig. 4A, the characteristic peak at 2057 cm−1 appears at −0.79 V versus RHE and increases gradually with the potential changing from −0.79 to −1.29 V versus RHE, assigned to the C≡O stretching vibration of *CO intermediate adsorbed on Cu2-C-1100-4 in the atop-bound configuration (44, 45). Clearly, the formation of *CO offers the key intermediate to promote the C–C coupling for C2H4 production.

In addition, the local chemical state and structure of catalysts during electrocatalytic CO2RR were detected by in situ Cu K-edge x-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) and the extended x-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra. The Cu K-edge XANES of Cu2(ophen)2 and Cu2-C-1100-4 present the identical absorption edge peak at 8981.8 eV, which is attributed to the 1s → 4p transition of Cu+ species (Fig. 4B) (46, 47). Upon applying the negative potential, the absorption edge peak of Cu+ slightly shifts toward lower energy with the potential increasing (Fig. 4C), which is more clearly distinguished in the derivative curves of the XANES spectra (fig. S22). Obviously, the fraction of Cu+ species is dominant even at −1.19 V versus RHE, demonstrating that the catalyst mostly remains in Cu+ during electrocatalytic CO2RR, which is consistent with x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) result (Fig. 4D).

The Fourier-transformed (FT) k3-weighted EXAFS curves of Cu2-C-1100-4 (Fig. 4E) further manifest the main peak at 1.50 Å, which is assigned to typical Cu–N/O first shell path without phase correction. In addition, the minor peak at 2.53 Å is ascribed to Cu–Cu bond of Cu2-C-1100-4, which is distinct from Cu–Cu bond of Cu foil. The distance of Cu–N/O and Cu–Cu is then estimated by the first shell fitting (table S8). Compared with the Cu2(ophen)2, the high curvature of Cu2-C-1100-4 leads to the lengthened Cu-O/N distance and the shortened Cu–Cu distance. XPS shows that both the Cu 2p1/2 and Cu 2p3/2 peaks of Cu2-C-1100-4 shift to higher binding energies (Fig. 4F), reaching a maximum difference of 0.18 eV for Cu2-C-1100-4 (30, 48). The Cu 2p shift in Cu2-C-1100-4 can be attributed to the stronger interaction between the curved Cu2 and mesoporous carbon (30, 48). One notices that the CN of Cu–N/O in Cu2-C-1100-4 only slightly decreases from initiate 2.8 to 2.6 under −1.19 V versus RHE, implying the stable configuration of Cu2(ophen)2 in mesoporous carbon (table S8).

Theoretical insight into strain effect on high C2H4 selectivity

The explicit structure of molecular catalyst provides us the unique opportunity for understanding of the activity origin. To inspect the correlation between the strain effect and the electrocatalytic performance at the atomic and electronic level, we carried out density functional theory (DFT) calculation using implicit solvation with Vienna Ab initio Simulation Program (VASP) (49). To model the mesoporous carbon, we used a sizable C176H16 graphitic sheet that had a curvature matching with the curvature of the SWCNT of 3, 4, or 5 nm in diameter. We adopted the Cu2(ophen)2 immobilized into SWCNT as the catalyst model along with Cu2(ophen)2 and Cu2(ophen)2 on flat graphene (Cu2-G) for comparison. The CO2RR pathway on Cu2(ophen)2 and Cu2-C-1100-Y with different strain from 0 to 9.34% (Cu2-G, Cu2-C-1100-5, Cu2-C-1100-4, and Cu2-C-1100-3) was then established and optimized (figs. S23 to S25). As shown in the reaction Gibbs free energy (ΔG) diagram in Fig. 5 (A and B), the transition from *COCO to *COCOH is the RDS for C2H4 formation, and the corresponding energy barrier of Cu2, Cu2-G, Cu2-C-1100-5, and Cu2-C-1100-4 is 0.9227, 0.8636, 0.8556, and 0.8365 eV, respectively. Clearly, Cu2-C-1100-4 has the smallest ΔG value among these catalysts, revealing that the molecular catalyst with suitable strain effect could accelerate the reaction toward C2H4.

We further investigated the charge transfer between Cu2(ophen)2 and carbon support of the different strain based on the Bader charge analysis. Fig. 5C illustrates the top and side views of the optimized charge-density distribution of Cu2-G, Cu2-C-1100-5, Cu2-C-1100-4 and Cu2-C-1100-3. The enhanced molecular-substrate interaction is caused by the charge transfer from Cu2(ophen)2 to carbon support. As illustrated in table S9, the corresponding Bader charge of Cu center of Cu2, Cu2-G, Cu2-C-1100-5, Cu2-C-1100-4, and Cu2-C-1100-3 is 0.6788, 0.6840, 0.7444, 0.7528, and 0.7220, respectively. Meanwhile, the total density of states (TDOS) of Cu2-C-1100-Y catalysts were calculated (fig. S26). Noticeably, the closer TDOS gets to the Fermi energy level, the more electrocatalytic activity of Cu sites (50). The calculated partial density of states (PDOS) diagrams for the transition metal d-band center of Cu2(ophen)2 with different carbon supports are presented in fig. S27, positioning at −2.5294 eV, −2.5178 eV, −2.5120 eV, −2.5083 eV, and −2.5103 for Cu2, Cu2-G, Cu2-C-1100-5 and Cu2-C-1100-4, and Cu2-C-1100-3, respectively. The PDOS diagrams illustrate that the d-band center of Cu2-C-1100-4 is much closer to the Fermi level. This result reveals that with the strain effect increasing, the band of Cu2(ophen)2 moves to the near the Fermi level for strengthening the binding strength with intermediate, giving rise to higher activity for electrocatalytic CO2RR.

Seen from the ΔG diagram, the *COCOH species is the key intermediate for C–C coupling during electrocatalytic CO2RR. Taking Cu2-C-1100-4 as a typical sample, comparison of production rate of C2H4 product from the CO2RR conducted in H2O and D2O could provide the information regarding the nature of the RDS. The rate of coupling between two COad is expected to be not affected by switching the solvent from H2O to D2O, because it does not involve any proton transfer. However, ethylene formation rate is reduced when the CO2RR is conducted in D2O as compared to H2O (fig. S28), suggesting that the RDS involves hydrogen transfer (51). So, we scrutinized the optimized configuration of *COCOH on dual Cu sites (Fig. 5D and fig. S29). As summarized in Fig. 5E, to be specific for Cu2-C-1100-4, the neighboring Cu1-Cu2 distance is shortened to 2.5078 Å, leading to the shrinkage of *CO···*COH (1.3031 Å) distance to promote the C–C coupling for generating C2H4. With further increase of strain effect, the neighboring Cu1-Cu2 distance is shortened to 2.4826 Å. However, this compression likely leads to the mismatch of the geometric configuration of the active site, which is not conducive to the C–C coupling. Altogether, increasing strain strengths the electrostatic interaction (electron transfer) between Cu dimers and mesoporous carbon substrate, but only an optimal adjacent Cu1-Cu2 distance will contribute to the outstanding CO2RR performance.

DISCUSSION

We demonstrate that the strain effect of mesoporous carbon support is capable of regulating the configuration of the inside Cu dual atoms, thereby reducing the ΔG of the RDS (*COCO to *COCOH) to facilitate the C2+ product formation. This work extends our cognition that the fixed structure of molecular catalysts may be effectively modulated by the supports, which will greatly enrich their applications in electrocatalytic conversion of small abundant molecules including but not limited to CO2.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Chemicals and materials

Triblock poly(ethylene oxide)–b–poly(propylene oxide)–b–poly(ethylene oxide) Pluronic F-127 (PEO106PPO70PEO106, Mw = 12,600) and Nafion 117 membrane (0.183 mm thick) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. HMTA, 1,10-phenanthroline, and 3-aminophenol (>98.5%) were achieved from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co. Ltd. NaOH (99.9%), aqueous ammonia (25 to 28%), TMB, and Cu(NO3)2·3H2O were bought from Aladdin. 3-Hydroxytyramine hydrochloride was purchased from InnoChem Reagent. Tris-HCl (pH 8.8, 1 M) was bought from NOV LAND. KHCO3 (99.99%) was achieved from Macklin Reagent. Ar (99.99%) and CO2 (99.999%) were bought from Beijing Jingchengqianxi Gases Company. Ultrapure water (Millipore Milli-Q grade) was used in all experiments with a resistivity of 18.2 megohms. All chemicals and solvents were used as received without further purification unless specifically mentioned.

Material characterizations

A Hitachi SU8220 field-emission scanning electron microscope was used to record the scanning electron microscopy images. TEM images were acquired on FEI Tecnai G2 F20 electron microscope and operated at 200 kV. SAXS measurements were conducted with a XEUSS 2.0 SAXS laboratory beamline with a Cu x-ray source under 8.05 keV (λ = 1.54 Å). Powder XRD patterns were obtained by using Rigaku D/MAX-TTRIII (CBO) x-ray power diffractometer at room temperature (Cu Kα radiation, λ = 1.54 Å) at a scanning speed of 5°/min. XPS measurement was investigated by ESCALAB 250 Xi XPS instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using Al Kα as the excitation source (hν = 1486.6 eV) and operated at 15 kV and 20 mA. The C1s binding energy (284.8 eV) of the adventitious carbon was used to calibrate the binding energy of the elements in the test sample. 1H NMR surveys were conducted on AVANCE III HD 400. Raman spectra were acquired on a Renishaw inVia plus Reflex spectrometer system. The spectra were excited using a 532-nm laser source. The surface areas were calculated from the adsorption data by the BET method in a relative pressure range from 0.02 to 0.20. The pore size distributions were obtained from the adsorption branches using the BJH method.

Synthesis procedures

Preparation of mesoporous carbon

The synthesis method was based on the previous works with slight modification (36, 52). The mesoporous carbon was prepared by classical nano-emulsion assembly approach by using Pluronic F127 as a template. In a typical synthesis of mesoporous carbon, 2.20 g of Pluronic F127, 1.10 g of 3-aminophenol, and 0.70 g of HMTA were dissolved in 52 ml of water solution at room temperature under magnetic stirring to obtain a clear solution. After stirring for 1 hour, 8.0 ml of 28 wt % aqueous ammonia was added into the solution and then stirred for 1 hour at room temperature. Afterward, the resulting solution was sealed in the 100-ml pressure flask and heated at 100°C for 24 hours under stirring. Subsequently, as-made brown products were collected by centrifugation and washed with water for at least three times and then kept under vacuum at 60°C overnight to obtain the product. Last, as-synthesized products were thermally treated at 900°, 1000°, 1100°, or 1200°C for 3 hours with a heating rate of 5°C/min under Ar (C-X-Y, where X represented the thermal treatment temperature, X = 900, 1000, 1100, or 1200°C; and Y stood for the pore size of the mesoporous carbon).

Preparation of mesoporous carbon with the tunable pore size

The synthesis method was based on previous works with slight modification (37). The pore size of the mesoporous carbon was tailored by adding TMB in the reaction system. The process was similar with the preparation of C-X-4, except that TMB was added in the above solution. In this work, 2 ml of TMB was added to obtain the polymerized TMB/F127/HMTA/3-aminophenol composite products with larger pore size. Last, the obtain products were thermally treated at 1100°C for 3 hours with a heating rate of 5°C/min under Ar in a high-temperature furnace (BTF-1200-PV, Anhui BEQ Equipment Technology Co. Ltd.). The corresponding mesoporous carbon product was denoted as C-1100-5.

Synthesis of Cu2(ophen)2

Cu2(ophen)2 was prepared according to the procedure in the previous report (39). Typically, 117 mg of 1,10-phenanthroline, 120 mg of Cu(NO3)2·3H2O, and 10 ml of water were added into a 23-ml Teflon reactor under magnetic stirring for 15 min. Subsequently, 2 M NaOH solution was added into the above reactor drop by drop to adjust pH 8 to 9, then sealed, and heated at 160°C for 120 hours. After cooling, the resulting deep dark block crystals were collected by centrifugation, washed three times with ultrapure water to remove the unreacted Cu(NO3)2, and dried in 60°C oven overnight to afford the final product. The XRD pattern of as-obtained metal complexes well matched that of pure Cu2(ophen)2.

Working electrode preparation

Dopamine (2 mg/ml) was dissolved in 10 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.5), and the hydrophobic carbon paper was dipped vertically into the polydopamine solution for 2 hours. Carbon paper coated with polydopamine was then dried with nitrogen streams. In a typical preparation procedure, 5 mg of mesoporous carbon catalyst was dispersed into 450 μl of ethanol and 50 μl of water solution with the dilute aqueous solution of polydopamine (2 mg/ml) to form a homogeneous ink by ultrasonication (53). Afterward, 100 μl of well-dispersed ink was uniformly loaded onto the hydrophobic carbon paper electrode with the size of 1 cm by 1 cm. Last, the electrode was thermally treated at 1100°C for 1 hour, and the surface polydopamine was removed to obtain the self-standing C-X-Y electrode without binder.

The Cu2-C-1100-4 or Cu2-C-1100-5 electrode was prepared by vacuum infiltration method. Cu2(ophen)2 (1 mg) was dissolved in 900 μl of CHCl3 to obtain yellow solution. The C-1100-Y electrode was immersed in the above solution and was kept at the room temperature for 30 min. Then, the prepared electrode was subjected to vacuum drying in a desiccator for 30 min at room temperature. Last, the prepared electrode was dried in a vacuum oven at 60°C to remove residual CHCl3 trapped inside the mesoporous carbon support.

To prepare the Cu2 electrode, the Cu2(ophen)2 (10 mg) was dispersed in 20 μl of Nafion solution (5 wt %, as catalyst binder), 680 μl of isopropanol, and 300 μl of deionized water and then sonicated for 1 hour to promote dispersion. Last, the uniform ink was directly coated on a glassy carbon electrode or hydrophobic carbon paper and dried overnight in a vacuum oven.

Au plating solution for chemical deposition gold film electrodes

The synthesis method was based on previous works with slight modification (24, 54). First, 154.4 mg of HAuCl4·4H2O and 61.1 mg of NaOH were dissolved in 3 ml of aqueous solution, and the solution turned from yellowish to orange-red. The orange-red solution became opaque when sonicated for 1 hour. Na2S2O3·5H2O (310.1 mg), NH4Cl (67.0 mg), and Na2SO3 (473.4 mg) were dissolved in 25 ml of H2O, which was then added in the above solution. The final solution changed from yellowish to transparent after continuous ultrasonication for 2 hours and standing overnight.

Preparation of Au film electrodes

Au thin films were prepared by chemical deposition procedure (54). The surface of Si prism electrode was etched by immersing in aqua regia solution. Subsequently, the Si electrode was polished on abrasive finishing machine with alumina pastes and then cleaned with ultrapure water. As-cleaned Si electrode was soaked in Piranha solution ( VH2SO4:VH2O2 = 3:1) for 15 min to remove the oxide layer and the surface became hydrophilic. Then, the Si electrode was immersed in 40% NH4F solution for 3 to 5 min to create a hydrophobic surface. After etching, the Si prism electrode was rinsed with ultrapure water. Last, the Si prism electrode was immersed in 136 μl of hydrogen fluoride‌‌ (HF) and 8 ml of Au plating solution at 50°C for 10 to 12 min to grow Au film, rinsed with deionized water, and dried in air. The catalysts ink was dropped on the Au-coated Si prism electrode.

In situ ATR-SEIRAS

A three-electrode Jackfish SEC spectroelectrochemical cell from PIKE Technologies was used for in situ ATR-SEIRAS tests (fig. S30). Au film electrode coated with catalysts was used as working electrode. Ag/AgCl (3.5 M KCl solution) electrode and Pt wire were served as the reference electrode and the counter electrode, respectively. Nicolet iS50 FT-IR Thermo Fisher Scientific spectrometer was equipped with the PIKE VeeMAX III variable angle ATR accessory and a MCT detector cooled with liquid nitrogen. We also collected the background in CO2-saturated 0.1 M KHCO3 without applied potential. In situ ATR-SEIRAS of Au film without catalysts was carried out to clarify the influence of the gold film layer. In situ ATR-SEIRAS spectra were recorded in CO2-saturated 0.1 M KHCO3 from open-circuit potential to −1.29 V versus RHE. All spectra were recorded with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 scans.

Electrochemical measurement

Electrochemical reduction was conducted using a CHI 660E (Shanghai CH Instrument Co. Ltd.) in a gas tight H-cell separated by cation-exchange membrane (Nafion 117). The reference electrode and the counter electrode were Ag/AgCl (in 3.5 M KCl solution) electrode and Pt gauze, respectively. A 0.1 M KHCO3 (pH 6.8) aqueous solution was prepared and purged with CO2 gas prior to electrolysis.

The gas products in cathodic chamber were directly analyzed by an online gas chromatograph (Shimadzu GC-2014C) at a flow rate of 25 ml/min. The gas products were detected and analyzed in a thermal conductivity detector and a flame ionization detector. The 1HNMR (Bruker AVANCE III HD 400) spectrum was measured with water suppression using a presaturation method to determine the liquid product. Before measurement, 0.9 ml of 0.1 M KHCO3 catholyte solution was mixed with 0.1 ml of D2O and 2.0–part-per-million (ppm) dimethyl sulfoxide of internal standard. In addition, all electrode potentials measured with an Ag/AgCl reference electrode were converted to potentials versus the RHE by the following equationE(versus RHE)=E(versus Ag/AgCl)+0.2046V+0.059V×pH

The FE of the gas products was calculated by the GC data using the following equationFEg=ppm×flow rate×nF×Patm×(RT)−1×Jtotal−1×100%where ppm was obtained directly from GC data, the CO2 flow rate was measured by a mass flow controller (Beijing FLOWS instruments Co. Ltd), n was the electron transfer number required to form the gas products (n = 2, 2, 2, 8, 12, 12, and 18 for H2, CO, HCOOH, CH4, C2H4, C2H5OH, and C3H7OH, respectively), F was the Faradaic constant (96,485 C/mol), Patm was the pressure, R was molar gas constant [R = 8.314 J/(mol∗K)], T was temperature (T = 298.15 K), and Jtotal was the total current density at an applied potential.

ECSA evaluation

The electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) of an electrode was evaluated by measuring the double-layer capacitance. The cyclic voltammetry (CV) scans were performed in the potential ranging from −0.09 to 0.11 V versus RHE, where no Faradaic process occurred, in 0.1 M KHCO3 aqueous solution saturated with Ar. The scan rates were 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 120, 160, and 200 mV/s. The double-layer charging currents between the anodic and cathodic current densities at selected potential of 0.01 V versus RHE were then plotted versus the CV scan rate, and the slope of the linear regression provided the double-layer capacitance.

In situ XAS

X-ray absorption spectra (XAS) was implemented at room temperature at beamline 1W1B of Beijing Synchrotron Radiation Facility (BSRF) using a Si(111) double-crystal monochromator to record data. BSRF’s electron storage ring was operated at 2.5 GeV with a maximum beam current of 250 mA in decay mode. An ionization chamber filled with inert gas was used for measurement in transmission and fluorescence modes. The preionization chamber absorbed 20%, the postionization chamber absorbed all, and the signal-to-noise ratio was optimal. The absorption edge of the standard copper foil (edge energy, 8979 eV) was used to calibrate the x-ray energy for each measurement. The Cu K-edge spectrum was collected by transmission mode for ex situ experiment and fluorescence mode for in situ experiment.

The sample and an appropriate amount of boron nitride were ground into fine particles with a mortar and pressed into thin slices with a tablet press for transmission mode measurement. The working electrode was prepared by coating electrocatalysts on the thin carbon paper with a thickness of 500 nm. The working electrode was installed onto a custom-designed in situ XAS backscattering fluorescence electrochemical cell. Pt mesh and Ag/AgCl (in 3.5 M KCl solution) were used as counter electrode and reference electrode, respectively. CO2 was injected under in situ measurement, and the spectra were recorded at different constant potentials in 0.1 M KHCO3. Note that, during in situ measurements, each data collection began after the current had been constant for ~5 min following applying specific potential. It took 11.9 min to collect a full x-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectrum of desirable quality with an energy range from −200 to +780 eV (k ~ 15 Å−1) around the Cu K-edge (8979 eV).

The obtained XAFS data were processed in Athena (version 0.9.26) for background, pre-edge line and post-edge line calibrations. Then, FT fitting was carried out in Artemis (version 0.9.26). The k3-weighted, k-range of 3 to ~12 Å−1 and R range of 1 to ~3 Å were used for the fitting of Cu foil; k-range of 3 to ~11 Å−1 and R range of 1 to ~3 Å were used for the fitting of Cu samples. The four parameters, coordination number, path length, Debye-Waller factor, and E0 shift (CN, R, σ2, ΔE0, respectively) were fitted without anyone was fixed, constrained or correlated.

Theoretical calculations

All the spin theoretical simulations through DFT in our work were performed on the VASP with the version 5.4.4 (55, 56). The generalized gradient approximation with the Perdew-Burke-Emzerhof functional form was used to evaluate the electron-electron exchange and correlation interactions, while the projector augmented wave methods were implanted to represent the core-electron (valence electron) interactions (57–59). Plane-wave basis function was set with a kinetic cutoff energy of 400 eV.

Following the previous reports, the mesoporous carbon was simplified to a SWCNT model with the specific diameter (60). The ground-state atomic geometries were optimized by relaxing the force below 0.02 eV/Å, and the convergence criteria for energy were set with the value of 1.0 × 10−5 eV per cell. Only Gamma points were considered in our simulations. Gaussian smearing was used for the both electronic structures and total energy of our models and stress/force relaxations. To better describe intermolecular interactions, van der Waals interactions were estimated by DFT-D3 method of Grimme (61). Solvation effect was considered in this work by using implicit solvation model with the dielectric constant was set at 78.4 (49). In aqueous solution, the electroreduction of CO2 to produce C2H4 was carried out through the following eight basic steps:

1) *COOH + H+ + e− → *CO + H2O;

2) 2*CO + e− → *COCO;

3) *COCO + H+ + e− → *COCOH;

4) *COCOH + H+ + e− → *CCO + H2O;

5) *CCO + 2H+ + 2e− → *CHCOH;

6) *CHCOH + H+ + e− → *CH2COH;

7) *CH2COH + 3H+ + 3e− → *C2H4 + H2O;

8) *C2H4 → C2H4.

Free energy under the standard condition (298.15 K) was calculated by the computational hydrogen electrode model (62). ΔG was calculated by the equation of ΔG = ΔE + ΔEZPE − TΔS + ∫CPdT, where the ΔE was the difference energy between each reaction step, ΔEZPE was the difference of the zero-point energy between each reaction step, ΔS was the difference of vibrational entropy between each reaction step, ∫CPdT was the enthalpic correction, respectively.
