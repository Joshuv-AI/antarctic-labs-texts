Critical care transfers of ventilator-dependent patients from operating theatres to Critical Care Units in a South African Metropole
Authors: Esther Cloete, Jacobus J Badenhorst, Anthony R Reed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVentilated patients in the operating theatre who need transfer to other facilities, can contribute to delays and inefficencies in surgical and theatre resources. In 2018, an interhospital retrieval service was established to provide transfers between state hospitals in the Cape Town Metropole exclusively for intubated, post-operative patients. This study aims to describe the utilisation of the retrieval system and gain insights into retrieval patterns.MethodsThis retrospective observational study analysed all post-operative patients using the retrieval system between state hospitals in the Cape Town Metropole from July 2018 – December 2021, as recorded in an existing SPRINTT-OR registry.ResultsA total of 291 patients were included. Most patients retrieved were male (n = 186, 63.9 %), with a mean age of 34.95 (S ± 13.59). The median time interval from request to ambulance arrival was 61 min. Most patients (n = 184, 63.2 %) were transferred from a single Large Metro District Hospital (LMDH). Trauma surgery was the most common diagnostic category (n = 123, 42.3 %), followed by infective causes (n = 79, 27.1 %) and Obstetrics & Gynaecology (n = 52, 17.9 %).ConclusionThis study provides insight into the retrieval needs of ventilated, post-operative patients across different healthcare facilities in an African metropolitan area who are transferred with a dedicated, specialised transfer service. It describes the system's use and provides insight into patient demographics, diagnoses, and transfer timing, as well as the role of Emergency Medical Services (EMS) in optimising the functioning of a healthcare system.Keywords: Interfacility transfer, Critical care retrieval service, Emergency medical service, Transfer systems

Introduction

Background

Surgical conditions account for approximately 33 % of the global disease burden [1]. Access to safe and timely surgical care in Africa remains limited [2,3]. Lack of access is especially pronounced in resource-limited contexts, where specialised professionals and health technology resources needed to provide high levels of critical care transfers are limited [4]. Across South Africa, Critical Care Units (CCUs) remain a scarce resource, yet the demand is increasing [5,6]. Western Cape anaesthesiologists identified a demand for transferring intubated, ventilated post-operative, critically ill patients from institutions without CCU facilities or insufficient CCU beds to higher-level healthcare facilities that can provide critical care. (the author's personal involvement in the system) Patients accepted for admission to a CCU, and who are already intubated and ventilated in theatre, while awaiting transport to the receiving CCU, then receive critical care management in the operating theatre. These ventilated patients awaiting transfer to another facility's CCU contribute to the delay of surgical procedures for other patients awaiting emergency and elective surgery. A recent analysis of critical care retrievals (CCR) in South Africa's private healthcare sector revealed that post-surgical patients accounted for 1.2 % of the diagnoses requiring critical care retrievals (CCR) [7]. However, there is a lack of local data on critical care retrievals of postoperative patients within the state health sector.

In South Africa, the availability of CCU beds in state hospitals – either in intensive care units (ICUs) or high-care units (HCUs) – is significantly lower than in private-sector hospitals [5]. Approximately 77 % of state hospitals in the country lack critical care facilities, while only 16 % of private sector hospitals do [5]. In the Western Cape, all regional and central hospitals have CCUs. Some large district hospitals have historical CCUs, but the new Large Metro District Hospitals (LMDHs) were built explicitly without them.

The absence of critical care beds increases morbidity and mortality among critically ill patients. This was especially evident during the COVID-19 pandemic, when the demand for ICU beds exceeded the available critical care capacity, resulting in increased mortality rates [8]. The Western Cape is considered one of the wealthier provinces in South Africa, with 27.8 % of individuals in the Cape Town metropolitan area having health insurance – the second-highest rate among metropolitan areas in the country [9]. Despite this, the majority still rely on state health services, highlighting the importance of addressing patient needs in the state health setting.

Available data shows that the average time interval to retrieve a patient requiring CCR to a CCU from a state hospital without a CCU in the Western Cape is 2.1 h [5]. Response time intervals in the Eastern Cape were the longest, averaging 6.5 h. In stark contrast, response time intervals in the private sector across all provinces averaged less than an hour [5].

Until 2018, the adult critical care retrievals were managed by any available Advanced Life Support (ALS) crew. The concerns were that this service was not time-sensitive and lacked the appropriate invasive monitoring equipment and infusion devices.

When SPRINTT-OR (Specialised Paediatric Retrieval Including Neonatal Transfer Team – Operating Room) was established in 2018, a critical care retrieval service (CCRS), SPRINTT (Specialised Paediatric Retrieval Including Neonatal Transfer Team), with ALS providers, was already operational for paediatric patients within the larger Western Cape Emergency Medical Services (EMS) service. Still, there was no established adult retrieval service. The SPRINTT-OR transfer service was established through an agreement between the SPRINTT critical care retrieval service and several health institutions in the Cape Town Metropole. Following the agreement, several scenario-based simulations were performed between facilities with several crews to identify challenges and optimise the service. The SPRINTT-OR service is dedicated to the interfacility transfer of already intubated, ventilated post-operative patients from the operating theatre or its recovery area to a higher level of critical care, where they have already met the admission criteria and have a confirmed bed for admission. The service has a targeted arrival interval time at the operating theatre of 30 min from the request. If this target is not met, the request is escalated.(Refer to supplementary material A)

A registry, approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the University of Cape Town, has been established to maintain a database of all recorded SPRINTT-OR transfers since 2018. No data on critical care retrievals for post-operative patients is available for comparison before implementing the SPRINTT-OR service. This study aims to describe the utilisation of retrievals and provide insights into retrieval needs and patterns in a resource-limited setting.

Methods

A retrospective, observational data analysis was performed on all patients transferred with the SPRINTT-OR system between July 2018 and December 2021. The data for the analysis was obtained from the SPRINTT-OR registry, which includes transfer data from various state hospitals in the Cape Town Metropole. According to the latest national census, the referring hospitals provide primary and secondary level care to >4,7 million people in the Cape Town Metropole [10]. The data included in the registry were collected by reviewing the original SPRINTT-OR paper transfer form (refer to supplementary material B), completed by the attending health practitioner requesting the transfer. All data was anonymised at extraction to a password-protected Microsoft Excel registry. The ongoing registry received approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the University of Cape Town (HREC reference number: R022/2021).

Whilst the critical care retrieval services are an important component of EMS, our analysis included only ventilated, post-operative patients who had been accepted for CCU admission and therefore met the criteria for the SPRINTT-OR service, and were transferred with the SPRINTT-OR team between July 2018 and December 2021. It is essential to note that the SPRINTT-OR agreement does not apply to patients awaiting surgery or to those receiving critical care outside of the theatre, including CCUs, surgical wards, and emergency centres. All patients retrieved using SPRINTT-OR are already accepted for direct admission to a CCU at the receiving hospital.

Data extracted from the registry revealed various characteristics, including age, sex, diagnosis requiring surgery, inotropic requirements during retrieval, time of retrieval request, and the duration from request to SPRINTT-OR team arrival in the theatre. The data was summarised using simple descriptive statistics. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean [standard deviation], while variables that did not follow a normal distribution were presented as median [interquartile range]. Distribution normality was assessed visually using graphs and appropriate statistical tests. Categorical variables were summarised as counts and percentages. SPSS Statistics software was used to process all data.

Results

Demographics

Table 1 provides an overview of the demographics of all patients who were ventilated and transferred under the SPRINTT-OR protocol. The total number of patients requiring interfacility transfer from an operating theatre and meeting the criteria for the SPRINTT-OR service in the Cape Town Metropole was 291. Among these patients, 63.9 % were male. 81,1 % of all male patients were 45 years of age or younger. The data for patient age followed a normal distribution (Skewness: 0.57; Kurtosis: 0.19), with a mean age of 34.95 (SD ± 13.60). The mean age for trauma patients decreased to 31 (SD ± 10.85).

Time interval to arrival

The median time interval from retrieval request to SPRINTT-OR team arrival in the operating theatre was 61 min (IQR, 45–105 min).

Diagnosis

The most common diagnostic categories were Trauma (42.3 %), Infection & Sepsis (27.1 %), and Obstetrics & Gynaecology (17.9 %). Among the Obstetrics & Gynaecology transfers, 54.9 % were due to bleeding-related complications, 25,5 % due to eclampsia and 7,8 % due to anaesthetic complications. Among the 291 patients, 10 (3.4 %) anaesthetic complications were reported, which included three cases of aspiration, two instances of high spinal blocks and isolated cases of bronchospasm and anaphylaxis. One suspected case of malignant hyperthermia was also reported. The remaining two cases were documented as anaesthetic complications relating to sepsis and trauma.

Time of day & day of the week

After-hour retrieval requests (16:00 – 08:00) accounted for 66.4 % of all retrieval requests. The lowest number of retrievals occurred between 04:00 and 10:00. Most retrievals took place on Fridays (19.5 %), while the fewest occurred on Saturdays (10.4 %).

Resuscitative requirements

Only in version 3 of the transfer form did we add the requirement for documenting the administration of adrenaline and packed red blood cells. Of the 173 subsequent transfers in 2020 and 2021, 77 patients (44.5 %) required an adrenaline infusion at the time of transfer, and 111 (64.1 %) received at least one unit of packed red blood cells before or during transfer.

Missing data

Missing or incomplete data accounted for <3.5 % of all categories, except for the age category, which had 5.5 % missing data.

Discussion

A total of 291 post-operative patients requiring ventilation were transferred by the service between 2018 and 2021. MPH initiated most transfers as the facility of origin. At the same time, GSH was the most frequent receiving facility—the most common diagnoses related to trauma, infection and obstetrics & gynaecology, with most patients being male, with a median age of 35 years.

Utilisation of the service

For state hospitals in South Africa that lack critical care services, most patients are transferred without the presence of critical care-trained emergency medical personnel [5,11]. Based on the experience of seasoned advanced life support providers routinely involved in CCR in the Gauteng province of South Africa, there is a lack of common understanding of critical care retrievals [12], despite a precise definition of CCR[13]. It was also highlighted that implementing clear inclusion and exclusion criteria for patient eligibility could significantly improve the allocation and optimisation of the limited CCR resources [12].

In light of this, the SPRINTT-OR retrieval system serves a specific purpose across the Cape Town Metropole by providing CCR specifically for intubated, post-operative patients, as evidenced by the number of patients from local hospitals who qualify for and utilise the system. The SPRINTT-OR service aims to mitigate confusion related to the appropriateness of the CCR by employing a specific protocol and referral criteria. The need to improve referral protocols for patient transport in the Western Cape has previously been highlighted [4]. This service model has probably improved timely emergency care across the Cape Town Metropole for the intubated, post-operative patient population requiring interfacility transfer.

Time interval to arrival

The median ambulance request-to-arrival interval of 61 min represents an improvement compared to the previous audit on critical care resources in South Africa, which reported an average time interval of ambulance arrival at the referring facility of 2.1 h in the Western Cape [5]. The COVID-19 pandemic emphasised the significance of theatre backlogs in Africa and South Africa [14]. Any reduction in the CCRS time interval to arrival for transferring patients already receiving post-operative critical care in an operating theatre has the potential to save money and valuable theatre time. This could improve outcomes for patients being transferred promptly and those awaiting theatre on emergency and elective theatre lists. Only one peer-reviewed costing model is available for operating theatre costs in South Africa [15]. Based on a secondary-level state sector hospital in the Western Cape, the model estimates theatre cost at approximately R64/min when using a theatre utilisation factor of 50 %. The financial implication, however, is only one aspect of the two-legged cost of theatre time. Opportunity cost is the potentially more costly aspect that needs to be addressed. When a patient awaiting transfer receives critical care in the operating theatre, the theatre remains inaccessible until the retrieval team arrives, primarily attributable to constraints in staff and resources. The managers of the emergency medical response service identified additional capacity available in the relatively protected SPRINTT service. They felt that the opportunity costs favoured using that service, along with their equipment.

The facility of origin and receiving facilities

Most patients (63.2 %) were transferred from MPH, a district hospital serving a population of over 600,000 without a CCU [16]. KDH, another prominent district hospital serving a catchment area of >400,000 people and lacking a CCU, ranked second in the number of transfers [16].

It is worth noting that district or regional hospitals with a CCU, albeit limited in capacity (VH - three HCU beds, two ventilated; NSH - four HCU beds, three ventilated), required significantly fewer transfers to tertiary institutions. In addition to reduced transfer requests, these hospitals occasionally alleviate strain on the healthcare system by providing HCU beds to intubated patients from other district/regional hospitals when tertiary receiving CCU facilities are unavailable. A disproportionate number of transfers originate from MPH. Further research comparing the cost of establishing a critical care facility at MPH to the cost of theatre time and utilisation of CCR resources would provide valuable insights. Any decision in this regard would have to consider that the Western Cape healthcare system is a tiered system structured into different levels of care [17]. Although MPH is a level 1 or large metro district hospital (LMDH) that typically does not have a CCU, several of Cape Town's LMDHs already have CCUs, and health system managers would need to consider these factors.

Patient profile

Most transferred patients were male (63.9 %), which aligns with previous data from transfers in the Western Cape [4]. The majority of male patients were young and required surgery for trauma-related indications. These findings are consistent with other available African data [18].

Diagnostic category

Some 87 % of the total transfers were attributed to three primary diagnostic categories: Trauma, Infection & Sepsis, and Obstetric & Gynaecological surgery.

Trauma surgery was the most prevalent diagnostic category. Trauma disproportionately affects populations in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), with over 90 % of global injury-related deaths occurring in these populations [19]. An ongoing large, multicentre epidemiological study on trauma patients in the Western Cape will be valuable in interpreting trauma patient outcomes. It will contribute to the discussion regarding the possible establishment of critical care beds at high-acuity district health facilities in the Western Cape [20].

Obstetric and Gynaecological surgeries represented a substantial portion of the transfers, aligning with the global rise in caesarean section deliveries [21]. In the Western Cape, a well-established four-tiered maternity care system is in place, with the majority of deliveries taking place at district health facilities. At the same time, high-risk pregnancies are referred to appropriate levels of care [22]. While precise risk stratification guidelines exist for this demographic, the need to transfer patients initially classified as low-risk to higher-level facilities with critical care capabilities remains an unexpected occurrence, as indicated by the results. This includes transfers due to bleeding complications and unbooked obstetric patients presenting with eclampsia. Anaesthetic adverse events were also more prevalent in the obstetric group (7,8 %) compared to the prevalence in all patients transferred (3,4 %).

Recognising the role of anaesthetic complications in prompting transfers (3.4 %) is essential. However, it is difficult to comment on its significance without data on whether the interhospital transfer was requested primarily because of the surgical diagnosis or as a direct effect of an intraoperative anaesthetic complication.

Patient condition

Post-operative, intubated patients transferred with the SPRINTT-OR service require respiratory and potentially other organ support, as indicated by the qualifying criteria for the service. It is unsurprising to see that almost half of the patients (44.5 %) required an adrenaline infusion during transport, confirming the critical clinical condition of these patients. The high requirement for blood products can largely be attributed to the prevalence of trauma patients, septic patients and bleeding obstetric patients requiring emergency surgery.

Referral patterns

Two-thirds of transfers occurred after regular working hours (08:00–16:00). A noticeable reduction in transfers was observed from 04:00 to 10:00, likely originating from multiple factors, including staff shift changes, limited operational theatres during this period, and the commencement of morning ward rounds. In the data, no particular patterns were observed regarding the day of the week when transfers were requested. This is inconsistent with the known patterns of trauma, which often occur predominantly over weekends [4]. Transfers were also evenly spread throughout the days of the month, with no observed peak.

Limitations

This retrospective study relies solely on available data collected through manually completed transfer forms, with the attendant risks of human error. Some missing data may be present, but efforts were made to minimise this, and any impact is considered negligible. The study primarily reflects a metropolitan context, covering approximately two-thirds of the Western Cape population. Generalisability is limited, particularly in rural areas where transfer patterns and disease profiles may differ. The SPRINTT-OR service is solely utilised by ventilated, post-operative patients receiving critical care in a theatre and represents a specific cohort of the broader patient population requiring CCR.

Conclusion

This study appeared to demonstrate an improvement in the median ambulance arrival time interval, and therefore, likely improved the availability of emergency operating theatres across the metropolitan area. It highlights the importance of a functional EMS supporting the optimal use of the broader healthcare system. Further audits and evaluations of the retrieval service model are necessary to enhance its fidelity and utility for future use, both locally and across Africa. Future studies should quantify the cost of critical care retrievals and the amount of theatre time lost when post-operative patients receive critical care in the operating room. This should be compared to the cost of establishing a critical care facility at hospitals with the highest utilisation of the CCRS.

Dissemination of results

We have used the data, through communication in meetings in our Metro West Technical Work Group, to lobby for the need for high care at MPH, especially for the trauma sub-group of patients, who seldom need complete intensive care treatment. This has placed the issue of transferring resources and establishing a new high-care unit high on the district leadership's agenda.
