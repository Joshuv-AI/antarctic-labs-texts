Malarial pneumonopathy in Malawian children with cerebral malaria
Authors: Hunter J. Wynkoop, Michael Lintner-Rivera, Terrie Taylor, Karl B. Seydel, Paul Pensulo, Mubarack Ayamie, Karen Chetcuti, Lorenna L. Moreira Vidal, Nicole F. O’Brien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRespiratory distress is a well-recognized clinical presentation in pediatric malaria and is associated with increased mortality. Traditionally described as “acidotic breathing,” this phenomenon has been attributed to compensatory hyperventilation secondary to metabolic acidosis. However, this explanation may not adequately capture causative mechanisms. We sought to quantify the prevalence of respiratory distress in children with cerebral malaria (CM) and to evaluate underlying pathophysiologic contributors.MethodsIn this prospective, observational study, 97 Malawian children aged 6 months to 12 years meeting the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria for CM were enrolled. Vitals were recorded, including oxygen saturation and oxygen supplementation when present. Clinical signs of respiratory distress were documented on admission using WHO definitions. Admission lactate levels and blood gas analyses were obtained to characterize acid–base status. A standardized 12-zone point-of-care lung ultrasound was performed to assess pulmonary aeration patterns.ResultsTwenty-seven participants (28%) met WHO-defined criteria for respiratory distress. Despite elevated lactate levels (median 7.0 mmol/L [4.2, 13.3]), median pH remained 7.41. Metabolic acidosis was the sole disturbance in only 12 children (12%). A primary respiratory alkalosis was observed in 33% of the cohort, and lung ultrasound revealed subcentimeter consolidations in 88% of all participants. Larger consolidations (≥ 1 cm) consistent with pneumonia were less common. Absence of diffuse edema on lung ultrasound made cardiac or renal failure an unlikely primary cause of respiratory distress. No child met criteria for pediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome. Respiratory distress was not associated with increased mortality in this cohort.ConclusionsOur findings challenge the longstanding attribution of respiratory distress in CM to metabolic acidosis alone. The frequent occurrence of a primary respiratory alkalosis, together with the near-universal presence of subpleural consolidations on lung ultrasound, suggests that alternative mechanisms—including pulmonary microvascular obstruction, interstitial inflammation, and dysregulation of the central respiratory drive—are likely contributing to the observed respiratory patterns. We propose the terminology malarial pneumonopathy to describe this broader spectrum of lung involvement. Reframing the traditional concept of “acidotic breathing” as malarial pneumonopathy may better reflect the spectrum of respiratory dysfunction in CM, improve diagnostic accuracy, and guide future research, clinical guidelines, and interventions.

Background

Malaria is responsible for the death of more than 1,000 children every day. Most of these deaths occur in sub-Saharan Africa and are secondary to Plasmodium falciparum [1]. Cerebral malaria (CM), severe malarial anemia (SMA), and malaria with respiratory distress are the most severe complications of P. falciparum malaria [2]. The presence of respiratory distress at admission in those with CM or SMA significantly increases mortality rates compared to those without respiratory symptoms [2–8]. Respiratory distress in pediatric patients with malaria has historically been attributed to a compensatory response to metabolic acidosis and is often referred to as “acidotic breathing” [9, 10]. However, this explanation fails to consider impaired pulmonary function previously described or abnormalities in the pulmonary vasculature observed during post-mortem examination [11, 12]. The increased mortality associated with respiratory distress in malaria mandates further investigations to better understand the mechanisms leading to this presentation.

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines respiratory distress in children with malaria as the presence of nasal flaring, intercostal indrawing, marked indrawing of the lower chest wall, or deep (acidotic) breathing [10]. Using this definition or other subjective descriptions, an estimated 16–40% of children with malaria present with respiratory distress [13–16]. Our objectives were to quantify the prevalence of respiratory distress using the WHO clinical definition and elucidate pathophysiologic contributors to respiratory distress in children with CM.

Materials and methods

This was a prospective, observational study of 97 Malawian children 6 months to 12 years of age with CM. All children were admitted to the Pediatric Research Ward at Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital in Blantyre, Malawi from March 2021 to May 2024. Children were eligible if they met the case definition of CM (Blantyre Coma Score (BCS) of ≤ 2, peripheral parasitemia with P. falciparum, and no other detectable cause of encephalopathy) as defined by the WHO. Exclusion criteria included shock, advanced human immunodeficiency virus, severe malnutrition, and recent head trauma. Eligible children were enrolled consecutively during the local malaria season when a study team member trained in point-of-care ultrasound was most likely available. No additional selection criteria were applied. All children had CM as this is the population routinely admitted to the research ward and represents the severe malaria syndrome with the highest mortality, allowing for standardized admission assessments and treatment bundles in a clinically high-risk group.

On admission to the Pediatric Research Ward, patients underwent a detailed clinical assessment. Vital signs and physical exam were documented on an established case report form. Signs of respiratory distress consistent with the WHO bedside clinical classification of severe malaria in high transmission areas were recorded: nasal flaring, subcostal retractions, chest indrawing, and deep breathing. An oxygen saturation to fraction of inspired oxygen ratio (SpO2/FiO2) was calculated based on admission oxygen saturation and oxygen requirements. A ratio < 264 was considered consistent with pediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome (pARDS) [17, 18].

Admission laboratory values, including lactate (Arkray Lactate 111 Pro 2, Minneapolis, MN) and glucose (Accucheck, Roche, USA), were evaluated using point-of-care machines. If present, hypoglycemia was corrected prior to enrollment, and children were enrolled only after glucose stabilization, ensuring they met the case definition of CM. P. falciparum parasitemia was determined by microscopic evaluation of Field’s-stained thick smears of peripheral blood. A capillary or venous blood gas including pH, carbon dioxide, bicarbonate, and base excess was obtained using an i-STAT machine (Abbott; Princeton, New Jersey). We applied Winter’s formula (Fig. 1) to assess whether a participant was appropriately compensating for metabolic acidosis when the pH was < 7.35 or if the child met the WHO criteria for metabolic acidosis – a base deficit of > 8 mEq/L, a plasma lactate ≥ 5 mmol/L, or a bicarbonate < 15 mmol/L [9, 19].Fig. 1Winter’s formula and application

A protocolized lung ultrasound examination was performed immediately following admission using a Butterfly iQ + ultrasound device (Butterfly Network Inc; Palo Alto, CA). Lung ultrasounds were performed by one of three pediatric intensivists with point-of-care ultrasound certification through the Society of Critical Care Medicine or two additional study team members trained by these intensivists. The pediatric lung preset and longitudinal views were used to complete the 12-zone examination. The anterior and lateral chest wall were divided, and imaging was obtained bilaterally in a parallel plane utilizing superior and inferior views. The worst ultrasound pattern observed in each zone was recorded and used to calculate the sum of the scores with a maximum total score of 36.

Additionally, an ultrasonic cardiac output monitor (USCOM, version 1.8.0., USCOM Ltd. Sydney, Australia) study was performed, and the cardiac index of participants was recorded. Participants also underwent a brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)(0.064-T Hyperfine Swoop® Guilford, Connecticut, USA) to evaluate the Brain Volume Score. Increased brain volume is an imaging finding associated with fatal CM and is rated on a scale from 1 to 8. A score of 1 or 2 = brain atrophy, 3 = no edema (normal volume), 4 or 5 = mild edema with no or localized loss of sulcal/cisternal markings, 6 = moderate edema with diffuse but incomplete loss of sulcal markings, 7 = moderate/severe edema with reduced grey/white matter differentiation and complete sulcal and cisternal effacement but without herniation, and 8 = severe edema with complete sulcal and cisternal effacement and evidence of herniation [21].

Gross outcomes (survived vs died) were captured for all participants. Values are reported as median (interquartile range), mean (standard deviation) or n (%) as appropriate. Comparisons between survivors and non-survivors were analyzed using the Fisher’s exact test for categorical data and Mann–Whitney test for continuous data. Two-sided p-values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant throughout. If a participant died before a test was completed, the value was considered normal for the analysis. This study was designed as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating investigation to characterize admission lung ultrasound and blood gas findings in children with CM. Accordingly, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, and the final sample reflects consecutive eligible admissions during the study period.

Our work began as an ancillary study to Treating Brain Swelling in Pediatric Cerebral Malaria (5U01AI126610-02), which was approved by the ethics committees at Michigan State University and the University of Malawi College of Medicine Research Ethics Committee (COMREC). Following the conclusion of the clinical trial, approval was received from the ethics committee at Nationwide Children’s Hospital in Columbus, Ohio and again by COMREC. Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

A total of 97 patients were enrolled. Patient demographics are present in Table 1. All patients met the WHO criteria for CM and four (4%) also met criteria for severe malarial anemia (SMA) with a packed cell volume < 15% at the time of admission to the Pediatric Research Ward. While only a small percentage had SMA, anemia was nearly universal (92% of participants) on admission when using WHO hemoglobin thresholds. A total of 27 (28%) children met at least one WHO criterion for respiratory distress. In this cohort, 93 patients had a blood gas at the time of admission. Four patients did not undergo this analysis due to unavailable i-STAT cartridges or machine error. An overview of the pH values can be seen in Fig. 3. While there was a significant difference in lactate values between children with and without respiratory distress, assessment of blood gas findings identified a median pH > 7.40 and a median base deficit of − 5 mmol/L for both groups (Fig. 4). Metabolic acidosis was the sole acid–base disturbance in only 12 participants (12%), with no difference in the frequency of occurrence between children with and without respiratory distress (Fig. 5). Further, respiratory alkalosis was the only identified blood gas abnormality in 20 participants (21%). Across the cohort, abnormalities on lung ultrasound findings were ubiquitous, with subcentimeter consolidations found in 85 of the 88 patients who had an ultrasound (97%). Thirteen patients had larger consolidations (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document}≥ 1 cm) more consistent with pneumonia. Eight patients had multiple B lines on imaging, which were often present adjacent to the larger consolidations. There were no patients with evidence of diffuse pulmonary edema or concerns for volume overload at the time of admission. Nine patients did not have analyzable ultrasound images due to the following logistical reasons: four lacked an available ultrasound trained provider between admission and randomization within the active clinical trial; one died prior to imaging; one was too unstable for imaging to be performed; two scans were performed on a non-study device; and one exam used an incorrect preset, precluding protocol scoring. While children with signs of respiratory distress had lower SpO2/FiO2 ratios, no participant met criteria for pARDS.

In the cohort, 19 (20%) participants died. Comparison of survivors and non-survivors can be found in Table 3. Respiratory distress was not associated with increased mortality. Specifically, 15% of patients with respiratory distress died (n = 4) and 21% of those without respiratory distress died (n = 15). Discussion

The key findings of this prospective, observational study of Malawian children with CM include: 1) 28% of children met the WHO definition of respiratory distress at the time of hospital admission; 2) pH values do not support the dogma that “acidotic breathing” is occurring in a vast majority of children; 3) primary respiratory alkalosis was a commonly observed acid-base abnormality; and 4) subcentimeter, subpleural consolidations were almost universally present on point-of-care lung ultrasound. Respiratory alkalosis was not associated with death. Alternatively, mortality was associated with markers of acidosis such as low pH, elevated lactate, and reduced bicarbonate levels. This study demonstrates that respiratory distress in pediatric CM cannot solely be attributed to a compensatory mechanism and may instead reflect heterogeneous underlying pathophysiology. Whether these findings carry independent prognostic or therapeutic implications will require larger prospective studies.

Respiratory distress in children with malaria is often referred to as “acidotic breathing” based on the commonly presumed physiological explanation [22]. This terminology is problematic if blood gas values do not reflect an acidotic pH. Secondary hypocapnia, often referred to as respiratory compensation, is hypocapnia that occurs when acidemia is present to bring the pH closer to physiologic values of 7.35–7.45. However, this secondary response does not completely restore blood acidity to normal levels and does not overcorrect acidemia [23]. If the pH is normal or alkalotic, a second acid–base disturbance is present. A common example in pediatrics is diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). In DKA, a metabolic acidosis and acidotic pH occurs due to the reduction of serum bicarbonate. A secondary response is triggered which manifests as hyperventilation and ultimately Kussmaul respirations. This breathing pattern is the body’s attempt at preventing a further fall in pH, but it cannot fully restore the pH to normal [24]. The DKA patient remains acidotic until the underlying pathology is addressed. In our cohort of CM patients, only 16 patients had a pH < 7.35 yet at total of 27 patients (28%) met WHO criteria for respiratory distress. Of the 16 acidotic patients, only 4 had deep breathing on admission, the clinical finding thought to be the best indicator of acidosis [25].

The median pH, CO2, and HCO3– of this cohort of pediatric patients with CM point towards the need to consider a different explanation for the occurrence of respiratory distress in this patient population. In our cohort, 32 (33%) patients presented with a primary respiratory alkalosis. Primary hypocapnia leading to respiratory alkalosis most commonly reflects alveolar hyperventilation caused by increased ventilatory drive arising from the lung, the carotid or aortic chemoreceptors, the brainstem chemoreceptors, or other central nervous system triggers. A list of common causes of respiratory alkalosis can be found in Table 4. [26, 27] Many of these factors are plausible contributors to primary hypocapnia in children with CM. Understanding the drivers of respiratory alkalosis in this population may provide insight into the cause of respiratory distress in some children. Hypoxemia or tissue hypoxia can be a major stimulus of alveolar ventilation. Children who suffer from malaria are almost universally anemic, which places them at risk for decreased oxygen delivery and subsequent tissue hypoxia. Additionally, a hallmark of P. falciparum is sequestration of parasitized erythrocytes in the microcirculation of various organs. The resulting microvascular occlusion could further decrease oxygen delivery to tissues [12, 22]. While hypoxemia could be contributing to the primary respiratory alkalosis we have seen in our cohort, it is unlikely to be the only cause. In order to elicit a respiratory response to hypoxemia, the PaO2 must fall and remain below 60 mmHg or an SpO2 equivalent of < 90% [28]. There were only three patients in our cohort with an SpO2 of < 90% on admission, and there were no patients in our cohort with an SpO2/FiO2 of < 264, the criteria needed to diagnose pediatric ARDS [17, 18, 28, 29]. The absence of severe hypoxemia, failure to meet the Kigali criteria in any participant, and lack of diffuse pulmonary edema make ARDS a very unlikely explanation for the ultrasound abnormalities observed. Although bilateral anterior subpleural consolidations may be seen in non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema, the above findings support the interpretation that the observed lung ultrasound abnormalities may precede or occur independently of overt gas-exchange impairment.

Other forms of organ dysfunction, including cardiac and renal failure, are also plausible contributors to respiratory compromise in this population. Reduced cardiac output may lead to tissue hypoxia and thereby serve as a potential driver of both respiratory alkalosis and clinical respiratory distress. However, only one child in our cohort had a cardiac index < 3.5 L/min/m2, and no participants demonstrated lung ultrasound findings consistent with diffuse pulmonary edema, a hallmark of decompensated heart failure. These findings suggest that impaired cardiac output was unlikely to be a major contributor to respiratory alkalosis in this cohort. Similarly, renal failure was unlikely to be the primary driver of the respiratory abnormalities observed. While it is tenable that the metabolic acidosis identified in some children was exacerbated by renal dysfunction—particularly given prior reports that more than 40% of children with CM have concomitant AKI—our lung ultrasound findings were not consistent with pulmonary edema [30–32]. Such edema would be expected in the setting of renal failure due to volume overload, hypoalbuminemia with reduced oncotic pressure, and systemic inflammation leading to increased pulmonary vascular permeability. There was also no difference in creatinine values when comparing those with respiratory distress to those without. Taken together, these data argue against renal failure as the principal contributor to the lung pathology observed in this cohort.

Mechanical stimulation of J receptors (juxta-pulmonary capillary receptors) in the lung results in rapid shallow breathing, mucous production, and bronchoconstriction [33–35]. J receptor stimulation could result from several processes in patients infected with malaria and contribute to the respiratory alkalosis we identified in our cohort. P. falciparum induces a systemic inflammatory response throughout the host and inflammatory cells infiltrating the pulmonary interstitium, a process that could lead to stretch and stimulation of J receptors [36–38]. It is also plausible that the previously described microvascular occlusion could contribute to interstitial congestion by creating an increase in pulmonary capillary pressure. Once again, this increase in pressure would stimulate the J receptors, increasing respiratory rate and inducing hypocapnia [33–35, 39]. Lastly, secondary infections are common in children with severe malaria, and pulmonary infection may result in J receptor stretch and stimulation [12, 40]. However, only 12 (14%) patients in our cohort had a consolidation of one centimeter or larger, which would be consistent with a clinically significant pneumonia [41, 42].

The most common finding on lung ultrasound was the presence of subcentimeter consolidations in one or more lung fields, an abnormality identified in 97% of imaged participants (n = 85). Subcentimeter consolidations have not been attributed to clinically significant pneumonia, but they could be sonographic evidence of viral lower respiratory tract infections in these patients [41]. Additional insights come from the COVID-19 literature where contrast-enhanced imaging demonstrated small subpleural consolidations were associated with perfusion defects. These perfusion defects were hypothesized to have been caused by ischemic or necrotic changes, potentially caused by microvascular thrombotic or embolic events [43–45]. In the context of severe malaria, the subcentimeter consolidations observed in our cohort may be compatible with ischemic changes as a result of microvascular occlusion, a well-described feature of P. falciparum malaria. It is unclear if this abnormality would result in J receptor stretch and respiratory alkalosis, but there was a trend towards significance when evaluating the association of subcentimeter consolidations and respiratory distress (Table 2). Larger prospective studies incorporating lung ultrasound, blood gas analysis, viral testing, and outcomes are needed to better define the mechanisms underlying our findings and their clinical relevance.

All participants had CM, and therefore, involvement of the central nervous system could be contributing to the respiratory alkalosis identified in our participants. Central chemoreceptors, located in the medulla, are instrumental in controlling respiratory drive. The respiratory centers in the brainstem generate the respiratory rhythm based on the information received from both the peripheral sensory system (which monitors oxygen content) and the central chemoreceptors (which monitor pH) [33, 46]. Cerebral malaria results in a wide range of structural brain abnormalities including in the pons and medulla [47, 48]. In a cohort of 16 children with CM in Zambia, 11 patients (69%) had brainstem involvement on MRI [47]. Potchen et al. also evaluated MRI findings in 152 Malawian children with clinically defined CM and found pontine changes in 63 of 152 children (42%). Neither depth of coma nor Brain Volume Score were associated with respiratory distress in our cohort. Future studies should evaluate specific MRI abnormalities to better understand the potential contribution of neurologic injury to both respiratory distress and respiratory alkalosis in children with malaria. Additionally, further exploration of blood gas results in those with non-CM severe malaria is needed.

Lastly, fever is a common cause of respiratory alkalosis and primarily contributes through its effect on respiratory rate and ventilation. The hypothalamus, which regulates body temperature, also influences the respiratory centers in the brainstem. As temperature rises, it stimulates the respiratory center leading to increased respiratory rate [49]. While children with malaria consistently present with fever, there was no difference in temperature in those with or without respiratory distress in our cohort. Therefore, fever may contribute to hypocapnia in these children, but it is likely not the primary cause of respiratory distress.

This study did have several limitations. First, our sample size and single location are limitations. Future work in larger sample sizes from other malaria endemic regions should be explored. Another challenge is the subjective nature of the bedside clinical classification of respiratory distress when defining severe malaria in children. For example, the terms “intercostal indrawing,” “chest indrawing,” and “deep breathing” can be defined differently by individual clinicians leading to the possibility we misclassified the presence of respiratory distress in this cohort. The restriction of the cohort to only include children with CM is also a limitation. Comparative data from children with other severe malaria syndromes, such as SMA, are needed to ascertain whether the respiratory and acid–base abnormalities observed here are specific to CM or represent features of severe malaria more broadly. It is possible there were more than four patients in this cohort with SMA, but it is unknown which participants received a blood transfusion prior to their arrival on the Pediatric Research Ward. Lastly, as a result of being in a resource-limited area working with critically ill children, data for several patients were incomplete as previously described.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides compelling evidence that respiratory distress in pediatric cerebral malaria cannot be solely attributed to metabolic acidosis. The high prevalence of respiratory alkalosis, the near-universal presence of subpleural consolidations on lung ultrasound, and the lack of clear association between respiratory distress and mortality underscore the need to reconsider traditional interpretations. The term “acidotic breathing” oversimplifies and likely incorrectly describes a complex, multifactorial process which may hinder appropriate diagnosis and treatment. We advocate for the adoption of the term “malarial pneumonopathy” to replace “acidotic breathing” in this population based on the plausible pathophysiologic contributors—including pulmonary microvascular obstruction and interstitial inflammation—driving respiratory abnormalities. Recognizing malarial pneumonopathy as a distinct clinical entity shifts the paradigm from oversimplified acid–base assumptions to a more nuanced understanding of malaria related respiratory dysfunction. Better understanding malarial pneumonopathy and its presence in other types of severe malaria is essential for improving diagnostic accuracy, guiding supportive care, and informing future research in critically ill children with malaria.
