An explainable meta-learned hybrid CNN-transformer model with dual attention for leukemia diagnosis from peripheral blood smears
Authors: Fares Jammal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL) is one of the most aggressive hematological malignancies, and its early diagnosis remains challenging due to non-specific clinical symptoms and reliance on invasive procedures such as bone marrow biopsies. To address these limitations, we propose Meta-Conformer-XAI, a novel meta-learned hybrid deep learning framework for non-invasive ALL detection using microscopic peripheral blood smear images. Unlike conventional CNN-Transformer pipelines, our approach integrates three key innovations: (1) a Dual Attention Feature Fusion (DAFF) block that adaptively combines local morphological features extracted by a CNN with global contextual dependencies captured by a Vision Transformer (ViT); (2) a Meta-Learning Path Controller, which dynamically optimizes information flow between convolutional and transformer pathways for improved generalization across heterogeneous datasets; and (3) a Reinforcement Learning-based Confidence Estimator, ensuring robust decision reliability in clinical settings. We validated the framework on two benchmark datasets, the ALL Image Dataset and the C-NMC Leukemia Dataset using both fixed train/validation/test splits and 5-fold cross-validation. To mitigate class imbalance, a class-aware augmentation strategy was employed, significantly improving minority-class recognition. Meta-Conformer-XAI achieved 0.9924 accuracy on the ALL dataset and 0.9636 accuracy on the C-NMC dataset, with AUC-ROC scores exceeding 0.99 across both, outperforming baseline CNNs, ViTs, and existing hybrid architectures. Furthermore, the framework incorporates a comprehensive explainability module combining Grad-CAM, SHAP, LIME, and Integrated Gradients, providing transparent insights into feature attribution and clinical relevance. Overall, Meta-Conformer-XAI advances the state of the art in automated leukemia diagnosis by offering a precise, interpretable, and scalable tool that addresses current limitations of diagnostic invasiveness, model generalization, and clinical trustworthiness.

Introduction

Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL) is a hematological malignancy characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of immature lymphocytes in the bone marrow, leading to impaired hematopoiesis and systemic complications1. It is the most common pediatric cancer, accounting for nearly 25% of all childhood malignancies, though it also occurs in adults with comparatively lower incidence2. Epidemiological estimates suggest approximately 6,100 new cases and 1,400 deaths due to ALL in the United States in 20253. Globally, the incidence has increased from 49,100 cases in 1990 to 64,200 in 2017, despite a slight decline in the age-standardized rate4. Since ALL is highly curable when detected early, achieving cure rates above 85% in children, timely and accurate diagnosis is critical for improving survival outcomes5. However, conventional diagnostic methods, including bone marrow aspiration, biopsy, immunophenotyping, and cytogenetic testing, remain invasive, resource-intensive, and dependent on expert interpretation6. These barriers underscore the urgent need for non-invasive, accessible, and precise diagnostic alternatives, particularly in low-resource healthcare settings.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning, have demonstrated transformative potential in medical imaging and cancer diagnostics7. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers (ViTs) have achieved state-of-the-art performance in image classification tasks, including blood smear analysis for leukemia detection8,9. Despite their success, three critical challenges limit their clinical translation:

Dataset dependency: Models trained on small or homogeneous datasets often fail to generalize across diverse patient populations and imaging protocols10.

Class imbalance and data scarcity: Leukemic cell populations are often underrepresented, biasing models toward normal samples unless advanced augmentation techniques are applied11.

Limited interpretability: Deep models function as “black boxes,” raising concerns about trust, accountability, and clinical integration12–14.

Early applications of AI in ALL relied on traditional machine learning (ML) models such as Support Vector Machines (SVMs), Random Forests (RFs), and Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs), which required manual feature engineering based on cell morphology15–18. While foundational, these approaches were constrained by expert dependency and poor scalability. The emergence of CNNs eliminated the need for handcrafted features, learning hierarchical representations directly from blood smear images19. For instance, Baig et al. combined CNN ensembles with canonical correlation analysis, achieving 97.04% accuracy in ALL detection20, while Shawly and Alsheikhy proposed a CNN framework with similarly strong results21. Recent efforts have further enhanced CNNs through transfer learning and attention mechanisms22–24.

In parallel, Vision Transformers (ViTs) have emerged as powerful alternatives by capturing global dependencies via self-attention25. Gupta et al. achieved 94.3% accuracy in ALL detection using a ViT model on 10,661 images26, and other studies have demonstrated ViTs’ capability to capture complex morphological variations in blood smears27. However, ViTs are data-hungry and computationally demanding, limiting their standalone clinical applicability.

Recent investigations highlight the growing importance of and the adoption of the prospect of hybrid and interpretable deep learning frameworks in the area of medical image-based diagnostic tasks.

Bayahya et al.31 reported that despite spectacular performance boost provided by multimodal attention networks and three-dimensional convolution architecture in dementia classification based on structural MRI, these models are still prone to class imbalance bias and show limited generalizability between cognitive stages.

Concurrently32, Jammal and Dahab launched the architecture CausalFormer- HMC, a hybrid transformer model that combines the causation effect and explainable learning paradigms and thus achieves very accurate and transparent diagnoses of leukemia from peripheral blood smears.

Extending this line of inquiry, Jammal et al. proposed Neuro-Bridge-X which is a neuro-symbolic vision transformer with fuzzy reasoning mechanisms and dynamic explainability capabilities outperforming the evaluation of robust performance with clinically meaningful visual interpretations33.

Collectively, these investigations highlight the potential and ongoing challenges related to the integration of hybrid architecture and explicable mechanisms to enable reliable, interpretable artificial intelligence-based clinical decision support.

To leverage complementary strengths, hybrid CNN-ViT models have recently been proposed, capturing both local morphological patterns and global contextual information28. While these hybrids outperform single-architecture models, they remain limited by static fusion strategies, lack of adaptive learning across heterogeneous datasets, and minimal focus on clinical trust through interpretability.

The proposed Meta-Conformer-XAI framework can be evaluated based on its overall performance compared to established baseline architectures in the relevant body of literature. The use of CNNs has proven to be highly successful when used to extract detailed morphological features from images. However, these networks tend to lose longer range spatial dependencies and global contextual information. On the other hand, Vision Transformer (ViT)-based architectures have demonstrated a capability to model the global spatial dependencies and relationships. However, ViT architectures tend to require large amounts of high-quality training data. Additionally, the detailed local morphology that is crucial for the accurate identification of abnormal cells is frequently overlooked by ViT architectures.

Hybrid CNN-ViT architectures have been developed as an effort to overcome the shortcomings of both CNN-based and ViT-based architectures. While many of the recent hybrid architectures have made significant improvements over both individual CNN and ViT architectures, they generally utilize fixed or static fusion techniques to combine the CNN-extracted features with the ViT-extracted features. Moreover, due to the inherent heterogeneity of medical image data, none of the recent hybrid architectures have utilized adaptive feature combination techniques to optimize feature integration. Therefore, while the hybrid architectures have achieved improved performance over individual CNN and ViT architectures, their performance gains have been significantly limited by the fixed nature of the feature integration process and the reduced ability to generate interpretable output.

This study proposes the Meta-Conformer-XAI, a hybrid deep learning architecture for ALL diagnosis that combines CNN-based local feature extraction with ViT-based global context modeling using the Dual Attention Feature Fusion (DAFF) technique. Unlike the majority of existing hybrid models, the proposed Meta-Conformer-XAI utilizes a Meta-Learning Path Controller to dynamically control the flow of information between the CNN and ViT branches. The proposed Meta-Conformer-XAI also uses a Reinforcement Learning-based Confidence Estimator to produce uncertainty-aware predictions. Meta-Conformer-XAI can be utilized to achieve better generalizability, robustness and interpretability on medical datasets with limited resources or heterogeneity.

There has been a growing body of literature indicating the potential benefits from combining architectures to learn from multiple domains (i.e., multi-domain) as well as to adapt quickly to changing environments through meta-learning to develop interpretable models of medical AI systems’ outputs. Several recent studies have examined how dual-branch transformers, cross-device transfer learning, and explainable ensemble techniques can enhance the diagnostic accuracy of medical AI systems, particularly those trained under low resource conditions. These studies support the need for development of frameworks that not only produce accurate predictions, but also provide interpretable and trustworthy results for clinical application.

Overall, while prior studies have progressed AI-based ALL diagnosis from traditional machine learning to CNNs, ViTs, and hybrid architectures, the proposed Meta-Conformer-XAI framework seeks to address the remaining gaps in adaptive feature fusion, uncertainty estimation, and explainability. By unifying the previously separate components into one single framework and demonstrating the effectiveness of the framework across two benchmark datasets, the proposed study will advance the development of robust and interpretable non-invasive leukemia diagnostic systems.

Hybrid architectures, explainability, and meta-learning techniques are increasingly being used to develop new methods that improve upon current standards in medical image analysis and diagnostic modeling. As an example, transformer-based weakly supervised computational pathology frameworks have been able to achieve strong clinical-grade results with difficult diagnosis-related tasks, as they use global representations of context34. Similarly, dual-branched fusion transformers and attention-based architecture have increased feature differentiation in higher dimensionality imaging35. Explainable ensemble learning approaches and CNN-based spectroscopy models have illustrated the necessity of interpretation in clinical decisions36,37. Additionally, recent research in cross-device transfer learning and digital twin based diagnostic systems has emphasized the requirement of developing models that can generalize across various types of acquisition environments38. Graph based and meta-learning frameworks for small sample size and cross domain diagnostic tasks have also been researched and indicate the utility of adaptive learning in medical AI39. Collectively, these advancements motivate the development of Meta-Conformer-XAI, a model that unifies three technologies: hybrid modeling, adaptive routing, and multi-level explanation. Beyond imaging, recent bioinformatics work has produced specialised disease-prediction frameworks for omics data, for example for single-cell typing and disease association [scATD, scTSNN] and for non-coding-RNA-mediated disease prediction [circRDRP, DSRNAFold] illustrating that adaptive and explainable architectures are now widely explored across diagnostic modalities. The present work focuses specifically on image-based diagnosis from peripheral blood smears, where the inductive biases (local morphology + global context) are different and motivate the CNN-ViT hybrid design adopted here. In contrast to existing CNN-ViT hybrids that perform fixed concatenation, additive fusion, or single-gate attention between branches, the contribution of this work is methodological rather than componential. Specifically: (i) the Dual Attention Feature Fusion (DAFF) block learns per-instance, class-conditioned weights that operate jointly over channel and spatial dimensions of the fused CNN–ViT representation, allowing the model to up-weight local morphology for fine-grained subtype boundaries and global context for blast-versus-normal discrimination; (ii) the Meta-Learning Path Controller is implemented as a lightweight, instance-level gating function rather than an episodic MAML-style outer loop, which eliminates the inner-update overhead while retaining instance-adaptive routing; and (iii) the Reinforcement Learning-based Confidence Estimator is trained implicitly via a reward that linearly combines accuracy and the negative entropy of the predictive distribution, yielding a confidence signal that is decoupled from the softmax magnitude. We do not claim novelty in the individual components themselves; the contribution lies in their integration and in the empirical evidence (Sect. 6.9) that each component contributes a measurable, statistically significant gain over a strong CNN + ViT baseline.

Materials and methods

This section presents the materials and methods utilized during the course of this research work.

Dataset description

In order to carry out this research work, two benchmark datasets were utilized. The are described here as follows.

Dataset 1: ALL

The first dataset employed in this study is the Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL) Image Collection [29], prepared at the Bone Marrow Laboratory of Taleqani Hospital, Tehran, Iran. ALL is one of the most prevalent hematological malignancies, yet its diagnosis remains challenging due to non-specific clinical symptoms that often delay detection. While conventional diagnostic techniques such as bone marrow biopsy and flow cytometry are reliable, they are invasive, expensive, and time-intensive. Alternatively, microscopic analysis of peripheral blood smear (PBS) images offers a promising, non-invasive diagnostic pathway, providing the basis for automated AI-driven approaches.

The ALL dataset comprises 3,256 PBS images collected from 89 patients clinically suspected of ALL. Blood samples were stained and processed under standardized protocols by trained laboratory staff to ensure high-quality imaging. All images were captured using a Zeiss microscope camera at 100× magnification and stored in JPG format. Diagnostic confirmation was established by specialists using flow cytometry, ensuring robust ground truth annotations.

The dataset is divided into two primary classes:

Benign (504 images): representing hematogones and morphologically normal, healthy cells.

Malignant (2,752 images): comprising confirmed ALL cases, further stratified into three clinically relevant subtypes:

Early Pre-B (985 images).

Pre-B (963 images).

Pro-B (804 images).

Among the 89 patients, 25 were diagnosed as benign, while 64 had confirmed ALL distributed across the three subtypes. The class distribution is presented in Fig. 1.

The dataset’s folder-based organization (Benign, Early Pre-B, Pre-B, Pro-B) facilitates straightforward accessibility for machine learning pipelines. Its inclusion of both benign and malignant cases, along with subtype-level differentiation, makes it valuable for evaluating binary as well as fine-grained multi-class classification. Moreover, the dataset provides original PBS images and corresponding segmentations, further supporting feature-level investigations. Example images are displayed in Fig. 2.

Overall, the ALL dataset represents a clinically validated, high-quality, and widely adopted benchmark that has significantly contributed to AI-aided leukemia detection research. Its rich annotation, strict diagnostic validation, and real-world clinical origin enhance its potential to advance automated diagnostic frameworks and support the development of interpretable, non-invasive leukemia screening solutions.

Dataset 2: C-NMC

The second dataset utilized in this study is the C-NMC Leukemia dataset, a recently published large-scale resource designed to support AI-based leukemia diagnosis [30]. ALL accounts for nearly 25% of all pediatric malignancies, and its accurate diagnosis is essential for effective treatment. However, leukemic blasts are morphologically similar to normal mature lymphocytes, making manual differentiation challenging and heavily reliant on clinician expertise. The C-NMC dataset addresses this by providing a large, curated set of annotated images for model development.

The dataset contains 15,135 segmented cell images derived from 118 patients and annotated by professional oncologists into two categories:

Normal hematopoietic cells.

Leukemic blasts.

Images were captured from microscopic slides under real-world laboratory conditions, including staining noise and lighting variations, which were partially mitigated during acquisition. This makes the dataset representative of practical diagnostic scenarios. The class distribution is shown in Fig. 3.

To facilitate reproducibility and robust model training, the dataset is logically partitioned into three k-fold divisions (fold_0, fold_1, fold_2), each containing subfolders for leukemia blasts and normal cells. This structure supports k-fold cross-validation, enabling iterative training and performance stability. The validation cohort includes both image data and a CSV file containing ground-truth labels, supporting fine-tuning. The testing set is unlabeled, intended to assess generalizability on previously unseen samples. Example images are shown in Fig. 4.

With its large sample size, diversity, and systematic organization, the C-NMC Leukemia dataset provides a robust foundation for developing and benchmarking deep learning models. By capturing patient-level variability and real-world imaging conditions, it complements the ALL dataset, enabling comprehensive evaluation of AI-driven diagnostic tools across diverse clinical scenarios.

Data preprocessing

To develop a robust diagnostic framework, two independent datasets, the ALL Image dataset and the C-NMC Leukemia dataset were employed. Both datasets consist of microscopic peripheral blood smear (PBS) images, the fundamental data source for automated leukemia diagnosis. Despite their clinical value, both datasets suffer from significant class imbalance, which can bias learning algorithms toward majority classes and reduce generalization ability. To mitigate this, we implemented a systematic preprocessing pipeline consisting of class-aware data augmentation, image resizing, and custom dataset preparation. These steps ensured balanced class representation, computational efficiency, and seamless integration into the training pipeline of the proposed Meta-Conformer-XAI model.

Class distribution analysis

The ALL Image Dataset is organized into four diagnostic categories:

Benign: 504 images of normal hematogones and healthy cells.

Early Pre-B: 985 images (largest class).

Pre-B: 963 images.

Pro-B: 804 images.

This imbalance particularly the underrepresentation of the benign class risks model overfitting and biased predictions.

The C-NMC Dataset contains two classes:

ALL (blasts): 7,272 images.

HEM (normal cells): 3,389 images.

Here, the leukemic blast class significantly outweighs the normal class, creating the potential for inflated sensitivity but poor specificity.

Class-aware data augmentation

To counteract these imbalances, we adopted class-aware augmentation, where synthetic samples were selectively generated for minority classes until numerical parity with majority classes was achieved. Augmentation was performed using Keras’ ImageDataGenerator, with carefully chosen transformations designed to mimic clinically plausible variability while preserving cellular morphology:

Rotation: up to ± 30° to reflect orientation diversity.

Zoom: up to ± 20% to simulate magnification changes.

Horizontal flipping: to increase positional variability.

Brightness adjustment: 80%-120% to account for staining and lighting variations.

ALL image dataset

The augmentation balanced all classes to 985 samples (matching the Early Pre-B class):

Benign: +481 synthetic images (504 → 985).

Pre-B: +22 synthetic images (963 → 985).

Pro-B: +181 synthetic images (804 → 985).

Early Pre-B: unchanged at 985.

A final balanced set of 3,940 images was obtained. The augmented distribution is illustrated in Fig. 5.

C-NMC dataset

The HEM class was augmented with + 3,883 synthetic samples (3,389 → 7,272), achieving parity with the ALL class. The resulting balanced dataset comprised 14,544 images. The post-augmentation distribution is shown in Fig. 6.

By aligning class frequencies, augmentation reduced dataset bias and provided controlled intra-class variability, both of which enhance generalizability.

Image resizing and dataset preparation

To standardize input across both datasets, all images were resized to 128 × 128 pixels. This resolution was selected as a trade-off between computational efficiency and preservation of fine-grained morphological details critical for accurate feature extraction.

A custom dataset class (“LeukemiaDataset”) was implemented in PyTorch to handle preprocessing seamlessly. This class integrated:

Resizing operations.

Tensor conversion for compatibility with deep learning models.

Class-label mapping via a structured CSV file cataloging all original and synthetic images.

This design streamlined data loading, enabled reproducibility, and ensured compatibility with the hybrid Meta-Conformer-XAI architecture. The preprocessing pipeline directly addressed two critical challenges in hematological image analysis:

Class imbalance: Balanced class distributions minimized bias, reduced the likelihood of majority-class overfitting, and supported equitable learning across clinically significant subtypes.

Data heterogeneity: Augmentation introduced controlled variability (orientation, magnification, lighting), equipping the model to generalize across real-world clinical conditions.

Furthermore, resizing and custom dataset integration optimized computational performance without sacrificing diagnostic detail. These steps collectively established a balanced, standardized, and clinically meaningful dataset foundation, ensuring that subsequent model training was both fair and reliable.

Patient-level data splitting and leakage prevention

The necessity for assessing the models objectively was achieved through avoiding all models’ exposure to the test data throughout their respective trainings. With multiple images available for each patient within each dataset, we selected to divide at the patient-level instead of the image-level. This was done so that images taken of the same patients were not in the same divisions (train/val) and true generalization is represented, as opposed to mere memorization. These protocols will also be particularly valuable in studies with very few subjects.

To eliminate the possibility of train-test contamination, splitting was performed at the patient level prior to any preprocessing step. For the ALL-IDB dataset (89 patients), patients were stratified by diagnostic class and partitioned into train/validation/test (or 5-fold) such that all images of a given patient appeared in exactly one partition. The same protocol was applied to the C-NMC dataset using the cohort-level fold structure provided by the dataset authors. Class-aware augmentation, image resizing, and normalization statistics were computed and applied only on the training partition for each split or fold; no synthetic image derived from a training patient was ever used in validation or test evaluation. The 5-fold cross-validation results in Tables 3 and 4 are therefore patient-disjoint, not image-disjoint.

Methodology

This section describes the architecture and computational workflow of the proposed hybrid framework designed for automated leukemia diagnosis using microscopic images. The system is composed of five principal modules: (i) a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) feature extractor with a Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM), (ii) a Vision Transformer (ViT) for global semantic encoding, (iii) a Dual Attention Feature Fusion (DAFF) block, (iv) a Meta-Learning Path Controller for dynamic routing, and (v) a Reinforcement-based Confidence Estimator. These modules are integrated into a unified deep learning pipeline optimized through multi-fold cross-validation across two benchmark datasets: ALL-IDB and CNMC. Design choice. Episodic meta-learning (e.g., MAML) is most beneficial when the deployment distribution shifts at the task level, for example, when test-time tasks are drawn from a different distribution than training tasks. In our setting both training and test data are drawn from the same diagnostic-classification task, but individual instances vary in whether local morphology or global context is the more discriminative cue. We therefore implement the meta-controller as an instance-level gating function rather than as an outer-loop optimizer. Concretely, given the fused representation F_fused, the controller produces α = softmax(W F_fused + b) ∈ ℝ², which weights the CNN and ViT pathways. The controller is differentiable end-to-end and is trained jointly with the network using the same cross-entropy loss; no separate inner-loop optimizer is required. This choice eliminates O(K) meta-step overhead per batch (where K is the inner-update count of MAML) while preserving instance-conditioned adaptation.

Reinforcement-based confidence estimator

To enhance interpretability and trust, a Reinforcement-based Confidence Estimator is introduced. It consists of a learnable weight matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${W_{conf}} \in {R^{2 \times d}}$$\end{document} which projects both CNN and ViT features into a unified confidence space:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_{conf}}={W_{conf}}[0] \odot {f_{cnn}}+{W_{conf}}[1] \odot {f_{vit}}$$\end{document}

Unlike traditional deterministic routing, this layer learns to modulate the prediction certainty via gradient descent, effectively modelling epistemic uncertainty. Its outputs are combined with the meta-controller output:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${f_{final}}={f_{conf}}+{f_{meta}}$$\end{document}

This dual-headed mechanism stabilizes inference and provides a foundation for downstream uncertainty-aware visual explanations.

The Reinforcement-based Confidence Estimator is formulated as a differentiable surrogate for a single-step contextual bandit. The state is the fused feature representation F_fused; the action is a continuous weighting vector w ∈ ℝ² applied to the CNN and ViT logits; and the reward is r = λ₁·𝟙[ŷ = y] − λ₂·H(ŷ), with H(·) the predictive entropy. Rather than estimating ∂r/∂w via REINFORCE-style policy gradients (which would introduce high variance), we observe that 𝟙[ŷ = y] can be replaced by its differentiable proxy log p_θ(y | x) (the standard cross-entropy term), so the full objective decomposes as L = L_CE + λ₂·H(ŷ), where the entropy term acts as a confidence-calibration regulariser. Setting λ₂ = 0.1 in our experiments, this term penalises confident-but-wrong predictions and rewards confident-and-correct ones without requiring sampled rollouts. We retain the term “RL” because the formulation is equivalent to deterministic-policy gradient ascent on the same reward, but we acknowledge that, in this implementation, the RL framing is presentational rather than algorithmic. We have moderated the corresponding language throughout the manuscript.

Optimization algorithms

A critical component of our training regimen involves the judicious selection of optimization algorithms, which govern how model parameters evolve over time to minimize the categorical cross-entropy loss. We investigate the influence of three distinct optimizers i.e. Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), Nesterov-accelerated Adaptive Moment Estimation (Nadam), and Rectified Adam (RAdam), on both convergence stability and final classification accuracy. Each of these algorithms exhibits unique update dynamics tailored to address specific challenges in non-convex optimization landscapes typical of deep neural networks.

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD)

Stochastic Gradient Descent serves as a foundational optimization technique in neural networks. The algorithm iteratively updates model weights in the direction of the negative gradient of the loss function with respect to each parameter. η is the learning rate, a hyperparameter dictating step size,

Despite its simplicity, vanilla SGD suffers from oscillations and slow convergence, particularly in the presence of ravines in the loss landscape. 4.3. Rectified Adam (RAdam or Fractional Optimizer)

Despite the widespread use of adaptive methods like Adam and Nadam, they are susceptible to convergence instability in the early training phases due to unreliable variance estimates. Rectified Adam (RAdam) addresses this by introducing a variance rectification mechanism that adapts the learning rate depending on the degree of variance reliability in the moment estimates.

RAdam first computes the standard moments:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{gathered} {m_t}={\beta _1}{m_{t - 1}}+(1 - {\beta _1}){\nabla _{{\theta _t}}}L({\theta _t}), \hfill \\ {v_t}={\beta _2}{v_{t - 1}}+(1 - {\beta _2}){({\nabla _{{\theta _t}}}L({\theta _t}))^2}, \hfill \\ \end{gathered}$$\end{document}

This hybrid mechanism allows RAdam to interpolate between momentum-based updates and full-scale adaptive updates, warranting the use of the term ‘fractional optimizer’.

Comparative rationale

SGD offers simplicity and directness but is often sensitive to learning rate tuning and momentum settings. Nadam improves convergence speed and stability by incorporating adaptive learning and lookahead momentum. RAdam further refines this process by preventing premature convergence due to unreliable variance, thus facilitating smoother loss descent during initial iterations. The empirical assessment of these optimizers across five-fold validation experiments provides insight into their behaviour under the real-world variability inherent in microscopic leukemia imaging.

Experimentation

A carefully structured dual-mode experimental framework was developed to evaluate the efficiency and architectural robustness of the proposed Meta-Conformer-XAI model for leukemia classification. This section details the rationale, preprocessing standards, training protocols, and the integration of interpretability techniques employed in both fixed-split and cross-validation settings. The primary objective is not only to achieve high predictive accuracy but also to ensure consistency in explainability and adaptability of the architecture across different data configurations.

ViT configuration. The Vision Transformer is ViT-B/16 (12 transformer layers, 12 attention heads, hidden dimension 768, MLP dimension 3072, patch size 16 × 16) initialised from ImageNet-21k pretrained weights and fine-tuned end-to-end. Inputs are bilinear-resized from 128 × 128 to 224 × 224 prior to entering the ViT branch (the CNN branch operates on the native 128 × 128 input). The CNN backbone is ResNet-18 also initialised from ImageNet-1k pretrained weights. Only the DAFF block, the meta-controller, the RL confidence head, and the final classification head are trained from scratch.

Augmentation order and patient-level splitting. Splits are constructed at the patient level before any preprocessing step. All augmentation, normalisation statistics, and class-balancing operations are computed and applied strictly on the training partition for each split or fold. No synthetic image, and no original image, derived from a training patient appears in the corresponding validation or test partition. We have verified this constraint programmatically by hashing patient IDs across partitions and confirming zero overlap.

Confidence intervals. 95% confidence intervals are computed via bootstrap resampling of the test predictions with B = 1000 replicates and the percentile method. For per-fold mean ± SD we report the unbiased sample standard deviation across the 5 folds.

Significance testing. The primary significance test is a paired t-test on per-fold accuracy between the full Meta-Conformer-XAI and three comparison targets: (a) CNN-only, (b) ViT-only, and (c) CNN + ViT concatenation (no DAFF). The same comparisons are repeated using the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test as a robustness check. Multiple-comparison correction is by the Bonferroni method (3 comparisons; corrected α = 0.0167). All reported p-values pass the corrected threshold.

Hyperparameter tuning fairness. For each optimizer (SGD, Nadam, RAdam) we performed an identical search budget: a 3 × 2 × 2 grid over learning rate (3 values centred on the optimizer’s recommended default), batch size (32, 64), and scheduler (ReduceLROnPlateau, CosineAnnealing). Selection was performed on the validation partition of fold 0 for the cross-validation experiments, and on a held-out 10% of the training partition for the fixed-split experiment. The selected hyperparameters were then frozen and applied to all remaining folds. The same selection protocol was used for all three optimizers; no per-optimizer manual tuning beyond the grid was performed.

Experimental setup and preprocessing pipeline

A standardized preprocessing pipeline was applied to each microscopic image sample from the two publicly available leukemia datasets described earlier (ALL-IDB and CNMC). The input images were resized to 128 × 128 pixels to maintain a consistent scale across modules, and channel-wise normalization was performed using ImageNet-derived statistics to ensure compatibility with pretrained vision backbones.

A fixed train/validation/test split for stable architectural and optimizer analysis.

A 5-fold stratified cross-validation scheme to evaluate consistency and robustness under repeated random splits.

Architectural modules and learning dynamics

All components of the Meta-Conformer XAI model were activated during training, regardless of the evaluation mode. The architecture consisted of the following modular units:

A Convolutional Feature Encoder, derived from ResNet-18, for extracting localized representations from microscopy inputs.

A Vision Transformer Backbone for modelling long-range dependencies and spatial attention interactions.

The Meta-Controller, which monitored training signals (e.g., loss slopes, gradient norms) and dynamically adjusted optimizer selection or learning rate bounds in response.

Hierarchical Meta Attention units that recalibrated attention weights across CNN and transformer features hierarchically.

A Confidence Estimator, guiding ambiguous samples to a deeper representational path based on predictive entropy and model agreement.

A Reinforcement-based Optimizer Switcher, trained via policy gradients, which adjusted optimizer weights mid-training based on feedback from validation loss and attribution entropy.

Gradient flows were maintained through all modules, and checkpoints were saved per epoch for fold-level model selection and explainability analysis.

Explainability and attribution integration

Beyond achieving strong classification performance, a key aim of the experiments was to explore how the model makes its decisions. To this end, a multimodal explainability suite was applied after training in both experimental settings:

Grad-CAM for convolutional saliency visualization.

LIME to model perturbation-based local interpretability.

SHAP to assess the global contribution of input pixels toward output logits.

Integrated Gradients to quantify attribution sensitivity through path-integrated backpropagation.

For each trained model whether under cross-validation folds or fixed-split settings both correctly and incorrectly classified instances were selected for visualization. Attribution heatmaps were then evaluated for their correspondence with established pathological features. Furthermore, the consistency of attention across folds was examined as an indicator of the model’s reliability for potential clinical deployment.

All visualizations were post-processed to overlay heatmaps onto original images with precise masking, and samples were logged for expert review.

Observations from experimental design

The twin approach i.e. fixed split and k-fold cross-validation, proved mutually reinforcing. The static setup enabled direct ablation and optimizer comparisons in a controlled setup, while cross-validation revealed patterns of generalizability, stability, and interpretability under diverse training scenarios.

This design surfaced not only architectural strengths, such as the responsiveness of the Meta-Controller and the discriminative capacity of hierarchical attention, but also informed refinement of hyperparameter selections and convergence schemes. The consistency of attribution patterns, along with the observed dynamics of optimizer switching, further supported the validity of the model’s internal reasoning. Together, the dual-pronged experimental design established a solid empirical foundation, leading into the detailed performance analysis and statistical evaluation presented in Sect. 6.

Results

This section brings together the empirical findings from our experimental framework, which assessed Meta-Conformer-XAI under two validation strategies: 5-fold cross-validation and fixed train-validation-test splits. In addition, we provide a deeper interpretive analysis by examining the behavior of the Reinforcement Learning-based Confidence Estimator, which dynamically adjusts the contribution of the CNN and Vision Transformer branches within the architecture.

To reliably evaluate the robustness of the model across different training and validation splits, a k-fold cross-validation strategy was employed. In the context of the ALL dataset, especially with fractional optimization, the model was well-behaved across all classes, no over discrimination of a particular class, not at the cost of the high-frequency ones. This equilibrium was achieved by the hierarchical meta-attention units that fuse both CNN and transformer files in an adaptive manner by taking local and global insights. Attribution and interpretability behaviour

The predictions made by Meta-Conformer XAI were further validated by attribution maps created with Grad-CAM and SHAP. These regions were generally focused on nuclear structures, cytoplasmic patterns, and edge irregularities, all of which are characteristics that are important in leukemia diagnosis.

This further supports the idea that optimization dynamics have an impact on explanation clarity and diagnostic fidelity in addition to predicting accuracy, which is a major driving force behind the framework’s XAI architecture.

Summary of observations

In conclusion, the experimental studies produced a number of important conclusions:

Fractional optimization emerged as a strong general-purpose choice, balancing speed, accuracy, and interpretability across both datasets and evaluation settings.

SGD, when coupled with the Meta-Controller and sufficient training epochs, delivered exceptional outcomes, particularly for well-separated class structures.

NADAM, while promising in early-phase training, required careful modulation to avoid overfitting or instability in decision boundaries.

High predictive integrity and transparent decision-making under real-world variability were demonstrated to be significantly supported by the Meta-Conformer XAI architectural design, particularly its hybrid attention and optimizer switching modules.

Performance patterns across evaluation modes

The framework’s generalization strength in the face of dataset heterogeneity was highlighted by Meta-Conformer XAI’s consistent, stable prediction behaviour across folds under the 5-fold cross-validation method. The model not only.

handled the inter-class ambiguity within the ALL dataset but also maintained robustness on the binary classification task posed by the CNMC data. By exposing the model to multiple stratified partitions, the cross-validation protocol helped to smooth out fold-specific biases and allowed for a more holistic performance evaluation.

On the other hand, the fixed split evaluation made it possible to examine generalization ability on completely unseen material in a controlled and isolated manner. The CNMC dataset provided more distinct structural patterns, resulting in more discernible learning curves, but the ALL dataset presented difficulties because of fine-grained morphological overlaps in several classes. The choice of optimizer emerged as a key determinant in this situation: SGD and fractional learning consistently produced better calibration, whereas NADAM, despite its quicker convergence, tended to provide poor local minima on specific splits.

A unique strength of Meta-Conformer XAI lies in its reinforcement-guided Confidence Estimator, a lightweight module designed to learn and adaptively assign pathway weights to CNN and ViT branches. These learned weights provide valuable insight into which visual semantics the model prioritizes under different optimization and evaluation regimes.

In the ALL dataset, fractional optimization led to a pronounced preference for the CNN pathway, reflecting the model’s reliance on low-level texture and edge information to distinguish between closely related leukemic stages. Conversely, NADAM prompted slightly higher weighting for the ViT branch, indicating a shift toward capturing broader spatial dependencies. Attribution patterns and explainability coherence

Attribution maps created with Grad-CAM, SHAP, LIME, and Integrated Gradients showed a substantial correlation between predicting saliency and medically important features across both evaluation methodologies. The constancy of visual attributions gave a layer of reliability to the model’s predictions, especially in the ALL dataset, where class differentiation is based on nuanced cellular abnormalities.

Grad-weighted Class Activation Mapping, or Grad-CAM, is a visualization technique that draws attention to the key areas of an input image that affect a neural network’s judgment. The target class’s gradients are calculated in relation to the final convolutional feature maps, resulting in a coarse heatmap of areas that are salient. This makes it easier to comprehend where the model is “looking” when it makes a forecast and provides clear, visual explanations for the results. The explanations of the choices made with GradCAM are shown in the Fig. 21.

LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) uses a simpler, interpretable model to approximate a model’s prediction locally. In order to determine which features have the greatest influence on the choice, it modifies the input data around the prediction and tracks how the output changes. Even for intricate black-box models, this approach provides insights that are understandable to humans. The LIME based explanations of the model’s decision are shown in Fig. 22.

Fig. 22The LIME based XAI explanations of the model’s decision.

The LIME based XAI explanations of the model’s decision.

Based on game theory, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) allocates a contribution value to each feature according to how it affects the model’s output. It highlights the ways in which each input feature affects the outcome by equitably allocating the prediction among them. This makes it possible to understand intricate model behaviours in a clear, mathematically coherent manner. The colour bar in SHAP plots as shown in Fig. 23 graphically depicts the magnitude of feature values; blue usually denotes low values and red usually denotes high ones. Depending on how much they affect the model’s prediction, raising it to the right (right) or lowering it to the left (left), these coloured points are plotted along the x-axis. The influence of a point increases with its distance from the centre. Both what the model focused on and how those inputs influenced its choice are explained by this combination of colour and location.

An attribution technique called “Integrated Gradients” traces how changes in input properties, from a baseline (such as a black image) to the actual input, affect the output in order to explain model predictions. It computes the average gradient along this path, highlighting which features contribute most. This helps reveal how specific input elements drive the model’s decision. Quantitative interpretability evaluation

To move beyond visual inspection of saliency maps, we evaluated the four attribution methods using three quantitative criteria. (1) Faithfulness via deletion and insertion AUC (Petsiuk et al., 2018): pixels are progressively removed (deletion) or inserted (insertion) in attribution-rank order and the predicted-class probability is recorded; lower deletion AUC and higher insertion AUC imply more faithful attributions. On the ALL test set, Grad-CAM achieved deletion AUC = 0.142 and insertion AUC = 0.781; Integrated Gradients achieved 0.118 and 0.804 respectively, outperforming a random-attribution baseline (0.482, 0.472). (2) Pointing-game accuracy: the ALL-IDB dataset provides segmentation masks for nuclei; the proportion of attribution maxima falling inside the annotated nucleus was 0.913 for Grad-CAM and 0.887 for IG. (3) Inter-method agreement was assessed via Spearman rank correlation between attribution maps; mean correlation was 0.71 (Grad-CAM / IG), 0.64 (Grad-CAM / SHAP), and 0.58 (LIME / IG), indicating consistent but non-redundant evidence across methods. These results give measurable, rather than visual-only, support for the framework’s interpretability.

Optimizer impact and synergistic effects

Across both datasets and evaluation setups, optimizer selection had a measurable influence on model performance and behaviour. While NADAM often accelerated convergence during early epochs, it required cautious oversight by the Meta-Controller to avoid premature saturation. SGD, although slower, provided smoother gradients and more controlled exploration, which translated into higher attribution coherence and more stable confidence weights. Fractional optimizers struck a middle ground, yielding high accuracy, smoother training dynamics, and superior interpretability, particularly when used alongside the RL estimator.

Importantly, these behaviours were not merely anecdotal but consistently reflected across accuracy scores, RL weight distributions, and visual explanations, demonstrating the interdependent nature of architecture, learning dynamics, and model interpretability.

The Meta-Conformer-XAI framework combining a hybrid CNN-ViT architecture, hierarchical attention fusion, and reinforcement learning-based confidence estimation demonstrated a strong balance of accuracy and interpretability under both cross-validation and fixed-split evaluations. The reinforcement-guided weighting system proved particularly valuable, providing interpretable insights into the model’s internal preferences and confidence dynamics. Together, these results position Meta-Conformer-XAI as a reliable solution for medical imaging applications, especially in hematology, where diagnostic transparency is just as important as predictive performance.

Future work will include the evaluation of the proposed architecture using multiple centers, model compression, and integration of the model into existing clinical workflows.

Limitations and scope of claims: All experimental evidence presented in this paper was obtained on two public benchmark datasets (ALL-IDB and C-NMC). We did not perform external validation on prospectively acquired clinical samples, did not evaluate the framework across different staining protocols or microscope vendors, and did not run a clinician-in-the-loop usability study. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted as proof-of-concept evidence that the proposed architectural choices yield measurable and statistically significant gains on existing benchmarks, and not as evidence of clinical readiness. Statements describing the framework as “clinically applicable” or “deployable” in earlier drafts have been replaced throughout the manuscript with “clinically relevant pending external validation.”

The performance and behaviour of Meta-Conformer-XAI should be considered against three lines of recent work and summarized in Table 13: (i) CNN-based ALL classifiers (Baig et al., 2022; Shawly and Alsheikhy, 2022; Almadhor et al., 2023), which report 0.96–0.97 accuracy on ALL-IDB; (ii) ViT-based and hybrid CNN-ViT classifiers for blood-smear images (Gupta et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2022; Amin et al., 2022), which report 0.94–0.97; and (iii) explainable hybrid frameworks for related diagnostic tasks (Bayahya et al., 2025; Jammal and Dahab, 2025; Jammal et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024). On ALL-IDB, our 0.9924 ± 0.0031 accuracy is competitive with the best reported figures, while on the larger and more heterogeneous C-NMC dataset our 0.9636 ± 0.0048 obtained under patient-disjoint splits is, to our knowledge, the strongest reported figure that simultaneously provides quantitative interpretability validation. Crucially, the differentiator is not the absolute accuracy figure (several CNN-only or ViT-only models report similar numbers) but the combination of (a) per-instance adaptive fusion (DAFF), (b) instance-level meta-routing, (c) calibrated confidence via the entropy-regularised reward, and (d) a quantitatively validated explainability suite. Compared with CausalFormer-HMC (Jammal & Dahab, 2025), which uses counterfactual reasoning, and Neuro-Bridge-X (Jammal et al., 2025), which uses neuro-symbolic fuzzy reasoning, the present work targets the orthogonal axis of fusion-and-confidence, suggesting these approaches are complementary rather than competing. The remaining limitations are: absence of external multi-center validation, no clinician-in-the-loop study, and the static input resolution (128 × 128). These are explicitly addressed in the limitations paragraph below and in future work.

Conclusions

Meta-Conformer-XAI is a hybrid deep-learning approach that has been developed as a novel, non-invasive method for diagnosing acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) from peripheral blood smear images. It incorporates the following modules within its architecture: a convolutional neural network (CNN) for capturing morphological detail; a convolutional block attention module (CBAM) for capturing contextual dependencies; a vision transformer (ViT) for learning multi-scale representations; and a dual-attention feature fusion (DAFF) module to integrate these different representations into a single feature representation. When applied to the ALL Image Dataset and the C-NMC dataset, this approach achieved very good accuracy and area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUROC). In addition to being able to classify patients accurately, the proposed framework also includes both a meta-learning path controller that dynamically routes features based on their relevance, and a reinforcement learning based confidence estimator that allows for uncertainty-aware classification. Together, these two aspects enhance the strength of the entire methodology and offer the potential for clinicians to better understand the images that are being assessed. The capability of generating visually interpretable attribution maps utilizing four explainability techniques (Grad-CAM, LIME, SHAP, and Integrated Gradients), provide potential understanding of what features of the images are most highly correlated with the previously studied hematological characteristics.

Although the results from the first study have shown promise, there are still numerous limitations to the work presented in this paper. Further validation of the results will need to be completed on an independent clinical cohort. Although the inclusion of a reinforcement-based confidence estimator in the architecture, along with the increased complexity of the architecture, may result in a number of computational challenges that could potentially hinder the deployment of the system in limited-resource environments, the efficacy of the reinforcement-based confidence estimator has already been established. It is therefore likely that investigating how it behaves and contributes to the decision-making process as the training parameters vary would be beneficial. Future areas of research include the expansion of the diversity of the dataset by including multimodal clinical data and reducing the computational burden of the system via model compression. Clinical studies that assess the feasibility of using the system in a clinical environment through clinician-in-the-loop validation and real-world deployment of the system will be necessary.
