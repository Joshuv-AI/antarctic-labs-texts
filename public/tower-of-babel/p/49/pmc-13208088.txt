Costal synostosis mimicking supraclavicular lymphadenopathy in a breast cancer patient: A diagnostic pitfall
Authors: Hicham Benramdane, Redouane El Hazzat, Mohammed Arghal, Imane Kamaoui, Hanane Hadj Kacem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe discovery of a supraclavicular mass in patients with a history of breast cancer is highly suspicious for metastatic lymph node involvement. We report the case of a 68-year-old woman with a history of left breast carcinoma treated by mastectomy one year earlier. During routine follow-up, clinical examination revealed a firm mass in the left supraclavicular fossa suggestive of supraclavicular lymphadenopathy. Ultrasound examination showed a poorly defined structure with marked posterior acoustic shadowing, making characterization difficult. Further evaluation with computed tomography demonstrated a fusion between the first and second ribs consistent with costal synostosis. This anatomical variant explained the palpable supraclavicular prominence that clinically mimicked nodal disease. This case highlights a rare diagnostic pitfall and emphasizes the importance of cross-sectional imaging in differentiating benign anatomical variants from metastatic lymphadenopathy in oncologic patients.Keywords: Breast cancer, Supraclavicular mass, Costal synostosis, Ultrasound, Computed tomography, Diagnostic pitfall

Introduction

In patients with a history of breast cancer, the detection of a new supraclavicular mass during follow-up raises strong suspicion of metastatic lymph node involvement. Supraclavicular lymphadenopathy is considered an important indicator of locoregional disease spread and may significantly influence staging, prognosis, and therapeutic management. Therefore, careful clinical and radiological evaluation of supraclavicular masses is essential in oncologic patients [1].

However, not all palpable supraclavicular masses correspond to lymph node pathology. Several benign conditions and anatomical variants may mimic lymphadenopathy, leading to potential diagnostic pitfalls. Among these, congenital anomalies of the ribs represent an uncommon but important differential diagnosis [2].

Costal synostosis, defined as the congenital fusion between adjacent ribs, is a rare anatomical variation that most frequently involves the first rib and may occur with the second rib or with a cervical rib. This anomaly is usually asymptomatic and often discovered incidentally on imaging studies. In some cases, however, it may present as a palpable supraclavicular mass or produce symptoms related to neurovascular compression [3].

Because of its rarity and unusual clinical presentation, costal synostosis may be misinterpreted as lymphadenopathy or other pathological masses, particularly in patients with known malignancies. Cross-sectional imaging, especially computed tomography, plays a crucial role in accurately identifying this anatomical variant and avoiding unnecessary investigations or misdiagnosis [3].

We report a case of costal synostosis between the first and second ribs presenting as a supraclavicular mass in a patient with breast cancer, highlighting an important diagnostic pitfall in oncologic imaging.

Case presentation

A 68-year-old woman with a history of left breast carcinoma (T2N0M0) diagnosed one year ago was referred for routine follow-up after mastectomy. At the time of initial staging and extension work-up, a left supraclavicular mass was present but overlooked, as it had not been detected clinically or on imaging, including CT scans. During her follow-up visit, the oncologist palpated a firm, fixed mass in the left supraclavicular fossa, measuring approximately 2 × 1.5 cm. The mass was deep, non-tender, and immobile, raising suspicion for metastatic supraclavicular lymphadenopathy. No other cervical, axillary, or infraclavicular lymphadenopathy was observed, and there were no associated skin changes, erythema, or local warmth. Routine laboratory tests were normal, and tumor markers including CA 15-3 and CEA were within normal limits.

High-frequency ultrasound of the left supraclavicular region revealed a poorly defined, hypoechoic structure with prominent posterior acoustic shadowing, limiting visualization of deeper structures. The lesion measured approximately 2.1 × 1.7 cm and showed no internal vascularity on Doppler imaging. The surrounding soft tissues appeared normal. The marked posterior acoustic shadowing suggested the presence of a dense structure, most likely of osseous origin. Differential diagnoses based on ultrasound findings included calcified lymph node, ossified soft tissue lesion, or an underlying bony anomaly. The absence of vascularity and the intense acoustic shadowing favored a benign bony structure rather than a pathological lymph node.

To clarify the nature of the mass, a contrast-enhanced cervical CT scan was performed. On CT imaging, a well-defined bony structure was identified in the left supraclavicular region, corresponding anatomically to a congenital fusion between the anterior arches of the first and second ribs. The lesion demonstrated cortical bone continuity with both ribs, without cortical destruction or irregularity. There was no associated soft tissue mass, periosteal reaction, or adjacent inflammatory changes. The surrounding muscles, vascular structures, and fat planes were preserved. No enlarged supraclavicular or cervical lymph nodes were identified (Fig. 1). Three-dimensional reconstruction confirmed that this was a benign anatomical variant, explaining the palpable supraclavicular mass (Fig. 2).

This finding had been retrospectively present on the initial staging CT scan but was overlooked. This diagnostic miss can be explained by several factors, including satisfaction of search bias after identification of the primary breast lesion, limited attention to the supraclavicular region, and low clinical suspicion for a congenital bony anomaly in this context. This highlights the importance of systematic image review and awareness of anatomical variants, particularly in oncologic patients where any palpable mass may be prematurely attributed to metastatic disease.

Based on these findings, a diagnosis of benign costal synostosis mimicking supraclavicular lymphadenopathy was established. No invasive procedures were performed, and the patient was reassured regarding the benign nature of the lesion. She continued routine oncologic follow-up, and subsequent visits demonstrated stable imaging findings with no evidence of recurrence or new lymphadenopathy. This case illustrates a rare diagnostic pitfall and emphasizes the importance of cross-sectional imaging in differentiating benign anatomical variants from metastatic disease in oncologic patients.

Discussion

Costal synostosis is a rare congenital anomaly characterized by the fusion of two adjacent ribs. It can occur at any level of the thoracic cage; however, involvement of the first and second ribs is particularly uncommon and rarely encountered in routine clinical practice. It is usually asymptomatic and incidentally detected on imaging, but can be easily overlooked, especially when imaging is performed for other clinical indications or without prior suspicion. In our case, the anomaly was missed during the initial staging CT, illustrating how such variants may escape detection even during targeted examinations [4].

The main clinical challenge lies in the differential diagnosis of supraclavicular masses, particularly in oncologic patients. In addition to costal synostosis, several entities should be considered, including cervical ribs, calcified lymph nodes, osteochondromas, and other congenital or acquired bony abnormalities. These conditions may present as palpable masses and closely mimic metastatic lymphadenopathy, especially in patients with a history of malignancy [5].

The term synostosis refers to the osseous fusion of two normally separate bones, resulting from abnormal segmentation during early embryologic development. Although uncommon, rib synostosis has been described in both anatomical and radiological literature, highlighting the importance of recognizing such variants to avoid diagnostic errors [5].

Imaging plays a central role in differentiating these entities. Ultrasound, often used as a first-line modality for superficial masses, may suggest a bony origin through marked posterior acoustic shadowing, but remains limited in precise characterization. Computed tomography is the modality of choice, as it provides detailed evaluation of bone architecture, demonstrates cortical continuity between fused ribs, and confirms the absence of an associated soft tissue component. It also allows exclusion of alternative diagnoses such as pathological lymphadenopathy or bone tumors. Three-dimensional reconstructions further improve anatomical assessment in complex cases [6].

In oncologic patients, any newly detected mass raises concern for recurrence or metastatic disease. Misinterpretation may lead to unnecessary invasive procedures such as biopsy or surgical exploration. Therefore, accurate recognition of benign anatomical variants such as costal synostosis is essential to avoid unwarranted interventions, reduce patient anxiety, and ensure appropriate clinical management [7].

This case highlights the importance of systematic image analysis and the need to include anatomical variants in the differential diagnosis, particularly in oncologic settings where the risk of overdiagnosis is significant.

Conclusion

Costal synostosis is a rare congenital anomaly that may mimic supraclavicular lymphadenopathy, especially in oncologic patients. In the presence of a supraclavicular mass with marked posterior acoustic shadowing on ultrasound, a bony origin should always be considered. CT is essential to confirm the diagnosis and avoid unnecessary invasive procedures.
