A Multi-Head Attention Transformer Model for Wearable in Situ Fall Detection
Authors: JINGXIAO TIAN, SHENGJIE XU, PETER GERSTOFT, MAHASWETA SARKAR, ALISON A. MOORE, PATRICK MERCIER, CHRISTOPHER PAOLINI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Falls represent a critical health hazard for elderly individuals, often leading to severe injuries and decreased quality of life. While existing fall detection systems predominantly rely on threshold-based algorithms or conventional machine learning approaches like CNNs and RNNs, they frequently exhibit limitations in capturing the complex temporal dependencies and spatial features inherent in fall data. This paper presents a novel multi-head attention transformer architecture specifically designed for wearable in situ fall detection. Our approach leverages the transformer’s self-attention mechanism to effectively model long-range dependencies in inertial measurement unit (IMU) sensor data collected from a shinbone-mounted device. The proposed architecture introduces several key innovations: 1) a position-aware embedding layer that preserves the temporal structure of IMU signals, 2) multi-head self-attention blocks that simultaneously attend to different signal features across varying time scales, and 3) a hybrid feature fusion module that combines global context with local patterns. Experimental results on our human subjects dataset demonstrate the critical importance of addressing dataset imbalance. A baseline model trained on imbalanced data achieved only 48.0% fall sensitivity, rendering it unsafe for practical use. By training our proposed transformer architecture on a large, balanced dataset with a specialized Focal Loss function, we increased fall sensitivity to 90.5% on the test set while maintaining an overall accuracy of 93.1% (validation best epoch: sensitivity 92.5% and specificity 95.8%, see Fig. 3). Furthermore, we present a complete hardware/software co-design, including a custom-designed wearable PCB with optimized power management and a comprehensive strategy for deploying our model on an ultra-low-power Lattice Semiconductor iCE40UP5K FPGA, achieving efficient operation with only 22 mA current consumption. We frame this study as technical feasibility and edge deployment validation; direct elderly clinical validation remains future work.

INTRODUCTION

Falls represent one of the most significant health risks for elderly populations worldwide, with the World Health Organization reporting that approximately 684,000 fatal falls occur globally each year [1]. Among adults over 65 years of age, falls are the leading cause of injury-related morbidity and mortality in the United States, with an estimated 29,000,000 falls reported in 2015, resulting in 33,000 deaths [2]. The economic burden associated with fall-related injuries exceeds $50 billion annually in the United States alone [3], underscoring the urgent need for effective technological interventions to detect and prevent falls.

Early detection and prompt response to falls significantly improve outcomes, with research indicating that the time interval between a fall event and medical assistance is inversely correlated with survival rates and reduced morbidity [4]. However, studies have shown that more than 20% of older adults who experience a fall remain on the ground for extended periods, often exceeding one hour [5]. This delayed response not only increases the risk of complications such as pressure sores, dehydration, and hypothermia but also exacerbates psychological effects like fear of falling, potentially leading to restricted activity and reduced independence.

Traditional fall detection approaches have predominantly relied on threshold-based algorithms that monitor acceleration peaks or angular velocities to identify fall events [6]. While these methods provide straightforward implementations, they often suffer from low sensitivity and high false alarm rates in real-world conditions [7]. More recently, machine learning techniques, including Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), have been applied to fall detection, achieving improved performance through their ability to automatically extract relevant features from sensor data [8]. Foundational discussion on machine learning and intelligent communications has also highlighted broader cross-domain opportunities for intelligent sensing systems [9]. Recent embedded and edge-oriented studies in 2024 further reinforce this trajectory, including wearable deep-learning sensors and edge-AI fall detection implementations for elderly-care scenarios [10], [11], [12], [13].

Despite these advancements, existing machine learning approaches still face significant challenges when deployed in wearable devices for in situ fall detection. CNNs, while effective at extracting spatial features, struggle to capture the complex temporal dependencies characteristic of human movements preceding and during fall events. Conversely, RNNs and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks effectively model sequential patterns but often face limitations in parallelization and computational efficiency, making them less suitable for low-power wearable implementations [14]. Furthermore, a crucial challenge often unaddressed is the inherent class imbalance in fall detection datasets, where non-fall events vastly outnumber falls, leading to models that are accurate on paper but have unacceptably high miss rates for real fall events, a fundamental challenge also confirmed in recent class-imbalanced sensor studies [15]. To better reflect recent progress, we explicitly position this work against recent wearable edge fall-detection studies reported in 2023–2024, including ultra-low-power FPGA deployment and edge inferencing baselines [2], [16].

In this paper, we introduce a multi-head attention transformer architecture specifically designed for wearable in situ fall detection. We choose the transformer primarily for algorithmic reasons: fall recognition in IMU data depends on long time-sequence context spanning pre-fall evolution, transition, and impact, and self-attention can explicitly model these long-range interactions while still preserving local dynamics through multi-head representations. In contrast, CNNs are constrained by local receptive fields unless substantially deepened, and LSTM-style recurrence can become less efficient for long-context modeling. We therefore do not claim that transformers are inherently easier to deploy on edge hardware, especially given recent advances in bit-serial and digit-serial acceleration for CNN/LSTM edge inference [17], [18], [19]. Instead, our contribution is a task-specific hardware–software co-design that makes this long-sequence transformer practical under strict FPGA resource and power limits.

Building upon our previous work in fall detection sensor development and placement optimization [2], [16], we implement our transformer model on an ultra-low-power Lattice Semiconductor iCE40UP5K FPGA. This implementation achieves high detection accuracy while maintaining the power efficiency required for practical wearable applications.

RELATED WORK

RADITIONAL FALL DETECTION METHODS

Fall detection systems have evolved significantly over the past two decades, transitioning from simple threshold-based approaches to sophisticated machine learning algorithms. Early fall detection systems predominantly relied on threshold-based methods, where falls were identified when acceleration or angular velocity measurements exceeded predefined thresholds [20], [21]. These approaches offered the advantages of straightforward implementation and low computational requirements, making them suitable for embedded systems with limited resources.

Threshold-based approaches, however, frequently exhibit high sensitivity to parameter settings and individual movement patterns, resulting in suboptimal performance across diverse user populations [7]. A comprehensive evaluation by Bagalá et al. [7] revealed that algorithms claiming detection sensitivity and specificity exceeding 90% in controlled environments frequently yielded substantially lower performance metrics (sensitivity: 57.0% ± 27.3%, specificity: 83.0% ± 30.3%) when evaluated on real-world fall data.

More sophisticated approaches have employed statistical and signal processing techniques. Bourke et al. [22] utilized a tri-axial accelerometer placed on the trunk to distinguish falls from activities of daily living (ADLs) by analyzing peak acceleration values. Klenk et al. [23] proposed using velocity features derived from accelerometer data, demonstrating improved discrimination between fall events and ADLs. These methods, while more robust than simple thresholding, still face challenges in generalizing across different types of falls and user characteristics.

MACHINE LEARNING APPROACHES IN FALL DETECTION

The limitations of traditional methods have motivated the application of machine learning techniques to fall detection. Initial machine learning approaches utilized classical algorithms such as Support Vector Machines (SVMs) [24], Random Forests [25], and k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) [26], typically operating on handcrafted features extracted from inertial measurement unit (IMU) data.

Khan and Hoey [27] employed an SVM classifier with features derived from a tri-axial accelerometer, achieving 97.8% accuracy in controlled laboratory conditions. Similarly, Anguita et al. [25] utilized Random Forests on a combination of time and frequency domain features extracted from smartphone accelerometer data, reporting 96.5% accuracy. While these approaches demonstrated improved performance compared to threshold-based methods, they still relied heavily on domain-specific feature engineering, potentially limiting their generalizability.

DEEP LEARNING FOR FALL DETECTION

The emergence of deep learning has prompted a paradigm shift in fall detection research, enabling end-to-end learning directly from raw sensor data without extensive feature engineering. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have been widely adopted due to their ability to automatically extract relevant features from time-series data. Musci et al. [14] implemented a CNN architecture for real-time fall detection on wearable devices, achieving 95.2% accuracy on the SisFall dataset [28].

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), particularly Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, have been employed to capture the sequential nature of fall events. Torti et al. [29] developed an LSTM-based fall detection system implemented on a wearable device, demonstrating 92.8% accuracy with significantly fewer false positives compared to threshold-based approaches. Similarly, Fakhrulddin et al. [30] proposed a hybrid CNN-LSTM architecture that leverages both spatial and temporal features, achieving 93.6% accuracy on the UMAFall dataset [31].

In our previous work [2], we developed a ConvolutionBatch Normalization-Scale-ReLU (CBSR) CNN model implemented on an ultra-low-power FPGA, achieving 83.5% accuracy in hardware simulation. While this approach demonstrated the feasibility of deploying deep learning models on resource-constrained devices, it highlighted limitations in capturing complex temporal dependencies characteristic of fall events.

TRANSFORMER MODELS FOR TIME-SERIES DATA

Transformer models, originally introduced by Vaswani et al. [32] for natural language processing tasks, have recently demonstrated exceptional performance in modeling sequential data across various domains. The self-attention mechanism, which forms the core of transformer architectures, enables the model to weigh the importance of different time steps when making predictions, effectively capturing long-range dependencies and complex patterns.

While transformers have achieved remarkable success in NLP and computer vision, their application to time-series data analysis, particularly in healthcare and wearable computing, remains relatively unexplored. Recent studies, however, have begun to demonstrate their potential in these domains. Li et al. [33] employed transformer models for ECG classification, achieving state-of-the-art performance by effectively modeling temporal dependencies in cardiac signals. Similarly, Gao et al. [34] proposed a Spatial-Temporal Attention Network (STAN) based on transformer architectures for human activity recognition using wearable sensors, demonstrating improved performance compared to CNN and LSTM approaches. More recent reports also extend transformer-family methods toward fall-related recognition tasks in smart IoT and edge computing settings [35], [36]. We also include recent edge-oriented fall-detection references from 2023–2024 to keep the literature context current for wearable deployment constraints [2], [16].

HARDWARE IMPLEMENTATION OF DEEP LEARNING MODELS

Implementing deep learning models on resource-constrained devices presents significant challenges, particularly for wearable applications where power efficiency is paramount. Traditional approaches to deploying neural networks on embedded systems have focused on model compression techniques such as pruning [37], quantization [38], and knowledge distillation [39].

Field-Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs) have emerged as promising platforms for implementing deep learning models in edge devices due to their flexibility, parallelization capabilities, and energy efficiency. Qiu et al. [40] demonstrated that FPGA implementations of CNNs can achieve up to 10x energy efficiency improvement compared to GPU implementations. Similarly, Wang et al. [41] proposed a Deep Learning Accelerator Unit (DLAU) for FPGAs that achieves significant speedup and energy efficiency for CNN inference.

In our previous work [16], we implemented a CNN-based fall detection system on a Lattice Semiconductor iCE40UP5K FPGA, achieving acceptable accuracy while maintaining ultra-low power consumption. This implementation utilized the Compact CNN Accelerator IP Core provided by Lattice Semiconductor, which is specifically designed to accelerate convolutional operations on resource-constrained devices.

RESEARCH GAP AND OUR APPROACH

Despite significant advancements in fall detection technologies, several challenges persist. Traditional machine learning approaches often struggle to capture the complex temporal dependencies characteristic of fall events, while deep learning models face implementation challenges on resource-constrained wearable devices. Furthermore, existing approaches frequently overlook the importance of optimal sensor placement, which our previous research has identified as a critical factor in fall detection performance [42].

Our work addresses these limitations by introducing a novel multi-head attention transformer architecture specifically designed for wearable in situ fall detection. By leveraging the self-attention mechanism, our approach effectively captures both short-term patterns and long-range dependencies in IMU data, enabling more accurate discrimination between fall events and ADLs. Furthermore, we demonstrate a comprehensive implementation strategy for deploying transformer models on ultra-low-power FPGAs, addressing key challenges related to memory constraints, computational efficiency, and power management.

METHODS

DATA COLLECTION AND PROCESSING

DATASET OVERVIEW

Our study utilized the SDSU HealthLINK Center Falls Dataset [43], which is based on induced falls of human subjects, expanding upon our previous work [2], [16]. The data collection process involved participants wearing a data logging device attached to their tibia (shinbone), a location our prior research identified as optimal for fall detection [42]. This paper compares two distinct modeling approaches to highlight the critical impact of dataset quality. Our baseline model (Model ImbalancedNet) used an imbalanced dataset of 9,195 samples (66.6% No Fall/33.4% Fall). Our advanced model (Model BalancedTransformer) used an expanded dataset of 42,351 samples (4.6x increase) with improved balance (65.2% No Fall / 34.8% Fall). We use these terms consistently throughout the manuscript: Imbalanced Dataset and Balanced Dataset.

The dataset includes recordings from various activities categorized as follows. Non-fall activities (ADLs) include standing (STD), walking (WAL), jogging (JOG), jumping (JUM), standing up (STU), sitting down (STN), stair climbing (SCH), car sitting in (CSI), and car sitting out (CSO). Fall activities include forward falling (FOL), forward falling with knees (FKL), backward falling sitting on a chair (BSC), and sideways falling (SDL). Each recording consists of 6-axis IMU data (3-axis acceleration and 3-axis gyroscope) sampled at 50 Hz using an STMicroelectronics LSM6DSM inertial measurement unit.

MULTI-HEAD ATTENTION TRANSFORMER ARCHITECTURE

MODEL OVERVIEW

Our proposed architecture consists of five main components: (1) input embedding layer, (2) positional encoding module, (3) multi-head self-attention blocks, (4) feature fusion module, and (5) classification head. A comprehensive schematic of this architecture is presented in Fig. 2. As illustrated, the model first processes the input signal through a series of four enhanced CNN layers, which act as a powerful feature extractor. The resulting feature maps are then augmented with sinusoidal positional encodings to preserve temporal information before being passed to the core of the model: four stacked Transformer Blocks. Within these blocks, multi-head self-attention mechanisms identify complex patterns across the time series. Finally, the output from the transformer is passed through a global average pooling layer and processed by an enhanced MLP classification head to yield the final class probabilities. This architecture is specifically designed to effectively capture both temporal dependencies and spatial features in IMU sensor data, enabling accurate discrimination between fall events and ADLs.

INPUT EMBEDDING

The input embedding layer transforms the raw sensor data into a high-dimensional representation suitable for processing by the transformer blocks. Given an input tensor X∈ℝT×F, where T=170 is the number of time steps and F = 6 is the number of features, the embedding layer applies a linear transformation followed by a non-linearity: (2) E=ReLUXWe+be where We∈ℝF×D and be∈ℝD are learnable parameters, and D=128 is the embedding dimension. This transformation maps each time step of the input sequence to a D-dimensional vector, resulting in an embedded representation E∈ℝT×D.

POSITIONAL ENCODING

To provide the model with information about the temporal ordering of the input sequence, we incorporate positional encoding. Unlike language models, where position information is somewhat arbitrary, the temporal sequence in fall detection is critical, as the order and timing of motion patterns directly influence the classification outcome.

We employ sinusoidal positional encoding as defined by Vaswani et al. [32]: (3) PE(pos,2i)=sinpos/100002i/D (4) PE(pos,2i+1)=cospos/100002i/D where pos is the position in the sequence and i is the dimension index. This encoding is added to the embedded representation: (5) E′=E+PE

The sinusoidal functions have wavelengths forming a geometric progression, allowing the model to learn to attend to relative positions with different scales. This is particularly important for fall detection, where patterns may occur at different time scales, from rapid acceleration changes during impact to gradual orientation shifts before a fall.

MULTI-HEAD SELF-ATTENTION

The core of our transformer architecture is the multi-head self-attention mechanism. For each head h, three linear projections are applied to create query (Qh), key (Kh), and value (Vh) representations: (6) Qh=E′WhQ (7) Kh=E′WhK (8) Vh=E′WhV where WhQ,WhK,WhV∈ℝD×Dh are the learnable parameters and Dh=D/H is the dimension per head, with H = 8 being the number of attention heads.

The attention mechanism computes a weighted sum of values, where the weights are determined by the compatibility between queries and keys: (9) AttentionQh,Kh,Vh=softmaxQhKhTDhVh

The outputs from all heads are concatenated and linearly transformed: (10) MultiHeadE′=Concathead1,…,headHWO where WO∈ℝD×D is a learnable parameter.

The multi-head attention allows the model to jointly attend to information from different representation subspaces and time scales. For fall detection, this enables the model to simultaneously focus on different aspects of the motion pattern, such as rapid acceleration changes, rotation rates, and longer-term orientation shifts.

TRANSFORMER BLOCKS

Each transformer block consists of a multi-head self-attention layer followed by a position-wise feed-forward network, with residual connections and layer normalization applied after each sub-layer: (11) Z=LayerNormE′+MultiHeadE′ (12) TransformerBlockE′=LayerNormZ+FFNZ where FFN is a two-layer feed-forward network with ReLU activation: (13) FFNZ=max0,ZW1+b1W2+b2

Our architecture employs four stacked transformer blocks, allowing the model to build increasingly abstract representations of the input sequence. The residual connections facilitate gradient flow during training, while layer normalization stabilizes the learning process.

MODEL TRAINING

TRAINING PROCEDURE

We trained our model using an 80:20 train-test split with strict subject-level partitioning, ensuring that data from the same participant never appears in both training and testing sets. All overlapping windows from one subject were kept in the same partition to prevent leakage through near-duplicate temporal segments. Strict subject-level validation is critical in biomedical machine learning because cross-subject leakage can inflate reported performance [47]. This protocol provides a more realistic estimate of generalization to unseen users.

The advanced model (BalancedTransformer) was trained for 100 epochs using the Adam optimizer with a batch size of 32, with an 80-epoch run showing excellent results. We employed a stable training strategy consisting of a 5-epoch linear warmup of the learning rate, followed by a ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler that reduced the learning rate when validation loss plateaued. To prevent overfitting while allowing the model to find an optimal solution, early stopping was implemented with a patience of 20 epochs. Additionally, gradient clipping with a threshold of 0.8 was used to prevent gradient explosion during training. The entire training history is depicted in Figure 3, which visualizes the convergence of training and validation loss, the corresponding increase in accuracy, and the learning rate schedule. The plots confirm a stable training process, with the model achieving its best validation performance at epoch 58.

To address class imbalance, we applied class weighting during training, with weights inversely proportional to class frequencies: (16) wc=NC×Nc where N is the total number of samples, C is the number of classes, and Nc is the number of samples in class c.

LOSS FUNCTION AND METRICS

A key innovation in our advanced model (BalancedTransformer) was the use of focal loss, which is specifically designed to address class imbalance. Standard cross-entropy loss can be overwhelmed by a majority class (non-falls), leading to models that achieve high accuracy but have poor sensitivity to the minority class (falls). Focal loss mitigates this by adding a modulating factor (1 – pt)γ to the crossentropy loss, which down-weights the loss for well-classified examples. This forces the model to focus on learning hard-to-classify examples from the minority class. This formulation is theoretically well-founded for severe class imbalance settings in biomedical ML pipelines [48]. It is defined as: (17) ℒFLpt=−αt1−ptγlogpt where pt is the model’s estimated probability for the ground-truth class, γ is the focusing parameter (we used γ = 2.0), and αt is a balancing parameter. In implementation, class-specific αt values were used (e.g., α = 0.25 for the majority class) with stronger weighting on the fall class, together with inverse-frequency class weighting. This strategy was instrumental in improving sensitivity while maintaining useful specificity.

The model was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall (sensitivity), F1-score, and area under the ROC curve (AUC). Because deployment is safety-critical, we report both threshold-independent discrimination (AUC) and threshold-dependent operating behavior. In particular, the deployed operating point prioritizes high sensitivity (0.905) while controlling false positives (FPR 0.055).

IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS

SOFTWARE IMPLEMENTATION

The model was implemented using TensorFlow 2.4.0 with the Keras API. For data processing and augmentation, we used NumPy, Pandas, and SciPy libraries. The training was performed on an NVIDIA V100 GPU, while inference performance was evaluated on both the CPU and our FPGA implementation.

The model hyperparameters were determined through grid search optimization, with the final configuration as follows: embedding dimension of 128, 8 attention heads, 4 transformer blocks, feed-forward network dimension of 512, dropout rates of 0.35 (transformer) and 0.25 (MLP), Adam optimizer with warmup and scheduler for learning rate, batch size of 32, and 100 training epochs.

To strictly adhere to the resource constraints of the Lattice iCE40UP5K FPGA (5,280 LUTs, 8 DSP blocks, 1 MB SPRAM), we rigorously analyzed the hardware implications of our architectural choices. Figure 4 demonstrates the impact of substituting the floating-point Softmax operation with a fixed-point ReLU approximation. While overall accuracy drops negligibly (from 93.4% to 93.1%), avoiding the expensive exponential function saves 9.5% of LUTs and reduces latency by approximately 25%. Furthermore, Figure 5 justifies our selection of 8 attention heads. This configuration optimally maximizes the 8 available DSP blocks for parallel matrix multiplications. Scaling to 16 heads exceeds the logical limits, forcing sequential DSP reuse and causing inference latency to spike to 5.42 ms, offering no practical benefit.

EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS

PERFORMANCE EVALUATION

CLASSIFICATION PERFORMANCE

We evaluated our multi-head attention transformer model against several baseline approaches, including threshold-based algorithms, traditional machine learning methods, and other deep learning architectures. Table 1 presents the classification performance of all methods on our test dataset.

To establish the statistical significance of our results, we employed 5-fold cross-validation with subject-level stratification (Fig. 6a), yielding a tight 95% Confidence Interval for the Transformer’s sensitivity (90.5% ± 1.8%). Following standard statistical methodologies for comparing supervised classifiers [49], McNemar’s test (Fig. 6b) decisively confirmed that the Transformer’s ability to classify previously missed falls is statistically significant against all baselines (p < 0.001).

Crucially, to decouple the benefits of our balanced dataset strategy from the architectural advantages of the Transformer, we conducted a 2 × 2 ablation study and a unified baseline re-training (Fig. 7). Applying the balanced data strategy to the baseline CNN drastically improved its sensitivity (from 48.0% to 78.3%), yet it remained below critical safety margins. Even when all strong baselines (LSTM, CNN-LSTM) were re-trained under the exact same balanced protocol with identical augmentations, the best baseline only reached 84.5% sensitivity. The Transformer’s dominant 90.5% sensitivity confirms its inherent superiority in capturing complex fall dynamics.

Our proposed advanced transformer model (Balanced-Transformer), trained on the balanced dataset with Focal Loss, achieved superior performance in the most critical metrics. The baseline model (ImbalancedNet), while showing a respectable 84.3% accuracy, had a dangerously low Fall Sensitivity of only 48.0%, meaning it failed to detect more than half of all falls. In stark contrast, our advanced model (BalancedTransformer) boosted Fall Sensitivity by 42.5 percentage points to 90.5%, a massive improvement in safety and reliability. This came with only a minor, acceptable trade-off in specificity and maintained excellent precision. This result clearly demonstrates that addressing the data imbalance problem was the single most important factor in creating an effective fall detection system. The significant improvement over the CNN-based approach previously implemented on FPGA (83.5% accuracy) [2] demonstrates the effectiveness of the transformer architecture in capturing the complex temporal patterns characteristic of fall events.

HARDWARE IMPLEMENTATION

CUSTOM WEARABLE HARDWARE DESIGN

The successful deployment of our transformer model for in-situ fall detection necessitates a hardware platform meticulously co-designed to meet the stringent constraints of a wearable device: ultra-low power consumption, a compact form factor, and sufficient computational throughput for realtime inference. To this end, we engineered a custom Printed Circuit Board (PCB) to serve as a robust, power-optimized host for our system. A 3D render and photograph of the final assembled board are presented in Figure 15, illustrating the compact component layout and mechanical mounting points (Fig. 15a) and the physical realization of the hardware design (Fig. 15b). The complete electronic schematic, shown in Figure 14, provides a detailed view of the interconnected subsystems, which are elaborated upon in the following sections.

CORE PROCESSING AND SENSING

The computational core of the device is the Lattice iCE40UP5K FPGA (U2), chosen for its ultra-low static power consumption and a resource profile well-suited for our quantized transformer accelerator. Its key specifications include 5.3K Look-Up Tables (LUTs), 1 Mb of Embedded SPRAM, 120Kb of Embedded Block RAM (EBR), and eight hardened Digital Signal Processing (DSP) blocks. These DSPs are particularly advantageous, as they efficiently accelerate the numerous multiply-accumulate (MAC) operations central to the transformer’s multi-head attention and feed-forward network layers, enabling high-throughput inference without the power overhead of a general-purpose processor.

High-fidelity motion data is acquired from an STMicroelectronics LSM6DSM (U9), a 6-axis Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) that combines a 3-axis digital accelerometer and a 3-axis digital gyroscope. As shown in Figure 14(c), the IMU is interfaced with the FPGA via a 400 kHz I2C bus, providing a continuous stream of raw motion data to the hardware preprocessing pipeline. The IMU’s programmable interrupt pins (INT1/INT2) are connected to the FPGA, enabling sophisticated, event-driven power management schemes such as wake-on-motion, which allows the FPGA to remain in a deep-sleep state until significant movement is detected.

POWER MANAGEMENT SUBSYSTEM

A robust power management subsystem is critical for ensuring operational stability and maximizing battery life. The device is designed to be powered by a single-cell Lithium-Polymer (Li-Po) battery. The power tree, shown in Figure 14(b), employs two dedicated Low-Dropout (LDO) regulators to create a dual-rail power architecture. An AZ1117-3.3 (U4) generates the primary +3.3V rail that powers the system’s I/O banks and most peripherals, including the IMU and flash memory. A second AZ1117-1.2 (U1) provides a separate, stable +1.2V rail exclusively for the FPGA core.

This dual-rail architecture is essential for meeting the distinct voltage requirements of the FPGA and, crucially, for isolating the sensitive core logic from I/O-induced voltage fluctuations, thereby preserving timing margins. Power integrity is further enhanced by a ferrite bead (FB1) for high-frequency noise suppression and extensive decoupling with ceramic capacitors placed close to the power pins of active components.

MEMORY AND CONFIGURATION

The FPGA configuration bitstream and the 0.50 MB quantized transformer model are stored off-chip on a 32 Mbit N25Q032A13ESC40F flash memory IC (U6). This non-volatile storage ensures the system is ready upon power-up. The FPGA’s controller SPI configuration mode is utilized to load the bitstream from this flash memory automatically at startup. The same SPI interface is subsequently used by the operational firmware to read the transformer model’s weights into the on-chip block RAM as needed during inference. For storing persistent devicespecific data, such as calibration offsets, a small AT24CS64 I2C EEPROM (U3) is also included.

PERIPHERALS AND EXTENSIBILITY

The design includes essential user interface components, such as three status LEDs (D3-D5) for visual feedback and a user input button (SW2). For debugging and data logging, a CH340C USB-to-UART bridge (U7) provides a convenient interface to a host computer. For future development, the PCB also incorporates an RN4871 Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) module (U10) and a DS3231M Real-Time Clock (RTC) (U5) with its own coin cell backup (BT2). While these components provide a clear path for adding wireless alert functionality or precise event timestamping, they were intentionally disabled during the power and performance testing for this study to isolate and accurately measure the core system’s metrics.

SYSTEM BLOCK DIAGRAM

The system consists of the following main components. The I2C Interface Controller manages communication with the LSM6DSM IMU sensor, configuring the sensor and receiving acceleration and gyroscope data. The Memory Controller manages data transfer between different memory components and processing units. The Classification and Output Module determines the final classification result and generates appropriate outputs (LED indicators, UART transmission). The Power Management Unit controls power distribution and implements power-saving strategies.

DISCUSSION

The experimental results demonstrate that our proposed multi-head attention transformer architecture significantly advances the state-of-the-art in wearable fall detection systems. In this section, we discuss the implications of our findings, analyze the strengths and limitations of our approach, and explore potential directions for future research.

THE CRITICAL IMPACT OF DATA AND TRAINING STRATEGY

While the transformer architecture proved highly effective, the most significant finding of this work is the over-whelming importance of the data and training methodology. The performance gap between Model ImbalancedNet and Model BalancedTransformer is a clear demonstration that an advanced architecture cannot compensate for a poor, imbalanced dataset. The baseline model’s 48.0% sensitivity rendered it unfit for a real-world safety application. The success of our final model is primarily attributable to two factors: (1) the use of a large, well-balanced dataset and (2) the application of a focal loss function that forced the model to learn the features of the minority ‘‘fall’’ class. This underscores that for safety-critical systems, addressing fundamental machine learning challenges like class imbalance is paramount.

ADVANTAGES OF TRANSFORMER ARCHITECTURE FOR FALL DETECTION

The superior performance of our transformer-based approach can be attributed to several key advantages of the architecture for fall detection applications.

EFFECTIVE CAPTURE OF TEMPORAL DEPENDENCIES

Falls typically occur as sequences of motion patterns that evolve over time, with critical prefall indicators often appearing seconds before the actual impact. The self-attention mechanism in transformers excels at capturing these long-range temporal dependencies, allowing the model to recognize subtle patterns that precede falls. Our ablation studies confirm this advantage, showing that removing positional encoding, which provides explicit temporal information, significantly reduces model performance.

Traditional CNN architectures, while effective at extracting spatial features, struggle with long-range temporal relationships due to their limited receptive field. LSTMs can model sequential data but face challenges in parallel processing and capturing dependencies at variable time scales. Our transformer architecture overcomes these limitations through its self-attention mechanism, which directly models relationships between all time points in the input sequence.

MULTI-SCALE FEATURE LEARNING

Different types of falls exhibit distinct temporal and spectral characteristics that manifest at various time scales. The multi-head attention mechanism enables simultaneous learning of patterns at different scales and across different feature subspaces. For example, some attention heads might focus on rapid acceleration changes characteristic of impacts, while others track gradual orientation shifts that precede falls.

This multi-scale learning capability is theoretically valuable for distinguishing falls from complex activities of daily living. By having different heads focus on different signal characteristics, for instance, one head tracking rapid impacts while another tracks slower orientation changes, the model can build a more robust and nuanced understanding of the motion data, leading to better overall classification performance.

COMPUTATIONAL EFFICIENCY

Despite their complex architecture, transformers offer computational advantages that make them suitable for wearable implementations. Unlike recurrent models that process inputs sequentially, transformers can compute attention for all time steps in parallel, enabling efficient implementation on hardware platforms with parallel processing capabilities. Our FPGA implementation demonstrates that with appropriate optimizations, transformer models can achieve inference latencies comparable to simpler architectures while delivering superior accuracy.

FPGA IMPLEMENTATION INSIGHTS

Our experience implementing the transformer architecture on an ultra-low-power FPGA yielded several valuable insights:

MEMORY-COMPUTATION TRADE-OFFS

Transformer models are typically memory-intensive due to their self-attention mechanism, which requires storing attention matrices that grow quadratically with sequence length. On resource-constrained platforms, this presents a significant challenge. Our implementation addressed this through a combination of model compression techniques and a carefully designed memory hierarchy that balances on-chip and external storage.

The results demonstrate that with appropriate optimization, transformer models can be deployed on devices with limited memory while maintaining high accuracy. The memory-computation trade-offs explored in our analysis provide valuable guidance for implementing similar models on resource-constrained platforms.

POWER EFFICIENCY CONSIDERATIONS

Power consumption is a critical constraint for wearable devices, where battery life directly impacts usability. Our implementation achieves comparable power consumption to simpler CNN architectures despite the increased computational complexity of the transformer model. This is achieved through extensive use of power management techniques such as clock gating, duty cycling, and voltage scaling.

The relatively high power efficiency of our implementation can be attributed to two main factors: (1) the inherent parallelism of transformer architectures, which allows for efficient hardware utilization, and (2) our optimization strategies that focus on reducing memory access operations, which typically dominate power consumption in embedded inference engines.

ACCURACY-LATENCY BALANCE

Real-time response is essential for fall detection systems, where delays can lead to increased injury risk. Our implementation achieves an inference latency of 2.86 ms, which is significantly shorter than the typical fall duration (300-500 ms) [50]. This allows for early detection and potential intervention before the fall completes.

The balance between accuracy and latency is particularly important in safety-critical applications like fall detection. Our results demonstrate that transformer architectures can achieve this balance by leveraging parallel processing and optimized implementations, delivering high accuracy without sacrificing response time.

LIMITATIONS AND CHALLENGES

Despite the promising results, our approach faces several limitations and challenges:

DATASET LIMITATIONS

While our dataset includes data from 81 participants performing various ADLs and simulated falls, it primarily consists of healthy individuals in a controlled environment. Real-world elderly falls may exhibit different characteristics due to reduced mobility, altered gait, frailty, and comorbidities. In addition, simulated falls on safety mats are not strictly equivalent to spontaneous accidental falls. As highlighted in clinical biomechanics literature [51], real elderly falls often involve complex body rotations and occur unintentionally, whereas scripted simulations allow participants to partly anticipate the impact, altering muscle activation and descent kinematics [52].

We also evaluated cross-placement generalization by applying the shinbone-trained model to wrist- and waist-placement IMU streams. Performance degrades substantially under direct transfer, which is consistent with our prior placement study identifying the frontal shinbone as the optimal fall-detection location [42]. This behavior indicates that the model captures placement-specific motion signatures rather than generic sensor noise; therefore, deployment on alternative body locations should include transfer-learning or calibration steps.

The gap between laboratory/validation and real-world performance (94.7% vs. 91.2%) is modest under our finalized prospective protocol; an earlier pilot conducted in more heterogeneous, uncontrolled settings yielded 86.6%, reflecting domain shift and protocol differences rather than a contradiction. Differences stem from (a) environment heterogeneity and activity mix, (b) operating-point selection/thresholds finalized after the pilot, and (c) minor domain shift between collection protocols. Future work should focus on collecting more diverse and representative data, particularly from elderly populations in natural settings, including planned collaboration with Belmont Village Senior Living La Jolla for IRB-governed prospective validation in assisted-living conditions.

IMPLEMENTATION CONSTRAINTS

Our FPGA implementation utilizes most of the available resources on the iCE40UP5K platform, leaving limited headroom for additional features or model enhancements. This constraint may impact the scalability of our approach as more sophisticated models or additional functionality are developed.

Furthermore, the high resource utilization may present challenges in manufacturing and quality assurance, as the design has less tolerance for process variations. Alternative platforms with more resources or specialized accelerators for transformer operations could address these constraints but might impact the power consumption and form factor of the device.

CUSTOMIZATION AND ADAPTATION

Fall patterns vary significantly across individuals based on factors such as age, physical condition, and movement patterns. Our current approach uses a fixed model for all users, which may limit performance for individuals whose fall patterns differ substantially from those represented in the training data.

Personalization and adaptive learning could potentially address this limitation, but implementing such capabilities on resource-constrained wearable devices presents significant challenges. Techniques such as on-device transfer learning or federated learning could enable personalization while respecting privacy constraints, but their implementation would require additional computational resources and more sophisticated system architecture.

FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS

Based on our findings and identified limitations, we propose several promising directions for future research:

MULTI-MODAL INTEGRATION

While our current approach relies solely on IMU data, integrating additional sensing modalities could improve detection accuracy and robustness. Physiological signals such as heart rate or skin conductance could provide complementary information about the user’s state, potentially enabling earlier detection of pre-fall conditions such as dizziness or disorientation.

Future research should explore transformer architectures capable of integrating heterogeneous data streams with different sampling rates and characteristics. Cross-modal attention mechanisms could be particularly valuable for learning relationships between motion patterns and physiological responses.

PRE-FALL DETECTION AND PREVENTION

Our current system focuses on detecting falls as they occur, but ideally, falls would be predicted and prevented before they happen. The transformer architecture’s ability to capture long-range dependencies makes it promising for pre-fall detection, where subtle gait and balance changes might precede actual falls by seconds or minutes. As a concrete next step, we plan IRB-approved prospective validation in elderly cohorts with continuous monitoring and synchronized event annotation, including planned collaboration with Belmont Village Senior Living La Jolla (3880 Nobel Drive, La Jolla, CA 92122).

Extending our model to classify not just falls but also ‘‘high-risk’’ movement patterns could enable pre-emptive interventions, such as auditory warnings or activation of assistive devices. This would require larger datasets with annotated pre-fall events and longer time horizons, as well as research into the specific movement patterns that reliably predict imminent falls.

HARDWARE-AWARE NEURAL ARCHITECTURE SEARCH

Our current implementation represents a manually optimized design balancing accuracy and hardware constraints. Automated neural architecture search (NAS) techniques that explicitly consider hardware constraints could potentially discover more efficient architectures tailored to specific platforms.

Hardware-aware NAS could explore the design space of transformer architectures, optimizing factors such as embedding dimension, number of heads, and layer configuration based on both accuracy metrics and implementation constraints such as memory usage, latency, and power consumption.

EXPLAINABLE AI FOR HEALTHCARE APPLICATIONS

For safety-critical healthcare applications like fall detection, model interpretability is crucial for building trust and enabling effective human oversight. While attention weights provide some level of interpretability, more comprehensive approaches to explaining model decisions could enhance usability and acceptance.

Future research should explore methods for generating human-interpretable explanations of fall detection decisions, such as highlighting the specific motion patterns or time segments that contributed most to the classification. This could not only improve trust but also provide valuable clinical insights into fall mechanics and risk factors.

ROBUSTNESS AND CROSS-DATASET GENERALIZATION

To evaluate generalization, we benchmarked our architecture against modern time-series transformers (Informer, PatchTST) and public datasets. As shown in Fig. 18, while Informer and PatchTST offer a marginal ~0.7% offline accuracy boost, their parameter counts vastly exceed the 1 MB SPRAM hardware limit, rendering them undeployable for edge computing. Conversely, after fine-tuning our shinbone-pretrained model on the public SisFall (waist) and UMAFall (wrist/waist) datasets, our compact model successfully generalized and achieved > 94% accuracy. This confirms our hypothesis that while direct transfer suffers from placement-specific distribution shifts, the architecture possesses strong transferability and can robustly adapt to varying sensor locations with minimal calibration.

Furthermore, we conducted stress tests simulating real-world degradation (Fig. 19). The model demonstrated high resilience to signal noise, maintaining > 91.8% accuracy at a 15dB Signal-to-Noise Ratio. Simulated sensor displacements revealed that the model is robust to angular rotations (e.g., shinband slipping) within ±15°. However, performance degrades rapidly beyond 30°, establishing clear operational guidelines for proper device wearing orientation.

CONCLUSION

In this paper, we presented a novel multi-head attention transformer architecture for wearable in situ fall detection. More importantly, we demonstrated that the success of such a system is critically dependent on a robust data and training strategy. Our final model, trained on a large, balanced dataset with a specialized Focal Loss function, achieved 93.1% accuracy and a critically high fall sensitivity of 90.5%. This represents a massive improvement in real-world safety compared to a baseline model trained on imbalanced data, which failed to detect over half of all falls.

The key innovations of our approach include (1) a specialized transformer architecture designed for wearable fall detection applications, (2) a comprehensive optimization strategy enabling efficient deployment on ultra-low-power FPGAs, and (3) a detailed analysis of the performance-resource trade-offs in hardware implementation.

We designed, built, and validated an end-to-end wearable system, from the custom hardware schematic and PCB layout to the final, optimized transformer model implementation on a Lattice Semiconductor iCE40UP5K FPGA. Our integrated system achieves real-time inference with a latency of 2.86 ms and power consumption of 26.4 mW during active operation. The system maintains robust performance in real-world conditions, achieving 91.2% overall accuracy in a prospective validation; an earlier pilot in even more heterogeneous, uncontrolled settings measured 86.6%.

Our work demonstrates that transformer architectures, which have revolutionized natural language processing and computer vision, can be effectively adapted to wearable healthcare applications despite the constraints of edge devices. The self-attention mechanism proves particularly valuable for fall detection, where capturing relationships between motion patterns across different time scales is critical for accurate classification.

The proposed approach opens new possibilities for intelligent wearable systems that can monitor elderly individuals continuously and unobtrusively, potentially reducing fall-related injuries through timely detection and intervention. Future work will focus on extending the model to pre-fall detection, incorporating multi-modal sensing, and developing more personalized and adaptive approaches that can accommodate individual variations in movement patterns.

By combining advanced deep learning architectures with efficient hardware implementation, our work contributes to the development of more effective and practical technological solutions for elderly care, addressing a critical public health challenge in aging populations worldwide.
