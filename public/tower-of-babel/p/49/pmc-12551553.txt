Crowding-out and crowding-in effects of out-of-pocket expenditures for non-communicable diseases care on household consumption patterns in Bangladesh: a cross-sectional analysis of the 2022 household income and expenditure survey
Authors: Md Tauhidul Islam, Adelakun Odunyemi, Mieghan Bruce, Khurshid Alam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to analyse the crowding-out and crowding-in effects of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) related out-of-pocket (OOP) health expenditures on household consumption in Bangladesh.ObjectiveDesignThis study used data from the nationally representative Bangladesh household income and expenditure survey (HIES) 2022.SettingEight divisions of Bangladesh.Participants14 395 households.Primary and secondary outcome measuresWe examined how household consumption patterns across 20 expenditure categories were impacted by OOP health spending for NCD management, with a focus on income-level disparities.ResultsIn Bangladesh, OOP health expenditures for NCDs crowded out household expenditures on essential items like food and rent. Across all households, OOP health expenditures for NCDs by 10 US$ crowded out −3.8 US$ of expenditure on food (95% CI −5.1 to –2.5), and more specifically on protein-rich foods (−2.0 US$, 95% CI −2.8 to –1.2), spices (−0.2 US$, 95% CI −0.3 to –0.1), and restaurant and café meals (−0.9 US$, 95% CI −1.4 to –0.5). Crowding-out was also seen for tobacco, rent, durable goods and miscellaneous. In lower-income households, expenditures on food (−4.1 US$, 95% CI −7.2 to –1.1), restaurant and café meals (−2.0 US$, 95% CI −3.1 to –0.8), spices (−0.4 US$, 95% CI −0.7 to –0.09), and rent (−3.1 US$, 95% CI −4.5 to –1.6) were significantly crowded out.ConclusionThis research demonstrates that NCD-related spending in Bangladesh reduces budgets for both food and non-food expenditures, with a stronger crowding-out effect on food items and rent, particularly in lower-income households. Effective financial and social protection mechanisms against NCDs are warranted to safeguard the consumption of the NCD-affected households in Bangladesh.

Background

Out-of-pocket (OOP) healthcare expenditure for non-communicable diseases (NCDs) is a significant policy concern due to the substantial economic burden it imposes.1 Globally, NCDs are projected to cost over 30 trillion US$ in the next two decades, potentially pushing millions of people below the poverty line.2 An analysis across 15 countries estimated that the average health expenditure for managing hypertension per person was approximately 630 US$, with direct costs reaching 1500 US$ and indirect costs about 280 USD.3 Projections on the global economic burden of diabetes suggest that the financial burden of diabetes is expected to increase from 1.3 trillion US$ in 2015 to 2.2 trillion US$ in 2030.4 In low-income and middle-income countries, catastrophic healthcare spending is prevalent, especially in households managing NCDs.5 Data from cohort studies reveal that these households face higher rates of impoverishment than those without NCDs.5

NCDs are one of the major factors that have been accounted for the poverty and inequality in Bangladesh,6 7 and it was estimated that annually NCD-related expenditure could risk 4.6% households in Bangladesh falling under the poverty line.8 A systematic review on the economic burden of NCDs in South Asia found that Bangladesh had the highest prevalence of catastrophic health expenditure and impoverishment caused by cardiovascular diseases.9 The health system in Bangladesh faces significant challenges in managing NCDs due to limited resources, inadequate infrastructure and reliance on OOP payments for healthcare services.10 11 Public healthcare facilities often lack essential medicines and diagnostic services needed for NCD management, forcing patients to purchase these from private providers or pharmacies at a higher cost.11

Many studies in Bangladesh have concentrated on the impoverishing impact of OOP healthcare expenditures,1214 often overlooking how these costs alter household consumption spending behaviour, particularly their crowding-out effect. In response to OOP health costs, households may adjust their consumption patterns by reallocating resources. If a household’s spending on a specific item increases, this is referred to as a ‘crowding-in’ effect. Conversely, when spending on other goods declines to accommodate healthcare costs, it is termed a ‘crowding-out’ effect.15 The crowding-out effect, in particular, warrants attention, as it reflects how OOP health expenditures force households to reduce consumption spending on vital goods and services, potentially leading to a deterioration in their living conditions. This reallocation can deepen the severity of poverty by further limiting access to essentials and lowering overall living standards. Thus, the broader consequences of OOP health expenses extend beyond financial hardship, emphasising the need to consider how they displace other crucial consumption expenditures in households.16

The aim of this study was to estimate the crowding-out and crowding-in effect of OOP health expenditures for managing NCDs among Bangladeshi households, and to assess whether this effect varies across different income levels. Specifically, we examined how household consumption patterns across 20 expenditure categories are impacted by OOP health spending for NCD management, focusing on income-level disparities. We hypothesised that households with higher OOP health expenditures would experience a crowding-out effect, where essential spending might be compromised to cover healthcare costs for the management of NCDs in Bangladesh.

Method

Data

Our study uses data from the Bangladesh household income and expenditure survey (HIES) 2022, which was conducted by the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS) with technical support from the World Bank. The HIES 2022 is a nationally representative household survey covering all eight divisions of Bangladesh, with a sample size of 14 395 households. The aim of the survey is to measure the living standards in the entire country; therefore, it contains a wide range of household information, such as demographic characteristics of household members, household income, expenditure, assets, housing and facilities.17

The HIES 2022 employed a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design, based on the sampling framework developed from the 2022 Population and Housing Census. The primary sampling unit (PSU) for the survey was the enumeration area (EA) from the Population and Housing Census, with each EA comprising approximately 100 households. In the first stage, the necessary number of PSUs was selected, followed by a comprehensive household listing within those chosen PSUs. In the second stage, 20 households were randomly selected from each of the identified PSUs for interviews.17

Forty enumeration teams conducted the survey in HIES 2022. Each enumeration team had a supervisor, two interviewers and two female facilitators. The household head or another informed adult provided data on income, expenditure, living circumstances, assets, etc. For some modules (eg, education, health status), eligible household members were interviewed directly, or information was provided by the head or knowledgeable adult if they were absent.17

Identification of households affected by NCDs

The HIES 2022 survey collected information on NCDs in households through two different approaches. Individuals in the household were asked whether they had been diagnosed with an NCD in the past 12 months. Additionally, the survey inquired if any household members had sought outpatient care (OPD) in the last 30 days or had been admitted to inpatient care (IPD) within the past 12 months for NCD management. A household was identified as affected by NCDs if any member reported having an NCD in the past 12 months, or sought OPD care in the last month, or was admitted to IPD within the last year for NCD management. In this context, NCDs include chronic heart disease, chronic bronchitis, hypertension, arthritis, diabetes, cancer, chronic kidney disease, mental disorders or paralysis.17 Considering this definition, 8179 households were affected by NCDs in the HIES 2022. Calculation of health expenditure for NCD management

Calculation of OOP health expenditures related to NCDs is also illustrated in figure 1. The HIES 2022 collected OOP health expenditure data through both the ‘consumption’ and ‘health’ modules. For our study, we chose to use the ‘health’ module, as it allows us to directly link OOP health expenditure data to the diagnoses of individuals within the household—something that is not possible with the ‘consumption’ module. In the ‘health’ module of HIES 2022, the costs of OPD and IPD care are linked to individuals who sought OPD services in the last 30 days or were admitted to IPD within the past 12 months for NCD management. Additionally, the cost of routine medications is linked to individuals who were diagnosed with an NCD in the past 12 months.17 Using this approach, we calculated comprehensive OOP health expenditures related to NCDs, including routine medication costs, OPD costs and IPD costs. Following that, individual-level data were aggregated at the household level, and the dataset was divided into NCD-spending and non-NCD-spending households. Routine medicine costs, other health costs and OPD costs were collected for the last 30 days, whereas IPD costs were collected for the past 12 months. To ensure consistency in the analysis, we converted IPD costs into monthly values by dividing them by 12.17

Construction of expenditure categories and total expenditure

HIES 2022 collected data on household expenditures for nearly 706 food and non-food items through the ‘consumption’ module. Based on these detailed expenditure data, we categorised the items initially into thirteen subgroups: food, tobacco, transportation, communication, housing, durable goods, rent, utilities, clothing, lifestyle and hygiene, education, healthcare and miscellaneous. Food items were further divided into subcategories such as staples, protein-rich foods, oil, fruits, vegetables, spices, and restaurant and café meals, resulting in a total of 20 subgroups. In HIES 2022, food expenditure was recorded for 14 days, with selected items collected weekly for 2 weeks. Non-food expenditures were recorded for either the past month or the past year. For this study, all consumption expenditures were converted into average monthly values for consistency in analysis.17 When calculating total expenditure, we subtracted the healthcare expenditures from the ‘consumption’ module and replaced them with the healthcare expenditures from the ‘health’ module. All expenditures in Bangladeshi taka (BDT) were converted to US$ using the mid-point exchange rate (93.5895 BDT=1 US$) from.18

Specification of the econometric model for the crowding-out or crowding-in effect

This study focuses on the contemporaneous relationship between OOP health expenditures related to NCDs and household resource allocation. We investigated how NCD-related OOP health expenditures and household resource allocation are associated at a specific point in time, depending on whether NCDs are treated via OPD, IPD or regular intake of medicine. We hypothesise that households prioritise NCD treatment by allocating a portion of their income for its management before considering other consumption needs. Management of NCDs is influenced by socioeconomic status, which, along with household demographics, directly shapes resource allocation patterns. By controlling for household demographics and socioeconomic status, we analysed the specific relationship between NCD management expenditures and household resource allocation using cross-sectional data, Bangladesh HIES 2022. For the crowding-out or crowding-in effect analysis, we followed the guidelines provided by John et al,15 which offer a detailed analytical procedure for tobacco spending. However, we adapted this approach to fit the context of OOP health expenditures related to NCDs.

The estimation of the crowding-out effect is based on the assumption that households make consumption decisions to maximise a single utility function. This assumption is important because families usually combine resources from all members and make collective decisions on spending based on everyone’s needs. Additionally, household expenditure surveys usually present spending as a whole for the entire family. As a result, this study analyses expenditures at the household level.19

The differences in the average budget share across expenditure categories between households with NCD-related spending and those without provide an initial insight into any potential trade-offs (crowd-in or crowd-out) that may have been made due to spending on NCD treatment. While the difference between the mean budget share for each expenditure category provides a preliminary assessment of the relationship between NCD-related spending and budget allocation, it does not account for potential confounding factors at the household level. These factors may influence allocation decisions independently of NCD spending, leading to biased estimates. To address this limitation, a more comprehensive econometric model is required. Such a model can systematically examine whether households with higher NCD-related spending allocate less to other expenditure categories and, if so, which specific categories are affected.15

The parameter α2i is the crowding-out coefficient from the equation. A negative α2i indicates crowding-out, as NCD-related OOP increases, the budget share for category i falls. A positive α2i indicates crowding-in, where NCD-related OOP and spending on category i co-move. Crowding-out refers to the reduction in household spending on non-health items (eg, food, education, housing) that results from increased NCD-related OOP. By contrast, crowding-in captures the situation where NCD-related OOP are positively associated with increased spending in another category (for example, transport costs or other health costs may rise alongside NCD-related OOP). These concepts not only describe current spending adjustments but also highlight how future increases in NCD-related OOP are likely to reshape household consumption patterns.

The above equation cannot be estimated using an ordinary least squares (OLS) method because the variables pnjqnj and lnMj and lnMj2 are likely endogenous. The OLS method may yield biased results; therefore, we applied the equation-by-equation instrumental variable (IV) Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS) approach, as recommended by John et al.15 Through this approach, we overcome the problem of endogeneity by introducing valid IV that are correlated with the endogenous variables but uncorrelated with the error term.15

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using the STATA V.17 (basic edition) and the results were graphically represented using the ggplot2 package of R V.4.3.1. The total expenditure and the share of each expenditure category were presented as means with SD for all households, as well as separately for households in three income levels: low, middle and high. These three household income categories were based on household consumption per capita. Household weight of HIES 2022 was applied while constructing the income categories. Household characteristics were presented as percentages by NCD spending status, with 95% CI for categorical variables and means with SD for continuous variables. Comparisons between households with and without NCD-related spending were conducted using two independent sample t-tests for continuous variables and χ² tests for categorical variables.

We conducted a two-sample student’s t-test to compare the budget shares allocated to 20 expenditure categories between NCD-spending and non-NCD-spending households, evaluating whether these differences are statistically significant. Following that, a series of statistical tests, detailed in online supplemental table 1, were conducted to ensure the validity of the test for endogeneity of consumption variables, validity of our IVs and heterogeneity in OOP expenditure shares between NCD-affected and non-NCD-affected households. The endogeneity test result, with a p<0.05 indicates that the regressors, pnjqnj and lnMj and lnMj2 in equations, are endogenous for most of the expenditure categories. This justifies the use of IV in the model. We thus instrumented these variables with ln(total income) and their squared values, and waiting the time to see a doctor. These are reasonable IVs because it is fair to assume that households make consumption decisions for other expenditure categories after predetermining NCD-related spending and that, thus, each expenditure’s share (other than NCD-related spending) is also determined ex post. These IVs are commonly used for studying crowding-out effects.15 16 21 Waiting time to see a doctor is assumed to be correlated with NCD-related spending but uncorrelated directly with the share of expenditure categories. Further, we have tested the validity of IV to assess whether they are weak or strong. A common rule of thumb indicates that an F-statistic of <10, in the case of a single endogenous regressor, suggests a weak instrument.15 IV estimation provides a consistent estimator if the instrument satisfies two conditions: (1) It is correlated with the endogenous variables (inclusion restriction) and (2) It affects the outcome only indirectly through the endogenous variables (exclusion restriction). In this study, we tested the inclusion restriction using F-statistics but could not test the exclusion restriction due to the equation being just identified.15 The Pagan-Hall test checks for heteroskedasticity, where a significant result (p<0.05) indicates that the errors are not homoskedastic.15 These test statistics justify our approach to use the equation-by-equation IV 2SLS approach to investigate crowding-out/in effect. The study further performs a Wald test showing differences in preferences between NCD-spending and non-NCD-spending households.15 The test results were insignificant (p>0.05) in the majority of cases, which indicates non-NCD-spending households may report zero spending on healthcare due to affordability, not because it is absent from their preferences. Therefore, in our model, we did not account for this heterogeneity.

We present the marginal effect per 10 US$ increase in OOP health expenses for NCDs on other household expenditure categories by household income level. This result was derived by first multiplying the original coefficient (based on a 1 US$ increase) by 10, and then again multiplying it by the average (mean) remaining household budget after NCD-related health spending. To interpret our results, we followed the methodological guidelines provided by John et al15 and drew on the applied example from Batbold et al,16 who estimated the crowding-out effect of OOP health expenditures on household consumption in Mongolia using a similar approach. The results are presented both as coefficients (online supplemental figure 1) and as corresponding values in US$ (figure 2).

Finally, analyses were adjusted for potential confounders, including household size, setting, divisions, proportion of males in the household, age, sex, education, marital status and earning status of the household head, presence of children under 5 years old, children aged 5–14 years, an individual over 60 years old, a woman of reproductive age (15–49 years), disabled person in the household, housing occupancy status, living in a slum, possession of a bank account or a computer. In the household, the presence of a separate dining room, type of toilet facility, type of wall and roof of the dwelling, and the sources of water and energy for the dwelling. These covariates were identified from the existing literature on Bangladesh12 21 and Mongolia16 addressing the same topic.

The dataset includes no identifiable respondent information.17 The study proposal received a thorough review by the Murdoch University Human Ethics Committee, which provided an ethics exemption (Protocol number 2022/123).

Result

Household expenditure for the management of NCDs

A total of 14 395 households from the HIES 2022 dataset were included in the analysis, with no households excluded or removed during the data cleaning process. Table 1 shows that 56.9% of these households reported spending related to NCD. NCD-affected households were most prevalent among high-income groups (64.1%, n=4674), followed by middle-income groups (58.0%, n=4713), with the lowest prevalence observed in low-income groups (49.2%, n=5008). On average, NCD-affected households spend 18.3 US$ for the management of NCDs, which accounts for 4.9% of the total household expenditure. The share of expenditure for NCD-related spending was lowest among low-income households (3.8%), followed by middle-income households (4.6%), and highest among high-income households (6.3%).

Crowding-out and crowding-in effect

Online supplemental table 3 demonstrates unadjusted differences (t-test) in consumption share between NCD-spending households and non-NCD-spending households, which preliminarily indicates that spending for the management may have an effect on household consumption expenditure. Figure 2 illustrates that on average, a monthly 10 US$ increase in OOP health expenditure on NCD was associated with a −3.8 US$ (95% CI −5.1 to –2.5) reduction in household food expenditure. A significant crowding-out effect for food was also observed for all three income categories of households. However, the magnitude of the crowding-out effect was largest for middle-income households (−12.0 US$, 95% CI –17.4 to –6.7). Among the food categories, protein-rich foods, spices and restaurant and café meals were negatively impacted as the budget was spent on managing NCDs for all households. Magnitude of the impact was highest for protein-rich foods (−2.0 US$, 95% CI −2.8 to –1.2), followed by restaurant and café meals (−0.9 US$, 95% CI −1.4 to –0.5) and spices (−0.2 US$, 95% CI: −0.3 to –0.1) respectively. For tobacco, across all households, we identified an impact of −0.6 US$ (95% CI −1.0 to –0.3), 3.3 times smaller than protein-rich foods and 1.5 times smaller than restaurant and café meals. When analysing the crowding-out effect of OOP health expenditures for NCDs by income tertile, we found that the impact on protein-rich foods was not significant only for low-income households, while the effect on staples was significant only for middle-income households (−1.6 US$, 95% CI −2.9 to –0.3). And restaurant and café meals were significant for both low (−2.0 US$, 95% CI −3.1 to –0.8) and middle-income households (−2.9 US$, 95% CI −4.7 to –1.1). NCD spending also crowded out spices, but only significant for low-income (−0.4 US$, 95% CI −0.7 to –0.09) and middle-income households (−0.6 US$, 95% CI −1.1 to –0.2).

Further, a significant crowding-out effect was observed for rent across all households (−1.5 US$, 95% CI −2.1 to –0.9); specifically, the impact was highest for low-income households (−3.1 US$, 95% CI −4.5 to –1.6), followed by middle-income (−2.5 US$, 95% CI −4.4 to –0.6) and high-income households (−0.8 US$, 95% CI −1.6 to –0.1) respectively. While analysing the impact by income category, the impact on durable goods was only significant for high-income households (−1.4 US$, 95% CI −2.5 to –0.4), and the impact on miscellaneous items was only significant for low- (−1.2 US$, 95% CI −2.1 to –0.2) and middle-income households (−2.0 US$, 95% CI −3.5 to –0.4). A significant crowding-in effect was observed for other healthcare—defined as healthcare expenditure remaining after deducting spending on NCD management from total healthcare expenditure. A monthly 10 US$ increase in OOP spending on NCDs was associated with an 8.1 US$ increase (95% CI 6.3 to 9.9) in spending on other healthcare across all households, with the largest increase among middle-income households (17.0 US$; 95% CI 10.4 to 23.6).

Discussion

This study examines the crowding-out effect of OOP health expenditures for NCDs on household consumption in Bangladesh. In contrast to commonly used approaches like the catastrophic expenditure method, which assesses the economic burden of healthcare, or the impoverishing effects method, which examines the impact on poverty,22 this study explores the influence of NCD-related OOP spending on household living standards by estimating the crowding-out and crowding-in effects. This framing also indicates how future increases in NCD-related OOP may alter consumption priorities within the household. This approach offers policymakers two important insights. It identifies specific areas of household consumption spending most impacted by OOP health expenditures, which can help optimise the use of government resources through targeted interventions. Additionally, it provides a broader understanding of how households adjust their consumption patterns, enabling more precise allocation of subsidies and financial assistance to the most vulnerable groups.162325

Although this is not the first study to examine the crowding-out effect of OOP health expenditures for NCDs on household consumption in Bangladesh, we have strengthened our analysis by addressing limitations from earlier research. The study by Datta et al21 linked household-level OOP health expenditure data to patients with NCDs, potentially leading to an overestimation of the effect since it is not possible to confirm that the OOP health expenditures were solely for NCD management; they may have been spent on other health conditions. In contrast, our study identifies individuals with NCDs and links them with NCD-related OOP health expenditures at the individual level, which we then aggregate at the household level. Additionally, we excluded OOP health expenditures unrelated to NCDs, ensuring a more accurate and robust representation of NCD-related spending. Furthermore, our findings are based on recently released data from the 2022 HIES in Bangladesh,17 offering the most up-to-date insights into this issue.

We discovered a significant crowding-out effect of OOP health expenditures for managing NCDs on essential items, particularly food items and rent. Reduced spending on food among households affected by NCDs may have detrimental effects on long-term human capital investment, particularly for children.26 This issue is especially concerning in Bangladesh, where nearly one-quarter of children under five are stunted and approximately 21% are underweight.27 The study also added that the crowding-out effect on food expenditure was greatest among low and middle-income households, consistent with findings from Pakistan, Bangladesh and Mongolia.16 21 28 Moreover, this study found that OOP health expenditures for NCDs led to a crowding-out effect on protein-rich food consumption in middle and higher-income households. This may be because lower-income households could not afford protein-rich foods due to their high cost;29 therefore, the impact was only observed in wealthier households. Additionally, middle and higher-income households might reduce specific protein-rich foods like beef or lamb following doctors’ advice,30 as many are patients with NCDs and recently sought treatment.

In Bangladesh, the trend of eating out is rising due to urbanisation, busier lifestyles and increasing incomes, with the convenience of diverse food options, such as street food and online delivery services, playing a key role.31 This practice is common not only among wealthier individuals but also among lower-income households. Many poorer families resort to eating out to cope with demanding job situations, despite being aware of the unhygienic conditions and poor nutritional quality of the outside food.32 The crowding-out effect of restaurant and café meals among low and middle-income households indicates that they are cutting back on meals, ultimately compromising their nutritional status.

Another significant and strongest crowding-out effect observed among low-income households relates to housing rent. After covering the NCD-related OOP health expenditures, many are unable to afford rent, jeopardising their living situation. Similar results have also been reflected in studies from Mongolia and India.16 24 The crowding-out effect particularly impacts lower-income households (particularly in cities), as most do not own their homes,33 making them more vulnerable to financial strain. The crowd-in effect of OOP health expenditures for NCDs on other healthcare suggests the presence of comorbidities or multiple health conditions in these households. Since individuals with NCDs are more susceptible to infectious diseases,34 this might further drive up their other medical expenditures.

Our study has certain limitations. One significant limitation is the cross-sectional nature of our data, which restricts our ability to explore the long-term effects of OOP health expenditures related to NCDs on household spending patterns. As our analysis highlights the association between NCD status and consumption displacement, it does not pinpoint the specific causal pathways through which NCDs influence household consumption. A Matching difference-in-difference design may have yielded further insights, but it requires household panel data.15 Our study’s cross-sectional design and low frequency allow us to simply record a snapshot of household consumption and not track dynamic changes in spending or other coping techniques among NCD-spending households over time. Future research could explore crowding-out effects by segmenting households according to the magnitude of their NCD expenditures, distinguishing between those incurring catastrophic costs and those managing more routine NCD spending.

Conclusion

Our findings reveal both crowding-out and crowding-in effects across various expenditure categories, with low-income households particularly vulnerable to reduced spending on essential items like food and rent. The findings emphasise the urgent need for targeted policy interventions to alleviate the economic burden of NCD-related healthcare costs. Specifically, addressing the healthcare financing system by implementing formal public health insurance in the form of universal health coverage, improving access to affordable medical services and providing support to low-income households through social protection mechanisms can mitigate the negative effects of OOP health expenditures for NCDs and thus safeguard the consumption of the NCD-affected vulnerable households in Bangladesh.
