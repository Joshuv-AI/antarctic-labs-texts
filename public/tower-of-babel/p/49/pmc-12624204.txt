Systemic immune‐inflammation index and pan‐immune value as prognostic indicators of clinical outcomes in mycosis fungoides: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Joon Min Jung, Myoung Eun Choi, Ik Jun Moon, Chong Hyun Won, Sung Eun Chang, Mi Woo Lee, Woo Jin Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBlood inflammatory markers may provide prognostic insights but remain underexplored in mycosis fungoides (MF). To evaluate the prognostic significance of baseline blood inflammatory markers, including the neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), eosinophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (ELR), monocyte‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (MLR), pan‐inflammation value (PIV) and systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII), in MF. This retrospective cohort study included MF patients diagnosed between 1997 and 2024 at a tertiary medical centre. Markers were analysed in relation to MF stage, morphology, overall survival (OS) and progression‐free survival (PFS). Among 195 patients, all markers were significantly elevated in advanced‐stage MF compared to early‐stage MF. Marker levels generally increased from patch to tumour‐stage MF but did not distinguish erythrodermic MF from tumour MF. All markers were associated with poor survival outcomes in univariable analysis. However, in multivariable analysis, only PIV and SII independently associated with poor OS and PFS. Patients with high PIV or SII were more likely to exhibit poor prognostic factors, including advanced stage, head and neck involvement and elevated serum lactate dehydrogenase levels, and experienced more frequent disease progression during follow‐up. Single‐centre retrospective design. Baseline PIV and SII can be significant prognostic markers in MF, offering enhanced risk stratification and guiding clinical management.

INTRODUCTION

Mycosis fungoides (MF) is the most common subtype of primary cutaneous T‐cell lymphoma (CTCL), accounting for approximately half of all primary cutaneous lymphomas. 1 , 2 It is characterized by the clonal proliferation of malignant T lymphocytes in the skin, typically presenting as erythematous patches, plaques or tumours, on non‐sun‐exposed areas. 2 Early‐stage MF usually follows an indolent course, whereas advanced‐stage disease often has poor outcomes. 3 , 4 , 5 The most widely accepted and established prognostic factor is the International Society for Cutaneous Lymphomas (ISCL)/European Organization of Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) staging system. 6 Additional adverse factors include lesion morphology and location, head and neck involvement, tissue eosinophil infiltration, serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and beta‐2‐microglobulin levels, the folliculotropic variant and large cell transformation. 3 , 5 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 However, the heterogeneity of MF and the limitations of these factors highlight the need for novel, accessible and cost‐effective prognostic markers.

Peripheral blood inflammatory markers, such as the neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), eosinophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (ELR), monocyte‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (MLR), pan‐inflammation value (PIV) and systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII), have been associated with tumour progression, survival and treatment outcomes in various cancers. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 These markers, derived from routine blood tests, provide insights into the tumour microenvironment and systemic immune dynamics, reflecting host–tumour interactions. Despite their utility in other malignancies, the prognostic implications of these markers in MF remain unclear. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 This study evaluates the prognostic significance of baseline NLR, ELR, MLR, PIV and SII in MF patients treated at a tertiary centre.

METHODS

Participants

After institutional review board approval, we identified MF cases diagnosed between January 1997 and May 2024 from the Asan Medical Center database. Cases were included if complete blood cell count data from peripheral blood samples were available within 3 months of the initial diagnosis. Immunocompromised patients and patients with concurrent infections, other malignancies, or pregnancy were excluded.

Variables of interest

The clinicopathological variables assessed included age at diagnosis, sex, time to diagnosis, MF morphology at diagnosis (patch, plaque, tumour or erythroderma), body surface area (BSA) involvement (BSA ≤ 30%, 30% < BSA ≤ 60% or BSA > 60%), clinical or pathological lymph node involvement, visceral involvement, blood involvement and tumour–node–metastasis–blood (TNMB) stage at diagnosis, 6 head and neck involvement, pruritus, large cell transformation, T‐cell receptor (TCR) gene analysis of skin lesions and treatments. As there are no standardized BSA thresholds for all MF morphologies, cut‐off values of 30% and 60% were selected pragmatically to facilitate analysis of inflammatory marker trends in relation to disease burden. 26 Chemotherapy included gemcitabine, CHOP (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisone) and brentuximab vedotin. Overall survival (OS) was measured from diagnosis to death or last follow‐up, while progression‐free survival (PFS) was calculated from diagnosis to disease progression (upstaging of TNM stage), death or last follow‐up. Laboratory data collected included absolute neutrophil count (ANC), absolute lymphocyte count (ALC), absolute eosinophil count (AEC), absolute monocyte count (AMC), platelet count (PLT) and serum LDH levels. NLR, ELR and MLR were calculated as ratios of ANC, AEC and AMC to ALC respectively. PIV and SII were defined as ‘ANC × AMC × PLT/ALC’ and ‘ANC × PLT/ALC’ respectively.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Normally distributed variables were analysed using Student's t‐test, while non‐normally distributed variables were analysed using the Mann–Whitney U‐test. Correlations among blood inflammatory markers were assessed using Pearson's correlation coefficient. Survival analysis utilized Kaplan–Meier curves. Optimal cut‐off values for each biomarker were determined by evaluating all possible dichotomization points and selecting the value that provided the most significant separation of Kaplan–Meier survival curves in the log‐rank test for OS and PFS. 27 Factors affecting survival outcomes were analysed using Cox proportional hazards models. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), with significance set at p < 0.05.

Association between blood inflammatory markers and disease burden at diagnosis

All inflammatory markers (median NLR, 2.46 vs. 1.64, p < 0.001; ELR, 0.13 vs. 0.06, p < 0.001; MLR, 0.36 vs. 0.20, p < 0.001; PIV, 44.0 vs. 18.2, p < 0.001; SII, 647.6 vs. 395.7, p < 0.001) were significantly higher in patients with advanced‐stage MF compared to those with early‐stage disease (Figure 1; Figure S1).

When stratified by MF morphology, all markers were significantly higher in tumour MF compared to patch MF. Additionally, NLR, PIV and SII were significantly higher in plaque MF compared to patch MF. ELR and MLR were the only markers that could differentiate plaque MF from tumour MF, while no markers showed significant differences between tumour MF and erythrodermic MF (Figure 2). A similar trend was observed in analyses based on T stage. All markers distinguished T3 from T2 or T1 stages but could not differentiate T4 from T3 stages. Furthermore, except for MLR, none of the markers showed significant differences between T1 and T2 stages (Figure S2). When analysing marker levels by BSA involvement, all markers, except for ELR, were significantly higher in patients with >60% BSA involvement compared to those with ≤30% BSA. However, no markers could distinguish the 30% < BSA ≤ 60% group from the ≤30% BSA group (Figure S3).

All markers were significantly elevated in patients with head and neck involvement, lymph node involvement or LDH elevation compared to those without these features at diagnosis. Furthermore, marker levels were higher in patients with pruritus, large cell transformation or clonal TCR rearrangement than in those without these features, although statistical significance was not achieved for certain markers (Table S1).

Prognostic implications of blood inflammatory markers

The optimal cut‐off values for inflammatory biomarkers were identified for OS and PFS. For OS, the optimal cut‐off values were NLR, 3.23; ELR, 0.22; MLR, 0.33; PIV, 103.5; and SII, 1434.0. The proportion of patients exceeding these thresholds was 13.8% (27/195) for NLR, 12.3% (24/195) for ELR, 17.4% (34/195) for MLR, 5.1% (10/195) for PIV and 5.1% (10/195) for SII. For PFS, the optimal cut‐off values were NLR, 3.94; ELR, 0.11; MLR, 0.29; PIV, 103.5; and SII, 1434.0. The proportion of patients exceeding these thresholds was 9.2% (18/195) for NLR, 29.7% (58/195) for ELR, 25.1% (49/195) for MLR, 5.1% (10/195) for PIV and 5.1% (10/195) for SII.

In the univariable analysis, all inflammatory markers were significantly associated with poor OS and PFS. However, in multivariable analysis, only PIV >103.5 and SII >1434.0 were independently associated with poor OS (PIV: hazard ratio [HR], 7.01; 95% confidence interval [CI], 2.05–24.01; p = 0.002; SII: HR, 5.09; 95% CI, 1.55–16.68; p = 0.007) and PFS (PIV: HR, 5.02; 95% CI, 1.80–14.02; p = 0.002; SII: HR, 4.29; 95% CI, 1.45–12.71; p = 0.009) after adjusting for age, sex, stage, LDH elevation and large cell transformation (Table 2; Figure 3). Additional factors affecting survival outcomes are shown in Table S2. We further evaluated the prognostic value of PIV >103.5 and SII >1434.0 in early‐ and advanced‐stage MF. Although elevated PIV and SII tended to be associated with worse outcomes in both subgroups, statistical significance was limited in the advanced‐stage subgroup in fully adjusted models (Table S3).

Clinicopathologic and prognostic differences in high versus low baseline PIV/SII

Baseline demographics, including age, sex and time to diagnosis, did not differ significantly between patients with PIV >103.5 or SII >1434.0 and those with PIV ≤103.5 or SII ≤1434.0. Advanced‐stage disease (8/10 [80%] vs. 36/185 [19.5%], p < 0.001), head and neck involvement (9/10 [90%] vs. 67/185 [36.2%], p = 0.002) and LDH elevation (5/10 [50%] vs. 18/185 [9.7%], p = 0.001) at diagnosis were significantly more common in patients with PIV >103.5 compared to those with PIV ≤103.5. In contrast, large cell transformation (3/10 [30%] vs. 14/185 [7.6%], p = 0.061) and clonal TCR rearrangement (5/6 [83.3%] vs. 105/152 [69.1%], p = 0.770) showed no significant differences between the groups. Patients with PIV >103.5 more frequently underwent aggressive treatments such as radiotherapy (6/10 [60%] vs. 39/185 [21.1%], p = 0.014) and chemotherapy (4/10 [40%] vs. 17/185 [9.2%], p = 0.011); however, disease progression or death occurred more often in this group (6/10 [60%] vs. 25/185 [13.5%], p = 0.001). Additionally, upstaging of N and M stages during follow‐up was observed in 20% (2/10) and 10% (1/10) of patients with PIV >103.5 respectively.

Similarly, patients with SII >1434.0 were significantly more likely to present with advanced‐stage disease (8/10 [80%] vs. 36/185 [19.5%], p < 0.001) and head and neck involvement (9/10 [90%] vs. 67/185 [36.2%], p = 0.002) compared to those with SII ≤1434.0. However, no significant differences were observed in LDH elevation (3/10 [30%] vs. 20/185 [10.8%], p = 0.184), large cell transformation (1/10 [10%] vs. 16/185 [8.6%], p = 0.061) or clonal TCR rearrangement (5/6 [83.3%] vs. 105/152 [69.1%], p = 0.770) between the groups. Aggressive treatments such as radiotherapy (7/10 [70%] vs. 38/185 [20.5%], p = 0.001) and chemotherapy (4/10 [40%] vs. 17/185 [9.2%] p = 0.011) were more frequently administered in patients with SII >1434.0. Despite these interventions, disease progression or death was significantly more common in this group (5/10 [50%] vs. 26/185 [14.2%], p = 0.010). Upstaging of N and M stages during follow‐up was observed in 20% (2/10) and 10% (1/10) of patients with SII >1434.0 respectively.

Correlation between blood inflammatory markers

Most markers were significantly and positively correlated with each other, except for ELR. ELR was significantly associated only with MLR (correlation coefficient, 0.28, p < 0.01) (Figure S4).

DISCUSSION

We evaluated baseline peripheral blood inflammatory markers (NLR, ELR, MLR, PIV, SII) in MF. Higher values were linked to advanced stage and worse survival and correlated with disease burden (overall/T stage, BSA and morphology at diagnosis). The signal was strongest in tumour‐stage MF (T3), likely reflecting increased tumour burden and systemic inflammation that may also influence immunotherapy outcomes in stage IIB. 28 These markers did not distinguish erythrodermic MF (T4) from tumour‐stage disease. This may reflect fundamental immunopathogenetic differences. Erythrodermic MF, especially when it overlaps with Sézary syndrome, is often characterized by peripheral blood involvement with circulating malignant T cells and reactive lymphocytosis, potentially masking inflammatory marker elevations that rely on relative neutrophil or monocyte increases. Moreover, previous studies suggest that erythrodermic MF has distinct cytokine profiles with Th2 polarization and elevated serum interleukin (IL)‐4, IL‐5 and IL‐13 levels, which may not directly correlate with neutrophil, monocyte or platelet‐mediated systemic inflammation. This immunological divergence may explain the poor discriminatory performance of these markers in this subtype. 2 , 25 In addition, previously reported poor prognostic factors, such as head and neck involvement, lymph node involvement, large cell transformation and LDH elevation, were generally associated with higher levels of these markers, further supporting their prognostic potential. 3 , 5 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 All markers also demonstrated significant correlations with each other, except for ELR, which not only showed limited correlation but also exhibited the lowest prognostic significance, as indicated by its hazard ratio in univariable analysis. Among the markers, PIV and SII emerged as independent prognostic factors of OS and PFS, even after adjusting for confounding factors, including stage, LDH levels and large cell transformation. These findings highlight the potential of these markers to serve as cost‐effective and accessible tools for identifying high‐risk MF patients who may benefit from closer monitoring and tailored therapeutic strategies.

The relationship between systemic inflammation and adverse outcomes in MF may be explained by several biological mechanisms. Neutrophils, monocytes and platelets play critical roles in cancer progression through their pro‐inflammatory and pro‐tumorigenic effects. 29 , 30 , 31 These cells contribute to a systemic inflammatory state that promotes tumour growth by enhancing angiogenesis, remodelling the extracellular matrix and releasing reactive oxygen species and cytokines. 29 , 30 , 31 Such processes create a tumour microenvironment conducive to invasion and metastasis. Peripheral blood eosinophilia and eosinophilic tissue infiltration have also been linked to advanced stages of MF, with tissue eosinophilic infiltration identified as an independent prognostic indicator of poor survival outcomes. 11 , 32 Concurrently, decreased lymphocyte levels may reflect impaired tumour surveillance, as lymphocytes regulate cell proliferation and angiogenesis by producing antitumour cytokines. 33 A previous study demonstrated that patients with Sézary syndrome exhibit not only reduced numbers of natural killer (NK) cells but also qualitative defects in NK‐cell function, suggesting that functional impairments in immune cells may also critically impact prognosis in CTCL. 34

Our findings align with previous studies emphasizing the prognostic relevance of inflammatory markers in MF, though prior research has primarily focused on NLR. For example, NLR was shown to be significantly higher in patients with advanced MF compared to those with early‐stage disease. 23 Similarly, another study reported a positive correlation between elevated NLR and disease progression. 22 However, conflicting results exist in the literature. One study found no significant association between NLR and clinical outcomes such as treatment demand or disease progression in early‐stage MF. 24 Additionally, NLR was not a significant prognostic indicator in a cohort that included erythrodermic CTCL patients. 25 Another study concluded that the prognostic utility of blood‐derived markers like NLR and PIV was limited, particularly in patients undergoing extracorporeal photopheresis. 35 These discrepancies may stem from differences in patient populations, disease‐stage distributions, study designs or cut‐off values for inflammatory markers. For instance, smaller sample sizes or shorter follow‐up periods in previous studies compared to ours may have limited their ability to detect significant associations. 25 , 35

Pruritus is the most common symptom in patients with MF, and its presence has been associated with poor prognosis, although the exact mechanism remains unknown. 36 A recent study demonstrated a significant correlation between pruritus and NLR in early‐stage MF, suggesting a potential link between pruritus and the systemic inflammatory milieu in these patients. 36 The findings of the present study further support the idea that pruritus may reflect systemic inflammation in MF and could have prognostic implications.

This study has several strengths, including its large cohort size and comprehensive evaluation of multiple blood inflammatory markers, which have been underexplored in MF. To our knowledge, this study is the first to address a critical gap in the literature by demonstrating the prognostic utility of composite markers such as PIV and SII in MF. However, there are notable limitations. The retrospective design inherently carries the potential for unrecognized biases, and the single‐centre setting may restrict the generalizability of our findings to broader populations. Additionally, the influence of concurrent inflammatory or infectious conditions on inflammatory marker levels could not be entirely excluded. Furthermore, the relatively short follow‐up duration for some patients may have underestimated the long‐term prognostic implications of these markers. Prospective multicentre studies with standardized protocols and extended follow‐up periods are needed to validate our findings and address these limitations.

In conclusion, this study highlights the prognostic significance of baseline blood inflammatory markers derived from routine blood samples, particularly PIV and SII, in MF. These markers could complement existing prognostic models, such as the ISCL/EORTC staging system, by providing additional insights into the systemic inflammatory and immune status of patients. Incorporating these simple and minimally invasive markers into clinical practice could enhance patient management by facilitating the identification of high‐risk individuals and guiding tailored work‐up and treatment strategies. Future research should focus on validating these findings in diverse populations and elucidating the biological mechanisms underlying the prognostic value of these markers in MF.
