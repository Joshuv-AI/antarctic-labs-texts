Pediatric Central Giant Cell Granuloma in the Nasal Cavity: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Renad Alhusayni, Abdulrahman Shareefi, Yasser A Sabbagh, Muhammad Raza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Central giant cell granuloma (CGCG) is a rare benign and locally aggressive osteolytic lesion that predominantly affects the mandible and maxilla in the head and neck region. Involvement of the nasal cavity is extremely uncommon in children, with only a few cases reported, and is typically managed via external or transoral surgical approaches. We report CGCG in a healthy 13-year-old girl presenting as an expansile lesion of the right nasal cavity with adjacent bony remodeling. Diagnosis was established through radiological and histopathologic evaluation. The tumor was resected entirely using an endoscopic endonasal technique without an external approach. The patient remained disease-free and asymptomatic at 36 months. This report is complemented by a concise literature review that addresses the pathogenesis, diagnostic considerations, and management strategies for CGCG. The case highlights the feasibility of a minimally invasive approach without external access for managing pediatric nasal CGCG. This technique achieved complete excision with excellent cosmetic and functional outcomes and is considered a safe alternative to more extensive surgical procedures.

Introduction

Central giant cell granuloma (CGCG) is an uncommon benign osteolytic lesion that may demonstrate locally aggressive behavior, driven by osteoclast-like cellular activity [1]. Histologically, it consists of a fibrovascular stroma containing numerous multinucleated giant cells, areas of hemorrhage, and occasional woven bone formation [2]. CGCG usually develops within the first three decades of life and predominantly affects women [3]. CGCG accounts for approximately 7% of all benign jaw tumors and has an estimated annual incidence of 1.1 cases per million population [4]. The mandible and maxilla are the most affected sites in the head and neck region [5]. However, other nearby structures, including the nasal cavity and the temporal bone, can also become involved in the disease process [5]. The exact etiology of CGCG remains unclear; however, it has been associated with factors such as prior trauma, inflammatory conditions, and possible genetic predisposition [4]. CGCG is broadly classified into aggressive and non-aggressive forms [1]. The non-aggressive variant is more common and typically presents as a slow-growing, painless lesion that may cause gradual cortical bone expansion [1]. In contrast, the aggressive variant is more frequently observed in younger patients and is characterized by rapid growth, larger lesion size often exceeding 5 cm, and a higher risk of recurrence [1]. Although imaging plays an important role in evaluating CGCG, the diagnosis is confirmed by histopathological examination [6]. Surgical management remains the mainstay of treatment [3]. The extent of surgery can range from conservative procedures, such as curettage, to more extensive resections, including maxillectomy or mandibulectomy with appropriate reconstruction [5]. There have been few cases of CGCG originating from the lateral wall of the nasal cavity. We report a pediatric case of a large giant cell tumor of the nasal cavity treated with endoscopic endonasal resection without an external approach by enucleation and curettage. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria [7].

Case presentation

A healthy 13-year-old girl was referred to the Department of Otorhinolaryngology with a complaint of progressive nasal obstruction, mostly on the right side. It had been increasing over the last six months. The patient reported epistaxis, facial deformity, and intermittent bloody epiphora. There was no history of pain, hyposmia, watery or purulent nasal discharge, facial numbness, teeth numbness, diplopia, visual disturbance, headache, or other neurologic symptoms. Pertinent negatives included the absence of prior trauma, previous sinonasal surgery, and a family history of similar disease. On physical examination, a reddish nasal mass was observed through the right nostril (Figure 1).

The mass was situated in the right nasal passage, arising from the floor of the nasal cavity and extending to the lower part of the nasal septum and the right inferior turbinate, with deviation of the nasal septum toward the left side. The mass completely occupied the right nasal cavity. Oral examination revealed a bulge in the right hard palate with no defect or fistula. There was no evidence of detectable cervical lymphadenopathy. A rigid nasal endoscope revealed dilated vessels with intact mucosa and a normal posterior one-third of the nasal cavity, including the nasopharynx.

Contrast-enhanced CT of the paranasal sinuses and MRI were performed, demonstrating an enhancing expansile soft tissue mass in the right nasal cavity that showed a low-attenuated area in the center. It was causing remodeling of adjacent bony structures, such as the alveolar process of the maxilla, thereby compressing and deviating the nasal septum to the left. It measured approximately 4 × 3 × 3.5 cm (Figures 2-4).

Various differential diagnoses were raised, including hemangioma, Brown tumor, extranodal natural killer/T-cell lymphoma (nasal type) (ENKTL), and CGCG.

Routine hematological and biochemical tests, including serum calcium, alkaline phosphatase, and parathyroid hormone levels, were within normal reference ranges. This was crucial in excluding a brown tumor related to hyperparathyroidism, which represents an important differential diagnosis of giant cell lesions.

Following a routine diagnostic evaluation, the patient underwent an endoscopic endonasal resection without an external approach utilizing 4-mm zero-degree and 30-degree telescopes. During surgery, local infiltration was performed using 3 cc of lidocaine with epinephrine 1:200,000. A topical decongestant, Xylometazoline hydrochloride 0.05%, was applied topically with a nasal pack. A right hemitransfixion incision was made with a continuation of an L-incision on the nasal floor anterior to the mass. Mucoperichondrial and mucoperiosteal flaps were elevated to expose the limits of the expanded bone. The lesion was then carefully dissected from the surrounding bone.

The mass was dissected and shelled out superiorly, followed by enucleation and curettage. A microdebrider, a 4.0-mm straight sinus blade (Tricut™, Medtronic, Dublin, Ireland), and a curette were used for complete removal. The hard palate bone was preserved. The medial boundary was defined by the maxillary crest and nasal septum. In contrast, the lateral boundary was formed by the inferior turbinate and the lateral nasal wall, including the pyriform aperture.

To confirm the diagnosis, an incisional biopsy of the right nasal lesion was performed. Histopathological examination demonstrated multiple lobules containing numerous multinucleated giant cells within a fibrovascular stroma, with areas of hemorrhage interspersed among bland spindle cells within an edematous stroma, and hemorrhage was prominent (Figures 5-7).

Discussion

This case report describes a rare CGCG arising from the nasal cavity floor and extending into the nasal septum in a healthy 13-year-old girl. CGCG involving the craniofacial skeleton is extremely uncommon, with only a limited number of cases reported in the literature [1,2,9,10]. Reports of CGCG in the nasal cavity with intracranial involvement are particularly scarce [3,5,9].

The diagnosis of CGCG requires a comprehensive approach integrating both imaging and histopathological evaluation [9]. Initial assessment is typically performed using CT and MRI, while definitive diagnosis is established through biopsy and histological analysis [2]. On CT, these lesions usually appear as well-defined expansile lytic masses, often demonstrating peripheral bone remodeling and occasional internal septations [4]. MRI findings commonly show areas of low signal intensity on both T1- and T2-weighted sequences, with heterogeneous signal characteristics within the lesion [4]. Variable contrast enhancement may also be observed [4]. Histologically, CGCG is characterized by numerous multinucleated giant cells distributed within a vascular stroma composed of spindle-shaped or epithelioid mononuclear cells [2,3]. Foci of osteoid formation may be present, supporting the diagnosis [6,9].

Although the literature on the treatment of CGCG is limited, surgery remains the cornerstone of disease management [10]. In addition to surgery, several non-surgical and adjuvant treatment options have been reported, including intralesional corticosteroids, interferon-α, calcitonin, and radiation therapy; however, these modalities are generally reserved for unresectable, recurrent, or aggressive lesions [3,9]. Surgical approaches include enucleation, curettage, functional endoscopic sinus surgery, and segmental resection [9]. The literature indicates that recurrence rates range from 11% to 35%, with lower recurrence following surgical resection (5.6-11.5%) compared with simple curettage (12.5-46%) [5]. Broad resection is typically reserved for cases with multiple recurrences or extensive infiltration of surrounding tissues [3,5]. In cases of large tumors, reconstructive surgery and airway management, including temporary tracheostomy, may be required [9].

Most previously reported cases have been managed using a transoral surgical approach, with generally favorable outcomes and low rates of complications and recurrence [3,6,9]. To the best of our knowledge, a single case report has described the use of an endoscopic-assisted endonasal approach combined with transoral excision for the treatment of CGCG through enucleation and curettage. This approach resulted in successful management, with no requirement for additional medical therapy, no significant morbidity, and no evidence of recurrence at nine- and four-year follow-up [6].

Considering the lesion’s location and the feasibility of achieving complete endoscopic resection, an endoscopic endonasal approach was selected over transoral or external approaches to minimize morbidity while ensuring complete tumor removal.

In contrast to previously reported cases, endoscopic endonasal resection was employed in our case without the need for a transoral or external approach. This technique provided superior intraoperative visualization, allowing complete tumor removal under direct vision and potentially reducing the risk of recurrence. The patient experienced rapid postoperative recovery without requiring reconstructive surgery, odontogenic intervention, or speech rehabilitation. At 36 months of follow-up, there was no evidence of recurrence or new symptoms. This favorable outcome underscores the effectiveness of the selected surgical approach in achieving durable disease control.

This report has several limitations, including its single-patient design and the rarity of CGCG in the nasal cavity, which limit generalizability. Additionally, longer follow-up is required to better evaluate long-term outcomes and recurrence risk.

Conclusions

Endoscopic endonasal enucleation and curettage represent an effective and minimally invasive treatment option for selected pediatric patients with nasal giant cell tumors. This approach allows complete tumor removal with excellent visualization while avoiding external approaches and their associated morbidity. Further research is needed to determine the benefits, risks, and efficacy of surgical approaches in the management of CGCG.
